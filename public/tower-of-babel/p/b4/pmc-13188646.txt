Stakeholders’ perspectives on community health nursing practice in the healthcare system of Iran: a qualitative study
Authors: Iman Sharifikia, Jamileh Farokhzadian, Azam Hosseinnejad, Camelia Rohani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHealth policies shape how healthcare systems organize authority, allocate resources and define professional roles. In many healthcare systems, redefining the scope and positioning of community-based providers has emerged as a policy response to demographic transitions, increasing chronic disease burdens and resource constraints. In Iran, community health nursing practice (CHNP) has been formally endorsed but remains weakly institutionalized within healthcare system governance and implementation structures. This study aimed to explore stakeholders’ perspectives on the roles and tasks of community health nurses to identify policy, governance and system-level factors that influence the practice of community health nurses within the healthcare system of Iran.MethodsThis is a descriptive qualitative study that was conducted between February 2024 and May 2025 using a conventional content analysis approach. The study is part of a broader research project with focus on community health nursing. Data were collected through semi-structured and in-depth individuals’ interviews with 18 participants who were selected by purposive sampling. They included faculty members, nursing directors, health deputies, the director of a comprehensive health centre and healthcare workers. Data analysis was guided by Graneheim and Lundman framework. Rigour of the study was ensured using the four criteria of qualitative research, and reporting adhered to the COnsolidated criteria for REporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guideline.ResultsFollowing iterative analysis cycles for extracting the meaning units, 385 initial codes were generated. These were organized into 11 subcategories and 3 main categories, consisting of “Establishing a job position,” “Facilitating integrated care delivery” and “Activating community engagement to promote well-being.” These three categories reflected the overarching theme of the study: “The gap between knowledge and practice of community health nurses.”ConclusionsThe findings showed a discrepancy between the existing knowledge and competencies of community health nurses and their actual roles within the healthcare system of Iran. Stakeholders described challenges related to role definition, integration into healthcare service delivery and participation in decision-making processes, which were perceived as influencing the implementation of CHNP within the primary healthcare network of the country.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12961-026-01472-7.

Introduction

The rising older population, increasing prevalence of chronic diseases, shortage of physicians and financial pressures have forced many countries to reassess their healthcare systems. These reforms often involve redefining professional roles, restructuring the workforce and strengthening the position of health professions, particularly nursing [1]. At the same time, challenges such as nurse shortages, unfavourable working conditions, the gap between education and practice and demographic transitions have further highlighted the need for policy reform [2, 3]. Nurses as the largest group of healthcare providers, are at the forefront of these reforms and are widely recognized for strengthening primary healthcare (PHC) [3]. This issue is also of critical importance in Iran and requires special attention.

The healthcare system of Iran has a combination of public and private sectors, with the public sector primarily managed by medical universities. Since the 1980s, Iran has been regarded as one of the region’s most successful countries in reducing child mortality and controlling communicable diseases [4]. This centralized system, supervised by the Ministry of Health and Medical Education, provides services nationwide through an extensive network of health houses and comprehensive health centres [5]. Despite these achievements, persistent challenges – including limited financial resources, unequal service distribution and a shortage of trained community health nurses – have reduced system efficiency [6]. Strengthening Community Health Nursing Practice (CHNP) and integrating community health nurses into this network could help address these gaps and enhance the effectiveness of PHC services in Iran [7].

Health policy in any country reflects societal values and priorities, often guided by principles such as quality improvement, self-care promotion and professional development. CHNP represents a specialized field that integrates nursing science with social sciences and public health [8–11]. Its aim is to promote physical, mental and social well-being through educational, clinical and social services in community-based settings [8]. Services provided by community health nurses include health assessment, education, counselling, home care and chronic disease follow-up. Importantly, they act as a bridge between communities and the PHC system by aligning services with health policies and population needs [12, 13].

Globally, many countries have formally incorporated CHNP into their healthcare systems, and in some cases, it has replaced physician-centred models [14–16]. In Asia, for example, Japan has elevated the role of community health nurses in response to population ageing [17]. While similar trends are relevant to Iran, structural and policy differences have limited the integration of CHNP into the national healthcare system of the country. Although PHC is the foundation of Iran’s healthcare service delivery model emphasizing prevention, comprehensive care, community participation and equity in access [3] the professional role of CHNP has not yet been formally institutionalized [18, 19]. Despite the central role of PHC in Iran, no national policy has formally institutionalized CHNP within the PHC network, and existing workforce policies continue to overlook community health nurses as independent healthcare providers, reinforcing a physician-centred model of care [18, 20]. Although academic education in community health nursing has expanded, weak implementation of deployment, role utilization and career policies has perpetuated the education–practice gap [21].

Despite initiatives to promote community-based services in Iran, community health nurses still face structural, managerial and legal barriers [21, 22]. Policymakers often maintain a hospital-centred approach, using CHNPs mainly for secondary prevention [23, 24]. In addition, the lack of formal role definitions, support structures and alignment between academic education and field practice has hindered professional development [25, 26]. These challenges highlight the need for deeper policy analysis. Previous studies have explored nurses’ practical contributions in family physician programs, home visits and comprehensive health centres [20], and identified professional challenges and education–practice gaps [18, 27], However, little attention has been given to policy processes, decision-making and stakeholders’ perspectives [18]. Overall, the core policy problem lies in inadequate policy design and weak implementation rather than agenda-setting or evaluation [26]. Current policy arrangements systematically constrain CHNP by limiting role definition, restricting professional autonomy and prioritizing hospital-centred care over community-based services [18].

To address this gap, the present study applied Walt and Gilson’s policy triangle [28] to analyse the role of community health nurses in the healthcare system of Iran (Fig. 1). This widely used model evaluates four key dimensions – content, context, process and actor – and their interactions in shaping and implementing health policies. Content refers to policy goals, operational plans and system-level priorities. Process encompasses agenda setting, policy formulation, implementation and evaluation. Context considers social, economic, political, cultural and environmental influences. Finally, actors include all stakeholders involved in or affected by policy formulation, implementation and evaluation such as health policymakers, nurse managers, faculty members and community health nurses [28]. The model has been extensively applied in developing countries and in nursing research to promote professional roles [29, 30]. Given the complexity of health policy processes and the need to capture stakeholders’ experiences, perceptions and interactions, a qualitative method is particularly appropriate for exploring how community health nurses are positioned and enacted within the healthcare system of Iran [31]. Accordingly, this qualitative study was designed to explore stakeholders’ experiences and perspectives regarding the roles, tasks and practice of community health nurses in the healthcare system of Iran.Fig. 1Walt and Gilson’s policy triangle model [28]

Methods

Study design

This descriptive qualitative study was conducted between February 2024 and May 2025 using conventional content analysis approach. The study is part of a broader research project aimed at developing a comprehensive healthcare service package for community health nurses in Iran. Ethical approval was obtained from the university ethics committee (No. IR.KMU.REC.1402.463). All procedures adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) guidelines. Written informed consent form was obtained from all participants. The study reporting followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guideline (Supplementary File 1) [32].

The policy triangle model informed the study at a conceptual level. It guided purposive sampling by ensuring the inclusion of key policy actors and informed the development of interview questions with attention to actors, processes and contextual factors related to community health nursing [28]. Data analysis was conducted inductively using conventional content analysis approach, while the policy triangle was applied as an interpretive lens to contextualize the emergent findings.

Research question

What are the experiences and perspectives of stakeholders regarding the position and performance of community health nurses in the healthcare system of Iran?

Participants

Data were collected through semi-structured, in-depth and individual interviews with 18 purposively selected participants from different provinces in Iran to capture diverse professional roles and experiences within the healthcare system and nursing education. Inclusion criteria were health policymakers, nurse managers, community health nurses, faculty deans and faculty members with at least 5 years of experience in community health nursing. All participants held current positions with direct involvement in policy formulation, decision-making, education or implementation related to community health nursing services. These stakeholder groups were selected because they represented key actors involved in policy-making, education, management and service delivery related to community health nursing. Exclusion criteria included fewer than 5 years of experience.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured, in-depth individual interviews with 18 participants using an interview guide (Supplementary File 2). Maximum variation sampling was operationalized by deliberately selecting participants from different provinces, organizational levels and professional roles within the healthcare system and nursing education. Key characteristics prioritized during sampling included geographic location, level of policy or managerial responsibility and years of professional experience in community health nursing. Sampling decisions evolved concurrently with data collection and analysis, with subsequent participants selected to expand emerging categories and ensure theoretical saturation. Interviews were conducted using an interview guide. Each session began with an introduction of the interviewer, a brief overview of the study’s objectives and a few introductory questions. To obtain more in-depth information, the main interview questions included the following:(1) How do you see the practice of community health nurses in the current healthcare system of the country?(2) Based on your experience, in which areas of CHNP do community health nurses have the capacity to play an effective role?(3) In the current healthcare system, in which areas of CHNP are community health nurses underutilized?(4) Which of the healthcare services needed by clients can be provided independently by community health nurses?

We conducted in-depth, semi-structured interviews to give participants the freedom to share their perspectives, which aligns with the inductive approach of this qualitative study. The interviews were conducted by two researchers (I.S.H. and A.H.), both with academic backgrounds in community health nursing and prior training in qualitative research methods, under the supervision of two experienced qualitative researchers (J.F. and C.R.). Although the interviewers were familiar with the field of community health nursing, they had no direct managerial or policy-making roles in the studied settings, which helped minimize power imbalance and potential bias. The interviews, lasting between 30 and 110 min, were conducted either in person or online. All interviews were digitally recorded, transcribed verbatim and systematically coded. Simultaneously, field notes were taken to capture contextual details and enhance the accuracy of the data analysis. Theoretical saturation was achieved when new interviews no longer generated novel codes [33]. This data collection method provided rich and diverse information pertinent to the research question.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted concurrently with data collection using conventional content analysis approach as described by Graneheim and Lundman [34]. This approach was selected due to limited prior theoretical and empirical evidence, allowing categories and themes to emerge inductively from the data [35]. Reflexivity was addressed throughout data collection and analysis through team discussions, reflective field notes and ongoing supervision to identify and critically examine potential assumptions and biases [36]. During the data collection period, no major national policy changes related to community health nursing were implemented and potential temporal variations were managed through concurrent analysis and constant comparison across interview. Transcripts were read repeatedly to achieve immersion. Meaning units were identified, condensed and coded. Similar codes were grouped into subcategories and categories on the basis of conceptual similarity. Categories were continuously refined to ensure internal consistency and clear distinctions. Themes were then derived through interpretive analysis, focusing on underlying meanings. MAXQDA 2018 software was used for data management and coding [37].

Rigour of the study

The trustworthiness of the findings was established using the four criteria of credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability [38].

Credibility was enhanced through a prolonged engagement with the data and the research setting over a 9-month period. This included continuous observation and the meticulous recording of field notes to ensure a deep and accurate understanding. In addition, peer checking was conducted monthly with two community health nursing faculty members, allowing for a thorough discussion of emerging data and interpretations. The research team’s background and potential biases were also regularly considered to maintain objectivity.

Dependability and confirmability were addressed through an audit trail, where interview transcripts, extracted codes and categories were systematically documented. These materials were regularly reviewed by the research team and three independent qualitative research experts to verify the consistency and objectivity of the analysis.

Transferability was bolstered by employing maximum variation sampling, which allowed the collection of a diverse range of opinions, experiences and observations from participants across different provinces and job positions.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the participants

A total of 18 participants were included in this study. The sample comprised 10 faculty members specialized in community health nursing (who also held positions in health policy), two nursing directors, three health deputies, one director of a comprehensive health centre and two healthcare workers from urban centres. The participants’ mean age was 49.5 ± 5.9 years, with a range of 35–65 years. The mean work experience was 22.4 ± 5.37 years. Overarching theme: the gap between knowledge and practice of community health nurses

Analysis of the interview data revealed an overarching theme: the gap between knowledge and practice of community health nurses. Participants consistently described community health nurses as knowledgeable and skilled professionals whose competencies were not fully utilized within the current healthcare system. They emphasized a discrepancy between the professional capabilities of community health nurses and the scope of activities they are able to perform in practice. The findings were organized into three main categories: establishing a job position, facilitating integrated care delivery and activating community engagement to promote well-being (Table 2). Each category comprises several subcategories derived from participants’ accounts and is illustrated with representative quotation.

Category 1: Establishing a job position

Participants consistently emphasized that the role of community health nurses within the Iranian healthcare system requires fundamental re-evaluation. They argued that redefining these roles could more effectively leverage the skills of community health nurses in providing primary healthcare, which would in turn lead to greater job stability. Community health nurses should be formally recognized as key actors in health policies. Participants also highlighted the need for developing standardized service packages that align with the country’s evolving epidemiological landscape and population needs. These measures could pave the way for a more established position for community health nursing within the Iranian health system.

Redefining roles

Participants stated that the duties and responsibilities of community health nurses were not clearly specified and often overlapped with those of other healthcare providers. They described existing role definitions as outdated and misaligned with current community health needs.“Community health nurses still do not have a clear role in the system. If the roles are redefined correctly, the nurse knows where to intervene and we know our expectations.” (N15).

“Community health nurses still do not have a clear role in the system. If the roles are redefined correctly, the nurse knows where to intervene and we know our expectations.” (N15).

New health policymaking

Participants described current policies as predominantly hospital-oriented, with limited attention to community-based nursing roles. They indicated that community health nurses were often not explicitly addressed in policy documents related to primary healthcare.“For years, our policies have been designed with hospitals in mind, not the community. When a new policy is created without a clear role for community health nurses, it is inevitable that our influence diminishes. It is time to rewrite these policies based on the real needs of the people and the community – only then can community health nurses truly find their place.” (N4).

“For years, our policies have been designed with hospitals in mind, not the community. When a new policy is created without a clear role for community health nurses, it is inevitable that our influence diminishes. It is time to rewrite these policies based on the real needs of the people and the community – only then can community health nurses truly find their place.” (N4).

Providing service packages in new policymaking areas

Participants discussed the absence of clearly defined service packages for community health nurses. They noted that nursing services were often fragmented and not formally structured within policy frameworks.“When community health nurses can provide specific service packages, such as for diabetes control or elderly care, their role becomes undeniable. We need to present our services in a way that policymakers can easily see and integrate into their programs. Currently, most of our services are scattered. If they were offered as coherent packages, the public would benefit more and policymakers would recognize the immense value of community health nurses.” (N5).

“When community health nurses can provide specific service packages, such as for diabetes control or elderly care, their role becomes undeniable. We need to present our services in a way that policymakers can easily see and integrate into their programs. Currently, most of our services are scattered. If they were offered as coherent packages, the public would benefit more and policymakers would recognize the immense value of community health nurses.” (N5).

Category 2: Facilitating integrated care delivery

Participants described challenges in providing care for diverse groups, particularly individuals with chronic conditions and vulnerable populations, within the current healthcare structure. They referred to the absence of integrated care models and clear referral pathways and noted that community health nursing practice is not consistently positioned within existing service delivery frameworks. Participants reported that community health nurses are involved in both direct care and coordination-related activities across departments and organizations, although these roles are often informal and inconsistently supported. They also described limitations in communication and referral processes between different levels of care, which were perceived as affecting continuity of services. Several participants emphasized the role of community health nurses in linking patients, healthcare teams and community resources within the health system.

Participation in healthcare delivery

Participants described the provision of direct services by community health nurses as part of their routine practice, particularly in relation to patients with chronic conditions and vulnerable groups. They referred to community health nurses’ involvement in patient follow-up, education and communication with patients, families and other members of the healthcare team. Participants reported that these activities were perceived as contributing to continuity of care and greater coherence in service delivery, while also noting variability in how consistently such roles were supported across settings.“When we follow up with a chronic patient, we must communicate with the doctor, nutritionist, psychologist and even the social worker. But there is still no regular structure for this interaction, which has often led to information not being exchanged among the healthcare team. This is where the absence of a nurse is felt” (N10).

“When we follow up with a chronic patient, we must communicate with the doctor, nutritionist, psychologist and even the social worker. But there is still no regular structure for this interaction, which has often led to information not being exchanged among the healthcare team. This is where the absence of a nurse is felt” (N10).

Inter-sectoral coordination and management

Participants described the absence of clear mechanisms for inter-sectoral coordination and management within the current healthcare system. They reported challenges related to communication among medical, nursing, public health and other relevant departments, which they perceived as contributing to fragmented care experiences for patients. Participants also referred to instances in which community health nurses were informally involved in coordinating care across sectors, while noting that the lack of formal structures for such coordination was viewed as a barrier to continuity of care.“We had a heart failure patient who was constantly moving between the hospital and our center. If a mechanism had existed to coordinate the hospital doctor, the follow-up nurse, our center, the patient and their family would not have had to endure such a difficult and confusing experience” (N6).

“We had a heart failure patient who was constantly moving between the hospital and our center. If a mechanism had existed to coordinate the hospital doctor, the follow-up nurse, our center, the patient and their family would not have had to endure such a difficult and confusing experience” (N6).

Intra- and inter-organizational collaboration

Participants described limited collaboration and inconsistent communication within and between healthcare organizations, including hospitals, health centres and support institutions. They reported that this lack of coordination often led to confusion for patients and disruptions in service continuity. Participants also referred to instances in which community health nurses were involved in coordinating care across different levels of the health system, although such roles were described as informal and not systematically defined.“If there was proper coordination between hospitals, health centres and even support organizations like social welfare, we would prevent many problems for patients. For example, a cancer patient undergoing chemotherapy needed psychological support in addition to treatment, but unfortunately, these departments worked separately” (N14).

“If there was proper coordination between hospitals, health centres and even support organizations like social welfare, we would prevent many problems for patients. For example, a cancer patient undergoing chemotherapy needed psychological support in addition to treatment, but unfortunately, these departments worked separately” (N14).

Improving patient referral processes between different levels of healthcare system

Participants described the absence of a clear and organized referral system as a challenge within the healthcare system. They reported that community health nurses were involved in guiding patients through different levels of care. Participants also referred to the role of community health nurses in following up patients’ conditions over time and facilitating communication among members of the healthcare team as part of current practice experiences.“A patient needed a cardiac consultation, but it took 3 weeks to see a specialist. This shows that the referral system is not only failing to guide patients timely but is also wasting their time.” (N4).“The biggest problem we face is the lack of a clear referral system for patients, especially when they move between primary care and the hospital. A community health nurse can play a key role in this because she/he is close to the patient and understands the system. If this role is properly defined, a significant amount of time and money can be saved” (N9).

Category 3: Activating community engagement to promote well-being

Participants emphasized the importance of community involvement in the delivery of community health nursing practice. They described experiences in which community health nurses engaged community members in decision-making processes, provided health-related education and participated in locally oriented training activities. Participants also referred to interactions between community health nurses, volunteers and social institutions and described the role of community health nurses as facilitating communication between the community and the healthcare system.

Active community participation in health-related decision-making

Participants described community participation in health-related decision-making as an important aspect of community health nursing practice. They referred to situations in which community health nurses listened to community members’ needs and concerns and involved them in discussions related to health programs. Participants also described the role of ongoing interaction and trust-building between community health nurses and community members in shaping these experiences.“When we went to a village for training, no one listened. But as we spoke to them sincerely and heard their concerns, their attitudes gradually changed. I realized that community health nurses can truly be a bridge between people and the health system” (N2).“People often believe that health decisions are made by others, with their opinions having no influence. However, when a community health nurse listens to and conveys their concerns, people begin to feel like partners in the decision-making process. This not only builds trust but also encourages their cooperation in implementing health programs” (N8).

Building social support networks to improve community health

Participants described the presence of social support networks as an element influencing community health activities. They referred to experiences in which community health nurses interacted with families, community groups and health volunteers. Participants also described training activities and direct communication between community health nurses and community members and characterized the role of community health nurses as facilitating participation within these interactions.“We visited areas where people were very isolated from health services due to a lack of information. When the community health nurse, in collaboration with teachers and local officials, spoke with people and explained things in simple terms, their motivation for self-care significantly increased” (N1).“A community health nurse is not just a service provider, but a networker. If the nurse brings together families, volunteer groups and even local leaders, the burden on the system will be reduced and people feel like they are part of the solution. This is where the nurse’s role in the healthcare system becomes more prominent” (N9).

Designing culturally indigenous interventions to improve healthy lifestyles

Participants described the design of culturally grounded interventions as part of their experiences with community health activities. They referred to the importance of understanding local beliefs, values and needs when developing health-related programs. Participants also reported that alignment of interventions with local cultural contexts influenced community acceptance and engagement.“We had a project in nomadic areas that combined health education with traditional storytelling. Because it was from the heart of the people’s culture, it was very easily accepted and helped improve health” (N11).“To help people in a village or neighbourhood change their lifestyles, we need to avoid using generic programs developed for urban areas. These communities have unique beliefs and traditions and we must speak their language and understand their culture to be effective. For example, people are more likely to adopt new nutritional recommendations if we adapt them to the local foods they already eat” (N3).

Self-care and health literacy training program for different groups

Participants described experiences related to self-care promotion and health education within communities. They referred to training activities that were adapted to local language, cultural context and everyday needs, particularly when working with vulnerable and older populations. Participants also reported that community health nurses’ awareness of cultural and linguistic differences shaped the way educational activities were delivered and perceived.“Once, I was leading a diabetes class for older adults in a local neighbourhood. Initially, they seemed disengaged, simply observing from their seats. But when I began using examples of diet and care that featured their own local foods, everything changed. They started asking questions and actively participating. It was then that I realized people learn and apply information more effectively when the teaching is adapted to their own cultural context” (N12).

Discussion

This qualitative study was conducted to clarify the perspectives and experiences of stakeholders regarding the role and tasks of community health nurses in the healthcare system of Iran. The findings revealed an overarching theme: the gap between knowledge and practice of community health nurses. This theme highlights that despite the professional capabilities of community health nurses, their potential is not being fully realized.

The data analysis also showed stakeholders’ solutions to help community health nurses strengthen their position in the health system, facilitate integrated care and increase community participation. When viewed through the lens of Walt and Gilson’s model, these findings underscore that advancing CHNP in the healthcare system of Iran requires a coordinated approach that addresses policies, processes, social context and collaboration among various stakeholders.

Establishing a job position

Our findings identified three key issues related to establishing a job position for community health nurses, with the most significant being the lack of a clear professional role in policy-making and public service delivery. This finding is consistent with previous research; for example, an integrative review identified limited involvement in policymaking and the absence of an organizational position as major challenges for community health nursing in Iran, emphasizing the need for clearer role definitions and integration into primary care policies [18]. However, our analysis moves beyond descriptive alignment with prior studies by demonstrating that this role ambiguity is embedded within broader governance and power arrangements rather than representing isolated policy gaps [39]. As a result, the translation of documented professional competencies into practice among community health nurses remains structurally constrained.

Stabilizing the position of CHNP within the Iranian health system therefore requires coordinated reform across all dimensions of the Walt and Gilson policy triangle. These include the development of clear policies and service packages (Content) [18], meaningful engagement of nurses in decision-making processes (Process) [39], attention to social and cultural conditions shaping care delivery (Context) [40] and strengthened interaction among nurses, policymakers and communities (Actors) [41]. Evidence shows that nurses are predominantly absorbed into hospital-based roles further illustrates how organizational priorities shape workforce deployment, thereby weakening the visibility and functionality of community-based nursing roles [27]. This pattern suggests that the marginalization of CHNP is not incidental but reflects a systemic preference for curative, physician-led models of care [42].

Stakeholders repeatedly emphasized the need for clearly defined roles and formal job descriptions for community health nurses within the “content” dimension of the Walt and Gilson model, this emphasis points to the absence of a binding legal and organizational mandate for community health nurses within the health system [41]. However, our findings indicate that role clarification alone is insufficient unless it is accompanied by structural authority and institutional legitimacy within policymaking processes [40]. Without such authority, formal role definitions risk remaining symbolic rather than operational.

This limitation becomes more evident when examining the process dimension of policymaking. Although existing studies highlight the importance of nurses’ participation in health policy formulation [43]. Our findings revealed that, in the context of Iran, such participation is largely advisory or consultative rather than decision-making in nature [39]. Consequently, community health nurses have limited influence over policy outcomes directly affecting primary and community-based care.

The challenge of institutionalizing service packages further illustrates the interaction between policy content and context. While international evidence demonstrates that well-designed community-based service packages can enhance equity and system effectiveness [44].

Our findings suggest that their implementation in Iran is constrained by centralized governance structures, budgetary rigidity and limited responsiveness to local epidemiological and social conditions [40]. In this regard, our study adds to existing literature by showing that without contextual adaptation and decentralized authority, such service packages risk remaining policy rhetoric rather than operational reality [43].

While international evidence demonstrates the effectiveness of community-based service packages [45].

Overall, our findings indicate that strengthening the position of community health nurses requires more than technical or procedural reforms. CHNP underutilization is conceptualized as a manifestation of structural power asymmetries, centralized governance and entrenched professional hierarchies within the healthcare system [3]. Addressing this gap therefore necessitates coordinated change across policy content, policymaking processes, contextual conditions and actor relationships to enable sustainable and effective community-based nursing practice.

Facilitating integrated care delivery

Our findings indicate that challenges in integrated care delivery are not just operational but are deeply embedded within the organizational structure of the primary healthcare system of Iran. While community health nurses are positioned close to communities and vulnerable populations, their ability to link services across different departments and levels of care is limited by fragmented governance and weak referral infrastructures, particularly in primary healthcare settings [43, 44].

From a policy systems perspective, improving CHNP requires more than just policy endorsement. Our findings reveal that the absence of structured coordination mechanisms across health sectors reflects broader systemic weaknesses in policy implementation, rather than a lack of willingness for professional collaboration [18]. This aligns with prior studies that emphasize the necessity for nurses to engage with physicians, health managers and institutions in order to reduce service fragmentation and duplication [46, 47].

Within the framework of Walt and Gilson’s model, these findings highlight significant gaps in both the “process” and “actors” dimensions [48, 49]. Specifically, community health nurses are expected to facilitate integrated care, but they are not formally embedded within decision-making hierarchies or operational planning structures, which limits their ability to effectively contribute [50].

In this model, the community health nurse plays a role beyond just providing direct services; they serve as facilitators of integrated care and advocates for patients within referral systems and broader professional interactions. However, while communication among patients, families and healthcare providers is considered a key nursing function [14, 48]. Our analysis showed that communication alone cannot compensate for the lack of formalized coordination authority and system-wide support [18]. This highlights a structural mismatch between the roles expected of community health nurses and the actual institutional capacity to support them.

Inter-sectoral coordination and referral management further illustrate this challenge. Although these functions are aligned with the “process” dimension of Walt and Gilson’s model [51], their inconsistent implementation in Iran is a direct result of fragmented accountability across organizations and care levels [52].

Consistent with national evidence, the lack of interdisciplinary collaboration and inter-organizational coordination continues to be a major barrier to nursing effectiveness in primary healthcare [1, 3]. Our study extends this body of literature by framing these barriers not as isolated organizational failures, but as outcomes of centralized governance and weak horizontal integration within the healthcare system [18].

Overall, the Walt and Gilson model emphasizes that effective integrated care depends on coordinated interactions among actors and the successful implementation of policy processes [50]. Our findings conceptualized the role of community health nurses as being positioned at the interface of policy and practice, but constrained by limited institutional authority to operationalize integration, which ultimately undermines the effectiveness of primary healthcare delivery [11].

Activating community engagement to promote well-being

Our findings indicate that community engagement in CHNP is shaped not only by professional capacity but also by structural and contextual conditions that regulate how communities interact with the health system in Iran [18]. While participants emphasized community empowerment and participation, these were framed more as aspirational responses to existing systemic gaps rather than as routine practices embedded in the healthcare system.

Within the Walt and Gilson policy model, this pattern reflects tensions in the “context” and “actors” dimensions, where limited social trust, centralized decision-making and weak institutional support significantly constrain meaningful community involvement [25]. Our findings suggest that barriers such as insufficient cultural support, fragile community infrastructures and limited motivational resources are symptoms of broader governance and resource allocation challenges rather than isolated operational problems [51, 53].

Although active community participation is widely recognized as essential for effective health policymaking [50]. Our study demonstrated that in the context of Iran, opportunities for such participation remain largely consultative rather than influential, limiting communities’ actual role in shaping health priorities [54]. This finding nuance existing literature by showing how the development of local social support networks further illustrates this dynamic. While collaboration with families, public institutions and community actors aligns with the “actors” and “social context” components of the Walt and Gilson policy model [42]. Furthermore, our findings revealed that these networks often depend on informal efforts by community health nurses rather than stable institutional arrangements, which raises concerns about sustainability [27].

Similarly, culturally indigenous interventions, which were described as effective in principle, face challenges in scalability. Their integration into formal service delivery structures is hindered by limited policy backing and inconsistent institutional support, despite being in alignment with the “context” component of the model [24, 55]. This reveals a gap between culturally informed practice and policy-level support, impeding broader implementation.

Self-care and health literacy initiatives similarly reflect this mismatch. While these programs correspond to the “content” dimension of the Walt and Gilson model [56]. Our analysis indicated that their impact is uneven, primarily due to variability in local resources, organizational commitment and inter-sectoral coordination [20]. As a result, while community engagement increases service use, it does not consistently lead to structural recognition of community health nurses within policymaking arenas.

Taken together, these findings suggest that community engagement in the healthcare system of Iran is constrained by centralized governance structures, limited decentralization of decision-making, and the marginal position of nursing within policy hierarchies [44]. Rather than viewing community health nurses solely as facilitators of participation, our study conceptualized them as intermediaries operating within structural limits that shape how community voices are mobilized and valued.

Given Iran’s demographic transition, resource constraints and the historically low influence of nursing in health policy, the gap between community-oriented mandates and institutional realities becomes a defining feature of CHNP [57]. This analysis extends existing literature by positioning community engagement not only as a professional practice issue but as a reflection of broader power relations and governance arrangements within the healthcare system.

Strengths and limitations

This study is part of a broader research project with a stable policy during the running project, and no major system-level changes affecting community Health Nursing roles occurred during the data collection period. One of the strengths of this study is the use of in-depth qualitative interviews with various key informants from diverse regions of the country. This broad geographical and professional range of participants enriched the data and increased the content validity of our findings. However, we also faced limitations. The small number of nurses working in comprehensive health centres made it difficult to access their experiences. In addition, the geographical distance and busy schedules of some participants posed a challenge to conducting face-to-face interviews, a problem we largely overcame by using online platforms like Skype and WhatsApp. The predominance of academically affiliated participants and the limited inclusion of national-level policymakers and other professional groups may have influenced the academic orientation of the findings and reduced the reflectivity of opposing perspectives. Future studies should therefore include senior health policymakers, financial decision-makers and other professional actors to capture broader policy-level viewpoints and potential institutional resistance.

Conclusions

This study identified a gap between the professional knowledge and competencies of community health nurses and their actual utilization within the healthcare system of Iran. The findings highlighted challenges related to unclear role definition, limited integration of CHNP into service delivery structures, and policy orientations that place less emphasis on preventive and community-based services. Participants described weaknesses in policymaking and implementation processes as factors influencing the positioning of community health nursing within the healthcare system. Overall, the results illustrate how the current policy and organizational context shapes the practice of community health nurses and their role at the interface between health policies and community needs.
