The complex nature of agency attribution: Neurophysiological signatures associated to monitoring self vs. external erroneous actions
Authors: Alba Gomez-Andres, Toni Cunillera, Xim Cerda-Company, David Cucurell, Antoni Rodriguez-Fornells
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAgency attribution refers to the ability to recognize ourselves as the authors of our actions and their consequences and is central to subjective experience and individual responsibility. Across three event-related potential (ERP) experiments, we characterized electrophysiological and oscillatory neural responses associated with agency prediction errors in healthy participants. Experiments 1 and 3 showed that incongruent hand movements (content agency prediction errors) elicited parietal P600-delta oscillatory activity linked to the attribution of agency to an external source, possibly reflecting judgments of external control and the updating of predictive mental models. In contrast, temporal agency prediction errors induced by delayed movement outcomes (400 ms) reduced internal agency attribution and elicited feedback correct-related positivity (FCRP) and feedback-related negativity (FRN) during correct and erroneous responses, respectively, likely reflecting increased uncertainty and the reactivation of error-monitoring processes. These findings may help clarify neural mechanisms underlying disrupted agency in pathological conditions and the phenomenological experience of “mineness”.

Introduction

In our daily life experience, we usually do not have trouble distinguishing our actions from those of others. Recent work on the sensory-motor basis of the Self distinguishes two key aspects of self-awareness: (1) the sense of ownership, by which we recognize our body as our own and as the basis of our experience with the world1 and (2) the sense of agency, the feeling of being the author of one’s own actions and their effects.1 This experience of being the agent is a very fluid and non-conscious ongoing flow of anticipations and sensory feedback2 about which we practically never have any doubt. Indeed, this positive agency attribution might index a default state of being in control of our actions,3 only made accessible to consciousness under specific metacognitive interrogation4 or after the elicitation of agency prediction error (i.e., mismatch between the predicted and the actual sensory consequence of a movement).

When explaining the elicitation of agency prediction errors, classic accounts rely on the comparator model,5,6 which suggests that agency attribution basically arises from internal cueing determined by the comparison between the sensory predicted outcome (i.e., efference copy) and the actual external consequences. From this perspective, a default internal agency attribution is usually present, only disturbed whenever a mismatch between the intended/predicted vs. actual outcome occurs, leading to an agency prediction error and to an external attribution of agency for the action. As the forward mechanism allows us to make very precise predictions on the expected action-outcomes, any evidence of content inconsistency or temporal asynchrony might elicit an agency prediction error. But this account has encountered difficulties explaining important evidence: (1) internal agency attribution seems resistant even when long delays (e.g., 1100 ms) are interposed between our actions and their outcomes,7,8 (2) we tend to overestimate our internal attribution even when we are not the actual agents responsible for the actions observed,9 and (3) it cannot easily accommodate clinical cases reporting agency disturbances in presence of preserved efference signals.7,8,10 To account for these objections and the complex nature of agency, alternative theories emphasize the involvement of higher-level cognitive processes that evaluate the effect of outcome-related information without the need to access internal predictive signals.9 Synofzik and colleagues11 proposed an integrative two-step model, including a lower-level sensorimotor predictive process, giving rise to the feeling of agency, built upon the processing of internal cues, and a higher-order, postdictive belief-based mechanism supporting judgements of agency, influenced by internal and external cues. From this integrative perspective, the attribution of agency can be interpreted as posterior according to the Bayesian cue integration framework,12 determined by the weighting and reliability of both internal and external cues.

One of the main problems on arbitrating between these different accounts is that most of the behavioral measures employed are unable to differentiate between predictive (internal) and postdictive (external) components that contribute to the emergence of agency. Furthermore, fMRI studies are unable to disentangle the specific functional role of certain regions commonly observed during agency attribution (e.g., angular gyrus vs. pre-motor/supplementary motor area (SMA)/dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC)), mostly due to a lack of temporal resolution.13,14,15,16 In the present study, relying on the precise timing of electroencephalography (EEG), and the fact that predictive and postdictive signals become available to the cognitive system at different time points, we aimed to provide new evidence on the neurophysiological basis implicated in agency attribution by inducing agency prediction errors using a modified version of a Flanker task (Figure 1).17 We measured event-related potentials (ERPs) and oscillatory activity across three experiments (Table 1), inducing agency prediction errors in terms of action-outcome content consistency (experiment 1 and 3) and action-outcome temporal asynchrony (experiments 2 and 3) while evaluating agency attribution as well as the sense of ownership. Results

Experiment 1. Content agency prediction errors

Behavioral performance

The participants’ performance was as expected for this paradigm, with a mean percentage of self-generated errors approx. of 10.5% ± 6.1 (mean ± SEM). As previously reported, a compatibility effect18,19 was found, with participants responding more accurately for compatible than for incompatible trials (mean percentage of self-generated errors, 1.7% ± 1.7 vs. 8.8% ± 4, respectively; t(24) = −11.6, p < 0.001, d = 2.3) as well as faster (mean RT: 284 ± 4 ms vs. 301 ± 5 ms; t(24) = −10.5, p < 0.001, d = 3.8). As expected, participants also showed significantly faster reaction time (RT) for self-generated errors compared to correct responses (mean RT correct: 293 ± 5 ms vs. mean RT self-generated errors: 237 ± 5 ms; t(24) = 22.7, p < 0.001, d = 2.3). Altogether, these results indicated a correct implementation of the Flanker task. As expected, when computing the post-error slowing effect,20 self-generated errors were followed by significantly slower correct responses than those correct reactions following correct responses (post-error slowing of self-generated errors = 15 ± 3 ms; t(24) = −4.3, p < 0.001, d = 2.5). Interestingly, we replicated Padrao et al.17 findings, showing a significantly slower RT for correct responses following external errors compared to correct (post-error slowing following external errors = 17 ± 3 ms; t(24) = −4.8, p < 0.001, d = 3.1), suggesting the engagement of control/monitoring systems despite having responded correctly.

Subjective agency questionnaire

Crucially for the present set-up, the appearance of external errors in error induction blocks led to an increase in external agency attribution (Figure 2A). Results on the Wilcoxon test revealed significant differences between the Standard and Error induction blocks for both external attribution questions: Q3 (“Sometimes, the digital hands seemed to be moving by themselves”) (W = 75, p < 0.001) and Q4 (“It sometimes seemed as if the errors were not caused by myself”) (W = 43, p < 0.001), but without affecting the internal attribution judgements (Q1: W = 347, p = 0.190; Q2: W = 347.5, p = 0.106). Source localization analysis

Following the ERP analysis, we performed source localization analyses to locate the source of the N2/P600 (difference external errors minus correct) and the ERN (difference self-generated errors minus correct) components. Firstly, we computed the surface voltage maps corresponding to the N2 component (270–330 ms) (Figure 3C). Surface voltage maps revealed an increase in negative voltage frontally distributed. The minimum-norm estimate at the 300 ms latency depicted a frontal source distribution, and a single main source located at the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) is reported (Talairach: x = −10.5, y = 46.1, z = 9.7).

For the P600 component (450–600 ms) (Figure 3C), the cortical surface voltage maps illustrated a centro-parietal distribution, as well as a parietal distributed source when using the minimum-norm estimate (480 ms). A main source located at the middle cingulate cortex (MCC) (Talairach: x = −2.5, y = 13.2, z = 21) was found when computing classical LORETA analysis recursively applied (CLARA).

On the other hand, we also examined the ERN at the time window 60–135 ms after the response onset. As can be seen in Figure 4C, the ERN surface voltage distribution was characterized by an increased negative voltage at fronto-central locations when estimating the cortical sources using the minimum-norm estimation (MNE) technique. In this case, the minimum norm image at the peak latency 90 ms after an error slip revealed a frontal midline location for the ERN (see Figure 4C, bottom-left for the CLARA distributed model, classic LORETA recursively applied) with a main source located at the ACC (Talairach: x = 5, y = 22.3, z = 34) at the time window 60–135 ms (Figure 4C, bottom-right).

Time-frequency analysis

N2 and P600 (time windows 200–400 and 400–600 ms): External errors oscillatory activity

When introducing an external error, an increase in relative power for the delta band (1–3 Hz) as well as for the theta (3–8 Hz) frequency band was registered, compared to correct and self-generated error trials (see Figure S1 for external errors condition). Importantly, when computing the contrast external errors minus correct, a frontal-theta and parietal-delta dissociation was observed (Figure 3D), corresponding to the N2/P600 ERP pattern previously reported (see Table S1 for rmANOVA results).

Cluster-based permutation analysis for the difference between external errors and correct responses corroborated the existence of a significant frontal positive cluster at the theta frequency band (3–8 Hz) (Figure 3D), in accordance with the latency and topographical distribution of the N2 component. When inspecting the delta frequency band (1–3 Hz), a significant cluster with a centro-parietal distribution was confirmed between 300 ms onwards (Figure 3D), resembling the latency and topographical characteristics of the P600 component (Figure 3B).

ERN (time window 50–150 ms): Self-generated errors oscillatory activity

As previously reported after error commission,23,24 a significant increase in the theta band (3–8 Hz) was encountered for self-generated errors during the 50–200 ms time window (Figure S1), displaying a frontal topographical distribution (see Table S1 for a complete outline of rmANOVA results). Repeated-measures cluster permutation tests for the contrast self-generated errors vs. correct confirmed a significant positive cluster (p < 0.05) at the theta frequency band (3–8 Hz) at the ERN time window (0–500 ms) (Figure 4D).

Association between delta oscillatory, P600 component and external agency attribution

Importantly, a positive correlation was found between centro-parietal delta oscillatory activity (1–3 Hz) recorded after external errors (difference between external errors minus correct trials) and the compound score for external agency attribution (r(23) = 0.41, p = 0.027) (Figure 5C), confirming a significant increase in delta activity when eliciting an agency prediction error, following our ERP results for the P600 component. Interestingly, this centro-parietal delta power increase was also positively correlated with the post-error slowing effect for external errors (r(23) = 0.38, p = 0.03) (Figure 5C), which is clearly in line with our findings regarding the P600 component.

Notably, when examining the relationship between the P600 component and delta power (1–3 Hz) at centro-posterior electrode locations, a significant positive correlation was observed (r(23) = 0.80, p < 0.001), supporting the P600-delta association (Figure 5E).

Interim conclusions experiment 1

Altogether, the current results show the elicitation of a specific neural pattern consisting of a delta/P600 component significantly associated with the external attribution of agency during the insertion of content agency prediction errors. Besides, the latency of these components highlights the key role of retrospective inferencing in the processing of agentic inferences, appearing later in the processing stages compared to internally attributed actions (i.e., ERN component after self-generated errors).

Experiment 2. Temporal agency prediction errors

Behavioral performance

Participants’ accuracy rates showed a mean percentage of self-generated errors of ∼10% (no-delay: 11% ± 1.3%; 150-delay: 12% ± 1.4%; 400-delay: 10% ± 1.3%) (mean ± SEM), which did not differ between conditions and were comparable with that reported in experiment 1. A larger percentage of self-generated errors were made for incompatible vs. compatible trials (no-delay: compatible 2% ± 0.4%, incompatible 9% ± 0.9%, t(24) = −11.5, p < 0.001, d = 10; 150-delay: compatible 2% ± 0.5%, incompatible 9% ± 0.9%, t(24) = −6.5, p < 0.001, d = 9; 400-delay: compatible 2% ± 0.4%, incompatible 8% ± 0.9%, t(24) = −14.22, p < 0.001, d = 8).

Also, faster RT for self-generated errors compared to correct responses was observed for all conditions (p < 0.001 in all cases). Likewise, a compatibility effect was found for all conditions when performing 3 × 2 rmANOVA (factors: action-outcome condition (no-delay vs. 150-delay vs. 400-delay) and Compatibility (compatible vs. incompatible)) in correct responses, showing a significant main effect of compatibility (F(1,23) = 90, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.80) as well as a main effect of action-outcome condition (F(2,46) = 15, pGG < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.40). A non-significant action-outcome condition × Compatibility interaction was observed (F(2,46) = 16, p = 0.541).

Subjective agency questionnaire

As shown in Figure 2B, a significant graded decrease following the pattern no-delay >150-delay >400-delay was encountered regarding internal attribution of agency, highlighting the influence of action-outcome asynchrony on internal attribution mechanisms.5,7,8,25,26,27 Bonferroni-corrected Wilcoxon pairwise comparisons revealed significant differences for all internal attribution items when comparing no-delay vs. 150-delay (Q1: V = 105, padj = 0.003; Q2: V = 82, padj = 0.031), No-delay vs. 400-delay conditions (Q1: V = 231, padj < 0.001; Q2: V = 207.5, padj < 0.001), as well as for 150-delay vs. 400-delay conditions (Q1: V = 174.5, padj = 0.004; Q2: V = 159, padj = 0.004).

Although median values were below 4 in all cases (corresponding to the response option “Neutral/Nor agree not disagree”), a significant increase in external agency ratings was observed in the 400-delay condition compared to both the no-delay (Q3: V = 20, padj = 0.003; Q4: V = 3, padj = 0.002) and 150-delay conditions (Q3: V = 17.5, padj = 0.027; Q4: V = 16.5, padj = 0.042).

Importantly, and crucial to the present study, no external agency attribution was reported in any case (Figure 2B), as opposed to the pattern observed during experiment 1 (external errors). When comparing the subjective experience of internal vs. external attribution of agency across experiments 1 and 2, a clear dissociation was encountered. When introducing agency prediction errors in terms of content consistency (experiment 1), a stronger external attribution experience was reported compared to temporal agency prediction errors (experiment 2) (Q3: W = 481, p < 0.001; Q4: W = 503.5, p < 0.001). On the contrary, temporal agency prediction errors (experiment 2) resulted in a significant reduction of internal-related reports during the 400-delay condition in contrast to the reports obtained during content agency prediction errors which did not show significant reductions (Q1: W = 462, p < 0.001; Q2: W = 397, p = 0.009).

Moreover, a significant decrease in the reported sense of ownership was observed for the 400-delay condition compared to the no-delay (Q7: V = 231, padj < 0.001) and 150-delay trials (Q7: V = 171, padj < 0.001; see Table S2 for a complete description of all comparisons).

Interim conclusions experiment 2

Overall, during the realization of this experiment, we observed a decrease in internal agency attribution and sense of ownership as the action-outcome delay increased (temporal agency prediction error); however, no increase was observed in external agency attribution as previously observed in experiment 1 (content agency prediction error). During the execution of correct responses, a frontally distributed FCRP component was observed during the 400-delay action-outcome condition. Also, an FRN modulation during self-generated errors committed at the longest delay was registered. Importantly, no parietal delta/P600 component was observed in this experiment, confirming that no content agency prediction error/external agency judgment was elicited.

Experiment 3. Delayed content agency prediction errors

Behavioral performance

As in experiment 1, both accuracy and RT were found to be influenced by the compatibility effect, showing larger error rates for incompatible trials (no-delay: compatible: M = 1.2% ± 0.3%; incompatible: M = 10.6% ± 3%; t(17) = −3.3, p = 0.004, d = 4.4; 400-delay: compatible: M = 1.1% ± 0.3%; incompatible: M = 10% ± 3%; t(17) = −3.2, p = 0.006, d = 4.1) as well as faster RT for compatible vs. incompatible trials—correct responses—(compatibility effect no-delay = 19 ms; t(17) = −5.94, p < 0.001, d = 2.36; compatibility effect 400-delay = 20 ms; t(17) = −5.8, p < 0.001, d = 2.2). No differences between action-outcome conditions were observed regarding either accuracy or RT for correct, self-generated errors or external error trials (all p > 0.05).

Assessment of error attribution and temporal synchronicity

Signal detection theory analysis was applied to calculate hit (self-generated errors detected as self-generated errors), miss (self-generated errors not detected), false alarm (FA) (correct responses detected as self-generated errors), and correct rejection rates (CR: correct responses detected as correct) for each participant regarding the error awareness question (“¿Have you committed an error?”) during self-generated errors and correct trials. The percentage of aware and unaware errors did not differ between conditions (no-delay: 88.1% ± 0.16; 400-delay: 86.6% ± 0.18; t(16) = 1.28, p > 0.2). No significant differences were found between action-outcome conditions for either Hit, Miss, FA, or CR ratios (all p > 0.05). We then computed the d-prime sensitivity measure for both action-outcome conditions, which revealed no significant differences between action-outcome delay conditions (no-delay: M = 2.66 ± 0.12; 400-delay: M = 2.38 ± 0.18; t(16) = 1.82, p = 0.09), indicating that action-outcome temporal synchronicity did not influence error perception and detection mechanisms.

Discussion

The ability to monitor ourselves as agents of our actions is crucial for experiencing a self-independent of the external world. In the present study, we examined how the multifaceted experience of being the agent of an action can be modulated by different types of agency prediction errors (content vs. temporal) and to what extent external cues are crucial to retrospectively infer the attribution of agency. Our findings show a specific delta/P600 component associated with external agency attributions (content agency prediction errors, experiments 1 and 3; see Table 1) whenever hand-movement incongruences were presented. Interestingly, the amplitude of the delta/P600 component covaried with the subjective intensity of external agency judgments (Figures 5A and 5C), but not so with the internal agency attribution category. However, this effect should be interpreted with caution given the limited sample size. Moreover, when introducing an action-outcome delay of 400 ms during content agency prediction errors (experiment 3), a shift forward in time of the delta/P600 component was observed tagged to the feedback appearance (Figure 7B), highlighting the role of the external sensory information in the conscious reconstruction of agency attribution judgments. In contrast, during experiment 2, in which we manipulated action-outcome temporal asynchronies eliciting temporal agency prediction errors but without introducing hand-movement incongruences,7,8,27 a frontal FCRP modulation associated with action monitoring was observed during delayed correct responses. Moreover, delaying the appearance of the external feedback for self-generated errors during the 400-delay condition was associated with the appearance of a frontal FRN-theta component.

Regarding the P600 modulation associated with external agency attribution judgments, a similar effect has been observed during language processing, generally related to structural reanalysis, repair, or integration following syntactic violations or ambiguities.30,31 Likewise, the P600 component is usually associated with the P3b-like family components under the context updating hypothesis,32 which states that the P300 may be a manifestation of the processes by which the mental model that one has of his/her available environmental information (context) is evaluated and updated whenever a relevant deviant stimulus is introduced. Importantly, the delayed appearance of the P600 effect comes to confirm its association with the retrospective aspects of the agentic experience,11,33 which depend on the computation and differential weighting of external (multimodal somatosensory, visual, and auditory reafferent representations), intentions (goals), and internal cues. Within this framework of optimal cue integration,11 an important avenue for future research is to evaluate the impact of different types of multisensory feedback on the integration processes underlying the retrospective construction of agency. For example, follow-up ERP studies could compare the effects of erroneous proprioceptive reafference (e.g., passive stimulation of the incorrect response hand) with those of altered visual feedback, as implemented in the present study (see also17). It is also important to consider the contextual factors under which agency disruptions are introduced. According to this model, the environment in which participants perform the task determines the prioritization of the most reliable sources of information, dynamically modulating the weighting of available cues, ranging from intentions and internal sensorimotor predictions (forward model) to reafferent and external feedback signals. For instance, future studies could manipulate different environmental conditions or degrees of embodiment during the experiment (e.g., varying ambient illumination or comparing real-body perception with embodiment in a virtual avatar from a first-person perspective; see17,34,35,36,37). Depending on the relative weighting of these cues, such manipulations may lead to measurable differences in the latency and morphology of the ERP components reported in the present study.

Notably, a strong positive correlation between increased P600 amplitude and delta-band power at posterior parietal electrode locations was found, reinforcing the delta/P600 linkage (Figure 5E), as well as its common source and functional interpretation. Delta oscillations have been related to several cognitive functions, such as the motivational relevance of the task and the salience of the target stimulus,38,39 as well as with reward processing40,41,42,43 and commission of motor errors.44 Moreover, parietal delta oscillations are considered to mediate signal detection and decision-making,45,46 modulating the rhythmic gain of information accumulation.47,48 These findings fit comfortably with “two-stage” decision-making models.49 Following this perspective, a fast, low-level non-conscious sensorimotor route allowing a rapid and rudimentary evaluation of evidence using internal cues might be engaged, providing us with the default, positive agency feeling or “minimal self”.1 This stage would be followed by a slower, more accurate high-level conscious route devoted to integrating external information and related to evaluative and context updating processes. From this view, the delta/P600 component might reflect the engagement of the latter processing stage, during which an external judgment of agency occurs indicating the need to implement control processes and update current mental models. In line with this interpretation, a significant positive correlation was observed between delta/P600 modulations and larger post-error slowing effects following external errors, supporting the existence of a possible link between agency monitoring and the engagement of control/error-monitoring processes during the execution of a voluntary action (Figures 5B and 5D). The delayed appearance of the delta/P600 component contrasts with the fast onset of the ERN component (Figure 4) associated with the low-level non-conscious action monitoring route,49 and elicited as soon as an internal mismatch between the intended movement and the information from efference copy occurs.50,51,52 Importantly, the appearance of the delta/P600 complex during the no-delay condition was first found in experiment 1 and afterward replicated in experiment 3. Most remarkably, when introducing an action-outcome delay of 400 ms during experiment 3, external errors continued to elicit the same oscillatory pattern, although latency-dependent on the occurrence of the feedback onset. Overall, we believe current results suggest that the parietal delta/P600 activity might be elicited by content discrepancies between intended/predicted vs. actual states, reflecting judgments of external control that force the system to update the predicted mental model at work using agentic inferential processing.

Interestingly, the parietal topographical distribution of the delta/P600 component seems consistent with the role of the posterior parietal cortex (PPC) in generating and maintaining internal forward models (i.e., efference copy), detecting mismatches between intended-actual states (i.e., agency prediction error), and monitoring voluntary actions.53,54,55,56 Several neuroimaging and lesion studies support this proposal, reporting PPC activations when experiencing external agency attributions following incongruent visual feedback for subject’s actions.7,57,58,59,60 Similarly, PPC lesions have been associated with experiencing external causal attributions of agency, for example in limb awareness deficits and alien hand syndrome patients,61,62,63,64,65 as well as in anosognosia for hemiplegia (i.e., paralysis denial), which is thought to be related to the impossibility of accessing the information derived from the external sensory feedback following damage to components of the frontoparietal network.66,67,68 Besides, hyperactivation of the right PPC in schizophrenic patients was reported in a PET study when experiencing delusions of control.69

In contrast to the findings reported in experiments 1 and 3, our results in experiment 2 showed a very distinct pattern when introducing temporal agency prediction errors (Figure 6). This experiment was designed based on cumulative evidence showing a gradual decrease in the reported experienced agency as action-outcome temporal delay increases, with an agency threshold around 200/300 ms.5,7,8,25,26,27,70 As such, we inspected the temporal agency prediction error introducing action-outcome delays of 0, 150, and 400 ms. Our results converge with previous ones showing a significant gradual reduction in the reported internal agency attribution of actions as the delay increases. Importantly, no effects on the external agency reports were encountered (in contrast to experiment 1), highlighting the relevance of distinguishing between both agency facets (internal vs. external attribution) as previously suggested.11,71 This data is coherent with a recent review on the effects of delay in the sense of agency.72 Indeed, this review showed that even though delays tend to diminish feelings of agency (as reviewed above), external attribution is usually undegraded, as people still perceive delayed outcomes as consequences of their own actions even when a strong temporal prediction error occurs. Thus, internal agency attribution might be affected by delays to some extent, as was observed in this study, but the delay does not impair the perception of causal relations between one’s action and feedback.

Interestingly, correct responses executed during the 400-delay condition elicited a frontal positive component approximately 200 ms after the feedback presentation (Figures 6A and 6C), which topographically resembles the FCRP or RewP component,73,74,75,76,77 characterized by an increase in frontal delta power (Figures 6C and 6D). The presence of the FCRP in the delayed condition corroborates the importance of distinguishing between feedback-locked components, especially in relation to the proposal that the FCRP is sensitive to the achievement of a positive reward or task goal.76,78 Several prior studies have linked the RewP/FCRP to affective processing79,80 and motivational states81,82 in addition to reinforcement learning signals and enhanced performance monitoring.83,84 Relevant to the present results, previous studies have reported similar increases in amplitude of the FCRP component when participants attributed action-outcome contingencies to themselves rather than to external sources,85 or when greater responsibility or controllability over outcomes was present during a task.86,87,88 Similarly, Yeung et al.89 showed that the modulations of the FRN component—most likely associated with the FCRP—were related to the degree of involvement in a gambling task.

The appearance of the FCRP during temporal agency prediction errors seems to point to a reactivation of monitoring processes under temporal uncertainty, in which a diminished feeling of ownership and internal agency is present, and attention to external confirmatory cues is increased to check the appropriateness of the previous response. Additionally, this finding is also convergent with the observation of depressed phasic dopaminergic activity in midbrain dopaminergic neurons for violations of temporal prediction of rewards, which is observed when the expected reward is delayed after an action.90 Supporting this idea, self-generated error trials in the 400-delay condition showed a frontally distributed negative deflection characterized by an increase of the medial-frontal theta power matching the FRN component82,91 (Figure 6A). This electrophysiological pattern is similar to the ones previously observed by Gentsch, Ullsperger, and Ullsperger92 and Steinhauser and Kiesel93 following the omission of the expected feedback and insertion of technical malfunctions, respectively. This result could be interpreted as a reinstatement of the previous ERN (following the dopamine reinforcement learning view,80), maybe indicating a need to re-evaluate or re-update their internal model, although it is intriguing considering that participants already know that they have committed an error, as evidenced by the elicited ERN in all conditions. Previous research has shown that even when negative feedback is fully expected, and no discrepancy is anticipated, the FRN component can still be observed, albeit with reduced amplitude.80 A possible interpretation is that continuous performance monitoring processes are maintained even under conditions of maximal certainty, supporting ongoing learning in case new associations need to be acquired or existing ones updated in response to unexpected feedback.91

Importantly, no differences were observed for the ERN across action-outcome delay conditions, indicating the preservation of internal error monitoring mechanisms working independently of outcome-related information (Figure 6A). Therefore, in agreement with Wen,72,94 it seems that action-outcome temporal delays act as agency cues, increasing uncertainty, affecting internal agency attribution, and increasing the chances of a possible external intervention or control,71 although not disruptive enough to produce an increase in the judgment of external agency attribution for those actions, at least in the healthy population as the one tested here. In both cases, the presence of the FCRP and FRN in correct and erroneous trials with temporal agency prediction errors speaks in favor of increased uncertainty about correctness with delayed feedback and the re-activation of error monitoring processes. Further studies might be required to evaluate this hypothesis.

Overall, the present pattern of results offers new evidence about the workings of agency attribution mechanisms, distinguishing between self- and externally generated actions and their underlying neurophysiological signatures, providing valuable insight regarding the integration of internal and external information, and emphasizing the importance of considering the many facets of agency and its inherent complexity. An important extension of the present study would be to evaluate, in pathological conditions associated with disruptions of agency (e.g., schizophrenia, anosognosia for hemiplegia, or alien/anarchic hand syndrome), the extent to which the delta/P600 component reported here can be elicited following externally generated errors using similar paradigms. Understanding how agency can be monitored in real time using EEG-ERP measures may inform future basic and clinical research on agency and its disruption. This could contribute to the development of novel therapeutic and clinical interventions, as well as potential applications in brain-computer interface systems and assistive technologies.

Limitations of the study

In this study, we used CLARA for source localization, a distributed inverse solution that iteratively restricts the solution space to improve spatial focality relative to traditional LORETA approaches.95 This approach is advantageous when the number and configuration of cortical sources are not known a priori, as in our comparison between self-generated and externally induced errors. However, because CLARA is based on LORETA, it incorporates spatial smoothness constraints that may produce spatially blurred estimates and potentially overestimate the extent of neural generators.96,97 In addition, low signal-to-noise ratios may affect the stability of the solution. Therefore, as with all EEG inverse solutions, the resulting source localizations should be interpreted cautiously and considered probabilistic estimates rather than precise anatomical localizations.98,99 Some other limitations regarding the design of our study and possible generalization of our results were also encountered. First, the requirement of explicit reports in the form of a questionnaire to assess the participants’ agency attributions might have induced a subjective bias in the results, due to its retrospective-so, interpretative-nature. Also, in experiment 2 we introduced action-outcome temporal delays in a block-wise manner, which may have allowed action-effect delay adaptations influencing our results. Future studies may tackle these concerns by evaluating the internal/external agency experience at the single-trial level and introducing action-outcome temporal delays in a randomized manner, respectively.

Finally, the sample composition across all experiments was not balanced with respect to biological sex, as substantially more females than males were recruited. As a result, the statistical power was insufficient to reliably assess for potential sex differences. Whether the agency-rated attribution effects reported here generalize similarly to both males and females therefore remains an open question. In addition, participants’ ancestry, race, ethnicity, or socioeconomic status were not recorded. Although our study did not aim to investigate how demographic variability influences the sense of agency, it would be worthwhile for future research to examine whether cultural differences associated with demographic diversity might modulate the effects observed in the present work.

Experimental model and study participant details

Participants

Right-handed graduate students were paid to participate in the study. Some participants were excluded due to artifact removal and/or a low rate of errors (Experiment 1: 2 exclusions; Experiment 2: 3 exclusions; Experiment 3: 2 exclusions). The final samples for each experiment were as follows (mean ± SD): i) Experiment 1: n = 25 (15 females, M = 24.3 years ± 4.31, ii) Experiment 2: n = 24 (16 females, M = 23.5 years ± 4.21) and iii) Experiment 3: n = 18 (12 females, M = 23.9 years ± 4.12). We did not record participants’ ancestry, race, ethnicity, or socioeconomic status.

In all cases, participants were naïve with respect to the aims of the experiment. The procedures of the experiment were approved by the Biomedical Research Institute of Bellvitge (IDIBELL) and Hospital Universitari de Bellvitge ethics committee (CEIC, Ref. PR254/15). Informed consent, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, was obtained from all participants prior to the commencement of the study.

Method details

Apparatus

An apparatus inspired by Nielsen’s (1963) paradigm and the Rubber Hand Illusion100,101 was built, a representation of which is shown in Figure 1. Participants were seated comfortably inside a Faraday chamber. A full HD 24.5-inch monitor displaying the experimental task at 200 Hz refresh rate was mounted on a wooden stand and adjusted to the subjects’ body. The monitor was oriented with an inclination of 30° on the horizontal plane. The participants were asked to put on a pair of white latex gloves and to place their hands on top of the wooden stand surface (hidden from their view due to the monitor overlap). Two fixed joysticks with a button at the top were attached to the wooden stand so that he/she could grab the joysticks and push the button with his/her thumbs (Figure 1A). After general instructions were given, the EEG cap was set up and the state of each electrode was checked. Finally, the room lights were turned off for the realization of the experiment.

Experimental design

A modified version of the Eriksen Flanker task51 was presented (Figure 1B). Stimulus presentation was controlled with EPrime (Psychology Software Tools Inc., Pittsburgh, PA) and MATLAB software version R2017a (The MathWorks, Inc. Natick, MA). Participants were required to focus on the central target arrow from a visual array of three arrows allocated vertically and to respond with the right or left hand (thumb press on button placed on the high-end of the fixed joystick) depending on the directionality of the target arrow. The flanker arrows located above and below the target arrow were presented to either facilitate the target response (compatible trials: all arrows in the same direction) or to prime the other response pattern (incompatible trials: flanker arrows pointing in the opposite direction than the target arrow). To optimize the number of errors, a proportion of 40-60% of compatible and incompatible trials, respectively, were presented in a random order. During the whole duration of a trial, a pair of life-sized hands mimicked the participants’ hands actions (closed hands holding the response device and performing the thumb button press movement) at a coherent position with respect to the participants’ hands and body posture. To remove morphological cues that could affect self-identification, the “virtual” hands were real hand (adult size) photographs wearing the same white latex gloves that participants were asked to wear (Figure 1).

Participants were instructed to respond both accurately and as fast as possible to the direction of the target arrow. No performance feedback (correct/error) was provided throughout the task. The duration of the target presentation for all trials was fixed to 150 ms followed by a response threshold of 1000 ms (Reaction Time -RT-). At the same time participants were responding, the virtual hand response movement -Observed Response- appeared for 100 ms (Figure 1B). A variable fixation slide (depending on the RT) appeared at the end of the trial. Participants were given instructions about how to reduce muscle artifacts by minimizing movement and to wait for a visual signal, an array of five asterisks appearing every 10 trials, to free blink for 5 seconds. Every 80 trials a block pause of 10 seconds was presented. A training phase (20 trials) was always performed before the experiment began to ensure an adequate speed-accuracy rate.

Procedure and experimental aims

Experiment 1. Content agency prediction error

Experiment 1 was designed to examine the electrophysiological and oscillatory activity associated to content agency prediction error and their effects on the different levels of agency attribution processing (internal vs. external agency attribution) (Table 1). Following Padrao et al.,17 the experiment always started with one Standard block (160 trials) during which a complete spatial and temporal congruence between action-outcome was presented allowing the illusion of ownership and a coherent internal attribution for the perceived movements without the need of being fully immersed in a virtual scenario.17 Following this first Standard block, two Error induction blocks of 640 trials each were performed. During the Error induction blocks, and only in some compatible trials (5% of the total number of trials, i.e., 64 trials in total for all Error induction blocks), an action-outcome content incongruence was inserted during which the “virtual” hands produced an unexpected response movement inducing an external error and therefore, a content agency prediction error (Figure 1B). Thus, in those external errors’ trials, when the participants pressed the button with one hand, it was the opposite “virtual” hand which performed the response movement, causing an agency prediction error. We chose to introduce 5% external errors, as this percentage approximately matches the minimum proportion of natural self-generated errors typically observed in the flanker task.17,51,102

External error trials were randomized throughout the block without additional constraints. Given the relatively low proportion of EE trials, the median distance between EE trials across participants was 19.42 ± 0.64 trials, ensuring that these events were temporally sparse within the task sequence. Furthermore, we avoided introducing external errors in incompatible trials to avoid the pre-activation of incorrect motor channels responsible for the increase of error rates in the incompatible trials (compatibility effect). Immediately after the Standard and Error induction blocks, the participant was asked to rate his/her subjective experience of internal vs. external attribution of agency for the action performed (see subsection below Subjective experience questionnaire).

Experiment 2. Temporal agency prediction error

With the aim of examining the temporal sensitivity for internal agency attribution in our experimental paradigm, we designed the following experiment introducing temporal agency prediction error using different action-outcome temporal delays (Table 1). The experiment was divided into 8 blocks of 160 trials each (maintaining the same proportion of compatible -40%- and incompatible -60%- trials as in Experiment 1). Two out of these 8 blocks were presented with a delay of 150 ms for the virtual hands’ response movements with respect to the participants’ action (action-outcome 150-Delay), and 2 other blocks with a delay set at 400 ms (action-outcome 400-Delay), following previous studies showing a gradually decreasing internal agency attribution as delay increases (Farrer et al., 2008; Farrer, Valentin & Hupe, 2013; Hara et al., 2015; Krugwasser, Harel & Salomon, 2019; Sato & Yasuda, 2005) (Figure 1C). In the remaining 4 blocks, no delay was introduced (No-Delay). The experiment always started with a No-Delay block, followed by either action-outcome 150-Delay or action-outcome 400-Delay block, in an interleaved manner and counterbalanced among participants. No trials were presented in which an external error was imposed (as in Experiment 1), and therefore, content agency prediction errors were not induced in this experiment. At the end of each block, participants were requested to rate their subjective experience of agency by answering a specific questionnaire (see subsection below Subjective experience questionnaire).

Experiment 3. Delayed content agency prediction error

Following the evidence obtained in Experiments 1 and 2, this new experiment aimed to further investigate the workings of agency attribution by delaying the occurrence of content agency prediction errors (Table 1). For such a purpose, an action-outcome 400-Delay was chosen based on our results on Experiment 2 showing a significant decrease in internal agency judgements during the action-outcome 400-Delay condition, which are in line with previous reports showing a decreased internal attribution after 300-400 ms delay.7,8,25,26,27 Two blocks of 600 trials (compatible - 40% - and incompatible - 60% -, 1200 trials in total) were performed consecutively following the same experimental setting as Experiment 1. In this case, 50% of the trials were action-outcome 400-Delay trials (600 trials), and the other 50% No-Delay trials (600 trials). As in Experiment 1, we introduced external errors randomly in 5% of the trials. Because all types of trials (No-Delay and action-outcome 400-Delay) and error conditions (self-generated errors and external errors) were randomized along the whole experiment, the subjective experience could not be evaluated after the completion of each block. Instead, we opted for inserting an Error awareness question in 50% of the expected erroneous responses and in the same number of Correct responses for each action-outcome delay condition by asking participants: “¿Have you committed an error?”. This response allowed us to study the relationship between temporal delay and error attribution for self-generated errors, without priming participants’ responses by presenting the question for both erroneous and correct answers (we did not introduce it during external errors as we already know from Experiment 1 that external errors are perceived and processed as not coming from the self).

A 7-item questionnaire (in Spanish) addressing internal (Q1 and Q2, i.e., “Most of the time, the movements of the digital hands seemed to be my own movements”), and external agency attribution (Q3 and Q4, i.e., “It sometimes seemed as if the errors were not caused by me”) was created.17,104,105 Two control questions (Q5 and Q6, i.e., “It seemed as if I had more than two hands”) about which we had no prior assumptions were also included. EEG recording

The electroencephalographic (EEG) signal was recorded from the scalp using sintered Ag-AgCl ring electrodes mounted in an elastic cap (Easycap, International 10–20 System locations) and located at 27 standard positions (Fp1/2, Fz, F3/4, F7/8, Fc1/2, Fc5/6, Cz, C3/4, Cp1/2, Cp5/6, T7/8, Pz, P3/4, P7/8, O1/2). Biosignals were referenced on-line to the right mastoid electrode and posteriorly re-referenced off-line to half of the signal acquired on the left mastoid electrode. Electrode impedances were kept below 5 kΩ. For all experiments, vertical eye movements were monitored with an electrode at the infraorbital ridge of the right eye. The electrophysiological signals were filtered online with a notch-filter (50 Hz) and a high-pass filter (0.016 Hz) and digitized at a rate of 250 Hz.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Behavioral analyses

To inspect the behavioral effects, we computed the RT and accuracy rates of the different kinds of trials (Correct, self-generated errors and external errors) and conditions (No-Delay, action-outcome 150-Delay, action-outcome 400-Delay). We explored the compatibility effect,18,19 in terms of RT and accuracy rates. To inspect compensatory mechanisms for erroneous responses, we computed the post-error slowing -PES- effect,20 by which errors are generally followed by more accurate and slower responses, reflecting cognitive control and monitoring mechanisms involved in behavioral adaptation.106,107,108,109 Compatibility effects were also considered for the post-error slowing computations,110 evidencing no significant differences in the distribution of compatible and incompatible trials for each condition (Chi-square test, all p-values > .10). Paired samples t-tests and d-Cohen for significant t-test results were employed.

ERPs analyses

EEG analyses were conducted using routines taken from the ERPLAB toolbox V6.1.4111 and custom routines from MATLAB (The MathWorks, Inc. Natick, MA). A high-pass filter of 0.1 Hz (second order Butterworth filter, 12-40 dB) was applied to the raw EEG data. To perform artifact rejection, we excluded epochs with step-like artifacts when the amplitude jumps in the electro-oculograms exceeded 25 μV (moving window = 400 ms, moving step = 10 ms) or in which activity was ±100 μV in any channel. No additional filtering was applied for the statistical analyses. Only for illustrative purposes a low pass filter of 15 Hz (second order Butterworth filter, 12-40 dB) was applied on the grand average waveforms.

ERPs were time-locked to the onset of the response and baseline-corrected to its preceding 100 ms. Time windows for statistical analyses of ERP voltages were chosen based on the peak-based method112 to determine the time window of interest by localizing the peak on the grand average waveform and then defining a symmetric time window centered on the peak (see Supplementary Tables).

Time frequency analyses

To study the brain oscillatory activity, we first generated large epochs including 2000 ms before and after the time window of interest. The same filtering as in the ERP analyses was applied. Data from each single trial were convoluted with a variable cycle complex Morlet wavelet between 4 and 10 cycles (logarithmic steps). Changes in time varying power (square of the convolution between the signal and the wavelet) in the frequency range from 1 to 40 Hz (linear increase) with respect to the baseline were computed for each trial and averaged for each participant before performing the grand average for the whole group.

In all experiments, to detect reliable differences and to adequately control for Type I errors when multiple comparisons were involved, the conditions of interest were submitted to a one-sample/repeated measures t-statistic two-tailed permutation test based on the cluster mass statistic113,114 using a family-wise alpha level of 0.05 by means of the Mass Univariate ERP Toolbox.115

In addition, we applied repeated measures ANOVA’s (rmANOVA) to perform confirmatory analysis. In all cases, the Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied whenever the sphericity assumption was not met. Furthermore, uncorrected degrees of freedom but corrected p-values are reported as pGG. In case a theoretically relevant interaction was found to be significant, we disentangled the effect by means of post-hoc t-tests (pBC). Effect sizes are reported as Partial eta-squared (ηp2) for significant rmANOVA results, and as d-Cohen for significant t-test results.

Source localization

Source localization analyses of the major generators of the ERP components of interest was performed using Brain Electrical Source Analysis software (BESA Research 7.0). Cortical source estimation sources using the minimum-norm estimation (MNE) technique116 was performed. This approach involves the computation of the source activities of a several regional sources evenly distributed over 1420 standard locations of the smoothed surface of a standard brain. A unique current distribution explaining the surface measurements is obtained by applying the minimum norm constraint, which selects the solution with minimum overall intensity using a regularization procedure that sets the balance between fitting the measured data (minimizing the residual) and minimizing the contributions of noise [in our case we employed the Tikhonov–Philips approach for spatial regularization (λ = 0.01)]. Finally, distributed source model analysis using CLARA (“Classic LORETA Recursively Applied”)117 was applied, performing a weighted LORETA with a reduced source space at each iteration. Compared to LORETA, this iterative approach reduces the blurring of the estimated sources while keeping the advantage of a predefined distributed source model, making it easier to determine the location of the source with maximal activity.96

Subjective feelings questionnaire

In order to evaluate the participant’s subjective agentic experience over their performed actions and the influence of action-outcome incongruences, we directly compared the participant’s ratings given immediately after finishing each experimental condition (Experiment 1: Standard vs. Error Induction block; Experiment 2: No-Delay vs. 150-Delay vs. 400-Delay blocks). Wilcoxon tests (pairwise comparisons) were employed for testing the possible differences regarding the participants’ scores on the questionnaire, with the significance alpha level adjusted to multiple comparisons (Bonferroni correction, p < 0.003 for Experiment 1 and p < 0.002 for Experiment 2).
