Integration of donor microbiota following FMT correlates with anti-PD-1 response in melanoma
Authors: Jessica L Fessler, Matthew R Olm, Edgar G Engleman, Justin L Sonnenburg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) has shown promise in improving anti-PD-1 therapy in melanoma, but the underlying microbial features remain poorly defined. We performed a strain-resolved metagenomic meta-analysis across three independent FMT plus anti-PD-1 melanoma trials (n = 41). Across cohorts, therapeutic benefit was linked to successful integration of donor microbiota, rather than increased diversity or engraftment of specific species. Responders acquired more donor-derived strains, exhibited greater post-FMT similarity to their donor, and maintained a more stable microbiome. Following FMT, non-responders’ microbiomes showed greater taxonomic instability, larger fluctuations in estimated microbial load, and increased abundance of pathogen-associated secretion system genes, whereas responders showed enrichment for microbial functions involved in community-level metabolism and communication. Finally, shifts in tumor-infiltrating immune profiles tracked with clinical outcomes and microbiome changes. Together these findings highlight that distinct patterns of microbiome restructuring, including stable community transitions and altered functional capacity, are associated with anti-PD-1 response following FMT.Subject terms: Immunotherapy, Metagenomics, Data integration, Melanoma

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting PD-1/PD-L1 have transformed the treatment of metastatic melanoma, achieving durable clinical responses in a subset of patients1. However, up to half of patients fail to respond, and overcoming resistance remains a major clinical challenge2,3. Among emerging modulators of ICI efficacy, the gut microbiome has gained considerable attention for its ability to shape systemic immunity and anti-tumor responses4,5.

Preclinical mouse studies show that manipulating the gut microbiome—through antibiotics, defined bacterial consortia, or fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT)—can alter anti-tumor immunity and sensitize tumors to ICIs6–9. Consistent with this, clinical cancer studies have reported associations between gut microbiome composition, including specific taxa, and response to ICI7,10–20. However, the bacteria implicated vary widely and the biological underpinning of these observations remains obscure. Despite this uncertainty, these findings have motivated dozens of clinical trials investigating whether FMT can improve immunotherapy outcomes in cancer21.

Three of the initial phase I trials have tested FMT in combination with anti-PD-1 therapy in metastatic melanoma22–24. Two of these trials (Baruch et al.22 and Davar et al.23) used FMT from donors who had achieved clinical responses to anti-PD-1 to treat patients who had failed prior ICI. Both reported promising responses in a subset of patients, including partial and complete responses. A third trial (Routy et al.24) combined FMT from healthy donors with anti-PD-1 in treatment-naïve patients, achieving a 65% overall response rate—higher than historical rates for anti-PD-1 monotherapy25–29 (Fig. 1a).

Individually, each trial found different microbes enriched in responders and non-responders following FMT but with inconsistent microbial signatures, and no unifying theme was evident across the three FMT trials. While these early results suggest FMT may enhance PD-1 blockade, the microbial and mechanistic factors underlying the benefit remain poorly defined30. Notably, while these studies demonstrate that donor microbes engraft following FMT, the extent to which donor strain integration and broader ecological dynamics are associated with clinical response across trials has not been extensively characterized. Prior analyses suggest engraftment may correlate with response, but these conclusions are mixed and likely limited by study-specific differences and limited resolution with the methods used24.

Several hypotheses may explain why FMT alters PD-1 blockade efficacy. The transfer of specific microbes could mediate response, which may exert anti-cancer or immunomodulatory effects through the production of microbial metabolites (e.g., short-chain fatty acids31–33 and inosine34) or via direct interactions with host immune cells. Alternatively, higher-order ecological effects, such as broader shifts in microbial community structure, diversity, or function, may influence host immunity relevant to ICI.

Here, we systematically characterize microbiome alterations associated with FMT-mediated modulation of anti-PD-1 response by applying a unified, strain-resolved metagenomic analysis across these three clinical trials (Fig. 1b). By constructing metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) and a custom genome database, we enable high-resolution, standardized comparisons of microbial composition, strain transmission, and functional capacity across studies. We find that clinical response is associated with increased donor strain engraftment and ecological stability, whereas non-response is characterized by microbial load fluctuations, community instability, and enrichment of functional pathways associated with pro-inflammatory pathobionts. These results define key taxonomic, ecological, and functional features linked to improved anti-PD-1 response, clarify the microbiome’s role in shaping immunotherapy efficacy, and inform the development of microbiome-targeted strategies to overcome resistance to PD-1 blockade.

Results

Microbial diversity is not associated with anti-PD-1 response following FMT

We first evaluated whether FMT alters gut microbiota diversity and richness (alpha diversity) to understand whether these changes were associated with response to anti-PD-1 therapy. Previous studies in other cancer types, such as non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and renal cell carcinoma (RCC), have linked reduced microbial diversity following antibiotic use to poorer outcomes with ICIs35. In melanoma, several studies have reported higher baseline alpha diversity associated with response to ICIs, although findings have been inconsistent7,10,36. Given these data, we asked whether FMT increases alpha diversity and whether greater diversity is associated with improved anti-PD-1 response.

We calculated the average Shannon alpha diversity index following FMT and found no significant change compared to baseline (Fig. 2a). When analyzing changes over time within each study, we identified distinct post-FMT patterns (Fig. 2b). In Baruch et al., Shannon diversity initially declined, then rebounded to baseline, likely reflecting the impact of preconditioning antibiotics used to facilitate engraftment. In contrast, Davar et al. and Routy et al. showed modest and variable increases in diversity, possibly due to differences in FMT dosing, route of administration, and preconditioning protocols37–39. These trends held across alternative diversity metrics (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b).

We next asked whether the magnitude of alpha diversity change post-FMT correlated with response to anti-PD-1. No significant associations were observed collectively, or within individual studies (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). Likewise, longitudinal analysis revealed no divergence in alpha diversity trajectories between responders and non-responders over time (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 1e). These findings suggest that while FMT variably influences microbiome diversity depending on the clinical protocol, changes in alpha diversity alone do not explain therapeutic response. This led us to investigate whether broader shifts in microbial community structure might better distinguish responders from non-responders.

Donor–recipient microbiome similarity and donor strain acquisition is associated with anti-PD-1 response

We next evaluated whether recipients’ post-FMT microbiomes became more similar to their respective donors, and whether the extent of convergence was associated with treatment response. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity showed that post-FMT recipient samples typically moved closer to their donor, indicating convergence (Fig. 2e, Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). This pattern was most apparent in Baruch et al., where post-FMT samples clustered tightly with their corresponding donor (Fig. 2e). To quantify these observations, we calculated the change in Bray–Curtis distance to the donor microbiome relative to baseline for each recipient. Specifically, we used the metric 1 – Fold change in Bray–Curtis distance to donor, such that positive values reflect increased similarity to the donor over time, while values near zero indicate little change. When aggregating data across all studies, responders showed greater and more sustained convergence toward their donor microbiomes compared to non-responders (Fig. 2f). While the magnitude and statistical significance of this effect varied across individual studies, the directionality was consistent (Fig. 2g), suggesting that the capacity to adopt donor-like microbial communities may contribute to improved therapeutic response.

Building on the observed convergence of responders’ microbiomes toward their donors, we next examined whether the transfer of donor-specific microbial strains underpinned these shifts. To assess strain transmission, we applied the strain-tracking tool inStrain40, a highly sensitive tool capable of detecting shared strains based on a shared average nucleotide identity (ANI) of 99.99% or higher. This high-resolution method enabled us to map strain transmission across all donor-recipient pairs (Supplementary Fig. 3a–c). To benchmark our results, we also applied StrainPhlAn, a phylogenetic marker gene-based method for strain-level profiling41. While both methods showed strong concordance and reported similar rates of donor-recipient strain sharing, StrainPhlAn identified a higher number of shared strains between unrelated individuals compared to inStrain, suggesting lower specificity (Supplementary Fig. 4). This distinction is particularly relevant given prior reports of donor strain engraftment in melanoma FMT trials that relied on marker gene-based approaches24. Thus, we used inStrain as a more stringent method for detecting true donor-derived strain engraftment, as has been reported40.

As expected, few shared strains were detected between unrelated individuals, while donor-recipient pairs exhibited significantly higher strain sharing (Fig. 3a). Recipients who shared the same donor also shared significantly more strains. To control for differences in sequencing depth, sample size, and study design, we calculated a strain sharing rate defined as the number of strains shared between a recipient and their donor, or between two recipients sharing a donor, divided by the minimum number of strains detected in either individual, yielding a conservative estimate of strain overlap. We observed substantial variability in donor strain acquisition across studies (Fig. 3b). Baruch et al. showed the highest rate of donor strain sharing, likely due to the use of preconditioning with antibiotics, combined oral and colonoscopic FMT delivery, and repeated dosing. In contrast, the Davar and Routy studies, which used less intensive protocols, showed lower rates of engraftment.

Motivated by the hypothesis that donor microbes capable of engaging host immunity might promote anti-tumor responses, we examined whether the immunogenic potential of donor strains influenced their engraftment. Using previously published IgA-coating profiles from healthy individuals as a proxy for mucosal immune recognition, we classified donor-derived strains as highly IgA-coated, lowly IgA-coated, or uncoated according to established thresholds defined by Olm et al.42 (Supplementary Fig. 5a). The transfer of donor-derived strains did not differ between responders and non-responders when stratified by IgA-coating status (Supplementary Fig. 5b). However, highly IgA-coated strains were significantly less likely to engraft than their lowly coated or uncoated counterparts (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). These findings suggest that either pre-existing immune recognition of certain microbes, or IgA coating itself may impede their successful transfer, identifying IgA coating as a potential barrier to donor strain engraftment during FMT.

To increase sensitivity to strain transmission events that might have been missed due to donor sample detection limits, we also calculated a putative donor strain sharing rate. This metric included strains acquired by two or more recipients of the same donor, not shared with unrelated individuals, and not observed in the donor sample itself—suggesting these strains likely originated from the donor but were below direct detection thresholds. Responders shared a higher rate of putative donor strains than non-responders, although this difference reached statistical significance only in the Baruch study (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 6a). Among donors with at least one responder and one non-responder recipient, responders acquired more donor strains than non-responders in 6 of 7 cases (Fig. 3d) (p = 0.0625; binomial test). Importantly, strain acquisition correlated strongly with overall beta diversity convergence toward the donor microbiome (Supplementary Fig. 6b), suggesting that strain-level engraftment and broader community shifts are tightly linked.

Acquisition of specific bacterial species is not linked to anti-PD-1 response

We next asked whether acquisition of specific bacterial taxa was associated with treatment outcomes in these trials. While certain bacterial families were more readily transmitted, as has been previously reported37–39 (Supplementary Fig. 7a), we found no consistent association between response to anti-PD-1 and the acquisition of any individual species, genus, or family of bacteria. Several taxa showed weak associations (unadjusted p < 0.05), but these did not withstand correction for multiple comparisons (Supplementary Fig. 7b, c). Notably, we observed taxa previously reported to correlate with ICI responsiveness—including Akkermansia muciniphila11,14 Bifidobacterium pseudocatenulatum14, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii10,23, and Bifidobacterium longum7,9—transmitting via FMT, but their acquisition did not differentiate responders from non-responders (Supplementary Fig. 7d–g). These results highlight the importance of general donor strain engraftment over the transfer of specific species, raising the question of whether broader community-level properties contribute to FMT-enhanced anti-PD-1 response.

Non-responders to therapy experience larger shifts in estimated microbial load and community structure following FMT

We hypothesized that the therapeutic benefit of FMT may depend not on specific microbes, but on the collective properties of the resulting community. In this model, the acquisition and incorporation of key metabolic or immunomodulatory functions, perhaps distributed across multiple species, may be more important for shaping host immunity and promoting anti-PD-1 response than the presence of individual taxa43, a concept rooted in the ecological principles of microbiome resilience and functional degeneracy or redundancy44. We explored whether broader ecological features, such as microbial load and community stability, might explain differences in response. Fecal microbial load, or the total density of microbes, has recently been highlighted as a major source of microbiome variation, often outweighing disease-associated taxonomic differences45, and fluctuations in load can directly impact host physiology and immunity46. As with load, community stability is an ecological property of the microbiota that reflects the consistency of its composition over time and its capacity to resist or recover from perturbations44,47, thus shaping its functional potential and interactions with the host. Microbiome instability has been linked to impaired colonization resistance and immune dysregulation48–50.

We used a previously validated machine learning model to infer absolute microbial load from metagenomic data45. Unlike standard composition or distance-based stability metrics, this approach estimates the total microbial biomass, providing an ecological measure that is complementary to changes in overall composition. Using this method, we observed marked differences in the dynamics of the estimated microbial load between responders and non-responders (Fig. 4a). Although on average estimated microbial load did not change significantly across the cohort (Supplementary Fig. 8a), non-responders exhibited a significantly larger fluctuation in estimated load compared to baseline than responders (Fig. 4b, c, Supplementary Fig. 8b). Notably, the magnitude of estimated microbial load change was inversely correlated with baseline microbial load (Fig. 4d), suggesting that individuals with lower starting density may be more susceptible to FMT-induced perturbations; however, lower starting load did not predict the extent of donor strain engraftment (Supplementary Fig. 8c).

These observations led us to assess microbiome compositional stability, hypothesizing that large shifts in microbial load might reflect broader disruption of community composition. We quantified beta instability, defined as the variation in beta diversity between consecutive longitudinal samples from the same individual. Higher beta instability indicates greater temporal shifts in community structure, while lower values reflect ecological resilience.

We found that beta instability was the highest immediately after FMT and then decreased over time, suggesting a period of volatility following FMT, followed by increasingly more stable community structure in the following months (Supplementary Fig. 8d). Non-responders showed significantly higher beta instability in the first month following FMT, indicating greater community disruption (Fig. 4e, f), whereas responders maintained more stable community structures, despite acquiring more donor strains. Beta instability was positively correlated with estimated microbial load fluctuations (Supplementary Fig. 8e), indicating individuals with larger changes in biomass also tended to exhibit greater shifts in taxonomic composition. Estimated microbial load is a model-based prediction of absolute biomass from species profiles, and in principle, composition could shift without changing load, or load could change without major compositional shifts. Thus, estimated load and beta instability, while related, are not necessarily linearly correlated, and their positive association post-FMT suggests biomass changes occur in parallel with broader community restructuring. To understand whether these changes in the estimated microbial load and community organization are associated with differential growth dynamics we used iRep51 to infer bacterial replication rates for each recipient. We observed that in the month following FMT microbial replication rates were positively correlated with beta instability (Fig. 4g), consistent with increased microbial turnover during periods of ecological disturbance52.

Together, these results suggest that ecological stability following donor strain integration, rather than increased richness or specific species acquisition, is associated with improved ICI response. Non-responders, by contrast, experience community destabilization following FMT, characterized by large shifts in microbial density, increased beta instability, and elevated microbial turnover. We wondered whether these ecological dynamics could be linked to functional changes in the microbiome that could provide mechanistic insight into anti-PD-1 response.

Functional pathway shifts following FMT differentiate responders and non-responders

We next asked whether the ecological differences we observed, including the stable donor integration in responders compared to non-responders, were reflected in changes in the functional capacity of the microbiome. Importantly, microbial functions can be distributed across distinct genes and phylogenetically diverse microbes, and enrichment of specific pathways does not necessarily reflect expansion of particular taxa. Using the KEGG Orthology (KO) database, we annotated microbial genes and compared their abundance in recipients before and after FMT.

When assessing the change in overall KO diversity (Shannon diversity and richness), we observed no difference between responders and non-responders (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). We next tested whether specific functional pathways changed differentially between groups using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)53 of KEGG pathways. While no individual KO significantly differentiated responders from non-responders after multiple testing correction (Supplementary Fig. 9c), several pathways emerged as significantly enriched (Fig. 5a), suggesting coordinated functional shifts associated with treatment response. The most enriched pathway in responders was the phosphotransferase system (PTS) (Fig. 5b, d), which mediates energy-efficient sugar uptake and carbon metabolism. Additional pathways enriched in responders included methane metabolism, galactose metabolism, carbon metabolism, ABC transporters, and quorum sensing, highlighting coordinated features of community-level metabolism and inter-microbial communication.

In contrast, non-responders showed increased abundance of bacterial secretion systems, particularly Type II and Type III systems (Fig. 5c, e, f), which are exclusively found in Gram-negative bacteria and are often used by pathogenic bacteria to inject effectors into host cells or secrete toxins54. Non-responders also displayed a post-FMT enrichment of lipopolysaccharide biosynthesis and O-antigen production pathways, both key components of the pro-inflammatory outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria (Fig. 5a). Together, many of the functions enriched in non-responders are characteristic of pathobionts that have been linked to chronic inflammation and immune dysfunction in other disease settings55–57. These functional signatures likely reflect ecological states permissive to pathobiont expansion rather than the action of any single microbial species.

In line with these functional shifts, non-responders exhibited greater microbial load fluctuations (Fig. 4a–c) and increased community instability (Fig. 4e, f), consistent with previous work demonstrating ecological disturbances in the gut, such as those induced by antibiotics or osmotic laxatives, may create a permissive niche for pathogenic bacteria48,58,59. Low microbial density has been proposed to impair colonization resistance, particularly when associated with low microbiota fitness, thereby allowing pathobionts to expand more readily60. Supporting this notion, we found that lower pre-FMT density was associated with larger load changes (Fig. 4d). Together, these findings suggest that ecological instability in non-responders may facilitate the outgrowth of microbes with distinct functional and metabolic capabilities that potentially undermine anti-PD-1 efficacy.

In summary, our findings suggest that functional enrichment of metabolic pathways in responders may support community stability, while the expansion of pathogenic and inflammatory functions in non-responders reflects ecological disruption that could undermine anti-PD-1 efficacy. These contrasting functional profiles raised the question of whether immunogenic features of the transferred microbes themselves might further modulate host immune responses.

Shifts in immune cell composition in the tumor microenvironment post-FMT correlate with response to anti-PD-1 and with gut microbiome changes

Building on our observation that gut microbiome restructuring was associated with clinical response, we next asked whether these shifts extended to the tumor microenvironment (TME). Using RNA-seq data from paired pre- and post-FMT tumor biopsies, Baruch et al.22 reported an enrichment of immune-related gene sets across all patients following FMT and increased intratumoral CD8⁺ T cell infiltration in some patients, particularly in the Donor 1 group, which included all three responders. To extend these findings, we quantified tumor immune remodeling at the individual patient level, identified specific immune and stromal cell shifts, and examined their relationship to microbiome features. We performed cell type enrichment analysis using xCell61 to infer changes in immune and stromal composition (Fig. 6a).

Principal component analysis (PCA) of cell type scores revealed that responders exhibited greater shifts in tumor cell composition from pre- to post-FMT than non-responders (Fig. 6b–d). To quantify the extent of these shifts, we calculated a Tumor Microenvironment (TME) Remodeling Score, defined as the Euclidean distance between each patient’s pre- and post-FMT coordinates in PCA space. This score captures the overall magnitude of immune and stromal composition change in the tumor following FMT. The direction of change was consistent across all responders, suggesting a shared remodeling trajectory in the TME. Loading plots indicated that post-treatment responder tumors were enriched for effector memory CD8+ T cells and conventional dendritic cells (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Both of these cell types are central to effective anti-tumor immunity, with dendritic cells promoting T cell priming and activation, and effector CD8+ T cells mediating direct tumor cell killing62. Their enrichment in responders suggests that FMT may help reprogram the TME toward a more immunologically active state capable of supporting PD-1 blockade efficacy. Correspondingly, these samples showed elevated T cell activation and type I interferon (IFNAR) gene signatures (Fig. 6e, f), consistent with enhanced innate and adaptive immune engagement. At baseline, however, tumors from eventual responders and non-responders did not differ significantly in T cell activation or IFNAR gene signature scores (Supplementary Fig. 10b, c), and PERMANOVA of xCell profiles likewise found no baseline separation between groups. The absence of separation between responders and non-responders pre-treatment is unexpected given the known predictive value of pre-existing T cell–inflamed tumors in other immunotherapy settings63,64 and suggests that baseline TME composition alone may be insufficient to explain outcomes in the context of FMT plus anti-PD-1 therapy. In contrast, a significant separation emerged post-treatment (p = 0.031), supporting that immune remodeling observed in responders post-FMT was not simply due to a pre-existing T cell–inflamed phenotype.

We further examined whether donor identity influenced the degree of immune remodeling. Recipients of Donor 1 exhibited more pronounced shifts in tumor immune composition than those who received FMT from Donor 2, suggesting that donor identity may influence the degree of immune remodeling (Fig. 6g). However, not all Donor 1 recipients achieved clinical responses, indicating that donor-related effects alone are not sufficient to determine treatment outcome. Notably, donor strain sharing was positively correlated with immune remodeling (Fig. 6h), as well as with T cell activation and IFNAR gene expression signatures (Fig. 6i, j). Similarly, microbial load fluctuations and beta instability—features associated with non-response—showed a negative, though not statistically significant, association with immune remodeling (Supplementary Fig. 10d–f).

Altogether, these results suggest that successful microbiome integration following FMT is associated with enhanced immune activation in the tumor microenvironment, supporting a connection between gut microbial dynamics, systemic immune engagement, and clinical benefit.

Discussion

Our study provides an integrated analysis of microbiome dynamics in three clinical trials of melanoma patients receiving FMT with anti-PD-1 therapy. Strain-resolved metagenomic and functional profiling revealed that stable donor microbiota engraftment and ecological resilience, rather than increased diversity or acquisition of specific taxa, are associated with improved responses. While prior analyses have highlighted taxa such as Akkermansia muciniphila or Bifidobacterium, our results support a complementary community-level model in which ecological stability and functional integration shape host-microbiome interactions relevant to anti-tumor immunity (Supplementary Fig. 11).

Consistent with this model, responders exhibited higher donor strain engraftment with minimal community structure disruption, whereas non-responders experienced marked fluctuations in microbial load and community instability. This instability was accompanied by higher microbial replication rates and enrichment in bacterial secretion system genes, suggesting that FMT into a volatile ecosystem may promote pathobiont expansion rather than beneficial community integration. Together, these findings reinforce the idea that ecological instability—characterized by load fluctuations, reduced resilience, and pro-inflammatory functions—may undermine FMT’s therapeutic potential.

Longitudinal profiling of melanoma patients receiving anti-PD-1 without FMT has shown that responders exhibit greater microbiome stability during therapy, with validation across independent cohorts65. These results reinforce the link between ecological stability and therapeutic benefit. Strain-resolved meta-analyses of FMT across non-cancer indications, including inflammatory and metabolic diseases, similarly support a model in which clinical success is associated with durable donor strain engraftment and post-FMT community states, shaped by recipient ecology and procedural factors37–39,66.

Recent phase 1 and 2 trials of FMT plus immunotherapy in melanoma, NSCLC and renal cell carcinoma highlight that clinical benefit may be linked to several context-dependent modes of microbiome remodeling, including loss of antagonistic baseline taxa67, selective strain acquisition or functional reprogramming68,69, and durable donor integration69. Together, these studies and our results suggest that donor engraftment is not a universal requirement for response but rather may reflect ecological compatibility and post-FMT community restructuring. This theme across oncology and non-oncology FMT studies underscores the importance of elucidating the basis of distinct ecological states and dynamics to enable design of translational microbiome therapeutics.

Importantly, these results do not establish causality between donor engraftment and improved outcomes, and our analysis sought only to identify reproducible associations observed across three independent melanoma FMT trials. Some recipients may have had a baseline immune state that both favored donor strain acquisition and predisposed them to anti-PD-1 response. Known determinants of immunotherapy efficacy, including tumor mutational burden, baseline immune activation, and systemic inflammation, likely interact with microbiome dynamics to shape treatment outcomes70. While our study leverages high-resolution metagenomics, the absence of metatranscriptomic or metabolomic data limits inference of active microbial functions which are instrumental for supporting a mechanistic link. Dissecting causality will require controlled interventional studies to isolate specific microbial functions or ecological states, which are beyond the scope of this clinical meta-analysis. Still, several patients who had previously failed ICI responded after FMT, suggesting that microbiome restructuring may contribute to renewed anti-PD-1 sensitivity.

Consistent with this, gut microbiome shifts correlated with immune remodeling in the tumor microenvironment. Responders exhibited greater changes in tumor-infiltrating dendritic cells and effector CD8+ T cells, accompanied by elevated T cell activation and type I interferon signaling—key orchestrators of successful anti-tumor immunity. Importantly, coordinated changes in the gut microbiome and TME were associated with clinical outcomes, whereas baseline TME features did not distinguish between response groups, despite the predictive value of pre-existing T cell-inflammation in other contexts63,64. These findings suggest that FMT plus PD-1 blockade can induce de novo immune activation in tumors not overtly inflamed at baseline. Although correlative, this is notable in patients previously refractory to ICI.

These results raise important considerations for future work. Ecological markers, such as microbial load stability, beta instability, and functional potential, may be more informative than alpha diversity or acquisition of historically “beneficial” bacteria. Given the malleability of the microbiome, it is worth exploring whether non-responders could be pre-conditioned to achieve the ecological states observed in responders. Building on the known importance of donor source, preparation, and treatment context, future studies should assess how different FMT protocols or donor selection strategies influence response, engraftment kinetics, and immune remodeling. Larger prospective studies and mechanistic models will be crucial to clarify whether specific microbial functions or immune pathways mediate the synergy between donor microbiota and PD-1 responsiveness. Finally, protocols must also account for the risk that FMT into an ecologically unstable gut may favor pathobiont expansion71, underscoring the need to encourage beneficial engraftment while avoiding unintended microbial blooms.

Overall, our findings suggest that FMT can remodel the gut microbiome in ways that support anti-PD-1 therapy, and that stable ecological integration is key to clinical benefit. These insights provide a foundation for optimizing future microbiome-targeted strategies to overcome immunotherapy resistance in cancer.

Methods

Metagenomics data accession and preprocessing

All original clinical studies were approved by the relevant institutional review boards and ethics committees at participating institutions, and all participants provided informed consent as described in the respective publications. This study involved only secondary analysis of de-identified, publicly available sequencing data, and therefore did not require additional institutional ethics approval. We analyzed metagenomics data from 11 publicly available datasets: eight from melanoma patients treated with immunotherapy, and three from melanoma patients treated with FMT plus anti-PD-1. Sample and donor level metadata for FMT studies are provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. Sequencing data were downloaded from the Sequence Read Archive and the European Genome Archive using the accession numbers listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Sequencing data from all studies were preprocessed and quality controlled using a standardized pipeline. Raw reads were processed using the BBTools suite (v38.90)72 Exact duplicates were marked using Clumpify (subs=0), and adapters and low-quality bases were trimmed using BBDuk (trimq=16, minlen=55). Trimmed reads were aligned to a masked human reference genome (hg19) using BBMap, and host reads were removed. FastQC73 (v0.11.9) was used to assess read quality. Overlapping paired-end reads were merged using BBMerge74 with recommended settings (rem k = 62 extend2 = 50 ecct vstrict).

Metagenome assembly and quality control

To construct a cancer-specific genome database, we leveraged metagenomes from 10 of these publicly available datasets7,10,12–16,18,22,23. Single sample metagenome assemblies and per-individual co-assemblies for each individual were generated using metaSPAdes75 (v3.13), incorporating merged and unmerged forward/reverse reads (-k 21,33,55,77) with error correction enabled. Assembly quality was evaluated with QUAST76 (v5.0), and contigs ≥1500 bp were retained for downstream analyses.

For each assembly, reads from all samples corresponding to that individual were mapped back to the contigs using Bowtie277 with the settings --very-sensitive -X 1000, and genome bins were generated based on contig coverage depth using MetaBAT278 (v2.15, default settings). Genome bin quality was assessed with CheckM79 (v1.1.2); bins with <50% completeness or >15% contamination were excluded.

From these metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs), species-level representative genomes were selected using a series of dereplication steps using dRep80 (v3.0.0). First, MAGs recovered in each study individually were dereplicated at 95% average nucleotide identity (ANI) using: dRep dereplicate -sa 0.95 --multiround_primary_clustering --S_algorithm fastANI -comp 75 -con 10. Next, non-redundant, high-quality MAGs from each study were compared against each other, and against representative genomes in the UHGG v1 database81 using the same command. Representative genomes were then selected using the default dRep scoring algorithm with an additional 25 points assigned to genomes assembled from the MAGs generated from the cancer patient datasets.

The final genome database contained 4,480 representative genomes, including 1,487 assembled from the cancer patient metagenomes. Taxonomic assignments for all representative genomes were determined using GTDB-Tk (r207)82. Open-reading frames (ORFs) from all genomes were predicted using Prodigal (v2.6.3)83. These genes were later annotated using KEGG Orthologues (see Gene annotation and pathway enrichment analysis).

Microbial relative abundance and composition analysis

To quantify the relative abundance of microbial species in each sample, metagenomic reads were mapped to the genome database described above using Bowtie277 with default parameters. The resulting BAM files were processed with inStrain profile using default settings40. A genome was considered present in a given sample if ≥ 50% of its base pairs were covered by at least one read (genome breadth > 0.5). Genome relative abundance was calculated as: (genome length * genome coverage) / total base pairs in sample.

Microbial community diversity and composition were assessed using both alpha and beta diversity metrics. For alpha diversity, species richness (number of observed species), Shannon diversity, and Inverse Simpson’s diversity were calculated from relative genome abundances using the vegan package84 (v2.6-4) in R. Alpha diversity fold-changes were computed relative to each subject’s baseline (pre-FMT) timepoint.

For beta diversity, phylogenetic (weighted and unweighted UniFrac) and non-phylogenetic (Bray–Curtis dissimilarity) distances were computed from species-level genome abundance profiles using the phyloseq package85 (v1.42.0) with a rooted bacterial phylogenetic tree generated with GToTree86 (default settings) and choosing an outgroup based on the longest tree branch terminating in a tip. Pairwise distances were calculated for all sample pairs within and between subjects, and average intra-individual distances over time, distances to donor samples, and fold-changes relative to baseline were computed in R. Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) was used to perform unsupervised clustering of samples using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity.

Differences in fold change in alpha diversity over time were assessed using the linear model Foldchangeinalphadiversity~time within individual studies. Associations between post-FMT alpha diversity and treatment response were evaluated using the linear model 1−Foldchangeinalphadiversity~response+study.

For beta diversity, proximity to the donor microbiome was quantified using one minus the fold change in Bray–Curtis dissimilarity relative to the baseline timepoint, or 1−DistancetodonorattimeXDistancetodonoratbaseline. Higher values indicate greater similarity to the donor microbiome post-FMT. Differences in donor similarity across treatment response groups were assessed using linear model 1−Foldchangedistancetodonor~response+study. Study-specific trends were also evaluated using study-stratified linear models.

Strain tracking analysis

Strain-level comparisons were performed using inStrain (v1.8.0)40. A genome was considered present in a sample if ≥50% of its base pairs were covered by at least one read as determined by inStrain profile. For each species detected in more than one sample within a given study, pairwise strain comparisons were performed using inStrain compare. For each species, distance matrices based on popANI values were generated and used to cluster subspecies into individual strains via hierarchical clustering with a 99.99% popANI threshold (implemented using the SciPy clustering package87). Strain sharing between sample pairs was assessed within each study based on this strain definition. The strain sharing rate was defined as the number of shared strains between two individuals, divided by the minimum number of strains detected in either individual, providing a conservative estimate of overlap. To increase sensitivity to transmission events that may have been missed due to limited detection in donor samples, putative donor strains were also identified. These included strains acquired by two or more recipients of the same donor that were not detected in unrelated individuals and were not observed in the donor sample itself. To facilitate pairwise strain comparisons across all samples in the Davar et al. cohort, which includes many longitudinal timepoints per subject, we concatenated all samples from the same individual into a single composite sample prior to computing strain-sharing values for Supplementary Fig. 3b. This approach allowed comparison across all individuals in a single run of inStrain compare, which would otherwise exceed memory limits. For all other analyses, including summary plots stratified by timepoint, samples were grouped into two subsets based on donor identity to reduce computational load while preserving temporal resolution.

Strain tracking determined using inStrain was compared to results obtained using StrainPhlAn441. Taxonomic profiles were generated from the preprocessed reads (described above) with MetaPhlAn4 (v4.0; database mpa_vJan21_CHOCOPhlAnSGB_202103) using default settings. Species-level abundance tables were next merged across all samples using merge_metaphlan_tables.py. For strain profiling, per-sample consensus marker sequences were generated from BAM files using sample2markers.py with the following parameters: breadth threshold = 100%, minimum reads aligning = 8, minimum base coverage = 1, minimum base quality = 30, and minimum mapping quality = 10. Strain-level phylogenetic trees were constructed for selected SGBs (species genome bins) using StrainPhlAn4, applying default settings for multiple sequence alignment and tree building (RAxML). To identify potential strain transmission events, the resulting SGB-specific phylogenetic trees were analyzed with strain_transmission.py using default parameters. Each sample within a study was compared for potential donor transmission events, and strain sharing rate was determined using the same definition as above, using the number of shared SGBs divided by the minimum number of SGBs detected in either sample.

IgA-coating classification and transmission analysis

Donor microbial strains were classified by IgA-coating status using published metagenomic IgA sequencing data from healthy individuals42. In this reference dataset, immune targeting was quantified using an IgA-positive (IgA+) probability score, calculated for each genome based on the ratio of a taxon’s relative abundance in the IgA+ versus native fractions. Following the classification scheme established by Olm et al., each genome was assigned to one of three IgA-coating categories based on their IgA+ probability score: High IgA coating (IgA+ probability > 1), Low IgA coating (0.005 <IgA+ probability <1), or No IgA coating (IgA+ probability <0.005). Genomes not present in the published reference dataset were labeled as Unclassified and excluded from the IgA-coating analysis. Strain transmission was evaluated for donor-derived strains detected post-FMT in each recipient. For each recipient, we calculated: (1) the proportion of acquired donor-derived strains in each IgA-coating category, and (2) transmission efficiency, defined as the number of acquired strains divided by the total number of donor strains detected in that IgA category.

Estimated microbial load analysis

Absolute microbial load was inferred from species-level taxonomic profiles using the Microbial Load Predictor (MLP) tool described by Nishijima et al.45. This method uses an eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) regression model trained on large-scale human fecal datasets (n = 34,539) with paired shotgun metagenomic sequencing and experimentally measured microbial biomass. The model predicts absolute microbial load solely from relative abundance data, enabling estimation of total microbial biomass from metagenomic profiles. Model inputs consist of MetaPhlAn4-derived species relative abundance profiles, which were used by the pretrained GALAXY/MicrobLiver model to estimate total fecal microbial load (cells per gram of stool).

For each sample, taxonomic profiles were generated using MetaPhlAn441 as described above. MLP was then applied to generate predicted (or estimated) microbial load values for each sample. To quantify changes in microbial load predictions, fold change relative to baseline (pre-FMT) was calculated for each post-FMT sample. The magnitude of change in microbial load was assessed by calculating the absolute log2 fold change in microbial load prediction for each subject averaged across all post-FMT timepoints. Associations between estimated microbial load dynamics and anti-PD-1 response were evaluated using the linear model: log2foldchangeinmicrobialloadestimation~response+study.

Microbiome community stability and replication analysis

Microbiome compositional stability was quantified using beta instability, defined as temporal variability in community structure across longitudinal samples. Beta instability was computed separately using both weighted and unweighted UniFrac distances, which respectively incorporate species abundance and presence/absence information while accounting for phylogenetic relationships. For each recipient, pairwise UniFrac distances were calculated between all sequential post-FMT samples using a rooted bacterial phylogenetic tree (described above). The average beta instability was defined as the mean UniFrac distance between all post-FMT samples within a given time window, with higher values indicating greater temporal shifts in community structure and lower values reflecting ecological stability.

Microbial replication rates were estimated using the iRep algorithm51. As bacterial cells replicate their genomes, DNA synthesis begins at a single origin of replication and proceeds bidirectionally toward the genome terminus. This results in higher sequencing coverage near the origin and lower coverage near the terminus in actively dividing cells. iRep uses this characteristic coverage gradient to estimate an index of replication (iRep score) from metagenomic data, providing a proxy for bacterial replication rate at the time of sampling. For each genome with sufficient coverage (breadth ≥0.5, mean coverage ≥5×), an iRep score was calculated per sample. Subject-level replication rates were computed by averaging iRep scores across detected genomes at each timepoint. Associations between beta instability, microbial load fluctuations, and microbial replication rates were assessed using correlation analyses and linear models, as described in the Statistical analysis and reproducibility section.

Gene annotation and pathway enrichment analysis

Microbial genes detected in this study were annotated against the KOfam database88 to assign KEGG Orthologues (KOs). Gene prediction was performed as described in the inStrain documentation (https://instrain.readthedocs.io/en/latest/user_manual.html#gene-annotation). In brief, KOfam annotations were generated using kofam_scan (v1.3.0) with default settings and the KOfam v103 database. KO annotations were used for downstream functional analyses of microbial community composition and dynamics.

For each sample, we used gene- and genome-level summary metrics to quantify KO presence and abundance across the community. Specifically, the number of genes annotated to each KO was normalized by the total number of genes detected in the sample, yielding the genes_fraction metric. This was used to calculate Shannon diversity and richness of KO annotations (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b).

We quantified KO-level changes using the genomes_fraction metric, which captures the proportion of detected genomes in each sample carrying a given KO. For each subject, KO abundances were averaged across all pre-FMT and all post-FMT samples; within-subject change was computed as: log2post−FMTaverage+1pre−FMTaverage+1. Subjects lacking either pre or post samples were excluded, and features with zero variance were removed. Group-level association with response status was tested using logistic regression (family = binomial) with NR as the reference level and study included as a covariate. KEGG classifications were used to group KOs for visualization and interpretation (Supplementary Fig. 9c).

To evaluate broader pathway-level differences, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the fgsea R package89. Each KO was assigned a rank score based on its differential change in abundance between Rs and NRs. Specifically, KOs were ranked using the signed -log10 of the logistic P value (unadjusted, described above), where the sign reflects the logistic coefficient for the KO’s per-subject log₂ fold change. Thus, large positive scores reflect KOs whose increases are associated with R, and negative scores with NR. The pre-ranked list was tested against KEGG pathways (minSize = 15, maxSize = 500); pathways with FDR < 0.01 were considered significantly enriched (Fig. 5a). Enrichment plots were generated for the top-ranked pathways (phosphotransferase system and bacterial secretion system) to visualize the running enrichment score across the ranked list of KOs (Fig. 5b, c). To visualize KO-level changes in the context of full KEGG pathways, we used KEGG Mapper90 to produce annotated pathway maps, in which each KO was color-coded based on the delta between Rs and NRs equal to Average responder log2 fold change – Average non-responder log2 fold change. Red represents KOs with higher post-FMT fold change in Rs compared to NRs, blue for those more enriched in NRs, and gray for KOs that were not detected or not included in the analysis (Fig. 5d, e).

Tumor immune cell composition analysis

RNA-seq data from nine patients with paired pre- and post-FMT metastatic tumor biopsies from the Baruch et al. trial were downloaded from NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) under BioProject ID GSE162436. Reads were processed using the nf-core/rnaseq pipeline (v3.11.0) executed via Nextflow with default parameters91. Reads were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome, and transcript quantification was performed using Salmon92. Gene-level expression estimates were derived from length-scaled TPM values. Immune and stromal cell-type composition was inferred using the xCell 64 cell-type panel applied to normalized gene expression data to generate enrichment scores per sample61. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on xCell scores to visualize shifts in tumor-infiltrating cell composition following FMT, and the Euclidean distance between pre- and post-FMT samples in PC1–PC2 space was used to quantify the extent of remodeling for each subject.

Immune activation was assessed using two manually curated gene sets. The T cell activation signature was based on an expanded immune-related gene set reported in Ayers et al.64, derived from melanoma patients treated with anti-PD-1 therapy. This set included: CD8A, CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, CXCL9, CXCL10, ICOS, GZMK, IRF1, HLA-DMA, HLA-DOA, HLA-DOB, and HLA-DMB. The IFNAR gene signature score was based on the expression of MX1, IFI44, LY6E, and OAS3—a core interferon-stimulated gene set—plus the IFNAR-signaling genes identified in Ourthiague et al.93: OAS2, AXL, TAP1, RTP4, ISG20, IL15RA, TREX1, CMPK2, LILRB4, IFIT3, MX2, and IFIT2. For both signatures, gene expression values were summed across all genes in each signature per sample, and the difference in scores from pre- to post-FMT was used to assess immune modulation.

PERMANOVA (adonis2, vegan v2.6-4) was performed on the full xCell immune and stromal cell-type enrichment score matrix, scaled to mean = 0 and variance = 1, using Euclidean distance. The test assessed differences in overall immune cell composition between R-pre and NR-pre and between R-post and NR-post groups, with statistical significance evaluated over 999 permutations.

Statistical analysis and reproducibility

Analyses were performed on biological replicates (human subjects) and no technical replicates were included. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. No data were excluded from the analysis. The experiments were not randomized, and investigators were not blinded to allocation during analyses and outcome assessment.

All statistical analyses were performed in R (v4.1.2) unless otherwise noted. Two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were generally used to compare differences between responders (R) and non-responders (NR) to anti-PD-1 therapy following FMT across a range of outcomes, including alpha diversity, strain sharing, estimated microbial load, beta instability, and immune signature scores. Where noted, paired t-tests were used for within-subject comparisons. Linear models were used to evaluate the effects of time, treatment response, and study cohort on microbiome parameters.

Differences in donor strain acquisition at the species, genus, and family levels were assessed using logistic regression models, with post-FMT acquisition (versus non-acquisition) of each taxon as the predictor and anti-PD-1 response status as the outcome, adjusting for study cohort. Models were implemented in R using the glm() function with a binomial error distribution: glm(response ~ taxon + study, family = “binomial”). Effect sizes (log odds ratios) and corresponding P values were extracted from model coefficients and used to generate volcano plots (Supplementary Fig. 7b).

Pearson correlation coefficients were used to evaluate associations between continuous variables, including strain sharing rate with donor, estimated microbial load dynamics, beta instability, iRep-based replication rates, and tumor immune scores. Unless otherwise noted, P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Multiple hypothesis testing correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method94 where applicable. Statistical significance in figures is denoted as follows: ns = not significant; *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001. Boxplots show the median (center line), interquartile range (box), and 1.5x the interquartile range (whiskers).
