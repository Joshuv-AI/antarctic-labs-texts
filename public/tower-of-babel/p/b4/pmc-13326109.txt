Defect-assisted charge transfer and plasmonic enhancement in ZnO nanotube/Au hybrids for ultrasensitive malathion detection
Authors: Khanh Duy Nguyen, Khuong Duy An Nguyen, Anh Thanh Tuan Pham, Thanh Duy Cu, Ngoc Van Le, Hung Vu Tuan Le
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Defect engineering in semiconductor substrates provides an effective strategy to strengthen the charge transfer contribution in surface-enhanced Raman scattering. In this work, oxygen vacancies are intentionally introduced into ZnO nanostructures through KCl-mediated chemical etching, which simultaneously converts ZnO nanorods into hollow nanotubes with enlarged effective surface area. The combined structural transformation and defect generation enhance molecule–substrate interactions and promote interfacial charge transfer. Using methylene blue as a probe molecule, the ZnO nanotube substrate achieves detection down to 10−8 M. An analysis based on the degree of charge transfer is used to discuss the qualitative trend in charge-transfer-related enhancement, showing that the optimized oxygen-vacancy-rich ZnO nanotube substrate exhibits the strongest charge-transfer-related response among the ZnO-only substrates. Subsequent decoration with Au nanoparticles forms a ZnO/Au heterostructure where localized plasmonic fields interact with vacancy-assisted charge transfer. This cooperative effect enables ultrasensitive detection of methylene blue with a limit of detection of 10−12 M and an analytical enhancement factor of 2.5 × 109. The optimized substrate further enables proof-of-concept detection of malathion, achieving a detection limit of 0.01 mg L−1 with an analytical enhancement factor of 2.8 × 106. These results highlight a vacancy–plasmon coupling strategy for constructing high-performance semiconductor–metal hybrid SERS platforms.

Introduction

Malathion is a widely used organophosphorus pesticide in modern agriculture owing to its high efficiency, broad-spectrum activity, and strong insecticidal performance. It inhibits cholinesterase activity in insects, thereby disrupting neural physiological functions and ultimately leading to poisoning and death.1 Despite its effectiveness, malathion poses potential risks to human health. It has been reported to exhibit carcinogenic properties, and environmental oxidation of malathion in water, air, or soil can produce malaoxon, a metabolite whose toxicity may be up to 60 times higher than that of the parent compound.2,3 According to the World Health Organization, malathion is classified as a class II carcinogen.4,5 In addition, the presence of malathion residues in agricultural commodities raises serious concerns regarding food safety and public health.4,6,7 These concerns highlight the necessity of developing rapid and highly sensitive analytical methods for the monitoring of malathion residues and for ensuring the safety of agricultural products and human health.

To date, a variety of analytical approaches have been employed for the determination of malathion and related pesticide residues, including electrochemical sensors,8,9 gas chromatography (GC),10 high-performance liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS),11 and ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS).12 Although these techniques enable reliable and accurate quantification, they generally depend on sophisticated and costly instrumentation, require complicated sample preparation procedures, involve relatively long analysis times, and demand skilled operators.2,13 Such limitations restrict their applicability for rapid on-site screening. Consequently, the development of a rapid, sensitive, reliable, and cost-effective platform for detecting malathion residues remains highly desirable.

Surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) has emerged as a powerful analytical technique offering nondestructive detection, high sensitivity, rapid response, in situ capability, and both qualitative and quantitative analysis of trace-level analytes. Accordingly, SERS attracts increasing interest for applications spanning environmental monitoring, food safety, biomedicine, and chemical analysis.2,14,15 The exceptional sensitivity of SERS primarily arises from two fundamental enhancement mechanisms: the electromagnetic mechanism, originating from the amplification of localized electromagnetic fields near plasmonic nanostructures, and the chemical mechanism, which involves charge-transfer interactions between the substrate and an adsorbed molecule under appropriate optical excitation. As charge-transfer channels become increasingly favorable and efficient, the Raman polarizability of the adsorbate increases, resulting in a stronger SERS response and a more significant chemical contribution to the total signal.14–16

Most SERS research continues to emphasize platforms dominated by electromagnetic enhancement from noble metals such as Au and Ag, in which dense “hot spots” are created through precise control over nanogaps and interparticle coupling to maximize plasmonic field localization.16–18 In contrast, approaches that intentionally exploit chemical enhancement in semiconductor substrates remain relatively less investigated. Although semiconductor oxide-based SERS platforms have attracted sustained attention over the past decades due to their low cost, tunable band structure, and high chemical stability, most studies still report limited sensitivity, with enhancement factors typically around 102–103.15,16,19,20 Such modest amplification is generally insufficient to achieve the sensitivity needed for practical analytical applications.

Recent studies have shown that oxygen-vacancy defects play a crucial role in boosting the SERS activity of semiconductor oxides. Oxygen vacancies enrich surface electronic states and modify surface chemistry, thereby strengthening molecule-substrate affinity and enabling more efficient interfacial charge transfer, both of which collectively amplify Raman signals through chemical enhancement.15–17,21 For example, Cong et al. reported that defect-rich W18O49 with abundant oxygen vacancies exhibited significantly enhanced SERS performance toward Rhodamine 6G (R6G), achieving a detection limit as low as 10−7 M and a maximum enhancement factor of 3.4 × 105. The enhancement was attributed to vacancy-induced surface states that facilitate charge-transfer processes through the chemical mechanism.17 Cao et al. demonstrated that Li-reduction-derived defective MoO2−x nanospheres exhibited substantially improved SERS sensitivity, achieving a detection limit of 10−8 M for R6G, mainly due to oxygen-vacancy-induced deep electronic levels serving as intermediate states for electron transfer and enhancing resonance coupling in the semiconductor-molecule system.19 In doped ZnO, Wang et al. indicated that surface-state energy levels can act as intermediate electronic states that enable charge transfer to the LUMO of adsorbed molecules under optical excitation, underscoring the role of surface-state-mediated chemical enhancement in semiconductor SERS.16 Likewise, oxygen-vacancy-rich substoichiometric WOx films have been reported to exhibit SERS performance comparable to noble metals without relying on “hot spots”, highlighting that defect-derived electronic states can provide efficient charge-transfer pathways and support excitonic resonances within the molecule-oxide system.22 Notably, Li et al. reported that oxygen-vacancy-engineered anatase TiO2 nanosheets decorated with Au nanoparticles achieved an enhancement factor of 1.7 × 108 for methylene blue. The vacancy-induced defect levels below the conduction band, in conjunction with the Au Fermi level, enabled additional photoinduced charge-transfer channels, underscoring the role of surface states in chemical enhancement.18 Collectively, these studies suggest that oxygen-vacancy engineering is an effective and straightforward strategy to enhance semiconductor-based SERS performance by promoting charge-transfer-driven chemical enhancement.

Among semiconductor oxides, ZnO stands out as a promising SERS material owing to its low cost, structural versatility, environmental friendliness, biocompatibility, and robust chemical and mechanical stability.23,24 Beyond these advantages, its surface defect chemistry can be deliberately tuned. In particular, KCl-assisted chemical etching provides a facile route that can increase the density of surface oxygen vacancies in ZnO.25,26 Therefore, engineering ZnO architectures with abundant surface defects and integrating plasmonic metals is expected to yield highly sensitive SERS platforms, benefiting from the combined electromagnetic and chemical enhancement mechanisms.

Herein, we report a high-performance SERS substrate constructed by decorating Au nanoparticles (AuNPs) onto surface-oxygen-vacancy-rich ZnO nanotubes (ZnONTs). KCl etching transforms ZnO nanorods (ZnONRs) into well-defined nanotubes under optimized conditions (85 °C, 18 h), generating a high density of surface oxygen vacancies and an enlarged effective surface area that facilitates molecular adsorption and interfacial charge transfer, thereby reinforcing the chemical contribution to SERS. The defect-rich ZnO nanotube substrate enables sensitive detection of methylene blue (MB) down to 10−8 M. After Au decoration, AuNPs are distributed on both the outer and inner surfaces of the nanotubes (ZnONTs18/Au), resulting in intense electromagnetic enhancement, while defect-assisted charge-transfer pathways may further contribute to the enhanced Raman response. As a result, ZnONTs18/Au shows high SERS performance for MB detection with a detection limit of 10−12 M and enables malathion detection down to 0.01 mg L−1. Furthermore, MB-based measurements show high spatial signal uniformity and satisfactory batch-to-batch reproducibility for the ZnONTs18/Au substrate, supporting its potential as a sensitive SERS substrate with satisfactory batch-to-batch consistency.

Experimental

Preparation of ZnONTs/Au substrates

All analytical-grade reagents used in this study were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich and used without further purification. All aqueous solutions were prepared using deionized water (18.2 MΩ cm). Detailed descriptions of the experimental procedures are provided in the following sections, and Fig. 1 schematically illustrates the overall synthesis and characterization strategy.

Schematic depiction of the experimental procedure. Stage 1: Synthesis of ZnONTs. Stage 2: Decoration of AuNPs on ZnONTs. Stage 3: SERS measurements.

Synthesis of ZnONTs

ZnONTs were synthesized on glass substrates via a two-step process involving ZnONR growth followed by controlled chemical etching, as shown in Stage 1 of Fig. 1. Initially, a ZnO seed layer was prepared via a sol–gel dip-coating approach using a solution containing zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn(CH3COO)2·2H2O, ≥98%) in ethylene glycol monomethyl ether (C3H8O2, 99.8%) with ethanolamine (MEA, ≥99.0%) as a stabilizing agent. The sol was stirred at 60 °C for 30 min, after which cleaned glass substrates were dip-coated, withdrawn at 2 cm min−1, and annealed at 250 °C for 30 min. The coating step was conducted for three cycles to ensure a uniform seed layer.

ZnONRs were subsequently grown on the seeded substrates by a wet chemical route using an equimolar (0.1 M) aqueous solution of zinc nitrate hexahydrate (Zn(NO3)2·6H2O, 98%) and hexamethylenetetramine (HMTA, 99%) at 90 °C for 4 h. Following growth, the samples were cleaned using deionized water to eliminate residual precursor species. Finally, the as-grown ZnONRs were converted into ZnONTs through a controlled chemical etching process in a 5 M KCl solution at 85 °C. After etching, the samples were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and dried in air. The etching time was varied to 16, 18, and 20 h.

Decoration of AuNPs on ZnONTs

AuNPs were deposited onto the ZnONTs via a UV-assisted photochemical reduction method, as illustrated in Stage 2 of Fig. 1. The ZnONTs substrates were immersed in an aqueous reaction mixture with a total volume of 20 mL, consisting of HAuCl4 (2 mM), methanol (MeOH) as a reducing agent, and deionized water. The solution was continuously irradiated using UV lamps (254 nm, 8 W) positioned at a fixed distance of 30 cm from the sample surface for 45 min, enabling in situ reduction of Au3+ ions and nucleation of AuNPs on the ZnONTs. After the photodeposition process, the samples were taken out of the reaction solution, thoroughly rinsed with deionized water to eliminate residual ions and byproducts, and subsequently dried in air at room temperature. The resulting Au-decorated ZnO nanotube substrates are denoted as ZnONTs/Au. For comparison of SERS performance, AuNPs were also deposited onto ZnONRs using an identical photochemical procedure, and the resulting sample is referred to as ZnONRs/Au.

Characterization techniques

The optical absorption properties of the prepared samples were examined using a UV-Vis-NIR spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, UV-3600) across the 300–1100 nm range. The morphological and elemental analyses were performed using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, Hitachi, S-4800) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray (EDX) detector. The crystal structures of the samples were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker, D8 Advance) with Cu Kα radiation. Structural Raman spectra were recorded using a Raman spectrometer (Horiba, XploRA PLUS) with 532 nm excitation to investigate phonon modes, lattice disorder, and defect-related features. Room-temperature photoluminescence (PL) spectra were obtained using a PL system (Horiba Jobin Yvon, 325 nm excitation) to study defect states in the samples. The surface chemical composition and electronic states were further characterized by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific).

SERS measurements

The SERS performance of the ZnONRs, ZnONTs, ZnONRs/Au, and ZnONTs/Au substrates was evaluated using methylene blue (MB, C16H18ClN3S, ≥95%) as a probe molecule. A series of MB aqueous solutions with concentrations ranging from 10−3 M to 10−13 M were prepared using deionized water. For SERS measurements, a fixed volume of 10 µL of each MB solution was drop-cast onto the corresponding substrates and dried in air before spectral acquisition.

SERS spectra were collected at room temperature using a Horiba XploRA PLUS Raman spectrometer equipped with a 532 nm excitation laser. Each spectrum was recorded with an accumulation time of 5 s. The laser was focused through a 100× objective, yielding a spot size of approximately 1 µm. Based on the MB sensing results, the substrate exhibiting the highest SERS sensitivity was subsequently employed for the detection of malathion, an organophosphate pesticide known to pose significant risks to the central and peripheral nervous systems.6

Spectral data treatment and analysis

The Raman and SERS spectra were acquired on a Horiba XploRA PLUS Raman spectrometer with the instrument-provided FLAT correction enabled in the acquisition software. Except for this acquisition-stage FLAT correction applied to the Raman and SERS spectra, no smoothing, baseline correction, normalization, or other post-acquisition preprocessing was applied to the Raman, SERS, or PL spectra. Peak intensities used for quantitative Raman and SERS analyses were extracted from the corresponding spectra without additional preprocessing.

For the estimation of the limit of detection (LOD), a blank-substrate-based signal-to-noise ratio (S/N) procedure was used. For each substrate type used in the LOD comparison, blank spectra were collected from ten separate analyte-free substrates under the same Raman acquisition conditions as the corresponding SERS measurements. For each blank spectrum, the noise level was evaluated as the standard deviation of intensity fluctuations in a peak-free spectral window near the selected marker peak, and the mean standard deviation obtained from the ten blank spectra was used as N for the corresponding substrate type. The signal (S) was defined as the height of the representative marker peak of each analyte above the local background level in the corresponding SERS spectrum. Blank spectra were used solely for noise estimation, not for spectral subtraction or baseline correction. The LOD was defined as the lowest tested analyte concentration at which the representative marker peak remained distinguishable with S/N > 3.

OriginPro (OriginLab Corporation, USA) was used for plotting, linear regression analysis, Gaussian deconvolution of the visible PL emission band and XPS core-level spectra, and Gaussian fitting of selected XRD peaks. The Gaussian deconvolution of the PL and XPS spectra was used only for component analysis and did not modify the measured spectra. The XRD patterns were plotted from the raw data without smoothing, background subtraction, baseline correction, or normalization; only the selected ZnO diffraction peaks used for lattice-parameter calculations were fitted using a Gaussian function to determine their peak centers. No Rietveld, Pawley, or Le Bail whole-pattern refinement was performed.

Results and discussion

Morphology and composition of ZnO and ZnO/Au nanostructures

In this study, ZnONRs are treated in a 5 M KCl aqueous solution at 85 °C for different times of 16, 18, and 20 h to obtain ZnONTs16, ZnONTs18, and ZnONTs20, respectively. The conversion mechanism is governed by selective chemical etching. In the absence of additional assisting agents, the dissolution process proceeds preferentially along crystallographic planes with higher surface energy. Preferential adsorption of chloride anions occurs at the polar (002) facets located at the nanorod tips. These (002) planes are metastable and possess relatively high surface energy; hence, the etching reaction is thermodynamically favored along the c-axis. Consequently, the central core of the nanorods is progressively dissolved, leading to the formation of tubular architectures through axial core removal.25,26

The morphological evolution of ZnONRs and the etched samples is examined by FESEM, as shown in Fig. 2. Fig. 2a–c reveal that the as-grown ZnONRs exhibit high density and good vertical alignment over a large area. The nanorods grow perpendicularly from the ZnO seed layer on glass, which is advantageous for the subsequent rod-to-tube conversion via KCl-assisted chemical etching. The ZnONRs possess well-defined hexagonal facets, with diameters mostly ranging from 80 to 90 nm and an average length of approximately 1.5 µm.

FESEM images of ZnONRs: (a) top view, (b) high-magnification image, and (c) cross-sectional view. ZnONTs obtained after chemical etching in 5 M KCl at 85 °C for different times: (d) 16 h, (e) 18 h, and (f) 20 h. (g) High-magnification FESEM image of ZnONTs etched for 18 h, clearly showing the hollow tubular structure.

After 16 h of etching (ZnONTs16, Fig. 2d), partial dissolution of the rod cores is observed. Several nanorods begin to develop hollow interiors; however, a significant fraction remains incompletely etched, indicating that the core removal process is still in progress. Increasing the etching duration to 18 h results in the successful formation of well-defined ZnO nanotubes (ZnONTs18), as clearly evidenced in Fig. 2e and g. Both inner and outer tube walls are distinctly distinguishable, confirming the effective dissolution of the cores while preserving the lateral walls. The hollow tubular morphology substantially enlarges the accessible surface area, which is highly beneficial for subsequent Au nanoparticle deposition and analyte adsorption in SERS applications. In addition, previous studies report that chemical etching leading to nanotube formation may increase the density of surface defects, particularly oxygen vacancies,25,26 which is further discussed in the following sections. The high-magnification FESEM image of ZnONTs18 (Fig. 2g) reveals clearly defined tubular cavities with wall thicknesses of approximately 10–20 nm. When the etching time is further extended to 20 h (ZnONTs20), excessive corrosion occurs (Fig. 2f). The hexagonal geometry becomes partially distorted, and several tube walls collapse or are severely thinned, indicating structural degradation due to over-etching. These observations demonstrate that prolonged exposure to the etching solution undermines structural stability.

Based on these results, ZnONTs18, fabricated under 5 M KCl at 85 °C for 18 h, represents the optimal condition, yielding well-defined tubular structures with enlarged surface area while maintaining structural stability. Therefore, ZnONTs18 is selected for subsequent Au nanoparticle deposition via UV-assisted photochemical reduction (ZnONTs18/Au). For comparison, ZnONRs are also decorated with Au (ZnONRs/Au) under identical conditions, and the resulting morphologies are presented in Fig. 3.

FESEM images of ZnONRs/Au (a and b) and ZnONTs18/Au (c and d) at low and high magnifications.

Fig. 3 shows the uniform distribution of AuNPs on the surfaces of ZnONRs/Au (Fig. 3a and b) and on both the inner and outer walls of ZnONTs18/Au (Fig. 3c and d). The average particle size estimated from FESEM images ranges from 15 to 30 nm. Notably, adjacent AuNPs are located in close proximity, forming narrow interparticle gaps that can generate electromagnetic hot spots. Such nanogap structures are expected to significantly enhance the electromagnetic contribution in SERS.

The elemental composition of ZnONTs18/Au is further analyzed by EDX, as shown in Fig. 4. The EDX spectrum confirms the presence of Zn, O, and Au, with calculated atomic percentages of 52.3 at% Zn, 37.9 at% O, and 9.8 at% Au. Elemental mapping images reveal homogeneous spatial distribution of Au across the ZnO nanotube surface, further confirming the successful deposition of AuNPs on ZnONTs18.

Optical properties of ZnO and ZnO/Au nanostructures

Fig. 5a displays the UV-vis absorption spectra of ZnONRs and ZnONTs obtained at different KCl etching times (16, 18, and 20 h), together with the Au-decorated samples (ZnONRs/Au and ZnONTs18/Au). All spectra display a pronounced absorption edge in the 350–400 nm range, which is characteristic of the band-to-band absorption of wurtzite ZnO and is consistent with the optical band-gap values estimated from the Tauc analysis (Fig. 5b). Compared with pristine ZnONRs, the etched samples exhibit a noticeable increase in absorbance across the ultraviolet to visible region, indicating a clear modification of the light–matter interaction induced by the rod-to-tube conversion. In particular, ZnONTs18 shows the highest absorbance among the nanotube samples, which can be attributed to the formation of a hollow and roughened tubular architecture that enhances multiple scattering and light trapping, thereby increasing overall light attenuation. Moreover, etching may introduce surface-related defect states, which may contribute to the extended absorption tail observed in the visible-near-infrared region.27,28 After Au decoration, additional broad absorption bands centered at approximately 529 and 533 nm emerge for ZnONRs/Au and ZnONTs18/Au, respectively, corresponding to the localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) of AuNPs and indicating effective plasmonic coupling with the ZnO nanostructures. Interestingly, these LSPR bands are located close to the laser excitation wavelength of Raman spectroscopy (532 nm), which is favorable for generating intense localized electromagnetic fields and enhancing the SERS response.

(a) UV-vis absorption spectra of ZnONRs and ZnONTs obtained at different KCl etching times (16, 18, and 20 h), together with ZnONRs/Au and ZnONTs18/Au. (b) Tauc plots of (αhν)2versus photon energy (hν) for the synthesized samples.

The corresponding Tauc plots ((αhν)2versus photon energy hν) shown in Fig. 5b were constructed using the absorption data in Fig. 5a, and the optical band gap was determined by linear extrapolation based on the direct-transition relation (αhν)2 = A(hν − Eg), where α is the absorption coefficient, hν is the photon energy, A is a constant, and Eg is the optical band gap.29 The extracted optical band gap values reveal only slight variations among the samples. Specifically, the optical band gap of ZnO decreases marginally upon etching, reaches a minimum for ZnONTs18, and then exhibits a weak recovery for ZnONTs20, suggesting a dependence on etching time. Such small optical band-gap variations are consistent with etching-induced structural and electronic perturbations associated with the formation of surface defect states in ZnO nanostructures.28,30 Notably, Au decoration does not introduce a pronounced change in the optical band gap, implying that the primary role of AuNPs lies in plasmonic and interfacial effects rather than in altering the intrinsic electronic structure of ZnO.31,32 Therefore, the enhanced light-harvesting capability of the nanotube architecture, particularly for ZnONTs18, provides favorable conditions for more efficient plasmonic excitation of metal nanoparticles attached to both the inner and outer surfaces of the nanotubes, giving rise to strong electromagnetic enhancement, while simultaneously promoting charge-transfer processes within the nanotube heterostructure, which is expected to be highly beneficial for achieving high SERS performance.

For all samples, the diffraction peaks located at 2θ ≈ 31.8°, 34.4°, 36.1°, 47.4°, and 62.7° are indexed to the (100), (002), (101), (102), and (103) crystal planes, respectively, which are in good agreement with the standard hexagonal wurtzite structure of ZnO (JCPDS card No. 36-1451). Notably, the diffraction peak at 2θ ≈ 34.4°, corresponding to the (002) plane, is sharp and significantly more intense than the other reflections, indicating a strong preferential orientation along the c-axis perpendicular to the substrate and confirming the high crystalline quality of the obtained ZnO nanostructures.

Compared with pristine ZnONRs, the ZnONT samples exhibit a gradual decrease in the intensity of the (002) diffraction peak with increasing etching time (Fig. 6a). This behavior is attributed to the KCl-induced selective etching mechanism, which preferentially proceeds along the polar c-axis of ZnO during the rod-to-tube transformation. As the inner core is progressively removed during etching, the amount of crystalline ZnO contributing to the (002) reflection decreases, resulting in a gradual weakening of the (002) peak intensity. After Au decoration, an additional weak diffraction peak appears at 2θ ≈ 38.2°, which is assigned to the (111) plane of face-centered cubic (fcc) Au and matches well with JCPDS File No. 04-0784, confirming the successful deposition of AuNPs on the ZnO surface. A higher diffraction intensity of the Au (111) peak is observed for ZnONTs18/Au compared with ZnONRs/Au in the enlarged XRD view (Fig. 6b), suggesting a higher Au loading density and/or improved crystallinity of AuNPs on the nanotube surface. This observation is consistent with the FESEM images, which reveal a more uniform and denser distribution of AuNPs on ZnONTs18 compared with ZnONRs, likely facilitated by the larger accessible surface area and hollow tubular architecture of the ZnONTs.

The obtained lattice parameters are very close to those of bulk wurtzite ZnO (a = 3.2488 Å and c = 5.2061 Å),29 indicating preservation of the ZnO crystal structure after etching and Au decoration. As shown in the enlarged XRD view (Fig. 6b) and Table 1, the fitted position of the ZnO (002) reflection exhibits a small shift toward lower 2θ values with increasing etching time. Because the peak positions were determined by simple peak fitting and the lattice parameters were subsequently calculated from these fitted values rather than obtained from whole-pattern refinement, this variation is interpreted as a small but systematic shift in the fitted peak position within the present fitting analysis. This trend is accompanied by a slight increase in the c parameter and d(002), suggesting possible lattice expansion and/or strain relaxation along the c-axis during the rod-to-tube conversion. After Au decoration, no additional ZnO-related diffraction peaks or systematic lattice-parameter changes indicative of Au incorporation into the ZnO lattice are observed. The derived ZnO lattice parameters remain close to those of the corresponding undecorated ZnO substrates, supporting that AuNPs are mainly surface-bound rather than incorporated into the ZnO crystal lattice.

Fig. 7 displays the Raman spectra of ZnONRs, ZnONTs16, ZnONTs18, and ZnONTs20, together with ZnONRs/Au and ZnONTs18/Au, revealing structural changes in the ZnO lattice induced by nanotube formation and Au decoration. Wurtzite ZnO crystallizes in the hexagonal space group P63mc, where the optical phonon modes at the Γ point can be expressed as Γopt = A1 + E1 + 2E2 + 2B1. Among these phonon modes, A1 and E1 are polar vibrations that split into transverse optical (TO) and longitudinal optical (LO) components due to macroscopic electric fields. In contrast, the nonpolar E2 modes are Raman active, whereas the B1 modes are silent in both Raman and infrared spectroscopy. The E2 phonons consist of a low frequency mode (Elow2) associated with vibrations of the heavy Zn sublattice and a high frequency mode (Ehigh2) related to vibrations of oxygen atoms in the ZnO lattice. The frequencies of all observed first- and second-order Raman modes are summarized in Table 2 and agree well with reported values for wurtzite ZnO.34–36

For the pristine ZnONRs, distinct first-order Raman peaks appear at approximately 92 and 430 cm−1, corresponding to the Elow2 and Ehigh2 modes, respectively. The Ehigh2 mode at 430 cm−1 is the most prominent feature, reflecting the high crystalline quality and well-ordered hexagonal wurtzite structure of ZnO nanorods.34,36,37 Additional Raman features include a peak at 198 cm−1 attributed to the second-order 2Elow2 scattering process, a band at 321 cm−1 associated with zone-boundary phonons (Ehigh2–Elow2), and a peak at 377 cm−1 assigned to the first-order A1(TO) mode.36,38,39 The band observed at 571 cm−1 corresponds to the longitudinal optical phonons A1(LO) and, in particular, E1(LO), which are highly sensitive to intrinsic defects such as oxygen vacancies, zinc interstitials, and their associated complexes.36,39–41 A weak feature around 656 cm−1 can be associated with TA + LO phonon combinations, while the broad band appearing in the 1000–1200 cm−1 region originates from overlapping second-order multiphonon processes involving 2TO and 2LO modes.37,39

Upon KCl etching to form ZnONTs, the overall Raman intensities exhibit a general decreasing trend, indicating progressive modification of the lattice structure. In particular, a pronounced reduction in the intensity of the Ehigh2 (427–424 cm−1) mode is observed with increasing etching time. This effect becomes most significant for ZnONTs20, where the substantial suppression of the Ehigh2 peak suggests increased lattice disorder induced by over-etching. Such degradation of crystalline order is consistent with the fragmentation of the nanotube walls and loss of structural integrity observed in the FESEM image of ZnONTs20 (Fig. 2f). Concurrently, the LO-related band becomes progressively broader and increasingly asymmetric as the etching duration increases, reflecting the strong sensitivity of LO phonons to defect-related structural perturbations in ZnO. To compare changes in the LO-related Raman feature among the samples, the intensity ratio ILO/IE2high is directly obtained from the measured spectra. As summarized in Table 3, this ratio increases from ZnONRs to ZnONTs18, reaches a maximum at ZnONTs18, and subsequently decreases for ZnONTs20. This behavior indicates that defect density and surface-related disorder increase as nanorods are converted into well-defined nanotubes due to the enlarged effective surface area, whereas excessive etching leads to partial destruction of the tubular walls and a consequent reduction in accessible defect-active sites. Similar defect evolution trends have been reported in ZnO nanostructures as nanorods are converted into nanotubes via KCl etching.25,42 In addition to intensity variations, all Raman modes exhibit a slight redshift with increasing etching time, as summarized in Table 2. This systematic shift toward lower wavenumbers is attributed to enhanced lattice strain and strengthened phonon–defect coupling arising from etching-induced intrinsic defects.43 Such Raman redshifts are fully consistent with the lattice expansion and strain relaxation inferred from the XRD analysis.

Despite these modifications, the characteristic Raman signatures of wurtzite ZnO remain clearly identifiable for all etched samples, indicating preservation of the ZnO phase. After Au decoration, the Raman signals of ZnO are significantly suppressed for both ZnONRs/Au and ZnONTs18/Au, accompanied by additional redshifts of the characteristic peaks. In particular, the Ehigh2 mode undergoes marked attenuation. This Raman quenching effect is mainly attributed to strong absorption of the 532 nm excitation by AuNPs deposited on the ZnO surface, as well as interfacial charge-transfer interactions in the ZnO/Au heterostructure, consistent with previous reports on Au-modified ZnO nanostructures.44

To further identify defect species in ZnO and ZnO/Au nanostructures, room temperature PL spectra are recorded and presented in Fig. 8a. All samples exhibit two characteristic emission bands. The ultraviolet (UV) emission centered at about 376 nm arises from near-band-edge (NBE) excitonic recombination in ZnO.32,36 Meanwhile, a strong and broad visible emission band centered around 600 nm is observed, which is commonly associated with intrinsic defect states in ZnO.45–47

After chemical etching of ZnONRs into ZnONTs in KCl solution for different etching times, both the UV and visible emission intensities of the ZnONT samples increase compared to those of ZnONRs. For the UV emission, the conversion from solid rods to hollow nanotubes introduces a tubular architecture with well-defined walls that can act as optical cavities. Multiple internal reflections and enhanced light confinement within the tube walls effectively promote radiative recombination, leading to enhanced UV emission. However, for the ZnONTs20 sample, the UV emission intensity begins to decrease due to excessive etching, which partially damages the tube walls and shortens the nanotube length, thereby weakening the optical cavity effect.25,48 Regarding the visible emission, controlled KCl etching increases the effective surface area and generates a high density of surface-related defects, particularly oxygen vacancies, during the rod-to-tube transformation, resulting in an overall enhancement of the defect-related visible emission for the ZnONT samples. At prolonged etching times, a similar over-etching effect leads to a decrease in visible emission intensity, which can be attributed to structural degradation of the nanotubes and the consequent reduction in surface defect density.25,48

Since oxygen vacancy-related surface defects have been reported to introduce intermediate energy levels within the ZnO bandgap and play a crucial role in facilitating charge-transfer processes in SERS,49–52 the present discussion focuses on the variation of the visible emission in this sample series. As shown in Fig. 8b, the broad and asymmetric visible band can be deconvoluted into three individual Gaussian components. The emission peaks appearing in the range of 569–591 nm are typically assigned to singly charged oxygen vacancies (VO+, green fit), while those located in the range of 626–656 nm correspond to doubly charged oxygen vacancies (VO++, orange fit).45–47 The long wavelength emission peaks observed between 699 and 739 nm are commonly attributed to zinc vacancies and/or oxygen interstitials (VZn/Oi, gray fit).46,47

Analysis of the deconvoluted PL spectra in Fig. 8b shows that the emission components associated with oxygen vacancies progressively increase and reach a maximum for the ZnONTs18 sample, indicating an enhanced formation of VO+ and VO++ during the rod-to-tube conversion. When the etching duration is further extended, the relative contribution of these oxygen vacancy-related emissions decreases for ZnONTs20. This trend is in excellent agreement with the defect evolution inferred from the Raman analysis discussed in Fig. 7, where the LO phonon modes associated with oxygen vacancies exhibit a similar dependence on etching time. Consequently, ZnONTs18 emerges as the most promising SERS substrate among the etched samples, owing to its high density of oxygen vacancies capable of generating surface state energy levels within the bandgap that enhance the probability of charge transfer.

After Au decoration, both ZnONRs/Au and ZnONTs18/Au exhibit a pronounced quenching of the UV and visible PL emissions. This behavior is ascribed to the suppression of radiative recombination of photogenerated electron–hole pairs caused by the intimate contact between ZnO and AuNPs. For the UV emission, electrons in the conduction band of ZnO are readily transferred to the lower Fermi level of Au. For the visible emission, the defect-related states of ZnO lie energetically close to the LSPR region of AuNPs, and the ZnO/Au interface enables plasmon-assisted electron transfer through plasmon coupling. As a result, electrons trapped at defect levels that would otherwise contribute to visible emission are transferred to the AuNPs, leading to a significant decline in defect-related PL intensity. Similar PL quenching phenomena have been widely reported for ZnO/Au heterostructures.32,36 In addition, Fig. 8 shows that the defect-related visible emission of ZnO/Au exhibits an obvious red shift relative to bare ZnO. This behavior reflects changes in surface defect states induced by interfacial charge-transfer interactions at the ZnO/Au junction after Au decoration, which is beneficial for promoting charge-transfer processes in SERS applications.53

Furthermore, the XPS technique was conducted to elucidate the surface chemical composition and electronic structure of ZnONTs18/Au, particularly to confirm defects such as oxygen vacancies in ZnO. As shown in the survey XPS spectrum (Fig. 9a), the presence of Zn, O, and Au elements is clearly identified, verifying the successful decoration of AuNPs on the ZnO nanotube surface. A weak C 1s signal is also observed, which can be attributed to adventitious hydrocarbons commonly introduced from atmospheric exposure during XPS measurements.54

(a) Survey XPS spectrum of ZnONTs18/Au. High-resolution XPS spectra of (b) Zn 2p, (c) O 1s, and (d) Au 4f, Zn 3p in ZnONTs18/Au.

Fig. 9b displays the high-resolution Zn 2p spectrum, featuring two well-defined peaks centered at 1022.0 eV and 1045.1 eV, corresponding to Zn 2p3/2 and Zn 2p1/2, respectively. The spin–orbit splitting of approximately 23.1 eV is characteristic of Zn2+ species in wurtzite ZnO, indicating that zinc exists in the oxidized state in the ZnONTs18/Au sample.55 The high-resolution O 1s spectrum (Fig. 9c) can be deconvoluted into three components located at 530.5, 531.8, and 532.7 eV. The low-binding-energy peak at 530.5 eV is assigned to lattice oxygen (OL) in the Zn–O framework, whereas the medium-binding-energy component at 531.8 eV is associated with oxygen vacancies (OV). The high-binding-energy contribution at 532.7 eV originates from chemisorbed oxygen species or surface hydroxyl groups (OC), consistent with previous reports on ZnO nanostructures.54,55 Notably, the pronounced OV component reflects a high density of oxygen vacancies in ZnONTs18/Au, which is in good agreement with the defect-related visible emission behavior observed in the PL spectra (Fig. 8). Fig. 9d presents the overlapping Zn 3p and Au 4f core-level spectra, which can be reliably resolved into four peaks located at 88.5 eV (Zn 3p3/2), 91.4 eV (Zn 3p1/2), 83.6 eV (Au 4f7/2), and 87.2 eV (Au 4f5/2). The Au 4f peaks exhibit a slight negative shift relative to bulk Au, suggesting the formation of negatively charged AuNPs. This shift is most likely induced by interfacial electron transfer and strong electronic coupling at the ZnO/Au heterointerface,55 further supporting plasmon-assisted charge redistribution, in accordance with the PL quenching behavior discussed in Fig. 8.

Taken together, the above optical, structural, and surface chemical analyses consistently indicate that the ZnONTs18 sample possesses a relatively higher density of oxygen vacancy-related defects compared with pristine ZnONRs and other etched ZnONTs samples. Moreover, FESEM and EDX mapping results confirm that AuNPs are well embedded and uniformly distributed on both the inner and outer surfaces of the nanotubular architecture in the ZnONTs18/Au hybrid. These combined features provide a reasonable basis to suggest that the synergistic coexistence of oxygen vacancies and AuNPs surface modification plays a crucial role in tailoring the interfacial properties of the ZnO/Au heterostructure. As a result, ZnONTs18/Au is expected to offer more favorable conditions for charge-transfer processes and electromagnetic coupling, rendering it a particularly promising candidate for high-performance SERS substrates, as further evaluated in the next section.

Among the ZnO-only substrates, the SERS intensities of the ZnONT samples are consistently higher than that of pristine ZnONRs and follow the order ZnONTs18 > ZnONTs20 > ZnONTs16 > ZnONRs. The greatest enhancement observed for ZnONTs18 is fully consistent with the structural and spectroscopic characterizations discussed earlier. FESEM observations indicate that an etching time of 18 h yields well-defined nanotubular architectures with enlarged surface area and readily accessible inner and outer surfaces, which favor effective adsorption of MB molecules. Concurrently, Raman and PL analyses demonstrate that ZnONTs18 possesses the highest density of oxygen vacancy-related defect states. These defects introduce surface-related energy levels within the ZnO bandgap, facilitating charge-transfer interactions between MB molecules and the ZnO surface and thereby enhancing the chemical contribution to the SERS response.16,49–52,57

Upon Au decoration, a pronounced enhancement in SERS intensity is observed for both ZnONRs/Au and ZnONTs18/Au substrates, as shown in Fig. 10. Remarkably, ZnONTs18/Au outperforms ZnONRs/Au, highlighting the synergistic effect arising from the coupling of plasmonic AuNPs with the defect-rich ZnO nanotube framework. XPS analysis confirms the coexistence of metallic Au and abundant surface oxygen vacancies at the ZnO/Au interface. These features promote charge-transfer interactions under laser excitation, while the spatial proximity of adjacent AuNPs enables efficient plasmonic coupling, generating intense localized electromagnetic hot spots within interparticle nanogaps and at AuNP–ZnO interfaces. The contributions from interfacial charge transfer and electromagnetic field localization collectively account for the markedly enhanced SERS response observed for the ZnONTs18/Au substrate.

To assess the low-concentration sensing capability toward MB, a series of MB solutions ranging from 10−3 to 10−13 M is drop-cast onto the as-prepared SERS substrates, and the corresponding spectra are shown in Fig. 11. A gradual decrease in SERS intensity is observed on all substrates with decreasing MB concentration. Using the blank-substrate-based S/N procedure described in Section 2.4, the LOD was determined as the lowest MB concentration that yielded a distinguishable 1623 cm−1 band with S/N > 3. The LOD values are 10−7 M for ZnONRs, 10−8 M for the etched ZnONT substrates (ZnONTs16, ZnONTs18, and ZnONTs20), 10−10 M for ZnONRs/Au, and 10−12 M for ZnONTs18/Au (Fig. 11a–f). The one-order improvement in LOD achieved by ZnONTs compared with pristine ZnONRs indicates that the oxygen-vacancy-related surface defects intentionally introduced during the rod-to-tube etching process play a key role in boosting the Raman response of the semiconductor ZnO substrates. In particular, these defect sites may increase the density of surface states and introduce associated surface-state energy levels within the ZnO bandgap,16,49–52,57 which can enhance the probability of charge transfer between MB and ZnO and thereby strengthen the chemical contribution. It is worth noting that in Fig. 11a–d the characteristic ZnO Ehigh2 mode at ∼424–430 cm−1 becomes more apparent at low MB concentrations, where the surface coverage of MB molecules is sparse. At higher MB concentrations, this lattice mode is largely masked by the nearby MB band around 444 cm−1 (assigned to skeletal deformation of C–N–C), resulting in an apparently merged band centered near ∼444 cm−1 in the low-wavenumber region.

To elucidate the charge-transfer behavior from a thermodynamic perspective, the interfacial electronic interactions between the SERS substrates and MB molecules are further analyzed. Based on the Tauc analysis (Fig. 5b) and the approach proposed by Xu et al.,62 the valence band (VB) and conduction band (CB) edges of ZnO are positioned at −7.39 eV and −4.19 eV, respectively. The Fermi level of Au is located at −5.1 eV,29 according to previous reports, while the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) levels of MB are situated at −6.11 eV and −4.25 eV, respectively.15,18 In addition, a distribution of oxygen-vacancy-induced surface-state energy levels (ESS) is schematically illustrated within the ZnO band gap. These ESS are energetically separated from the CB minimum and are qualitatively located approximately 1.6–2.3 eV below the CB edge, as inferred from previously reported ZnO-based systems.16,49–52,57

On the basis of these energy-level positions, the possible charge-transfer pathways are summarized in Fig. 12a and b. Under 532 nm laser excitation (2.33 eV), which is comparable to or exceeds the energy differences between relevant electronic states, multiple charge-transfer-related resonance processes may become energetically accessible and can contribute to the Raman enhancement of MB adsorbed on the ZnO and ZnO/Au substrates. These possible processes include molecular resonance (µmol) within the MB molecule, excitonic resonance (µex) associated with ESS in ZnO, photon-induced charge-transfer resonance (µPICT) involving electron transfer among ZnO, Au, and MB, and ground-state charge-transfer resonance (µGSCT) arising from electronic redistribution driven by favorable energy-level alignment.

Schematic energy-level diagram illustrating the possible charge-transfer processes between (a) ZnO and MB, (b) ZnONTs18/Au and MB under 532 nm excitation. (c) ρCT values used to discuss the a qualitative indicator of the relative contribution of the charge-transfer mechanism for ZnO and ZnO/Au hybrid SERS substrates.

For the ZnO-only substrates (Fig. 12a), ESS can act as intermediate electronic states that facilitate defect-assisted electron transfer toward the LUMO of MB under optical excitation. The rod-to-tube transformation induced by KCl etching enriches surface oxygen vacancies, introducing additional defect-related states within the band gap and facilitating electron-transfer processes that can enhance the chemical contribution to the SERS activity. With the incorporation of AuNPs (Fig. 12b), the presence of a metallic Fermi level establishes additional possible electron-transfer pathways among ZnO, Au, and MB. The favorable energy-level alignment between the CB of ZnO, the Fermi level of Au, and the frontier molecular orbitals (HOMO and LUMO) of MB can promote photo-induced and ground-state charge redistribution, thereby facilitating interfacial electron transfer. The interplay between defect-mediated transitions and Au-assisted electron transfer can contribute to the enhanced Raman response of MB adsorbed on the ZnO/Au hybrid substrates.

To discuss the qualitative trend in the charge-transfer-related contribution, the degree of charge transfer (ρCT) was considered according to the mode-selective intensity analysis proposed by Lombardi and Birke, as expressed in eqn (5):635where κ denotes an index identifying a specific Raman mode. In this formalism, Iκ(CT) represents the measured intensity of mode κ when a charge-transfer resonance provides an additional contribution, Iκ(LSPR) represents the reference intensity of the same mode associated with the plasmonic contribution, and I0(LSPR) corresponds to the intensity of a selected totally symmetric reference mode. It should be noted that ρCT is mode-dependent; therefore, in the present work, the ρCT values are used to compare the qualitative trend in charge-transfer-related enhancement among the substrates measured under identical conditions.

For this comparison, two characteristic MB bands were selected. The 1394 cm−1 band, assigned to symmetric C–N stretching, was used as the totally symmetric reference band, whereas the 1437 cm−1 band, assigned to asymmetric C–N stretching, was used as the charge-transfer-sensitive non-totally symmetric band. According to the model described by Lombardi and Birke, Franck–Condon-type contributions mainly enhance totally symmetric modes, whereas Herzberg–Teller vibronic coupling can enhance non-totally symmetric vibrations under charge-transfer resonance conditions. Accordingly, the relative enhancement of the non-totally symmetric 1437 cm−1 band with respect to the totally symmetric 1394 cm−1 band can be used to follow the qualitative trend in the charge-transfer-related contribution. In this context, the ρCT values are discussed as relative indicators of charge-transfer-related response, and small numerical differences among individual values are not used for statistical comparison. Among the ZnO-only substrates, the ρCT values increase from ZnONRs to ZnONTs18 and then decrease for ZnONTs20. This trend indicates that the optimized oxygen-vacancy-rich ZnO nanotube substrate exhibits the strongest charge-transfer-related response among the ZnO-only substrates. This observation is consistent with the Raman and PL analyses, which show that ZnONTs18 possesses the highest density of oxygen-vacancy-related defects among the etched ZnO substrates. These defect-induced surface states can favor interfacial charge-transfer interactions between MB and ZnO, thereby strengthening the chemical contribution to the SERS response.

After AuNP loading, the ρCT values of ZnONRs/Au and ZnONTs18/Au are lower than those of the ZnO-only nanotube substrates, while their overall SERS intensities are markedly enhanced. This behavior suggests that plasmonic electromagnetic enhancement becomes more prominent in the Au-decorated systems. Nevertheless, ZnONTs18/Au still shows a higher ρCT value than ZnONRs/Au at both MB concentrations, indicating that the oxygen-vacancy-rich nanotube framework can still favor charge-transfer-related interactions even in the presence of Au-induced plasmonic enhancement. Overall, the ρCT analysis supports the qualitative trend that oxygen-vacancy engineering enhances charge-transfer-related interactions in ZnO nanotubes, while Au decoration introduces a stronger plasmonic electromagnetic contribution.

To assess uniformity, SERS spectra of 10−10 M MB are collected from ten randomly selected spots within a 5 mm × 5 mm region on the same ZnONTs18/Au substrate (Fig. 13a). The batch-to-batch reproducibility is further examined using five independently prepared ZnONTs18/Au substrates synthesized under identical conditions (Fig. 13c). Overall, the ZnONTs18/Au substrate exhibits high SERS sensitivity, high spatial signal uniformity, and satisfactory batch-to-batch reproducibility. These results suggest that the proposed fabrication strategy can produce structurally uniform ZnO/Au hybrid substrates with acceptable reproducibility under the present experimental conditions.

Conclusion

In this work, a defect-engineered ZnO/Au hybrid SERS platform is developed via KCl-assisted rod-to-tube transformation followed by Au nanoparticle decoration. The KCl etching process promotes the formation of surface oxygen vacancies, thereby increasing the density of vacancy-related surface states within the ZnO band gap. As supported by the MB-based analysis, these defect-induced states can facilitate interfacial charge-transfer interactions, thereby enhancing the chemical contribution to SERS. Consistently, among the semiconductor-only substrates, ZnONTs18 exhibits the highest density of oxygen-vacancy-related defects and achieves the best performance for MB detection, with an LOD of 10−8 M. The analysis based on the degree of charge transfer is used to discuss the qualitative trend in charge-transfer-related enhancement and shows that ZnONTs18, as the optimized oxygen-vacancy-rich ZnO nanotube substrate, exhibits the strongest charge-transfer-related response among the ZnO-only substrates. After Au loading, the optimized ZnONTs18/Au substrate achieves markedly enhanced MB detection, reaching an LOD as low as 10−12 M and an AEF of 2.5 × 109. The enhanced MB signal can be attributed to the synergistic interplay between plasmon-induced electromagnetic enhancement and defect-assisted charge-transfer interactions associated with the oxygen-vacancy-rich nanotube framework. The optimized substrate further enables proof-of-concept malathion detection with an LOD of 0.01 mg L−1 and an AEF of 2.8 × 106. Moreover, MB-based uniformity and reproducibility tests show high spatial signal uniformity (RSD = 5.82%) and satisfactory batch-to-batch fabrication reproducibility (RSD = 10.16%) for the ZnONTs18/Au substrate. Overall, these results demonstrate the effectiveness of the KCl-assisted defect engineering and Au nanoparticle decoration strategy for constructing highly sensitive ZnO/Au SERS substrates and provide a proof-of-concept demonstration of SERS-based malathion detection using standard solutions. The present findings establish a useful basis for future real-sample validation, including recovery tests, evaluation of matrix effects, selectivity and interference assessments, and extended reproducibility studies, to further advance this platform toward practical pesticide sensing.
