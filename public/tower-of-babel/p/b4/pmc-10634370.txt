Electric Field-Driven Self-Assembly of Gold Nanoparticle Monolayers on Silicon Substrates
Authors: Firdous
Ahmad Deader, Yawar Abbas, Ahsanulhaq Qurashi, Mahmoud Al-Qutayri, Vincent Chan, Moh’d Rezeq
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractNanoparticles (NPs) bridge the gap between bulk materials and their equivalent molecular/atomic counterparts. The physical, optical, and electronic properties of individual NPs alter with the changes in their surrounding environment at the nanoscale. Similarly, the characteristics of thin films of NPs depend on their lateral and volumetric densities. Thus, attaining single monolayers of these NPs would play a vital role in the improved characteristics of semiconductor devices such as nanosensors, field effect transistors, and energy harvesting devices. Developing nanosensors, for instance, requires precise methods to fabricate a monolayer of NPs on selected substrates for sensing and other applications. Herein, we developed a physical fabrication method to form a monolayer of NPs on a planar silicon surface by creating an electric field of intensity 5.71 × 104 V/m between parallel plates of a capacitor, by applying a DC voltage. The physics of monolayer formation caused by an externally applied electric field on the gold NPs (Au-NPs) of size 20 nm in diameter and possesses a zeta potential of −250 to −290 mV, is further analyzed with the help of the finite element simulation. The enhanced electric field, in the order of 108 V/m, around the Au-NPs indicates a high surface charge density on the NPs, which results in a high electric force per unit area that guides them to settle uniformly on the surface of the silicon substrate.

Introduction

The chemical and physical characteristics of self-assembled nanoparticles (NPs) vary significantly from those of bulk materials,1,2 or even from the corresponding individual NPs.3,4 Considering this fact, NPs have been classified as a new class of functional materials5 and have found widespread applications in various fields, including electronics, optics, catalysis, and sensing.1,6 Therefore, a precisely controlled method of these NPs in terms of size, shape, and interparticle spacing is essential for fully utilizing their new collective and synergistic features.2,6 NPs, both in colloidal and powdered form, have unique and significant applications in medicine,7 nanoelectronics,8−10 nanosensors,9 fundamental research,11 and other industrial applications.12−14 The uniform size and shape of NPs achieved through various types of synthesis have been overwhelmingly improved to increase the efficiency of NP-based devices in terms of their conductivity, sensitivity, selectivity, etc. However, multiple hurdles have been posed to nanotechnology researchers when it comes to forming NP monolayers on substrates.15 For instance, when a droplet of a colloidal solution containing the target NPs is drop-casted on the surface of the substrate, it spreads onto the surface by convective flow resulting in a particle flux toward the drying front.16 During this drying process, the NPs come closer to each other due to the evaporation of the solvent in the solution. As a result, the electric repulsion force between NPs decreases, resulting in monolayer islands and clusters of NPs on the substrate surface.

Various methods have been developed for the 2D self-assembly of NPs on a substrate surface, including Langmuir–Blodgett (L–B) deposition technique, which is commonly employed for two-dimensional (2D) self-assembly of hydrophobic organic ligand-coated NPs into a close-packed monolayer film at the air–water interface.17 Then, monolayers are transferred to the solid substrates using the classical Langmuir–Schaefer (L–S) method, where a hydrophobic surface, such as a carbon-coated transmission electron microscopy (TEM) grid, is horizontally touched to the monolayer of NPs to lift it.18,19 However, this method is limited to hydrophobic surfaces, and a large area of the monolayer is often challenging to obtain. Spin coating is another standard technique for the two-dimensional self-assembly of NPs, although it frequently faces difficulties in obtaining monolayers. Instead, it usually yields multilayers or NP clusters.20 In another report, researchers fabricated a centimeter-sized monolayer of NPs by controlling the evaporation direction of the solvent and the flux of NPs in the solution. Two solvents of distinct volatilities are used to control the evaporation of the solvents, and the L–S deposition technique is utilized to transport the NPs film from the liquid phase to the solid substrate.21 A synthetic procedure to obtain the monodispersed nanocrystals on a large scale in a single reaction is also developed, where direct synthesis of iron and iron oxide nanocrystals are grown when the metal–surfactant complex is used as a growth source.22 A well-defined periodic arrays of gold nanospheres (Au-NSs), such as hexagonal and triangular, have also been investigated on the patterned polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) mold.23 In one of the recent studies, the 2D self-assembly of alkanethiolate-capped Au-NPs is demonstrated when negatively charged Au-NPs are drop-casted onto toluene, and a monolayer film of closely packed Au-NPs forms at the air–liquid interface of a toluene droplet. Liquid toluene evaporates and a 2D monolayer of Au-NPs settles on the solid substrate.24 These reports focus on the monolayer formation of NPs using sophisticated chemistry, and precise positioning of NPs in targeted location is often challenging to obtain.

The NPs can exist in two forms; powdered and in the solution. In the solution form, the NPs are stabilized using stabilizing agents like citrate25 and thiolate ligands.26 The surfactant coating on these NPs creates a repulsion barrier that prevents their cores from agglomerating irreversibly. In the solution, there are two fundamental interactions between the particles: core–core attractions and ligand–ligand repulsions.17 This allows the NPs to modify their relative positions until they form a low-energy, self-organized pattern, such as 2D monolayers.27,28 It is also investigated in one of the experiments using SNIFTIR spectra of Cit-Au-NPs (111) that, via COOH hydrogen bonds, the adsorbed citrate on the Au-NPs surface interacts with a different kind of citrate anion in the solution.29 In another work, using atomic-resolution TEM analysis, researchers discovered two to three molecular layers of citrate molecules on Au-NPs.30 These citrate layers on the Au-NPs are formed due to the hydrogen bonding of the free COOH group attached to the citrate molecules.31 Citrate trianions (citrate3–) are known to have an electrostatic stabilizing effect on Cit-Au-NPs, citrate layers’ electric repulsion force, which helps to stabilize Au-NPs.32,33 Some researchers have used an externally applied electric field for the directed assembly of nanomaterials.34 The suspended nanomaterials must be charged either by functionalization or by adsorption of charged species. The movement or migration of nanomaterials toward the electrodes due to the application of an electric field is called electrophoresis.35 With the electrophoresis technique, 110 nm diameter Au-NPs have been assembled in a predefined position with minimal defect sites for possible optical applications.36 Similarly, the frequency-dependent AC voltage-based assembly of Au-NPs has been demonstrated by dropping the Au-NPs colloid between the predefined electrodes.37 Unlike these reports, our work focuses on the self-assembly of Au-NP monolayer formation on the surface of the substrate.

In this work, we report the monolayer self-assembly of citrate-stabilized Au-NPs, by vertically applying an electric field on the n-Si substrate during the reduction in the electric repulsion force due to the evaporation of solvent. The citrate-capped Au-NPs carry a negative zeta (ζ) potential on their surface,24,38 which is further confirmed by Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM). These negatively charged Au-NPs are attracted toward the positively biased silicon substrate. Because of the high electric field near the surface of NPs with very small sizes, and hence a high charge density39−41 the electric force drags the NPs down uniformly, this results in the electric field-driven self-assembly of a Au-NPs monolayer. Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) images depict that NPs under the electric field effect are quite uniformly arranged on the silicon substrate. For comparison, the process is repeated in the ambient environment, without the electric field and with an opposite-field polarity (negative polarity). The FESEM images of all cases exhibited a clear evidence of the effect of the positive electric field polarity on the NPs monolayer assembly on the silicon surface. The impact of the high electric field on monolayer formation is further corroborated by a simulation model performed using COMSOL Multiphysics software.

Experimental Procedure

Gold NPs (Au-NPs) are capped with the citrate ligands around the surface; because of citrate ligands on Au-NPs, they carry a negative ζ-potential, which prevents the agglomeration of Au-NPs.24,38 This negative potential around the surface of Au-NPs is utilized to drag them on the silicon substrate. These Au-NPs are distributed in a citrate solution for stabilization and surface functionalization. If Au-NPs without citrate surfactant are dispersed in deionized water (DI) water, the process would still work but under a much higher electric field, since these NPs are metal NPs, and they get polarized due to the strong electric field.

Citrate-stabilized Au-NPs of diameter 20 nm were purchased from Ted Pella, INC. Silicon substrates were purchased from University Wafer, Inc. with 5–10 Ω cm resistivity. 1 cm × 1 cm silicon substrate was sequentially cleaned using acetone, followed by ethanol, then rinsed with DI, and dried using N2. The NP solution was centrifuged for 20 min at 6000 rpm to increase the concentration of NPs from 7.0 × 1011 to ∼5.0 × 1013 particles/mL. The supernatant solution was decanted in another test tube, and the precipitate solution of the NP was later used in the experiment.

Figure 1 shows the complete processes of sample preparation. This includes the centrifugation, decantation of supernatant, sonication, and drop-casting of precipitate solution. The concentration of the precipitate-containing solution is increased by ∼100 times, which is suitable for the monolayer formation when the solution is drop-casted on the silicon substrate in the electric field environment. Also, the centrifugation of NP colloidal solution ensures that the precipitated particles are of similar sizes, ∼20 nm in our case. Before drop-casting the NP solution on the silicon substrate, the solution was sonicated to distribute the NPs in the citrate solution uniformly. Two μL of Au-NPs solution were drop-casted on three cleaned n-Si substrates, where one substrate was kept in ambient and the other two were kept in the high electric field environment.

Preprocessing of Au-NPs solution; the solution is centrifuged for 20 min at 6000 rpm, and the precipitate is used for drop casting. The precipitate solution is sonicated for 30 min for the uniform film. Finally, 2 μL of NPs solution is drop-casted on the silicon substrate and left for evaporation under the effect of the electric field.

Figure 2 depicts the experimental setup for the electric-field-self-assembly of Au-NPs on the silicon substrate. Figure 2a shows a schematic of the experimental setup, with a parallel plate capacitor structure for applying a uniform electric field on the Au-NPs solution droplet on the silicon substrate. The parallel plate capacitor is built from copper plates separated by 0.7 cm. The silicon substrate is then placed on the center of the positively biased plate, and a DC voltage of 400 V is applied between the plates, which creates a uniform electric field of ≈ 5.71 × 104 V/m, between the plates. The solution was left to dry in the electric field for ∼40 min, and once the solution is fully dried the voltage is turned off and the silicon substrate is removed and again cleaned sequentially by acetone followed by ethanol and rinsed with DI water.

Results and Discussion

The examination of UV–visible absorption spectra is widely regarded as one of the most reliable techniques for verifying the formation of Au-NPs.43 The NPs’ size and shape may be anticipated by measuring the intensity of absorption of incident light from the UV–visible source44 due to the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) effect shown by the Au-NPs.45,46

UV–vis spectroscopy for the Au-NPs colloid was conducted for the original and a higher concentration. We observed that for the centrifuged Au-NPs colloid the intensity and the corresponding absorption peak wavelength were higher than that of the original concentration. In fact, the increase in intensity and wavelength indicates a higher concentration of Au-NPs colloid after centrifuging,47 as shown in Figure 3, the absorption peaks for 20 nm Au-NPs can be seen at λmax = 520 nm and λmax = 540 nm for lower and higher concentration respectively as shown in Figure 3. UV–vis spectroscopy of gold NPs of diameter 20 nm before and after the centrifugation. The inset is the image of the colloid of Au-NPs vial before and after centrifugation.

To study the effect of the electric field and field polarity on Au-NPs layer formation on the surface of the n-Si substrate, with a native silicon oxide layer on the surface, the SEM images of samples prepared under different conditions were analyzed, as shown in Figure 4. Figure 4 show SEM micrographs of the films when the substrate is on neutral, negatively biased, and positively biased substrates, respectively. Figure 4a shows agglomerated films of Au-NPs when the colloidal droplet was drop-cast on a neutral silicon substrate and left to dry in the ambient environment. Figure 4b shows dispersed clusters of Au-NPs when the droplet was drop-cast on a negatively biased substrate and then left to dry. These dispersed clusters of Au-NPs are mostly caused by the repulsion of a negatively biased plate on the negatively charged and suspended Au-NPs in the solvent. Figure 4 shows the formation of electric field-driven self-assembly of uniform Au-NPs monolayers when the solution is drop-casted on a positively biased substrate. The formation of a well-distributed monolayer of Au-NPs is speculated to be caused by the balanced electric repulsion and surface tension horizontal forces, and due to the effect of the net electric force on the negatively charged Au-NPs toward the surface of the substrate. Figure 4 demonstrate well-arranged Au-NPs. However, some small vacancies and irregularities were observed on the surface, as in Figure 4f, and this can be attributed to some dips or contamination on the surface, where the electric field is not perfectly uniform. To produce monolayers at specific locations on the substrate, the electric field can be applied at the area of interest where the NPs are intended to settle. The charged NPs will follow the electric force toward the surface and settle on the desired location.

AFM and Kelvin Probe Characterization

Figure 5 shows the principle of measurement of the surface topography and the potential on the NPs. Two different AFM tips were used: one is a gold-coated conductive tip for KPFM and another one is a Si tip for the topography of the Au-NPs monolayer. We would like to indicate that the gold coated tips, which are needed for KPFM, have a slightly bigger apex diameter than the silicon tips which we used for the topography, as shown in Figure S5. The monolayer of Au-NPs formed on the silicon substrate was analyzed first in a topography AFM microscope, as shown in the schematic in Figure 5a. The AFM image in Figure 5b confirms the formation of the monolayer of Au-NPs. However, it is worth indicating here that the resolution of AFM is directly dominated by the AFM probe apex size and shape, as demonstrated in Figure S5. This explains the slight difference in contrast and shapes of NPs on the surface, as shown in Figure 5b. To further investigate the monolayer formation of the NPs, the AFM probe was scanned over an edge of the monolayer, as shown in Figure 5c. The topography profile along the scan line, the dashed horizontal line in Figure 5c, is taken as the AFM tip crosses over the edge of the monolayer and then moves on the surface of the substrate. The height of the monolayer is found to be approximately 20 nm, which is consistent with the sizes of the used NPs, as demonstrated in Figure 3.

Further analysis was conducted on the NPs monolayer by utilizing the Kelvin probe technique. Through the utilization of KPFM, it is possible to visually represent the surface potential of materials at the nanoscale.48 Since KPFM is a noncontact mode of AFM, it is a nondestructive technique to measure the surface potential of the materials at the nanoscale. This capability of measuring the surface potential of the material is crucial to know the type and distribution of the charge on the surface of the sample.

Figure 5d depicts a schematic illustration elucidating the fundamental principles of KPFM. The surface potential distribution across the monolayer of Au-NPs is exhibited in Figure 5e, ranging from approximately −250 to −290 mV. However, it is worth noting the existence of elevated negative potentials in the red areas, as appears in Figure 5e, can be attributed to surface irregularities and contaminants.

COMSOL Simulation

To elaborate on the physics of monolayer formation, we have built a finite element simulation model using COMSOL Multiphysics software, as shown in Figure 6. Figure 6a shows the electric field around the Au-NPs in the initial stage when they are scattered in the colloidal solution. Figure 6b,c illustrates the time evolution of NPs alignment due to the externally applied voltage and Au-NP’s negative zeta potential. These Au-NPs showed a very high electric field around them on the order of 108 V/m, which indicates a high surface charge density and hence high electric force per unit area on them. As the Au-NPs are moving down due to the electric field force and the solvent is evaporating, the electric repulsion force between the Au-NPs will be balanced by the side citrate surface tension force. When these NPs reach the surface of the silicon substrate, they form an arranged monolayer, as depicted in Figure 6d.

COMSOL simulation of electric field effect on the negatively charged Au-NPs, under a positively biased substrate. (a) Electric field distribution on the randomly scattered charged Au-NPs. (b,c) Au-NPs come closer to each other due to the effect of the vertical electric field. (d) Au-NPs settle and form a monolayer on the silicon substrate.

Conclusions

In this work, we developed a process for in situ formation of Au-NP monolayers on a silicon substrate. An optimum concentration of negatively charged Au-NPs are drop-casted on the silicon substrate in an external electric field of ∼5.71 × 104 V/m. The negative potential of ∼−(250 to 290 mV) on the surface of Au-NPs is confirmed with the help of KPFM, and the formation of monolayers of ∼20 nm Au-NPs is observed directly in SEM. The physics-based model, COMSOL Multiphysics, revealed that the mechanism of in-situ self-assembly is caused by the high electric field on the Au-NPs, and their high charge density, which consequently causes the drift of the Au-NPs toward the positively biased silicon substrate. This method will pave the way for the fabrication of monolayers on complex structures in a simpler process compared to other conventional methods.
