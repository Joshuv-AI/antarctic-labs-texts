Cerebral venous thrombosis in non-bacterial infections: a systematic review
Authors: Matheus Campos Medeiros, Ezequias Batista Martins, Lusiele Guaraldo, Guilherme Amaral Calvet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis systematic review summarizes evidence on cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT) following non-bacterial (viral, fungal, parasitic) infections, excluding COVID-19, by describing etiology, clinical features, management, and outcomes.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines and PROSPERO registration (CRD42023445694), we searched MEDLINE/PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and LILACS from inception to July 31, 2025. Two reviewers independently performed study selection, data extraction, and quality assessment using Joanna Briggs Institute tools.ResultsFrom 14,127 records, 278 publications from 1958 to 2025 (262 case reports and 16 case series), encompassing 322 patients were included. Median age was 39 years (IQR 25–55); 54% were male. Viral infections (35%; primarily varicella zoster and HIV) and fungal mucormycosis (35%) were the most common etiologies. While neuroimaging was performed in 95% of cases, specific pathogen identification (biopsy, culture, PCR) was used in fewer than half. Mucormycosis typically involved the cavernous sinus and was observed alongside high mortality (68%). Viral infections often affected multiple sinuses and were reported in association with more favorable outcomes (54% favorable). Anticoagulants were administered in 93% of viral cases, but were administered less frequently in mucormycosis (25%). Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) were prescribed for only nine patients. Recanalization was reported in 17% of cases, although follow-up data were unavailable or unreported for most (81%).ConclusionsNon-bacterial infections, notably viral pathogens and mucormycosis, are significant causes of CVT with distinct clinical profiles. Management strategies varied substantially by specific etiology, emphasizing urgent diagnostic intervention. Future guidelines should prioritize specific pathogen diagnostics and investigate the role of DOACs for infectious CVT.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04721-y.Keywords: Sinus thrombosis, intracranial; Central nervous system viral diseases; Mycoses; Parasitic diseases; Anticoagulants; Systematic review

Introduction

Cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT) is a rare yet potentially severe neurological condition characterized by the partial or complete obstruction of the dural venous sinuses and/or cerebral cortical veins. This obstruction impairs cerebral venous drainage and may result in edema, intracranial hypertension, and hemorrhagic events [1]. CVT accounts for approximately 0.5% to 3% of all strokes, predominantly affecting individuals under 55 years of age, with a particular prevalence among women of reproductive age due to the influence of hormonal and obstetric factors [1].

Historically, the association between infections and CVT is well-documented, particularly in intracranial bacterial infections such as otitis, mastoiditis, and meningitis [2, 3]. However, in recent decades, there has been growing recognition that non-bacterial agents, such as viruses, fungi, and parasites, may also be involved in the pathophysiology of CVT [4]. These agents act through inflammatory or immune-mediated mechanisms, or by direct invasion of the central nervous system [5, 6]. The increasing diversity of pathogens, especially in immunocompromised patients, has challenged the understanding of their pathogenic mechanisms, as well as the diagnostic and therapeutic approach to the disease [7].

While the association between bacterial infections and CVT is well-established, and more recently, COVID-19-associated CVT have been documented [8–10], a comprehensive synthesis of CVT associated with other non-bacterial infections is lacking. Existing literature reviews on infectious causes of CVT often include bacterial without providing a detailed, pathogen-specific analysis of viral, fungal, and parasitic etiologies separately. Furthermore, there is no systematic review that exclusively focuses on these non-bacterial, non-COVID-19 pathogens to elucidate their distinct clinical profiles, imaging patterns, management strategies, and outcomes. This gap limits the ability of clinicians to recognize and tailor therapy for CVT caused by these specific agents. This systematic review aimed to summarize the evidence regarding the occurrence of CVT secondary to a wide spectrum of classical viral, fungal, and parasitic infections, excluding the distinct entity of COVID-19. The review focused on elucidating the implicated etiological agents, underlying medical conditions, predisposing factors, diagnostic methods, clinical manifestations, sites of CVT, treatment strategies, and patient outcomes.

Methods

Systematic review conducted according the Guidelines for the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [11], and registered in International prospective register of systematic reviews (PROSPERO, CRD42023445694).

Search strategy

The literature search was conducted in MEDLINE/PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science (WoS), Embase, and the Latin American and Caribbean Health Sciences Literature (LILACS)/BVS. Furthermore, a snowball search [12] was performed by screening the reference lists of all included studies to assess their eligibility for inclusion. Keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH Terms) related to “intracranial thrombosis”, “infections”, and their variations were combined using Boolean operators (AND, OR) as detailed in Table S1. The search was restricted to human studies, with no limitations on publication language or date, and was conducted up to and including July 31, 2025.

Study selection

Studies were selected based on the PECO framework to ensure alignment with the systematic review objectives. The eligibility criteria were defined as follows: Population (P): individuals of all age groups; Exposure (E): presence or clinical history of non-bacterial infectious diseases (viral, fungal, or parasitic); Comparison (C): not applicable for this descriptive synthesis; and Outcome (O): development or documented occurrence of cerebral venous thrombosis (CVT).

Exclusion criteria comprised review articles, opinion pieces, editorials, and case reports with insufficient, incomplete, or non-extractable primary data. Additionally, studies that presented only pooled patient analyses without individual-level data, those exclusively including patients diagnosed with COVID-19 or bacterial diseases, as well as studies whose full text could not be retrieved, were also excluded. Patients diagnosed with COVID-19 were excluded because of SARS-CoV-2-associated CVT has been extensively characterized in recent specialized literature [9, 10, 13, 14]. Given the disproportionately large volume of COVID-19 data, its inclusion would have skewed the analysis of rarer viral, fungal, and parasitic etiologies, which represent the focus of this systematic synthesis.

We imported all retrieved records into an EndNote X9 library [15] for deduplication. The resulting records were then exported to the Rayyan platform [16], where a second deduplication procedure was conducted.

Study selection was conducted through an initial screening of titles and abstracts, followed by a subsequent full-text assessment, performed independently by three reviewers (M.C.M., E.B.M., and G.A.C.). Any disagreements in the selection process were resolved through consultation with another reviewer (L.G).

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two reviewers (M.C.M. and E.B.M.), who entered the information into a structured electronic form created in the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) program, a tool designed for data management [17], with any discrepancies being resolved by a third reviewer (G.A.C.).

The extracted information included: (1) General information: study title, journal name, year, volume, issue, page numbers, language, abstract, author names, country, and their affiliations; (2) Study characteristics: design (case report or case series), publication type (full article, abstract/poster), country where the study was conducted; (3) Patient characteristics: age (in days, months, or years), sex at birth, race/ethnicity; (4) Documented underlying conditions and predisposing factors; (5) Diagnostic methods used to confirm the infectious disease, detailed imaging examinations; (6) CVT characteristics, including single or multiple thromboses and their specific locations; (7) Etiological agents (viruses, parasites, and fungi); (8) Clinical presentation of CVT, including symptom onset (acute, subacute, chronic), mental status (normal or altered), and the presence of focal neurological signs or seizures; (9) Detailed treatment regimens, including the use of anticoagulants, antivirals, antiparasitics, antibiotics, anticonvulsants, and any medical or surgical interventions or procedures; (10) Outcomes, including neurological disability assessed using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) [18]; 11) Follow-up imaging results specifically concerning the CVT (recanalization, unchanged, or worsened).

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tools for case reports and case series [19]. Two reviewers (M.C.M. and E.B.M.) independently assessed the quality of the included studies, and any discrepancies were resolved through consensus with a third reviewer (G.A.C.).

Analysis

Descriptive analyses were used to summarize the data. In categories regarding comorbidities, diagnostic workup, and anatomical distribution of thrombosis, categories were treated as non-mutually exclusive to reflect the clinical complexity of individual cases.

For the categorization of infection etiologies, the following criteria were adopted: (1) Fungal infections were classified into two distinct groups: fungi associated with mucormycosis (e.g., Rhizopus spp., Mucor spp.), recognized for their specific pathogenicity and clinical relevance in invasive syndromes, and fungi not associated with mucormycosis (e.g., Candida spp., Aspergillus spp.), which involve distinct pathogenic mechanisms, clinical presentations, and therapeutic approaches. This division was based on taxonomic criteria, documented etiological associations with mucormycosis in the literature, and diagnostic confirmation within the included studies. (2) Even in the presence of opportunistic infections, HIV was not classified solely as a predisposing factor, as it is also recognized as a primary etiological agent of CVT due to its intrinsic pathophysiological mechanisms, such as chronic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, which promote thrombus formation. (3) In cases of coinfection involving viral, fungal, or parasitic agents, both pathogens were listed as etiological, given the difficulty in clearly distinguishing between HIV-induced immune dysfunction, direct viral effects, and the thrombogenic potential of the secondary agent.

Results

The search strategy returned 14,127 records (flow diagram in Figure S1).After the exclusion of duplicates and application of inclusion criteria, 238 studies were deemed suitable for inclusion in this review. Forty additional studies were included after snowball search. Thus, 278 publications were included in the systematic review.

Among the included studies (Tables S2a-f), 16 (6%) were case series and 262 (94%) were case reports. With regard to publication type, scientific articles accounted for 247 cases (89%), while poster presentations constituted the remaining 31 (11%). Publications yielded information from 1958 to 2025, with 42% of the publications occurring from 2010 to 2019, followed by the subsequent period from 2020 to 2025, with 28% of the publications (Figure S2). The list of articles included in this review is presented in the Supplement.

The included studies showed a significant geographic concentration, with 72% of the evidence originating from Asia and North America (Fig. 1). Detailed distribution by continent and country is provided in Table S3.

Of the studies analyzed, publications were identified in ten distinct languages, with the majority (n = 247, 89%) being published in English (Table S4).

Patient characteristics

The studies included 322 patients. Among these, 54% (n = 175) were male, with ages ranging from 2 to 87 years (median = 39 years; IQR: 25–55), while 147 (46%) were female, with ages ranging from 35 days to 86 years and a median age of 42 years (IQR: 23–57).The majority of cases (n = 264/322, 82%) occurred in individuals above 20 years of age. CVT was frequent among young adults aged 30 to 64 years, constituting 56% of the cases (n = 179/322), but was also present in pediatric and elderly populations (Table S5).

Information about race/ethnicity was reported for only 41 out of 322 individuals (13%), and was distributed as follows: 39% (n = 16/41) Asian, 34% (n = 14/41) White, 15% (n = 6/41) Black/African American, and 12% (n = 5/41) Hispanic.

Underlying conditions, predisposing factors, clinical, methods of diagnosis, and imaging

The underlying conditions and predisposing factors were categorized into 11 distinct groups. The most prevalent category was infections, which may be associated with the causative agent of CVT or act as predisposing factor for thrombosis, such as sinusitis, meningitis and orbital cellulitis (n = 149/322; 46%), followed by the category of cardiovascular and metabolic conditions (n = 131/322; 41%), with diabetes mellitus the most common underlying medical condition (n = 117/322, 36%) (Table 1 and detailed in Table S6).

Among the included cases, the majority (n = 265/322, 82%) presented a subacute onset of symptoms. An altered mental status was observed in 57% of patients (n = 184/322) (Table 1). Furthermore, seizures or focal neurological manifestations were described in 98% of cases (n = 314/322) (Table 1).

The vast majority of cases (305/322, 95%) were assessed using neuroimaging via computed tomography (CT) and/or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Microbiological culture testing was performed in 46% (149/322), whereas a biopsy procedure was conducted in 39% (n = 127/322) (Table 1).

Neuroimaging was nearly universal (95%), with MRI and CT being the primary modalities for diagnosis and vascular assessment (Table 1). Notably, parenchymal brain lesions were absent in over half of the cohort (57%), while ischemic and hemorrhagic events were less frequent.

Type of infection and etiologies

A comprehensive etiological analysis of the 322 included cases of CVT revealed 352 causative agents. Viral infections (most frequent: varicella zoster and HIV) and fungal mucormycosis were the most prevalent, identified in 114/322 (35%) and 112/322 cases (35%), respectively. Other non-mucormycosis fungal infections were documented in 63/322 cases (20%); while purely parasitic infections were less common (15/322 cases, 5%). Among the mixed infections, viral and non-mucormycosis fungal co-infections were observed in 15/322 cases (5%), whereas associations between viral and parasitic agents, as well as viral and mucormycosis agents, were rare, each occurring in a single instance (0.3%). A single case was attributable to an oomycotic infection (0.3%). Table 2 summarizes infection types and detailed etiologies.

Treatment, outcome, and imaging follow-up

The most frequently reported therapeutic approaches were anticoagulation, which was employed in 173 patients (54%), followed by the administration of antifungal agents (n = 160; 50%) and antibiotic therapy (n = 132; 41%). Interventional or surgical procedures were described in 129 cases (40%). Other pharmacological treatments included antiviral, anticonvulsants corticosteroids (15%) and antiparasitics in 69 (21%), 51(16%), 47(15%) and 14 patients (4%), respectively.

Among the cases who reported use of anticoagulants, unfractionated heparin (n = 96, 56%), vitamin K antagonists (n = 76, 44%), and low molecular weight heparin (n = 53, 31%) were the most cited, while only 9 patients (5%) were prescribed DOACs (detailed therapeutic intervention in Table S8).

Clinical outcomes assessed by the modified Rankin Scale demonstrated that 95 patients (30%) achieved a full recovery, while 46 (14%) remained with no significant disability. Outcomes involving slight disability were recorded in 31 patients (10%, moderate disability in 7 (2%), moderately severe disability in 3 (1.0%), and severe disability in 5 (2%). Sixty-eight patients (21%) died, and the outcome was not documented in 67 (21%).

Regarding imaging follow-up, 79 cases (25%) did not undergo serial imaging. Recanalization was identified in 56 patients (17%), whereas 4 (1%) exhibited no interval change on follow-up imaging studies performed after the diagnosis of CVT. Worsening thrombosis was reported in only one patient. Unknown or unreported data were recorded in 182 cases (57%).

Mucormycosis showed a predilection for the cavernous sinus (65% of cases involved this sinus). Conversely, viral infections demonstrated very low cavernous sinus involvement (4%) but a high frequency of thrombosis in the transverse sinus (76%), sigmoid sinus (78%), sagittal sinus (73%), and straight sinus (91%).Non-mucormycosis fungal infections also showed an association with the cavernous sinus (31%).

The anatomical finding reinforces the pattern of single vs. multiple CVT. Mucormycosis, affecting primarily a single sinus (the cavernous), was associated with a single CVT (54%). Viral infections, affecting multiple sinuses, were associated with multiple CVT (59%).Antibiotic use was common in patients with fungal infections (45% in mucormycosis, 31% in non-mucormycosis) but less frequent in viral infections (22%).

Viral infections were associated with the highest frequency of favorable outcome (mRS 0–2: 54.2%), alongside a higher rate of anticoagulant administration (63.9%). Conversely, mucormycosis was characterized by a high proportion of deaths (68.3%) and a lower frequency of anticoagulation (16.9%).

Temporal trends

Temporal analysis spanning 1958–2025 indicated a marked evolution in diagnostic and management standards. Early cases were often defined by clinical suspicion or post-mortem findings (autopsy: 6.8%), while the era post-2010 was characterized by nearly universal neuroimaging (95%). Documentation of anticoagulant use also shifted temporally; only 22% of all patients receiving anticoagulants (n = 38/173) were reported prior to 2011, while DOACs were used in 5% of cases, primarily within the most recent period. Furthermore, while the overall mortality rate was 21.1% (n = 68/322), longitudinal data demonstrated a sharp decline. Despite an equal absolute number of deaths in both timeframes (n = 34 up to 2010; n = 34 from 2011 to 2025), the mortality rate fell from 32.7% in the earlier era to 15.6% in the modern era, as 67.7% of the total cohort (n = 218) was published after 2010.

Quality assessment

Using the JBI Critical Appraisal Tool for Case Reports (Table S10), the overall methodological quality of the included case reports was high, with the majority of items scoring “yes” in over 90% of reports, while the quality of case series studies (Table S11) was variable.

Discussion

This systematic review of 278 studies comprising 322 patients with CVT secondary to non-bacterial infections reveals a distinct epidemiological and clinical profile. Viral infections (35%) and fungal mucormycosis (35%) were the predominant etiologies of CVT. A diagnostic gap was identified, with specific pathogen confirmation performed in fewer than half of the cases despite near-universal neuroimaging.

Reported management strategies and outcomes differed drastically by etiology: viral infections were most frequently associated with favorable outcomes and with the highest reported use of anticoagulant. In contrast, mucormycosis was linked to the worst prognosis, accounting for most deaths, and anticoagulant use was infrequently reported in these cases. This stark contrast likely reflects profound differences in disease severity, anatomical involvement, and bleeding risk, which strongly confound any assessment of treatment efficacy from this observational data. Recanalization data were largely unavailable for most patients.

A striking finding of this review is the male predominance (54%) and the concentration of cases in the 30–64 age group (56%), in contrast to the well-established female of childbearing age predominance in CVT [1, 20–23]. This shift can be explained by the replacement of hormonal risk factors with metabolic and infectious ones. Diabetes mellitus (36.3%) was the leading comorbidity, acting as a primary catalyst for mucormycosis (35% of cases), an angioinvasive infection that frequently affects middle-aged and older adults [24]. Similarly, the significant role of HIV as an independent prothrombotic agent (12.4% of cases) contributes to this male-skewed demographic [25], particularly in regions where the epidemic follows this pattern. Geographic patterns also play a role; with 45% of studies originating from Asia, our data may reflect regional variations in the prevalence of diabetes and specific endemic pathogens. Finally, the possibility of reporting bias must be considered. In the clinical literature, CVT in a young woman is often perceived as ‘classic,’ whereas cases occurring in males or older adults associated with rare infections are more likely to be perceived as ‘novel’ and thus reported as case studies.

The absence of race/ethnicity data in 87% of cases limits the ability to analyze potential disparities and underscores methodological shortcomings in the available literature [26].

The main etiologies described were viral (35%) and fungal (35%) infections, with varicella zoster virus, HIV, and mucormycosis being the most frequent.

Mucormycosis, in particular, demonstrated a fulminant course with high mortality due to its angioinvasive nature, leading to endothelial damage, necrosis, and thrombotic occlusion of proximate vessels. This explains its predilection for the cavernous sinus (often extending from rhino-orbital disease) [24] and its fulminant course with high mortality, particularly in immunocompromised patients [24, 27]. In contrast, viral pathogens (e.g., VZV, HIV) are thought to induce CVT through indirect mechanisms: vasculitis, immune-mediated endothelial activation, or systemic hypercoagulability [28, 29]. This diffuse vascular insult may explain the higher frequency of multiple sinus involvement (e.g., transverse, sigmoid) and the more subacute presentation. Parasitic infections may cause CVT through vessel occlusion by larvae or cysts, or via a severe systemic inflammatory response.

Clinical presentation was heterogeneous: altered mental status occurred in 57% of cases, while seizures and focal neurological deficits were frequently reported, consistent with classical descriptions of CVT [1, 30]. A subacute onset predominated, particularly in viral and parasitic infections, often delaying recognition and initiation of treatment [31].

The cavernous and transverse sinuses were the most commonly affected venous structures, frequently with multiple sinus involvement. This pattern is clinically relevant, as it correlates with more severe disease, greater risk of complications, and poorer outcomes [7]. Although not diagnostic in isolation, the distribution of venous thrombosis may provide useful clues when integrated with clinical and microbiological data [6, 32].

Magnetic resonance imaging and computed tomography were the most widely employed diagnostic modalities, whereas laboratory confirmation through PCR, culture, or biopsy were infrequently pursued. This gap impedes accurate identification of causative pathogens, driving reliance on empirical therapies and negatively influencing prognosis [1, 23, 33]. Optimal care requires integration of neuroimaging with systematic microbiological testing to guide targeted treatment.

Anticoagulation with heparin was the most frequent therapeutic approach, including in patients with small venous hemorrhages, in line with international guidelines supporting its safety and efficacy [1, 32, 34]. Nearly half of the reported cases required surgical intervention, reflecting the severity of infection-associated CVT and the frequent need for multidisciplinary management. Complete recovery was achieved in only one-third of patients, while mortality reached 21%, markedly higher than the 5–10% typically reported in non-infectious CVT [23, 35].

Empirical use of antifungals and antibiotics was common, reflecting clinical severity and diagnostic uncertainty. Although justified in selected high-risk scenarios, this practice increases risks of drug toxicity and antimicrobial resistance [36–38]. The Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) recommends restricting empirical antifungal therapy to high-risk patients, with prompt reassessment following microbiological confirmation [39]. The observed divergence between practice and guideline recommendations underscores the need for greater diagnostic precision.

Recent studies, including RE-SPECT CVT, SECRET, and ACTION-CVT, demonstrated comparable efficacy and safety of DOACs relative to vitamin K antagonists in non-infectious CVT [40–42]. A meta-analysis further supported similar effectiveness with superior recanalization rates [43]. However, clinical trials specifically enrolling patients with active infection are lacking, and thus DOACs cannot yet be recommended as standard therapy in this context. Low-molecular-weight heparin remains the first-line option, while DOACs may be considered on a case-by-case basis, balancing infection type, bleeding risk, and comorbidities.

Application of the JBI checklist revealed that only one-third of case reports documented adverse events. Nevertheless, 86% reported post-intervention outcomes, and nearly all adequately described diagnostic procedures. Among case series, all but one applied validated diagnostic criteria and 81% used appropriate statistical methods, though only 69% ensured consecutive inclusion, introducing risk of selection bias. These findings highlight the need for more rigorous reporting standards, greater adherence to CARE guidelines, and improved methodological consistency across studies [44]. Geographic concentration of publications in Asia and North America undermines global representativeness, particularly for underreported regions such as South America and Africa [45, 46].

The substantial lack of longitudinal follow-up, which was unknown or unreported for 81% of the cohort, represents a critical barrier to evaluating long-term outcomes. Consequently, recanalization, a key biological marker of CVT resolution [47], was documented in only 17% of cases. In non-infectious CVT, studies like the ISCVT [23] have shown that most patients achieve some degree of recanalization, which often correlates with clinical stabilization. The absence of these data in our review prevents a robust correlation between specific treatments (e.g., anticoagulation vs. surgical debridement) and vascular recovery. It remains uncertain whether the infection-induced thrombus exhibits a different biological behaviour or ‘resistance’ to standard heparin therapy compared to traditional CVT etiologies.

Furthermore, the reliance on case reports (94%) introduces significant survival and publication bias. Clinical literature is prone to reporting extremes, either ‘miraculous’ outcomes or uniquely catastrophic cases, while routine, uncomplicated cases often go unpublished. This bias directly impacts our 21% mortality estimate. While this rate is markedly higher than the 5–10% reported in general CVT populations, it likely reflects a selection of the most severe infectious presentations. Therefore, our results may overestimate the lethality of non-bacterial infections in CVT due to the exclusion of milder, unreported cases. These limitations underscore the need for prospective, multicenter registries to establish a more accurate epidemiological and therapeutic landscape for infection-associated CVT.

The wide temporal span of this review (1958–2025) reveals significant shifts in the clinical landscape of infection-associated CVT. Early cases were frequently characterized by clinical suspicion or post-mortem findings, with autopsy documented in 6.8% of the total cohort. Conversely, the post-2010 era, which accounts for 67.7% of the total patients (n = 218) is marked by nearly universal neuroimaging (95%) [34, 48].

Management practices also transitioned over time; our analysis shows that only 22% of all recorded anticoagulant use (n = 38/173) occurred prior to 2011, while the emergence of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) is a contemporary phenomenon representing 5% of cases. These temporal shifts have a profound impact on survival estimates; while the overall mortality rate was 21.1% (n = 68/322), the fatality rate declined from 32.7% in the pre-2011 era to 15.6% in the modern era. Notably, while the absolute number of deaths was identical between the two timeframes (n = 34 up to 2010; n = 34 from 2011 to 2025), the doubling of the patient denominator in recent years highlights that historical cases indeed inflate cumulative mortality and lower overall anticoagulation documentation.

The findings of this review confirm international recommendations for the use of heparin, preferably low-molecular-weight heparin, as first-line therapy even in cases with venous hemorrhage [32, 34]. However, relevant divergences are observed in antifungal management, since guidelines advocate targeted therapy, whereas published reports frequently describe empirical use without microbiological confirmation, reflecting the tension between clinical urgency and evidence-based practice [36–39]. Although guidelines recommend targeted treatment, empirical antifungal therapy was common, highlighting the conflict between clinical urgency and antimicrobial stewardship. Regarding DOACs, recent guidelines such as AHA/ASA 2024 [1], CSBPR 2024 [49], and the German S2k 2023/24 [34] recognize them as maintenance options in non-infectious CVT, but no evidence yet supports their use in infection-associated CVT. With respect to follow-up imaging, the CSBPR explicitly recommend repeat venous imaging at 3–6 months; [49] however, our review revealed incomplete follow-up and a low rate of documented recanalization. The guidelines and the findings of this review emphasize the frequent need for surgical and multidisciplinary approaches, which are essential for the proper management of these complex cases.

Furthermore, the strong confounding effect of anticoagulation indication and disease severity must be emphasized. The decisions to administer anticoagulation, perform surgery, or use specific antimicrobials were based on clinical judgment in complex, individual cases. Therefore, the observed associations between specific treatments and outcomes (e.g., anticoagulation use with better outcomes in viral infections) cannot be interpreted as evidence of efficacy but rather as descriptions of current practice within distinct clinical scenarios.

Our findings highlight the necessity of an etiology-driven approach. Diagnostically, a high index of suspicion for mucormycosis should be triggered by CVT involving the cavernous sinus, especially in patients with diabetes, ketoacidosis, or immunosuppression. This scenario mandates urgent imaging of the sinuses and orbits, followed by tissue biopsy for culture and PCR. Conversely, viral etiologies should be considered in patients with multiple sinus thromboses and appropriate risk factors (e.g., HIV, herpes zoster rash), necessitating specific serology or CSF PCR.

Therapeutically, management must address both the infection and the thrombosis. While anticoagulation with heparin is a cornerstone of CVT treatment, its application in active infection requires a careful risk-benefit assessment, particularly in angioinvasive fungal disease where the bleeding risk may be elevated. Antifungal therapy for mucormycosis requires aggressive, early surgical debridement combined with high-dose liposomal amphotericin B. The role of DOACs in this population remains uncertain due to a lack of data and potential drug interactions.

This review exposes critical deficiencies in the evidence base, which must direct future research. Key priorities include establishing prospective, multicenter registries to overcome selection bias by consecutively enrolling patients with infection-associated CVT under standardized protocols. Such registries are essential to define true incidence, identify baseline predictors and mortality rates, and employ uniform follow-up to evaluate recanalization and long-term outcomes, thereby achieving global representativeness with crucial data from underreported regions such as South America and Africa. Furthermore, diagnostic pathway studies are needed to evaluate integrated algorithms that combine early advanced neuroimaging (e.g., high-resolution MRI) with systematic microbiological testing, including PCR, serology, and biopsy. Regarding therapeutic comparative effectiveness, multicenter trials are urgently needed to assess treatment strategies and determine the optimal duration of therapy, particularly the role of DOACs in infection-associated CVT. Finally, pathogen-specific studies to investigate biomarkers of endothelial injury, inflammation, and pathogen burden could significantly aid in diagnosis, risk stratification, and monitoring treatment response.

Conclusions

Non-bacterial infections represent important but complex risk factors for CVT, influencing disease presentation, management, and outcomes. The high mortality and limited rates of complete recovery underscore the need for improved diagnostic integration, standardized therapeutic protocols, and high-quality prospective studies. Strengthening the evidence base is essential to refine clinical guidelines and optimize patient care in this challenging condition.
