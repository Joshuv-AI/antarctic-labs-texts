GAS5 as a therapeutic target in breast cancer through apoptosis induction
Authors: Alisha Badal, Zelinda Engelbrecht, Marianne J. Cronjé
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The ongoing discovery of long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) involvement in carcinogenesis prevention and development has made significant strides in breast cancer therapeutics. lncRNA growth arrest-specific 5 (GAS5) is downregulated in breast cancer, and its downregulation is linked to advanced clinical staging and grading as well as poor survival outcomes. The apoptosis modality is the most sought-after cell death modality in cancer therapeutics and a major challenge to the current treatment paradigm is drug resistance. This review examines GAS5 as a therapeutic target and an inducer of apoptosis through various mechanisms, including the role of GAS5-derived small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs) in the prevention of carcinogenesis, GAS5 as a microRNA (miRNA) sponge, thereby upregulating several tumor suppressors that promote apoptosis. Moreover, GAS5 is a riborepressor of the glucocorticoid receptor, hindering inhibitors of apoptosis. Additionally, GAS5 sensitizes breast cancer to radiotherapy, chemotherapy and endocrine therapy and alleviates drug resistance either directly through overexpression or indirectly through the administration of drugs that increase its expression, consequently promoting apoptosis. GAS5 is involved in various crucial signaling pathways in breast cancer, such as the PI3K/AKT/mTOR, Wnt/β-catenin, and NF-κB pathways and is itself regulated by many pathways. Recognized as a potent tumor suppressor, GAS5 presents a compelling opportunity for more profound research as a potential game-changing therapeutic target in breast cancer.

Introduction

Breast cancer takes the forefront as the most prevalent malignancy and the leading cause of female mortality globally [1]. By 2040, cases are projected to surge by 40% and mortality by 50% compared to 2020 [2, 3]. Despite advancements in therapy, drug resistance and drug-induced side effects pose significant challenges to the current treatment paradigm [4, 5]. Additionally, treatment efficacy is limited by the vast heterogeneity of breast neoplasms and their subtypes [4, 6]. Identifying crucial therapeutic targets and biomarkers is paramount for combating the growing cancer burden.

Protein-coding genes in carcinogenesis are extensively studied to identify therapeutic targets; however, protein-coding genes account for less than 2% of the human genome. The remaining 98% of genomic DNA is transcribed into non-coding RNA (ncRNA) genes that were previously cast aside as ‘junk DNA’ due to their inability to code for proteins [7–10]. Advancements in RNA sequencing unveiled ncRNAs instrumental in various biological processes, including differentiation, regulation of gene expression, development, and metabolism. Subsequently, resulting in an explosion of ncRNA research and a newfound appreciation of ncRNA genes [11]. ncRNAs are categorized based on nucleotide length into lncRNAs (> 200 nt) and short RNAs (sRNAs) (< 200 nt). The latter can be further classified into several subtypes, including miRNAs, small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs), and snoRNAs, among others [12].

The interplay of lncRNAs in oncology is gaining momentum as emerging research underscores their indispensable role as important regulators, biomarkers, and targets for various genetic or epigenetic diseases [13]. lncRNAs can regulate several pathways within a cell under pathological and non-pathological conditions. Expression levels of lncRNAs are often dysregulated in carcinogenesis, and specific lncRNAs serve as an oncogene or tumor suppressor [14]. GAS5 represents a promising avenue for therapeutic intervention in cancer, attributable to its various tumor suppressor functions. Furthermore, GAS5 is frequently downregulated across a range of malignancies [15, 16]. This review examines the diverse mechanisms GAS5 may encapsulate to induce apoptosis in breast cancer. Emphasis will be placed on GAS5 snoRNAs in carcinogenesis prevention, GAS5 as a miRNA sponge and GAS5 as a riborepressor. Thereafter, the downregulation of GAS5 in breast cancer tissue and its implication on drug resistance will be further discussed.

GAS5

Six ‘growth arrest-specific’ genes were identified in a study exploring NIH 3T3 mouse fibroblast cells undergoing the G0 phase (quiescent cell state). These genes, collectively referred to as GAS genes, include GAS5. The researchers utilized a subtraction library method to clone cDNA from serum-starved NIH 3T3 cells to identify the GAS genes, as documented in Schneider et al. [17]. GAS5 was further structurally characterized and isolated from the genomic DNA of NIH 3T3 cells and found to accumulate in growth-arrested cells [18]. Moreover, GAS5 was recognized as a potential tumor suppressor gene [19]. GAS5 is unique among its fellow GAS genes as it only codes for lncRNA instead of protein-coding mRNAs [20]. The GAS5 gene encodes ten box C/D snoRNAs in its introns (three of which U44, U74, and U78 may generate miRNAs) [21]. Additionally, GAS5 facilitates miRNA binding sites in its exons, contains a riborepressor in the 3’ exon, and contains small open reading frames that display conservation among primates [20]. GAS5 comprises ~ 630 nucleotides and 12 exons, is a 5'-terminal oligopyrimidine tract (5’-TOP) RNA family member and is positioned on chromosome 1q25. GAS5 has alternative transcriptional start sites and many splice forms. Transcription of the exons may produce a mature GAS5a or GAS5b lncRNA dependent on alternate splicing [16, 22].

GAS5 and apoptosis induction in breast cancer

Apoptosis and cancer therapy

Restoration of the apoptotic cell death modality in neoplasms is the most sought-after mechanism provoked by approved anti-cancer drugs [23, 24]. Apoptosis harnesses the inherent ability to inhibit carcinogenesis from its initiation stage to the final stage of oncogenic development [25]. Additionally, apoptosis prevents pro-inflammatory death responses that may contribute to undesirable drug side effects and treatment prognosis [26–28]. The efficacy of many cytotoxic drugs is hindered by their inability to execute apoptosis successfully to the end, leading to drug-resistant neoplasms [24, 29].

Role of GAS5 snoRNAs in carcinogenesis prevention and apoptosis

The snoRNAs harbored in the introns of the GAS5 gene have been linked to carcinogenesis prevention. In colorectal cancer cell lines, GAS5-derived snoRNA expression is induced in response to DNA damage, and this process is dependent on p53 and independent of the RNA processing enzyme, DICER. Additionally, GAS5-derived snoRNA expression levels demonstrate a close association with p53 expression levels [30, 31]. Reduced GAS5 expression is associated with cell-cycle arrest and correlates with increased p53 and other DNA damage response proteins, such as Breast Cancer Gene 1 (BRCA1) and Growth Arrest and DNA Damage-inducible, alpha (GADD45A) [31, 32]. Zhou and Chen [31] hypothesized that GAS5 behaves as a signaling molecule in response to DNA damage, promoting GAS5-derived snoRNA expression and thereby activating p53 signaling. As a result, this could form a hypothetical feedback loop that increases p53 expression, promotes G1 cell cycle arrest, and prevents DNA-damaged cells from progressing through mitosis. However, the precise molecular mechanisms involved are not yet fully understood [31].

In contrast, Krell et al. [30] proposed a hypothesis that p53 may transcriptionally activate GAS5-derived snoRNAs to ‘streamline’ ribosomal RNA maturation and modification, consequently improving translational efficiency for proteins required to respond to DNA damage. This hypothesis is based on the understanding that snoRNAs play a crucial role in ribosomal function. Additionally, p53 acts as a mediator in ribosome biogenesis. During nucleolar stress, p53 serves as a signaling link between ribosome biogenesis and the cell cycle. Alternatively, Krell et al. [30] hypothesized that these snoRNAs may act outside the ribosome as small RNA fragments derived from snoRNAs, influencing post-transcriptional gene regulation without DICER [30]. To verify these hypotheses, experiments such as snoRNA overexpression, subcellular localization mapping, and transcriptome profiling are necessary to clarify the GAS5-derived snoRNA and the p53-dependent DNA damage response.

MCF-7 (Luminal A) breast cancer cells underwent apoptosis after transfection with GAS5-expressed sequence tags, independent of other apoptotic stimuli. GAS5 transcripts induced apoptosis in a caspase-dependent or independent manner, possibly attributed to the combination of exon/intron compositions, mainly the snoRNA composition [33]. PIWI-interacting RNAs (piRNAs) are single-stranded, non-coding RNAs of 26–31 nt length. He et al. [34] studied small RNAs derived from the GAS5 gene and identified that certain snoRNAs were representative of piRNAs. The study indicated that pi-sno75 (derived from snoRNA U75) expression was reduced in breast cancer with respect to healthy tissue. Induction of pi-sno75 overexpression in breast cancer led to the upregulation of the tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (TRAIL) via histone modification of the TRAIL gene’s promoter. TRAIL activates the extrinsic apoptosis pathway by binding to death receptor-4 (DR4) and death receptor-5 (DR5). GAS5-derived piRNAs, such as pi-sno75, enhance TRAIL expression through histone modification, thereby promoting this apoptotic signaling [34]. The snoRNA U44 (SNORD44) derived from the GAS5 gene is a member of the large C/D box family involved in directing 2′-O-ribose methylation in ribosome biogenesis. Low expression of snoRNA U44 in breast cancer and Head and Neck Squamous Cell carcinoma is linked to poorer prognosis and survival outcomes [35, 36].

GAS5 miRNA interaction and its effect on apoptosis and drug sensitivity

miRNAs are key gene regulators and affect all aspects of cancer progression, operating as either tumor suppressor miRNAs or disease-associated oncogenic miRNAs (oncomiRs) [37]. miRNAs base pairs with target mRNAs and negatively regulate their expression by inhibiting transcript translation [38]. miRNAs play a significant role in regulating the expression of diverse protein-coding genes and are closely associated with the GAS5 gene [39]. In breast cancer, GAS5 operates as a competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) by competing (with protein-coding mRNAs) and sequestering or “sponging” miRNAs from their mRNA targets, thus preventing the degradation of the target mRNA transcript at the post-transcriptional and translational levels [40].

A study conducted by Li et al. [41] demonstrated that GAS5 expression is significantly downregulated in the most aggressive type of breast cancer, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), compared to non-malignant tissue. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses revealed that patients with lower GAS5 expression have shorter survival times than those with higher GAS5 expression. GAS5 expression was lower in TNBC cell lines MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-468 compared to breast cancer cell lines MCF-7, T47D, BT474, and the non-malignant breast cell line MCF-10A. The authors uncovered that GAS5 functions as a ceRNA for the oncomiR miR-196a-5p, consequently increasing the expression of Forkhead Box Protein O1 (FOXO1—involved in cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis) and preventing phosphorylation of the oncogenic Phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K/AKT) pathway. As a result, ectopic expression of GAS5 induces apoptosis and inhibits the proliferation of the two TNBC cell lines [41]. GAS5 as a riborepressor of the glucocorticoid receptor

Glucocorticoid receptors (GRs) belong to the nuclear receptor family and are transcription regulators that respond to hormone levels [46]. Most isoforms of GAS5 contain a DNA binding domain that serves as a riborepressor of the glucocorticoid receptor (GR). GAS5 suppresses GR transcriptional activity by binding to the DNA binding domain of the GR via its double-stranded glucocorticoid response element (GRE) mimic sequence. Thus, preventing the transcription of glucocorticoid-responsive genes, one of which is the anti-apoptotic cellular inhibitor of apoptosis protein 2 (cIAP2) [47].

cIAP2 interacts with tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 2 (TRAF2) and facilitates the recruitment of cIAP2 to complexes associated with TNF receptors 1 and 2. Subsequently inhibiting caspase-8 and the extrinsic apoptosis pathway [48]. HeLa cells transfected with GAS5-expressing plasmids exhibited increased susceptibility to apoptosis following dexamethasone treatment (a GR agonist). This effect was attributed to GAS5's riborepressor capabilities, which facilitated the inhibition of cIAP2 via the GR. In contrast, when GAS5 was silenced using siRNA, the expression of cIAP2 was restored, and cells exhibited an increase in survival despite dexamethasone treatment [47]. Although glucocorticoids are utilized as a treatment for hematopoietic malignancies, elevated expression of the GR is correlated with a weak prognosis and heightened cancer progression in cases of early-stage TNBC [49]. cIAP2 is overexpressed in TNBC and is renowned for degrading apoptotic caspases via attaching caspases to ubiquitin molecules. Moreover, cIAP2 is implicated in epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), a key process in cancer progression [50]. cIAP2 plays a significant role in activating the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) transcription factor. Furthermore, in breast cancer, NF-κB also activates cIAP2. NF-κB activation fuels the progression of highly invasive and aggressive inflammatory breast cancer and is also constitutively activated in TNBC [51, 52]. As a result, GAS5 cIAP2 inhibition could indirectly dampen the activity of the NF-κB pathway, reduce EMT and induce apoptosis through its riborepressor capabilities.

GAS5 and the NF-κB pathway

NF-κB is a key player in tumor progression by directing chronic inflammation, promoting angiogenesis, and upregulating anti-apoptotic genes [53]. The constitutive NF-κB activation in breast cancer promotes high metastatic potential and resistance to chemotherapy, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, and targeted therapy [54]. Proinflammatory signals such as Tumor Necrosis Factor (TNF) activate NF-κB, which consists of a p50 and p65 heterodimer. In response to an inflammatory signal, the IkappaB kinase (IKK) complex phosphorylates IkappaB (IκB), marking it for degradation. Consequently, NF-κB, free from IκB inhibition, translocates to the nucleus to regulate target gene expression [55].

A study by Curci et al. [56], in which GAS5 was overexpressed in HeLa cervical cancer cells, confirmed via RNA immunoprecipitation that GAS5 directly interacts with the NF-κB subunit p65 and results in increased DNA binding. Additionally, GAS5 downregulated NF-κB inflammatory target genes, such as TNF-α, and Nuclear Receptor Subfamily 3 Group C Member 1 (NR3C1). Therefore, despite increased DNA binding between GAS5 and NF-κB, the inflammatory target genes were not transcribed, highlighting that DNA binding alone was insufficient to promote transcription. Furthermore, GAS5 overexpression together with glucocorticoid treatment in HeLa cells revealed a synergistic effect based on reduced NF-κB DNA binding, attenuated IκB and phosphorylated p65 levels, ultimately dampening NF-κB signaling [56]. These findings indicate that GAS5 is a promising target for inflammatory cancers that can inhibit inflammatory gene expression, even in the presence of NF-κB bound to DNA. Moreover, combining GAS5 with glucocorticoids could lead to enhanced treatment options. According to Li et al. [57, 58], GAS5 overexpression in colorectal cancer inhibited inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-10 (IL-10) and the angiogenesis inducer vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF-A) via NF-κB and Erk1/2 pathways. GAS5 overexpression significantly reduced p-NF-κB p65 levels. This reinforces GAS5 as a modulator of inflammation and angiogenesis in a tumor microenvironment [57, 58].

GAS5-mediated apoptosis via intracellular Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) and redox balance

ROS are produced by the mitochondria and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidases and are neutralized by antioxidant enzymes to prevent toxicity in non-malignant cells. Malignant cells, however, harbour high basal ROS levels due to enhanced metabolic demand, and compensate via upregulating antioxidant defences, heightening their sensitivity to additional oxidative stress. This altered redox balance can trigger cell death pathways such as apoptosis or autophagy [59, 60]. In malignant melanoma A375 cells, siRNA-mediated knockdown of GAS5 increased cellular ROS superoxide anion (O2-) levels. The elevated ROS activity was mediated by NADPH oxidase 4 and Glucose-6-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (G6PD), which subsequently inhibited apoptosis via Bcl-2 upregulation [61]. In another study, the overexpression of GAS5 in melanoma enhanced the tumour suppressor cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor (CDKN1C), suppressed Enhancer of Zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) (histone methyltransferase), and decreased histone H3 lysine 27 modifications (H3K27) trimethylation (repressive histone mark). The reduction of EZH2-mediated trimethylation of H3K27 at the CDKN1C promoter lifted transcriptional repression of CDKN1C, leading to its upregulation. This sequence of events subsequently promoted oxidative stress and apoptosis [62]. The above studies demonstrate the diverse mechanisms by which GAS5 regulates both ROS and epigenetic pathways to trigger apoptosis.

GAS5 downregulation in breast cancer and its implication on drug resistance

GAS5 regulation

The nonsense-mediated decay (NMD) pathway surveillances mRNA to degrade any premature stop codons that could lead to truncated proteins, which promote disease [63]. The NMD pathway regulates GAS5 because the gene's short open reading frame is terminated with a stop codon at exon 3 [64]. The mammalian target of the rapamycin (mTOR) pathway regulates mRNAs containing 5'TOP sequences [65]. Since GAS5 is a 5'TOP family member, the translation initiation of the GAS5 gene depends on mTOR signaling [64]. Therefore, in actively growing cells with increased mTOR activity, the translation of the GAS5 open reading frame is stimulated but ultimately leads to GAS5 inhibition due to the NMD pathway degrading the transcript. Inhibition of the mTOR pathway prevents GAS5 transcript translation and degradation by the NMD pathway, consequently increasing GAS5 levels [19]. In various malignancies, including breast cancer, rapamycin's inhibition of the oncogenic mTOR pathway has been associated with an increase in GAS5 expression. As a result, a reciprocal relationship exists between GAS5 and mTOR [40, 51]. Other oncogenic pathways capable of regulating GAS5 at the post-transcriptional level include Neurogenic locus notch homolog protein 1 (Notch-1). According to Pei and Wang [66], silencing Notch-1 by siRNA increased GAS5 expression in T47D breast cancer cells and subsequently inhibited cell proliferation; the precise mechanism is not fully understood. Aberrant methylation of GAS5 in cervical cancer and hypermethylation of the GAS5 promoter in gastric cancer led to decreased GAS5 expression [67, 68].

GAS5 downregulation influences breast cancer progression

GAS5 expression levels are significantly attenuated in breast cancer compared to normal breast epithelium tissue [33]. GAS5 downregulation has been observed in major breast cancer subtypes, including Luminal A, HER2-positive, invasive ductal carcinoma, and TNBC [40]. However, the downregulation of GAS5 in TNBC is more significant compared to the luminal A subtype [69]. The downregulation of GAS5 in breast cancer is linked to advanced TNM staging and grading, poor disease-free survival [70], aggressive phenotypes and lymph node metastasis [41, 71]. Thus, GAS5 is widely acknowledged as a critical tumor suppressor in breast cancer. A significant reduction in its expression accelerates the progression of the disease and facilitates apoptosis evasion [71, 72].

GAS5: A key ally in combating breast cancer drug resistance

GAS5 not only affects cancer progression but also alters the sensitivity of breast cancer to well-known anti-cancer drugs, either directly or indirectly, through various mechanisms, often leading to the induction of apoptosis (Table 1). Radiation therapy is a mainstay for breast cancer treatment; however, radioresistance remains a therapeutic challenge [76]. Chemoresistance remains another hurdle in cancer treatment. Approximately 90% of chemotherapy failures in invasive cancers are the result of drug resistance [77, 78]. GAS5 induces radiotherapy sensitivity [73] and restores sensitivity to some of the most potent chemotherapeutic and hormonal therapy drugs currently used [79, 80]. More than 60% of breast cancers are estrogen receptor-positive, and Tamoxifen is commonly used as an antagonist of ERα66; however, drug resistance limits its efficacy [81]. According to Jiang et al. The overexpression of p-glycoprotein (P-gp/ABCB1) in breast cancer plays an essential role in acquired drug resistance by coding for the multidrug resistance 1 (MDR1) gene [45]. According to Chen et al. [45], GAS5 expression was reduced when ABCB1 levels were increased in ADR resistant MCF-7 breast cancer cells. Overexpression of GAS5 promoted apoptosis and ADR efficacy and attenuated ABCB1 expression levels [45]. Similarly, the modulation of ABC transporters by GAS5 improves drug sensitivity in other malignancies [82]. In pancreatic cancer, GAS5 increased sensitivity to the antimetabolite Gemcitabine by acting as a ceRNA for miR-21. Overexpression of GAS5 increased apoptosis, suppressed proliferation, and decreased Multidrug Resistance-Associated Protein 1 (MRP1), MDR1, and ABCG2 expression [82, 83]. Moreover, in hepatocellular carcinoma, the RNA-binding motif protein 38 (RBM38) binds and stabilizes GAS5, restoring its expression. Increased GAS5 levels downregulated the multidrug resistance proteins P-gp, ABCG2, and MRP1, resulting in sensitivity to the multikinase inhibitor, sorafenib [82, 84].

The tyrosine kinase small molecule inhibitor Lapatinib raises GAS5 expression levels, inhibiting trastuzumab-resistant breast cancer proliferation [70]. GAS5 can increase sensitivity to the anthracycline chemotherapeutic drug Doxorubicin (also known as ADR) [44, 45], the antimicrotubule chemotherapeutic drug Paclitaxel and the platinum-based alkylating agent Cisplatin [43]. It also enhances sensitivity to natural compounds such as Curcumin [74] (see Table 1).

GAS5 and autophagy cell death

The cell death mechanisms elicited by GAS5 are not limited to apoptosis in cancer. Autophagy, a lysosome-dependent cell death modality, exhibits a dual role in cancer by either promoting cell survival as a stress response or cell death when overly activated [85]. In MCF-7 breast cancer, the overexpression of GAS5 led to an increase in the levels of unc-51-like autophagy activating kinase (ULK)1/2 proteins, which are crucial regulators of autophagy initiation, resulting in reduced breast cancer proliferation and invasion [86]. In contrast, GAS5 overexpression in glioma cells enhanced Cisplatin sensitivity by suppressing excessive autophagy through the induction of mTOR signaling [87]. The above studies highlight the context-dependent effects of GAS5 on autophagy. According to Zhang et al. [88], in colorectal cancer, GAS5 serves as a molecular sponge for miR-34a, thereby relieving miR-34a-mediated repression of SIRT1 type III nuclear deacetylase (Sirtuin1). The increased SIRT1 promotes mTOR signaling, which in turn suppresses macroautophagy and induces apoptosis. In addition, the authors uncovered that the mTOR signaling pathway suppressed GAS5 expression and created a negative regulatory feedback loop with miR-34a. This GAS5/miR-34a/SIRT1/mTOR feedback loop is associated with sustaining autophagy at a balanced level in colorectal cancer. Therefore, the mechanism contributes to tumor suppression and points out GAS5's multifaceted role in modulating cell death and survival in colorectal cancer [88].

GAS5 and other tumor-suppressor lncRNAs in breast cancer

Similarly to GAS5, other well-known tumor suppressor lncRNAs, such as Maternally Expressed Gene 3 (MEG3), Long Intergenic Non-Protein Coding RNA 472 (LINC00472) and NF-κB Interacting lncRNA (NKILA), are downregulated in breast cancer. MEG3 functions as a miRNA sponge, regulator of the miR-4513/PBLD axis, and increases the expression and translocation of NF-κB to activate the p53 pathway to induce apoptosis in breast cancer [89]. Additionally, MEG3 overexpression negatively regulates the AKT pathway in breast cancer [57, 58]. The primary localization of GAS5 in breast cancer occurs in both the nucleus, where it functions as a riborepressor of the glucocorticoid receptor, and in the cytoplasm, where it serves its ceRNA role by sponging oncomiRs [51]. In contrast, MEG3 is predominant in the nucleus as chromatin speckles with minor cytoplasmic activity as a ceRNA [89]. Like GAS5, the upregulation of MEG3 has been shown to enhance sensitivity to paclitaxel and promote apoptosis in breast cancer [90]. In contrast, GAS5 is involved in a broader spectrum of drug sensitivity (see Table 1) compared to MEG3. In parallel to GAS5, MEG3 sponges miR-21, to deactivate the PI3K/Akt pathway [91]. Since miR-21 also negatively regulates GAS5 in breast cancer, this indicates a possible, though unconfirmed, interplay between these lncRNAs.

LINC00472 is downregulated in breast cancer due to promoter methylation [89]. While GAS5 functions via diverse mechanisms as an apoptosis inducer in breast cancer, LINC00472 exerts a direct epigenetic regulatory function in breast cancer. LINC00472 inhibits MEK/ERK signaling and recruits DNA methyltransferases to methylate the promoter of the DNA replication factor, Minichromosome maintenance complex component 6 (MCM6), thereby reducing DNA replication and epigenetically inhibiting proliferation [92]. Similarly to GAS5, upregulated LINC00472 can function as an inhibitor of NF-κB or as a ceRNA to inhibit tumorigenesis [89] and is localized in both the nucleus and cytoplasm [92].

NKILA directly attaches to the functional domain of IκB and suppresses the NF-κB pathway. The overexpression of NKILA in breast cancer inhibited proliferation and promoted apoptosis via inhibition of the NF-κB pathway [93]. Similarly to GAS5, NKILA can increase the sensitivity of Cisplatin in breast cancer. However, NKILA’s mechanism of action is via inhibition of p65 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation [43, 94]. NKILA is localized in both the nucleus and predominantly in the cytoplasm in breast cancer [95]. Overall, these tumor-suppressive lncRNAs share some common pathways and localization but exhibit diverse mechanisms of action in breast cancer. Notably, GAS5 is distinctive for its multi-modal approach to promote apoptosis, its evolutionary origins as a host gene for many snoRNAs, its ceRNA activity for oncomiRs, and its role as a riborepressor of the GR (uncommon in many lncRNAs). Additionally, GAS5 is involved in a wider range of inducing drug sensitivity (Table 1) directly or indirectly, and its downregulation and apoptosis potential are reported among various cancers [40, 51].

GAS5 and oncogenic lncRNAs in breast cancer

Several oncogenic lncRNAs are upregulated and involved in breast cancer progression; some of the most common are HOX transcript antisense RNA (HOTAIR), Metastasis-associated lung adenocarcinoma transcript 1 (MALAT1), and H19 imprinted maternally expressed transcript (H19). In contrast to GAS5, these oncogenic lncRNAs are involved in promoting proliferation, invasion, and therapy resistance. Furthermore, oncogenic lncRNAs function as a ceRNA for tumor suppressor miRNAs to maintain oncogene expression and may upregulate the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, whereas GAS5 is a ceRNA for oncomiRs and downregulates the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in some contexts. Additionally, some of the oncogenic lncRNAs increase resistance to the same drugs for which GAS5 improves sensitivity, highlighting their opposition [51, 89]. Conclusion

GAS5 represents a promising therapeutic target in breast cancer treatment due to its ability to induce apoptosis, which is widely regarded as the most desirable form of cell death, through a variety of mechanisms. These mechanisms include the GAS5-derived pi-sno75 epigenetic modification of the TRAIL gene promoter to activate apoptosis, its role as a ceRNA to maintain tumor suppressor genes, and its capability to act as a riborepressor of the GR and inhibit cIAP2. GAS5 induces apoptosis via cell death receptors, mitochondrial signaling, or inhibition of inhibitors of apoptosis. Notably, decreased levels of GAS5 in breast cancer are correlated with advanced clinical staging and grading, occurrences of lymph node metastasis, reduced survival rates, and the process of EMT. GAS5 can be regulated via NMD and mTOR, NOTCH-1 and aberrant promoter methylation. Furthermore, GAS5 has been shown to enhance breast cancer's sensitivity to various treatment modalities, including radiotherapy, chemotherapy and endocrine therapy. It may mitigate drug resistance through direct overexpression or indirectly through the administration of drugs that increase its expression, consequently promoting apoptosis. GAS5 is implicated in several critical signaling pathways within breast cancer, including the PI3K/AKT/mTOR, Wnt/β-catenin, and NF-κB. As a recognized tumor suppressor, GAS5 warrants further examination as a potential therapeutic target in breast cancer.

Current and future prospects

GAS5 downregulation significantly influences breast cancer progression (see "GAS5 downregulation influences breast cancer progression" section), and overexpression improves drug sensitivity (see Table 1), reinforcing that GAS5 expression is of valuable importance in breast cancer treatment. mTOR inhibitors are often used to increase GAS5 expression levels [19] but are associated with toxicity and drug resistance [106, 107]. However, combination strategies with mTOR and CDK4/6 inhibitors have shown potential to reduce drug resistance and toxicity in breast cancer [108]. Common mTOR inhibitors with breast cancer treatment potential include Everolimus, Temsirolimus, Sirolimus, and Ridaforolimus [107]. The combination therapy of mTOR inhibitors with endocrine therapy has shown significant clinical potential in metastatic breast cancer. Indeed, the combination of mTOR inhibitors with targeted or chemotherapeutic agents is in over 40 clinical trials and has demonstrated some promising efficacy [107]. The FDA-approved Everolimus has shown significant improvement in progression-free survival in the phase III BOLERO-2 trial [109]. Furthermore, real-world data confirms long-term survival benefit from Everolimus for advanced HR +/HER2 − breast cancer [110].

Multiple PI3K/AKT/mTOR inhibitors are currently in clinical trials, with Alpelisib (PI3K inhibitor; SOLAR-1 trial combining Alpelisib and Fulvestrant) and Capivasertib (AKT inhibitor; CAPItello-291 trial combining Capivasertib and Fulvestrant) already having received FDA approval for breast cancer [111]. However, drug resistance with mTOR inhibitors and combination treatments is still challenging. Pre-clinical breast and prostate cancer studies demonstrate that mTOR inhibitors Rapamycin, Everolimus, and Temsirolimus increase GAS5 levels and suppress hormone-sensitive cancer. In contrast, hormone-independent cells exhibited resistance to these agents as well as resistance to combined mTORC1/mTORC2 and dual PI3K/mTOR inhibitors. Hormone-sensitive cells depicted higher levels of GAS5 compared to hormone-insensitive cells, which may explain the high resistance to treatment observed in hormone-insensitive cells. Furthermore, in prostate cancer and lymphoid cells, GAS5 is functionally required for mTOR inhibition [80].

Given that multiple mTOR inhibitors and PI3K/AKT/mTOR inhibitors are already in clinical trials or FDA-approved and show greater efficacy in combination treatments, this suggests that GAS5 may have relevant clinical potential due to its influence on the downregulation of the oncogenic PI3K/AKT/mTOR axis. Pre-clinical studies of GAS5 already indicate the importance of GAS5 expression and GAS5's potential in drug sensitivity. In addition, GAS5 employs multiple mechanisms to induce apoptosis and inhibit carcinogenesis in breast cancer, as well as modulate the P-gp ("GAS5: A key ally in combating breast cancer drug resistance" section). This is particularly useful in hormone-insensitive cells that have low GAS5 expression, high P-gp expression and gain resistance to PI3K/mTOR inhibitors. Additional investigations of GAS5 should employ delivery systems to upregulate GAS5 expression and determine efficacy with various combination therapies with chemotherapy agents, endocrine therapy and mTOR inhibitors. Furthermore, the link between GAS5 and attenuated NF-κB pathway activation should be investigated in inflammatory breast cancers, given the current evidence of NF-κB modulation by GAS5 in cervical cancer. Future studies should also focus on GAS5-mediated apoptosis, particularly in relation to reactive oxygen species (ROS) and changes in redox balance within the context of breast cancer. Additionally, the ability of GAS5 to influence other cell death modalities in addition to apoptosis and autophagy should be explored. The interaction of GAS5 with various miRNAs and proteins should be further studied to broaden the therapeutic intervention spectrum.

GAS5 can be upregulated in breast cancer by small molecules, gene therapy, exosome-based delivery, and nanoparticle systems. Small molecules can interact with miRNAs at both transcriptional and post-transcriptional levels or participate in RNA decay pathways. They offer valuable solubility, bioavailability, and are metabolically stable. Challenges include ensuring optimal specificity to prevent interaction with various oncogenic miRNAs, delivery optimization, and managing immune responses that require broader screening methods [112, 113]. The small molecule NP-C86 prevents NMD of GAS5, stabilizing GAS5 expression and enhancing insulin sensitivity since GAS5 affects the GR [114].

Gene therapy with CRISPRa/i systems can be used with reasonable specificity; however, in vivo applications of large Cas proteins are challenging, and immunological responses need further validation or assessment. Exosomes are naturally secreted vesicles that are cargo for lncRNAs. They offer advantageous low antigenicity, minimal toxicity, and are unaffected by endocytosis and phagocytosis. Delivery challenges include costly large-scale production, and circRNAs can activate RIG-I–mediated immune response unless chemically modified [112]. Recent advancements include engineering exosomes with targeted ligands and troubleshooting RNA loading techniques to ensure elevated specificity [115]. Lipid nanoparticles (LP) and gold nanoparticles (AuNP) with targeting ligands can encapsulate and deliver lncRNAs. Challenges include entrapment in the endocytic compartment upon delivery, and specificity runs the risk of off-target uptakes and may trigger flu-like immunogenic responses [112]. Recent advancements in LNP design include programmable lipid nanoparticles that exhibit detailed spatiotemporal control, improving drug delivery and reducing off-target effects [116]. We are of the opinion that focus should be on the clinical evaluation of GAS5 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target in breast cancer. Recent innovations in delivery systems and ongoing improvements in targeted delivery showcase promising tools to improve treatment outcomes and induce apoptosis through GAS5-based therapies.
