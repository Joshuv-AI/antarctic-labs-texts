Medetomidine in the unregulated drug supply
Authors: Isabelle Miles, Justin J. Koh, Paxton Bach
Published: 2026

Medetomidine is a common adulterant in the opioid supply in North America

An α-2 adrenergic agonist approved only for veterinary use, medetomidine is similar to xylazine, but substantially more potent.1 Medetomidine is increasingly present in the unregulated drug supply, found in 85% of all opioid samples recently tested in Toronto, Canada.2

Medetomidine should be suspected when patients present with features of opioid intoxication but do not respond to naloxone

Patients may present with features of opioid overdose, along with prolonged sedation, and profound sinus bradycardia. 1,3 Medetomidine intoxication cannot be reversed,1 although naloxone should be administered to treat the opioid component of an overdose.

Patients who abruptly discontinue use of unregulated opioids are at risk of medetomidine withdrawal

Signs and symptoms can include hypertension, tachycardia, tremors, diaphoresis, hyperthermia, and intractable nausea and vomiting, with onset as soon as 4 to 6 hours after discontinuing use.3,4 Medetomidine withdrawal can cause QTc prolongation and metabolic derangements, including hypokalemia.3

Withdrawal is primarily treated with prompt administration of α-2 agonists (clonidine, dexmedetomidine)

Higher doses than usual are often needed to achieve symptom relief, and standardized best practices have not been established. Initial doses for oral clonidine range between 0.1 and 0.3 mg, guided by vital signs and symptom severity. Higher doses, such as 0.6 to 0.9 mg, may be required for more severe presentations, usually administered in a monitored setting. Loading doses are typically administered every 2 to 4 hours (up to 3 doses) until symptoms improve, followed by similar or decreased doses every 6 to 12 hours, with tapering as tolerated.3,5 Intravenous dexmedetomidine should be considered for severe symptoms or inability to tolerate oral medications, with a transition to oral clonidine after stabilization.3,5 Dopamine antagonists (e.g., olanzapine) may be more effective than usual antiemetics, but should be used with caution in those with QTc prolongation.3 Concurrent opioid withdrawal should be treated with short-acting opioids and/or initiation or continuation of opioid agonist therapy.3

Medetomidine withdrawal can be a medical emergency

Undertreated withdrawal can rapidly progress to lifethreatening hemodynamic instability.3–5 Prompt recognition and early treatment are key, and patients should be managed in a setting with access to intensive care. Early addiction medicine and toxicology consultation is recommended for suspected medetomidine withdrawal.
