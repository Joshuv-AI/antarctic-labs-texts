Association of hemoglobin glycation index with frailty and all-cause mortality in hypertensive patients
Authors: Zebin Lin, Yongqi Dong, Xinlong Di, Guike Lai, Shengfang Wang, Yipin Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveHypertensive patients are prone to frailty, but the exact link between them remains unclear. This study aims to evaluate the HbA1c glycated index (HGI) as a biomarker for frailty and mortality in hypertensive patients.Methods1773 hypertensive participants were included in the CHARLS database. HGI was calculated as measured HbA1c minus predicted HbA1c. Associations with frailty and mortality were evaluated via multivariable logistic regression, Cox regression, mediation analysis, and threshold detection.ResultsHGI was independently associated with frailty (adjusted OR = 1.28, 95% CI:1.03–1.60), with diabetes mediating 27.8% of this association. It also showed a U-shaped relationship with all-cause mortality (inflection point: HGI=-0.15). In the overall hypertensive population, when HGI ≥ -0.15, there is no significant association between HGI and mortality risk (adjusted HR = 1.17, 95% CI: 0.92–1.50). But in the frailty subgroup with HGI ≥-0.15, per 1-unit HGI increase 60% higher mortality risk (HR = 1.60, 95% CI:1.11–2.32). For HGI <-0.15, higher HGI was linked to lower mortality (adjusted HR = 0.68, 95% CI:0.49–0.95), with a similar trend in the frailty subgroup (adjusted HR = 0.36, 95% CI:0.13–0.97).ConclusionThis study demonstrates that the HGI serves as a biomarker for both frailty and mortality in hypertensive patients. Elevated HGI is associated with increased frailty risk, while its U-shaped relationship with all-cause mortality highlights critical implications for risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01994-0.

Introduction

Hypertension is one of the most common chronic diseases and a leading risk factor for ischemic heart disease, stroke, other cardiovascular diseases (CVD), chronic kidney disease, and dementia [1]. In China, the prevalence rate of hypertension has reached 23.2%, and it is becoming more prevalent among younger populations [2, 3]. Frailty, a geriatric syndrome characterized by diminished physiological reserves and heightened vulnerability to stressors, demonstrates a prevalence ranging from 2.3% to 20.2% that escalates with advancing age [4]. Notably, this condition appears to be particularly prevalent among hypertensive populations; in China, approximately 13% of elderly hypertension patients are affected by frailty [5], a figure which lies near the upper end of the range reported for the general elderly. This suggests that hypertension may be associated with an increased susceptibility to frailty. Emerging evidence reveals a bidirectional interplay between frailty and hypertension: chronic conditions like hypertension are recognized as significant risk factors for frailty development [6], while cardiovascular complications associated with hypertension have been implicated in frailty pathogenesis [7, 8]. Notably, hypertensive patients with concurrent frailty exhibit significantly elevated mortality risks [9]. The identification of intervenable biomarkers for frailty in hypertensive populations therefore becomes crucial for implementing early preventive strategies and optimizing clinical outcomes.

The hemoglobin glycation index (HGI) is calculated as the difference between the observed and predicted glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels, serving as a marker to measure interindividual variability in hemoglobin glycosylation [10]. This approach helps alleviate the limitation of using HbA1c alone, failing to fully reflect individual variability and fluctuations in blood glucose levels. Prior studies have confirmed that HGI is associated with insulin resistance (IR), a recognized risk factor for the development of arterial hypertension and strongly linked to adverse outcomes in hypertensive patients [11–13]. Additionally, IR represents a significant potential contributor to frailty [14], with its pathological mechanisms primarily involving multifaceted metabolic and physiological dysfunctions. In skeletal muscle, IR can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, impairing the oxidative phosphorylation process and reducing adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, thereby rendering muscles prone to fatigue and weakness and significantly compromising physical function [15]. Simultaneously, IR can induce anabolic resistance, characterized by a diminished response to insulin and amino acid stimulation, resulting in impaired muscle protein synthesis and further accelerating muscle loss and the progression of sarcopenia. More critically, a vicious cycle exists between insulin resistance and chronic inflammation: IR can activate inflammatory responses, triggering the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which in turn exacerbate insulin resistance and muscle catabolism, collectively promoting the onset and development of frailty [16, 17]. Based on this association, we hypothesize that elevated HGI is associated with an increased risk of frailty in hypertensive populations. To fill this research gap, we utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to investigate the risk of frailty and its prognostic implications of HGI in Chinese adults with hypertension.

Methods

Study population

This is a prospective longitudinal cohort study. The study population was sourced from the baseline survey data (Wave 1) of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Inclusion criteria were as follows: aged over 45 years and self-reported hypertension. Exclusion criteria comprised: inability to complete fasting blood glucose testing, missing critical variables (inability to assess and calculate the frailty index, missing HbA1c data), and failure to participate in the follow-up survey at Wave 5. The participant enrollment process is presented in Fig. 1. The Peking University Ethics Committee approved the CHARLS study, and all participants provided written informed consent.

HGI calculation

A linear regression model was employed to analyze the relationship between fasting blood glucose (FBG) and actual HbA1c, as shown in Fig. 2A. The overall significance of the linear regression model was examined using an F-test, which indicated statistical significance (F = 2176.34, p < 0.001). The resulting linear regression equation is: Predicted HbA1c = 0.309* FPG + 3.408. In this study, the HGI was calculated by subtracting the predicted HbA1c value from the actual HbA1c value.

Definitions and outcome

Baseline data were rigorously collected by trained interviewers using structured questionnaires, encompassing: (1) Demographic Characteristics: Gender, age, residence, educational attainment, household income, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. (2) Physical Measurements: Height, weight, body mass index (BMI), systolic and diastolic blood pressure (SBP/DBP). (3) Medical History and Medication Use: Self-reported physician-diagnosed conditions (e.g., heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia) and current medications. (4) Laboratory Parameters: White blood cell count (WBC), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), creatinine, C-reactive protein (CRP), uric acid, hemoglobin, cystatin C, fasting plasma glucose (FPG), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c).

In this study, frailty was determined through the calculation of the Frailty Index (FI), which has been validated in the CHARLS database and other cohorts [18–22]. The FI was constructed using 32 items covering variables such as comorbidities, physical function, disability, depression, and cognition. For more detailed information on the FI, please refer to Supplementary Table S1. To address missing data, optimize data utilization, and ensure indicator quality, we allowed up to 20% missing variables (≤ 6 items) when calculating the FI. For each participant, the FI was calculated as the sum of current health deficits divided by the number of evaluated items. Therefore, the FI ranges from 0 to 1, with higher scores indicating greater frailty. Frailty was defined as an FI ≥ 25%.

CHARLS has completed five waves of surveys, with follow-ups conducted every 2 to 3 years in 2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. The mortality outcome during follow-up was defined as all-cause mortality.

Statistical analysis

Minor baseline data missingness (< 20%) was deemed acceptable, and the k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithm was employed for missing value imputation [23].

Patients with hypertension were grouped based on the frailty index (FI), where FI < 25% defined the non-frail group and FI ≥ 25% defined the frail group. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality of continuous variables. For normally distributed data with homogeneous variances, Student’s t-test was applied; when variances were heterogeneous, Welch’s t-test was used instead. For non-normally distributed data, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed. Chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test were used to analyze differences in categorical data. Restricted cubic spline plots were used to fit binary logistic regression models and demonstrate the dose-response relationship between frailty status and HGI. Binary logistic regression analysis was performed to calculate the odds ratio (OR) of HGI associated with frailty, with variable selection using a stepwise backward method based on maximum likelihood estimation. Generalized additive models (GAMs) with linear regression were fitted to quantify the change relationship between HGI and frailty index. Given the potential role of diabetes in the development of frailty, a mediation analysis was conducted to quantify the extent to which the observed association between HGI and frailty could be statistically explained by pre-existing diabetes. To further explore the association between HGI and long-term prognosis in hypertensive patients, restricted cubic spline plots were used to fit Cox regression models and illustrate the dose-response relationship between HGI and mortality. Elastic Net regression was applied for variable selection, and the selected variables were included as major confounders in threshold effect analysis based on Cox regression to adjust the risk ratio of HGI. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 (two-sided). Data analysis was performed using R language (R for Mac version 4.2.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of participants stratified by frailty status. The frail group (N = 352) was significantly older (median age 63 vs. 60 years, p < 0.001), more likely to be female (59.7% vs. 51.0%, p = 0.004), and resided more frequently in rural areas (70.2% vs. 56.9%, p < 0.001) compared to the non-frail group (N = 1,421). Frail participants had lower education levels (96.6% with primary education vs. 87.8%, p < 0.001), higher prevalence of disability (34.9% vs. 13.8%, p < 0.001), and poorer cognitive function (median score 10.0 vs. 12.5, p < 0.001). They also exhibited significantly higher depressive symptoms (CESD10 median 13 vs. 6, p < 0.001), lower grip strength (27 vs. 32 kg, p < 0.001), and greater prevalence of chronic conditions including diabetes (19.0% vs. 10.8%), heart disease (39.2% vs. 17.5%), stroke (13.9% vs. 2.5%), and arthritis (65.9% vs. 29.3%) (all p < 0.001). Medication use patterns differed substantially, with frail individuals more frequently using medications for arthritis (27.6% vs. 9.4%), heart disease (23.9% vs. 10.1%), and stroke (7.7% vs. 1.1%) (all p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in BMI, waist circumference, or most blood biomarkers except for slightly higher HbA1c (5.30% vs. 5.20%, p = 0.006) in the frail group.

Association between HGI and frailty

The restricted cubic spline plot fitted with binary logistic regression revealed a linear relationship between HGI and the risk of frailty (P for non-linearity = 0.002), as shown in Fig. 2B. Univariate logistic regression analysis indicated that each 1-unit increase in HGI was associated with a 41% higher risk of frailty (95% CI: 1.18–1.68, P < 0.001). After adjusting for potential confounders using stepwise backward selection in multivariate logistic regression analysis, including level of education, age, gender, rural area, drinking status, disability, dyslipidemia, diabetes, lung diseases, heart disease, history of stroke, chronic kidney disease, arthritis, asthma, dominant hand grip strength, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, white blood cell count, and platelets, HGI remained an independent risk factor for frailty in hypertensive patients (OR: 1.28, 95% CI: 1.03–1.60, P = 0.027). Mediation analysis

To quantify the extent to which the observed association between HGI and frailty could be explained by pre-existing diabetes, we conducted a explanatory mediation analysis. This analysis decomposes the total association into a component that is statistically shared with diabetes (indirect effect) and a component that is independent of diabetes (direct effect). The mediation analysis was performed using the mediation package in R. A quasi-Bayesian approximation approach with 100 simulations was applied to estimate the total, direct, and indirect effects. Frailty was modeled as a binary outcome, with HGI as the independent variable, diabetes as the mediator, and adjustments for age and gender as covariates. Results of the mediation analysis revealed a significant total effect of HGI on frailty (coefficient = 0.048, 95% CI: 0.023–0.075, p < 0.001). The indirect effect mediated by diabetes was also significant (coefficient = 0.014, 95% CI: 0.004–0.024, p < 0.001), accounting for 27.8% (95% CI: 7.2–85.7) of the total effect. After adjusting for diabetes, the direct effect of HGI on frailty remained significant (coefficient = 0.035, 95% CI: 0.003–0.060, p = 0.040), as illustrated in Fig. 2E.

HGI and long-term mortality in hypertensive patients

During a median follow-up of 101 months, 253 participants (14.3%) experienced all-cause mortality. A restricted cubic spline plot fitted with Cox proportional hazards regression was used to demonstrate the relationship between HGI and long-term all-cause mortality risk in hypertensive patients. The analysis revealed a statistically significant U-shaped curve for the association (P for non-linearity test = 0.002), indicating a non-linear dose-response relationship.​ The inflection point of the U-shaped curve, where the mortality risk transitioned from decreasing to increasing, was observed at an approximate HGI value of −0.15, as depicted in Fig. 2F.

For variable selection, Elastic Net regression—a hybrid regularization method combining L1 and L2 penalties—was employed to handle potential multicollinearity and high-dimensional data. Using 10-fold cross-validation, the optimal regularization parameter λ was selected based on the “one-standard-error rule” to balance model complexity and prediction accuracy (detailed information on the included variables is provided in Supplementary Table S3.). This approach identified the λ value of 0.075, which minimized the mean squared error (MSE) while ensuring parsimony. Through this process, age, gender, CRP, and cystatin C were selected as key risk factors significantly associated with all-cause mortality risk (Table S3), serving as critical covariates for subsequent threshold effect analysis in Cox regression model.

Given the observed threshold effect in the association between HGI and long-term mortality risk in hypertensive patients, stratified Cox regression analysis was performed based on HGI < −0.15 and HGI ≥ −0.15. For HGI < −0.15, each 1-unit increase in HGI was associated with a 38% lower mortality risk (unadjusted HR: 0.62, 95% CI: 0.45–0.86, P = 0.004). After adjusting for age, gender, CRP and cystatin C, the association remained significant, with HR of 0.68 (95% CI: 0.49–0.95, P = 0.022). In contrast, no significant association was observed between HGI changes and mortality risk among patients with HGI ≥ −0.15 (HR: 1.17; 95% CI: 0.92–1.50, P = 0.210), as shown in Table 3.

The subgroup analysis stratified by frailty status further revealed that among individuals with frailty and HGI < −0.15, higher HGI was associated with a negative correlation with mortality risk (HR: 0.36, 95% CI: 0.13–0.97, P = 0.043). A similar trend was observed in non-frail individuals (HR: 0.70, 95% CI: 0.48–1.00, P = 0.050). Conversely, among those with HGI ≥ −0.15, HGI exhibited a positive association with all-cause mortality risk (HR: 1.60, 95% CI: 1.11–2.32, P = 0.012), a relationship not detected in the non-frail subgroup. Subgroup analysis results are detailed in Supplementary Table S4.

Discussion

This study revealed that the HGI serves as an independent risk factor for frailty in hypertensive patients. Specifically, each 1-unit increase in HGI was associated with a 28% elevated risk of frailty (adjusted OR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.03–1.60), and HGI demonstrated a linear positive correlation with frailty index. Notably, diabetes mediated 27.8% of the association between HGI and frailty. This association is partially attributable to the fact that individuals with high HGI are more likely to have pre-existing diabetes, a known risk factor for frailty, while another part of the association operates through pathways independent of clinical diabetes. Furthermore, HGI exhibited a U-shaped relationship with all-cause mortality: when HGI < −0.15, higher HGI was linked to reduced mortality risk (adjusted HR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.49–0.95), whereas HGI ≥ −0.15 corresponded to a significant upward trend in mortality, particularly pronounced among frail individuals (HR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.11–2.32, P = 0.012). These findings suggest that HGI may act as a biomarker for both frailty and mortality risk in hypertensive populations, with its threshold effect (HGI=−0.15) offering critical insights for stratified clinical interventions.

In hypertensive populations, while the precise pathophysiological mechanisms linking HGI to frailty remain incompletely understood, accumulating evidence highlights key pathways involving biological aging, IR, and enhanced inflammatory-oxidative stress responses [24, 25]. These mechanisms likely serve as critical pathways bridging hypertension and frailty. As a metric reflecting the discrepancy between observed and predicted HbA1c levels, HGI captures glycemic variability influenced by erythrocyte lifespan, transmembrane glucose gradients, enzymatic abnormalities, and genetic factors [26]. This nuanced measure of glycemic control offers unique insights into the complex metabolic profiles of hypertensive patients, beyond the limitations of HbA1c alone. When HGI is elevated, it indicates that an individual’s hemoglobin glycosylation level is higher than expected, which is closely associated with IR. Insulin resistance is not only a significant risk factor for hypertension but also closely linked to the development of frailty [27]. Specifically, IR reduces the sensitivity of muscle cells to insulin, thereby triggering energy metabolism disorders that impair muscle function and increase the risk of frailty [28, 29]. Previous research suggests that the link between IR and frailty in older adults is often more pronounced in the context of central obesity [30]. In our study, however, HGI remained an independent risk factor for frailty despite no significant difference in waist circumference between the groups. This suggests that HGI may capture heterogeneous metabolic dysregulation associated with frailty that is not reflected by traditional adiposity measures like waist circumference or BMI. Diabetes represents the end-stage manifestation of IR. From this perspective, the mediating role of diabetes observed in this study (27.8%) appears to confirm that IR and hyperglycemia-induced endothelial dysfunction and microvascular damage play critical roles in the pathogenesis of frailty [31]. Meanwhile, the significant direct effect (72.2%) suggests that HGI may influence frailty through pathways beyond glucose metabolism. Accelerated biological aging emerges as a plausible mechanism, supported by a previous cross-sectional study demonstrating that individuals with low HGI (≤–0.271) exhibited 5.29-fold higher odds of possessing longer leukocyte telomere length (LTL) compared to those with high HGI (>0.085) – a relationship that remained significant after adjusting for HbA1c levels [24]. Another possible reasonable mechanism is inflammation and oxidative stress, which is one of the pathophysiological mechanisms that coexist with hypertension and frailty [32]. In hypertension, oxidative stress damages vascular endothelial cells, causing vascular dysfunction and impairing blood supply to systemic tissues and organs. This oxidative stress also activates inflammatory responses, triggering the release of multiple inflammatory cytokines that exacerbate tissue damage and functional impairment [33]. Therefore, we speculate that elevated HGI may reflect heightened oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, thereby increasing susceptibility to frailty. Supporting this, a cross-sectional study of 7,323 nondiabetic U.S. adults found that inflammatory biomarkers (CRP, PMNL, and monocytes) increased linearly with rising HGI [25]. HGI was also strongly correlated with dermal advanced glycation end products (AGEs), whose interaction with their receptor (RAGE) initiates signaling cascades leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and reactive oxygen species (ROS) production [34]. Although these mechanisms provide reasonable approaches, the full role of HGI in the frailty development of hypertensive individuals remains to be explored. It is emphasized that more in-depth research is needed to analyze the causal relationship and determine targeted intervention measures.

In additional analyses on the prognosis of hypertensive populations, we found a significant U-shaped relationship between HGI and all-cause mortality in hypertensive patients, indicating the complex impact of glycemic heterogeneity on long-term outcomes. The observed findings are not entirely unexpected. A previous study using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is consistent with our findings [35]. Importantly, this association appeared to be amplified in the frailty subgroup. Specifically, our analysis revealed a significant inverse association when HGI levels fell below the threshold of −0.15 (adjusted HR = 0.36; 95% CI: 0.13–0.97), indicating reduced mortality risk with increasing HGI in this range. Conversely, a markedly elevated risk emerged when HGI exceeded this inflection point (adjusted HR = 1.60; 95% CI: 1.11–2.32), demonstrating a positive association between higher HGI levels and mortality risk above the threshold. This non-linear relationship may be closely related to the glycemic state and the body’s compensatory capacity reflected by HGI.

Mechanistically, a low HGI state—where measured HbA1c levels fall below predicted values—may indicate efficient glycemic regulation or a reduced glycotoxic load. On one hand, this could reflect enhanced insulin sensitivity or lower inflammatory burden, physiological traits consistently linked to diminished cardiovascular damage risk. However, this does not imply that extremely low HGI is completely beneficial. The observed U-shaped relationship suggests that mortality risk increases when HGI deviates significantly from the threshold inflection point in either direction. This phenomenon is likely associated with more frequent episodes of hypoglycemia, a well-established risk factor for cardiovascular mortality [36]. On the other hand, HGI elevation above the threshold may signify exacerbating IR, heightened oxidative stress, or activated chronic inflammation [34]. These pathological processes synergistically drive vascular endothelial injury, myocardial dysfunction, and multiorgan failure, ultimately elevating mortality risk. Notably, the impact of HGI is particularly pronounced in frail patients, potentially due to their diminished physiological reserves and reduced capacity to buffer metabolic disturbances. This vulnerability may amplify the cascade from minor metabolic imbalance to systemic decompensation in frail individuals [37].

The findings of this study offer valuable insights for refining clinical risk stratification in hypertensive patients. Clinicians can assess the risk of frailty progression in hypertensive patients by measuring the HGI and utilize its threshold effect to implement personalized glycemic management, particularly in those with comorbid frailty. For individuals with HGI < − 0.15, attention should be paid to the risk of hypoglycemia associated with intensive glycemic control. Those with HGI ≥ − 0.15 should be classified into a high-risk management group—even in the absence of diabetic-level HbA1c—as they may be considered at high risk for diabetes or have “hidden” metabolic dysregulation. It is recommended that such patients undergo an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) at an earlier stage to screen for prediabetes or diabetes, while more actively promoting lifestyle interventions (e.g., Mediterranean diet, resistance exercise). When selecting antihypertensive therapy, priority may be given to agents with metabolic benefits or insulin-sensitizing properties (such as ACEIs/ARBs or SGLT2 inhibitors). Additionally, enhanced monitoring and management of other cardiovascular risk factors, such as inflammatory markers, should be implemented. The U-shaped relationship between HGI and mortality offers a new perspective for exploring the “optimal glycometabolic window.” Future research should further validate the generalizability of this threshold across different ethnic and age subgroups and clarify the prognostic value of dynamic monitoring of HGI changes.

Limitations

This study still has the following limitations that need to be interpreted with caution. Although the data used in this study derive from a longitudinal cohort, the exploration of the relationship between HGI and frailty risk was essentially cross-sectional in nature. Despite multivariate adjustment and mediation analysis to explore associational pathways, residual confounding factors may still influence the results, thus precluding the establishment of a causal relationship between HGI and frailty. Additionally, the survival analysis was based on a single baseline HGI measurement, failing to capture the impact of dynamic changes in HGI over time. Finally, the diagnosis of hypertension relied on self-reporting, which may be subject to recall bias.

Conclusion

This study preliminarily investigates HGI as an independent risk factor for frailty in hypertensive populations. Elevated HGI is linearly associated with an increased risk of frailty, partially mediated by diabetes, and exhibits a U-shaped association with all-cause mortality. Though these findings facilitate the development of risk stratification strategies, future studies are warranted to validate the causal relationship and explore dynamic HGI monitoring to optimize clinical outcomes in hypertension.
