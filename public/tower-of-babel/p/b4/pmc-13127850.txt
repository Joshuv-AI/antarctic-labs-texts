Expert Clinician Insights Into the Diagnosis and Treatment of Men With Antisocial and Borderline Personality Disorder: A Qualitative Study
Authors: Jillian Helen Broadbear, Michael Gihan Bhagwandas, Samuel Crowley, Lukas Cheney, Sathya Rao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesMen with presentations consistent with borderline personality disorder (BPD) and antisocial personality disorder (ASPD) are highly visible in community and forensic services. However, mis/underdiagnosis may be a consequence of their lower than expected engagement with mental health services, and when they do engage, systematised diagnostic biases may inaccurately differentiate these two subtypes. Given the substantial harms experienced by men who have personality disorder (PD), it is important to understand how personality disorder can be better identified and supported in clinical and forensic settings.MethodsTen nationally and internationally recognised clinician participants who have expertise in the diagnosis and treatment of men with PD (80% male; 60% psychologists, 40% psychiatrists) participated in individual, semi‐structured qualitative interviews. Participants shared their observations regarding PD presentations in men and recommendations for maximising treatment engagement in community and forensic settings. Transcripts from the 60–90‐min interviews were analysed using inductive and deductive thematic analysis.ResultsThe analysis identified four themes: Getting men through the door, Unmasking Complexity, Holding Steady in the Face of Risk, and Addressing Shame through Connection. Men with BPD tend to under‐report their symptoms and distrust mental health services. Clinicians may struggle to differentiate BPD from ASPD, as men's emotional dysregulation often manifests as anger. Participants emphasised appealing to the person's self‐interest, building a trusting therapeutic alliance, and carefully addressing shame to facilitate treatment engagement.ConclusionsHalf of the expert clinicians were employed in forensic settings where men with BPD are disproportionately prevalent, rendering their perspectives especially valuable. Assessment of empathetic functioning can help counterbalance clinicians’ overemphasis on aggressive behaviours when differentiating BPD from ASPD in men. Incorporation of gendered attributes and targeting associated defensive barriers may improve treatment engagement and effectiveness for men with personality disorder.Keywords: antisocial, borderline, men, personality disorder, qualitative study

Introduction

Personality Disorders (PDs) are characterised by enduring impairments in self‐concept, interpersonal functioning, and dysfunctional patterns of behaviour which deviate markedly from cultural expectations (American Psychiatric Association 2013). PDs are associated with an increased risk of death by suicide (Broadbear et al. 2020; Skodol et al. 2002), violence (Haw et al. 2001), unemployment (Coid et al. 2006), and reduced quality of life (IsHak et al. 2013). The community prevalence of PDs is estimated at 12% (Volkert et al. 2018), with a significantly higher prevalence in forensic settings; it is estimated that 65% of male prisoners and 21% of female prisoners meet criteria for one or more PDs (Fazel and Danesh 2002).

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (Fifth Edition; DSM‐5; American Psychiatric Association 2013) separates PDs into three clusters: Cluster A (odd and eccentric disorders), Cluster B (emotional and erratic disorders), and Cluster C (anxious or fearful disorders). Cluster B comprises 4 subtypes: antisocial, borderline, histrionic and narcissistic. Studies exploring sex differences in the prevalence of Cluster B PDs report that men are more likely to be diagnosed with antisocial personality disorder (ASPD) and narcissistic personality disorder (NPD), while women are more commonly diagnosed with borderline personality disorder (BPD) and histrionic personality disorder (HPD) (Jane et al. 2007; Klonsky et al. 2002; Looper and Paris 2000; Lynam and Widiger 2007). Epidemiological studies have reported non‐significant differences in the prevalence of BPD among men and women in the community (González et al. 2016; Tomko et al. 2014). Despite this, women account for 75% of patients diagnosed with BPD in clinical settings (Grant et al. 2008; Lenzenweger et al. 2007). Explanations for this disparity include the influence of gender bias in diagnostic assessment (Becker and Lamb 1994), as well as a greater tendency for men with BPD to engage with drug and alcohol services and the criminal justice system (Wetterborg et al. 2015).

BPD and ASPD in Men

Whereas women make up 75% of patients diagnosed with BPD in clinical settings, the opposite trend is observed for ASPD in men, for whom 75% of people given this diagnosis are male (Skodol and Bender 2003). This discrepancy raises questions concerning whether the lower representation of BPD‐diagnosed men in clinical settings may be attributable to a gender‐based bias favouring the identification and diagnosis of ASPD in male patients. BPD is characterised by pervasive instability in emotion regulation, interpersonal relationships, chronic suicidality, and identity disturbance (Biskin and Paris 2012), whereas ASPD is distinguished by a disregard for right and wrong, lack of remorse, reckless behaviour, and consistent disregard for the rights of others (American Psychiatric Association 2013). Among men, impulsive and externalising behaviours such as interpersonal aggression and drug misuse are clinical features in both BPD and ASPD (Yu et al. 2012; Newhill et al. 2009; Bayes and Parker 2017), and high rates of comorbidity between these disorders have been observed in male populations (Grant et al. 2008). Prevalence studies show that ASPD co‐occurs in 57% of male psychiatric patients diagnosed with BPD, and in 5.7% to 27% of men with BPD in community samples (Black et al. 2007; Black et al. 2010; Grant et al. 2008; Tadić et al. 2009). When compared with BPD or ASPD alone, comorbid BPD and ASPD is associated with increased risk of suicide (McGirr et al. 2007), violence (Freestone et al. 2013), and criminal re‐offending (Howard et al. 2014).

Identifying BPD in men can pose significant challenges within forensic settings, where clinicians may lean towards diagnosing ASPD based on the patient's history of problematic behaviours and criminal offending, potentially leading them to overlook underlying BPD symptoms. These difficulties are further compounded by their high rates of comorbidity in forensic populations, up to 91% among male offenders (Wetterborg et al. 2015). In community settings, the underdiagnosis of BPD in men may also be influenced by symptom overlap and comorbidASPD, with their shared externalising features complicating the process of differential diagnosis. Aggression is a clinical feature in both ASPD and BPD (Yu et al. 2012; Newhill et al. 2009), and among men with BPD, maladaptive externalising coping mechanisms may be misinterpreted as being ‘antisocial’.

The Impact of Stigma

Globally, men are twice as likely as women to die by suicide (World Health Organization 2021), which increases to a three‐ to four‐fold difference in Western countries (Värnik 2012). A primary contributing factor may be men holding more stigmatising attitudes and beliefs about mental illness (Gonzalez et al. 2005), deterring them from help‐seeking and disclosing their distress to others. Stigmatising beliefs among men have been attributed to traditional masculine ideals, including self‐reliance and stoicism (Boysen 2017). For men with PD, the impact of stigma may be particularly pronounced, as evidence suggests that across the community, PDs are associated with more stigmatising attitudes than other psychiatric diagnoses (Furnham et al. 2015; Wright and Furnham 2014). Common attitudes regarding people with PD are that they are dangerous, manipulative, and that their dysfunction is caused by a lack of personal control rather than by an underlying mental health condition (Sheehan et al. 2016). Consequently, the impact for men with PD may be twofold, comprising both public stigma and self‐stigma.

As well as being a barrier to treatment engagement, stigmatising attitudes may persist within the treatment setting itself. It is well‐known that healthcare professionals often harbour discriminatory attitudes towards people with PDs (Lawn and McMahon 2015). PDs commonly associated with men, such as ASPD and NPD, are usually considered untreatable, leading to exclusion from mental healthcare services (Salekin 2002). Furthermore, the threat perceived by mental health service providers in association with men diagnosed with these Cluster B PDs (due to externalised anger) likely contributes to their reluctance to engage with these patients.

Treatment for Men with PD

Research on treatment outcomes for men with PD remains limited. Most studies on PD treatment outcomes focus on BPD in women. Historically, BPD has been perceived as a predominantly female disorder; therefore, much of what is known about its presentation and response to treatment is based on findings from female populations (Sansone and Sansone 2011). In a Cochrane systematic review investigating the efficacy of different psychological treatments for BPD, only 11% of participants in the 28 studies reported in the review were male. Notably, the authors cautioned against generalising the findings to men while noting the exclusion of participants with antisocial features in most reported studies, which may have inadvertently excluded men with BPD (Stoffers‐Winterling et al. 2012).

Nevertheless, a randomised controlled trial (RCT) examining treatment outcomes for men with BPD and antisocial features suggested that DBT was an effective treatment approach for improving impulsivity and emotion regulation (Bianchini et al. 2019). These results supported by a subsequently published study reporting the efficacy of DBT in significantly reducing self‐harm, physical aggression, and criminal offending in men with comorbid BPD and ASPD (Wetterborg et al. 2020). Similar improvements for this population were noted in an earlier evaluation of MBT, evidenced by reductions in anger, frustration, paranoia, self‐harm, and suicide attempts (Bateman et al. 2016). Taken together, BPD‐specific treatments may have considerable utility for improving mental health outcomes in men with co‐morbid BPD and ASPD.

The Current Study

The overlap between ASPD and BPD, combined with discriminatory attitudes toward men with Cluster B disorders within healthcare systems, increases the risk of misdiagnosis. Such misclassification can result in poorer treatment outcomes, as men with BPD who are incorrectly diagnosed with ASPD are denied access to evidence‐based interventions that have demonstrated efficacy for BPD. Additionally, men with comorbid BPD and ASPD are at increased risk of violence towards self and others. Therefore, an appropriate distinction of the two diagnoses is necessary to ascertain risk and deliver individualised, effective treatments. Identifying and effectively treating men with PDs is a growing priority given the substantial distress and burden incurred by affected individuals, their families, and communities. However, there remains a lack of consensus regarding how best to distinguish BPD from ASPD in men and, having done so, what constitutes effective, evidence‐based treatment.

Although there are several previous studies describing patient experiences of living with PD, including men with PD (Broadbear et al. 2025; Cha et al. 2024; Dean et al. 2024; Treloar 2009), there is a notable gap in understanding clinicians’ experiences of working with this population. Young (2021)1 is the only such study exploring the perspectives of clinical psychologists in the UK through a discourse analysis, focusing on the social construction of PD in men. However, their study did not explore what constitutes effective treatment for men with PD. Moreover, while participants were required to have a minimum of 3 years’ experience as psychologists, the study did not account for their experience in working specifically with PD. Given the ambiguity associated with the rigour of discourse analysis, a more structured and targeted approach is warranted, especially one that captures the insights of clinicians from various disciplines with substantial expertise in diagnosing and treating men with PD, both in Australia and internationally.

In the current study, we interviewed a representative sample of clinicians with expertise working with men who have PD in community and forensic settings. Guided by their insights and recommendations, we aimed to answer the following questions:

Methods

Design and Participants

This study employed an interpretivist qualitative approach, using semi‐structured interviews (refer to Supporting Material 1) to elicit expert clinicians’ subjective experiences to explain what's already known and to generate new understandings (Reichertz 2014; Schwartz‐Shea and Yanow 2012). Eleven expert clinicians were invited to participate in the study. Ten completed video‐ and audio‐recorded interviews, while one clinician, due to employer restrictions, was interviewed without recording. As a result, their data could not be included in the analysis. Participant selection was informed by the investigators’ knowledge of each clinician's expertise, their national and international prominence in the field of PDs (publications, conference presentations, treatment innovation), extensive clinical experience with male patients, and peer recommendations (particularly for participants working in forensic settings). The investigators were employed as researchers and clinicians in a specialist PD service so were well positioned to recognise expertise in the field. Eligibility criteria required a minimum of 5 years’ experience in PD‐related clinical practice. Using a snowball sampling method, participants were briefed on the study's aims and interview questions prior to participation. Demographic and professional characteristics are summarised in Table 1. Notably, over half of the participants had more than ten years of experience in the field, and 80% had contributed to PD‐related research with implications for clinical practice and policy development. This study was reviewed and approved by the Eastern Health Human Research Ethics Committee (LR20/065/84772).

Data Collection

Individual interviews took place via a videoconferencing platform and were conducted by one of the authors (JB). Verbal consent was acquired prior to the interview, including consent to audio and video recording. The semi‐structured interview schedule was designed to explore several key themes identified from existing literature, highlighting the under‐representation of men with PD in mental health services. The interview questions specifically asked about: i. Factors likely to influence men's help‐seeking behaviours for PD‐related difficulties; ii. Diagnostic approaches to better differentiate BPD from ASPD in men; iii. Male‐specific barriers to treatment engagement and ways to address these in community and forensic settings; and iv. Recommendations regarding changes in treatment content and delivery that might better reflect the needs and expectations of men affected by PD. During the interviews, lines of questioning were adapted to delve deeper into areas of interest and clarify emergent themes. Each interview lasted between 60 and 90 min. Individual transcripts were offered to each participant for review.

Data Analysis

Interview data were prepared for analysis by transcribing all recordings verbatim, after which the transcripts were imported into the software programme NVivo 12 for data management and coding. After the initial transcription, transcripts were read and reread by one of the authors (MB) so that they could familiarise themselves with the data. Thematic analysis was conducted using a deductive–inductive approach (Fereday and Muir‐Cochrane 2006). This approach enabled the researchers to begin with well‐established knowledge and research questions, while remaining open to new insights emerging from the data.

To ensure methodological rigour, a deductive coding framework was developed. This framework, based on the study's research aims and interview questions, guided the identification of themes aligned with the study objectives. As a next step, the reliability of the codes was tested through independent coding by three researchers and subsequent comparison to ensure consistency and reduce bias. The coded data were then summarised, and initial themes were identified, capturing both anticipated and new patterns. The researchers then incorporated inductive codes to reflect participant insights not anticipated in the original framework, allowing for flexibility and in‐depth interpretation. The inter‐coder reliability scores ranged from κ = 0.75 to κ = 0.85, indicating substantial to almost perfect agreement between the reviewers and greater confidence in the results (Landis and Koch 1977). Finally, the researchers connected related codes and refined them into overarching themes through collaborative discussion and iterative analysis. The analysis was recursive in nature, involving repeated engagement with both the data and existing literature to confirm known concepts and identify novel findings. Findings were discussed as a team, discrepancies were resolved, and four themes that aligned with each of the study's aims were developed. Reflexive discussions about each researcher's positionality were held throughout the coding process, enhancing the credibility and dependability of the findings. Supporting Material 3 ‐ Reflexivity outlines the researchers’ positionality, offering context for their interpretive stance.

Results

A thematic analysis of interviews with expert clinicians led to the development of four core themes: (1) Getting Men Through the Door, (2) Unmasking Complexity, (3) Holding Steady in the Face of Risk, and (4) Addressing Shame Through Connection. Participants identified major barriers to assessment and treatment engagement for men with PD. They highlighted the frequent exclusion of men with PD from clinical services, suggested strategies to boost treatment engagement, and offered best practice recommendations. Theme One: Getting Men Through the Door

This theme revolves around stigma and masculinity, language and culture and systemic implications, which were seamlessly interwoven in the participants’ narratives.

Two participants described barriers and enablers to engaging men with PDs in treatment. They noted that men with PD are often difficult to engage and typically avoid contact with community mental health services. One participant (Clinician 3, C3) attributed this avoidance to men's negative attitudes toward mental health care and stigmatising beliefs about mental illness, both of which deter help‐seeking. To overcome these barriers, C3 recommended avoiding language that may trigger self‐stigma and instead framing treatment in terms of self‐improvement, personal growth, and interpersonal effectiveness:

So you have to try to organise so that you're not just telling them they're mentally ill and they're in the mental health service, …some will accept that without problem, but a lot even react to the term mentalisation because it's got mental in it and they say, “well I'm not having that”. So, you know, we advertise, …, you know, for, “do you have problems actually expressing yourself to other people?”, so they understand this sort of thing, “do you have problems with linking with others in a way that you find helpful?”

(C3)

The influence of cultural masculine ideals on men's willingness to engage in treatment was identified as a key barrier to access. C4 endorsed this view, emphasising the need to appeal to men's sense of self‐interest by framing treatment as an opportunity to regain control and mastery over their lives:

In terms of pitching it to personality‐disordered men, why would they want to come for treatment? Well, you've got to offer them something that they want, rather than something that you think they want. And I think often what they want is a, you know, a sense that their life will go the way that they want it to, not the way that other people want it to. So… so, really projecting it and pitching it as the opportunity to be your own master, rather than being bossed around by other people, the opportunity to… to have skills to manage things without causing yourself harm or defeating yourself or wreaking havoc. But… but not framed in the same way that you'll make other people's lives better as a result, I don't think they care about that. I think you've got to really… really picture that self‐interest.

(C4)

However, challenges particular to recruiting men with ASPD into treatment programmes were highlighted by C3, who discussed the need to raise awareness and recognition of ASPD across community services and professions, including among police officers and social services, to assist with engaging these men:

You've got to get other systems and workers … to recognise antisocial personality disorder, so you know, having a sort of how‐do‐you‐recognise‐them educational component is a good idea and so that probation or police system or the social services… or other groups can pick them up and say actually “is this what they're talking about?” because …, they just think this is a horrible bad person who should stop doing what he's doing… it alerts people that this is a problem that might be able to benefit from treatment.

(C3)

Participants identified a range of barriers that impede men with PD from engaging with mental health services, including stigma, mistrust, and culturally reinforced masculine ideals. Participants emphasised that traditional diagnostic language and therapeutic framing often alienate male patients, with terms like “mentalisation” triggering resistance due to misconstrued associations with weakness or pathology. Instead, participants recommended reframing treatment as a pathway to self‐improvement, which may appeal to men's sense of autonomy and self‐interest. The narratives identified in this theme also highlight the importance of systemic engagement, with suggestions to educate frontline professionals, such as police and probation officers, to better recognise antisocial traits and refer men to appropriate services.

Theme 2: Unmasking Complexity

Narratives in this theme describe two key ideas: clinicians’ experiences of diagnostic ambiguity and the process of uncovering the patient's emotional dynamics. These highlight the strategic and reflective nature of clinical practice, where accurate diagnosis requires moving beyond observable features to uncover the underlying psychological mechanisms that shape behaviour.

Four participants discussed challenges that arise when diagnosing BPD and ASPD in men, and the importance of making a detailed clinical assessment and formulation to ensure patients receive an accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment. One participant (C2) reflected on how men with PD tend to underreport the severity of their symptoms, suggesting that assessment clinics consider lowering their diagnostic threshold when conducting an assessment with men: “It may well be that for a lot of men, if they're actually asking for help, we should jump on that. Even if the severity of things might not seem as high”.

In their experience of pitfalls that exist around making a correct PD diagnosis in men, participants focused on the challenge of distinguishing between BPD and ASPD. Adding to this, C7 noted that BPD often goes undetected in men, particularly when it co‐occurs with antisocial behaviour:

I think clinicians working in this space are much more likely to see antisocial personality disorder than to see the function of the behaviour as trying to regulate emotion… but yeah, to me what's most important is obviously looking at the function of the behaviour and seeing you know, it might be violence, it might be substance abuse. But what is it doing for this person in this context, and are we really seeing somebody who has a lot of emotional dysregulation?

(C7)

C2 shared similar challenges and noted how men with PD often initially present with behaviours characteristic of ASPD. However, once clinicians engage more with them during assessment and treatment, they find that these behaviours are more reflective of BPD symptomology:

There's been some clients that people have really struggled to work with and … formulate, and it's only when they have shifted, I think, to a mindset that it's more … reflective of a borderline way of coping that suddenly these behaviours take on a different quality.

(C2)

Two participants who worked in forensic settings expressed scepticism towards the diagnosis of ASPD. They opined that in a lot of cases, ASPD has little explanatory value and offers limited insight into what would constitute effective treatment for the patient:

It seems to me that antisocial personality disorder doesn't say anything particularly meaningful… people who are breaking rules, people with this criminal background, people with conduct disorder, you know, that's people that we're working with, I guess, and so it starts to become something that's going to be [of] limited … value.

(C1)

The difficulties associated with distinguishing ASPD from BPD in men were frequently raised by participants. A detailed conceptualisation of their distinguishing differences was offered by C5, who had extensive experience treating both ASPD and BPD in men. Their first suggestion related to the importance of examining the patient's empathic functioning, developing a clear sense of how they position themselves in relation to others:

In borderline personality disorder, they tend to be…not necessarily over‐empathic, but they get absorbed into other people's emotional states very easily or very quickly, while that doesn't really happen in antisocial.

(C5)

In contrast with the BPD patient's tendency to over‐empathise with others, C5 went on to describe how men with ASPD tend to display little desire or capacity to empathise with others:

They tend to reduce their attempts of empathic understanding of others, so they don't bother much, and in that way they seem to be very self‐centred and self‐driven, in a sense, and expect other people to follow them, as it were, or to maybe see it from their point of view, but they certainly don't do it the other way round. Whereas that's rather different in borderline personality disorder, they'll try to get themselves empathised with but they'll also empathise with others, sometimes too much, so that's an area you want to look for.

(C5)

The second key difference described by C5 relates to how the person expresses their emotional frustration. They observed that men with ASPD are more likely to express their emotional frustration outwardly through aggressive behaviours:

The second area is the expression of their … emotional frustration and difficulties, and I think that in anti‐social it's more likely to be outwards and aggressive, an externalising sort of behaviour rather than self‐denigration, criticism, or self‐harm … So just look for it, just sort of more outwardly and so on, and when they're discussing things, they certainly don't show very much remorse and so on, …which is in the descriptions…but in BPD they do show [remorse], they wish it hadn't happened and it's all awful and things like that.

(C5)

Participants described significant challenges in accurately diagnosing BPD and ASPD in men, largely due to symptom overlap, high comorbidity, and gendered expressions of distress. Participants noted that men often underreport symptoms and present with externalising behaviours that may be misinterpreted as antisocial traits rather than indicators of emotional dysregulation. Several clinicians emphasised the importance of understanding the function of these behaviours, advocating for diagnostic formulations that consider underlying emotional processes rather than surface‐level conduct. While men with BPD may over‐identify with others’ emotions, men with ASPD may be self‐centred and lack reciprocal empathy, rendering assessment of empathic functioning as a key differentiator for both disorders. Participants also expressed scepticism about the utility of ASPD as a diagnostic label, arguing that it often lacks explanatory value and may lead to treatment exclusion.

Theme 3: Holding Steady in the Face of Risk

This theme explores the emotional and clinical complexities clinicians face when working with men with PD, including the impact of negative countertransference, the importance of accurately assessing and managing perceived versus actual risk, and the need for attitudinal shifts among clinicians to foster empathy and therapeutic openness.

When asked about barriers to treatment access, five participants described how men with PD are frequently excluded from treatment programmes due to the strong aversive emotional reactions their maladaptive behaviours can evoke in clinicians and service providers:

I feel like men with personality disorder miss out so much, probably because they present in a way that's not very likable, that makes people feel unsafe or that makes people think they're just an a**hole as opposed to a troubled person.

(C6)

As a mitigation strategy, participants emphasised the importance of clinicians appropriately reflecting upon and managing the various feelings that are likely to arise in themselves. They identified this as a key aspect in the therapeutic care of men with PD:

I think, with men, it's potentially the kind of aggressive interpersonal style that people find off‐putting, and so you know, having ways to effectively [recognise] that and be confident and manage that is probably quite key… it's really important for clinicians to think about how their own responses to that can impact the way that they relate to someone.

(C9)

Similar suggestions were offered by C7, who was employed in a forensic setting. They suggested maintaining an open and non‐judgmental attitude and reflecting upon one's pre‐conceived ideas when learning of the patient's offending history to manage negative countertransference:

It's important to be self‐aware, non‐judgemental, and open in your approach if you are interested in working with this group – having preconceived ideas is really unhelpful and counterproductive. If your attitude is open from the outset, in my experience its always possible draw out explanations… that allow you to adopt an empathetic and compassionate response.

(C7)

While participants recognised the importance of managing negative countertransference, they also spoke about times when feelings of fear and dread are legitimate:

There'll be some people that would be too… too violent or aggressive or too risky that we would simply say these are people who should… should probably only be seen in a correctional setting in terms of safety.

(C2)

Participants offered suggestions for mental health service providers who are considering working with men with PD who exhibit high‐risk behaviours. C1 emphasised the importance of the therapeutic setting, suggesting that larger organisations are better able to ensure therapist safety when engaging with patients who have a history of antisocial behaviour:

A big public organisation can handle them and can support an individual therapist. One to one, some of these clients just would provoke too much anxiety and the sense of risk, particularly, for stalkers, there's the risk that they become attached in negative ways to their therapist.

(C1)

As a precaution, participants suggested clinicians assess new patients for risk of aggression, using standardised measures that assess for antisocial traits, e.g. Psychopathy Checklist (PCL). Similarly, C9 suggested conducting risk assessment of prospective patients and spoke about the importance of obtaining collateral information regarding a patient's history of offending to ensure clinician safety:

I think if you're wanting to treat men with PD, if there is more of a history of externalised dysregulation and perhaps more of a history of things like violence or coercive control, or if there's other sort[s] of forensic history. I feel very strongly you must have a good sense of their risk and what that forensic history looks like.

(C9)

Participants shared the emotional and clinical challenges clinicians face when working with men with PDs. Participants described how maladaptive behaviours in male patients often evoke strong aversive emotional reactions, contributing to their exclusion from treatment programmes. Clinicians emphasised the importance of self‐awareness and reflective practice to manage countertransference, alongside the need for accurate risk assessment and organisational support to ensure safety. A shift in clinician attitudes—toward greater empathy and openness—was seen as essential for improving therapeutic engagement and outcomes.

Theme 4: Addressing Shame Through Connection

This theme encompasses clinicians’ reflections for tailoring treatment approaches for men with PD by building trust and managing defensiveness, addressing shame as a therapeutic focus, and considering gender composition in group therapy.

When reflecting on their experience of treating men with PD, three participants discussed ways in which men and women differ, and how these differences may inform their approach to treatment. C6 spoke about the importance of developing trust with men, and how this is a key requirement for establishing therapeutic rapport:

Look, I think the interpersonal dynamics with men in many respects are much more straightforward than they are with women…with men, once you work to establish their trust and they understand a bit about you and what you're there for, I mean, generally speaking, they're a really good judge of character in terms of if you're genuine or not… you know, there for the right reasons and genuinely wanting to offer support… then the rest is easy once you've got their trust you can you know, really utilise the group process to support them.

(C6)

While highlighting the importance of building trust and establishing rapport, participants noted that this often takes longer with men due to their defensiveness about engaging with services. Participants characterised men with PD as being highly defended against feelings of vulnerability, noting how this tendency creates many barriers and obstacles during the early stages of therapeutic engagement. When targeting these defences, C4 suggested that focusing on understanding and managing shame in men who experience PD is a key aspect in the therapeutic process:

Relationship building takes longer. Getting the developmental history takes a lot longer, because they're so defended, so that early part of engagement is the real challenge… They are seen by the criminal justice system as high risk, because they're not taking responsibility for their behaviours. But we're now beginning to recognise that they're not [taking responsibility] because it fills them with shame, so the denial is not because they don't accept that they've done something wrong, but doing so publicly is very shameful. So, the work on shame is an integral part of working with male PDs.

(C4)

Building on the importance of addressing shame in treatment, C7 emphasised the value of inspiring men early in the therapeutic process to foster self‐acceptance. They recommend delivering group therapy in male‐only formats, suggesting that mixed‐gender environments may hinder authentic self‐expression due to gender‐related social dynamics. In contrast, they noted male‐only groups as environments that promote peer connection, allowing men to inspire and learn from one another:

I also think that there's some real benefits to keeping men separate, men separate from women, perhaps early in treatment, to actually let them inspire each other. … not be trying to pay attention to those social issues that come up, and it's not just the heterosexual men and women, but just the gender issues that show up in mixed groups. You know, even gay men are still likely to show a different repertoire; they're going to act in some ways differently in front of women, it doesn't have to be a kind of sexual tension, it can be just… just gender again, gender differences.

(C7)

However, C4 shared a slightly different perspective on gender separation not being necessary for BPD groups, but as being more beneficial for ASPD groups:

I don't think you need to separate the genders, particularly the sex, you know, in BPD groups, we never have, and yeah, I mean, you can have some difficulties, but we've not done so, no. But I do, I mean, I think the anti‐social PD groups have worked better being all men than they would have done being mixed.

(C4)

Participants reflected on tailoring psychological treatment for men with PD by emphasising the importance of building trust and managing defensiveness—particularly during the early stages of treatment, when shame and vulnerability often present as significant barriers to therapeutic engagement. Addressing shame was seen as central to therapeutic progress, with participants noting that denial and avoidance may reflect deep discomfort rather than a lack of taking responsibility. Views on how best to allay this discomfort varied. Some clinicians advocated for male‐only group formats, suggesting that mixed‐gender environments may inhibit authentic self‐expression due to gender‐related social dynamics. Male‐only groups were seen to foster peer connection and self‐acceptance, particularly for men with ASPD.

Discussion

The goal of this study was to draw on the experiences of clinicians who had expertise in diagnosing and treating men with PD ‐ particularly BPD and ASPD ‐ given the high prevalence and visibility of these presentations in clinical and forensic settings. The purpose of the study was to develop guidance on how best to identify and engage this population in treatment, by utilising these expert insights. Based on our review of the literature and clinical experience of the investigators, the four aims of this study were to identify: i) Factors likely to influence men's help‐seeking behaviours for PD‐related difficulties; ii) Diagnostic approaches to better differentiate BPD from ASPD in men; iii) Male‐specific barriers to treatment engagement and ways to address these in community and forensic settings; and iv) Recommendations regarding changes in treatment content and delivery that might better reflect the needs and expectations of men affected by PD.

In alignment with the above aims, the four themes developed in this study offer a nuanced understanding of the clinical challenges and opportunities when working with men with PD. The findings highlight how socially constructed masculine ideals shape help‐seeking behaviours, how diagnostic ambiguity between BPD and ASPD can obscure treatment pathways, how clinicians need to navigate their own emotional responses and safety concerns, and how men's shame and defensiveness impact therapeutic engagement.

Adapting mental health recruitment strategies to reflect concepts relating to masculinity has been identified as an effective way of increasing help‐seeking among men experiencing mental health issues more broadly (Sagar‐Ouriaghli et al. 2019). The present study extends these recommendations by emphasising the importance of interagency collaboration to assist in the coordination of clinical pathways for men with PD. These suggestions focused on the need for staff training within community services that frequently encounter men experiencing PD, with law enforcement as a particular example.

In Australia, there are currently no specific policing guidelines pertaining to the management of people with PD. The Department of Health and Victoria Police Protocol for Mental Health (2010) only mentions PD as an example of someone who does not require involuntary treatment or does not have a mental illness. It is therefore not surprising that Victorian police officers frequently harbour the misconception that people with PD are excluded from the Victorian Mental Health Act (2022; Martin and Thomas 2015). If successfully implemented, such training will enable better identification and understanding of the underlying drivers of PD‐related behaviours, increasing the likelihood that men who exhibit symptoms consistent with PD will be referred to mental health services.

Expert advice from participants emphasised the importance of conducting a thorough longitudinal diagnostic assessment. This would likely improve the identification of BPD in men who have co‐occurring ASPD. The importance of this recommendation is supported by studies showing that in the absence of a BPD diagnosis, men with co‐occurring ASPD are often excluded from evidence‐based treatment, or from any treatment at all (Robitaille et al. 2017). Indeed, it is encouraging to note that treatment outcomes for men with co‐occurring BPD and ASPD reflect significant positive change following BPD‐focused interventions (e.g. Bateman et al. 2016; Wetterborg et al. 2020), further underscoring the importance of a comprehensive diagnostic assessment.

Some participants in our study expressed a degree of scepticism towards the diagnosis of ASPD and felt that it was often hastily given to men due to their history of criminal offending and unlikely to lead to effective treatment and support options. Participants recounted common experiences of diagnosing BPD in men who had initially received a diagnosis of ASPD, whose borderline‐like features only became apparent during prolonged therapeutic engagement. Given that BPD is primarily distinguished from ASPD through the presence of internalising symptoms (Anderson et al. 2022), it is important to understand these experiences in the context of masculine ideals (i.e. self‐reliance and emotional stoicism), which may inhibit men from emotionally engaging in therapy (Cochran 2005; Levnant, 1998). An important insight concerned the capacity of men with BPD to experience empathy, which may be absent in ASPD. This may be particularly important in forensic populations where co‐occurring psychopathy is more likely (Marsden et al. 2019).

The tendency to diagnose ASPD may also be due in part to the inherent limitations of the DSM‐5's categorical system for PDs, which is prone to significant symptom overlap (Bach et al. 2022). This makes it challenging to diagnose PDs, often leading to diagnostic confusion and subjective bias. The ICD‐11's approach to PD marks a significant shift from the categorical framework of previous diagnostic systems, adopting a dimensional model that prioritises the evaluation of psychosocial impairment and distress (Bach et al. 2022). This model promotes a more holistic assessment of personality dysfunction by recognising degrees of severity ‐ mild, moderate, and severe ‐ and enabling a nuanced characterisation of individual trait expressions (Bach and First 2018). By focusing on overall severity rather than discrete diagnostic categories, the ICD‐11 framework offers a promising alternative for addressing diagnostic overlap and comorbidity, thereby enhancing diagnostic clarity (Bach and First 2018). This diagnostic approach may be of particular benefit to men presenting with ASPD and BPD traits. Participants’ reflections on managing negative countertransference align with previous research documenting the strong emotional reactions clinicians often experience when treating patients with PD ‐ particularly patients with a history of antisocial behaviour (Sheehan et al. 2016; Van den Bosch et al. 2018). Such reactions can contribute to clinician reluctance or refusal to engage therapeutically with this population, underscoring the need for clinicians to have access to structured support and reflective practice to mitigate treatment exclusion.

Treatment exclusion of men with a history of antisocial behaviour is a serious problem, given the significant burden their behaviour may entail for society and themselves when left untreated (Quinsey et al. 1998). Participants’ broad recommendations that clinicians maintain an open and empathetic stance reflect accepted therapeutic principles for managing negative countertransference with patients who have a history of antisocial behaviour (Van den Bosch et al. 2018), which, when successfully managed, is positively related to outcomes (Hayes et al. 2011).

When discussing the management of negative countertransference, clinician participants emphasised that perceptions of threat are sometimes legitimate and require explicit risk management. Previous studies describe the high degree of suicide and self‐harm risk associated with having a PD diagnosis – BPD in particular ‐ and safety concerns that mental health professionals experience when working with these patients (Broadbear et al. 2020; Skodol et al. 2002). The suggestions participants offered for assessing risk (obtaining information from collateral sources, using standardised measures to assess antisocial personality traits) are consistent with guidelines for the management of ASPD (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence 2009). A notable insight offered by study participants was their belief that perceptions of risk were often overblown for men with PD, with clinician anxieties best managed through gaining more clinical experience and managing their own pre‐existing biases and beliefs. This is supported by previous studies showing that greater contact with patients who experience PD increases positive attitudes among clinicians and their willingness to work with this population (Egan et al. 2014; Sheehan et al. 2016).

There was notable agreement among participants regarding the content and structure of psychological treatment that may improve its efficacy for male patients. They pointed out the value of focusing on the development of trust and rapport, which, although it may take longer with male patients, was identified as the key to treatment engagement. The challenge of achieving this was attributed to men with PD being highly defended against feelings of vulnerability and shame (Osherson and Krugman 1990), requiring patience and open‐mindedness on the part of the clinician. The consensus regarding the benefits of mixed sex group treatment was BPD‐specific, with male‐only groups recommended for men with ASPD (Bateman 2022).

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength is the design of this study and its methodological rigour, achieved through a hybrid inductive–deductive approach to thematic analysis. Researchers commenced the study with well‐established knowledge and clearly defined research questions, which guided the development of a semi‐structured interview guide. This design provided sufficient structure to explore established concepts while allowing flexibility to uncover novel insights. The analytic process focused on the study's aims, while remaining open to new insights, enhancing both depth and relevance.

Nevertheless, there were some notable limitations. During the interviews, it became apparent that many participants used ‘men’ as shorthand for describing heterosexual male patients who exhibit gender‐role conformant behaviour. Therefore, the findings from the present study may not be representative of all people with PD who identify as men. Future research would benefit from exploring the experiences of clinicians who work with men who have BPD across diverse sexual orientations, including those who exhibit more traditionally feminine diagnostic features. Such investigations could deepen understanding of how intersecting identities shape clinical presentation, service engagement, and treatment outcomes, and help ensure that therapeutic approaches are inclusive and responsive to the needs of all men with PDs.

Having a single interviewer contributed to consistency across all interviews, ensuring uniformity in tone, pacing, and question delivery. However, this approach may have introduced limitations, such as repetitive probing or questions shaped by narratives shared in earlier interviews. In saying that, consistent with the interpretivist epistemology underpinning the study, the researchers acknowledge that their preconceptions and worldviews were inseparable from the research process and formed a critical aspect of interpreting the data (Pillow 2003). Reflexive discussions throughout the coding process strengthened the credibility and dependability of the findings.

Lastly, interview questions shared with participants beforehand may have influenced responses by prompting reflection or preparation, shaping their narratives. Nonetheless, this decision was made deliberately to support participants’ engagement, and enable them to draw on their considerable expertise, particularly given the sensitive nature of the topic and the demands on their time.

Conclusions

This study offers a novel and clinically grounded contribution to our understanding of diagnosis and treatment of PD in men, particularly when presenting with BPD and ASPD. By drawing on the insights of expert clinicians across psychology and psychiatry, the study illuminates the nuanced challenges of working with this population in both community and forensic settings. A key finding of this study is the importance of assessing empathic functioning as a potentially underutilised diagnostic marker for differentiating BPD from ASPD in men ‐ an insight that may help counterbalance the diagnostic overemphasis on externalising behaviours such as aggression and criminality. The study also highlights how masculine socialisation—including ideals of stoicism and self‐reliance—can obscure internalising symptoms and contribute to diagnostic misclassification, particularly in forensic contexts.

Expert clinicians described how shame and defensiveness serve as significant barriers to treatment engagement while offering strategies for reframing therapeutic language in order to appeal to men's sense of autonomy and self‐interest. The findings also underscore the importance of systemic engagement, including interagency collaboration and training for frontline professionals, to improve recognition and referral pathways for men with PD.

Importantly, the study challenges the utility of ASPD as a standalone diagnosis, with participants expressing scepticism about its explanatory value and its role in treatment exclusion. Instead, the findings advocate for a more nuanced, dimensional approach to diagnosis, such as that offered by the ICD‐11, which may better capture the complexity of PD presentations in men. Finally, the study contributes to treatment literature by emphasising the therapeutic importance of addressing shame, building trust, and considering gender composition in group therapy formats ‐ particularly the potential benefits of male‐only groups for men with ASPD. Taken together, these findings advance current knowledge by offering gender‐sensitive, contextually informed recommendations for assessment and intervention, highlighting the need for attitudinal and systemic shifts to improve outcomes for men with PD.
