A Multicenter Study of Pulmonary Critical Care Trainees’ Perception of Airway Management Training during Fellowship
Authors: Christopher Ghiathi, Anthony R. Lanfranco, Janae K. Heath
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is significant variability in airway management training among pulmonary and critical care medicine (PCCM) fellows.ObjectiveTo assess the airway management training of PCCM fellows, specifically evaluating the role of the institutional approach to intubations (anesthesia-predominant primary operators vs. PCCM-predominant) to the overall fellows’ educational experience.MethodsWe performed a survey of PCCM fellows at 14 institutions to assess the perceptions of airway management training. Summary statistics were used to analyze the Likert scale responses. Based on our hypothesis of a relationship of a fellow’s experience to the institutional approach to intubations, we stratified responses based on whether the primary operator for airway management for critically ill patients was generally anesthesiology or PCCM providers. Statistical significance was determined using either t testing (after converting Likert-style responses to continuous variables) or analysis of variance for categorical variables (P < 0.05).ResultsRespondents included 132 fellows across 14 PCCM programs (47% response rate), spanning all fellowship years (29% in first year [n = 38], 31% in second year [n = 41], 26% in third/fourth year [n = 34]). Seventy percent (n = 80) of fellows were very or somewhat satisfied with their airway management training. In fellows training where anesthesia providers primarily perform airway management (29% [n = 34]), satisfaction with training was significantly lower, as was the self-assessment of one’s airway management skills. The location of airway management experience in anesthesia-predominant programs was mainly the operating room (69% [n = 22]), versus mainly in the intensive care unit for PCCM-predominant programs (94% [n = 77]). The number of completed intubations was lower for fellows in anesthesia-predominant programs (P = 0.02).ConclusionWe found significant differences in fellows’ experiences in airway management based on the institutional approach to intubations (anesthesia-predominant primary operators vs. PCCM-predominant). Ongoing focus on strategies to optimize competency-based airway management training is needed, particularly at programs where anesthesia involvement limits the amount of clinical exposure.

Airway management and endotracheal intubation are essential skills for critical care physicians and deemed necessary components of pulmonary and critical care medicine (PCCM) fellowship training (1). Despite this, there is no consensus on optimal training approaches for airway management and endotracheal intubations in the fellowship setting (2). As a result, significant heterogeneity exists in exposure and training across PCCM programs for this potentially high-risk procedure (2–4).

Furthermore, these differences in training approaches have the potential to negatively impact patient care by promoting trainees through graduation who are not yet ready for independent practice in the domain of airway management. One survey (5) found up to 40% of fellows believed that they would not be competent to perform airway management independently after fellowship graduation, in part because of differences in fellowship training. Ensuring PCCM physicians demonstrate proficiency and competency in this procedure is of paramount importance to ensure optimal patient outcomes, and understanding the factors contributing to this variability and the intersection of this variability with the ultimate training experience is needed to better inform guidelines for training.

One potential source of training variability has been attributed to differences in institutional factors and culture, such as anesthesiology procedural dominance within an institution. A recent survey analyzing intubation experiences by PCCM fellows highlighted this factor, finding that the anesthesia team served as the primary operator for airway management in nearly a quarter of respondents (3). To date, however, impact of the institutional approach to intubations (anesthesia-predominant primary operators vs. PCCM-predominant) to the overall fellows’ educational experience has not been thoroughly evaluated.

Therefore, our objective was to assess the experiences of PCCM fellows in airway management and endotracheal intubation via a multiinstitutional survey, stratifying responses based on the institutional approach to intubations (anesthesia-predominant primary operators vs. PCCM-predominant).

Methods

We performed a survey to assess the perceptions of airway management training in PCCM fellowships. The survey tool (see Table E1 in the online supplement) was developed by the authors, and reviewed by experts in survey design, medical education, and procedural teaching. Survey domains included: 1) current curricular approaches to airway management; 2) airway management experience (across endotracheal intubations, supraglottic airways, etc.); and 3) perceived importance of endotracheal intubation. The survey also assessed where airway management experiences occurred (intensive care unit vs. operating room) and the primary operator (anesthesia team vs. PCCM team). Finally, to assess an individual’s perceived competency, the survey tool analyzed self-assessed competency across various PCCM procedures using a 4-point entrustment scale (1 = “I do not feel comfortable performing this procedure even with supervision”; 2 = “I can perform this procedure with direct supervision”; 3 = “I can perform this procedure with a supervisor available to be called into the procedure if needed”; and 4 = “I can perform this procedure independently.”)

Before dissemination, the survey was pilot tested across representative fellows (within PCCM) at the University of Pennsylvania and the University of California San Francisco, focusing on clarity of survey items as well as technical issues related to the survey platform. The survey was iteratively adjusted based on feedback before dissemination. Participants in the pilot testing were not included in the dataset.

The survey was disseminated via an online platform (RedCap) to PCCM fellows across 14 institutions in September 2021. The included programs were purposely selected to capture representation across diverse geographic regions and fellowship sizes (see Table E2 for list of surveyed programs). Subsequent reminder e-mails were sent at weekly intervals over 4 weeks to enhance the response rate.

Summary statistics were used to analyze the Likert scale responses (6, 7), including descriptive analyses of curricular approaches to airway management, fellows’ procedural experiences, and perceived importance of an airway management skill set to ultimate practice. Because the scope of the survey analyzed curricular experiences as well as self-assessments, the survey was sent to all fellows in training. However, as our sample included fellows spanning multiple years in training, we did a sensitivity analysis excluding fellows in their first year in training, given the potential for a fellow without significant airway exposure biasing the results.

To assess the relationship to the institutional approach to intubations, we stratified responses based on whether the primary operator for airway management for critically ill patients was generally anesthesiology or PCCM within an institution. This was defined within a given institution based on answers to the question “In your medical intensive care unit, how frequently is an anesthesia team performing intubations for critically ill patients without coronavirus disease (COVID-19)?” Fellows who responded “always” or “very often” were grouped as training at “anesthesia-predominant” programs, whereas other responses were grouped as training at “PCCM-predominant” programs.

Statistical significance was determined using either t testing (after converting several Likert-style responses to continuous variables) or analysis of variance for categorical variables, using a P value of 0.05 for statistical significance. Given the exploratory nature of the study (and to minimize the risk of type 2 error), the P value was not adjusted for multiple comparisons.

The study was determined to be exempt from review by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board.

Results

A total of 132 fellows across 14 PCCM programs (47% response rate) completed the survey. Some survey results have been previously published in the form of an abstract (8). Respondents were predominantly from academic medical centers (97% [n = 109]), spanning all fellowship years (29% in first year [n = 38], 31% in second year [n = 41], 26% in third/fourth year [n = 34]) (Table 1).

The majority of respondents (n = 104 [88%]) strongly agreed that airway management was an important skill set for their future career, which did not differ based on year in training or career trajectory (research focused or clinical focused).

Seventy percent (n = 80) of PCCM fellows described being very or somewhat satisfied with their airway management training. In fellows training at institutions where airway management is predominantly managed by anesthesia (29% [n = 34]), satisfaction with training was significantly lower than in other programs.

Fellows described educational experiences across a range of airway management skills, including endotracheal intubation (92% [n = 121]), laryngeal mask airways (83% [n = 110]), bag mask ventilation (88% [n = 117]), and administration of medications (84% [n = 111]). Opportunities for education in these discrete skills did not differ between program types.

Fellows also reported a variety of curricular approaches to airway training, including simulation (80% [n = 106]), cadaver experiences (26% [n = 34]), lectures (73% [n = 96]), and online content (30% [n = 40]). The curricular approaches varied between PCCM-predominant and anesthesia-predominant programs. Specifically, availability of cadaver training programs was more common in anesthesia-predominant programs (33% [n = 32] vs. 6% [n = 2]; P < 0.001). It was more common for programs that were anesthesia predominant to report no designated airway management curriculum (6% [n = 2] vs. 0% [n = 0]; P = 0.02) compared with PCCM-predominant programs. There were no significant differences between simulation and lecture-based content between programs.

The location of airway management experience in anesthesia-predominant programs was mainly the operating room (69% [n = 22]), versus mainly in the intensive care unit for PCCM-predominant programs (94% [n = 77]). The number of reported intubations was lower for those in anesthesia-predominant programs than PCCM-predominant programs (P = 0.02).

Fellows perceived an overall lower degree of competency when performing endotracheal intubations (mean ± standard deviation, 2.77 ± 0.90) compared with all other procedures (Table 2). Although there were not significant differences in the perceived degree of competency of other commonly performed PCCM procedures, such as bronchoscopy, arterial line placement, central venous catheter placement, or thoracentesis, there was a significant difference in the self-assessment of one’s airway management skills between programs that were PCCM predominant versus anesthesia predominant in airway management (Table 2). There was increasing perceived competence with increasing fellowship years in training for all procedures, including endotracheal tubes.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that there are significant differences in fellows’ experiences in airway management based on the institutional approach to intubations (anesthesia-predominant primary operators vs. PCCM-predominant). Specifically, we found lower fellow satisfaction with training and fellow self-assessments of competency in programs where the anesthesia team serves as the primary operator of airway management. This represents a significant source of training variability across PCCM fellowship programs, further highlighting the need for a robust, standardized educational approach in airway management, particularly at programs where anesthesia involvement limits the amount of clinical exposure for PCCM fellows.

Beyond the fellows’ satisfaction and self-assessments, we also found notable differences in overall hands-on experience between programs. In anesthesia-predominant programs, the majority of airway management experience by fellows was in the operating room, as opposed to the intensive care unit for PCCM-predominant programs. There were also significant differences in total numbers of completed endotracheal intubations during training between programs. Although there is no guidance within PCCM on the number or location of intubations required to ensure trainees are ready for independent practice, these large differences are striking and have not been reported in the literature previously.

These findings have significant ramifications for fellows in training at anesthesia-predominant programs, noting the unique challenges to airway management within the intensive care units (including higher incidence of complications and higher failure rates) (9, 10). Although there is no designated number of endotracheal intubations necessary to be deemed competent by the ACGME for PCCM trainees, prior studies have endorsed a minimum number of endotracheal intubations in emergency medicine, ranging from 50 to more than 200 intubations to achieve competency, which fellows at anesthesia-predominant programs are well below (11, 12). Ensuring training opportunities for fellows within anesthesia-predominant programs is critical to ensure fellows are prepared for independent practice at the conclusion of fellowship training. Furthermore, ensuring PCCM fellows are able to demonstrate competency in the unique context of managing critically ill patients is an important facet of patient safety.

Institutional variations in the performance of endotracheal intubation and other airway management skills have been previously described in the literature (5). However, to our knowledge, this is the first publication performing a multicenter comparison of the educational experience, satisfaction, and perceived skill of PCCM fellows between anesthesia-predominant and PCCM-predominant programs. Our research reaffirmed prior work by Brady and colleagues, which suggested that a significant minority of PCCM fellows are training at anesthesia-predominant programs (5). Yet, our research adds to this finding, showcasing that those differences have significant impacts on fellows’ training and experience.

To address the educational needs of fellows at anesthesia-predominant programs, further understanding of the drivers of these experiential differences is needed. It remains unclear if the educational focus by programs should be on the curricular offerings or optimizing the hands-on experiences of trainees. Regardless, these differences highlight the need for a robust and multifaceted airway management curriculum across diverse institutions and should incorporate concepts of deliberate practice, escalating difficulty, and spaced repetition in keeping with simulation best practice (13).

Limitations

There were several limitations to our work. One limitation of our study is generalizability, as we surveyed fellows training within large academic institutions, specifically during the COVID-19 pandemic, resulting in significant changes to educational approaches and intubation practice patterns. Of our respondents, we were unable to account for the total amount of clinical time fellows completed during fellowship, which ranged between 18 and 30 months depending on the institution and fellowship track (research vs. clinical). Similarly, we were not able to account for variability in critical care rotations or differential clinical expectations across various critical care rotations, which may vary between and within institutions. Our study also may miss variability within institutions that may be driven by some of these educational differences, such as quality of teaching, feedback, and assessment provided, as well as patient population, for which we are unable to adjust. Beyond this, our study was also limited to combined pulmonary–critical care programs (and did not assess critical care medicine–only programs or the impact of the coexistence of these programs on PCCM fellows’ experience), which may result in different findings.

Furthermore, our survey was limited by potential biases due to low response rate, although our response rate of 47% is considered to be a sufficient response rate in physician surveys, without significant risk of this bias (14). Our findings are also subject to recall bias, particularly among upper-year fellows. In addition, there were also gender differences in our respondents, with more women represented in the anesthesia-predominant programs. There is recent evidence to suggest gender differences in procedural opportunities, which could manifest in our dataset focused on intubation opportunities (15). Finally, although our research demonstrates a difference in learners’ attitudes, satisfaction, and perceived skill via self-assessments, it is known that there is a poor correlation between self-assessments and true competency (16). Importantly, fellows do not have a “gold standard” by which to compare their training experience, which further impacts their self-assessment of training quality. Unfortunately, we were unable to assess the true procedural competency of respondents. Linking airway management training approaches to actual procedural skill would be an important area of future work.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates there are significant difference in fellows’ experience in airway management based on the institutional approach to intubations. Fellows at anesthesia-predominant programs were less satisfied with their airway management experience and felt less prepared to perform intubations independently than fellows at PCCM-predominant programs. Fellows at anesthesia-predominant programs reported performing fewer intubations overall and did a larger proportion of their intubations in the operating room than fellows at PCCM-predominant programs. These findings highlight the need for a unique strategy for diverse institutions to ensure fellows achieve airway management competency, particularly at programs where anesthesia involvement limits the amount of clinical exposure.
