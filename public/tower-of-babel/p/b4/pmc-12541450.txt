Freezing enhances CO2 mineralization in Ca2+-containing solutions
Authors: Minghao Sun, Dong Gao, Bowen Li, Joseph S. Francisco, Guoying Bai, Xinxing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThis study demonstrates that frozen solution acts as a self-amplifying carbon sink. We reveal a cryogenic acceleration mechanism on atmospheric CO2 mineralization where freezing greatly accelerates CaCO3 crystal formation. In artificial seawater, nearly 4 times carbon is trapped permanently as stable CaCO3 upon freezing—a process that occurs across polar oceans. These findings highlight that global warming driven by excess CO2 emission contributes to ice melting, potentially impairing critical carbon sequestration systems (e.g., polar seawater), further intensifying global warming.

Since the mid-19th century, emissions of CO2, the major anthropogenic greenhouse gas, have risen steadily, driving severe global warming and climate change (1–3). Restoring balance between carbon sequestration and emissions has become an important research focus (4). It is critical to fully uncover the hidden natural carbon sinks to acquire an accurate estimation of the global carbon budget. For instance, mineral ion-rich aqueous systems, such as seawater, salt lakes, hot springs, and alpine lakes, can serve as efficient carbon sinks not only by physically dissolving atmospheric CO2 but also by permanently immobilizing them into stable minerals such as CaCO3 (5–7). However, the factors influencing these natural carbon sinks remain relatively underexplored.

A significant portion of Earth’s water systems, including polar seas and alpine lakes, frequently experience freezing (8). Reaction rates generally decrease at lower temperatures according to the Arrhenius equation, but often deviate unexpectedly upon freezing (9, 10). Rather than an extrapolation of the Arrhenius plot of above-freezing data, the reaction rates under freezing conditions can decrease more or less than the Arrhenius values or even increase and surpass the room-temperature rates in some cases (11, 12). Furthermore, freezing can confer unique reaction selectivity and controllability (13, 14). The underlying mechanisms are often attributed to the freezing-concentration effect (15), cage effect (16, 17), electric potential caused by imbalanced ion distribution (18, 19), or catalytic effect of ice surface (14, 20, 21). Therefore, investigating how freezing impacts the atmospheric CO2 mineralization in mineral ion-rich aqueous systems is crucial for refining our understanding of the global carbon budget.

Herein, utilizing model systems such as Ca(OH)2 solutions, binary NaCl–CaCl2 solutions, and artificial seawater, we investigate the impact of freezing on atmospheric CO2 mineralization, revealing a counterintuitive acceleration. In Ca(OH)2 solution, the mineralization acceleration depends on the parameters influencing the concentrations of the reactants confined within the boundaries of ice crystals, including quenching temperature (the temperature used for freezing) and annealing temperature (the temperature used for maintaining the icy state). Based on these results and the observation on multistage evolution of CaCO3 crystal number and size, we propose the acceleration mechanism as follows: Freezing enhances nucleation by concentrating reactants within the crystal boundaries, which causes supersaturation and lowers the nucleation energy barrier; after thawing, the mineralization is also accelerated due to the barrier-free crystal growth, which is a result of lower supersaturation caused by the enhanced reactant consumption in the previous frozen stage. In artificial seawater, CO2 mineralization under freezing conditions sequesters nearly 4 times as much carbon as that under non-freezing conditions, suggesting that oceans, as one of the major natural carbon sinks (22), have been underestimated in calculating global carbon sink. We also estimate that in the Arctic Ocean during March, the additional permanent CO2 sequestration induced by freezing could reach 291 million tons, accounting for approximately 0.48% of the total regional atmospheric CO2 content.

Results and Discussion

Freezing-Induced Acceleration of Atmospheric CO2 Mineralization in Ca(OH)2 Solutions.

Fig. 1A shows the experimental procedures of atmospheric CO2 mineralization in Ca(OH)2 solutions undergoing different treatments. Briefly, the freshly prepared Ca(OH)2 solutions were subjected to two different treatments: One involved freezing and thawing (referred to as FT)—freezing the solution in liquid nitrogen followed by thawing in a 25 °C water bath, while the other maintained the solution stagnant in a 25 °C water bath without freezing (referred to as Non-FT). Due to the strong basicity of Ca(OH)2, both FT and Non-FT spontaneously captured atmospheric CO2, leading to reduced concentrations of the free ions (Ca2+ and OH−) in the solution. Immediately after the FT or Non-FT process, the solution conductivities of both samples were measured to assess the rates of atmospheric CO2 mineralization. This entire process was referred to as a single cycle.

The conductivity changes of the Ca(OH)2 solutions with five repeated FT or Non-FT cycles are shown in Fig. 1B. The conductivity of FT was lower than that of Non-FT after every cycle. After 5 cycles, white precipitates were observed in both solutions. Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S1) demonstrated that they were crystalline calcite CaCO3. These results suggest that freezing accelerates the mineralization of atmospheric CO2. The number and size variations of the CaCO3 crystals were also investigated. As shown in SI Appendix, Fig. S2, the number of CaCO3 crystals per unit solution volume was counted via an optical microscope (Materials and Methods). After the first cycle, the number of CaCO3 crystals for FT was approximately 5.8 × 104 mm−3, while for Non-FT it was 2.4 × 104 mm−3 (Fig. 1C). After the fifth cycle, the number for FT increased to 16.1 × 104 mm−3, higher than 11.0 × 104 mm−3 observed for Non-FT. The size of the CaCO3 crystals in solution was measured by the dynamic light scattering (DLS) method (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). After each cycle, the average size of CaCO3 crystals for FT was at least 20% larger than that for Non-FT (Fig. 1D). Therefore, the acceleration of CO2 mineralization by freezing can be reflected by the enhancement of both the number and size of the CaCO3 crystals.

Compared to Non-FT, FT underwent not only the phase change from liquid to solid (icing) but also the temperature decrease (cooling). To figure out which one was accounted for the acceleration, we investigated the effects of icing and cooling, respectively. To study the effect of the cooling temperature, we performed Non-FT at temperatures of 25 °C, 15 °C, 5 °C, and −5 °C. At −5 °C, the Ca(OH)2 solution was supercooled and did not freeze. As shown in Fig. 1E, above 0 °C, the rate of decrease of the Ca(OH)2 solution’s conductivity (k, the negative of conductivity-cycle slope) increased as the temperature dropped, suggesting that the mineralization rate of atmospheric CO2 increased with temperature decreasing when the temperature was above 0 °C, which can be ascribed to the increased CO2 solubility at lower temperatures (23). However, when the temperature further decreased to −5 °C, k went down, indicating a decrease in the mineralization rate. The k value for FT (the temperature for FT is far below −5 °C) was larger than those for all the Non-FT temperatures, demonstrating that the cooling effect was not the main reason for the acceleration. To study the icing effect, two supercooled (−6.0 °C) Ca(OH)2 solutions were treated differently—one was triggered to freeze by introducing a metal rod that was previously cooled in liquid nitrogen, while the other was kept in the supercooled state. After each cycle, much lower conductivity value was detected for the frozen solution (Fig. 1F). These results suggest that icing was the major factor that accelerated the mineralization.

Factors Affecting Atmospheric CO2 Mineralization Acceleration.

We investigated the atmospheric CO2 mineralization dependence on the quenching temperature (Tquenching), annealing temperature (Tannealing), and CO2 desublimation. The quenching experiment involved freezing the solutions at a specific Tquenching followed by thawing, whereas the annealing experiment included an additional step of maintaining the frozen state for some time at a specific Tannealing apart from the freezing and thawing steps (see Materials and Methods for the details).

For the quenching experiment, SI Appendix, Fig. S4 shows the conductivity change with repeated FT cycles at different Tquenching. As Tquenching increased from −196 °C to −20 °C, k first decreased and then increased, with the minimum value occurring at −60 °C (Fig. 2A). As Tquenching increased, the ice grain size increased, and the grain boundary density decreased (Fig. 2B). This resulted in a higher concentration of the reactants because ions tended to be dissolved in the liquid water left in the ice grain boundaries (SI Appendix, Fig. S5) (24). We calculated the concentration degree as the ratio of the reactant concentration within ice grain boundaries to that before freezing (see SI Appendix, Fig. S6 and Table S3 for calculating details). In addition, since CO2 desublimation occurs below −56.6 °C at atmospheric pressure, we also investigated the influence of Tquenching on CO2 desublimation degree, which was the ratio of post-FT-cycle conductivity to the initial conductivity of pure water. (SI Appendix, Table S4). As shown in Fig. 2C, with Tquenching increasing, concentration degree increased while the desublimation degree decreased. The opposite trends contributed to the inverted pyramid plot of k versus Tquenching. Fig. 2D describes the relationship between k and the two factors, where the non-zero coefficients of concentration degree and desublimation degree indicate the combined influences of freezing-concentration effect and CO2 desublimation on CO2 mineralization rate. SI Appendix, Fig. S7 shows the correlation between k and concentration degree with Tquenching above the CO2 desublimation temperature.

For the annealing experiment, Fig. 2 E–G shows k, ice crystal images, concentration degree, and desublimation degree at various Tannealing. At higher Tannealing, concentration degree tended to be higher because ice crystals grew larger at higher Tannealing (SI Appendix, Table S5), so the conductivity of Ca(OH)2 solution decreased more rapidly (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). However, desublimation degree did not vary (SI Appendix, Table S6) because Tannealing was higher than the desublimation temperature of CO2. Therefore, the positive correlation between k and concentration degree was observed but the coefficient of desublimation degree was 0 (Fig. 2H). In addition, the coefficient of concentration degree in Fig. 2H, 5.9, was higher than the 2.6 value derived from the correlation corresponding to Tquenching above the CO2 desublimation point in the quenching experiment (SI Appendix, Fig. S7), suggesting that the contribution of concentration degree was more prominent in the annealing experiment compared to the quenching experiment. This difference can be attributed to the fact that the annealing experiments had longer freezing time.

The freezing-concentration mechanism can be further demonstrated by the conductivity change and kFT/kNon-FT with the increase of initial Ca(OH)2 concentrations (Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Under all the tested concentrations, kFT/kNon-FT was larger than 1, suggesting the universality of freezing-induced acceleration. Importantly, kFT/kNon-FT increased first (region I) and then decreased (region II). In region I, where the Ca(OH)2 concentration was relatively low, the acceleration induced by freezing-concentration enhanced. In region II, where the initial Ca(OH)2 concentration was high, even with the freeze-concentration effect, the increased concentration in the grain boundaries was not the rate-determining factor, so the mineralization rate increase for FT slowed down (SI Appendix, Fig. S9J). This variation is consistent with previous reports that the acceleration is weakened when the concentration is high (25).

Mechanism of Freezing-Induced Atmospheric CO2 Mineralization Acceleration.

We further investigated in detail the CaCO3 formation process during a single cycle of FT and Non-FT by monitoring the solution conductivity and the number and size of CaCO3 crystals with an optical microscope. As shown in Fig. 4A, the rate of decrease in conductivity of FT became smaller after thawing but was still larger than that of Non-FT, suggesting that the influence of freezing continued after thawing. We compared the number and size variations of CaCO3 crystals for FT and Non-FT (Fig. 4B). For Non-FT, crystals began to form immediately as the reaction started, and after 2 min, the rate of crystal quantity increase slowed down. In contrast, no CaCO3 crystal for FT was observed by the microscope during the frozen state of Ca(OH)2 solution. However, at 2 min when the Ca(OH)2 solution began to melt, a few observable CaCO3 crystals appeared, and then, the number of observable CaCO3 crystal increased rapidly, reaching twice that for Non-FT eventually. Similarly, the size of observable CaCO3 crystals for FT was not larger than that for Non-FT until 4 min. These observations suggest that 1) CaCO3 existed in the form of tiny crystal nuclei that could not be observed by an optical microscope before melting. The Raman spectra (Fig. 4C) of the freezing-dried Ca(OH)2 solution sample in the ice grain boundary showed the characteristic CaCO3 peak at 1086 cm−1, verifying that tiny CaCO3 crystals indeed formed during the frozen state. 2) There must be other factors that accelerated CO2 mineralization after melting.

Based on the above results, the mechanism of the freezing-enhanced CO2 mineralization is illustrated in Fig. 4D. For FT, when the Ca(OH)2 solution freezes (stage 1), the ice crystals exclude the solute ions into the confined ice grain boundary, where water remains as liquid. This is the freezing-concentration effect. As a result, a large quantity of small CaCO3 crystal nuclei form because of lower nucleation barrier resulted from higher supersaturation. At this stage, the reactants are mainly consumed for nucleation but less for crystal growth, leaving the crystals too small to be observed by the microscope. After melting (stage 2), the tiny CaCO3 crystals grow larger and quickly become observable by an optical microscope. For Non-FT, much fewer nuclei form in the initial stage without the freezing-concentration effect. Additionally, since less reactants are consumed in stage 1 for Non-FT, new nucleus formation is sustained in stage 2, which can be reflected by the wider size distribution of the CaCO3 crystals (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). These factors make the crystal growth in Non-FT lag behind FT, with larger CaCO3 crystals observed in FT than in Non-FT (Fig. 4B). Because nucleus formation needs to surmount the nucleation barrier but crystal growth is barrier-free, the rate at which the reactants are consumed in stage 2 of Non-FT is slower than that of FT. Therefore, apart from the direct consequence of stage 1 that more nucleus formation accounts for the faster CO2 mineralization in FT, the tendency that the reaction is more likely to focus on the barrier-free growth also results in faster CO2 mineralization at stage 2.

To summarize, freezing optimizes the nucleation and crystal growth processes toward the mineralization-favored direction by directly or indirectly impacting reactant concentration and energy barrier at the different stages. This picture of nucleation and growth dominance at different stages in FT can be expressed as a “synchronous mode,” which is more favored for crystal formation than the “asynchronous mode” in Non-FT.

Freezing-Induced Atmospheric CO2 Mineralization in Complex Natural Carbon Sinks.

We conducted experiments using binary NaCl–CaCl2 solutions of a series of pH values and artificial seawater. For the binary NaCl–CaCl2 solution system at pH 8.5, no significant CaCO3 crystal was observed in either Non-FT or FT after a short period (e.g., 48 h) (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). This can be attributed to the limited CO32− formation under this pH. At pH 10.0, freezing induced CaCO3 crystal formation within 48 h, while very few crystals were observed in Non-FT (SI Appendix, Fig. S12). At pH 10.5, freezing led to obvious CaCO3 crystal formation after just 12 h, with increase in both the number and size of crystals over time (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). In contrast, Non-FT produced a small amount of CaCO3 crystals even after 48 h. At pH 11.0, both FT and Non-FT produced CaCO3 crystals within a relatively short period (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). However, the crystal number in FT was much larger than that in Non-FT. Furthermore, the crystal number and size showed a steady upward trend over time in FT, but in Non-FT, the crystal number remained low, and the crystal size displayed a very wide distribution with no obvious increase.

For the artificial seawater, CaCO3 crystal formation was insensitive to pH variations within the range of 7.5 to 8.5 (SI Appendix, Figs. S15 and S16), which encompassed most reported global ocean pH values (26). Typical optical microscope images of the artificial seawater samples after different periods showed that CaCO3 crystal number in FT increased more rapidly than that in Non-FT (Fig. 5A). The crystal size in FT showed a slight yet steady increase, with a narrower size distribution compared to the sparse and dispersed distribution observed in Non-FT (SI Appendix, Fig. S16). To quantitate the CO2 sequestered, the change of Ca2+ concentration with time was monitored (Fig. 5B and SI Appendix, Fig. S17, see Materials and Methods for the Ca2+ concentration measurement). It was evident that for both FT and Non-FT, the Ca2+ concentrations initially decreased and reached a plateau, and the Ca2+ concentration in FT decreased more rapidly and reached a lower plateau compared to Non-FT. Given that each Ca2+ ion consumed one CO2 molecule (SI Appendix, Fig. S18), the mass of CO2 sequestered permanently through mineralization was determined by the concentration change of Ca2+. The CO2 sequestered in FT was about 4 times that in Non-FT (Fig. 5B).

We next expand the carbon sink estimation to a large scale. Taking the Arctic Ocean as an example, sea ice volume can be roughly calculated by multiplying the sea ice area by its depth (27). The obtained distribution of sea ice volume is illustrated in Fig. 5C. The increment of CO2 sequestration resulting from freezing can be estimated by expanding the above laboratory data in Fig. 5B. The estimation considered the total ice but not merely the surface ice layer, because this freezing-induced acceleration occurred not only at the ice–air interface (SI Appendix, Fig. S19). As shown in Fig. 5D, the ice on every km2 of Arctic Ocean surface could additionally fix up to 87.5 tons of CO2 into CaCO3. The mass of CO2 sequestered permanently in sea ice across the Arctic Ocean in March 2024 was approximately calculated to be 4 × 108 tons, 4 times that sequestered in equivalent mass of seawater (Fig. 5E). The monthly average incremental CO2 sequestration in 2024 due to freezing accounted for approximately 0.31% of the total CO2 content (Fig. 5F) in the atmosphere above the Arctic Ocean (14.09 × 106 km2) (28–31). This value is much larger than the current monthly increase rate of atmospheric CO2, which is 0.06% observed in 2024 (31). It is important to note that the estimates were made without considering complex factors such as wind and ocean currents. Despite this limitation, our findings do demonstrate the potential contribution of freezing to natural carbon sinks.

Conclusion

In summary, we reveal the role of freezing in accelerating the mineralization of atmospheric CO2 in various Ca2+-containing solutions. The acceleration is influenced by the parameters such as quenching temperature, annealing temperature, and initial concentration of reactants in solution. Based on the impacts of freezing on CaCO3 nucleation and growth, a crystal formation-favored nucleation and growth mechanism is proposed. These findings not only provide insights into the counterintuitive reaction rates at subfreezing temperatures but also offer a view for recalculating the global carbon budget. This study also indicates that large-scale ice melting under continued global warming could compromise this natural carbon sequestration pathway upon freezing, potentially leading to atmospheric CO2 concentrations that surpass current climate models’ estimates.

Materials and Methods

Materials.

Ca(OH)2 (AR, ≥95%) and Na2SO4 (AR, ≥99%) were purchased from Energy Chemical. NaCl (AR), CaCl2·2H2O (AR), KCl (GR), and NaHCO3 (AR) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. MgCl2·6H2O (AR) and NaOH (Ph. Eur., BP, NF, ≥98%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt dihydrate (EDTA-2Na·2H2O, AR, >98%) was purchased from Dalian Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Calconcarboxylic acid (Indicator) was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. Phenolphthalein (Indicator) was purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 37%) was purchased from Tianjin Kemiou Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Polyethersulfone membrane was purchased from Tianjin Jinteng Experiment Equipment Co., Ltd. Milli-Q ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ cm) was used for all experiments.

FT and Non-FT Processes for Ca(OH)2 Solutions.

First, a 0.2 mg mL−1 of Ca(OH)2 solution was prepared and filtered by a polyethersulfone membrane with the pore size of 0.1 μm, and the initial Ca(OH)2 solution conductivity was measured at 25 °C. The filtration step was performed to remove impurities and sporadic CaCO3 crystals that may have formed, ensuring a uniform starting point for the reactions in both FT and Non-FT. Afterward, a 3 mL aliquot of Ca(OH)2 solution was transferred to a test tube for the FT or Non-FT experiment. For FT, the Ca(OH)2 solution was first immersed in liquid nitrogen for 1 min to ensure complete freezing, following which the frozen solution was transferred to a water bath maintained at 25 °C and left undisturbed for 7.5 min to thaw. For Non-FT, the Ca(OH)2 solution was maintained stagnant in a 25 °C water bath for 8.5 min. Following either the FT or Non-FT process, the conductivity of both solutions was immediately measured to assess the mineralization rate of atmospheric CO2. Subsequently, the samples underwent additional cycles of FT or Non-FT. Other FT and Non-FT experiments were the same as those described above, but different freezing parameters (such as Tquenching, Tannealing, and the duration for maintaining the ice state) and different experimental samples [Ca(OH)2 solution, pure water, binary NaCl–CaCl2 solution, or artificial seawater] were used.

Cooling and Icing Effect Measurements.

To study the effect of the cooling temperature, we performed Non-FT at different temperatures (25 °C, 15 °C, 5 °C, and −5 °C) and FT with the temperature of −196 °C. To study the icing effect, two test tubes containing 3 mL of Ca(OH)2 solutions were immersed in an ethanol bath for 5 min to obtain the supercooled state (−6 °C). One of the samples was then triggered to freeze by introducing a metal rod that was previously cooled in liquid nitrogen (time of this process: 0.5 min), after which the frozen solution was transferred to a 25 °C water bath to thaw for 7 min. The other supercooled sample was placed in a 25 °C water bath for 7.5 min.

Experiments of Factors Affecting the Mineralization Acceleration.

For the experiments regulating Tquenching, aliquots of 3 mL Ca(OH)2 solution were immersed in ethanol bath of different temperatures (−15 °C, −20 °C, −30 °C, −40 °C, −60 °C, −80 °C, or −196 °C) for 3 min to ensure complete freezing, and then, the frozen solutions were immersed in a water bath maintained at 25 °C for 7.5 min to thaw. For the experiments regulating Tannealing, aliquots of 3 mL Ca(OH)2 solution were first immersed in liquid nitrogen for 1 min to ensure complete freezing and then transferred to an ethanol bath at a certain temperature (−2 °C, −10 °C, −30 °C, or −50 °C) to anneal for 30 min, as this duration ensures that the recrystallized ice grain size reaches equilibrium (32). Afterward, the frozen solutions were immersed in a 25 °C water bath for 7.5 min to thaw. To study the concentration effect, the Ca(OH)2 solutions with different initial concentrations were used in FT and Non-FT experiments. The experimental procedures for CO2 desublimation degree measurement were identical with the steps of experiments regulating Tquenching and Tannealing, except that the sample was ultrapure water.

Observation of the Polycrystalline Ice Crystals.

To observe the polycrystalline ice crystals formed under varying Tquenching, aliquots of 18 μL Ca(OH)2 solution were dropped from a height of 1.5 m onto a coverslip precooled to a certain Tquenching (33). Then, the frozen solution imaging was performed with an optical microscope. To observe the polycrystalline ice crystals formed under varying Tannealing, aliquots of 18 μL Ca(OH)2 solution were dropped onto a coverslip precooled to −60 °C. Then, the frozen solution was warmed to a certain Tannealing at a rate of 5 °C min−1. After maintaining at this temperature for 30 min, the frozen solution imaging was performed with an optical microscope. The width and length of ice grain boundaries were measured by ImageJ. The mean value of the widths of 10 randomly chosen ice grain boundaries in each image was recorded.

FT and Non-FT for Binary NaCl–CaCl2 Solutions.

The binary NaCl–CaCl2 solution was prepared by dissolving NaCl (30 g) and CaCl2·2H2O (1.3 g) into 1,000 mL ultrapure water, and then, pH of the solution (initial pH = 8.5) was adjusted to 10.0, 10.5, or 11.0 using NaOH. Afterward, aliquots of 3 mL NaCl–CaCl2 solutions with varying pH were added into centrifuge tubes. For FT, the NaCl–CaCl2·solutions were immersed in liquid nitrogen for 1 min to ensure complete freezing and then transferred to an ethanol bath at −10 °C to store. For Non-FT, the NaCl–CaCl2·solutions were maintained at room temperature. At every 12-h interval, the samples in FT were transferred to a water bath maintained at 25 °C and left undisturbed for 10 min to thaw.

FT and Non-FT for Artificial Seawater.

The artificial seawater was prepared according to ISO 19679-2017 with a formula of NaCl (22 g), MgCl2·6H2O (9.7 g), Na2SO4 (3.7 g), CaCl2·2H2O (1.324 g), KCl (0.65 g), and NaHCO3 (0.20 g) dissolved in 1,000 mL ultrapure water and then pH of the artificial seawater (initial pH 7.9) was adjusted to 7.5, 7.75, 8.0, 8.25, or 8.5 using NaOH or HCl. Afterward, aliquots of 3 mL artificial seawater with varying pH values were subjected to FT or Non-FT treatment. For FT, artificial seawater was immersed in liquid nitrogen for 1 min to ensure complete freezing and then was placed in a foam box. Subsequently, the foam box was sealed tightly and stored in a refrigerator at −20 °C. For Non-FT, the foam box containing artificial seawater was sealed and stored at room temperature. It is worth noting that ultrapure water was added to the bottom of the foam box to increase humidity and thus suppress the evaporation of artificial seawater. After a certain reaction time, samples in FT were thawed at 25 °C, and samples in both FT and Non-FT were analyzed for CaCO3 crystal formation and Ca2+ concentration. To investigate the influences of solution volume and freezing rate on mineralization acceleration, we also performed the experiments by using aliquots of larger volume (100 mL) and higher quenching temperature (−20 °C). All other experimental procedures remained consistent with those described above.

Measurements of CaCO3 Crystal Number and Size.

To measure the CaCO3 crystal number per unit Ca(OH)2 solution volume, a 2 μL aliquot from each completed FT or Non-FT cycle was sandwiched between coverslips for uniform dispersion, followed by optical microscopy imaging and crystal counting. To determine the CaCO3 crystal size after every cycle, hydrodynamic diameters were measured by the DLS method. To observe in-situ changes in CaCO3 crystal number and size during a single cycle, for FT experiment, a 0.5 μL aliquot of freshly prepared Ca(OH)2 solution was deposited onto a coverslip placed on a cryostage precooled to −30 °C, inducing immediate freezing. Then, the cryostage temperature was raised to 25 °C at a speed of 15 °C min−1 and maintained at 25 °C for 6 min. As for Non-FT experiment, the Ca(OH)2 solution was deposited onto a coverslip at 25 °C and maintained at this temperature for 8 min. Both conditions underwent continuous optical microscopy imaging. Crystal number and size (the largest length of each crystal along any axis) were quantified using ImageJ. To quantify CaCO3 crystal number and size in binary NaCl–CaCl2 solutions and artificial seawater, 2.5 μL aliquots were deposited onto coverslips and imaged via an optical microscope, with crystal number and size similarly analyzed by ImageJ.

Measurement of Ca2+ Concentration of Artificial Seawater.

The Ca2+ concentration was determined following the EDTA titration method with calconcarboxylic acid as the indicator. In detail, a standard solution of EDTA-2Na·2H2O with the concentration of 0.01 M, an aqueous NaOH solution with the concentration of 2 M and the calcium red indicator (mixture of 1 g of calconcarboxylic acid and 100 g of NaCl) were prepared. Subsequently, 200 μL of artificial seawater to be titrated was transferred a centrifuge tube, and then, 800 μL of ultrapure water was added to dilute the solution, following which 40 μL of NaOH solution and 4 mg of calcium red indicator were added to the solution. At this point, Mg2+ in the solution precipitated as Mg(OH)2 and the solution became purplish red (calconcarboxylic acid chelated with Ca2+appears to be red). Then, the solution was titrated to measure Ca2+ concentration by the standard solution of EDTA-2Na·2H2O. When the color of solution transferred from purplish red to bright blue, the Ca2+ chelation with calconcarboxylic acid was completely plundered by EDTA (in basic solution, calconcarboxylic acid that failed to chelate with Ca2+ appears to be blue). Finally, the Ca2+ concentration of artificial seawater was obtained from the equation:C(Ca2+)=CEDTA-2Na·2H2O×V(EDTA-2Na·2H2O)V(artificial seawater)×40.08,

in which C(Ca2+) (g L−1) and CEDTA-2Na·2H2O (M) represent the concentrations of Ca2+ in artificial seawater and standard solution of EDTA-2Na·2H2O, respectively; V(artificial seawater) (mL) and V(EDTA-2Na·2H2O) (mL) represent the volumes of artificial seawater and consumed standard solution of EDTA-2Na·2H2O, respectively.

Characterizations.

The solution conductivity was measured by a conductivity meter (Mettler LE703). The PXRD analysis was carried out on Bruker D8 Discover using Cu Kα radiation with 6° min−1 increments. The hydrodynamic diameters of CaCO3 crystals were examined by a Malvern Zetasizer (Nano-ZS90). The optical microscope images were photographed with a digital camera (Ando 230) fitted on the optical microscope (Leica DM 2700M). The solution temperatures were controlled by ethanol bath (Greatwall DHJF-8005B) or cryostage (Instec mK2000). Raman spectra were measured on HORIBA LabRAM HR Evolution with a monochromatic laser of 532 nm wavelength as the excitation source. The element composition of mineralization product in artificial seawater was analyzed using an energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS, Bruker XFlash 6|100) equipped on a scanning electron microscope (SEM, TESCAN GAIA3).
