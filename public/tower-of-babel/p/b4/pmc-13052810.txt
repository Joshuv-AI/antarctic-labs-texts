Tofacitinib in the treatment of cardiac sarcoidosis: towards steroid-sparing disease management
Authors: Lukas Frischknecht, Melina Stüssi-Helbling, Yuan Tian, Anna Mallone, Aresh Farokhnia, Ronny Buechel, Antonios G Kolios, Jakob Nilsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiac sarcoidosis (CS) creates complex treatment challenges, especially for patients who fail to respond to immunosuppressive drugs, including second-line tumour necrosis factor inhibitors. These refractory cases frequently lead to serious complications and worse prognosis, prompting exploration of new therapies like Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors.MethodsThis study included eight patients with refractory CS or probable CS treated with the JAK inhibitor tofacitinib at the University Hospital Zurich from 2020 to 2024. Treatment outcomes were assessed through changes in myocardial as well as nodal and pulmonary inflammation via 18-F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/CT (18F-FDG PET/CT) scans, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), corticosteroid use and blood neopterin levels.ResultsSeven out of eight patients (87.5%) who received tofacitinib achieved inactive or remitting disease as assessed by repeat myocardial 18F-FDG PET/CT, with a decrease in both standardised uptake value (SUVmax and cardiac metabolic activity. In the treated patients, LVEF stabilised or improved, and the corticosteroid dose was substantially reduced, with the average daily prednisone dose dropping from 5.94 mg to 2.5 mg. Blood neopterin levels, indicative of macrophage activation, as well as extra cardiac SUVmax (nodal and pulmonal) as assessed by repeat 18F-FDG PET/CT also decreased in a majority of the treated patients.ConclusionTofacitinib shows promising results in managing treatment-refractory CS, with seven out of eight patients achieving significant reduction in myocardial inflammation and corticosteroid dependency. However, further studies with larger prospective cohorts are essential to solidify these findings and assess the drug’s efficacy and safety profile in this complex patient group.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Cardiac sarcoidosis (CS) is associated with significant morbidity and mortality and is typically treated with corticosteroids and steroid-sparing immunosuppressive agents. However, a substantial proportion of patients remain refractory or intolerant to conventional therapies, including tumour necrosis factor inhibitors. Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors have shown promising results in cutaneous and pulmonary sarcoidosis, but data on their use in CS are limited to isolated case reports.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

In this retrospective cohort of eight patients with therapy-refractory CS, tofacitinib was associated with suppression of myocardial inflammation in seven of eight patients as assessed by 18-F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/CT. Treatment was accompanied by stabilisation or improvement of left ventricular function and reduction in corticosteroid dose, without an observed increase in thromboembolic or ischaemic events during follow-up. This represents the largest reported case series to date evaluating JAK inhibition in CS with extended follow-up.

Introduction

Cardiac sarcoidosis (CS) poses significant challenges in clinical practice due to its association with severe cardiac complications, such as arrhythmias, heart failure and sudden death. Although corticosteroids have traditionally been the first-line therapy for CS, their use is complicated by a high relapse rate when used as monotherapy and by numerous significant long-term side effects.13 These considerations highlight the need for exploring alternative treatment options. Methotrexate (MTX), azathioprine, adalimumab and infliximab are commonly used as steroid-sparing immunosuppressants in CS, based on several studies showing reduced relapse rates and reduced rates of cardiac adverse events.1 4 5 However, the use of antimetabolites is associated with high discontinuation rates due to side effects as well as a high rate of treatment failure.5 6 The tumour necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi) infliximab and adalimumab have demonstrated benefits in reducing granulomatous inflammation and improving cardiac function in patients with CS resistant to conventional therapy.7 8 A recent study further corroborates the clinical utility of TNFi in CS, highlighting their safety and efficacy.6 A clinically significant number of patients, however, do not respond to TNFi-based therapies or have contraindications, needing alternative immunosuppressive treatment options.

Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors, such as tofacitinib, introduce a novel therapeutic strategy for the immunomodulation of sarcoidosis. These inhibitors specifically target the JAK family of kinases, which are integral in the phosphorylation and subsequent activation of signal transducers and activators of transcription. JAK inhibition (JAKi) thus could interrupt the cytokine signalling pathways that fuel inflammation in sarcoidosis.9 Tofacitinib’s mode of action holds the potential for a more targeted therapeutic approach, potentially minimising the systemic side effects commonly seen with broader immunosuppressive agents. Preliminary applications of tofacitinib in cutaneous and pulmonary sarcoidosis, as well as in isolated cases of CS, have demonstrated encouraging results.10 11 Our study builds on these preliminary findings by showing a long-term treatment response in a larger case series with extended follow-up.

Methods

Patient population

The study included eight therapy-refractory patients with CS or probable CS who were treated with tofacitinib at our Sarcoidosis Center of Excellence between 2020 and 2024. Diagnosis of CS was based on the 2016 Japan Circulation Society expert consensus criteria.12 One patient did not meet these criteria but had clinical findings and imaging findings by cardiac MRI (cMRI) and 18-F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/CT (18F-FDG PET/CT) consistent with CS and was classified as probable CS according to American Heart Association (AHA) 2023 consensus statement.13

Clinical data collection

Baseline clinical and demographic characteristics for patients diagnosed with CS or probable CS were collected from electronic medical records (online supplemental table 1). The data included patient age, sex, symptoms, disease duration and extracardiac organ involvement. Histological confirmation of extracardiac sarcoidosis was recorded where available.

Cardiac complications at diagnosis were evaluated, including high-grade atrioventricular block and sustained ventricular tachycardia. Data from structural imaging modalities of the heart (cMRI and transthoracic echocardiography (TTE)) were documented at baseline and follow-up when available. Prior therapies, including non-biologic and biologic immunosuppressive therapy, were recorded. Details of therapy initiation were documented, including the highest dose of tofacitinib administered, as well as the time interval from the first manifestation of sarcoidosis to the initiation of tofacitinib therapy. Finally, adverse events, defined as infections requiring treatment, thromboembolic events and cardiac events (ie, acute ischaemia and arrhythmias) were documented during follow-up.

18F-FDG PET scans

Serial 18F-FDG PET/CT imaging was performed prior to therapy initiation or modification and approximately 6 months afterward, with follow-up scans conducted every 6–12 months during stable therapy. Imaging was conducted on a 5-ring PET/CT scanner (GE Healthcare, Discovery MI, Chicago, Illinois, USA), and data analysis was performed using the GE Advanced Workstation 4.7 software (GE Healthcare). To suppress physiological myocardial glucose uptake, patients adhered to a strict carbohydrate-free, high-fat diet for 24 hours before the scan. This protocol aimed to reduce blood glucose and insulin levels. On the day of imaging, blood glucose levels were measured, and a body weight-adjusted dose of heparin (50 IU/kg) was administered 15 min before 18F-FDG injection. Scanning commenced 60 min after tracer administration and included an acquisition from the neck to the thighs.

Pathological uptake patterns in the left ventricular myocardium were assessed following the Japanese Society of Nuclear Cardiology guidelines, focusing on focal or focal-on-diffuse uptake patterns.12 PET-CT scans not showing these patterns were repeated or excluded from our analysis. The mediastinal blood pool’s mean standardised uptake value (SUVmean) was measured in the ascending aorta and multiplied by 1.5 to determine the lower SUV threshold for pathological myocardial uptake. The affected myocardium was delineated using this threshold, enabling the calculation of the affected myocardial volume and the SUVmean. Cardiac metabolic activity (CMA) was derived by multiplying the affected myocardial volume by its SUVmean.14 15

Data analysis

All plots and analyses were generated using R within the RStudio (V.2024.09.1+394) environment. The following R packages were used in this study: tidyverse (V.2.0.0) for data manipulation and visualisation, readxl (V.1.4.3) for importing Excel files, lubridate (V.1.9.3) for handling date and time data, flextable (V.0.9.4) for creating online supplemental table 1 and officer (V.0.6.3) for exporting results to Word documents. Statistical comparisons of measurements before and after treatment were performed using paired t-tests.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Eight patients with CS or probable CS were included in this retrospective study. Seven patients met the 2017 Japan Circulation Society expert consensus for CS. One patient did not meet these criteria but had clinical findings, along with imaging findings by cMRI and 18F-FDG PET/CT consistent with CS, and was classified as probable CS based on current AHA guidelines.13

The mean age was 51.6±9.1 years, with an equal distribution of men and women (50% each, online supplemental table 1). The average disease duration was 9.5 years. Histological confirmation of extracardiac sarcoidosis was documented in six patients (75%). The most common extracardiac manifestations were pulmonary and nodal involvement, while one patient had isolated CS.

At baseline, cardiac complications were observed in the majority of patients (62.5%). High-grade atrioventricular block was documented in two patients, and sustained ventricular tachycardia in two patients. Additionally, three patients had an implantable cardioverter defibrillator placed as secondary prophylactic therapy. Cardiac fibrosis detected by cMRI was present in five patients at baseline, and the mean left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) as assessed by TTE was 47.8%. A detailed overview of additional clinical characteristics is provided in online supplemental table 2.

At first diagnosis, corticosteroids were initiated in seven patients (87.5%). One patient received high-dose corticosteroids (250 mg methylprednisolone), while six received lower-dose prednisone (PDN) (20–50 mg daily). All patients had prior exposure to additional systemic immunosuppressive therapy before starting tofacitinib therapy, with an average of five previous treatments per patient. Previous therapies are shown in online supplemental table 1 and included; hydroxychloroquine (n=3, mean 330 mg daily), azathioprine (n=2, mean 125 mg daily), cyclophosphamide (n=2), leflunomide (n=1), MMF (n=1), MTX (n=8, mean 16.2 mg subcutaneously (s.c.) weekly), adalimumab (n=5, 40 mg weekly s.c.), infliximab (n=5, mean 5,5 mg/kg body weight (range: 400–800 mg) every 4 weeks intravenously), golimumab (n=2, mean 100 mg every 4 weeks s.c.), certolizumab (n=1). If possible, corticosteroids were tapered during the initial therapy, and additional immunosuppressive therapy was adjusted based on treatment response and treatment-related adverse effects. In addition to the immunosuppressive therapy, all patients received best guideline-based heart insufficiency therapy with regular visits in our cardiology ambulatory prior to the start of tofacitinib and through all the follow-up.

Tofacitinib therapy

Tofacitinib therapy was initiated in all patients due to either intolerance to previous therapy or treatment failure with previous therapy (indicated by elevated biomarkers and/or sustained FDG PET uptake) and was given at a maximum dose of 20 mg daily. The average time from the first manifestation of sarcoidosis to the start of tofacitinib therapy was 6.9 years. The mean duration of tofacitinib therapy at the last follow-up was 11.67±8.99 months. The tofacitinib dose was adjusted individually based on clinical response and tolerability with a mean daily dose of tofacitinib in the cohort of 17.5 mg (range: 10–20 mg/day). Five patients received 20 mg daily, two were treated with 15 mg daily and one patient was on 10 mg daily at the end of the study period.

Suppression of myocardial 18F-FDG uptake

Seven out of eight patients (87.5%) who received tofacitinib achieved inactive or remitting disease as assessed by repeat myocardial 18F-FDG PET/CT. Representative pre-tofacitinib and post-tofacitinib 18F-FDG PET/CT scans from three patients (Nr. 2, Nr. 4 and Nr. 7) are shown in figure 1A–C and demonstrate a marked treatment response.

Effect of tofacitinib on corticosteroid dose and cardiac function (LVEF)

The impact of tofacitinib on cardiac function was evaluated by comparing LVEF, as assessed by TTE before and after treatment initiation. LVEF remained stable or slightly improved following treatment with tofacitinib in all patients (figure 3B). Before JAKi initiation, the mean LVEF for the whole cohort was 47.8%, which increased slightly to 50% at the last follow-up (p=0.034).

Among the eight patients, two (patient Nr. 2 and 3) had an LVEF <40% at the time of JAKi initiation and both demonstrated stable or improved LVEF at the last follow-up PET/CT scan.

Change in PDN dose was also analysed across the cohort. Tofacitinib initiation was associated with a reduction in corticosteroid dependence in the treated patients. The mean corticosteroid dose decreased from 5.94±4.21 mg to 2.50 ±2.67 mg daily at the last follow-up, with a trend suggesting reduced corticosteroid dependence following tofacitinib treatment (p=0.055) (figure 3C). Four out of eight patients experienced a reduction in PDN dosage following tofacitinib therapy, with three patients having stable PDN dosage and four patients remaining without additional corticosteroid treatment at the end of the study period.

Effect of tofacitinib on biomarkers and nodal and pulmonary 18F-FDG uptake

We also assessed blood neopterin levels, a marker of macrophage activation, across the cohort (figure 3D). Six out of eight (75%) patients (patient Nr. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6) showed a reduction in neopterin levels after therapy initiation (from 2.88±0.585 ng/mL to 1.92±0.830 ng/mL). Patients Nr. 7 and 8 remained stable, with a slight increase below our neopterin cut-off level of 2.5 ng/mL. Across all patients (Nr 1–8), the mean neopterin level decreased from 2.52±0.846 ng/mL before treatment to 1.94±0.705 ng/mL after treatment; however, this reduction did not reach statistical significance (p=0.116).

To further assess inflammatory dynamics using 18F-FDG PET/CT, changes in SUVmax were analysed for pulmonal (p=0.472) (figure 3E) and nodal involvement (p=0.142) (figure 3F). For SUVmax pulmonal, patient Nr. 3, 4 and 5 showed a reduction in standardised uptake values. In patients who had no pulmonary involvement at baseline (patients 1, 2, 6 and 7), the findings remained stable throughout follow-up. Similarly, for SUVmax nodal, patient Nr. 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 demonstrated reductions, supporting the effect of tofacitinib in suppressing nodal inflammation. Patients 6 and 7 showed no nodal involvement at baseline and last follow-up. Notably, patient Nr. 8 showed an increase in both pulmonal and nodal SUVmax, which also aligned with an increase in SUVmax, CMA and neopterin in this patient, which was in contrast to the reductions seen in the rest of the cohort.

Assessment of adverse events

During the treatment with tofacitinib, no increase in infections requiring treatment was observed. However, due to the increased reported risk of herpes zoster, all patients received active immunisation with Shingrix prior to the start of therapy with tofacitinib.

In two patients (patient 2 and 3), while on treatment with tofacitinib, significant cardiac arrhythmias (ventricular tachycardia) requiring electrical cardioversion were recorded. Both patients had previously documented myocardial fibrosis on cMRI and severe dilated cardiomyopathy with reduced LVEF confirmed by echocardiography. At the time of the event, 18F-FDG PET showed no evidence of cardiac inflammation in either patient. Importantly, no patient experienced a venous thromboembolic event or an acute cardiac ischaemic event, even at doses of 20 mg/day.

Discussion

In this observational study, we evaluated the effect of tofacitinib in patients with CS. Our findings suggest that tofacitinib, a JAK inhibitor, may offer a promising therapeutic approach in patients with refractory CS who have failed first-line and second-line immunosuppressive therapy, including TNFi.

Our primary finding was that tofacitinib resulted in significant suppression of myocardial 18F-FDG uptake in seven out of eight (87.5%) patients with longstanding, therapy refractory CS. As previously published by our group,6 we used SUVmax and CMA as a parameter to assess therapeutic response. The question of whether a complete resolution of myocardial 18F-FDG uptake is necessary or if a certain degree of reduction is sufficient to prevent the development of myocardial fibrosis and cardiac events still needs to be elucidated. However, in our observations, seven patients improved, suggesting that tofacitinib effectively attenuates myocardial inflammation in these responders. This anti-inflammatory effect appeared independent of the concomitant corticosteroid dose in our observational study.

To date, the safety and efficacy of tofacitinib in sarcoidosis has been reported exclusively in small observational studies and case series, predominantly involving patients with cutaneous or pulmonary manifestations.91618 Importantly, only two cases have specifically investigated patients with cardiac involvement.10 16 To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the effect of tofacitinib in a small cohort of patients with CS and an extended treatment duration. Our findings demonstrate that tofacitinib is both safe and effective, with a treatment response in seven out of eight patients with refractory disease. Interestingly, the patient who did not respond to tofacitinib therapy (Patient 8) received a maximum dose of 15 mg and the lack of response might have been dose-related in this case. We are currently exploring an increased dose of 20 mg in this patient, but the treatment response has not been evaluated at this time.

Importantly, cardiac function, assessed by LVEF, remained stable or showed improvement in patients receiving tofacitinib. While the absolute increase in LVEF was modest, the stabilisation of cardiac function is a critical therapeutic goal, particularly in patients with advanced CS. Of interest, both patients with an LVEF <40% at baseline showed stable or improved function under tofacitinib therapy, highlighting its potential benefit in patients with impaired systolic function.

This is particularly important given that some studies have indicated an increased thromboembolic and cardiovascular risk under JAK inhibitor therapy.19 However, similar to other publications,20 we were not able to demonstrate these side effects even though our results should be interpreted with caution due to the small number of patients. On the contrary, and in line with other publications,10 we were able to show a clear positive response to tofacitinib, with therapeutic effects at doses of up to 20 mg/day.

The immunosuppressive regimen in our cohort was highly individualised, reflecting the complexity of managing CS. All patients were on corticosteroids and/or additional immunosuppressive therapy prior to initiating tofacitinib, with six out of eight receiving TNFi prior to treatment with tofacitinib. The encouraging treatment response to tofacitinib seen in our analysis is especially notable given the treatment refractory nature of the CS to numerous immunosuppressive therapies, including TNFi in several of the included patients. Importantly, tofacitinib initiation was associated with a reduction in corticosteroid dependence in these patients, which could mitigate long-term steroid-related adverse effects. As none of the 8 patients required escalation or restarting of PDN after tofacitinib, this therapeutic strategy is in line with current disease management of sarcoidosis towards a steroid-sparing approach.2123

While these results are encouraging, several limitations must be considered. The sample size was small, and the single-centre observational design, as well as the heterogeneity of the prior therapy, inherently introduced selection bias. The outcome in our study focused mainly on myocardial FDG uptake; long-term follow-up is necessary to further assess effects on cardiac events. Furthermore, the lack of a control group limits the ability to definitively attribute the observed effects to tofacitinib alone. Prospective studies with larger cohorts and standardised follow-up protocols are needed to validate our findings and better define the role of JAKi in CS management.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that tofacitinib may be an effective therapy for patients with refractory CS, demonstrating significant suppression of myocardial inflammation and stabilisation of cardiac function. Hence, JAK inhibitors warrant further investigation as a promising therapeutic strategy, potentially offering a targeted and effective treatment option for this challenging patient population.
