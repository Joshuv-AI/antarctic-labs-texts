RADA16 as a novel hemostatic and regenerative agent in urology: European Association of Urology endourology up-to-date overview
Authors: Ali Talyshinskii, Bhaskar Kumar Somani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of reviewSelf-assembling peptide (SAP) hydrogels represent a novel class of synthetic biomaterials with growing relevance in surgery. Among them, the ion-complementary peptide RADA16 has gained attention as an athermal, transparent, and biocompatible hemostatic agent. While its use is increasingly reported in multiple surgical specialties, evidence specific to urology remains fragmented. This review aims to summarize the physicochemical properties, mechanisms of action, and current clinical evidence for RADA16-based hydrogels, with a particular focus on urological applications.Recent findingsRADA16 rapidly self-assembles into a transparent, extracellular-matrix-like nanofibrillar hydrogel upon exposure to physiological fluids, providing effective local hemostasis without reliance on the coagulation cascade. Preclinical and clinical data from other surgical fields demonstrate rapid bleeding control, favorable safety, and potential regenerative effects. Emerging urological evidence suggests that RADA16 is effective in managing hemorrhagic cystitis, radiation-induced hematuria, and bleeding during prostate surgery, including robot-assisted radical prostatectomy and benign prostate surgery. Beyond hemostasis, RADA16 may support wound healing and promotion of re-epithelialization. However, the current evidence base is limited by small sample sizes, lack of comparative studies, heterogeneous methodologies, and short follow-up.SummaryRADA16-based hydrogels represent a promising adjunctive hemostatic option in urology, offering technical advantages such as transparency, absence of thermal injury, minimal swelling, and applicability in confined or high-risk settings. Robust prospective, comparative, and cost-effectiveness studies are required to define its definitive role in routine urological practice.Keywords: bleeding, hemostatic, hydrogel, RADA16

INTRODUCTION

Self-assembling peptides (SAPs) are promising substances characterized by their ability to be organized into three-dimensional hydrogels upon contact with physiological solutions [1]. Among them, the ion-complementary peptide RADA16, originally developed as a platform option for cell seeding in tissue engineering, should be especially highlighted [2]. However, due to its composition of alternating charged amino acids and the formation of a nanofibrillar hydrogel structure resembling the extracellular matrix, this peptide is gaining popularity in surgery as a local hemostatic agent for endoscopic and minimally invasive procedures [3]. Urology is no exception, and recently, there has been growing interest in RADA16 as an athermal hemostatic agent with several advantages over traditionally used agents, such as transparency, no swelling during installation, and the ability to apply it in a controlled manner to hard-to-reach areas. However, existing reviews do not cover urology and instead provide a general overview of the role of hydrogels in surgery without specifically addressing urology, which limits the urological community's understanding of the prospects for using RADA16 and explains the number and fragmentation of related studies [4]. Thus, this review is devoted to the unification of fundamental data on the entire family of RADA16-based hydrogels, as well as the generalization of available data on the use of this peptide in various urological scenarios, which together is aimed at familiarizing the urological community with this hemostatic option.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

In November 2025, a comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and Google Scholar databases to identify current evidence on the use of the self-assembling peptide hydrogel RADA16 in medicine with a specific focus on urology. The following search terms and combinations were used: ‘RADA16’, ‘self-assembling peptide’, ‘self-assembling peptide hydrogel’, ‘PuraStat’, ‘PuraBond’, ‘PuraShield’, ‘SAP hydrogel’, ‘hemostasis’, ‘regeneration’, ‘urology’, ‘hematuria’, ‘radiation cystitis’, ‘HoLEP’, ‘RARP’, ‘TURBT’, and ‘endoscopic hemostasis’. Additional manual searches of reference lists, conference abstracts, and grey literature were performed to ensure completeness.

All types of publications relevant to the topic were considered, including experimental studies, preclinical models, case reports, case series, technical notes and observational clinical studies, given the early stage of evidence development for RADA16 in urology. No date restrictions were imposed, but preference was given to literature published during the last 2 years.

The findings were organized into several thematic domains to provide readers with a comprehensive understanding of the current state of knowledge regarding RADA16: historical development and classification of SAPs, physicochemical and mechanistic properties, hemostatic effects, regenerative and extracellular-matrix-mimicking functions, and biocompatibility and safety. Urology-specific evidence was synthesized separately and categorized according to major clinical applications, including prostate surgery, management of hematuria and radiation cystitis, endoscopic procedures, and emerging tissue engineering approaches. Future research directions and potential clinical roles were highlighted based on gaps identified in the existing literature. Given the narrative and comprehensive design of this review, formal registration in PROSPERO was not required and was therefore not undertaken.

HISTORY

The first mentions of the development of SAPs date back to the early 1990s, when Zhang et al. discovered a short repeating peptide, EAK16-II (AEAEAKAKAEAEAKAK), which is capable of forming β-sheet structures and nanofibrils in an aqueous environment under physiological conditions [5]. Subsequent studies of this phenomenon revealed its architecture, based on the alternation of negatively and positively charged amino acids (Glu/Asp and Lys), which, due to hydrophobic hydrogen and electrostatic interactions, provided the conditions for such peptide folding and formed the basis of the concept of ion-complementary peptides capable of independently assembling into three-dimensional hydrogels in an aqueous environment [6]. When determining the functional characteristics of various combinations, a RADA scheme was identified, including positively charged arginine (R), negatively charged aspartate (D), and neutral hydrophobic alanine (A). Analysis of similar peptides led to the understanding that 8–16 structural amino acids are sufficient to achieve a similar assembly with predictable viscosity, which led to the development of the 16-amino acid peptide RADA16-I (RADARADARADARADA), which subsequently became a reference for the development of similar substances due to a number of benchmark characteristics, such as transparency, water content of more than 97%, high biocompatibility, and gradual degradation to amino acids without toxic metabolites [7]. In the 2000s, RADA16 became the basis for an entire field in biomaterials and tissue engineering. It was used as a universal ‘framework’ to which short bioactive motifs (angiogenic, neuro-regenerative, and anti-inflammatory) were added without disrupting the self-assembly ability of the original fragment, which contributed to the emergence of modified variants of RADA16 for stimulating angiogenesis, nerve tissue regeneration, accelerated wound healing, and local delivery of drugs and biomolecules [8]. Nevertheless, in surgery, this peptide has a promising role primarily as a hemostatic agent, which is confirmed by the accumulation of subsequent thematic publications and the launch by 3-D Matrix of a 2.5% peptide solution in the form of a transparent gel called PuraStat, which received CE marking in 2014 and 2016 for use during endoscopic and surgical procedures in the EU and Australia, respectively [4]. The guidelines of the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) mention PuraStat as one of the modern endoscopic hemostatics for the upper and lower gastrointestinal tract. In 2019, the next representative of this class with CE marking, PuraBond (PuraStat), appeared on the market, positioned as an identical hemostatic agent but with expanded indications for use, including during open and laparoscopic procedures. Finally, in 2022, 3-D Matrix announced a third representative, PuraShield, primarily positioned as a hemostatic agent for ENT surgery. It should be noted that, despite the differences in names and areas of application, PuraStat, PuraBond, and PuraShield are the same self-assembling peptide RADA16-I in a concentration of about 2.5% and have a common CE marking as Class III medical devices for human use as adjunctive hemostatic agents [9].

MECHANISM OF ACTIONS

Physical and chemical properties

RADA16 is a synthetic 16-amino acid peptide with a repeating ion-complementary sequence, RADARADARADARADA, which provides an alternation of charged and hydrophobic amino acid residues of positively charged arginine (R), hydrophobic alanine (A), and negatively charged aspartate (D). This architecture is characterized by the possibility of forming antiparallel β-sheets due to stabilization by a network of hydrophobic, hydrogen, and ionic bonds. In an acidic environment, partial self-assembly of the peptide into thin fibrils is possible, but without full three-dimensional organization, which explains the initial state in the form of a viscous solution [10]. Due to its structure, RADA16 exhibits thixotropy, the property of thinning and reducing viscosity under mechanical stress and restoring its original characteristics at rest, which ensures adequate injection delivery using a syringe or catheter and explains the popularity of this hemostatic agent in endoscopic procedures [11]. Furthermore, upon contact with physiological fluids (blood, urine), pH buffering occurs, as well as an increase in ionic strength and self-assembly of the peptide due to the formation of β-sheets into nanofibrils several nanometers in diameter and several microns in length, which subsequently form an extracellular matrix (ECM)-like organized three-dimensional matrix in the form of a transparent hydrogel with a high water content (up to 99%) within a few seconds, while RADA16 loses its thixotropic properties and, due to its physicochemical properties and surface tension, is retained on the tissue surface [12]. Thus, from a physicochemical point of view, RADA16 is a pH-sensitive self-assembling nanofibrillar gel, which in the clinical formula (2.5% solution) transforms from a viscous solution into an elastic transparent matrix directly on the surface of the wound or mucous membrane.

Hemostasis

The above characteristics explain the hemostatic mechanism of RADA16, which is based on the properties of the matrix itself without direct activation of coagulation factors [13]. Thus, it has been demonstrated that when applied to a vascular anastomosis or suture, the peptide seals the area of leakage. In models of gastrointestinal mucosal damage, the gel forms a continuous layer on the surface of the defect and around small vessels, sharply reducing blood. Clinical and experimental studies in gastroenterology and ENT surgery have also shown high efficacy in diffuse bleeding after endoscopic submucosal dissection, polypectomy, tonsillectomy, and endoscopic interventions in the nasal cavity [9], and the possibility of performing hemostasis in areas that are difficult to access with clips or coagulation has been noted [4,9]. When RADA16 gel was applied to wounds in the brain, liver, femoral artery, skin, spinal cord, and other models, immediate cessation of bleeding was observed, along with a reduction in hematoma volume and improved visualization of the field. According to available data, the continuous layer formed by the gel on the bleeding surface does not significantly activate complement components (C3a/C4) or platelets but acts as a structured scaffold, mechanically retaining blood components and coagulation factors and promoting the formation of a fibrin layer under the matrix by creating a low-turbulence environment [10].

Wound healing and regeneration

As noted earlier, when assembled in an optimal environment, RADA16 is presented as a three-dimensional nanofibrillar network resembling an ECM with a high specific surface area, which is conceptually suitable for adhesion, migration, and survival of seeded cells, which was confirmed by in-vitro studies using cells from various lines, demonstrating more physiological morphology and behavior compared to 2D culture [14]. Dzierżyńska et al.[15] reported development of three new peptide biomaterials based on the RADA16-I hydrogel functionalized with a sequence (AAPV) cleaved by human neutrophil elastase and short biologically active peptide motifs, namely GHK, KGHK, and RDKVYR. Moreover, the possibility of supporting targeted differentiation of stem cells when combining RADA16 with bioactive motifs or growth factors has been demonstrated [16]. This characteristic offers promising options for using the gel not only as a hemostatic agent but also as a means of supporting regeneration regardless of the area of damage. Thus, Gil et al.[17] stated that PuraStat treatment has a positive protective effect in the cecal sidewall injury model and significantly reduces abdominal adhesion formation at the interface of the injured cecum and overlying peritoneal sidewall defect. Preclinical studies suggest that RADA16-based hydrogels may also reduce excessive fibrosis and adhesion formation while promoting more organized tissue repair. As a result, RADA16 functions not only as an effective hemostatic agent but also as a permissive scaffold that supports physiological wound healing and regeneration across a range of tissue types.

Biocompatibility and biodegradation

Due to its composition, after hydrogel formation, RADA16 gradually undergoes hydrolysis and proteolytic degradation over several days, forming short peptide fragments and free L-amino acids. Some experimental studies have demonstrated complete removal of the gel from the application site without the formation of fibrous remnants. Thus, when RADA16 is used locally in surgery, the degradation components are not absorbed into the systemic bloodstream in significant concentrations, and also have no effect on the overall nitrogen balance and blood coagulation parameters, which makes it attractive in cases where the patient is taking anticoagulants/antiplatelet drugs, as well as in interventions in hard-to-reach areas with a high risk of complications. The controlled and predictable biodegradation profile of RADA16 also contributes to its safety in hard-to-reach or anatomically sensitive regions. The absence of significant swelling during gel formation and degradation minimizes the risk of compression, obstruction, or distortion of surrounding tissues. Collectively, these characteristics make RADA16 well suited for use in endoscopic, minimally invasive, and confined surgical environments where both safety and precision are paramount.

Advantages over existing hemostatic agents

In terms of its overall physical and chemical properties, RADA16 differs significantly from traditional agents (powders, sponges, and adhesives), which potentially gives it the role of a universal hemostatic agent regardless of the area of application. First, when RADA16 comes into contact with physiological fluids, it forms a transparent hydrogel that does not interfere with visualization and allows for controlled application while simultaneously observing the wound and assessing the effectiveness of hemostasis in real time, which is valuable in endoscopy due to the initial lack of direct visibility of the surgical field, especially in cases of diffuse bleeding (micro-oozing). Secondly, given its synthetic structure and the absence of plasma, animal, or human proteins, the use of RADA16 minimizes the risk of pathogen transmission, which has a positive effect on its versatility in terms of biocompatibility [18]. Thirdly, RADA16 hydrogel forms a thin adhesive layer without significant swelling, which allows it to be used in narrow lumen areas with minimal risk of obstruction or anatomical changes, as demonstrated in a sheep endoscopic sinus surgery model. Fourth, unlike most traditional hemostatics, which act primarily as mechanical barriers or coagulation matrices, RADA16 combines a physical hemostatic effect with potential regenerative activity [19]. Finally, one of the main advantages of RADA16 is its initially liquid form and thixotropic properties, which enable controlled application of the hydrogel through cannulas and catheters, as well as the coating of surfaces with complex geometries, ensuring high dosing accuracy and uniform coverage.

Urological evidence

The above-described features of RADA16 and its applicability in all surgical modalities explain its growing popularity in various medical disciplines, including urology. Currently, there are six publications devoted to the use of this hydrogel in certain variations of hemorrhagic cystitis or prostatic surgery.

Darwazeh et al. published a case report of a 73-year-old male patient who presented to a hospital emergency department multiple times with visible hematuria and clots due to hemorrhagic radiation cystitis (RHC). Due to unsuccessful sodium hyaluronate bladder instillations, the patient was scheduled for PuraStat, which was applied to each hemorrhagic locus during air cystoscopy [20▪▪]. Another case report was published by Latham et al. describing recurrent hematuria in a 73-year-old man following bladder neck incision refractory to instillation of a hyaluronic acid-based agent (iAluRil). During the next episode of hematuria, 7.5 ml of PuraStat was applied in a thin layer to the area of visible inflammation and the bladder neck, also during air cystoscopy. The authors note the absence of recurrent episodes over the next 2 months, although the patient developed bladder neck contracture, which, according to the authors, is not a direct complication of hydrogel use [21▪▪]. More informative is the publication by Kam et al., who described the first pilot series of 15 patients with RHC after prostate cancer (PCa)-related radiation therapy. After standard cystoscopy with control of active sources of bleeding, complete evacuation of fluid from the bladder and CO2 insufflation to a pressure of 8–15 mmHg were performed to tamponade venous bleeding. This was followed by the application of PuraStat to the telangiectasia loci and evacuation of the gas 5 min after the application of the hydrogel. During a 6-week follow-up, the authors noted a 93% reduction in the frequency and intensity of relapses, with 60% experiencing complete cessation of hematuria. During a 3-month follow-up, two patients underwent re-administration of the hydrogel, while another patient underwent salvage cystectomy due to a complete lack of effect. As the authors themselves note, repeated cystoscopies showed regression of the visual picture of increased vascular pattern and telangiectasia, confirming the regenerative potential of RADA16 along with its hemostatic effect [22▪] (Table 1).

RADA-16 use for cystitis

Munien et al. presented an observational study involving 29 patients who underwent nerve-sparing robot-assisted radical prostatectomy due to PCa with the application of PuraStat to the nerve-vascular bundle (NVB) and prostate bed during the procedure. According to the results, the average blood loss was 363 ml, and in all cases, adequate hemostasis was achieved without the need for diathermy. In addition, the surgical team's perception of the hydrogel was evaluated, showing a high level of satisfaction, especially in terms of transparency, readiness for use, and ease of manipulation under conditions of limited visualization [23▪▪]. Chandrasekhar et al. published a case series describing the results of RARP in 10 patients using PuraBond. Five milliliters of hydrogel was applied to the prostate bed and vesicourethral anastomosis through the assistant's port. According to the results, in all cases, there was no need to use other hemostatic modalities or blood transfusions, and there were no cases of hematuria or anastomotic failure during the subsequent 3 months [24▪]. Sid Ahmed and Nkwam described the use of PuraBond during HoLEP in a 62-year-old patient with a prostate volume of 152 cm3 and severe LUTS. After completion of enucleation, bipolar diathermy, and fluid evacuation, 5 ml of hydrogel was applied to the prostatic fossa. On day 28 of follow-up, the patient reported significant improvement in symptoms and no urinary irritation symptoms, no rehospitalizations, and no adverse events for 3 months [25▪▪] (Table 2). Finally, one clinical trial of PuraStat for BPH-related Aquablation (NCT06948331) is currently registered, with the primary objective of assessing how PuraStat affects post-operative hematuria, which will make a significant contribution to understanding the horizons of use of RADA16-based hydrogels.

Conclusions across studies

RADA16 is a synthetic self-assembling peptide hydrogel that rapidly forms a transparent, ECM-like 3D matrix under physiological conditions, enabling precise application and effective hemostasis even in confined or hard-to-reach sites [26,27]. Beyond bleeding control, RADA16 supports wound healing by promoting keratinocyte and mucoasl epithelial cell adhesion, proliferation, and migration, thereby accelerating re-epithelialization through endogenous recruitment of laminin-332. These properties position RADA16 as a biocompatible, resorbable scaffold with potential applications in tissue regeneration and future drug delivery.

Limitations

The available data on using RADA16 self-assembling peptide hydrogels in urology has several important limitations. Most published studies focus on individual clinical cases or small groups of patients from a single center. These studies often lack control groups and have short follow-up periods. As a result, they do not provide enough evidence to make definite conclusions about the long-term effectiveness and safety of the treatments being studied. In most studies, RADA16 was used as an adjuvant, not as a standalone agent to stop bleeding. It was often combined with diathermy or other treatments. This makes it difficult to assess how much RADA16 alone helped achieve hemostasis. Some studies indicate that repeated treatments might be necessary and that surgery could be needed to save a life. They also highlight the uncertainty about how well the drug works in terms of function, including nerve repair, scar formation, and the return of erectile function. Additional limitations include the lack of standard criteria for assessing both bleeding control and quality of life. The technical sensitivity of the method, which necessitates a ‘dry’ environment and air/CO cystoscopy, poses a significant challenge. Moreover, there are no randomized comparative studies or clinical–economic evaluations available. Considering all the available evidence, the current level of certainty is low. This points out the need for larger, well-planned studies to clarify the role of RADA16 in urology (Table 4).

Future perspectives

In light of the physicochemical properties and existing developments in other medical fields, the results of the RADA16 application in urology correspond to the early clinical implementation stage. Therefore, the main prospects for integrating this hydrogel into routine urological practice are related to standardizing the delivery method, expanding indications, and increasing the level of evidence. Thus, in accordance with the advantages claimed by the manufacturer, some authors have directly pointed out the value of the thixotropic properties of RADA16, which ensures its easy delivery to the area of interest and should be studied in other cases of refractory hematuria, which is particularly attractive in light of the regenerative properties of the hydrogel. In minimally invasive and robot-assisted surgery, the authors note that the absence of thermal tissue damage makes RADA16 a candidate for wider application, but these conclusions remain preliminary. Virtually all researchers emphasize the need for large prospective and comparative studies with long-term follow-up to assess hemostasis stability and impact on erectile function and continence, as well as comparative and clinical–economic effectiveness relative to alternative methods (Table 5).

Future steps of RADA-16 studies

CONCLUSION

Clinical evidence suggests that RADA16 self-assembling peptide hydrogels represent a potentially valuable hemostatic adjunct in urology, exhibiting considerable technical viability, a positive safety record, and effectiveness in managing both intraoperative and persistent urological hemorrhage. At the same time, the current research is limited to single clinical cases and small groups without control groups and short follow-up periods. In most studies, RADA16 was used as an additional agent. rather than a main one. Therefore, RADA16 seems to be a promising hemostatic. agent. However, its role in clinical practice needs to be confirmed through extensive, forward-looking studies that compare it to other options and include long-term follow-up.
