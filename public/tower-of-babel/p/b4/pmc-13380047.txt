Cancer-associated left ventricular thrombus with preserved ejection fraction: A multicenter descriptive study
Authors: Ramzi Ibrahim, Mohamed Allam, Christopher Kanaan, Nikita Rafie, Sherif Ahmed, Juan Maria Farina, Dan Sorajja, Balaji Tamarappoo, Reza Arsanjani, Chadi Ayoub
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLeft ventricular thrombus (LVT) is usually associated with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), yet malignancy-related hypercoagulability may promote LVT despite preserved systolic function.MethodsWe performed a retrospective descriptive cohort study across three Mayo Clinic tertiary centers. Adults with confirmed LVT, active malignancy, and preserved LVEF, defined as LVEF ≥50% at LVT diagnosis, were included. Outcomes included all-cause mortality, ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack, clinically significant bleeding, LVT resolution, and discharge anticoagulation strategy.ResultsTwenty-three patients were included. Mean age was 66 years, 17 patients were men, and mean LVEF was 57.3%. All-cause mortality occurred in 14 patients, clinically significant bleeding in 5, and no ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack events were observed. LVT resolution occurred in 20 patients. At discharge, 6 received direct oral anticoagulants and 17 received warfarin.DiscussionCancer-associated LVT may occur despite preserved LVEF and identifies a high-risk phenotype requiring further study.

Introduction

Left ventricular thrombus (LVT) is classically associated with impaired left ventricular systolic function, most often after myocardial infarction or in dilated cardiomyopathy [1], [2]. In these settings, thrombus formation is attributed to myocardial injury, blood stasis, and systemic or local hypercoagulability, and clinical suspicion is typically highest among patients with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), apical akinesis, aneurysm formation, or extensive regional wall motion abnormalities. Cancer is associated with a complex prothrombotic milieu [3]. Therefore, malignancy-associated hypercoagulability may provide a mechanistic basis for LVT even when global left ventricular systolic function is preserved [4], [5], [6]. However, LVT in patients with active malignancy and preserved LVEF has been described primarily in isolated case reports and small historical series [4], [5], [6]. We performed a multicenter retrospective descriptive study to characterize the clinical profile, malignancy spectrum, anticoagulation patterns, and outcomes of patients with cancer-associated LVT and preserved LVEF.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective descriptive observational study using electronic health record data from the Mayo Clinic Enterprise, including tertiary academic centers in Arizona, Florida, and Minnesota. Study coordination, data extraction, and analysis were performed by investigators in the Department of Cardiovascular Medicine, Mayo Clinic Arizona. The Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board deemed the study exempt from ethical board oversight and approval (protocol #25–014378).

Patients were identified through retrospective query of echocardiography reports, imaging documentation, and clinical records for terms consistent with LVT. Candidate records were manually reviewed to confirm LVT, active malignancy, and preserved LVEF at diagnosis. This study was designed as a descriptive cohort of confirmed cases and was not intended to estimate the incidence or prevalence of LVT among all patients with cancer.

Adults aged ≥18 years were included if they had documented LVT, active malignancy at LVT diagnosis, and preserved LVEF, defined as LVEF ≥50% on transthoracic echocardiography. Patients were excluded if LVEF was <50%, active malignancy could not be confirmed, or LVT was suspected but not confirmed. Reports stating only “possible LVT” or “cannot exclude LVT” were excluded.

Active malignancy was defined as at least one of the following at LVT diagnosis: ongoing systemic antineoplastic therapy or radiation therapy, newly diagnosed cancer within the preceding 6 months, recurrent or progressive disease, metastatic disease, or active hematologic malignancy under treatment or clinical surveillance.

LVT was defined as a discrete left ventricular intracavitary mass consistent with thrombus, distinct from adjacent myocardium and endocardial structures, and documented by the interpreting cardiologist. Supporting features included visualization in more than one imaging plane, apical or mural location, clear demarcation from the endocardial border, and exclusion of artifact, trabeculation, tumor, or vegetation when documented. LVT resolution was defined as complete disappearance of the previously documented thrombus on follow-up imaging; partial regression without complete disappearance was not classified as resolution.

Anticoagulation strategy was defined by the regimen prescribed at hospital discharge after LVT diagnosis and categorized as warfarin or direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC). When available, the specific DOAC agent was abstracted. Longitudinal adherence, total anticoagulation duration, warfarin time in therapeutic range, and subsequent regimen changes were not consistently available because many patients received portions of oncologic care, anticoagulation monitoring, and follow-up outside the Mayo Clinic Enterprise. Anticoagulation comparisons were therefore descriptive and not intended to infer comparative effectiveness.

Clinical outcomes included all-cause mortality, ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA), clinically significant bleeding, documented venous thromboembolism, non-cerebral arterial embolic events (including venous thromboembolism and arterial embolism), and LVT resolution. Clinically significant bleeding was defined as bleeding requiring medical evaluation, hospitalization, transfusion, anticoagulation interruption, procedural intervention, or otherwise classified by treating clinicians as clinically important. Because cause-specific mortality data were incomplete, all-cause mortality was retained as the primary mortality endpoint.

Follow-up began at LVT diagnosis. For mortality, follow-up extended to death or last clinical encounter. For bleeding and embolic outcomes, follow-up extended to event or last clinical encounter. For LVT resolution, follow-up extended to first imaging documenting complete resolution or last imaging showing persistent thrombus. Continuous variables are reported as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range [IQR]); categorical variables are reported as number and percentage.

Results

A total of 559 patients were originally identified with LVT, of whom 23 had active malignancy, confirmed LVT, and preserved LVEF. Mean age was 66 (16) years, 17 patients (73.9%) were men, and mean LVEF at LVT diagnosis was 57.3% (6.7%). Hypertension was present in 8 patients (34.8%), diabetes mellitus in 7 (30.4%), prior stroke or TIA in 2 (8.7%), and atrial fibrillation in 2 (8.7%). 6 (26.1%) patients had prior percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass grafting. Mean serum creatinine was 1.03 mg/dL, and median follow-up was 5.8 years (IQR, 3.1–9.1 years). LVT was diagnosed by transthoracic echocardiography in all patients, with all LVT appearing in the left ventricular apex.

At discharge, 17 patients (73.9%) received warfarin and 6 (26.1%) received a DOAC; all DOAC-treated patients received apixaban. Anticoagulation was initiated a median of 2 days after diagnosis (IQR, 0–3 days), with 18 patients (78.3%) started within 3 days. Among available documentation, median recorded anticoagulation duration was 1176 days (IQR, 109–2087 days), and 13 patients (56.5%) had documented anticoagulation for more than 1 year. Because portions of longitudinal care occurred outside the Mayo Clinic Enterprise, this reflects documented duration in available records rather than confirmed total therapy duration.

Overall, 14 patients (60.9%) experienced at least one major adverse outcome, defined as all-cause mortality, ischemic stroke or TIA, or clinically significant bleeding (Table 1). All-cause mortality occurred in 14 patients (60.9%), with median time to death of 1.9 years (IQR, 0.8–3.1 years). Clinically significant bleeding occurred in 5 patients (21.7%), with median time to bleeding of 1.2 years (IQR, 0.6–2.4 years). No ischemic stroke or TIA events were observed. LVT resolution occurred in 20 patients (87.0%), whereas persistent thrombus was observed in 3 (13.0%). Median time to thrombus resolution was 0.7 years (IQR, 0.4–1.5 years). Among DOAC-treated patients, mortality occurred in 2 of 6 (33.3%), bleeding in 1 of 6 (16.7%), and LVT resolution in 6 of 6 (100%). Among warfarin-treated patients, mortality occurred in 12 of 17 (70.6%), bleeding in 4 of 17 (23.5%), and LVT resolution in 14 of 17 (82.4%). These crude comparisons were unadjusted and descriptive only.

Discussion

In this multicenter descriptive cohort of patients with active malignancy, confirmed LVT, and preserved LVEF, we observed frequent adverse clinical outcomes despite frequent thrombus resolution. More than half of patients died during follow-up, approximately one-fifth experienced clinically significant bleeding, and no ischemic stroke or TIA events were observed.

The occurrence of LVT despite preserved LVEF likely reflects the interaction of malignancy-associated hypercoagulability with patient-specific myocardial and vascular factors. Cancer can promote thrombosis through tissue factor expression, inflammatory cytokine activation, platelet activation, endothelial injury, procoagulant microparticles, neutrophil extracellular traps, and treatment-related vascular toxicity [3], [4], [5], [6]. DeGroat and colleagues previously described LV thrombi in patients with malignancy and normal wall motion, suggesting that cancer-associated hypercoagulability may contribute to intracardiac thrombosis even without overt systolic dysfunction [6]. Our study extends these observations by providing a contemporary multicenter description of patients with cancer-associated LVT and preserved LVEF.

Thrombus resolution was common, but imaging-based disappearance cannot definitively distinguish dissolution from embolization. Clinically silent embolization or events managed outside the study institutions cannot be excluded. The absence of observed stroke or TIA may reflect early anticoagulation, small sample size, competing mortality, thrombus morphology, or incomplete capture of outside events. Crude outcomes appeared numerically different between DOAC- and warfarin-treated patients, but anticoagulant selection was nonrandomized and may have reflected cancer type, prognosis, bleeding risk, renal function, drug-drug interactions, anticipated procedures, clinician preference, and follow-up logistics. These findings should not be interpreted as evidence of comparative effectiveness or safety.

This study has important limitations. The retrospective design introduces selection bias, incomplete documentation, and residual confounding. The sample size was small, limiting statistical power and precluding multivariable adjustment. Anticoagulant selection was subject to indication bias, and adherence, total anticoagulation duration, warfarin time in therapeutic range, regimen changes, and some outcomes could not be reliably captured for all patients. Cancer stage, active treatment status, regional wall motion abnormalities, thrombus size, and morphology were not uniformly documented. Cause of death was not uniformly adjudicated. Finally, this was a descriptive cohort of confirmed cases rather than a denominator-based screening study and cannot estimate LVT incidence or prevalence among patients with cancer.

In conclusion, cancer-associated LVT may occur despite preserved LVEF and appears to identify a high-risk phenotype with substantial mortality. Thrombus resolution was common, but optimal anticoagulation strategy, treatment duration, imaging surveillance, and embolic risk remain uncertain.
