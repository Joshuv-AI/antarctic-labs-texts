Mastectomy Patients’ Perspectives on Breast Reconstruction at a Tertiary Care Center in Eastern Saudi Arabia
Authors: Raseel B. Almutairi, Batool Y. Altayeb, Zahra N. Alqarrashi, Lyan M. Alghamdi, Zahra H. Alghazwi, Seba Samer Abduljabbar, Laila H. Alshawaf, Reem K. Aljehani, Mariam M. AlQurashi, Eiman Mohammed AlShammari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Breast reconstruction surgery (BRS) following mastectomy offers psychological and physical benefits; however, its acceptance remains limited among patients. Cultural perceptions, lack of awareness, and limited communication with healthcare providers may contribute to this gap.Objectives:To assess knowledge, misconceptions, attitudes, barriers, and motivators related to BRS among mastectomy patients in Eastern Saudi Arabia, and to explore associations with demographic and clinical factors.Methods:This cross-sectional survey was conducted from February to June 2025 at King Fahd Hospital of the University, Al Khobar, Saudi Arabia, and included all women who underwent mastectomy for breast cancer between 2016 and 2025. Data were elicited using a self-administered questionnaire assessing demographics, breast cancer history, knowledge and misconceptions about BRS, attitudes, and perceived barriers and motivators.Results:Of 144 eligible patients, 100 completed the questionnaire (response rate: 69.4%). Only 15% of patients had undergone BRS. Most patients had moderate misconceptions (85%), while 22% had excellent knowledge about BRS. Nonetheless, 65% had a positive attitude and 52% expressed willingness to undergo BRS. Key barriers included fear of additional surgery (42.4%) and lack of knowledge (38.8%), while key motivators were restoring body image (93.3%) and improving self-esteem (80%). In the univariate analysis, significant associations were observed between knowledge levels and factors such as age (P = 0.010), education (P < 0.001), income (P = 0.008), and current/past employment in healthcare (P = 0.008). However, in the multivariate logistic regression analysis, no variable was found to be an independent predictor of high knowledge of BRS.Conclusion:In breast cancer patients who underwent mastectomy in Eastern Saudi Arabia, there is positive attitude and willingness toward breast reconstruction surgery, yet the proportion of patients who underwent the surgery is low. The primary barriers identified are modifiable, and thus enhanced patient education and counseling may improve decision-making about breast reconstruction surgery.

INTRODUCTION

Breast cancer is a major global health problem that affects the well-being of women. In 2022, it was the most prevalent cancer among women in 157 of 185 countries.[1] In Saudi Arabia, breast cancer was the most common cancer among Saudi nationals in 2020, with a prevalence rate of 17.8%, and was responsible for 31.4% of all cancers recorded in women.[2]

Mastectomy is a widely used treatment for breast cancer in patients with large or multifocal tumors.[3] Despite its clinical benefits, mastectomy can affect a patient’s body image and emotional well-being. Thus, breast reconstruction, a surgical procedure aimed at rebuilding the shape and appearance of the breast, can provide better cosmetic and psychological results by improving body image, enhancing self-esteem, reducing feelings of disfigurement, increasing quality of life, and improving sexual well-being. A systematic review of USA-based studies conducted in 2024 showed a significant increase in breast reconstruction rates, from approximately 36% in 2004 to nearly 63% in 2016.[4] Nationwide data regarding the prevalence of breast reconstruction after mastectomy in Saudi Arabia are lacking. However, a study conducted at King Abdulaziz University Hospital in Jeddah reported that only 38.6% of the participants were willing to undergo breast reconstruction and the main motivation was to regain femininity.[5]

Nevertheless, several barriers limit the widespread use of this procedure, such as age, ethnicity, income, cultural or religious beliefs, and fear of complications.[6] More importantly, patient misconceptions and concerns about the safety, timing, and potential impact of reconstruction on cancer outcomes continue to pose significant obstacle.[7] Currently, in the Gulf countries, there is a notable gap in understanding attitudes toward breast reconstruction among breast cancer patients. This is particularly true in regard to cultural and religious perceptions, which have a substantial influence on the acceptance of post-mastectomy breast reconstruction.[8]

In Saudi Arabia, there are no comprehensive analyses of knowledge, misconceptions, attitudes, barriers, and motivators toward breast reconstruction, especially among women in the Eastern Province, who account for the highest number of cases.[2] Therefore, this study aimed to provide a better understanding of the knowledge, misconceptions, attitudes, barriers, and motivations about breast reconstruction surgery among women undergoing mastectomy at a Saudi tertiary care center in the Eastern Region. In addition to investigating the association with demographic and socioeconomic factors, this study explored the quality of prior knowledge and the role of healthcare providers.

METHODS

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted between February 20, 2025, and June 4, 2025, at King Fahd Hospital of the University (KFHU), Al Khobar, Saudi Arabia. KFHU is a tertiary care and teaching hospital that includes a specialized breast surgery unit, performing nearly 300 breast cancer surgeries annually, of which around 30 are mastectomy cases. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki, 2024. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Imam Abdulrahman Bin Faisal University prior to study initiation. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. The confidentiality and anonymity of the participants were maintained throughout the study.

Participants and sampling

The target population included women patients who had undergone mastectomy for the treatment of breast cancer at KFHU between April 2016 and February 2025. The 9-year study period was selected based on the availability of complete and accessible patient records within the hospital database. The inclusion criteria were adult women who underwent mastectomy for breast cancer within this timeframe. Women who underwent mastectomy for reasons other than breast cancer treatment, and those with cognitive or functional impairments that limited their ability to participate were excluded from the study.

Data collection tools and variables

The questionnaire was developed following an extensive literature review[589101112] and in consultation with an expert panel of four. The panel included three consultant surgeons from different subspecialties, namely, breast oncology surgery, plastic breast reconstructive surgery, and breast disease and oncoplastic surgery, along with an operating room nurse manager with >10 years of experience in perioperative and surgical nursing.

The panel reviewed all items for content validity, clarity, and cultural suitability. Revisions were made accordingly. The finalized English version was translated into Arabic using a forward–backward translation process consistent with WHO guidelines, with discrepancies were resolved through consensus. Internal consistency of the questionnaire was preliminarily assessed during the pilot phase (n = 10). Subsequently, Cronbach’s alpha was recalculated using the full study sample to provide a more reliable and robust estimate of internal consistency.

The questionnaire assessed the participants’ knowledge, attitudes, and experiences related to breast reconstruction and consisted of 9 main sections. The average completion time was approximately 15 minutes. The first section gathered demographic and socioeconomic data such as age, menopausal status, employment status, employment in the healthcare field, and place of residence. The second section included breast cancer-related data, including: age at mastectomy, receipt of adjuvant therapy (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, hormonal therapy), type of mastectomy (unilateral or bilateral), metastasis status, treatment completion status, and family history of breast cancer.

The third section assessed prior knowledge of breast reconstruction, including whether the participants had heard of the procedure and whether their breast surgeons had discussed it with them. The fourth section measured factual knowledge through three multiple-choice questions related to the definition, timing, and types of breast reconstruction. Patients were classified as having excellent (3/3, correct), good (2/3), weak (1/3), or poor (0/3) knowledge levels.

The fifth section evaluated misconceptions using seven belief-based statements rated on a 5-point Likert scale. The total misconception score ranged from 7 to 35 and was categorized into high (≥25), moderate (14–24), and low (<14) levels of misconception. The sixth section assessed patients’ attitudes toward breast reconstruction using seven items covering domains such as body image, mental health, self-esteem, quality of life, and social and sexual well-being. Each item was rated on a 5-point scale. The total attitude score ranged from 7 to 35 and was categorized as positive (≥25), neutral (14–24), or negative (<14). These categories were defined based on the overall scoring structure of the instrument to facilitate meaningful interpretation of participants’ attitudes.

The seventh section inquired about whether the participants had undergone breast reconstruction, and if so, whether it was immediate or delayed. The eighth section investigated barriers to breast reconstruction, including religious and social beliefs, lack of surgical or family support, limited-service availability, financial burden, and health-related or psychological concerns. The ninth section explored the potential motivators for undergoing reconstruction, including psychological and physical benefits, physician recommendations, spousal support, and social or lifestyle factors.

Although the instrument was newly created, the chosen thresholds were aligned with the scoring approaches commonly used in previous descriptive studies accessing knowledge and attitudes related to breast reconstruction and breast cancer care. Therefore, the cutoffs served as conceptually guided and field-consistent thresholds, intended to facilitate meaningful interpretation of the descriptive data rather than functioning as empirically derived psychometric criteria.

Pilot testing

A pilot study involving 10 mastectomy patients was conducted to evaluate the questionnaire’s clarity, comprehensibility, and feasibility. The pilot testing confirmed that all items were easily understood and culturally appropriate, and only minor linguistic adjustments were made accordingly. Internal consistency was preliminarily assessed during the pilot phase (n = 10) using Cronbach’s alpha. This was done to test the reliability of the questionnaire before administering it to the main study sample. The misconceptions section (7 Likert-scale items) yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.794, and the attitude section (7 Likert-scale items) yielded a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.796, indicating acceptable internal consistency.

Data collection process

Participants were identified through the hospital’s breast surgery records. Eligible participants were contacted by phone call and provided with a link to an online Google Form questionnaire via WhatsApp. Participants who did not complete the questionnaire within two days received a reminder message, followed by a second phone call 1 week later. Those who still had not responded were given an additional 2-day extension, after which data collection was closed. This structured follow-up process was implemented to maximize response rates while maintaining voluntary participation and confidentiality.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). All items in the online questionnaire were set as mandatory fields, and therefore no missing data were encountered; consequently, no missing data handling procedures were applicable. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ demographic characteristics, breast cancer history, and responses to survey items. The data were reported as means and standard deviation for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. To assess participants’ levels of knowledge, misconceptions, and attitudes toward breast reconstruction surgery, responses were categorized and scored based on predefined criteria. Knowledge levels were classified as poor, weak, good, or excellent; misconception levels were categorized as low, moderate, or high; and attitudes were categorized as positive, neutral, or negative. Inferential analysis was conducted to assess associations between variables. The association between patients’ demographic and clinical characteristics and their levels of knowledge, misconception, and attitude was assessed using the Chi-square test of independence (χ2). When appropriate, Fisher’s exact test was used to compare small cell counts. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. A multivariable (multiple) logistic regression analysis was performed to identify independent predictors of breast reconstruction surgery acceptance. Variables with a P value < 0.05 in the bivariate analysis were included in the model. Adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported.

RESULTS

A total of 220 mastectomy patients were identified during the study period. Of these, 22 had died, 42 could not be reached due to outdated or incorrect contact information, and 2 were unable to participate due to cognitive impairment. Further, 10 participants were excluded because they had participated in the pilot study. Therefore, the questionnaire was distributed to 144 eligible participants, of whom 100 completed the survey, for a response rate of 69.4%. The internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) for the misconceptions based on the full dataset of 100 participants was 0.832 across the 7 Likert-scale items, indicating acceptable reliability. The attitude section demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.901 across the 7 Likert-scale items.

The mean age of the patients was 55.2 (±11.4) years, with only 4% being aged <40 years. In addition, most patients were Saudi (60%), married (63%), had children (89%), menopausal (75%), not employed (54%), had mastectomy for one breast (93%), and a family history of breast cancer (36%) [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

This study found that that among breast cancer patients who had undergone mastectomy at a tertiary hospital in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia, being aged <40 years, single, currently or previous employment in healthcare, having a postgraduate degree, and a monthly family income of ≥25,000 Saudi Riyals were factors associated with good to excellent knowledge of breast reconstruction surgery. In addition, patients aged 40–45 years and non-Saudis had higher levels of misconceptions. Further, of the 15% of patients who had undergone breast reconstruction surgery, the most common motivators for undergoing surgery were achieving physical harmony and restoring a natural appearance, the desire to regain self-acceptance, boosting self-esteem, and greater freedom in clothing choices. The most common barriers were unwillingness to undergo additional surgery, lack of knowledge about the procedure, and fear of surgery or anesthesia. These findings provide insight into the influencing factors and related misconceptions regarding breast reconstruction surgery following mastectomy.

In the current study, nearly half of the patients had received information regarding breast reconstruction from their breast surgeons, plastic surgeons, or other doctors. In contrast, a study from the Western region of Saudi Arabia found that only slightly more than one-fourth of breast cancer patients had received information regarding breast reconstruction from doctors, and also that 41.5% of patients were unaware of their options and decision for undergoing breast reconstruction following mastectomy.[5]

One of the most important factors influencing the decision to undergo breast reconstruction is knowledge regarding the surgery and its results, and lack of knowledge or misconceptions regarding this is potentially one of the most important barriers.[891314] In this study, only 22% of patients demonstrated excellent knowledge, while 34% had poor knowledge of the procedure. Further, 85% of the patients showed a moderate level of misconception, especially those aged 40-49 and non-Saudi women. Collectively, this highlights the role of surgeons, specifically breast surgeons, in improving knowledge levels that could potentially increase the adoption rates of this breast reconstruction following mastectomy.

Most patients in the current study had a positive attitude towards breast reconstruction surgery (65%), were willing to undergo the procedure (52%), and believed that breast reconstruction was important for improving body image (77%), enhancing mental health (74%), and boosting self-esteem (71%). This is in line with the findings of Mortada et al.[15] which found that in the general population of Saudi Arabia, the overall attitude towards breast reconstruction surgery is positive and considered to improve the quality of life of the patients.[15] However, only 15% of patients underwent the procedure in our study, which is similar to that reported from Jeddah (16.5%).[9] Collectively, these findings suggest that the relatively low level of breast reconstruction surgeries in Saudi Arabia is more likely due to other barriers rather than negative attitude and perception toward the procedure.

In the current study, the main barrier to undergoing surgery was an unwillingness to undergo additional surgery (42.4%), whereas the primary motivator was to achieve physical harmony with a natural appearance (93.3%), which is very similar to one study from Jeddah, where the primary barrier was to avoid additional surgeries (31.3%), and the main motivator was to regain femininity (26.8%).[5] A 2013 study from Jeddah reported inadequate information on the procedure (63%) and its complication concerns (68%) were reported as the main barriers.[9] The contrast between the older and more recent studies indicate improvements in knowledge impartment regarding the procedure in Saudi Arabia.

Our study found a statistically significant association between the awareness and knowledge levels and demographic variables such as age and education level. These associations were also observed in the study by Awan et al.,[9] who demonstrated that higher educational attainment was significantly associated with greater awareness and increased likelihood of considering breast reconstruction surgery. Our results also align with those of Michael et al., who reported that age and level of education were significantly associated with a positive attitude towards breast reconstruction.[16] Similarly, a study from Riyadh found that patients aged ≤40 years were significantly more willing to undergo breast reconstruction.[17]

Patients in this study were recruited from a single hospital in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia; therefore, generalizations should be made with caution. Therefore, a large-scale multi-center and multi-city study is required to provide more generalizable findings. Future studies can also assess the patients’ perceptions of breast reconstruction surgery after receiving counseling or among those who have undergone the procedure.

CONCLUSION

This study found that in breast cancer patients who underwent mastectomy in the Eastern Province of Saudi Arabia, there is positive attitude and willingness toward breast reconstruction surgery and recognition of its psychosocial benefits. However, the proportion of patients who underwent the surgery is relatively low. The primary barriers were unwillingness to undergo additional surgery, limited knowledge, and fear of surgery, and thus enhanced patient education and counseling may improve the number of patients undergoing of breast reconstruction.
