Evaluating the Correlation between IQ and Behavior of 5–10-year-old Children in Dental Operatory: A Cross-sectional Study
Authors: Shweta Chauhan, Neha Sheoran, Bhavna Gupta Saraf, Charul Gupta, Shikha Vashist, Parul Garg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildren with higher intelligence quotients (IQs) tend to manage dental stress better, follow instructions, and cooperate calmly. Lower IQs may correlate with increased anxiety and reduced compliance. Recognizing these behavioral patterns enables clinicians to tailor supportive approaches, enhancing treatment experiences and outcomes.AimThis study aimed to evaluate and compare the relation between children's IQ and their behavior in the dental operatory.Materials and methodsThis study evaluated how the IQ of children aged 5–10 years influenced their cooperation during dental treatments. A total of 180 children with no previous dental history, requiring pulpectomy or extraction in primary teeth, were selected for the study. All participants received local anesthesia before the procedure. The children's IQs were measured using the Raven's Progressive Matrices test, and the treatments were subsequently performed. Cooperation levels were assessed using the Modified Frankl's behavior rating scale, ranging from 1 (definitely negative) to 5 (definitely positive). The study analyzed the relationship between IQ scores and the type of cooperative behavior during dental procedures.ResultsThis study establishes a statistically significant link between higher IQ and better behavioral cooperation in children during pediatric dental procedures. Integrating cognitive assessment can enhance behavior management strategies and optimize clinical outcomes.ConclusionHigh IQ enhances children's adaptability in dental settings, enabling tailored behavior modification strategies for more effective treatment outcomes.Clinical significanceUnderstanding a child's IQ helps dentists choose better behavior techniques, improving cooperation and treatment success.

Introduction

Dental visits can trigger fear and discomfort, leading to unpredictable reactions like crying or resistance. Negative experiences may cause dental anxiety, reducing treatment acceptance. Effective behavior management ensures better cooperation and long-term oral health.1–4

Childhood dental fear and behavior management problems arise from multiple factors, including parenting styles, personality traits, sociocultural influences, parental experiences, and anxiety. A child's adaptive skills and coping strategies significantly impact their response to stressful dental environments and treatment acceptance.2–5

Anxiety heightens awareness and pain perception, making dental procedures more distressing and reducing a child's cooperation during treatment. Effective management improves their experience.6–8 Dental anxiety is a common fear but tends to decrease with age as coping mechanisms improve.9

Dentistry emphasizes the human connection between practitioners and patients. Despite technological advances, pediatric dentists must manage diverse cooperation levels in children, ensuring effective treatments while creating positive experiences to reduce dental anxiety.10 Wright et al. emphasize two key goals for dental teams: providing effective, efficient treatment while fostering a positive attitude in patients to enhance cooperation and dental experiences.11 Caregivers must consider both the physical health and mental well-being of patients for effective treatment.12

Raven's Progressive Matrices define intelligence as a general ability, distinguishing fluid intelligence (reasoning and problem-solving) from crystallized intelligence (acquired knowledge). Fluid intelligence is not fixed—it evolves with maturation and experiences, shaping cognitive skills. Various factors, including education and environment, influence its development over time.13–15

Intelligence quotients measure an individual's relative intelligence using standardized tests. It affects a child's understanding of causes, consequences, information processing, and instructions effectively.16 IQ affects a child's behavior, cooperation, and response during dental procedures and treatment.17,18 IQ is considered a key indicator of success across various aspects of life, reflecting cognitive ability. Studies suggest that low IQ is linked to psychological disorders in childhood, influencing emotional regulation, learning difficulties, and social adaptation, affecting overall development.19,20

Studies confirm that general intelligence plays a crucial role in a child's anxiety levels during their first dental visit. Research suggests that children with low IQ (<68) require significantly more time to accept treatment, likely due to heightened stress and difficulty processing unfamiliar experiences.21

Children with intellectual disabilities face complex challenges, with limited research on their behavioral conditions. Low intellectual capacity affects daily life, as intellectual impairment exists on a continuum. Personalized support is essential, regardless of test scores, ensuring care tailored to individual needs, abilities, and preferences.22

This study examines the link between children's IQ levels and their performance and cooperation during dental treatment. Few studies focus on IQ's impact on behavior in medical settings, but existing research aids in refining behavior control techniques to predict and improve young patients' cooperation and treatment experiences.

Material and Methodology

Study Design and Ethical Compliance

A descriptive retrospective cross-sectional investigation was carried out at the Department of Pediatric Dentistry, Sudha Rustagi College of Dental Sciences and Research (SRCDSR), Faridabad, India. The research targeted healthy pediatric patients newly referred for dental care. Institutional ethics clearance was secured from SRCDSR's Ethics Committee (Registration No. EC/NEW/INST/2022/2719), and the protocol was prospectively registered with the Clinical Trials Registry–India under reference number CTRI/2025/07/089976. The study was conducted over a three-month period, spanning November 2024 to January 2025.

Participant Selection and Criteria

One hundred and eighty children (52 females, 128 males) aged 5–10 years were recruited upon obtaining informed parental consent. Eligibility criteria required participants to present with at least one primary tooth indicated for pulpectomy or extraction under local anesthesia. Only children classified as ASA I (American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status Classification I) with no documented allergy to anesthetic agents were enrolled. Subjects were excluded if their first session, i.e., IQ evaluation session, exceeded 30 minutes in duration or if they failed to meet the inclusion parameters.

Clinical Workflow and Cognitive Assessment

At the initial clinical visit, a comprehensive oral evaluation and necessary radiographic imaging were performed by a pediatric dentist. Candidates requiring pulpectomy or extraction under anesthesia were considered for inclusion. Cognitive ability was assessed using Raven's Progressive Matrices (RPM), a tool well-suited for the selected age range. This evaluation was carried out in a neutral, nonclinical setting prior to enrollment to minimize environmental bias. Visual records included:

Results

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 21.0 (SPSS for Windows; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL), with the statistical significance threshold set at p < 0.05. Since data were normally distributed, inferential comparisons were conducted using one-way ANOVA, followed by Dunnett's post hoc test to evaluate intergroup differences (Figs 4 to 6).

Discussion

Avoidant behaviors in pediatric dental practice are commonly attributed to dental fear and anxiety (DF/DA), terms frequently used synonymously in clinical literature. DF/DA constitutes a primary factor contributing to behavioral management difficulties and treatment avoidance, often necessitating alternative approaches, including conscious sedation or general anesthesia. Current evidence indicates that 5 to 52% of children experience clinically significant DF/DA, presenting substantial challenges for treatment delivery. Despite significant advances in pain management and clinical techniques, the prevalence of dental anxiety has remained relatively stable over five decades.24

Dental anxiety typically emerges during early childhood and can establish persistent avoidance patterns that compromise lifelong oral health. The manifestation varies from specific stimulus-related fears, such as injection or drill phobias, to generalized anticipatory anxiety. Regardless of triggering factors, children demonstrate consistent emotional responses characterized by distress and resistance to treatment.

Sunil et al., identified a multifactorial etiology underlying the development of dental anxiety in children. Key contributors include inherent personality traits, anticipatory pain perception, and prior traumatic dental encounters. Importantly, social learning mechanisms—particularly the transmission of anxiety from parents or peers—can elicit apprehension even in the absence of direct negative experiences. Hematophobia, especially during procedures involving visible bleeding, further exacerbates anxiety responses. Recognizing these psychosocial and behavioral determinants is critical for pediatric dentists in formulating empathetic, child-centered strategies that mitigate anxiety, enhance cooperation, and foster long-term positive attitudes toward oral health care.25

Middle childhood, particularly ages 6–9 years, represents a critical period for dental anxiety development. Vlad et al. investigated this population using the Abeer Children Dental Anxiety Scale (ACDAS) and salivary cortisol biomarkers in 389 children. Their study demonstrated high dental anxiety prevalence, with girls showing significantly greater susceptibility (OR: 1.533, p = 0.041). Children with anxiety exhibited significantly elevated salivary cortisol levels compared to nonanxious peers (median: 1.251 vs 1.091 ng/mL, p < 0.001). A moderate positive correlation between cortisol levels and ACDAS scores was observed (r = 0.411, p < 0.001), providing biological validation of self-reported anxiety measures. These findings confirm that children in early school years can reliably report their emotional states and validate ACDAS as an effective anxiety assessment tool during this developmental stage.26

The study population comprised children aged 5–10 years, representing the middle childhood developmental period characterized by enhanced emotional regulation, cognitive maturation, and formation of initial attitudes toward healthcare experiences. This developmental stage is notable for increased self-control and emerging sense of responsibility. Previous research by Vlad et al. has consistently documented high dental anxiety prevalence within this age group.26 Importantly, all study participants were attending their first dental visit, eliminating the potential confounding influence of prior dental experiences on anxiety levels and behavioral responses, thereby ensuring valid baseline assessments.

Intelligence quotient (IQ) encompasses two fundamental components: fluid intelligence and crystallized intelligence. Fluid intelligence represents the capacity for abstract reasoning, problem-solving, and logical thinking, typically evaluated through non-verbal assessments. Crystallized intelligence reflects accumulated knowledge and verbal skills acquired through education and life experiences, commonly assessed through language-based evaluations.27

While IQ serves as an established predictor of academic and social success, traditional assessments may not capture emotional factors that influence performance. An anxious child with high cognitive ability may demonstrate impaired test performance due to emotional interference. Emotional quotient (EQ) assessment provides valuable insights into how emotions affect behavior and learning.28 The combined evaluation of IQ and EQ offers clinicians a more comprehensive understanding of a child's developmental profile, as highlighted by Dave et al.'s research emphasizing the clinical value of emotional assessment in pediatric populations.2

The Raven's Colored Progressive Matrices (CPM) represents a well-established non-verbal intelligence assessment designed to evaluate abstract reasoning and problem-solving abilities central to Spearman's g-factor of general cognitive capacity. This instrument demonstrates particular utility in pediatric populations where verbal limitations may compromise traditional assessment approaches. The CPM's nonverbal format and culturally neutral design enable equitable evaluation across diverse linguistic and sociocultural backgrounds, making it ideal for children, individuals with language impairments, and multicultural clinical settings. Its widespread application in educational and psychological evaluations across countries, including the United Kingdom and Australia demonstrates global relevance in measuring cognitive potential independent of prior learning or verbal proficiency.29

Kazem et al. further validated the Colored Progressive Matrices' psychometric reliability and cross-cultural applicability, demonstrating effective measurement of abstract reasoning with minimal cultural bias, consistent with Spearman's theoretical framework. In developing nations, including Arab countries, the absence of locally standardized intelligence assessments—often due to financial and infrastructure limitations—necessitates the adaptation of established Western instruments such as the Raven's Colored Progressive Matrices. Local standardization of such tools enhances diagnostic accuracy in educational and clinical settings while establishing culturally relevant norms, facilitating meaningful international comparisons, and advancing cognitive assessment practices in pediatric populations.30

The present study evaluated the influence of children's intellectual quotient (IQ) on behavioral responses during dental treatment, specifically examining the correlation between cognitive ability and cooperation as measured by the Modified Frankl Behavior Rating Scale. Results demonstrated a statistically significant positive association, indicating that higher IQ levels predict improved cooperative behavior during dental procedures.

Children with lower cognitive capabilities required extended adaptation periods to the dental environment and demonstrated reduced cooperation during clinical interventions. Conversely, children with higher IQs exhibited superior cooperation, with those in the superior intelligence range displaying consistently positive behavior. Children with borderline or below-average IQ scores tended toward uncooperative responses. These findings establish a clear relationship between cognitive ability and dental cooperation, suggesting that higher IQ facilitates better communication skills, emotional regulation, and stress management during dental procedures.1,3,31

Aminabadi et al. support these observations, demonstrating that individuals with higher emotional intelligence exhibit lower anxiety levels, attributed to enhanced emotional regulation and more effective coping strategies.3 This reinforces the interconnected nature of cognitive and emotional factors in pediatric dental behavior.

Several studies corroborate our findings regarding the IQ-behavior relationship in pediatric dentistry. Khosrozadeh et al. investigated children aged 5–10 years and demonstrated a significant positive correlation between IQ and cooperative behavior during dental treatment using Frankl's Behavior Rating Scale. Children with higher IQ scores showed notably greater cooperation, while those with lower scores required extended acclimatization periods and displayed less favorable behavior. These findings reinforce the clinical relevance of cognitive assessment in pediatric dentistry and suggest that IQ may serve as a predictive marker for behavioral management strategies.1

Shetty et al. reported consistent findings, demonstrating significant positive correlations between IQ scores and behavioral responses in typically developing children using Frankl's Behavior Rating Scale. However, this association was not observed in children with hearing and speech impairments, suggesting that sensory limitations may moderate the relationship between cognitive ability and cooperative behavior during dental procedures.17

AbdelRazek et al. examined the relationship between children's IQ and dental fear and anxiety (DFA) during initial dental visits in 88 children aged 6–12 years. Higher IQ scores correlated with reduced DFA and enhanced cooperation, while parental anxiety showed a positive correlation with children's fear levels. These findings emphasize the importance of addressing both cognitive and emotional factors in pediatric dental care delivery.18

Boka et al. investigated children's psychological functioning and its impact on dental anxiety and behavior during local anesthesia administration. Children with stronger pro-social traits demonstrated lower anxiety and greater cooperation, while high dental fear scores predicted uncooperative behavior. Notably, parental dental anxiety showed no significant influence on children's responses, and children with longer intervals since previous dental visits exhibited improved behavior during anesthesia, highlighting experience as a moderating factor.32

In contrast to our study, Asokan et al. identified a significant inverse relationship between IQ and oral health-related quality of life in children aged 10–11 years. Although dental anxiety showed negative correlation with both IQ and oral health-related quality of life, this relationship was not statistically significant. These findings suggest that cognitive ability may influence children's perception of oral health and their behavioral responses during dental care.33

The consistent findings across multiple studies indicate that cognitive assessment may serve as a valuable screening tool for predicting behavioral responses in pediatric dental patients. Children with higher cognitive abilities appear better equipped to understand treatment procedures, communicate effectively with dental staff, and employ appropriate coping strategies during potentially stressful situations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a statistically significant positive correlation (p < 0.001) between children's intellectual quotient and cooperative behavior during dental treatment. Children with higher IQ scores consistently exhibited superior cooperation, while those with lower cognitive abilities required extended adaptation periods and showed reduced cooperation. The 58.44-point IQ difference between the least and most cooperative groups establishes cognitive ability as a reliable predictor of pediatric dental behavior. These findings suggest that cognitive assessment using culturally neutral tools like Raven's Colored Progressive Matrices can serve as a valuable screening method for developing individualized behavioral management strategies in pediatric dental practice.
