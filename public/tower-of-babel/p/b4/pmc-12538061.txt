Association between serum leptin and accelerated biological aging in US adults: Findings from NHANES III
Authors: Kai Wei
Published: 2025

Introduction

Obesity has been increasingly recognized not only as a risk factor for chronic diseases but also as a contributor to the aging process [1]. A growing body of evidence has shown that obesity indicators, such as visceral fat [2], are associated with biological aging measures. Leptin, an adipocyte-derived cytokine, plays a central role in energy homeostasis and is closely linked to the degree of adiposity [3]. Despite its strong connection with obesity, it remains unclear whether elevated leptin contributes to biological aging or merely reflects adiposity-related physiological changes. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the association between serum leptin levels and biological age acceleration using data from the Third National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES III).

Method

Data were obtained from the NHANES III, a nationally representative cross-sectional survey conducted in the United States between 1988 and 1994. We initially identified 6,415 adults aged ≥20 years with available serum leptin data. Serum leptin concentrations were measured from stored fasting serum samples collected during the morning examination using a radioimmunoassay with a polyclonal antibody against recombinant human leptin. Serum leptin values were log-transformed to approximate a normal distribution prior to analysis. We calculated three validated biological age algorithms: Phenotypic Age (PhenoAge), Klemera–Doubal Biological Age (KD-BioAge), and Anthropometric Age (AnthroAge). PhenoAge and KD-BioAge were constructed as previously described, using clinical chemistry and hematologic biomarkers. AnthroAge was developed in a framework similar to PhenoAge but relied exclusively on anthropometric measures. Specifically, it was derived from a sex-stratified Gompertz proportional hazards model predicting 10-year all-cause mortality, with height, weight, and waist circumference combined to capture body composition–related biological aging while minimizing measurement complexity [4]. For each aging algorithms, biological aging acceleration was calculated as the difference between biological and chronological age, with positive values indicating accelerated biological aging. Covariates included demographics, socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors, systolic blood pressure, laboratory biomarkers, self-reported chronic conditions, and prescription medication use. Multiple imputation was addressed missing values in covariates. Survey-weighted multivariable linear regressions were used to examine associations between serum leptin and three biological aging measures. Analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1, with two-sided p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

After adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, lifestyle, and clinical factors, each one-unit increase in log-transformed leptin (corresponding to roughly a 2.7-fold higher leptin concentration) was associated with a 1.01-year higher PhenoAge acceleration (95% CI: 0.68–1.35; p < 0.001), a 1.14-year higher KD-BioAge acceleration (95% CI: 0.66–1.63; p < 0.001), and a smaller but significant increase in AnthroAge acceleration (β = 0.36; 95% CI: 0.11–0.60; p = 0.008) (Fig. 1).

Stratified analyses revealed significant effect modification by age, sex, and obesity status. By age group, the associations were evident among participants younger than 60 years, but not among those ≥60 years. When stratified by sex, leptin was associated with accelerated PhenoAge in both men and women, with no evidence of interaction (p = 0.176). In contrast, the association with KD-BioAge was stronger in women (p for interaction = 0.010), and the association with AnthroAge was observed only in men (p for interaction = 0.004). With respect to obesity, the effect estimates were small in the normal-weight group, but increased markedly with higher BMI, showing significant associations in both overweight (PhenoAge: β = 1.44, 95% CI: 0.76–2.12; KD-BioAge: β = 1.61, 95% CI: 0.77–2.45) and obese participants (PhenoAge: β = 1.54, 95% CI: 0.97–2.12; KD-BioAge: β = 2.11, 95% CI: 1.07–3.14; AnthroAge: β = 2.21, 95% CI: 1.44–2.98), with all p for interaction <0.05.

Discussion

In this nationally representative sample of U.S. adults, we provide the first evidence that higher circulating leptin levels are linked to accelerated biological aging. These associations were robust across three distinct aging measures and further showed heterogeneity by age, sex, and obesity status, suggesting that the impact of leptin on aging may not be uniform across population subgroups.

Stratified analyses revealed that the associations were more pronounced in adults younger than 60 years, consistent with the evidence that altered leptin signaling and inflammatory responses may exert greater influence earlier in the aging process [3]. Further subgroup analyses indicated that the associations were stronger in overweight and obese participants, suggesting that excess adiposity may amplify leptin’s influence on biological aging. In this context, chronically elevated leptin can drive low-grade inflammation and oxidative stress, leading to DNA damage and senescence [1], while leptin resistance, common in obesity, disrupts central energy regulation and worsens metabolic dysfunction [5].

Our results underscore the potential utility of leptin as a biomarker of aging risk in population studies, with consistent associations observed across multiple biological aging measures. However, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Longitudinal analyses and mechanistic studies are needed to clarify whether leptin directly drives biological aging or primarily signals excess adiposity.
