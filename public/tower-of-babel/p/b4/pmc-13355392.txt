Antiseptic Efficacy and Ocular Surface Toxicity of Povidone-Iodine and Chlorhexidine for Endophthalmitis Isolates
Authors: Anam Ahmed, Heather Durkee, Alison Rodriguez Leiva, Salomon Merikansky, Suraj Paudyal, James Lai, Felipe Echeverri Tribin, Alexander Alfonso, Jorge Maestre-Mesa, Landon J. Rohowetz, Harry W. Flynn, Nicolas A. Yannuzzi, Darlene Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo compare the antimicrobial efficacy and ocular surface toxicity of two antiseptic agents: povidone-iodine (PI) and chlorhexidine gluconate (CHX) against clinical endophthalmitis isolates and to perform preliminary assessments of epithelial viability and inflammatory cytokine response following antiseptic exposure in a corneal tissue model.MethodsA well diffusion assay was used to assess inhibition zones of PI (5% and 10%) and CHX (0.05% and 0.1%) against 34 microbial isolates, including Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria and fungi. Additional testing was conducted using lower concentrations of PI (0.025% and 1%). Corneal epithelial tissue models, both uninfected and infected with Staphylococcus epidermidis, were treated with two antiseptic agents. Cell viability was assessed by MTT assay, and inflammatory response was evaluated by quantifying IL-8 via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay at multiple time points.ResultsPI at 5% and 10% demonstrated statistically significant broader antimicrobial inhibition across most isolates compared to CHX (P < 0.05), although CHX had greater efficacy against P. aeruginosa. PI at 0.025% showed no inhibition. CHX-treated corneal tissue retained higher viability (greater percent of living cells) in both healthy and infected models (71%–100%) compared to PI (9%–17%). PI-treated tissue showed higher IL-8 proinflammatory cytokine levels, and CHX elicited minimal inflammatory response.ConclusionsPI and CHX demonstrated broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity in vitro, with PI exhibiting greater antimicrobial activity across most isolates. CHX was associated with higher corneal epithelial viability and a lower inflammatory response of measured cytokines.

Endophthalmitis is a rare but devastating ocular infection that can lead to irreversible vision loss. It may arise from exogenous sources such as intraocular surgery, ocular trauma, or intravitreal injections, as well as from hematogenous spread.1,2 The most frequently implicated pathogens in post-procedural endophthalmitis include Staphylococcus epidermidis, Streptococcus mitis/oralis, and Staphylococcus aureus.1

To mitigate the risk of endophthalmitis, proper ocular surface antisepsis is a cornerstone of perioperative care. Povidone-iodine (PI) is the gold standard for antisepsis.3 PI consists of iodine, which provides its bactericidal properties, conjugated to povidone, its hydrophilic carrier. Together, these compounds provide broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity.4,5 Concentrations between 5% and 10% are typically used, as lower concentrations have been shown to be minimally effective.4 The kill time ranges from 15 to 120 seconds.6 Despite its efficacy, PI can be associated with ocular surface irritation, which has led to the evaluation of alternative antiseptic agents.7

One such alternative antiseptic is chlorhexidine gluconate (CHX), a bisbiguanide with membrane-disrupting antimicrobial action.8 Due to a lack of robust clinical data demonstrating its effectiveness in preventing post-injection endophthalmitis and concerns about corneal toxicity, CHX has not been widely adopted as a first-line ocular antiseptic in the United States.4 The concern regarding the ocular toxicity of chlorhexidine is primarily associated with nonaqueous formulations; aqueous chlorhexidine was initially used as a contact lens disinfectant and has been incorporated into treatment for acanthamoeba keratitis with few reports of intolerance or toxicity.8–11 Aqueous CHX at concentrations of 0.05% to 0.1% has been reported to be effective and better tolerated on the ocular surface than PI.7,9 A recent meta-analysis found no statistically significant difference in endophthalmitis rates between the two antiseptics when used for intravitreal injection antisepsis, suggesting comparable clinical efficacy despite their distinct chemical properties and in vitro profiles.12 There are also multiple perioperative factors that may influence endophthalmitis rates, including pre-filled syringes, mask use, patient movement, and no-talking policies.13,14

PI remains the standard for ocular antisepsis, yet there is also interest in CHX as an alternative, particularly in patients who experience discomfort with PI. International practice patterns also vary, with aqueous CHX used in select settings.7,9 Despite the widespread use of both PI and CHX in ophthalmology, data are limited regarding the in vitro efficacy of CHX and PI against the impact of clinical endophthalmitis isolates on corneal cells.15,16 The purpose of this study was to examine the in vitro antimicrobial efficacy of PI and CHX against patient-derived endophthalmitis isolates and perform preliminary evaluation of the ocular toxicity in a corneal tissue model.

Methods

Organism Selection and Characterization

Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained from the University of Miami Miller School of Medicine Sciences Subcommittee for the Protection of Human Subjects, and the research adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki (IRB Protocol Study ID #20070960). Retrospective review of the ocular microbiology database was performed to identify and retrieve microbial isolates collected from patients with endophthalmitis at the Bascom Palmer Eye Institute (n = 34) from June 2021 to December 2025. Endophthalmitis isolates were selected, as most cases of post-procedural endophthalmitis are due to the ocular surface flora that are introduced intraocularly, often despite antiseptic preparation.1 Organisms were selected to be inclusive of a diverse panel of isolates routinely recovered from endophthalmitis, including Staphylococcus epidermidis (methicillin-resistant [MRSE], n = 1; methicillin-susceptible [MSSE], n = 1), Streptococcus mitis/oralis (n = 2), Streptococcus sanguinis (n = 2), Staphylococcus lugdunensis (n = 2), methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA, n = 2), Enterococcus faecalis (n = 2), Bacillus spp. (n = 2), Escherichia coli (n = 2), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (n = 2), Morganella morganii (n = 2), Klebsiella pneumoniae (n = 2), Haemophilus influenzae (n = 1), Candida albicans (n = 5), Candida glabrata (n = 2), Aspergillus spp. (n = 2), and Fusarium spp. (n = 2). Clinical data were also collected and analyzed, including patient demographics, date of presentation, and clinical context of endophthalmitis (etiology).

Well Diffusion Assay

A well diffusion assay was performed to assess microbial susceptibility to the two antiseptics based on an established protocol.17 All bacterial isolates were standardized to a concentration of 1.5 × 108 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL in tryptic soy broth. For the fungal strains, the starting concentration was 1 × 106 CFU/mL. Mueller Hinton agar plates (blood-supplemented Mueller Hinton plates specifically for Streptococcus species, Sabouraud dextrose agar for Aspergillus and Fusarium spp., and chocolate agar plates for Haemophilus influenzae) were prepared and inoculated with each organism using a sterile cotton swab to ensure even distribution across the agar surface. Wells were aseptically created in the agar using a sterile borer (approximately 10 mm in diameter), and each well was filled with 350 µL of the following antiseptic solutions: PI at concentrations of 5% and 10%, and CHX at concentrations of 0.05% and 0.1%. Additional well diffusion tests were performed using lower concentrations of PI (0.025% and 1%) in a selected subset of isolates (n = 12; S. epidermidis, S. mitis/oralis, MRSA, P. aeruginosa, E. coli, and C. albicans), in order to perform preliminary testing on an emerging clinical practice in which dilute PI is added to balanced salt solution during vitrectomy washout.18

All plates were incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. Following incubation, the diameter of the zone of inhibition (ZOI) surrounding each well was measured in millimeters using a ruler. A measurement of 10 mm was interpreted as no inhibition, representing the size of the well alone. A larger ZOI suggests greater antimicrobial activity.17 Results were analyzed to determine the relative susceptibility of each isolate to the different antiseptic solutions. Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Inhibition zones were compared across organism categories using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for each antiseptic concentration. Post hoc Tukey pairwise comparisons were conducted with adjusted P values to account for multiple testing. Additional comparisons between Gram-positive versus Gram-negative isolates and yeast versus filamentous fungal isolates were performed using independent sample t-tests. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Corneal Epithelial Tissue Model

An in vitro corneal epithelial tissue model (EpiCorneal; MatTek Corporation, Ashland, MA, USA) was prepared for testing according to the manufacturer's instructions. The tissue wells were divided into two groups: (1) non-infected, to test the toxicity of the antiseptics on the corneal epithelial cells; and (2) infected, to test the toxicity of antiseptics when bacteria are also present.

For Group 1 (non-infected), 50 µL of PBS (control), 5% PI, 10% PI, 0.05% CHX, or 0.1% CHX was added to the cells. Tissue wells were incubated at 37°C (5% CO2), and 100 µL of media was collected at three time points (30 minutes, 1 hour, and 24 hours). Fresh medium was added after each collection to ensure that the cells were adequately hydrated.

For Group 2 (infected), 25 µL of S. epidermidis (MSSE strain from above, concentration of 106 CFU/mL) was added to each of the wells and incubated for 4 hours at 37°C (5% CO2). After the 4-hour incubation with the bacteria, the antiseptic agents were added to the infected wells in the same manner described above in Group 1. All test conditions were tested in duplicate and collected media samples were frozen in individual Eppendorf tubes at −80°C for further testing. Tissue viability of all wells (both non-infected and infected groups) was measured 36 hours after the initial application of PBS or antiseptic agent using an MTT assay (MTT Tissue Viability Assay; MatTek Corporation).

Cytokine Quantification

Quantification of IL-8, IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α cytokines collected from the corneal tissue cells was performed through Invitrogen enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assay kits (Human IL-8 ELISA Kit, KAC1301; Human IL-6 ELISA Kit, BMS213-2; Human IL-1β ELISA Kit, BMS224-2; and Human TNF‑α Ultrasensitive ELISA Kit, KHC3014; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For each ELISA assay, background absorbance was subtracted, and a standard curve was generated according to the recommendations in the respective protocols. Concentrations for unknown samples and controls were determined from the standard curve.

Results

Antimicrobial Susceptibility—Well Diffusion Assay

A total of 34 clinical isolates were evaluated, including Gram-positive bacteria, Gram-negative bacteria, yeast, and filamentous fungal isolates. Patient demographic data and clinical characteristics corresponding to the included microbial isolates were included for 31 patients. Clinical data were unavailable for three patients; of the remaining cases, 25 were obtained via vitreous direct sampling and six via vitreous wash. The patients consisted of 22 males and nine females, with a median age at presentation of 69 years (range, 23–88). Endogenous (n = 12) and acute postoperative (n = 12) etiologies comprised the majority of cases. Acute postoperative cases included cataract surgery (n = 9), corneal transplant (n = 2), and glaucoma surgery (n = 1). Additional etiologies included keratitis-related infections (n = 3), bleb-related infections (n = 2), and one case each of post–intravitreal injection and trauma.

For all organisms, higher antiseptic concentrations produced greater ZOIs compared to lower concentrations (Table 1). Overall, PI at 5% and 10% demonstrated larger ZOIs than CHX at 0.05% and 0.1% across most isolate groups (Figs. 1, 2). When stratified by organism category, Gram-positive isolates demonstrated significantly greater inhibition than Gram-negative isolates for all antiseptic concentrations (5% PI: mean difference 13.67 mm, P < 0.001; 10% PI: mean difference 16.70 mm, P < 0.001; 0.05% CHX: mean difference 9.46 mm, P < 0.001; 0.1% CHX: mean difference 10.41 mm, P < 0.001). Similarly, yeast isolates demonstrated significantly greater inhibition compared to filamentous fungal isolates for all antiseptics (5% PI: mean difference 27.07 mm, P < 0.001; 10% PI: mean difference 30.43 mm, P < 0.001; 0.05% CHX: mean difference 20.14 mm, P < 0.001; 0.1% CHX: mean difference 21.29 mm, P < 0.001).

Corneal Toxicity—MTT Assay

In this preliminary study, corneal epithelial tissue viability varied with antiseptic application, decreasing for both PI and CHX at higher antiseptic concentrations (Fig. 3). PI showed the greatest effect on viability, with viability rates of 17% (5% PI) and 11% (10% PI); 0.05% CHX had a viability of 71%, and 0.1% CHX had 41% viability. In the corneal tissue wells infected with Staphylococcus epidermidis, tissue viability decreased to 84% (Fig. 4). PI treatment on the infected cell models resulted in 17% viability for the 5% concentration and 9% for the 10% concentration. For CHX, the 0.05% concentration maintained almost 100% viability, and the 0.1% concentration had 94% viability.

In non-infected tissue samples, IL-8 increased in samples over time, regardless of treatment. The greatest concentration of IL-8 compared to the tissue control was seen in samples treated with both concentrations of PI (Fig. 5A). Among all samples, 10% PI had the greatest log fold difference. IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 were either below the assay detection threshold or expressed at low levels within the corneal epithelial tissue model and therefore were not included. Data points labeled 1 and 2 in the figure represent independent replicates for each experimental condition. Likewise, IL-8 was detected in all infected tissue samples and increased over time (Fig. 5B). Discussion

This study investigated the antimicrobial efficacy and ocular surface toxicity of PI and CHX at clinically relevant concentrations. Both agents demonstrated broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity in vitro, with PI exhibiting the highest inhibitory effect across most isolates. CHX was associated with higher epithelial viability and lower expression of IL-8. Taken together, these findings highlight the differential impacts of PI and CHX in terms of both antimicrobial efficacy and tissue toxicity, which may be relevant when selecting antiseptic agents for ophthalmic use.

In the current study, both PI and CHX demonstrated broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity. PI at 5% and 10% concentrations produced consistently larger ZOIs compared to 0.05% and 0.1% CHX. Organism-dependent variation in inhibition was noted, with C. albicans and Staphylococcus lugdunensis showing the greatest susceptibility to both PI and CHX. Interestingly, although P. aeruginosa showed the smallest zones overall for both antiseptics, CHX had larger ZOIs than PI for this organism. This matches findings in in vitro studies that noted lower minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for CHX compared to PI for P. aeruginosa, which translates to less CHX needed to halt bacterial growth.16,19

In the low-concentration PI well diffusion assays, 0.025% PI failed to inhibit any of the tested ocular isolates, and 1% PI had minimal inhibition. These results are consistent with the established MICs for PI ranging from 0.039% to 0.625%.16,20 These in vitro results indicate that low-concentration PI, as used in intraoperative washout, provides minimal antimicrobial activity.

The well diffusion assay was utilized in our study, as it is a standardized microbiological method for comparative antimicrobial assessment where larger ZOIs are associated with greater antimicrobial activity.17,21,22 This allowed us to compare the relative antimicrobial performance at clinically relevant concentrations. This type of assay was also selected because it may partially approximate the diffusion behavior of topical antiseptics across ocular surface tissues because it incorporates radial diffusion through solid media. It is important to note that diffusion-based assays generate concentration gradients within the medium and do not provide direct quantitative determination of MICs or minimum bactericidal concentrations (MBCs). However, the MIC and MBC ranges for PI and CHX are well described in prior literature (ranging from 0.0001% to 0.0016% for CHX and from 0.039% to 0.625% for PI).16,20,23,24

In this study, 5% PI reduced epithelial cell viability more than 0.05% CHX (17% vs. 70%). In preclinical rabbit models, 2.5% and 5% PI formulations induced severe epithelial disruption and delayed wound healing, whereas aqueous CHX at concentrations of 0.1% to 4% did not impair corneal abrasion healing.25–27 In clinical prospective studies, worse ocular surface staining scores and dry-eye symptoms were seen in eyes treated with PI compared with CHX.8,28

The greater cell toxicity in PI versus CHX groups was further amplified in the infected corneal tissue model experiments, where tissue treated with CHX retained high viability but PI-treated infected tissue still exhibited toxicity. Although interesting, these results must be interpreted in the context of the limitations of the MTT assay, which evaluates the viability of all mitotically active cells.29 Confirmatory bacterial viability assays would be needed to show that the higher MTT signal in the CHX wells was not due to bacterial cells. However, it is interesting to note that, in the infection-only conditions, the cell viability signal was lower than the tissue-only non-infected cells, which could suggest that antiseptic exposure was the primary determinant of the reduced viability signal.

Cytokine measurements showed that PI-treated tissue elicited greater IL-8 expression compared to CHX-treated samples. IL-8 was present in every sample and steadily increased over the 30-minute to 24-hour period, with a more pronounced rise observed in the PI-treated group. Elevation in IL-8 has been shown to correlate with increased leukocyte infiltration and inflammatory damage, and prolonged or excessive IL-8 activity tends to worsen tissue damage and visual prognosis.30,31 The lower cytotoxicity and reduced cytokine induction observed in CHX may reflect its mechanistic selectivity for microbial membranes and lower affinity for mammalian cell membranes at dilute concentrations.32–34 In contrast, PI is less selective and may extend to host epithelial cells through oxidative damage via free iodine.35

It is worth noting that some cytokines were undetectable across time points, particularly in IL‑1β and TNF‑α. This may reflect concentrations below the detection threshold of the ELISA assays rather than complete absence of inflammatory signaling. Future studies incorporating higher sensitivity kits, as well as more complex tissue or animal models, may further clarify the cytokine dynamics following antiseptic exposure. Another limitation of the study is the low replicate number in the tissue experiments.

This work represents a proof-of-principle study, and future studies with expanded replicate numbers will be required to validate and extend these findings. These findings should also be interpreted within the limitations of in vitro exposure conditions, as ocular surface clearance mechanisms in vivo, including tear film dilution, tear turnover, blinking, and nasolacrimal drainage, are expected to markedly reduce both the effective concentration and exposure time of these agents at the corneal epithelium. Finally, although a diverse panel of clinically derived bacterial and fungal isolates was included, broader generalizability would require evaluation of additional isolates and species.

Antiseptic choice remains an important consideration prior to ocular procedures and surgeries; although PI is the preferred first line, CHX has been employed more frequently. Endophthalmitis after intravitreal injection is a rare complication, with recently reported rates around 0.028%, or 1 in 3544 injections. Multiple single-center studies and a recent meta-analysis involving over 400,000 intravitreal injections have reported no significant difference in post-injection endophthalmitis rates between PI and CHX.8,9,12,36,37 Future optimization of ocular antisepsis protocols may depend not only on antimicrobial performance but also on understanding how antiseptic selection influences ocular surface response and patient tolerability.

In summary, PI demonstrated greater antimicrobial efficacy, but also greater in vitro ocular surface toxicity and inflammatory response. CHX demonstrated less in vitro epithelial cell toxicity while also producing broad-spectrum antimicrobial inhibition. This in vitro work provides an initial comparative framework for evaluating both antimicrobial efficacy and epithelium response to clinically utilized concentrations of PI and CHX. Future studies with larger sample sizes and more complex models are warranted to validate these findings and further optimize ocular antisepsis protocols.
