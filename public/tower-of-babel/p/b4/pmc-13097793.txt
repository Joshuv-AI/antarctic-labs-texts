Targeting the FGF19/FGFR4 positive feedback loop to disrupt ERK-mediated tumor progression in head and neck cancer
Authors: Yi-Fang Yang, Pei-Lun Yu, Chih-Yu Chou, Yu-Hsuan Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHead and neck cancer (HNC) is a biologically heterogeneous malignancy with limited actionable therapeutic targets. The fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) family comprises receptor tyrosine kinases implicated in tumor progression; however, their specific roles in HNC remain incompletely defined.MethodsGenomic alterations, transcriptomic profiles, and clinical relevance of FGFR1–FGFR4 were assessed in the TCGA/HNC cohort and in an independent validation set. Gain- and loss-of-function assays were performed to examine FGFR4 in cell motility, clonogenicity, and chemoresistance. Bioinformatic analyses and pharmacological perturbations were used to delineate FGFR4 regulatory mechanisms.ResultsAmong FGFR family members, FGFR4 was consistently overexpressed in HNC tissues and associated with poor prognosis. Functionally, FGFR4 promoted migration, invasion, and clonogenicity without affecting proliferation. Mechanistically, FGFR4 induced ERK activation, which upregulated RUNX3 and subsequently increased MMP2 expression. Pharmacological ERK inhibition suppressed RUNX3-MMP2 axis and attenuated migration and invasion in FGFR4-overexpressing cells. FGFR4 overexpression also conferred resistance to cisplatin, 5-fluorouracil, and docetaxel. Trametinib partially restored chemosensitivity in vitro and markedly enhanced cisplatin efficacy in vivo. Additionally, ERK activation increased both FGFR4 and FGF19 expressions, whereas ERK inhibition suppressed FGF19 and FGFR4 upregulation observed in HNC cells. Moreover, exogenous FGF19 further induced FGFR4 expression and ERK activation, consistent with a self-reinforcing FGF19–FGFR4–ERK feedback loop.ConclusionFGFR4 functions as an oncogenic driver in HNC, promoting tumor progression through the ERK–RUNX3–MMP2 axis and mediating chemoresistance via FGFR4–ERK signaling. The ERK-dependent induction of FGF19 and FGFR4 establishes a positive feedback circuit that sustains oncogenic activation. Targeting the FGF19/FGFR4 axis, particularly when combined with MEK/ERK inhibitors, represents a promising strategy to overcome resistance in HNC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-07999-1.

Background

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNC) is a prevalent malignancy with a rising global incidence attributed to multiple risk factors, including tobacco use, alcohol consumption, betel nut chewing, and human papillomavirus (HPV) infection [1]. HPV-related and -unrelated HNCs display distinct biological and clinical behaviors, with HPV-related HNC associated with better prognosis and reduced therapeutic resistance [1]. A comprehensive genomic analysis of 120 patients with locoregionally advanced HNC further demonstrated that transcriptomic programs differ substantially between HPV-related and -unrelated HNC [2]. Specifically, HPV-unrelated HNC is characterized by co-amplification of specific genes on chromosomes 11q13 and 11q22 and by recurrent somatic mutations in TP53, CDKN2A, FAT1, and AJUBA, whereas HPV-related tumors more often exhibit focal amplification of PIK3CA and E2F1 and recurrent deletion of TRAF3 [3]. However, many of these alterations have not been translated into clinically actionable targets, underscoring the need to identify druggable pathways to improve risk stratification and clinical outcomes.

The fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) family comprises four conserved single-pass transmembrane receptor tyrosine kinases (FGFR1–FGFR4), each containing three extracellular immunoglobulin (Ig)-like domains, a transmembrane segment, and an intracellular tyrosine kinase domain [4]. FGFRs can exist as preformed, ligand-independent dimers, and ligand binding, together with requisite cofactors and coreceptors, induces conformational rearrangements that enable trans-autophosphorylation and full receptor activation, leading to downstream signaling [5, 6]. Oncogenic FGFR signaling, arising from diverse mechanisms—including gene amplification, activating mutations, gene fusions, and aberrant ligand-dependent activation—contributes to tumorigenesis, cancer progression, and therapy resistance [7]. These alterations activate canonical cascades such as MAPK–ERK, PI3K–AKT, and JAK–STAT; however, the magnitude and relative engagement of these pathways are context-dependent, thus underscoring the complexity of targeting this pathway in cancer [7–9].

Dysregulation of FGFR signaling is observed in approximately 30–50% of HNC cases [10]. Reported alterations include FGFR1 amplification in HPV-unrelated tumors [11], FGFR2 and FGFR3 mutations in HPV-related HNC [2], and FGFR4 variation, predominantly in the form of single-nucleotide polymorphisms [12–14]. Although FGFR-directed therapies, including small-molecule inhibitors and monoclonal antibodies, have shown therapeutic promise, results from preclinical studies and clinical trials have been inconsistent [7]. Functional studies have shown that HNC cells overexpressing wild-type FGFR display highly variable sensitivity to FGFR inhibition [15, 16]. In contrast, a clinical trial that selected patients based on tumoral FGFR mRNA levels reported favorable responses to the pan-FGFR inhibitor rogaratinib [17]. These discrepancies suggest that therapeutic responsiveness may be governed by regulatory mechanisms other than genomic alterations alone. Moreover, the prognostic relevance of individual FGFR members in HNC remains incompletely defined. To address these gaps, we systematically profiled the expression of FGFR family members in HNC and investigated their clinical associations and regulatory mechanisms, with the goal of defining actionable subtypes to refine FGFR-targeted treatment strategies and improve patient stratification.

Materials and methods

In silico DNA, mRNA profiles, and survival analysis

FGFR family genetic alterations, including co-occurrence and mutual exclusivity, were examined using cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/). Correlations in mRNA expression levels among FGFR family members in HNC tissues were evaluated using the GEPIA dataset (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/). Differential FGFR4 level between tumor and normal tissues was assessed in the TCGA/HNC cohort. Overall survival analysis was conducted using OncoLnc (https://www.oncolnc.org/), with patients stratified by the median FGFR4 level.

To investigate FGFR4-associated pathways, differentially expressed genes (1.5-fold change) from the GSE34828 dataset were subjected to Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) to predict canonical pathways and upstream regulators. Significant upstream regulators were defined as those with a Z-score < − 2 and P < 0.05. Potential transcription factor binding sites within the MMP2 promoter were predicted using the JASPAR database (Vertebrates) and visualized with the UCSC Genome Browser based on ENCODE ChIP-seq data.

Clinical specimens and tissue microarrays for HNC

Tissue samples were fixed in 10% buffered formalin for 24 h at room temperature and subsequently embedded in paraffin. Malignant tissue specimens were obtained from 99 patients diagnosed with HNC and incorporated into a TMA, with each position containing two tissue cores. Matched adjacent noncancerous tissues located more than 1 cm from the tumor margin were also collected from 11 cases. The cohort comprised 92 male and 7 female patients, all with histologically confirmed squamous cell carcinoma and no evidence of distant metastasis at initial presentation, with the tumors predominantly originated from the oral cavity. All patients underwent surgical resection at Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital between January 2010 and December 2016. Clinical variables recorded included age, sex, American Joint Committee on Cancer T and N classifications, overall stage, substance use, and disease status. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital (approval no. KSVGH23-CT8-10), and the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Immunohistochemistry

IHC on TMA sections was performed according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Briefly, 4-µm sections underwent antigen retrieval in Tris–EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) for 10 min, followed by blocking for 30 min at room temperature. The antibodies and conditions are listed in Table S1. After washing, the sections were treated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (TACH02D; BioTnA) for 30 min, developed with DAB (1:20) for 1 min, and counterstained with hematoxylin for 5 s. Slides were scanned using the MoticEasyScan Pro system at 400× magnification. Staining for FGFR4, RUNX3, and MMP2 was quantified using HistoQuest software (version 7.1; TissueGnostics) with a deep learning–based nuclear segmentation algorithm. FGFR4 expression in tumor tissues was classified as high (> 80% positive cells) or low (≤ 79% positive cells).

Cell lines and conditioned medium

CAL27, SAS, HSC3, MTCQ1, and MTCQ2 cells were cultured in DMEM (HyClone; Cytiva; SH30003.02) supplemented with 10% FBS (Cytiva, SH30396.03) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin–amphotericin (PSA; Biological Industries; 03‑033‑1B). TW1.5 cells were maintained in DMEM/F12 (HyClone; SH30004.04) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% PSA. For conditioned medium (CM) collection, TW1.5 cells were plated at 1 × 106 cells per 6 cm plate overnight. The following day, the cells were washed with serum-free medium and cultured in 1% FBS DMEM/F12 treated with or without U46619 or PD98059 for 48 h at 37 °C. After treatment, CM was collected into a Microsep™ Advance centrifugal concentration device (molecular weight cut-off: 3 kDa; PALL, New York, USA). Total protein concentration of the concentrated CM was quantified using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit. Equal amounts of CM protein were then loaded onto 10% SDS–PAGE gels and visualized by Coomassie blue staining as a loading control.

Lentiviral infection

Lentiviral particles encoding short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting FGFR4 were obtained from the National RNAi Core Facility Platform (Taipei, Taiwan), with target sequences listed in Table S1. For FGFR4 overexpression, the pHAGE-FGFR4 lentiviral vector (Addgene plasmid #116743) [18] and the corresponding control vector pHAGE-puro (received from Christopher Vakoc; Addgene plasmid #118692) were used. To generate overexpression lentiviruses, 293T cells were co-transfected with the expression vector, pMD.G, and pΔ8.91 packaging plasmids using the calcium phosphate transfection method. FGFR4 cDNA was overexpressed in TW1.5, HSC3, and MTCQ2 cell lines. Polybrene (8 µg/mL) was added during the infection of TW1.5, HSC3 and MTCQ2 cells with viral supernatants (1 × 105 cells/well). The cells were cultured for subsequent analyses after 72 h of infection.

Reverse transcription–quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from HNC cells using VeZol Reagent (Vazyme; R411-02) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the isolated RNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara Biotechnology; RR037A) following the manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR was performed using qPCRBIO SyGreen Mix Lo-ROX (PCR Biosystems Ltd.) to measure target gene expression. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1. The RT-qPCR data are presented as the results obtained from at least three independent replicates.

Boyden chamber assay

Invasion assays were performed using polycarbonate membrane precoated on the underside with fibronectin (10 µg; Thermo Fisher; 33016015) and on the upper surface with Matrigel (Corning; 354234) at room temperature for approximately 3 min. Membranes without Matrigel coating were used in migration assays. The lower chamber contained complete medium (DMEM + 10% FBS) as a chemoattractant. Cells were harvested, resuspended in serum-free DMEM at 3 × 105 cells/mL, and 50 µL was added to the upper chamber of each well. The inserts were incubated for 15–18 h at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. For MMP2 inhibition experiments, TW1.5/FGFR4 cells were pretreated with an MMP2 inhibitor (5–10 µM) for 30 min before seeding, and the inhibitor was maintained throughout the assay. In the RUNX3 silencing experiments, cells were transfected with RUNX3 small interfering RNA (siRNA) (100 nM) prior to seeding, following the manufacturer’s protocol. ERK signaling inhibition was achieved by pretreating cells with PD98059 (25 µM) and maintaining the inhibitor during incubation. After incubation, membranes were stained with crystal violet for 30 min at room temperature, rinsed, and air-dried. Migrated or invaded cells were quantified by counting under a light microscope at 16× magnification. Data were expressed as mean cell count per field and compared with the corresponding control conditions. All invasion and migration assay data are based on at least three independent replicates.

Colony formation assay

CAL27/shluc, CAL27/shFGFR4-1, CAL27/shFGFR4-2, SAS/shluc, SAS/shFGFR4-1, SAS/shFGFR4-2, TW1.5/Vector, TW1.5/FGFR4, HSC3/Vector, HSC3/FGFR4, MTCQ1/shluc, MTCQ1/shFgfr4-1, MTCQ1/shFgfr4-2, MTCQ2/Vector, MTCQ2/Fgfr4, TW1.5/FGFR4/control siRNA-A, and TW1.5/FGFR4/siRUNX3 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 4,000 cells per well. After incubation for 7 days at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO₂, colonies were fixed with 1% formalin and stained with crystal violet for 30 min at room temperature. Colonies with a diameter greater than 0.1 cm were counted using ImageJ software (version 1.53t; National Institutes of Health). All colony formation assay data are based on at least three independent replicates.

Cell viability and caspase activity assays

For baseline viability measurements, the cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5,000 cells per well in complete medium and incubated for 24–72 h at 37 °C. For chemosensitivity assays, cells were treated for 72 h with cisplatin, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), or docetaxel, either alone or in combination with trametinib (a MEK1/2 inhibitor, 1 µM) or rogaratinib (a pan-FGFR inhibitor, 1 µM). In both assays, cell viability was determined by adding 10 µL MTT reagent (5 mg/mL, Cyrus Bioscience) to each well and incubating for 4 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured using a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cell apoptosis was examined using a Caspase-Glo® 3/7 Assay system (Promega; G8091). FGFR4-overexpressing cells were plated at 5,000 cells per well and incubated overnight. The cells were treated using only chemotherapy drugs or using them combined with trametinib (1 µM) or rogaratinib (1 µM) for 48 h. Cell viability and caspase-3/7 activity data are reported as the average of at least three independent replicates. A plate reader (FLUOstar Omega; BMG Labtech GmbH) was used to assess luminescence signals.

Immunoblotting

Proteins were extracted from the indicated HNC cell lines (CAL27/shluc, CAL27/shFGFR4-1, CAL27/shFGFR4-2, TW1.5/Vector, TW1.5/FGFR4, TW1.5/FGFR4/control siRNA-A, TW1.5/FGFR4/siRUNX3, and those subjected to pharmacological treatments) using RIPA lysis buffer. Protein concentrations were determined with the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 23225). Equal amounts of protein (50 µg) were resolved on 10% SDS–PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk prepared in PBST (0.1% Tween-20 in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies against FGFR4, RUNX3, MMP2, phospho-ERK, total ERK, FGF19, and β-actin. Table S1 lists the primary antibodies and immunoblotting conditions. After washing, membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (anti-mouse IgG [1:5,000; Jackson ImmunoResearch; 115-035-003] or anti-rabbit IgG [1:5,000; Jackson ImmunoResearch; 111-035-003]). Protein bands were visualized using ECL™ Western Blotting Reagents (Cytiva; XR-IGE-RPN2106).

siRNA transfection

RUNX3 siRNA (locus 1p36.11; sc-37679) and control siRNA-A (sc-37007) were purchased from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc. Cells (1 × 106) were seeded in 6 cm culture dishes containing complete medium and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Transfection was performed with 100 nM RUNX3 or control siRNA using TransIT-X2 transfection reagent (MIR 6000; Mirus Bio LLC) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cells were subjected to subsequent experiments after 48 h.

Promoter assay

The cells were plated in 6 cm dishes (3 × 106 cells/dish) containing a complete medium for 24 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, they were transfected with 1 µg of pLenti6.3/V5-DEST_RUNX3 (DNASU plasmid #HsCD00852782) or control pLenti6.3/V5-DEST-GFP (received from Lynda Chin; Addgene plasmid #40125), 1 µg of human MMP2 promoter (received from Etty Benveniste; Addgene plasmid #53432), and 0.25 ng of pLAS2w.RFP-C (National RNAi Core Facility Platform, Taipei, Taiwan) using the TransIT-X2 transfection reagent. The cells were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C, trypsinized, replated into 96-well plates (1 × 105 cells/well), and incubated overnight. A plate reader (FLUOstar Omega; BMG Labtech GmbH) and a ONE-Glo™ Luciferase Assay System (Promega, #E6110) were used to assess RFP fluorescence and luciferase activity.

FGF19 treatment

The TW1.5 cells were plated in 6 cm dishes (1 × 106 cells/dish) and incubated overnight at 37 °C. They were treated with recombinant human FGF19 (100 ng/mL) and cultured in 1% FBS DMEM/F12 for 48 h at 37 °C; they were eventually subjected to subsequent experiments.

Murine subcutaneous tumor implantation model

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Kaohsiung Veterans General Hospital (Approval No. IACUC-2510-2710-25045) and conducted in accordance with applicable institutional and national animal care regulations. SAS cells (5×106) in 100 µL of 1× PBS were subcutaneously injected into the right dorsal region of 6-week-old male NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid/JNarl mice. One week after injection, the mice were randomly assigned to one of three groups (n = 5): vehicle control, cisplatin (2 mg/kg, twice weekly), or cisplatin plus trametinib (cisplatin 2 mg/kg, twice weekly; trametinib 1 mg/kg, once daily). Cisplatin and trametinib were intraperitoneally administered in every experiment. Tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: 0.52 × length × width2; tumors were measured twice per week. The mice were sacrificed after 26 days; tumor tissues were collected for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation from at least three independent experiments. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the Student’s t-test for parametric data and the Mann–Whitney U test or Fisher’s exact test for nonparametric data. For multiple group comparisons, one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied. Correlations between variables were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation. The prognostic significance of covariates was evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression in both univariate and multivariate models. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. All statistical analyses were two-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Genetic landscape and expression correlations of the FGFR family in HNC

To investigate the significance of the FGFR family in HNC, we analyzed genetic alterations in FGFR1 − FGFR4 using the cBioPortal database. As shown in Figure S1A, FGFR1 exhibited the highest rate of genetic alteration (8%), followed by FGFR3 (4%), FGFR4 (1.1%), and FGFR2 (0.9%). Across HNC cohorts, mutations were the most common alteration type for FGFR2–FGFR4 (Figure S1B). Co-occurrence analysis revealed mutual exclusivity among most FGFRs, except between FGFR1 and FGFR2, which tended to co-occur (Figure S1C). However, at the transcriptomic level, expression correlation analysis (Figure S1D and Table S2) demonstrated significant correlations between FGFR1 and FGFR2 (Spearman’s correlation coefficient: 0.38, P = 5.5 × 10− 19), and between FGFR2 and FGFR3 (r = 0.26, P = 1.6 × 10− 9). The FGFR4 level also positively correlated with that of FGFR1 (r = 0.17, P < 0.001) and FGFR2 (r = 0.16, P < 0.001). These findings highlight both the genetic diversity and expression correlations among FGFR members in HNC, underscoring the need for further investigation into their individual clinical and functional roles.

FGFR4 is upregulated in HNC tissues and serves as a negative prognostic biomarker

To evaluate the clinical relevance of FGFRs in HNC, we analyzed the expression levels of FGFR1–FGFR4 in the TCGA/HNC cohort. Among these, only FGFR4 was significantly upregulated in tumor tissues (n = 500) compared with that in normal tissues (n = 44) (P < 0.01; Fig. 1A). Subgroup analysis revealed no significant difference in FGFR4 levels between patients with HPV-related and those with HPV-unrelated HNC (Fig. 1B). Kaplan–Meier analysis using the OncoLnc portal showed that higher FGFR4 level (n = 248) was significantly associated with poorer overall survival (P = 2.54 × 10− 2; Fig. 1C). This finding was validated at the protein level in an independent HNC TMA, in which FGFR4 protein expression was markedly upregulated in tumor tissues (n = 99) compared with that in normal tissues (n = 11) (Figs. 2A–B). Moreover, patients with high FGFR4 protein expression (n = 52) had significantly worse overall survival than those with low FGFR4 expression (n = 47) (P = 2.9 × 10− 2; Fig. 2C). In univariate Cox analysis, FGFR4 expression was significantly associated with poor overall survival (P = 4.8 × 10− 2), with borderline significance in multivariate analysis after adjustment for T and N classifications (P = 5.4 × 10− 2; Table S3). Additionally, FGFR4 expression significantly correlated with cigarette use (P = 2.7 × 10− 2; Table S4). Collectively, these findings highlight FGFR4 as the only FGFR family member consistently upregulated in HNC and associated with poor prognosis, supporting its potential as a clinically relevant biomarker and candidate therapeutic target.

FGFR4 promotes migration, invasion, and clonogenicity in HNC cells

Given the clinical association between high FGFR4 expression and poor prognosis in patients with HNC, we evaluated the functional role of FGFR4 in HNC progression. We first assessed endogenous FGFR4 mRNA levels across HNC cell lines and identified CAL27 and SAS cells as having high FGFR4 expression levels, whereas TW1.5 and HSC3 cells exhibited low levels (Figure S2A). Based on these expression patterns, FGFR4 was knocked down in CAL27 and SAS cells and was overexpressed in TW1.5 and HSC3 cells. Efficient FGFR4 knockdown was confirmed by RT-qPCR (Fig. 3A). Knockdown of FGFR4 markedly reduced migratory and invasive capacities, as assessed via the Boyden chamber assays (Fig. 3B). Conversely, overexpression of FGFR4 in TW1.5 and HSC3 cells notably enhanced migration and invasion abilities compared with that in the vector control cells (Figs. 3C–D). Colony formation assays further demonstrated that FGFR4 knockdown reduced clonogenicity, whereas FGFR4 overexpression increased it (Figs. 3E–F). However, MTT assays revealed no consistent differences in cell proliferation across HNC cell lines following FGFR4 knockdown or overexpression (Figs. 3G–H). After lentiviral FGFR4 knockdown, overexpression of FGFR4 rescued the migration, invasion, and clonogenicity in CAL27 cells (Figures S2B–E).

FGFR4 promotes HNC cell migration and invasion via MMP2

To identify the signaling pathways regulated by FGFR4, we performed Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) using the GSE34828 dataset to examine differentially expressed genes between control and FGFR4-knockdown OVCA432 cells (1.5-fold change). The analysis identified “Molecular Mechanisms of Cancer” as the top-ranked canonical pathway (Fig. 4A). To explore potential downstream effectors, we conducted an upstream regulatory analysis (Z-score < -2, P < 0.05) and focused on recurring molecules associated with cell migration and invasion, among which MMP2 emerged as a convergent target of multiple transcription factors (Table S5). Protein–protein interaction analysis using the STRING database indicated an indirect connection between FGFR4 and MMP2 (Fig. 4B). Validation experiments showed that FGFR4 knockdown markedly reduced MMP2 expression levels in CAL27 and SAS cells, whereas FGFR4 overexpression increased MMP2 levels in TW1.5 and HSC3 cells (Figs. 4C–D). Suppression of MMP2 using an MMP2 inhibitor attenuated the enhanced migration and invasion in TW1.5/FGFR4 cells, whereas invasion was predominantly inhibited at 10 µM (Fig. 4E). Consistent with this, MMP2 was upregulated in tumor tissues compared with normal tissues in the TCGA/HNC cohort (Fig. 4F). Collectively, these findings identify MMP2 as a key downstream effector of FGFR4-mediated metastatic phenotypes.

Upregulated FGFR4 induces RUNX3-mediated MMP2 expression in HNC cells

To identify transcriptional regulators upstream of MMP2, we focused on SMAD7, RUNX3, and ATF3, which were predicted to regulate MMP2 based on IPA upstream regulator analysis (Table S5). Motif analysis using the JASPAR database (Vertebrates; matrix IDs: RUNX3 MA0684.2 and ATF3 MA0605.3) identified multiple high-confidence RUNX3 motifs (relative score > 0.80) within the proximal MMP2 promoter region, with the top-ranked site showing a relative score of 0.936. By contrast, predicted ATF3 motifs were less conserved and exhibited lower binding scores (Figure S3A). To assess potential occupancy, we examined ENCODE RUNX3 ChIP-seq data via the UCSC Genome Browser and observed a peak upstream of MMP2 overlapping the predicted motif sites, supporting potential RUNX3 occupancy at this locus (Figure S3B). Among the candidate transcription factors, only RUNX3 levels were significantly and positively correlated with both FGFR4 and MMP2 in the TCGA/HNC cohort (Figures S3C–D). The MMP2 promoter reporter assay showed that MMP2 promoter activity was upregulated in RUNX3-overexpressing cells (Figures S3E–F), indicating the role of RUNX3 in regulating MMP2 transcription.

Next, we examined whether FGFR4 mediated RUNX3 expression in HNC cells. Knockdown of FGFR4 downregulated RUNX3 levels, whereas overexpression of FGFR4 upregulated RUNX3 (Figs. 5A–B). To experimentally validate this axis, RUNX3 was silenced in TW1.5/FGFR4 cells, confirming effective modulation of RUNX3 expression levels (Fig. 5C). siRNA-mediated RUNX3 silencing in TW1.5/FGFR4 cells resulted in downregulated MMP2 levels and markedly reduced clonogenicity (Figs. 5C–D), migration, and invasion (Fig. 5E), supporting a requirement for RUNX3 in FGFR4-mediated metastatic phenotypes. Moreover, IHC analysis of the TMA showed that FGFR4 expression was positively correlated with both RUNX3 (r = 0.446, P < 0.01) and MMP2 (r = 0.311, P < 0.01) expression levels (Fig. 5F). Collectively, these findings identify an FGFR4–RUNX3–MMP2 axis underlying FGFR4-driven metastatic potential in HNC.

FGFR4–ERK signaling reduces chemotherapy sensitivity in HNC cells

Given the observed association between FGFR4 expression and poor clinical outcomes, we investigated whether FGFR4 contributes to chemotherapy resistance in HNC cells. Compared to the vector controls, TW1.5/FGFR4 cells exhibited reduced sensitivity to cisplatin, 5-FU, and docetaxel, as demonstrated by viability assays (Fig. 6A). As MEK/ERK signaling is a canonical pathway downstream of FGFR4 and has been implicated in FGFR4-driven therapeutic resistance [20–22], we examined the effects of trametinib, a selective MEK1/2 inhibitor. TW1.5/FGFR4 cells exhibited increased trametinib sensitivity, as evidenced by a lower IC50 than that in control cells (Figure S4A), indicating a dependence on ERK signaling.

We next examined the effect of rogaratinib, an oral pan-FGFR inhibitor reported to suppress ERK phosphorylation [23]. As shown in Figure S4B, FGFR4-overexpressing cells also displayed increased sensitivity to rogaratinib, further supporting the role of FGFR4–ERK signaling in drug resistance [22]. To assess whether inhibition of FGFR4–ERK signaling could overcome chemoresistance, we co-treated TW1.5/FGFR4 cells with trametinib or rogaratinib in combination with cisplatin, 5-FU, or docetaxel. Both inhibitors enhanced chemosensitivity of FGFR4-overexpressing cells (Figs. 6B–C), indicating that the targeting of FGFR4–ERK signaling can reduce FGFR4-mediated chemoresistance. Cisplatin was selected for further in vivo studies because (1) ERK inhibition using trametinib concordantly reduced cell viability and increased caspase-3/7 activity in cisplatin-treated FGFR4-overexpressing HNC cells (Figs. 6D–E), and (2) cisplatin is the backbone of systemic therapy for HNC in most therapeutic settings [24]. The in vivo experiment demonstrated that the combined trametinib and cisplatin treatment effectively suppressed the growth of xenograft tumors (Figs. 6F–H).

The FGFR4–ERK feedback loop reinforces FGFR4 expression and drives HNC progression

Given that ERK signaling is also a pivotal pathway mediating FGFR4–driven cancer progression [25, 26], we investigated whether ERK functions upstream of RUNX3 and MMP2 to regulate metastasis-related phenotypes in HNC. Western blot analysis revealed that FGFR4 knockdown markedly decreased ERK phosphorylation (pERK), whereas FGFR4 overexpression increased pERK expression (Figs. 7A–B). Inhibition of ERK using the MEK inhibitor, PD98059, attenuated the enhanced migratory and invasive capacities (Fig. 7C) and downregulated the expression of RUNX3 and MMP2 in TW1.5/FGFR4 cells (Fig. 7D), suggesting that FGFR4 promotes metastatic phenotypes through the ERK–RUNX3–MMP2 signaling pathway.

Notably, we observed that treatment with PD98059 reduced FGFR4 expression in FGFR4-overexpressing cells (Fig. 7D), suggesting that ERK signaling might mediate FGFR4 expression. Given that FGF19 is the primary ligand of FGFR4 and possesses both autocrine and endocrine properties that amplify FGFR4-driven oncogenic signaling [27] and that a previous study demonstrated FGF19–FGFR4 activation can induce ERK signaling in HNC cells [28], we determined whether ERK activation could, in turn, transcriptionally regulate FGF19 expression. As shown in Fig. 7E, FGFR4 overexpression upregulated FGF19 levels, whereas PD98059 treatment suppressed FGF19 levels in TW1.5/FGFR4 cells.

To further clarify the regulatory role of ERK, we treated TW1.5 cells with the ERK activator U46619, which increased pERK levels and concomitantly upregulated FGFR4 and FGF19 expression. Conversely, ERK inhibition with PD98059 reduced pERK, FGFR4, and FGF19 expression in TW1.5 cells (Fig. 7F), suggesting that FGFR4 and FGF19 may be co-regulated downstream of ERK activity. To assess whether ERK-mediated FGF19 expression could induce FGFR4–ERK signaling in HNC cells, we added exogenous FGF19 to parental TW1.5 cells. FGF19 treatment increased both FGFR4 and pERK expression (Fig. 7G). Next, we explored the potential transcriptional mediators linking ERK activity to the regulation of FGF19 and FGFR4 expression in HNC cells. FGFR4 overexpression and knockdown upregulated and downregulated ETS2 levels, respectively (Figures S5A–B). U46619 treatment induced ETS2 expression at 30 min and increased FGF19 and FGFR4 expression at 60 min in HNC cells (Figures S5C–D). These results showed that ETS2 may be involved in FGFR4–ERK signaling in HNC cells. Supporting our experimental findings, transcriptomic analysis of the TCGA/HNC cohort revealed a significant positive correlation between FGF19 and FGFR4 expression (r = 0.38, P = 1.4 × 10− 19; Fig. 7H). Collectively, these findings support the existence of an FGF19–FGFR4–ERK positive feedback loop that amplifies FGFR4 signaling and may contribute to tumor progression and therapy resistance (Fig. 7I).

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that FGFR4 is a prognostically relevant FGFR family member across independent datasets. Functionally, forced FGFR4 expression enhanced cell migration, invasion, and clonogenic growth without affecting proliferation. Mechanistically, FGFR4 overexpression upregulated the transcription factor RUNX3, resulting in increased MMP2 expression in HNC cells. FGFR4 also induced ERK activation, and pharmacological ERK inhibition suppressed RUNX3 and MMP2 expression and reduced invasion, confirming that FGFR4-driven tumor progression depends on the ERK–RUNX3–MMP2 axis. Therapeutically, FGFR4 conferred chemoresistance to cisplatin, 5-FU, and docetaxel in vitro, which was partially reversed via trametinib or rogaratinib treatment. Trametinib enhanced the antitumor efficacy of cisplatin in vivo, implicating the role of the FGFR4–ERK pathway in chemotherapy responsiveness. Importantly, ERK activation enhanced both FGFR4 and FGF19 expression, and ERK inhibition suppressed the FGF19 upregulation observed in HNC cells. Exogenous FGF19 further induced FGFR4–ERK activity, consistent with a self-reinforcing FGF19–FGFR4–ERK feedback loop that amplifies oncogenic signaling in HNC.

The FGFR4 gene is located on chromosome 5q35.1 and encodes an 802–amino acid protein of approximately 95–110 kDa [29]. Unlike FGFR 1–3, which can generate IIIb and IIIc isoforms via alternative splicing, FGFR4 exclusively expresses the IgIIIc isoform, enabling high-affinity ligand binding [30]. Upon ligand engagement, FGFR4 canonically activates downstream signaling cascades such as Ras–Raf–MAPK, PI3K–AKT, and JAK–STAT to regulate processes such as embryonic development, tissue repair and differentiation, bile acid homeostasis, and metabolic regulation, while also contributing to various pathological conditions [31–33]. Although oncogenic genomic alterations of FGFR4 are less frequent than those of other FGFRs, aberrant expression or activation of FGFR4 or its ligands (e.g., FGF19) is frequently observed in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), rhabdomyosarcoma, breast, colon, and lung cancers, where it is associated with poor clinical outcomes and tumor aggressiveness [34–36].

Data on FGFR4 in HNC, particularly regarding its expression, remain limited, although existing evidence reports FGFR4 overexpression in approximately 16–35% of cases [37]. The FGFR4 Gly388Arg polymorphism is a extensively studied variant in HNC, having been linked to disease susceptibility [12, 13], advanced tumor stage, and inferior survival [14], particularly in conjunction with TP53 mutations [38]. It has also been reported to exhibit a tendency toward increased cisplatin sensitivity [12]. Mechanistically, the Gly388Arg variant alters the transmembrane domain, exposing a membrane-proximal STAT3-binding motif that enhances STAT3 recruitment and tyrosine phosphorylation [39]. Recent evidence also indicates that ligand-dependent dysregulation contributes to FGFR4-mediated HNC progression. Specifically, FGF19, a hormone-like FGF, is amplified in patients with HNC, leading to its overexpression and increased autocrine secretion. This, in turn, constitutively activates FGFR4-dependent ERK/AKT–p70S6K–S6 signaling to promote tumor growth [28]. Additionally, high-dose melatonin induces endoplasmic reticulum stress through the PERK–eIF2α–ATF4 pathway, which transcriptionally upregulates FGF19 expression. The resulting increase in FGF19 then activates FGFR4 signaling, upregulating vimentin and promoting epithelial–mesenchymal transition, ultimately enhancing the metastatic potential of HNC cells [40]. In our study, we found that FGFR4 expression was upregulated in HNC cells and correlated with poor prognosis. Gain- and loss-of-function experiments further demonstrated that FGFR4 promotes HNC cell migration and invasion via the ERK–RUNX3–MMP2 axis.

In this study, we identified RUNX3 as a downstream effector of FGFR4–ERK signaling that mediates HNC cell migration and invasion through MMP2. RUNX3, a Runt-domain transcription factor encoded on chromosome 1p36.11 [41], canonically functions downstream of TGF-β and Wnt/β-catenin signaling to promote apoptosis and restrain proliferation [41]. Accordingly, RUNX3 has traditionally been regarded as a tumor suppressor, with its inactivation attributed to promoter hypermethylation, histone modifications, or cytoplasmic mislocalization [42–44]. However, emerging studies have shown that RUNX3 can exert oncogenic effects under specific conditions. For instance, in p53-deficient conditions, RUNX3 loses its interaction with p53 and aberrantly activates MYC transcription, thereby promoting tumorigenesis [45]. In HNC, RUNX3 is frequently overexpressed, partly due to epidermal growth factor signaling and epigenetic dysregulation, such as promoter demethylation. RUNX3 overexpression may upregulate IGFBP2, CXCL14, and EPHA4 to enhance tumor proliferation, invasion, and resistance to apoptosis [46]. Consistent with the oncogenic role of RUNX3, our results showed that ERK activation is required for FGFR4-mediated upregulation of RUNX3. However, the precise mechanism by which ERK modulates RUNX3 remains to be elucidated.

Notably, we observed that ERK activation increased both FGFR4 and FGF19 expression, whereas pharmacological ERK inhibition attenuated their upregulation. Increased FGF19 expression observed in FGFR4-overexpressing cells was attenuated by ERK inhibition. Moreover, exogenous FGF19 induced both FGFR4 expression and ERK activation. These findings support the existence of a self-reinforcing FGF19–FGFR4–ERK signaling loop that may sustain oncogenic activity in HNC. Although our data support ETS2 as an ERK-responsive transcription factor contributing to this regulatory circuit, ERK signaling is known to activate multiple downstream transcription factors, including AP-1 and MYC [47, 48]. It is therefore plausible that additional ERK-dependent transcriptional regulators may also participate in this feedback loop. Similar ligand–receptor positive feedback mechanisms have been reported in other malignancies. In HCC, FGF19/FGFR4 and HGF/c-MET jointly upregulated the transcription factor ETV4 via the ERK1/2 pathway, and ETV4, in turn, transcriptionally upregulated FGFR4, forming an FGF19–ETV4–FGFR4 positive feedback loop that enhanced immunosuppression and metastasis [26]. Another HCC study demonstrated that small extracellular vesicle–derived von Willebrand factor (vWF) promoted FGF2 expression in endothelial cells, which then activated a self-amplifying FGFR4–ERK1 loop to drive angiogenesis and metastasis [49]. In colorectal cancer, FGF19 induced ELF4 expression via the ERK1/2–SP1 axis. ELF4 transcriptionally upregulated FGFR4, creating an FGF19–ELF4–FGFR4 loop that promoted metastasis while also activating SRC as a parallel downstream effector [50]. Collectively, these findings provide a mechanistic explanation for the sustained overexpression of FGFR4 observed in multiple cancers and highlight the therapeutic potential for co-targeting FGFR4 signaling and its feedback regulation in HNC.

In this study, we demonstrated that FGFR4 overexpression confers resistance to cisplatin, 5-FU, and docetaxel agents that comprise the standard regimen widely used in induction chemotherapy [51]. Beyond induction, these agents are also frequently employed in curative-intent settings, including concurrent chemoradiotherapy and adjuvant therapy, and in salvage settings [52]. Mechanistically, this resistance was mediated by ERK pathway activation, representing a mechanism distinct from that of the previously reported AKT–SOX2 axis and cancer stem cell enrichment [53, 54]. Recent computational and experimental study further supports this concept, showing that FGFR4-driven tumors exhibit context-dependent vulnerability to ERK inhibition [22]. In HCC, FGFR4 inhibition leads to adaptive ERK reactivation, and dual blockade of FGFR4 and MEK synergistically suppresses tumor growth [22]. In this study, FGFR4-overexpressing HNC cells were more sensitive to trametinib, a selective MEK1/2 inhibitor, and rogaratinib, a pan-FGFR inhibitor known to suppress FGFR–ERK signaling [23]. Both agents have demonstrated clinical activity [17, 55], and combining either inhibitor with standard chemotherapeutics restored drug sensitivity, indicating that FGFR4-mediated chemoresistance is ERK-dependent and pharmacologically reversible. Consistent with these in vitro findings, trametinib treatment enhanced the antitumor efficacy of cisplatin in subcutaneous xenograft models.

This study possesses some limitations. First, because our TMA cohort primarily consisted of patients with oral cavity cancer, the identified prognostic relevance of FGFR4 mainly pertains to HPV-negative diseases; hence, further subtype-specific analyses are warranted. Nonetheless, prior evidence indicates that FGFR4–ERK signaling contributes to oncogenic progression in various malignancies, implying that its biological relevance extends beyond a single etiological subtype [26, 49, 50]. Second, we used rogaratinib and trametinib to validate FGFR4–ERK signaling dependency; however, the clinical translation of these drugs must carefully consider therapeutic limitations, including optimal dosing, tumor-selective delivery, safety profiles, and the potential for compensatory signaling within the FGFR network, which may limit the durability of pathway inhibition. Addressing these issues will be essential to increase the therapeutic window of FGFR4–ERK-targeted therapies in HNC. Finally, although FGFR4 overexpression was associated with adverse outcomes, its value in predicting treatment response remains unclear. Additionally, FGFR4–ERK inhibition reversed chemoresistance in our models; however, the long-term resistance mechanisms associated with sustained pathway blockade warrant further investigation. Hence, prospective biomarker-driven studies incorporating higher-resolution molecular profiling are required to evaluate the predictive utility of FGFR4 pathway activity and inform rational combination strategies.

Conclusion

Taken together, our findings demonstrate that FGFR4 functions as a key oncogenic driver in HNC, and its overexpression predicts poor patient survival. Mechanistically, FGFR4 promotes HNC cell migration and invasion through the ERK-activated RUNX3–MMP2 signaling axis. In addition, FGFR4–ERK signaling confers resistance to cisplatin, 5-FU, and docetaxel, which can be effectively reversed by pharmacological inhibition of the FGFR4–ERK pathway. Notably, we identified a self-sustaining FGF19–FGFR4–ERK positive feedback loop that reinforces FGFR4 overexpression and signaling activity. Collectively, these results provide mechanistic insights into FGFR4-mediated metastatic potential and chemoresistance and highlight the FGFR4–ERK axis as a potential therapeutic target in HNC (Fig. 7I).
