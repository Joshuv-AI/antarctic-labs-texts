Evaluation of bladder wash for postmortem toxicological analysis: Comparison with urine in 50 forensic cases
Authors: Maxime Alexandre, Emma-Rose Legrand-Brault, Romain Pelletier, Brendan Le Daré, Isabelle Morel, Renaud Bouvet, Thomas Gicquel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In postmortem toxicology, urine serves as a reference matrix for determining the circumstances of death. However, urine may be unavailable or unsuitable for analysis in certain forensic cases. In this context, bladder washing (BW) has recently been proposed as an alternative matrix, although supporting evidence remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the reliability and forensic relevance of BW for postmortem toxicological investigations. Toxicological screening was performed using LC-PDA-QDa, LC-MS/MS, and LC-HRMS after liquid–liquid extraction in paired urine and BW samples from the same individuals. Data from 50 autopsy cases were analyzed to compare the performance of BW with urine in terms of analytes and metabolites detection. The cohort included 50 cases (80% male, mean age 42 years), with a mean postmortem interval of 1.2 days and a mean sampling delay of 2.98 days. A 95.7% concordance was observed between the two matrices, with 395 compounds detected in urine and 378 in BW, corresponding to 91 different xenobiotics. These findings demonstrate that BW can serve as a reliable and representative alternative when urine is unavailable, allowing a broad panel of substances to be detected using standard analytical workflow. Standardization of BW collection and analysis could enhance postmortem toxicological investigations and provide forensic practitioners with comprehensive toxicological information in challenging cases.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00414-026-03909-8.

Introduction

Postmortem samples are essential for toxicological investigation in the forensic setting. In the determination of the cause of death, the analysis of available biological fluids allows for the detection of toxic or therapeutic substances that may have contributed to death. A wide range of biological matrices can be analyzed, with the most commonly collected being blood, urine, vitreous humour and occasionally solid tissues [1–4]. Each matrix is characterized by a specific detection window. The collection of multiple matrices is therefore of particular interest, as it provides complementary information and enables a more comprehensive assessment of the toxicological status of the deceased [5].

Blood and urine play a central role in postmortem toxicology. Peripheral blood typically reflects substance concentrations over a relatively short pre-mortem time window, whereas urine provides information on substances ingested or absorbed over a longer period preceding death [4]. Thus, some substances or drugs can be rapidly metabolized and eliminated, rendering them difficult to detect in blood while remaining detectable in urine. The combined analysis of these two matrices is therefore crucial for an accurate assessment of toxic exposure. Urine is often considered a valuable matrix in postmortem toxicology, as it may contain parent compound or metabolites that remain detectable after they have decreased or degraded in blood, mainly due to prior renal excretion and differences in postmortem redistribution and enzymatic activity [6].

However, urine may not be available at autopsy for various reasons, including empty bladder or bladder trauma. Bladder wash (BW) sampling during autopsy has been proposed as an alternative matrix for several years and was first described at a scientific conference in 2022 [7]. Although it is mentioned in the Guidelines for Collection of Biological Samples for Clinical and Forensic Toxicological Analysis [4], BW remains infrequently performed in routine pratice. Recent studies suggest that BW may represent a valuable alternative specimen when urine is unavailable [8–11], and a recent study has recommended its systematic collection [12]. BW may contain detectable substances similar to those identified in urine; however its biochemical composition indicates that it is not simply diluted urine, but rather a complex mixture of residual urine and necrotic urothelial material [11]. Despite its promising potential as an alternative biological matrix, further studies are warranted to expand the existing validation data, including direct comparisons between BW and paired urine samples, and to confirm its analytical reliability and reproducibility, as well as its relevance and applicability in forensic science.

The aim of this study was to determine the reliability and forensic applicability and relevance of BW as an alternative matrix in postmortem toxicology. To this end, toxicological findings obtained from paired urine and BW samples collected during the same autopsy were compared in a cohort of 50 cadavers. The analytical strategy combined three complementary toxicological screening approaches (LC-PDA-QDa, LC-MS/MS, and LC-HRMS) following a single extraction protocol, providing a comprehensive assessment of the substances detectable in both matrices.

Materials and methods

Population

Biological samples were obtained from autopsies performed between October 4, 2024, and March 21, 2025. For each case, demographic data (sex, age), post mortem interval, sampling interval and origin of death were collected. Causes of death were classified according to the categories defined in French legal frameworks (Civil, Criminal, and Criminal Procedure Codes) as already described [13–15].

Urine and bladder wash sampling

BW samples were collected whenever urine was detected and deemed suitable for sampling during the autopsy. This protocol was carried out by 13 different operators. A rigorous protocol was implemented for the collection of both urine and BW samples to prevent any sampling variability between operators. During each autopsy, an initial urine sample was collected using standard techniques. Subsequently, the bladder was opened by incising its roof, and its contents were evacuated through aspiration. After complete drainage, the bladder was filled with 10 mL of sterile 0.9% sodium chloride solution. This instilled solution was then aspirated and collected. Both native urine and BW fluid were stored in a urinary collection tube at -20 °C.

Extraction protocol

To perform toxicological analyses on both matrices (BW and urine) across all chromatographic systems, a single extraction procedure was applied. One mL of each biological sample was diluted with 4 mL of LC-MS grade water and spiked with 20µL of internal standard (Flurazepam at 50 mg/L). This mixture was transferred into an extraction tube (“Toxitube A”) containing 1.8 g of a powdered mixture (1.53 g of sodium chloride, 0.14 g of sodium bicarbonate, and 0.14 g of sodium carbonate) and 2.3 mL of an organic solvent mixture (0.77 mL of Heptane, 0.38 mL of isopropanol, 0.58 mL of 1,2-dichloroethane and 0.58 mL of dichloromethane).

Samples were vortexed and placed under rotary agitation during 10 min before being centrifuged at 2,200 g for 10 min at + 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, evaporated to dryness under nitrogen at 50 °C and reconstituted with 100 µL of mobile phase (acetonitrile/5 mM ammonium acetate (15:85 v/v) at pH = 3.8) [16]. This reconstituted solution was further diluted tenfold in mobile phase and transferred into an injection vial for analysis by liquid chromatography coupled to photodiode array detection and single-quadrupole mass spectrometry (LC-PDA-QDa).

Toxicological analysis

LC-PDA-QDa

Samples were analyzed by liquid chromatographic (LC) with ultraviolet (UV) photodiode array detection (PDA) coupled to mass spectrometer (QDa) (Acquity QDa, Waters®). Seven µL were injected into an Acquity UPLC I-Class PLUS system (Waters®), and the separation was carried out using a Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 1.7 μm,, Waters®) coupled with a VanGuard Acquity UPLC, BEH C18 PreColumn (2,1 × 5 mm, 1,7 μm, Waters®) at a flow rate of 0,45 mL/min at 55 °C. Mobile phases were composed with 5 mM ammonium acetate at pH = 3,8 and acetonitrile. The gradient was initially consisting of 25:75 v/v at 11.2 min to 100% acetonitrile at 12 min corresponding to the end of the analysis. A quality control sample was injected before each analysis (Supplementary Table 1). Data were compared to a personal spectral library containing 610 xenobiotics (Supplementary Table 2), including drugs and medications, using Empower 3.7.0. software (Waters®).

Targeted LC-MS/MS screening

Targeted LC-MS/MS screening was carried out by liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS) using a Xevo TQ-XS quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters ®) with an ESI ionization source. The instrument was coupled to an Acquity Class-H Plus equipped with a quaternary pump system (Waters®). The column was held at 50 °C and the autosampler was set at 15 °C. Five µL of sample diluted to 1/10 were injected. LC separation was performed on an HHS C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 1.8 μm; Waters®) with a binary gradient of elution at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min, consisting of ammonium formiate 5 mM pH = 3/0.1% formic acid (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B). The gradient started with 13% solvent B for 0.5 min and then ramped up to 50% solvent B over a period of 10 min, and finally up to 95% solvent B within 0.75 min. The gradient was then maintained at these conditions for 1.5 min before recycling back to 13% solvent B in 0.5 min and then held at 13% solvent B for 2.5 min. The MS/MS data were acquired in multiple reaction ion monitoring (MRM) mode using positive electrospray ionization using 224 MRM transitions corresponding to 224 xenobiotics including drugs and medications using MassLynx (Waters®) (Supplementary Table 2). A quality control was injected before each analysis (Supplementary Table 1).

Non-targeted LC-HRMS screening

A non-targeted screening was carried out as already described [17]. Liquid chromatography-high resolution mass spectrometry (LC-HRMS) analyses were performed using Orbitrap Q Exactive™ mass spectrometer coupled to an UltiMate 3000 pump (Thermo Scientific®). LC separation was performed on a Accucore Phenyl Hexyl (100 × 2.1 mm, 2.6 μm) (Thermo Scientific®). The mobile phases were composed of ammonium formiate at 2 mM and formic acid 0.1% in water (phase A) and ammonium formiate at 2 mM and formic acid 0.1% in methanol and acetonitrile (50/50) (phase B). LC gradient (15 min) begin in an isocratic mode at 1% phase B for 1 min and was increased from 1 to 99% of phase B during 9 min followed by a 1.5 min plateau with 99%, then a 3.5 min equilibration step with 1% of phase B. The flow rate was 500 µL/min, the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C. For mass spectrometry, the instrument operated alternately in ESI positive and negative mode in the same run, the range for acquisition were respectively m/z 70–1000. Ion precursor selection was performed in the data dependent mode of operation where the most intense ion from the previous scan was selected for fragmentation. Full scan data were acquired for each ionization mode at a resolution of 35,000 FWHM, with an AGC target of 1e6 and a maximum injection time of 120 ms. Source parameters were as follows: source voltage + 3.0 and – 4.0 kV, sheath gas flow 60 units, auxiliary gas flow 10 units, capillary temperature 320 °C, S-Lens RF level 60 units. MS/MS data were acquired at a resolution of 17,500 FWHM with an AGC target of 1e5, maximum injection time was 50 ms, a TopN of 5 in positive mode and 2 in negative mode, an isolation window of m/z 2.0. The normalized collision energy (NCE) was stepped at 17.5, 35 and 52.5, and the dynamic exclusion time set at 3 s. A Quality control was injected before each analysis (Supplementary Table 1). Data were compared to a commercial library containing 1551 xenobiotics including drugs and medications using Trace Finder 4.1 clinical software (Thermo Scientific®) (Supplementary Table 2).

Data retreatment

LC-PDA-QDa, LC-MS/MS and LC-HRMS screening data results obtained for urine and BW were collected and compared to each other. These results correspond to the qualitative identification of xenobiotics obtained using each appropriate software and merged together. Urine was considered the reference matrix used for comparison. Data were collected in an Excel file (Microsoft®, 2016 Version) and reprocessed in Prism 9.5 to generate a graph. The concordance rate was calculated using the following formula: number of different compounds commonly found in two matrices/total number of different compounds found in the two matrices.

Statistical analysis

Proportions were compared using the chi-squared test. All statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism (version 9.5, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Population

The study population comprised 50 individuals, predominantly male (n = 40, 80%) (Table 1). The mean age was 42 years, with a median of 40 years and a range from 6 to 92 years. The mean postmortem interval, defined as the time between death (when) and body discovery, was 1.20 days. The median delay between death and autopsy sampling was 2 days. Accidental deaths were the most common (n = 27; 54%), followed by natural causes (n = 14; 28%), homicides (n = 6; 12%), and suicides (n = 3; 6%).

Screening results by matrix and detected xenobiotics

Between 2 and 20 molecules were detected per samples. In 40 cases, urine and BW yielded concordant results whereas discrepancies were observed in only 10 cases (Fig. 1). Overall, 92 different xenobiotics were identified across the 50 autopsy cases using the three combined screening methods. A total of 395 detections were recorded in urine, of which 378 were also identified in BW, corresponding to a concordance rate of 95.7% between the two matrices (Table 2).

In line with this high level of concordance, most commonly detected substances (e.g. paracetamol, opioid substitution therapy, hypnotic, antidepressant, antipsychotics drugs, cardiotropic drugs and most of antiepileptic drugs) were consistently identified in both matrices. Drugs of abuse, including cocaine, heroin, ketamine, and MDMA, were also widely detected with cocaine and its metabolites (BZE, EME) being the most prevalent (Table 2).

By contrast, the few cases in which urine allowed greater detection than BW involved almost exclusively metabolites. These included cocaine derivatives (BZE, EME, cocaethylene, norBZE), certain benzodiazepines (nordiazepam and oxazepam), opioid metabolites (norcodeine, noroxycodone, 6-MAM), as well as caffeine and cotinine (Table 2). With the exception of phenobarbital, for which only a single sample was available, the results collectively show that the lowest concordance percentages (< 80%) were observed for metabolites (EME, 6MAM, norcodeine, noroxycodone). Only two compounds were detected more frequently in the BW, namely cocaethylene and norBZE (Table 2).

Using LC-PDA-QDa, 156 molecules were consistently identified in both urine and BW, while 27 xenobiotics were detected exclusively in urine and 23 exclusively in BW (Fig. 2A). Targeted LC-MS/MS screening identified 298 molecules shared between the two matrices, including 16 specific to urine and 13 specific to BW (Fig. 2B). LC-HRMS enabled the detection of 319 molecules common to both matrices, alongside 42 detected only in urine and 22 only in BW (Fig. 2C). By combining the three approaches and their own libraries, we observed a total of 377 shared molecules identified among the 50 autopsy cases (Fig. 2D). These results are consistent with the number of distinct molecules identified by each method, with LC-PDA-QDa, LC-MS/MS, LC-HRMS, and the combined approach detecting 53, 65, 87, and 91 different compounds, respectively. Thus, as might be expected, LC-PDA identifies the fewest molecules and LC-HRMS non-targeted screening identifies the widest range of xenobiotics in urine/BW.

Figure 2E compares concordance rates in xenobiotic detection across the different analytical approaches. Interestingly, the combined approach that yielded the lowest rate of discrepancies (Fig. 2E). Taken together, these results show that the combined analytical approaches strategy provides the highest level of concordance between urine and BW analyses.

Regarding the causes of death, among the 50 analyzed samples, 23 revealed the presence of substances that were not implicated in the death. Following a multidisciplinary assessment conducted to establish the forensic conclusions, the detected substances were considered the primary cause of death in 17 cases. In 10 additional cases, they were deemed contributing factors in association with other traumatic or medical causes.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to determine the reliability and forensic relevance of BW as an alternative matrix to urine in postmortem toxicology. The BW sampling procedure described here was straightforward and minimally time-consuming during autopsy. Sample volumes were consistent across operators, indicating good reproducibility, simplicity and accessibility of BW, as already described in the literature [8, 9, 11]. To ensure an objective comparison between urine and BW, the same rapid and simple sample preparation protocol routinely used in our laboratory [16] – was applied to all samples, including identical dilution conditions. This approach differs from that used in previous studies on BW [8, 9].

Overall, 50 authentic BW samples were compared with paired urine samples using three complementary liquid chromatography-based toxicological screening methods with different analytical detection techniques (PDA-QDa, MS/MS and HRMS/MS). This combined analytical strategy provided robust and complementary results. We identified a large panel of 91 different substances using the standard workflow integrating the three methods, with a high concordance rate between the two matrices (95.7%).

The detected molecules were fairly representative of those commonly reported in the literature on postmortem urine and BW analyses [8, 9, 11], including frequently consumed molecules such as paracetamol/acetaminophen (N = 26; 52%), cotinine (nicotine metabolite) (N = 33; 66%) and caffeine (N = 45; 90%). Classical drugs of abuse were also identified, including cocaine and/or its metabolites (N = 13), ketamine (N = 6), 6-MAM (heroin metabolite) (N = 4) and MDMA (N = 1) [18, 19]. The parent molecules and their main metabolites were identified in each case. Commonly found medications were also identified such as benzodiazepines, hypnotics, antidepressants and some neuroleptics, in agreement with previous reports on substances detected in postmortem urine [20].

Satisfactory results were obtained with all analytical approaches, although the number of detected molecules differed depending on the sensitivity of the instrumentation and the coverage of the library. Thus, the numbers of molecules detected commonly in urine and BW were 156, 298, and 319 with the LC-PDA-QDa, LC-MS/MS, and LC-HRMS methods, respectively. This is expected, as higher analytical sensitivity and broader spectral libraries increase the likelihood of xenobiotics detection. Nevertheless, each method can be applied to BW extracts and allows for the detection of most of the target compounds, as shown by Steuer et al. (2025) and Rodda et al. (2025), who used only LC-MS/MS [8, 9]. This findings suggest that the use of ultra high sensitivity instrumentation, such as a high-resolution mass spectrometer, may not be strictly required for BW analysis. The results of this study thus demonstrate that it is possible to obtain high-quality data using a range of tools, from high-precision instruments (HRMS) to less advanced methods (LC-PDA-QDa). Interestingly, the integration of the three complementary analytical methods provided BW results that more closely matched those obtained in urine.

Among the 50 cases analyzed, only 10 exhibited discrepancies when combining the three analytical methods, corresponding to only 19 out of 395 molecules undetected in BW compared to urine. These discrepancies primarily involved metabolites of diazepam (nordazepam, oxazepam and temazepam), opiates metabolites (6-MAM, norcodeine, noroxycodone) and cocaine metabolite (EME). Similar finding have been reported by Steuer et al. (2025) [8]. We hypothesize here that BW induces dilution of compounds already present at low concentration in urine, causing them to fall below the instrumental detection threshold. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies reporting lower sensitivity for drug detection in BW compared with urine when using conventional screening approaches (e.g., immunoassay or untargeted MS/MS) [8, 11]. However, although certain metabolites were not detected, the corresponding parent compounds were identified in most cases, minimizing the impact on overall toxicological interpretation.

In addition, these discrepancies may reflect the specific biochemical nature of BW, which is not simply diluted urine but rather a mixture of urine and necrotic urothelium [8, 11]. Interestingly, two molecules (cocaethylene and norBZE) were detected more frequently in BW than in urine. Given its high lipophilicity and weakly basic nature, cocaethylene may undergo lysosomal trapping, leading to its accumulation within urothelial cells. In contrast, norBZE, being amphiphilic with reduced passive diffusion, may be sequestered intracellularly via transporter-mediated uptake, such as through organic cation (OCT) and anion (OAT) transporters [21, 22]. Taken together, these results indicates that regardless of the biochemical composition of BW, it allows for the relevant identification of numerous xenobiotics using different analytical tools.

This work should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, the sample preparation described in this study does not necessarily allow all the molecules to be identified. Thus, cannabis derivatives can be difficult to detect using this extraction. Furthermore, as the analytical workflow relied on liquid chromatography, volatile compounds such as ethanol were not assessed. Dedicated analytical strategies would be required for these substances, although previous studies have reported the detection of alcohol and cannabinoids in BW [8, 9, 11]. Second, the identified compounds cannot be quantified here, as these are purely qualitative urine screening methods. However, detection profiles were highly comparable between urine and BW across the three analytical techniques, supporting the overall robustness of BW as an alternative matrix. These results suggest that BW represents a valuable alternative when urine samples are not available, without compromising the identification of relevant xenobiotics.

In all cases, the toxicological results obtained from BW samples led to the same forensic conclusions as those drawn from urine analyses. These results suggest that BW samples may constitute a reliable alternative matrix for forensic interpretation when urine is unavailable, as this does not alter the toxicological conclusions.

Conclusion

This study provides strong evidence that BW represents a robust and reliable alternative to urine for qualitative toxicological screening in postmortem investigations. Across 50 authentic forensic cases analyzed using three complementary analytical platforms, BW and urine exhibited a high degree of concordance in the detection of xenobiotics. Minor discrepancies, mainly related to metabolites, were expected given physiological differences in matrix composition and did not compromise the overall interpretation of toxicological findings.

Beyond its analytical performance, BW offers notable practical advantages. It can be easily collected at autopsy when urine is unavailable or insufficient, requires no specific preparation, and is compatible with conventional extraction procedures and chromatographic systems routinely used in forensic laboratories. These features make BW a versatile and accessible matrix, enabling meaningful toxicological interpretation even in challenging postmortem scenarios.

Altogether, our findings support the inclusion of bladder wash as an alternative biological matrix in forensic toxicology protocols. Its systematic consideration could enhance the reliability, reproducibility, and interpretative consistency of postmortem toxicological investigations, particularly when traditional matrices are lacking.
