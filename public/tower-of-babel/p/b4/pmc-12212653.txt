Serum IP-10 increase correlated with PEG-IFNα response in nucleot(s)ide analogs-treated patients with chronic hepatitis B
Authors: Wen-Xin Wang, Xiaoyan Li, Xue-Yuan Jin, Rui Jia, Hong-Min Wang, Shuang-Nan Zhou, Xin Zhang, Ying-Ying Gao, Fu-Sheng Wang, Junliang Fu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsTo investigate the association between serum IP-10 and HBsAg levels in chronic hepatitis B (CHB) patients previously treated with nucleot(s)ide analogs (NAs) followed by combined treatment with an NA and pegylated interferon alpha (PEG-IFNα).MethodsNinety-nine patients with serum levels of HBsAg <3000 IU/mL and HBV DNA <20 IU/mL who received prior NA treatment were enrolled. Participants were administered either NA monotherapy (NA group) or combination therapy with PEG-IFNα (Add-on group). Laboratory indicators and IP-10 levels were assessed in serial peripheral blood samples collected at 12- and 24-week intervals. The outcome of this study was a loss or >1 log10 IU/mL decline in serum HBsAg.ResultsAfter 48 weeks of antiviral therapy, none of the 27 NA group patients and 15 of the 72 Add-on group patients achieved HBsAg loss. Baseline serum HBsAg and IP-10 levels were equivalent across both groups. The combination treatment led to a decrease in serum HBsAg levels and an early increase in IP-10 levels. Furthermore, a moderate increase in IP-10 levels at weeks 12 or 24 was correlated with loss and decline of HBsAg in the Add-on group. Receiver operating characteristic curve and regression analyses demonstrated that a moderate increase in serum IP-10 levels at weeks 12 or 24 was predictive of HBsAg loss and decline in the Add-on group (p < 0.05).ConclusionAn early and moderate increase in the serum IP-10 level was correlated with responses to PEG-IFNα among patients with CHB treated with NAs.Keywords: IP-10, Pegylated interferon alpha, HBsAg, Predictor, Functional cure

Introduction

Chronic infection with hepatitis B virus (HBV) may progress to hepatitis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma, with various complications [1,2]. Long-term clinical outcomes in patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) are markedly enhanced following a functional cure characterized by the sustained absence of serum HBV DNA and hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) [3,4].

Nucleos(t)ide analogs (NAs) and interferon (IFN)α are available as antiviral drugs with different mechanisms of action [5]. NAs have a stronger inhibitory effect on HBV DNA replication, while IFNα possesses a greater capacity to promote serologic negative conversion of antigens. However, IFN-based therapy carries the potential to induce adverse events, limiting its clinical application. Consequently, there is an urgent need for indicators that can effectively predict immune responses and clinical outcomes early, thereby facilitating the optimization of clinical strategies for CHB.

The exact mechanisms of HBsAg clearance have not been fully elucidated. As an integral component of the host immune response, cytokines possess the capacity to directly impede viral replication or indirectly modulate various immunization processes [6,7]. Evidence from prior research indicates that cytokines, particularly IFNγ-inducible protein-10 (IP-10), may play a pivotal role in predicting immune control of hepatotropic viruses [[8], [9], [10]]. In addition, hepatic flares temporally correlate with elevated serum levels of IP-10 [11]. Furthermore, the plasma level of IP-10 and hepatic mRNA expression of the IP-10 gene exhibit a significant correlation [9], enabling insights into hepatic conditions through serum IP-10 assessment.

However, the predictive value of IP-10 for antiviral efficacy remains uncertain. A previous study reported that higher baseline intrahepatic IP-10 levels correspond to improved prognoses in patients with CHB receiving entecavir therapy, with reductions in serum IP-10 and HBsAg levels displaying a positive correlation [12]. Nonetheless, reports indicate that a slow decline in IP-10 levels during entecavir treatment acts as a predictor for hepatitis B envelope antigen (HBeAg) seroconversion [13]. Among CHB patients receiving pegylated IFNα (PEG-IFNα) and adefovir, high baseline IP-10 levels appear to be linked to favorable responses in HBeAg-positive patients; by contrast, no such association was observed in HBeAg-negative patients [9]. In addition, serum IP-10 concentration is reportedly correlated with serum levels of HBV DNA and HBsAg, as well as indices of liver inflammation and fibrosis in CHB [12], seemingly contradicting the notion that high levels of IP-10 predict loss of HBeAg or HBsAg [14].

In short, the role of serum IP-10 levels in predicting antiviral efficacy in patients with CHB is complex and controversial. The profile and clinical significance of serum IP-10 levels in patients with low HBsAg levels, for whom a functional cure is readily available, remains unclear. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to investigate the role of serum IP-10 levels in the early prediction of the loss and reduction of HBsAg in NA-treated and virally suppressed CHB patients.

Methods

Population and study design

In this study, patients with CHB who visited the No. 8 clinic of the Fifth Medical Center of the Chinese PLA General Hospital from May 2018 to December 2018 and met the enrollment criteria were enrolled consecutively. The enrollment criteria were as follows: 1) diagnosis consistent with Guidelines for Prevention and Treatment of Chronic Hepatitis B [15]; 2) age between 18 and 65 years; 3) persistently negative serum HBV DNA and quantitative HBsAg <3000 IU/mL after more than 1 year of NA therapy; and 4) willingness to participate in follow-up. Exclusion criteria included the following: coinfection with other hepatitis viruses; cirrhosis, liver cancer, or other decompensated liver disease; unwillingness to participate in the study: and treatment with IFNα/PEG-IFNα, glucocorticoids, or other immunomodulatory therapies within 6 months prior to enrollment. Enrolled patients were categorized into one of the following two treatment groups on the basis of individual patient preference and clinician recommendation: NA monotherapy (NA group) or combination therapy with an NA and PEG-IFNα (Add-on group). Peripheral blood samples were collected every 12 weeks in the Add-on group and every 24 weeks in the NA group. The primary outcome was serum HBsAg loss at 48 weeks of treatment, and the secondary outcome was the decrease in serum HBsAg compared with baseline at 48 weeks of treatment. The results of HBsAg profiling over 48 weeks of treatment were used to classify the clinical outcomes into complete response (CR, HBsAg loss), partial response (PR, a decline in HBsAg >1 log10 IU/mL but not loss), and no response (NR, neither HBsAg loss nor a decline in HBsAg >1 log10 IU/mL). The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fifth Medical Center of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (2018-032-D).

Clinical and laboratory tests

Serological parameters of HBV, including HBV DNA, HBsAg, HBeAg, were routinely tested in the central clinical laboratory. Serum HBV DNA levels were measured by the COBAS AmpliPrep/COBAS TaqMan real-time PCR assay, with a lower detection limit of 20 IU/mL (Roche Molecular Systems, Inc., Branchburg, NJ, USA). Serum HBsAg levels were quantified by an Elecsys HBsAg II quant II immunoassay (Roche Diagnostics GmbH, Mannheim, Germany), with a lower detection limit of 0.05 IU/mL. Quantification of serum HBsAg <0.5 IU/mL was defined as HBsAg loss. Serum levels of IP-10 were determined using a flow cytometer with the AIMPLEX kit (Aimplex Biosciences, Inc., Beijing, China), in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical analysis

Medians (quartiles) were reported for continuous variables. Statistical significance of differences between two groups were determined using the Mann–Whitney U test; for differences among three groups, the Kruskal–Wallis H test was used. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-squared test. Spearman correlation was used to determine correlations among continuous variables. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and regression analyses were used to determine the predictive effect of IP-10 on the primary and secondary outcomes. In the bilateral test, a p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistical difference. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS (version 25.0; IBM, Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes of patients

In this study, we enrolled a total of 99 eligible patients (84 males and 15 females), 39.4% of whom were HBeAg positive (Table 1). Twenty-seven patients were assigned to the NA group to receive NA monotherapy, and 72 patients were assigned to the Add-on group to receive combination therapy with an NA and PEG-IFNα. No significant differences were observed in age, sex, or levels of HBsAg, HBeAg, or alanine aminotransferase (ALT), between the NA and Add-on groups at baseline.

After 48 weeks of follow-up, no patients in the NA group experienced a loss or a decline of >1 log10 IU/mL in the HBsAg level. However, in the Add-on group, 15 patients (20.8%) achieved a CR and 12 (16.7%) achieved a PR. The remaining 45 patients (62.5%) showed no HBsAg loss or decline >1 log10 IU/mL (NR group).

Profiles of serum HBsAg and IP-10 levels in the NA and add-on groups

At baseline, serum IP-10 levels were comparable between the NA and Add-on groups, with no statistical difference. Following antiviral treatment in the Add-on group, the serum HBsAg level showed a gradual decrease, while the serum IP-10 level showed a significant upward trend prior to a slight decrease. By contrast, in the NA group, the serum HBsAg level remained stable, while the serum IP-10 level showed a slight downward trend (Fig. 1).

Correlation between serum IP-10 profiles and antiviral outcome in the add-on group

During combination therapy, the highest increase in serum IP-10 levels was observed in the NR group, and the lowest in the CR group at weeks 12 (p = 0.028) and 24 (p = 0.041). Furthermore, the increase in serum IP-10 level was significantly higher in the NR group than in the CR group at weeks 12 (p = 0.010) and 24 (p = 0.016) (Fig. 2). From baseline levels, the serum IP-10 levels at week 12 had increased by 0.31 (0.15), 0.38 (0.17), and 0.41 (0.19) log10 pg/mL (p < 0.001, p = 0.002, and p < 0.001) in the CR, PR, and NR groups, respectively. At week 24, the increases were 0.25 (0.27), 0.37 (0.12), and 0.42 (0.20) log10 pg/mL (p < 0.001, p = 0.002, and p < 0.001), respectively.

The reduction in HBsAg levels from baseline at week 48 in the Add-on group was negatively correlated with the increase in serum IP-10 levels at week 12, but did not reach statistical significance (r = −0.225, p = 0.068, Fig. 3B). This reduction in HBsAg levels was also significantly negatively correlated with the increase in serum IP-10 levels at week 24 (r = −0.338, p = 0.005, Fig. 3C).

Furthermore, the increase in serum IP-10 level at week 12 was significantly positively correlated with ALT levels at week 12 (r = 0.289, p = 0.015, Fig. 3D) and the increase in ALT levels from baseline at week 12 (r = 0.346, p = 0.004, Fig. 3E).

Loss and decline of serum HBsAg at week 48 predicted by early changes in serum IP-10 levels

ROC curves were utilized to determine whether baseline serum HBsAg levels, serum IP-10 levels, or their combination could predict the loss or a >1 log10 IU/mL decrease from baseline of serum HBsAg at week 48. The area under the curve (AUC) for baseline serum HBsAg level to predict serum HBsAg loss at week 48 was 0.892 (0.799–0.986; p < 0.001). The AUC of serum IP-10 increase at week 12 to predict HBsAg loss at week 48 was 0.706 (0.575–0.838; p = 0.014), with a cut-off value of 0.4 log10 pg/mL. Compared with baseline HBsAg levels alone, an increase in serum IP-10 at week 12 combined with baseline HBsAg levels did not significantly improve the prediction of HBsAg loss at week 48 (AUC: 0.892 vs. 0.895) (Fig. 4A). For predicting a decrease in serum HBsAg by 1 log10 IU/mL at week 48, the baseline serum HBsAg level demonstrated an AUC of 0.703 (0.565–0.841; p = 0.005). Furthermore, the combination of increased serum IP-10 at week 12 enhanced the predictive efficacy (AUC: 0.753, 0.630–0.877; p = 0.001). Notably, the cut-off value for the increase in serum IP-10 was also 0.4 log10 pg/mL (Fig. 4B). In addition, an increase in serum IP-10 at week 24 indicated a subsequent loss or decrease of >1 log10 IU/mL in serum HBsAg at week 48, mirroring the observations at week 12 (Fig. 4C, D).

Regression analysis was used to further substantiate the predictive capability of serum IP-10 in the efficacy of the 48-week combination therapy (Table 2). Univariate regression analysis indicated that elevations <0.4 log10 pg/mL in serum IP-10 could predict either loss or a reduction of >1 log10 IU/mL in serum HBsAg levels at week 48. Furthermore, multivariate regression analysis demonstrated that elevations <0.4 log10 pg/mL in serum IP-10 were an independent predictor of serum HBsAg loss (p = 0.029).

Discussion

The optimal objective in the management of patients with CHB is achievement of HBsAg loss, a milestone denoting functional cure linked to improved long-term clinical outcomes, including reduced rates of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma development [16]. In patients with prior NA treatment, the combination antiviral therapy of NA and PEG-IFNα has a favorable effect on obtaining a functional cure [16,17]. Nonetheless, in this study, we only observed HBsAg clearance in ∼20.8% of patients, a rate comparable to that of selected patients, but falling short of the level considered satisfactory in clinical practice. Considering the promise of improvements in cure rate and potential adverse events associated with PEG-IFNα, it is essential to explore immunological biomarkers that predict the functional cure of CHB.

IFNα induces various biological effects, including antiviral and immune regulation, through the activation of numerous downstream IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs). Among these ISGs is IP-10, also known as chemokine C-X-C motif ligand (CXCL)10, a member of the CXC subfamily of chemokines, which is expressed by monocytes and endothelial cells in response to induction by IFN-γ and tumor necrosis factor. Furthermore, IP-10 has chemotactic properties and can activate T lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells [18], playing a pivotal role in antiviral immunity via binding to the receptor CXCR3 [9,12]. Studies have shown that elevated levels of IP-10 during the combination treatment of NA and PEG-IFNα are associated with more substantial reductions in HBsAg levels during the immune-tolerant phase of CHB [19].

However, IP-10 has been demonstrated to function as a pro-inflammatory chemokine and a marker of the liver inflammation response [11,19]. Patients with severe hepatitis and liver fibrosis show increased IP-10 expression both in the circulation and the liver [12]. High levels of IP-10 in the liver are associated with the pathogenesis of chronic hepatitis, and promote immune-mediated liver damage [18,20]. IP-10 levels in CHB have also been shown to be positively associated with HBV DNA and HBsAg levels [12].

This study aimed to assess the variations in serum IP-10 levels to further examine their clinical significance in the therapeutic outcomes of combination therapy in NA-treated and virologically suppressed CHB patients. Notably, patients receiving combination therapy exhibited significantly elevated serum IP-10 levels, whereas those on NA monotherapy showed stable levels. As an ISG, IP-10 exhibits heightened expression following PEG-IFNα treatment [18], which may explain the lack of a notable rise in IP-10 levels in the NA group. In addition, the limited reduction in HBsAg levels among patients undergoing NA monotherapy could contribute to this observation, as indicated by previous findings linking serum IP-10 with serum HBsAg [12]. A significant positive correlation was observed between the early increase in IP-10 and the early increase in ALT in the combination group. Early elevation of ALT during combination therapy often heralds immune activation and is indicative of the restoration of CD56bright NK cells and favorable outcomes [21].

Following 48 weeks of combined therapy, patients exhibiting a poor response showed the most substantial increase in IP-10 levels during the initial stages of treatment, whereas those with a favorable response displayed only a slight increase in IP-10 levels during these stages. Later in the combination treatment, serum IP-10 levels exhibited decreases. Hepatitis B flares can manifest in two opposite directions. Beneficial liver inflammation is associated with spontaneous normalization of ALT and a decline in virological markers, whereas detrimental liver inflammation may manifest as persistent hepatitis and even progress to decompensated cirrhosis or liver failure [22]. PEG-IFNα modulates the immune system and upregulates NK cells and T cells, as evidenced by increased inflammation levels [23]. As a pro-inflammatory cytokine, IP-10 reflects the level of liver inflammation [11,19]. Therefore, significantly increased IP-10 levels may indicate excessive liver inflammation, potentially representing a detrimental flare that is not conducive to HBV clearance. A gradual decrease in serum IP-10 levels may indicate viral control, suggesting a link between low IP-10 levels and effective virus control [18]. The mechanism by which the amplitude of IP-10 elevation or reduction affects the efficacy of IFN-based therapy requires further exploration.

Hepatitis B is a heterogeneous disease characterized by variations in virus replication levels and immune status, which may explain the seemingly conflicting clinical significance of alterations in serum IP-10 levels. A retrospective study found that elevated baseline IP-10 levels were associated with virological response in patients with a serum HBsAg level of 3.9 log10 IU/mL, positive HBeAg status, presence of HBV DNA, and elevated ALT levels [18]. In contrast, this study included patients with undetectable HBV DNA, negative or markedly low HBeAg levels, and normal ALT levels.

In this study, we conducted a longitudinal follow-up of patients with low HBsAg levels during combination therapy and analyzed the fluctuating patterns of circulating IP-10 levels. Our findings indicate that a moderate increase in IP-10 levels may predict a loss and a significant reduction in HBsAg levels. Nevertheless, this study was constrained by several factors, including its single-center design, small sample size, and the sole focus on serum IP-10 expression without concurrent hepatic expression analysis. The small sample size potentially weakened the strength of the conclusions. Further investigation is warranted to substantiate the conclusions and elucidate the underlying mechanism. Furthermore, this study did not ascertain the specific cell types responsible for IP-10 secretion, nor the specificity of the IP-10 response to IFN-α. Further research is required to identify the cell types involved in IP-10 secretion during IFN-α therapy and to find more precise biomarkers for predicting anti-HBV efficacy. Despite these limitations, our study provides novel insights and potentially valuable contributions toward achieving a functional cure in CHB using serological markers.
