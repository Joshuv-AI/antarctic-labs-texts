Correlation study of discomfort symptom clusters and self-management in patients with knee osteoarthritis: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yabin Guo, Peipei Zhao, Biyun Zeng, Yang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to explore the relationship between knee discomfort symptom clusters and self-management in old patients with knee osteoarthritis(KOA), providing a basis for clinical improvement of patient symptoms and self-management.MethodsStratified multistage random sampling was adopted to select KOA patients in Changsha and Zhangjiajie, Hunan Province, and their discomfort symptoms and self-management were investigated using the Self-assessment of Knee Discomfort Symptoms of Middle-aged and Older People Questionnaire and the Self-Management of Knee Discomfort Questionnaire for Middle-aged and Older People. Self-management of the survey included symptom management, daily life management, emotion management, and information management. Exploratory factor analysis was used to identify symptom clusters. Pearson correlation analysis and stratified multiple linear regression analysis were used to explore the relationship between discomfort symptom clusters and self-management.Results651 old patients participated in the study. Mean participant self-management entry score (2.53 ± 0.67), 74.8% of patients had low self-management. Of the 21 knee discomfort symptom weights, 12 symptoms with > 80% incidence were selected to form 3 symptom clusters: “Pain-Related Self-Perceived Symptom Cluster” “Limited joint movement symptom cluster”“ Muscle-related self-perception symptom clusters”. All symptom clusters were positively correlated with self-management in KOA patients(r = 0.129–0.382, P < 0.05). BMI, pain-related self-perceived symptom cluster, limited joint movement symptom cluster and muscle-related self-perception symptom clusters significantly associated self-management (P < 0.01).ConclusionOlder patients with knee osteoarthritis exhibit suboptimal self-management capabilities, which are significantly associated by BMI and specific symptom clusters (pain, movement, and muscle perception). Management strategies focusing on these symptom clusters, rather than isolated symptoms, may provide a more effective pathway to improve patient self-management and clinical outcomes. Future research should continue to explore the network distribution of discomfort symptoms and understand the micro-interactions between symptoms and symptom clusters.Keywords: Knee osteoarthritis, Self-management, Symptom cluster

Background

Osteoarthritis of the knee(KOA) is a common disease worldwide, about 22.9% of people aged 40 and over are affected. It is considered the 11th highest contributor to global disability in the older adults along with hip OA [1]. As of today, however, there is no cure or spontaneous remission for KOA [2]. Guidelines for the treatment of KOA point to the ability to self-manage as a good way for patients to improve their condition [3]. Previous studies [4–6] have shown that self-management approaches such as Tai Chi, health education, and knee bracing can effectively improve pain symptoms and physical function in patients with KOA. However, patients often complain about joint pain, numbness, soreness, stiffness, and other knee discomfort symptoms coexist, and this status quo poses a considerable challenge to patient self-management.

Self-management is defined as “the ability of an individual to manage inherent physical symptoms, psychosocial, and lifestyle changes” [7]. KOA patients are also often confused about how they should manage their multiple joint complaints. Recognizing symptom clusters then provides new ideas for solving this problem. In 2003, Kim et al. [8] defined the concept of symptom clusters as the simultaneous occurrence of 2 or more interrelated symptoms that form a stable group. Identifying symptom clusters is a commonly used scientific downscaling method to categorize symptoms with the same mechanism of occurrence to simplify the complex relationships between symptoms in real-world clinical practice [9]. It also helps clinical practitioners to better identify targets in the KOA population that can be precisely targeted for intervention. Research on symptom clusters has been well-established in other chronic conditions, particularly in cancer management. For example, a study [10] has identified clusters such as the “psychological-somatic, dysphagia, fatigue-pain, and gastrointestinal symptom clusters” in cancer patients, which have significantly influenced treatment approaches and patient care. Previous study [11] has shown that symptomatic KOA is associated with higher mortality rates. Therefore, an in-depth study of the symptomatology of KOA, exploring the relationship between symptom clusters and self-management, could help healthcare professionals to accurately manage their patients and may improve patient survival to some extent.

Studies have shown wide variation in the use of self-management [12, 13]. There are indications that clinical pain severity as well as socio-demographic factors (e.g., ethnicity/race, gender, age, education) are associated with the decision to use self-management strategies [13–15] and may be driven by differences in traditional medical care and greater disease burden [16]. Most contemporary research still focuses on the exploration of individual symptoms (especially pain) and patient self-management. Pain is a major discomfort symptom in patients with KOA, two previous studies [17, 18] have shown that the severity of pain is an important factor in using self-management strategies. In our previous study [19], we analyzed the influencing factors on self-management of knee discomfort in 9640 Chinese middle-aged and older adults and further found that the knee discomfort symptoms degree was an independent influencing factor on self-management. However, this study only included four of the more common symptoms and did not explore other potential knee discomfort symptoms.

Therefore, our present study comprehensively investigated 21 common symptoms of knee discomfort and explored the comprise symptom clusters, as well as the association of these symptom clusters on self-management. To our knowledge, few studies have explored the relationship between symptom clusters and self-management in patients with KOA [19, 20]. It is unclear what symptom clusters are present in patients with KOA and how they relate to self-management. Understanding this evidence is important for developing individualized and precise symptom management strategies. Therefore, the aims of this study were (1) to investigate the current status of knee discomfort and self-management in patients with KOA, (2) to generate symptom clusters in KOA patients, and (3) to understand the relationship between symptom clusters and self-management in KOA patients. Based on the literature and theoretical framework, we hypothesized that: Symptom clusters are significantly associated with self-management levels.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted between June to October 2020 in Hunan Province, China. The Ethics Committee of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University approved this study(NO: 202001018). The research process followed the Helsinki Declaration of the World Medical Congress and other relevant provisions. Participants received educational resources on managing KOA from a medical professional at the end of the study.

This study used stratified, multi-stage, random overall sampling to collect samples. The 14 cities/continents in Hunan were divided into 5 parts according to geographic location (Xiangzhong includes Loudi and Shaoyang cities and Yiyang city. Xiangdong includes Changsha, Xiangtan, and Zhuzhou. West Hunan includes Xiangxi Tujia and Miao Autonomous Prefecture, Huaihua City, and Zhangjiajie City. South Hunan includes Hengyang City and Chenzhou City as well as Yongzhou City. North Hunan includes Changde City and Yueyang City). After sampling, the local neighborhood committee/village committee was contacted to provide the list of registered persons as the survey respondents, and the investigator conducted the door-to-door survey. This study was conducted by professional physicians from the School of Public Health, Central South University, who participated in the whole process of guidance(The screening process is shown in Figure 1).

Flowchart for screening the subjects(N=651)

Participant

Inclusion criteria: (1) Participants had normal communication and comprehension skills; (2)met the KOA criterion: (a) having being told by a health care provider that they had KOA; or (b) met the diagnostic criteria for KOA in the Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Osteoarthritis issued by the Orthopaedic Branch of the Chinese Medical Association in 2021 [21]: ① recurrent knee pain in the last 1 month; ②X-ray radiographs (in the standing position or the weight-bearing position) showed a narrowing of the joint space, osteosclerosis and/or cystic changes of the subchondral bone, and formation of a bony cumbersome bone on the edge of the joint; and ③Age ≥ 50 years old; ④ morning stiffness time ≤ 30 min; ⑤ bone friction sound (sensation) during activity. Diagnosis can be made if the diagnostic criteria ①+(any 2 of ②, ③, ④, ⑤) are met. In this study, the hospital had diagnosed ①+③+④ for diagnosis. The diagnosis of KOA was made by patient self-report. The diagnostic criteria shows strong agreement with the Altman criteria [22] in the assessment of symptoms and physical signs, and the criteria used in this study are more aligned with the clinical characteristics of the Chinese population. (3) Providing informed consent. Exclusion criteria: ① Patients aged <60 years were excluded; ② Those unable to complete the survey due to severe comorbidities(e.g., limited to a history of stroke or other neurological condition) and/or cognitive impairment, as these conditions may affect the patient’s ability to understand and accurately respond to the questionnaire content; ③ Diseases diagnosed differently from KOA, such as rheumatoid arthritis.

The sample size was calculated using the formula for cross-sectional studies: n = u2α/2p(1 − p)/d2, where uα/2=1.96, when α = 0.05, p is the prevalence of KOA [23](which is 21.51%), and d is the admissible error (which is 0.035 in this study). This yielded a minimum required sample size of approximately 530 participants. Assuming a 10% non-response rate, at least 589 participants were required.

Instruments

Demographic and clinical information

The demographic information questionnaire was designed based on a literature review and consultation with orthopedic experts, encompassing participants’ demographic information and clinical factors including age, gender, BMI, education level, marital status, household, long term residence(region: city or country), and presence of co-morbidities (i.e., chronic diseases other than KOA).

Knee joint discomfort scale

The questionnaire was developed by Zhao et al. [24] in 2020 and consists of 21 entries; it was used to assess the prevalence, and severity of symptoms. Participants were asked to rate the severity of symptoms. Symptom entries are rated on a scale of 0 to 4, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. Among them, items 15 and 16 are reverse scored. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.949 in our sample.

Knee joint discomfort self-management scale

The scale was developed by Zhao et al. [25] in 2020, and used to investigate KOA patient self-management. A specific description is available in a previously published article [19]. The scale includes 4 dimensions of symptom management, daily life management, emotion management, and information management, with a total of 27 entries. It is rated on a 5-point Likert scale with entries ranging from 1 to 5 and a total score from 27 to 135. Among them, items 15 and 16 are reverse scored. In the survey, participants were asked how often each entry appeared (none, rarely, sometimes, often, always). The mean entry score was calculated; a mean entry score of < 3 was considered low self-management, 3–4 was considered moderate self-management, and ≥ 4 was considered high self-management [25]. The total Cronbach’s α coefficient of the scale was 0.946, the Cronbach’s α coefficients of the factors were 0.798 ~ 0.900, and the internal consistency of the scale was good.

Data collection procedure

Liaison men are placed in selected communities/villages and trained uniformly. Liaison men assisted researchers in gaining contact and trust with the participants and ensuring authentic access to data. In addition, all research assistants underwent rigorous standardized training to ensure they followed uniform guidelines while assisting. The training covered how to convey questions neutrally and avoid leading or suggesting answers to participants. The study conducted a small pre-survey to check the logic, flow, and comprehensibility of the questionnaire before its official release.

Research assistants explained the purpose, content, and possible risks of the study to the participants. The paper or electronic questionnaires were distributed after signing the informed consent form. Completed paper questionnaires were collected on the spot to ensure timely retrieval. For electronic questionnaires, a secure online platform was used, and participants were provided with a unique link to access the survey. Reminders were sent via text message or email to encourage completion. Participants completed the questionnaire on their own, except for older adults with severe visual impairment, who could ask a research assistant to objectively fill in the questionnaire on their behalf. The research assistant read each question aloud and recorded the participant’s responses verbatim without interpretation or influence. To maintain consistency, all research assistants underwent standardized training on how to assist participants with visual impairments. Finally, research assistants reviewed, corrected errors on time, and confirmed that the information was qualified. Before data analysis, we conducted a comprehensive check and cleaning of the data, including consistency checks, outlier handling, and standardization of data formats to ensure data integrity and accuracy.

Statistical analysis

Using IBM SPSS 25.0 to analyze data. Participant symptom incidence was categorical variables and described as frequency and percentage (%); symptom severity and self-management entries sores were continuous variables described as mean (± SD). After the Shapiro-Wilk normality test(P > 0.05), the average scores for self-management were normally distributed. Therefore, t-tests and ANOVA were performed to identify differences in self-management scores according to demographic and clinical characteristics.

Performing exploratory factor analysis with principal axis factoring to identify symptom clusters. Varimax rotation was used to maximize the variance of the loadings within each component. Factors with eigenvalues > 1.0 and symptoms with factor loadings > 0.5 were extracted to form symptom clusters [26].

Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine the relationships between symptom clusters and self-management. Subsequently, a hierarchical regression analysis was conducted using variables that showed statistical significance in the univariate analysis. In the first step, sociodemographic variables were entered as covariates to control for potential confounding. In the second step, symptom cluster variables were added to the model to evaluate their independent associations with self-management in patients with KOA. In addition, the multi-collinearity of independent variables in the model was analyzed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). The statistical significance was set at two-sided P < 0.05.

The effective response rate for the initial recruitment was 99.10%. Among the participants, 298 were male and 353 were female, with a mean age of (71.38 ± 7.66) years. Based on BMI categories, 133 participants were underweight, 365 were normal weight, and 153 were overweight. Comorbidities were reported in 89.7% of the participants. Discussion

Given the high prevalence of KOA worldwide, self-management strategies enable individuals to engage in long-term disease mangement, which is critical to maintaining optimal functioning and health-related quality of life for KOA patients [27]. To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the relationship between clusters of joint discomfort symptoms and self-management in KOA patients. KOA patients reported the co-occurrence of multiple symptoms, all with a high prevalence, but their self-management was low. Notably, the three symptom clusters identified via exploratory factor analysis were found to be positively associated with self-management decisions, suggesting a potential link between symptom perception and behavioral engagement.

Self-management in our cohort of older adults with KOA was poor. The mean score was just 2.53 ± 0.67, with nearly 75% of participants falling into the low-level category—a figure significantly worse than that reported by Chen et al. [28]. This gap likely stems from the low education levels in our sample (90.8% primary school or below), rather than just differences in assessment tools. Most notably, patients struggled to find reliable health information. Few could effectively use digital channels like WeChat or even traditional media to understand their condition. This confirms earlier findings that patients often arrive at orthopedic consultations with little understanding of their diagnosis or treatment options [29, 30]. Given these literacy barriers, we need simpler, more accessible interventions. While low education makes digital adoption harder, co-designed tools like the TREK “My Knee” web-kit [31] show that tailored content works. We propose taking this further: an evidence-based mobile platform that guides patients through consultation, care, and rehabilitation, ensuring they have the right support throughout their clinical journey.

In this study, BMI and comorbidities emerged as key sociodemographic factors associated with self-management. Notably, the finding that higher BMI correlates with better self-management initially appears counter-intuitive, given the link between obesity and poor health behaviors. However, this positive association can be interpreted through two mechanisms. First, from a physiological perspective, the increased mechanical loading on knee joints in high-BMI patients often exacerbates pain and functional limitations. This elevated symptom burden may create a “symptom-driven” necessity, compelling patients to engage more actively in self-management strategies (e.g., medication adherence, physical therapy) to mitigate daily suffering [28]. Second, from a healthcare utilization perspective, patients with higher BMI frequently have higher rates of comorbidities and medical visits. Consequently, they are more likely to receive intensive health education and lifestyle counseling [32], thereby enhancing their health literacy compared to patients with lower BMI. Furthermore, although comorbidities did not reach statistical significance in our regression analysis—likely due to heterogeneity in type and severity—their clinical relevance remains critical. High BMI often coexists with chronic conditions such as cardiovascular disease. As noted by Kang et al. [33], multimorbidity introduces complexity that can hinder effective self-management. Patients often face conflicting regimens prescribed by multiple specialists [34].Therefore, for KOA patients with comorbidities, a multidisciplinary approach is essential to help patients prioritize conflicting health needs and optimize self-management decisions over time [35].

Knee discomfort was highly prevalent in our cohort, with 12 specific symptoms each affecting over 80% of patients. To better understand how these symptoms interact, we used symptom science theory to group them into clusters. We chose Principal Component Analysis (PCA) for this task because, unlike Maximum Likelihood estimation, PCA remains robust even when clinical data—like ours—do not strictly follow a multivariate normal distribution. The analysis revealed three distinct groups: Pain-Related Self-Perceived Symptom Cluster, Limited joint movement symptom cluster, and Muscle-related self-perception symptom clusters. This expands on Jenkins et al. [36], who found similar phenotypes in a much smaller sample (n = 75). Interestingly, patients with higher scores in the pain and functional clusters showed better self-management behaviors. This suggests that when patients clearly perceive distinct symptom patterns, they may be more likely to monitor their health and adjust their behaviors accordingly. Clinically, these clusters support a personalized approach. For instance, patients in the pain cluster may benefit most from medication adjustments and routine physical therapy, while those with functional limitations require rehabilitation focused on range of motion. The muscle-related cluster, however, may need psychological support alongside physical therapy to manage the distress caused by muscle fatigue. Our study’s main strength lies in its large sample size (n = 651). Moving forward, researchers should use network analysis to pinpoint “driver” symptoms that trigger these clusters and apply longitudinal models (e.g., latent growth curve) to track how these symptom patterns evolve over time. A key finding of this study is that symptom clusters are significantly associated with self-management in patients with KOA. Specifically, the Pain-Related, Limited Joint Movement, and Muscle-Related Symptom Clusters all showed positive correlations with self-management behaviors. This aligns with Ginnerup-Nielsen et al. [17], who observed that higher symptom severity often co-occurs with active self-management strategies. We hypothesize that the physical burden of the disease may serve as a behavioral cue. The immediate discomfort of the pain-related cluster may encourage measures to mitigate deterioration, while functional limitations may signal disease progression, increasing the likelihood of behavioral adjustments. Similarly, the association between the muscle-related cluster and self-management is noteworthy. As noted in a systematic review by Øiestad et al. [37], knee extensor weakness is a critical risk factor for KOA progression. It is plausible that patients perceiving this muscle weakness may become more vigilant about their physical stability, thereby engaging more actively in strengthening exercises or fall-prevention strategies as part of their self-management routine. However, our regression model accounted for only 13.1% of the total variance in self-management. This modest explanatory power suggests that self-management is multifactorial. Unmeasured variables—such as health literacy, social support, socioeconomic status, psychological resilience (e.g., self-efficacy), and doctor-patient communication quality—likely play pivotal roles, as posited by the Individual and Family Self-Management Theory. Future research should incorporate these broader psychosocial determinants to fully capture the complexity of patient engagement.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between symptom clusters and self-management behaviors. While we hypothesized that symptom burden correlates with self-management, reverse causality cannot be ruled out; for instance, poor self-management may exacerbate disease progression and symptom severity. Longitudinal studies or randomized controlled trials are needed to elucidate the directional nature of these relationships and to track the temporal evolution of symptom clusters. Second, as this study was conducted in a single province in China, the findings may be influenced by region-specific factors, including distinct lifestyle patterns (e.g., prevalence of manual labor) and environmental conditions. Consequently, the generalizability of these results to other geographic regions or cultural contexts may be limited. Third, although door-to-door surveys facilitated data collection, this recruitment strategy may introduce selection bias by overrepresenting accessible populations (e.g., retired older adults or homemakers). Finally, PCA is inherently exploratory. Future research should employ Confirmatory Factor Analysis to validate the proposed symptom structure and use longitudinal models to develop clinically relevant interventions based on symptom trajectories.

Conclusion

This study revealed that old patients with KOA have lower self-management skills, BMI, pain-related self-perceived symptom cluster, limited joint movement symptom clusters and muscle-related self-perception symptom clusters significantly influence self-management in KOA patients. Healthcare professionals should focus on rural areas and patients with co-morbidities, expanding access to disease information on the Internet and increasing patients’ awareness of the risk of disease. Future research should continue to explore the network distribution of discomfort symptoms, understand the micro-interactions between symptoms and symptom clusters, and search for “driver” symptoms that trigger other symptoms in the clusters and control them.
