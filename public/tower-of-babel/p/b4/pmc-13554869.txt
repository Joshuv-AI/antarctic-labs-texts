Short QT syndrome (SQTS) is a rare inherited cardiac channelopathy characterized by abnormally shortened ventricular repolarization, typically reflected by a QT interval ≤330 ms on a resting 12-lead electrocardiogram. SQTS is associated with a significantly increased risk of ventricular arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death (SCD) in the absence of structural heart disease.1 The underlying pathophysiological mechanism is generally attributed to accelerated myocardial repolarization, most commonly due to gain-of-function mutations in genes encoding cardiac potassium channels, and less frequently due to loss-of-function mutations in L-type calcium channels.2,3 Despite advances in genetic characterization, a substantial proportion of clinically diagnosed SQTS cases remain genetically unresolved, with pathogenic variants identified in only a minority of patients.4 Recent evidence highlights persistent gaps in the understanding of the genetic and molecular basis of SQTS, limiting risk stratification strategies.5 Here, we report a case of SQTS associated with a synonymous variant in the ryanodine receptor type 2 (RYR2) gene, potentially expanding the genetic and mechanistic spectrum of this condition.

History of Presentation

A 48-year-old man was referred to our cardiology unit for evaluation of a transient loss of consciousness consistent with syncope. The episode occurred while sitting, without prodromal symptoms, and resolved spontaneously without postictal confusion or seizure-like activity. Physical examination was unremarkable. A 12-lead electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm at 70 beats/min, normal PR interval (160 ms) and QRS duration (85 ms), and a shortened corrected QT interval (QTc) of 320 ms measured in leads II and V5 (Figure 1).

Past Medical History

The patient was a former smoker with no significant past medical history. Family history was notable for SCD, as the patient's sister died at the age of 16 years.

Differential Diagnosis

Transthoracic echocardiography showed preserved biventricular systolic function and an aneurysmal interatrial septum. A contrast bubble study revealed a right-to-left shunt during the Valsalva maneuver, consistent with a patent foramen ovale, which was interpreted as an incidental finding. Brain magnetic resonance imaging showed no structural abnormalities or ischemic lesions. A head-up tilt test was positive for mixed vasovagal syncope (VASIS I [Vasovagal Syncope International Study]), suggesting a reflex-mediated mechanism for the syncopal episode.

Investigations

Twenty-four-hour Holter monitoring showed a QTc range between 322 ms and 368 ms, with no significant arrhythmias detected. Exercise treadmill testing was negative for myocardial ischemia or clinically relevant arrhythmias; the QTc interval increased from 310 ms at rest to 390 ms at peak exercise. Given the clinical suspicion of a familial channelopathy, comprehensive genetic testing was performed. This revealed a synonymous c.2760 G > A variant in the RYR2 gene, resulting in no change at the protein level (p.Glu920=). The variant was classified as of uncertain significance and is not currently considered pathogenic.

Management

The case was discussed within a multidisciplinary team including a medical geneticist, an electrophysiologist, and a cardiologist specialized in inherited cardiac diseases. Considering the markedly shortened QTc interval and the positive family history of SCD, but in the absence of a pathogenic genetic variant or documented ventricular arrhythmias, an implantable loop recorder was placed for long-term rhythm monitoring.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At 12-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, and no arrhythmic events were recorded. No specific pharmacological therapy was initiated, and regular clinical and device-based follow-up was recommended.

Discussion

QTS is a rare inherited arrhythmogenic disorder associated with an increased risk of malignant ventricular arrhythmias and SCD.1 Clinical manifestations range from asymptomatic presentations to syncope or SCD, which may represent the first manifestation of the disease. To date, several genetic subtypes of SQTS have been identified. Types 1 to 3 are associated with gain-of-function mutations in potassium channel genes (KCNH2, KCNQ1, KCNJ2), whereas types 4 to 6 are linked to loss-of-function mutations in L-type calcium channel genes (CACNA1C, CACNB2, CACNA2D1). More recently, a seventh subtype has been associated with mutations in the SLC4A3 gene. However, the overall diagnostic yield of genetic testing remains limited, with pathogenic variants identified in only 10% to 20% of clinically affected individuals.4 In the present case, the patient fulfilled criteria for a probable diagnosis of SQTS based on the markedly shortened QTc interval (320 ms) and a positive family history of SCD. However, no clearly pathogenic genetic variant was identified. The only genetic finding was a synonymous variant in the RYR2 gene. RYR2 encodes the cardiac ryanodine receptor, a key regulator of intracellular calcium release during excitation–contraction coupling. Pathogenic RYR2 variants are classically associated with catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, a condition characterized by adrenergically mediated ventricular arrhythmias.6 In this case, exercise testing was negative and the clinical presentation was not suggestive of catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia.

Although RYR2 variants have not been previously associated with SQTS, the central role of calcium handling in cardiac electrophysiology raises the hypothesis that alterations in RYR2 function could exert a modulatory effect on myocardial repolarization. Importantly, synonymous variants are traditionally considered functionally neutral; however, emerging evidence suggests that they may influence gene expression through effects on messenger RNA stability, splicing, translational efficiency, or protein folding.7 In inherited cardiac channelopathies, such mechanisms have been proposed as potential contributors to disease susceptibility. Nevertheless, in this case, in silico prediction tools do not support a deleterious effect, and the affected residue is not evolutionarily conserved. Therefore, the pathogenic role of the identified variant remains uncertain and may represent an incidental finding, although a subtle modulatory effect on cardiac electrophysiology cannot be completely excluded.

From a clinical standpoint, the patient was considered at intermediate arrhythmic risk. This assessment was based on the presence of a markedly shortened QT interval and a family history of SCD, balanced against the absence of documented ventricular arrhythmias, the likely vasovagal origin of syncope, and the uncertain significance of the genetic finding.

Accordingly, a conservative management strategy was adopted, consisting of continuous rhythm monitoring with an implantable loop recorder rather than prophylactic implantable cardioverter-defibrillator insertion, in line with contemporary guideline recommendations.

Conclusions

This case highlights the challenges of risk stratification in patients with SQTS, particularly in the absence of documented ventricular arrhythmias or clearly pathogenic genetic variants. It underscores the need for cautious interpretation of variants of uncertain significance and supports a tailored, surveillance-based approach in selected patients with intermediate-risk profiles. Finally, this report suggests that further research is needed to clarify the potential role of noncanonical genetic variants, including synonymous substitutions, in the pathophysiology of SQTS.
