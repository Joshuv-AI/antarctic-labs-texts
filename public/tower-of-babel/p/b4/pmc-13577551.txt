Prestin as a context-dependent biomarker of outer hair cell stress: Integrating preclinical and clinical evidence
Authors: Cheng-Yu Hsieh, Jia-Ni Lin, Chuan‐Hung Sun, Yu-Hsuan Wen, Chuan-Jen Hsu, Chen-Chi Wu, Yi-Fan Chou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe early detection of hearing loss remains a major clinical challenge. In many patients, once functional changes become evident on pure-tone audiometry, their recovery is limited. Outer hair cells (OHCs) are essential for cochlear amplification, and their injury is a common pathway in many forms of sensorineural hearing loss. However, reliable biomarkers that reflect hearing impairment in real-time are still lacking. Prestin, a motor protein located in the lateral membrane of OHCs, has been proposed as a potential marker of cochlear stress, but findings across studies vary, and its clinical significance remains uncertain. In this review, we re-examine current evidence to determine under which biological and clinical conditions serum prestin provides meaningful information. We examined data from models of cisplatin exposure, aminoglycoside ototoxicity, noise injury, cyclodextrin-related stress, and age-related hearing loss, along with available human studies. In acute injury models, circulating prestin often shows a transient increase during early OHC membrane damage, suggesting that it reflects active cellular stress. In contrast, chronic degenerative conditions and aging are associated with reduced cochlear prestin expression, which represents a gradual loss of OHC reserve rather than ongoing membrane injury. Differences in assay platforms, isoform detection, and sampling timing may contribute to inconsistent findings. Overall, prestin should not be viewed as a marker of hearing loss severity. Rather, it may indicate acute OHC membrane stress in specific contexts. When interpreted according to etiology and timing, it may help identify early cochlear stress and may support earlier monitoring or otoprotective intervention in selected settings.

INTRODUCTION

Despite major advances in audiological testing and inner ear research, the clinical management of cochlear disorders still relies primarily on functional assessments. These tests often detect damage only after it has become irreversible. Mild or early cochlear injury may occur without measurable changes in hearing thresholds [1]. Standard tools such as pure-tone audiometry and auditory brainstem responses provide valuable functional information. However, they have limited sensitivity for detecting early or subclinical outer hair cell (OHC) stress [23]. A critical clinical gap remains in identifying biological indicators of ototoxic drug exposure, chronic noise exposure, age-related hearing loss, and tinnitus without overt threshold shifts. In contrast to liver or kidney function, where serum markers such as aspartate aminotransferase, alanine transaminase, blood urea nitrogen, and creatinine support early detection and monitoring, no widely accepted biomarker exists for cochlear injury [4]. As a result, hearing loss is often identified only after significant damage has occurred, limiting the possibility of early intervention.

Clinically, we still lack a biological marker that reflects early OHC stress. A reliable marker would allow earlier detection and better risk stratification, and may help bridge experimental findings with clinical disease [56]. Early identification is critical because prompt intervention may help prevent progression to permanent hearing loss. Among emerging candidates, circulating prestin has attracted increasing interest [7]. As an OHC-specific motor protein, prestin has emerged as a candidate indicator of cochlear stress and ototoxic risk, with potential relevance for earlier detection in selected contexts [8].

This manuscript was developed as a narrative review of circulating prestin across different biological and clinical contexts. Relevant studies were identified through PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar using terms related to prestin, OHC injury, ototoxicity, noise-induced hearing loss, age-related hearing loss, sudden sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL), and tinnitus. We primarily considered English-language studies published up to February 2026. Studies were included if they were directly relevant to prestin biology, OHC injury, or circulating prestin in preclinical or clinical settings. Mechanistic, tissue-level, animal, and human evidence were considered separately during synthesis.

MOLECULAR STRUCTURE AND MECHANISM OF PRESTIN

Members of the solute carrier 26 (SLC26) family typically function as anion transporters, facilitating the movement of anions (including chloride, bicarbonate, sulfate, and oxalate) across cell membranes [9101112]. These transporters are essential for maintaining pH balance, regulating fluid secretion, and preserving ion homeostasis. Prestin (SLC26A5), a distinctive integral membrane protein within the SLC26 family, serves as a specialized electromechanical transducer in OHCs [13]. Structurally, prestin forms a symmetric homodimer containing 14 α-helical transmembrane (TM) segments, with substantial N- and C-terminal regions extending into the cytoplasm. Each subunit comprises two overlapping structural domains: a core domain and a gate domain. Upon anion binding, the core domain forms an anion-binding pocket, whereas the gate domain controls ion flow and enables the protein’s essential electromotility [14].

Prestin has diverged dramatically from other SLC26 family members, evolving into a unique piezoelectric motor rather than functioning as a conventional anion transporter [1516]. While typical SLC26 proteins actively transport anions across membranes, prestin repurposes these anions as voltage sensors to power its electromechanical activity. This functional transformation arose from key evolutionary mutations in the TM6 helix, specifically at residues G274 and G275 [14]. These glycine substitutions abolish the standard TM transport mechanism, blocking anion translocation across the cell membrane. This structural configuration allows the TM6 helix to provide the flexibility necessary for outward bending and conformational shifts at the protein-lipid interface.

THE FUNCTIONAL ROLE OF PRESTIN

Prestin, the motor protein localized to the basolateral membrane of cochlear OHCs, provides the molecular basis of electromotility essential for mammalian auditory amplification [131718]. Unlike ATP-driven motors, prestin directly converts electrical voltage into mechanical movement via voltage-dependent conformational changes. Operating at microsecond speeds, prestin responds to membrane potential and tension by exploiting anion binding as an allosteric voltage sensor, triggering rapid conformational transitions between contracted (depolarization) and expanded (hyperpolarization) states without completing traditional ion transport cycles [19]. This voltage-driven piezoelectric mechanism produces rapid changes in cell length and membrane area that actively amplify basilar membrane vibration.

Prestin knockout mice exhibit complete loss of OHC electromotility, resulting in 40-60 dB hearing threshold elevations and abolishment of cochlear amplification, despite intact hair cell structure [1620]. Salicylate acts as a competitive reversible inhibitor of prestin by binding to the intracellular anion-binding site, thereby stabilizing the expanded conformation and the contracted-expanded transitions that drive OHC electromotility [14]. These studies demonstrate that prestin stands as the indispensable molecular engine underlying OHC electromotility and the mammalian cochlear amplifier. From the profound hearing loss observed in knockout models to the specific inhibitory mechanisms of salicylate, evidence underscores that even subtle alterations in prestin function or conformation can drastically disrupt auditory sensitivity.

PRESTIN AS A BIOMARKER: CURRENT EVIDENCE

Prestin is the motor protein responsible for OHC electromotility and cochlear amplification. Because conventional audiologic tests, such as pure-tone audiometry, mainly detect established hearing loss, they may miss early or reversible OHC dysfunction. As summarized in Table 1, current evidence from both experimental models and human studies suggests that circulating prestin changes in a context-dependent manner across different cochlear disorders. In this literature, it is important to distinguish between different levels of evidence. Mechanistic and cellular studies support biological plausibility, whereas tissue-level expression studies inform cochlear localization and regulation. By contrast, animal and human circulating prestin studies are more directly relevant to its value as a blood-based biomarker. Ototoxicity

Cisplatin

Cisplatin-induced ototoxicity is currently the most consistently supported context for considering prestin as a pharmacodynamic biomarker. In animal studies of mice and guinea pigs, cisplatin exposure induces a transient, time-dependent increase in serum prestin levels, typically peaking within several days after treatment and subsequently declining as OHC damage progresses [21]. Notably, this elevation often precedes detectable shifts in auditory brainstem response (ABR) thresholds and has been reported together with histological evidence of OHC injury [621].

Consistent with these animal findings, human studies have shown that serum prestin levels may rise during or shortly after cisplatin treatment, in some cases before overt deterioration is detected by pure-tone audiometry [722]. Moreover, early increases in circulating prestin have also been associated with ototoxic changes identified by audiologic measures such as pure-tone audiometry and distortion product otoacoustic emissions [722]. Taken together, these studies support the concept that circulating prestin may reflect early OHC stress, rather than established cell loss. Prestin can serve as a promising candidate biomarker of early OHC stress in cisplatin ototoxicity, although further validation is still needed before clinical implementation.

Aminoglycosides

Unlike cisplatin, the role of prestin in aminoglycoside‐induced ototoxicity is less clearly defined at the clinical level. Preclinical evidence suggests that prestin-expressing OHCs are susceptible to aminoglycoside-induced apoptosis and that prestin-related membrane and ionic properties may influence cellular susceptibility [23]. Further animal studies demonstrate that the expression of prestin mRNA could be downregulated upon initial exposure to aminoglycoside before overt OHC apoptosis and measurable hearing loss [23], and animal circulating biomarker evidence has also suggested associated changes in circulating prestin [24]. It has been suggested that this early decrease in prestin expression may serve as a protective response against the injury due to aminoglycoside. Longitudinal human circulating biomarker evidence linking aminoglycoside exposure to changes in prestin levels is currently lacking.

Cyclodextrin (CDX)

Cyclodextrin-induced ototoxicity provides a well-controlled preclinical model for studying prestin change. Available evidence is currently limited to animal circulating biomarker studies in guinea pig models. CDX exposure induces a rapid and transient serum prestin burst, characterized by a ~134 kDa form that peaks within 1–3 days and returns to baseline within two weeks [825]. Notably, this elevation occurs even in the absence of OHC loss, suggesting that prestin may reflect acute OHC stress [26]. Human data following CDX exposure are currently unavailable.

Noise exposure

Noise exposure highlights the importance of distinguishing acute from chronic cochlear stress. In preclinical studies, acoustic overexposure can induce changes in prestin expression or localization within OHCs, and animal circulating biomarker evidence suggests that there is a temporal pattern of change in serum prestin level after noise-induced hearing loss, which may relate to hearing loss severity [32]. Human circulating biomarker evidence, by contrast, suggests that serum prestin levels have a negative association with noise exposure [27], consistent with the concept of progressive OHC loss and reduced prestin reserve. These findings support a dual interpretation in which acute noise exposure may elicit short-term prestin release reflecting OHC stress, whereas chronic exposure more closely reflects cumulative OHC depletion [28]. Clinically, this pattern supports the use of prestin in conjunction with otoacoustic emissions or extended high-frequency audiometry for risk stratification rather than as a standalone diagnostic marker.

Age-related hearing loss

In the aging cochlea, both the expression and functional integrity of prestin are progressively compromised. Preclinical evidence in aged animal models consistently demonstrates a decline in prestin expression within OHCs, accompanied by reduced cochlear sensitivity and diminished otoacoustic emissions [33]. This age-related reduction in prestin may contribute to weakened cochlear amplification in presbycusis, but it should be interpreted within the broader multifactorial pathology of age-related hearing loss.

Consistent with these experimental findings, studies have reported lower circulating prestin levels in older individuals and in patients with sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) compared with age-matched healthy controls [34]. Notably, the relationship between prestin levels and hearing function may be frequency-dependent. Human circulating biomarker evidence also suggests that the relationship between serum prestin and hearing function may be frequency-dependent, with a stronger association reported for low-frequency than for high-frequency hearing thresholds [35]. Overall, prestin appears to reflect OHC reserve in age-related hearing loss, but its clinical relevance depends on hearing frequency and the cumulative burden of cochlear degeneration.

Sudden sensorineural hearing loss

In acute idiopathic SSNHL, mechanistic evidence is limited by scarce preclinical data; however, it is biologically plausible that acute cochlear insults involving OHCs lead to prestin release. Human circulating biomarker evidence from clinical studies by Sun et al. and Saadat et al. reported higher circulating prestin levels in SSNHL patients during the acute phase compared with controls, followed by a gradual decline over time, suggesting that circulating prestin reflects acute cochlear stress rather than permanent damage [29]. Notably, prestin elevation was not uniform: only about half of the patients showed increased levels, with substantial inter-individual variability.

Prestin levels also did not scale with hearing loss severity, with elevations more apparent in mild-to-moderate SSNHL than in severe cases. This pattern implies that prestin is most informative when OHCs are stressed but still viable, and less so after extensive cellular loss. In a subset of patients, higher acute-phase prestin levels were associated with improved hearing recovery [30], confirming the concept of prestin elevation due to reversible OHC involvement. Overall, current evidence suggests that prestin is unlikely to serve as a universal diagnostic or prognostic biomarker for SSNHL. Instead, its potential role may be to identify subgroups of patients with significant OHC involvement and to complement audiometric and other clinical assessments.

Tinnitus

Similarly, in tinnitus, mechanistic evidence from preclinical concepts of OHC dysfunction without overt threshold elevation provides indirect support for prestin involvement. Human circulating biomarker evidence from a prospective case–control study conducted in 2025 explored the relationship between serum prestin levels and tinnitus. The study involved 89 participants, including 49 with chronic tinnitus and 40 controls. The results indicated a significant increase in the 97 kDa isoform of prestin in the tinnitus group, suggesting possible OHC involvement [31]. However, this hypothesis remains preliminary and requires further replication, particularly because tinnitus pathophysiology is heterogeneous and is not necessarily OHC-driven, even in patients with normal audiograms.

These findings suggest that circulating prestin does not follow a uniform pattern. Rather, its behavior appears to depend on both timing and disease context. In acute injury settings, transient elevation may reflect early OHC membrane stress, whereas in chronic or advanced damage, lower levels may reflect depletion of OHC reserve rather than ongoing injury. This framework may help explain discrepancies across studies, but it should be viewed as a conceptual synthesis of current evidence rather than a universally validated biological rule [Figure 1].

TRANSLATIONAL PERSPECTIVES AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Prestin’s unique role makes it a promising candidate for translational biomarker research in cochlear injury. Prestin reflects biological stress at the level of the cochlear amplifier and can be measured through a simple blood test. This feature may be particularly relevant in ototoxicity, where early biological changes could complement functional monitoring and potentially support timely otoprotective intervention. From a translational perspective, prestin monitoring may also help clinicians evaluate otoprotective treatment response before irreversible hearing loss develops [36]. Before prestin can be used routinely, assay standardization, prospective validation, and clinically approved detection platforms are still needed.

Future work should move beyond single-marker approaches. Combining prestin with other biomarkers, such as otolin-1, and integrating molecular results with functional tests, including otoacoustic emissions and extended high-frequency audiometry, may provide a more comprehensive approach to precision otology. Within audiology, a deeper understanding of prestin’s electromechanical function helps to shape our view of cochlear amplification [37].

Limitations and challenges

Despite growing interest, the clinical utility of prestin as a biomarker faces several ongoing challenges. Detection methods vary considerably across studies. ELISA assays have shown inconsistent sensitivity and specificity, and differences between commercial antibodies may lead to variable results. Recent reports suggest that some kits lack sufficient specificity, highlighting the need for improved antibody design or alternative approaches such as mass spectrometry. Western blotting can provide isoform-level information, but it is labor-intensive and not suitable for routine clinical use. In addition, circulating prestin levels are low and require sensitive, standardized detection methods.

Interpretation is further limited by biological and preanalytical variability. Serum prestin levels may be affected by injury timing, disease context, sample handling, hemolysis, environmental noise exposure, and inter-individual baseline differences. In addition, hearing loss is biologically heterogeneous, so prestin-related changes should not be interpreted as reflecting a single pathological process across different etiologies. Therefore, these limitations highlight the need for standardized sampling protocols, longitudinal studies, and better-defined reference ranges before prestin can be used reliably in clinical settings.

CONCLUSION

Prestin is a promising biomarker of OHC stress, particularly in acute cochlear injury. However, its current clinical value remains limited by assay variability, biological heterogeneity, and the lack of standardized sampling and validation. Based on current evidence, prestin should not be viewed as a universal marker of hearing loss severity. Instead, it may serve as a context-dependent and time-sensitive indicator of acute OHC membrane stress or injury in selected settings. Rigorous validation and standardized clinical studies are essential before broader clinical implementation can be considered.
