Determinants of human papillomavirus vaccine non-uptake among adolescent girls in Ethiopia: evidence from National Immunization Survey
Authors: Kibir Temesgen Assefa, Abebaw Gebeyehu Worku, Achenef Asmamaw Muche, Bisrat Misganaw Geremew, Mulat Adefris Woldetsadik, Kassahun Alemu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo assess determinants of human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine non-uptake among adolescent girls in Ethiopia.DesignCommunity-based cross-sectional study.SettingEthiopia.ParticipantsA weighted sample of 5341 adolescent girls.MethodsA secondary analysis was conducted using the 2024 Ethiopian National Immunization Evaluation Survey dataset. A two-stage stratified sampling technique was used to select 467 enumeration areas (EAs). Within each EA, 30 households with adolescent girls aged 15–18 were systematically selected. Data were collected using a semi-structured questionnaire. Mixed-effects logistic regression was used to identify individual-level and/or household-level, and community-level determinants. Associations were presented using adjusted ORs with 95% CIs and statistical significance was set at p<0.05.ResultsIndividual and household-level determinants of HPV vaccine non-uptake include age 17–18 years (adjusted OR (AOR)=1.41; 95% CI 1.16 to 1.72), illiteracy (AOR=3.03; 95% CI 2.14 to 4.28), not currently attending school (AOR=2.84; 95% CI 2.24 to 3.60), poor knowledge (AOR=8.91; 95% CI 6.63 to 11.99), unfavourable attitude (AOR=4.24; 95% CI 3.34 to 5.37) and living in the poorest households (AOR=1.48; 95% CI 1.04 to 2.10). Community-level determinants were urban residence (AOR=1.40; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.95); and living in Addis Ababa (AOR=2.73; 95% CI 1.29 to 5.74), Afar (AOR=4.73; 95% CI 2.08 to 10.77), Dire Dawa (AOR=2.69; 95% CI 1.21 to 5.98), Harari (AOR=2.09; 95% CI 1.05 to 4.14) and Somali (AOR=3.68; 95% CI 1.61 to 8.38).ConclusionThe determinants of HPV vaccine non-uptake were older age (17–18), illiteracy, school non-attendance, poor knowledge, unfavourable attitude, living in the poorest households, urban residence and living in Addis Ababa, Afar, Dire Dawa, Harari and Somali. The findings call for improved health literacy, knowledge and attitude through health extension programmes and targeted outreach in underserved urban and pastoralist settings.Keywords: Adolescents, Ethiopia, HPV Vaccine

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a common sexually transmitted, double-stranded DNA virus that causes nearly all cervical cancers, claiming over 342 000 lives annually.1 2 Of these, 90% of deaths occurred in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).2 3 Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) has the highest incidence and mortality rates of cervical cancer,4 with Ethiopia reporting one of the highest burdens—approximately 7445 cases and 6000 deaths annually in 2020.5 6 The HPV vaccine is a highly effective, safe and cost-effective public health intervention for preventing HPV-related cancers.3 7 It was first introduced in 2006 and could reduce global cervical cancer deaths by 30–40% among women.8 The WHO recommends HPV vaccination for girls and boys aged 9–14, with one or two doses and expanded eligibility for older and immunocompromised individuals.9 10

Despite global efforts to scale up HPV vaccination, non-uptake and disparities persist with only one in eight girls vaccinated in 2023, particularly in LMICs including those countries in SSA.11 While high-income countries had only 20–30% non-uptake rate, SSA countries experienced high non-uptake ranging from 46% to 88% and lag behind the WHO’s 90% target.11 As of 2024, only 28 African nations had introduced HPV vaccines, achieving a mere 33% coverage for the first dose.12 Ethiopia launched its HPV vaccination programme in 2018, primarily targeting 14-year-old girls.11 With support from the Global Alliance for Vaccines and Immunisations, WHO and Girls Effect, the country has implemented school-based campaigns, community awareness initiatives and healthcare worker training to boost vaccine uptake.13 14 Despite these efforts, the 2024 National Immunization Program Evaluation survey reported a non-uptake of 46%, with significant regional disparities ranging from 26.4% in Benishangul Gumuz to 67.2% in the Somali region.15 Many Ethiopian subnational studies also reported high non-uptake16,21—from 33.5% in Minjar Shenkora of Shoa16 to 68% in Southwest Shoa.17

Non-uptake of the HPV vaccine and coverage discrepancies remain a significant public health challenge in Ethiopia, partly reflecting inadequate investigation of underlying determinants. Earlier studies have partially identified individual-level determinants among adolescent girls, such as socio-demographic characteristics, knowledge and attitudes.16,21 However, community-level determinants including residence, administrative region, community literacy, community poverty and community media exposure remain unexplored. These unmeasured factors may lead to incorrect inferences and limit the application of findings for policy and programme design. Furthermore, the clustered nature of health behaviours within communities and administrative hierarchies necessitates the explicit modelling of community-level determinants. These underscore the need for a comprehensive and in-depth investigation of determinants using a nationally representative dataset and multilevel modelling that account clustering effect. This work informed tailored interventions to accelerate Ethiopia’s progress toward the WHO’s 90% vaccination target by 2030. The findings would contribute to global efforts to eliminate cervical cancer, particularly in LMIC settings. Therefore, this study aimed to identify individual, household and community-level determinants of HPV vaccine non-uptake among adolescent girls in Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study setting and data source

The study was conducted in all regions and the two city administrations of Ethiopia, except Tigray, from March to July 2023. Tigray region was not included due to conflict in the area. Data were obtained from the 2024 National Immunization Program Evaluation survey, conducted by the Ministry of Health of Ethiopia and its partners in collaboration with Addis Ababa University, Jimma University, Haramaya University, Hawassa University and University of Gondar.15 This survey was the first of its kind and conducted to evaluate the immunisation programme in Ethiopia.

Study design, population and sampling

We conducted a secondary analysis of a community-based cross-sectional survey carried out as part of the 2024 National Immunization Program Evaluation of Ethiopia. The survey included households, health facilities, children (12–35 months), women, adolescent girls (15–18 years), individuals aged 12 and above and community representatives. But for our study we took only adolescent girls aged 15–18 years. As Ethiopia adopted a single-age cohort strategy targeting only 14-year-old girls due to limited implementation capacity, adolescents aged <14 years were not included. The lower age limit of 15 years was chosen because girls vaccinated at age 14 in the previous year had turned 15 by the time of data collection while the upper age limit of 18 years was determined based on the observed age distribution (oldest one) in the survey data.15 The sample size was calculated using a single population proportion formula, assuming a 75% HPV vaccination coverage (taken from international agency for research on cancer), a 95% confidence level, a 2% margin of error, a design effect of 3 and a 10% non-response rate. The calculated sample size was weighted to ensure national representativeness, resulting in a total of 5341 adolescent girls included in this analysis. A detailed description of the sampling approach is available in the original survey.15

The survey used a two-stage stratified sampling method. In the first stage, 467 enumeration areas (EAs) were randomly selected from each region and city administration based on the Ethiopian Statistical Services (ESS) sampling frame. An EA is a defined geographical area assigned to a single enumerator, with an average of 181 households, and was characterised by location, residence type (urban or rural) and estimated household size. The number of EAs per region or city administration was determined proportionally by dividing the estimated childhood immunisation (Penta 3) sample size by 30. The selected EAs from each region and city administration were then stratified into urban (21.4%) and rural (78.6%) strata. In the second stage, a fixed number of 30 households were selected from each EA using equal probability systematic selection, prioritising those with at least one eligible adolescent girl aged between 15 and 18 years. The number of adolescent girls per regions and city administrations was determined based on the proportion or conversion factor of adolescent girls (above 14 years). Field teams delineated EAs using ESS maps and created household sampling frames. When multiple eligible participants were present in a household, one was randomly selected. Revisit attempts were made two times for absent participants, and non-consenting households were replaced by the next eligible household (figure 1).

Measurement

Outcome variable

The outcome variable was HPV vaccination status. Participants who did not receive any dose were categorised as not vaccinated (non-uptake), while those who received at least one dose were classified as vaccinated.15

Exposure variables

The independent variables were categorised into two levels:

Individual and household-level factors were age, literacy status, school attendance, religion, parental education, parental occupation, wealth status, knowledge of HPV, cervical cancer and the HPV vaccine, attitude towards HPV vaccination, relationship with the household head and living arrangements.

Community-level factors included place of residence, community poverty, community literacy, community media exposure and administrative region.

Community-level factors included place of residence, community poverty, community literacy, community media exposure and administrative region.

Operational definitions

Knowledge of HPV, cervical cancer and HPV vaccine

The total knowledge score was calculated using 32 knowledge assessment items, with each correct answer scored as 1, resulting in a range of 0–32. Using Bloom’s cut-off,22 we categorised knowledge as high (23.25–32.0, ≥75%) or low (0–23.24,<75%).

Attitude towards HPV vaccine

Attitude, measured using 14 items on a 5-point Likert scale, was categorised as positive for scores ≥75% and negative otherwise.22

Community poverty

High community poverty was defined as the proportion of adolescents within a cluster (EA) who belong to households categorised in the poorest and poorer wealth quintiles, and low otherwise. It is calculated by dividing the number of adolescents in the lower two wealth quintiles by the total number of adolescents in the cluster.23

Community literacy

High community literacy was estimated based on the proportion of adolescents within a cluster whose fathers, mothers and themselves have formal education (primary and above) and low otherwise.23

Community media exposure

High community media exposure was defined as the proportion of adolescents in a cluster with access to at least one mass media source (radio or television), low otherwise.23

Data collection tools, procedures and quality management

A standardised, semi-structured questionnaire was used to collect data through face-to-face interviews. The survey tools were translated into five major local languages—Amharic, Afar, Afaan Oromoo, Sidama and Somali—to ensure cultural and linguistic appropriateness. Vaccination status was crosschecked using vaccination cards, caregiver or adolescent recall confirmed by probing for vaccine administration details and health facility records. To ensure data quality and reliability, data collectors underwent 7 days of training on tools, ethics and use of Open Data Kit. The questionnaire was pretested, revised and checked for completeness on a daily basis. Data were uploaded to a central server for validation and cleaned to address missing data, inconsistencies and outliers.

Data analysis

Data were cleaned to ensure consistency and identify missing values. Recoding and exploratory analysis were carried out. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were presented through tables, graphs and text. The proportion of HPV vaccine non-uptake was computed to describe study participants. Using directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), we mapped hypothesised causal relationships and identified confounders, mediators and colliders. The potential DAGs were created, and minimally sufficient adjustment sets for exposure variables were derived using the online resource.24 The association between each exposure variable and HPV vaccine non-uptake was examined while controlling for the confounding effects of other variables guided by DAG. The DAG used for adjusting confounders in each exposure variable are attached as supplementary material (online supplemental files 1; 2).

To account for the hierarchical structure of the data—where individual and household-level determinants are nested within community-level determinants—and due to the binary nature of the outcome variable, we employed a mixed-effects logistic regression model. Fixed effects at both the individual and household-level, and community-level were included to estimate the adjusted ORs (AORs) of HPV vaccine non-uptake. To capture the unobserved heterogeneity between communities and to ensure accurate SEs, a random intercept was incorporated at the cluster level. Four sequential models were developed. Model I (the null model) included no explanatory variables and was used to determine the suitability of the data for multilevel modelling by assessing the presence of a clustering effect. Model II included only individual and household-level determinants, while Model III included only community-level determinants. Model IV (the full model) included individual, household and community-level determinants simultaneously to assess their independent effects after accounting for clustering. Model fit was evaluated using the Akaike and Bayesian information criteria (AIC and BIC), with lower values indicating better fit. Intraclass correlation coefficients and median OR (MOR) were used to quantify variation between clusters, and proportional change in variance (PCV) measured the variability explained by each model. Variance inflation factor was used to check for multicollinearity among predictor variables. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05, and results were reported as AOR with 95% CIs. Sampling weights were used in the analysis to account for complex survey design and non-response, making survey results unbiased and representative. These weights were computed as the inverse probability of selection for the EA (primary sampling unit) and household (secondary sampling unit).

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics

Of 5341 weighted samples, 3245 (61%) were aged 15–16 years, with a mean age of 16.3 years (SD±1.1). Most adolescents were able to read and write (4543; 85.1%), attending primary education (3602; 67.4%) and currently attending school (3410; 63.8%). Orthodox Christianity was the most practised religion (2528; 47.3%), followed by Islam (1906; 35.7%). The majority of adolescents (4173; 78.1%) were daughters of household heads, with 3940 (73.8%) of mothers and 3101 (58.1%) of fathers lacking formal education. Around 3404 (64%) of mothers were housewives, and 3915 (73.3%) of fathers were farmers (table 1).

Random effect results and model comparison

In the null model (Model I), significant community-level variance (1.4, p<0.001) and an intraclass correlation coefficient of 29% justified the use of mixed-effects modelling, indicating that nearly one-third of the variation in HPV vaccine non-uptake was attributable to clustering or random effects. The PCV showed that Model IV explained 35% of the variance. An MOR of 3.1 indicates substantial community-level variation, with one individual being three times more likely to be unvaccinated than another due to community differences. With the lowest AIC (4844.6), Model IV provided the best fit, capturing vaccine non-uptake by incorporating individual-level, household-level and community-level determinants (table 2).

Determinants of human papillomavirus vaccine non-uptake among adolescent girls

Adolescent girls aged 17–18 years were 41% more likely to be unvaccinated than those aged 15–16 (AOR=1.41; 95% CI 1.16 to 1.72). Respondents who could not read or write (AOR=3.03; 95% CI 2.14 to 4.28) and those not currently in school (AOR=2.84; 95% CI 2.24 to 3.60) were almost three times more likely to remain unvaccinated compared with their counterparts. Respondents with poor knowledge of HPV, cervical cancer and the HPV vaccine had nine times higher odds of being unvaccinated (AOR=8.91; 95% CI 6.63 to 11.99), and those with poor attitudes were four times more likely to remain unvaccinated (AOR=4.24; 95% CI 3.34 to 5.37). The odds of being unvaccinated were nearly two times higher among adolescents in the poorest wealth quintile compared with those in the richest (AOR=1.48; 95% CI 1.04 to 2.10). Regarding community-level factors, adolescent girls living in urban areas were 40% more likely to remain unvaccinated compared with those in rural areas (AOR=1.40; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.95). compared with girls residing in Benishangul Gumuz, those living in Addis Ababa (AOR=2.73; 95% CI 1.29 to 5.74), Afar (AOR=4.73; 95% CI 2.08 to 10.77), Dire Dawa (AOR=2.69; 95% CI 1.21 to 5.98), Harari (AOR=2.09; 95% CI 1.05 to 4.14) and Somali (AOR=3.68; 95% CI 1.61 to 8.38) were 2.7, 4.7, 2.7, 2.1 and 3.7 times more likely not to receive HPV vaccine, respectively (table 3).

Discussion

Despite the proven life-saving potential of the HPV vaccine in preventing cervical cancer, non-uptake remains a global challenge, particularly in LMICs.8 Ethiopia’s 2024 National Immunization Programme Evaluation survey found that almost half (46%) of adolescent girls were not vaccinated against HPV. This study aimed to identify individual, household and community-level determinants of HPV vaccine non-uptake. Individual and household-level determinants include older age (17–18 years), illiteracy, school non-attendance and poor knowledge and attitudes, while community-level determinants include urban residence and living in Afar, Somali, Harari, Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa. These findings underscore that targeted catch-up vaccination and tailored community-based education strategies should be prioritised for older, out-of-school, low-literacy adolescents and underperforming regions.

Our findings showed that nearly half of adolescent girls did not receive the HPV vaccine, indicating a significant gap in coverage despite national scale-up efforts. This result implies that Ethiopia is not on track and must intensify its efforts to achieve the WHO’s goal of vaccinating 90% of girls against HPV by 2030.8 Progress towards this target remains a global concern. A recent systematic review found that global HPV vaccination coverage among girls aged 9–14 years was only 47.6%, with only 15 countries achieving 90% first-dose coverage.25 Although administrative reports before 2023 in Ethiopia indicated high coverage of the HPV vaccine (95% for the first dose and 90% for the second dose),26 our findings contradict this high coverage. This discrepancy may be due to differences in denominators between administrative data and population-based surveys, as well as our inclusion of hard-to-reach populations who are often missed by routine reporting systems.27 The low uptake in our study may also be attributed to Ethiopia’s reliance on school-based delivery, which excludes out-of-school girls, the single-age-cohort strategy targeting only 14-year-old girls and the failure to fully routinise HPV vaccination.28 In response, Ethiopia made transformative policy shifts in 2024, including implementing a nationwide multiage cohort (MAC) vaccination campaign targeting girls aged 9–14 years, switching to a single-dose schedule and planning to integrate HPV vaccination into routine immunisation which improved coverage, vaccine access and programme efficiency.29 Although Ethiopia has made notable progress, our study suggests that sustained efforts are needed to reach all adolescents, particularly those who are out-of-school and living in hard-to-reach areas.

In this study, older adolescent girls aged 17–18 years were more likely to remain unvaccinated against HPV compared with girls aged 15–16 years. This finding may be attributable to Ethiopia’s adoption of a single-age-cohort vaccination strategy that focuses only 14-year-old girls, meaning that girls aged 17–18 years had already aged out of the eligibility window during the early years of implementation, consistent with findings from Kenya.30 Furthermore, the school-based delivery, which primarily targets younger, in-school adolescents systematically excludes older and out-of-school girls. Older adolescent girls are more likely to have left the school system—either by completing their education or transitioning to higher levels where HPV vaccination is not yet implemented.28 Moreover, schools serve as the main source of vaccine information and consent dissemination; therefore, leaving school reduces adolescents’ exposure to accurate information and opportunities for vaccination—a pattern consistently observed among out-of-school youth in SSA.31 However, a study conducted in Rwanda revealed a contrasting finding, with higher uptake among older adolescents, which could be attributed to the implementation of an MAC catch-up campaign targeting girls up to 18 years, increased autonomy in health decision-making and higher perceived risk of HPV infection in older age groups.32 These findings underscore the need for tailored strategies, such as catch-up HPV vaccination, to address the needs of older adolescents.

The current study showed that adolescent girls who cannot read or write, and those not currently enrolled in school, were more likely to remain unvaccinated than their counterparts. This can be explained by Ethiopia’s predominantly school-based HPV vaccination approach—in 2026, 90% of health facilities conducted school-based vaccination, while only 73% implemented community outreach, disproportionately disadvantaging illiterate and out-of-school girls.28 33 Our findings indicate that Ethiopia falls short of achieving the WHO’s equity principle of vaccinating girls regardless of school attendance or socioeconomic status, consistent with global evidence that reaching out-of-school girls remains the single greatest challenge.34 However, a study conducted in the USA found no association between school non-attendance and HPV vaccine uptake, which may be due to the availability of alternative delivery strategies such as community outreach programmes.35 Illiteracy may also contribute to low uptake of the HPV vaccine because it limits access to written materials and understanding of health information, makes adolescent girls dependent on families or friends for health-related decisions and increases their vulnerability to misinformation and hesitancy.36 These findings highlight the need for sustained implementation of community-based approaches to reach illiterate and out-of-school adolescents.

This study found that adolescent girls with poor knowledge of cervical cancer, HPV and the HPV vaccine were more likely to be unvaccinated. This association is straightforward and can be explained both theoretically and empirically. The Knowledge-Attitude-Practice model37 posits that knowledge is a cognitive precursor to attitude formation and behavioural adoption; adolescents with poor knowledge cannot rationally assess their risk of cervical cancer or the benefits of HPV vaccination.38 The Health Belief Model39 also explains that knowledge directly influences perceived susceptibility, severity and benefits—constructs that determine health behaviour. Adolescents with poor knowledge of the severity of HPV infection or cervical cancer cannot perceive themselves as susceptible, and those unaware of HPV vaccine efficacy cannot accurately assess its benefits.40 Poor knowledge among participants might be due to limited information dissemination strategies, inadequate health education and failure of healthcare workers’ to provide necessary information, as observed in Nigeria41 and Ghana.42 In Ethiopia, where the infertility myth is pervasive,43 poor knowledge is compounded by widespread misinformation about vaccine safety and inadequate countering of rumours circulating on social media.44 These findings highlight the need to design age-appropriate, culturally tailored health education and risk communication strategies delivered through multiple channels to build trust and address knowledge gaps regarding cervical cancer, HPV and its vaccine.

This study found that adolescent girls with negative attitudes towards the HPV vaccine were more likely to remain unvaccinated. This result highlights the critical role of attitude in determining vaccine uptake, consistent with studies from Uganda45 and South Africa.46 Our finding can be explained by the Theory of Planned Behaviour, which posits that attitudes—shaped by personal beliefs, social norms and access to information—strongly influence behavioural intentions and subsequent actions.47 Negative attitudes towards the HPV vaccine stem from specific beliefs, such as concerns about safety (particularly infertility myths), efficacy, side effects, low perceived benefit and moral objections that the HPV vaccine encourages early sexual activity, all of which can fuel vaccine hesitancy and reduce uptake.48 These attitudes can be addressed using strategies outlined in the Elaboration Likelihood Model: in societies where negative attitudes are deeply entrenched, peripheral route persuasion using trusted communicators such as religious leaders and peer educators may be more effective than central route persuasion through information provision alone.49 This finding underscores the importance of developing culturally appropriate communication strategies that involve trusted community figures to help mitigate negative attitudes.

The present study found that adolescent girls from the poorest households were more likely to be unvaccinated than their counterparts, consistent with studies conducted in Uganda,50 Denmark51 and Japan.52 This finding indicates structural inequities in HPV vaccine delivery, which can be explained by multiple interconnected pathways—financial, knowledge-related, psychosocial and supply-side—that disproportionately burden poor households. Although HPV vaccines may be free or subsidised in many settings, hidden costs such as transportation fees may act as deterrents.53 Adolescents in poorer households may have fewer educational opportunities and reduced access to information sources.54 Psychosocial pathways include lower perceived vaccine benefits among poor families, who prioritise immediate survival needs over long-term prevention.55 Health facilities are also concentrated in wealthier areas, leaving poor communities underserved and service quality is often lower in poor areas.56 These findings suggest the need to design tailored, multi-pronged strategies to address inequities, including home-to-home vaccination to minimise transportation barriers, pro-poor outreach with flexible scheduling and oral communication via trusted community figures.

The finding that adolescent girls from urban areas, including the city administrations of Addis Ababa and Dire Dawa, have higher odds of being unvaccinated is unexpected, though it is supported by studies from Uganda57 and Nigeria.58 Unlike rural residents who receive HPV vaccine information from knowledgeable and trusted professionals such as health extension workers and teachers, urban populations are exposed to social media misinformation without adequate counter-messaging from relevant authorities. Recent national data report that 48% of urban respondents hold misconceptions about vaccine safety, which substantiates our claim.59 Moreover, urban populations are heterogeneous, with some adolescents living in informal settlements or slums with poor healthcare infrastructure, inconsistent service delivery and high population mobility.60 The lower uptake in urban areas may also be related to programmatic focus, as Health Extension Programmes are more intensive and structured in rural than in urban settings in Ethiopia.61 In addition, because more than three-quarters of the sample in this study were from rural areas (75.9%), this may have resulted in more precise rural estimates, while the smaller urban subsample (24.1%) may over-represent underserved urban adolescents, inflating the observed non-uptake in urban areas. However, our finding contrasts with another study conducted in Ethiopia that reported higher vaccine uptake in urban areas.62 These results highlight the need to develop strategies to combat digital misinformation and to design tailored approaches for urban slums.

This study showed that adolescent girls living in predominantly pastoralist regions of Harari, Afar and Somali were more likely to remain unvaccinated against HPV than girls living in Benishangul Gumuz. This finding revealed significant structural and geographical inequities in HPV vaccination. The low uptake in these areas can be attributed to factors such as poor access to healthcare, a nomadic lifestyle, cultural beliefs unfavourable to vaccination and limited health education coverage. Geographical barriers—remote health facilities compounded by constant mobility in search of water and pasture—make static health services difficult to access.63 Given Ethiopia’s school-based HPV vaccination strategy, limited school enrolment in these regions also exacerbates the problem.64 Furthermore, health system constraints, including an inadequate number of healthcare workers to cover remote areas, poor transport infrastructure and frequent cold chain failures, undermine vaccine uptake.65 These findings highlight the need to address pastoralist inequities by integrating HPV vaccination with existing mobile health service platforms, engaging clan and religious leaders through culturally appropriate strategies and investing in solar-powered cold chain and motorcycle transport.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The use of nationally representative weighted data enhanced the generalisability of the findings and ensured reliable estimates. The large sample size provided sufficient power to detect significant associations between predictors and HPV vaccine non-uptake, and increased the policy relevance of the findings. The data collection tools and protocols were standardised at the national level, which improved data quality. However, some limitations were also acknowledged. The exclusion of the Tigray region due to conflict may have resulted in either an overestimation or underestimation of national HPV vaccine non-uptake. As this was a secondary analysis, some potentially important factors, such as childhood vaccination history, sexual history and maternal cervical cancer screening history, among others, were unmeasured.

Conclusion

The high proportion of HPV vaccine non-uptake in Ethiopia was influenced by individual, household and community-level determinants, including older adolescent age (17–18 years), illiteracy, not currently attending school, poor knowledge, unfavourable attitudes, poor household wealth status, urban residence and living in Addis Ababa, Dire Dawa, Harari, Afar and Somali regions. These factors hinder Ethiopia’s progress towards achieving the WHO’s target of 90% HPV vaccination coverage by 2030, which is a key strategy for eliminating cervical cancer as a public health concern. Our findings indicate that interventions should prioritise catch-up vaccinations for older adolescents, develop community-based delivery strategies to reach illiterate and out-of-school adolescents and improve knowledge and attitudes through school and community-based education involving trusted community figures. Addressing socio-economic inequities by implementing tailored outreach vaccination strategies in underserved regions, using media to counter misinformation—especially in urban areas—and using existing systems such as the health extension programme to reach hard-to-reach areas are also recommended. Future research should employ qualitative methods to explore the unexpected association between urban residence and HPV vaccine non-uptake identified in this study.
