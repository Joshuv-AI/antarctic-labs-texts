Developmental programming in chickens including transgenerational nutrition, chemosensory mechanisms and in ovo essential oils, organic acids, and pre- pro- syn-biotics; role of epigenetic modifications
Authors: Eugeni Roura, Asad A Khaskheli, Mila MY Meijer, Marta Navarro, Shahram Niknafs, Xiaodan Zhou, Hoai TT Nguyen, Henry van den Brand, Zehava Uni, Peter Ferket
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe post-hatching period in chickens is marked by the transition in nutrient source from the endogenous liquid yolk to exogenous solid feed, and the vulnerability to environmental pathogens underscoring the importance of an early development of the digestive and immune systems. This review explores innovative strategies to address these challenges, including developmental programming through broiler breeder interventions, and in ovo applications. Developmental programming mechanisms evolved as evolutionary strategies to optimize the survival of future generations in anticipation of environmental challenges including nutritional scarcity. These may involve chemosensory pathways influenced by dietary volatile compounds (VOCs) becoming one of the main factors determining feeding behaviour and appetite immediately after hatch. Dietary VOCs are transferred from the breeder diet to the fertile egg, shaping innate food preferences in the next generation. Other plant functional compounds include essential oils (EO) which are also partially transferable to the developing embryo with functional activities relevant to gut health in chickens. Carvacrol (the main active compound in oregano EO) can be supplemented through breeder diets to influence embryonic development. A systematic study on in ovo applications of EO identified compounds enhancing embryonic growth and the development of the digestive and immune systems. Changes in the pH of egg compartments (particularly albumen and yolk) respond to the metabolism of the developing embryo. These compartments show a high buffering capacity required for a tight control of the pH. The in ovo application of organic acids has the potential of changing compartmental pH and modulate enzymatic activities. Thus, it is possible to control nutrient release by controlling pH dynamics. Several of the environmental effects on developmental biology involve epigenetic mechanisms. A review of current epigenetic modifications relevant to chicken embryonic and post-hatch development has been outlined. In summary, this review highlights trans- or inter-generational mechanisms and novel in ovo strategies that have the potential to improve post-hatch robustness in chickens.Keywords: Broiler breeders, Transgenerational nutrition, In ovo, Essential oils, Epigenetic mechanisms

Introduction

The period immediately after hatching is the most critical in the life of a chicken (Uni and Ferket, 2004). Day-old chicks need to transition through a major change in the source of nutrients from the endogenous fat-rich liquid yolk to the exogenous carbohydrate-rich solid feed. Several key functional features of the gastrointestinal tract (GIT) develop in late stages of embryonic development, highlighting the importance of the prehatching. These features include pancreatic and brush-border enzymes and the small intestinal villi, among other GIT features (Uni et al., 2003). This, in turn, results in an immature GIT with limited capacity to digest dietary proteins, carbohydrates, and fatty acids from solid foods immediately after hatch (Moran, 1985). In the first days after hatching, up to 5 % of hatchlings do not survive or show suboptimal performance with long-lasting effects on high disease susceptibility (Uni and Ferket, 2004). Consequently, the optimisation of embryogenesis and the prehatching period has the potential of improving broiler chicken resilience post-hatch (Kadam et al., 2013; Siwek et al., 2018; Reicher et al., 2020).

Several aspects may affect embryonic development and chick robustness and mortality post-hatch. First, the nutritional status of broiler breeders significantly affects the composition of the fertile egg and embryonic development. Feed-restricted programs, designed to ensure long-term breeder production, can negatively impact the nutritional status of developing embryos, resulting in low nutrient stores in ovo, such as in the yolk, liver, and muscle (Noble and Ogunyemi, 1989; Heijmans et al., 2022a,b). Second, maximizing the growth potential of the developing embryo can result in a more mature GIT and immune system, which is essential for producing robust one-day-old chicks. This may be achieved by increasing the availability of essential nutrients through altering albumen and/or yolk nutrient mobilization or through in ovo interventions such as nutrient supplementation (also referred to as in ovo feeding -IOF-) or altering the acid-base balance and compartmental pH (Peebles, 2018; Branum et al., 2022). Third, the transition to solid feed post-hatch presents a challenge, since low feed intake during this period can be highly detrimental (Uni and Ferket, 2004). In ovo chemosensory programming mechanisms may help mitigate this issue (Niknafs and Roura, 2018; Roura and Foster, 2018).

This review explores strategies to improve the robustness of chicks at hatch associated with nutrition and metabolism and the development of the digestive and immune systems. Other very important environmental aspects (i.e., in-shed for broiler breeders or in hatchery for embryo development) or physiological systems (e.g., respiratory or circulatory systems) are outside the scope of this review. The content of this review has been organised into two main sections, the first addressing the nourishing and developmental programming of the embryo through maternal diets, and the second highlighting recent advances on in ovo interventions with emphasis on plant essential oils (EO) and extracts, pre-, pro- and syn-biotics, and acidifier applications (Fig. 1). The understanding of epigenetic mechanisms overarching many of the developmental programming mechanisms warrants further investigation. Thus, a third section has been added reviewing the relevance of epigenetic mechanisms involved in developmental biology events in chickens.

Transgenerational nutrition and developmental programming

Broiler breeder nutrition practices have focused mainly on optimizing pullet development of (pre-pubertal), the optimal production of fertile eggs and hatchability indices (pubertal) and maintaining for as long as possible an efficient productive life of the breeder (post-pubertal). This, in turn, has led to the development of precision feeding systems (Zuidhof, 2020). Less has been researched on balancing broiler breeder nutrition to optimize the health and growth of the broiler chicken, where many questions remain unsolved (Calini and Sirri, 2007; Chang et al., 2016). Moreover, one of the main gaps of knowledge remaining is around how changes in broiler breeder nutrition may affect embryonic development (Van Emous et al., 2015; Heijmans et al., 2022a).

Pre-hatching or developmental programming refers to physiological and/or behavioural adaptive changes in the progeny triggered by the exposure to environmental (including nutritional) stimuli endured by the parental generation and transferred to the next generation (Jammes et al., 2011). These intergenerational mechanisms represent an evolutionary strategy to optimise the survival of the progeny in the face of an anticipated environmental challenge such as high temperatures, shortages in nutrient supplies, or simply associated with a preference for safe food choices available in constantly evolving ecosystems (Hynd et al., 2016).

Nourishing the chicken embryo

The nutrition of broiler breeder hens plays a fundamental role in the development of the chicken embryo (Oviedo-Rondón et al., 2020). Nutrient transfer from maternal fluids (i.e., blood) into the egg is influenced by maternal health status and age but can also be manipulated through the composition of the diet. The impact of broiler breeder nutrition on progeny has been recently reviewed, but many aspects remain unknown (Calini and Sirri, 2007; Chang et al., 2016). A summary of the main single nutrients is provided below.

The balance of essential amino acids in broiler breeder diets is crucial for fertile egg production, hatchability, and chick quality (Chang et al., 2016). A high energy-to-protein ratio in breeder diets was associated with reduced chick size at hatch, associated with a lower egg weight (Heijmans et al., 2022a). However, low-density diets (low in amino acids and energy) have been shown to improve progeny growth and mortality, although they can also inhibit immune responses, particularly in late stages of breeder life and in low-weight eggs (Enting et al., 2007). These findings suggested that the absolute amount of protein and the ratio with energy are both important factors in optimizing embryonic development.

The type and level of fat in broiler breeder diets can potentially improve the body weight of the progeny at hatch (Kidd, 2003). In addition, a deficient level of linoleic acid in maternal diets was associated with high embryonic mortality (Leeson and Summers, 2001). The degree and nature of unsaturation of the fatty acids (particularly the omega-9/omega-6/omega-3 ratios) supplied from the yolk has the potential to affect immune development in the developing embryo and inflammatory immune responses in hatchlings (Cherian and Sim, 1993; Elkin et al., 2018). However, little attention has been paid to the ability of maternal dietary fats to influence offspring carcass yield and quality, a field that warrants further investigation.

Embryonic development has been correlated with the amount of yolk antioxidants that protect against the abundant reactive oxygen species produced, particularly in late stages of embryonic growth (Deeming and Pike, 2013). Oxidative stress may inhibit the spread of the embryonic circulatory system, potentially causing myocardial hypertrophy (He et al., 2013). Antioxidant protection can be delivered through the maternal diet, with vitamins A and E shown to be transferred to the yolk (Elkin et al., 2018). In addition, vitamin D3 also has intergenerational effects on optimizing progeny development. In minerals, the highlights relate to maternal dietary concentrations of Se, Zn, and Mn, which are associated with chick growth and health, feathering, and viability (Van den Brand et al., 2023; Ferket, 2024).

The review of the literature highlights the lack of knowledge on the impact of the nutrition of the broiler breeder on embryonic development. In addition, common indicators of broiler breeder performance do not seem to be fully aligned with indicators of the optimal nourishment of the chicken embryo. Thus, there are relevant gaps of knowledge on how to improve the nutrition of broiler breeders to optimize embryonic development. One of the mechanisms that may facilitate this connection might be related to transgenerational epigenetic modifications (see last section of this review).

Chemosensory mechanisms and feed volatile organic compounds (VOCs)

Complementary to nutrient transfer mechanisms, breeders have the capacity to communicate with the future developing embryo through a chemical language that depicts environmental fingerprints. These mechanisms are referred as chemosensory signals. The immediate post-hatching phase in chickens involves a critical transition from endogenous yolk absorption as the main nutrient source, which is partly absorbed directly into the small intestine, to the reliance on exogenous solid feed swallowed through the oral cavity (Van der Wagt et al., 2020). A successful early feeding strategy post-hatch depends on a rapid adaptation to consuming enough solid feed and a sufficiently mature digestive system. Early post-hatch feeding was reported to stimulate residual yolk utilisation and intestinal maturation, resulting in improved growth performance (Noy and Uni, 2010). The sensory properties of feeds play a critical role in modulating early feed intake in chickens (Roura et al., 2013; Niknafs and Roura, 2018; Roura and Foster, 2018). Broiler chickens possess an acute sense of taste and smell, allowing them to make dietary choices based on nutritional and organoleptic principles immediately after hatching. Thus, post-hatch feeding behaviours are shaped by pre-hatch chemosensory signals partially originating from maternal diets, a phenomenon referred to as chemosensory conditioning (Bertin et al., 2010).

Plants produce a wide array of volatile organic compounds (VOCs), often associated with developmental stages including flowering or ripening (Goff and Klee, 2006). Collectively, the group of VOCs released by the same plant at any stage generates a flavor fingerprint that may stimulate ingestion of nutritious foods, while trigger avoidance of potentially toxic or simply poor food choices (Pichersky et al., 2006). Many of these VOCs are linked to cell components providing nutrition or other health benefits (e.g., antioxidant, antimicrobial, etc) to the animal, thus, perceived as positive incentives of consumption. A few examples include hexanal and cis-3-hexenal (derived from essential fatty acids linoleic acid and linolenic acids, respectively), 2- and 3-methylbutanal, 3-methylbutanol and 2-phenylethanol (derived from essential amino acids leucine, isoleucine and phenylalanine, respectively). For example, oxidoreductases mediate the reduction of medium-chain fatty acid aldehydes to alcohols and the oxidation of carveol (a monoterpene) to release the main component of caraway spice extract carvone (Bate et al., 1998; Bouwmeester et al., 1998).

In chickens, Xiang et al. (2019) identified eighteen VOCs in shells of fertile eggs, including carotenoid-derived 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one. Carotenoids are only produced by plants, thus, the authors concluded that the hen diet was the original source. Unpublished work from our group indicates that volatiles from breeder diets are transferred to the forming fertile egg, potentially shaping the post-hatch feeding behaviour. Using gas chromatography, around forty-five VOCs were identified in a broiler breeder diet based on wheat, barley and soybean. Half of these volatile compounds were traced in hen blood samples and transferred to the albumen and/or the yolk of the fertile egg at lay (D0). At hatch (D21) a significant abundance of these compounds indicated that the developing chicken embryo had been exposed to these VOCs during the entire development. This transfer of VOCs from the maternal diet into the fertile egg has the potential to influence the in ovo olfactory experience of the developing embryo and shape post-hatch feeding behaviour in chickens (Bertin et al., 2010).

The chemosensory conditioning strategy relates to a continuity in the sensory profiles between maternal and post-hatching diets. While the formulation principles of meeting different nutrient requirements and feeding strategies need to be sustained, the use of matching ingredients (as similar as possible) may optimise early acceptance and feed intake immediately after hatching. This sensory matching has also been referred as “iso-sensorial” feed formulation. One of the alternative strategies of implementing chemosensory conditioning is the injection of selected VOCs directly into the egg (in ovo). The in ovo practice has been developed with the potential to supplement required nutrients (also referred as in ovo feeding -IOF-) and/or other functional compounds and is reviewed in the next section.

In ovo applications: nutritional and functional supplements

In ovo technologies were initially developed to apply Marek’s disease vaccination (Sharma and Burmester, 1984; Gildersleeve et al., 1993). More recently, these technologies have been adapted to deliver essential nutrients and other functional compounds (including probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics and essential oils among others) with the aim of improving embryonic development and deliver more resilient day-old chicks (Fig. 2). In ovo application of nutrients (in ovo feeding -IOF-)

It is acknowledged that other reviews with higher emphasis on this topic have been recently published (Peebles, 2018; Ferket, 2024). In brief, the supplementation of nutrients to boost embryonic development has been commonly referred to as IOF. In ovo feeding was initially developed as a strategy to supply additional amino acids to the developing embryo (Al-Murrani et al., 1982; Ohta et al., 1999). Further development of the technique led to a wider array of nutrients supplemented in ovo, hence, the concept of IOF (Uni and Ferket, 2004). In ovo feeding is typically administered in the amniotic fluid during the late stages of embryonic development, primarily at a time compatible with co-administration with the Marek’s disease vaccine after embryonic day 17 (from here onwards the embryonic day is referred with an “E” in front of the day: e.g., E17). Several comprehensive reviews on this topic have been published in recent years (Peebles, 2018; Kpodo and Proszkowiec-Weglarz, 2023). This section highlights a list of nutrients and their impacts on embryonic development when administered through IOF and are summarised in Table 2.

In ovo feeding has been associated with significant improvements in body weight and gastrointestinal development at hatch. Several amino acids (i.e., Arg, Gln, Leu, Lys, Met, and Thr) and hydroxy-methyl-butyrate improved GIT development as indicated by increased weight of digestive organs, villi height and crypt depths, brush border enzyme activity (e.g., jejunal sucrase-isomaltase activity), intestinal barrier and nutrient absorption (Smirnov et. al., 2006; Reicher et. al., 2022). In addition, IOF has been shown to enhance the expression of nutrient transporters, SGLT-1, PEPT-1, and NaK-ATPase, thereby potentially improving nutrient absorption (Ferket, 2024). Other benefits of IOF include increased breast muscle size at hatch after injection of dextrin and β-hydroxy-β-methylbutyrate–calcium salt (Kornasio et al., 2011), improved bone development by increasing bone ash with organic minerals and vitamin D3 (Yair et al., 2013, 2015), and an enhanced the humoral immune response after injection of Thr (Kadam, 2008).

Some nutrients applied using IOF have been shown to modulate the development of the immune system at hatch, these including amino acids (i.e., Arg, Gln, Gly, Ile, Leu, and Lys), sugars (maltose, sucrose and dextrin), linoleic acid, vitamins (B9, C, D and E) and selenium (see Table 2). These have been shown to enhance immunoglobulin secretion (IgA and IgM) and both humoral and cell-mediated immune responses in the post-hatching period. In ovo feeding of some amino acids, sugars and selenium also appeared to influence cytokine release, most notably the pro-inflammatory IL-6, which could enhance the chick’s ability to withstand environmental challenges shortly after hatching. Furthermore, folic acid administration to the yolk sac of broiler chicken embryos on the 11th day of incubation has been shown to induce histone methylation at IL2 and IL4 promoters, thereby potentially influencing post-hatch immune functions in growing chickens through the epigenetic regulation of immune genes (Li et al., 2016).

Glycogen is a crucial energy source for chicks, particularly during the hatching process when stores tend to be depleted by the end of pipping (Uni et al., 2005; De Oliveira et al., 2008; Ferket, 2024). In ovo feeding has the potential to alleviate glycogen shortages by stimulating reserves in the liver and muscles. This was demonstrated through IOF of Arg and a combination of maltose, sucrose, dextrin, and vitamin B9. Additionally, this combination of sugars and vitamin B9 enhanced insulin-like growth factor (IGF)-II, a critical foetal growth factor (see Table 2 for additional references).

A critical aspect associated with embryonic development is oxidation which strongly relates to some vitamins (mainly C and E) and minerals (mainly Selenium). The chicken embryo is very sensitive to oxidative stress due to the release of ROS during β-oxidation of PUFA, the main energy source during embryogenesis (Surai, 2002). The level of yolk antioxidants has been associated with the rate of embryonic development (Deeming and Pike, 2013). In ovo administration of Vitamin E was not effective, while Vitamin C and Arg seemed to improve antioxidant status as measured through malondialdehyde (MDA) superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione (GSH) (El-Senousey et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2018). Moreover, IOF of vitamin E in turkey and broiler embryos enhanced humoral and cellular immune system components at the expense of reducing hatchability at high doses (above 10 IU) in turkeys (Gore and Qureshi, 1997). In addition, vitamin D3 (25-hydroxycholecalciferol), improved bone development and folic acid changed plasma metabolomic profiles including P, glucose, and cholesterol together with increased body weight at hatch (Bello et al., 2014; Nouri et al., 2017). The main highlight on trace minerals supplementation has been around selenium. The IOF of sodium selenite injection has been associated with positive results around increasing the resilience against challenges with Clostridium perfringens and coccidiosis (Eimeria maxima), improving immune response (increasing IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8 and antibody titres) and antioxidant status (Lee et al., 2014). Other mineral IOF around organic forms of Zn, Mn, and copper have identified potential limitations with adverse effects on hatchability and growth, but also potential opportunities on bone mineralisation improvement (reviewed by Peebles, 2018).

A summary of main IOF effects has been collated into Table 2. Other recent reviews with more focus in nutritional aspects of IOF have been published recently (i.e., Peebles, 2018; Ferket, 2024). Overall, the technology offers great potential to boost embryonic development in birds. However, to this date, there has been no ample adoption of IOF commercial practices (excluding the Marek’s disease vaccine, which is not included in the IOF concept). This might be due to insufficient consistency of results or a relatively low return on investment. Adding additional value to IOF with alternative in ovo applications may facilitate industry adoption. Thus, one of the aims of this review was to give an update on non-nutrient in ovo interventions with emphasis on plant EO and extracts and other bioactive compounds as displayed in the following sub-sections.

In ovo applications of plant essential oils (EO) and extracts

The global movement to reduce antibiotic use in livestock production contributed to raising the use of EO and plant extracts as feed additives. Essential oils consist of mixtures of lipophilic plant metabolites, which are valued for their antimicrobial, antioxidant, appetite-stimulating, and digestion-enhancing properties (Brenes and Roura, 2010; Khaskheli et al., 2025). The interest in EO applications have also reached in ovo as one of the emerging practices (Table 3). For instance, the injection of clove, thyme, and cinnamon EO into the air cell at E10 and as feed supplements at day 35 post-hatch revealed significant improvements in performance, liver function, and antioxidant activity (El-Kholy et al., 2021). Other EO, such as rosemary, black seed oils, and thyme have also shown positive impacts such as on immune parameters when injected in the form of nanoparticles (Saki and Salary, 2015). The flow dynamics of EO after in ovo injection (at E17.5) into the amniotic fluid, were studied using carvacrol (the main component of oregano essential oil) as a model. The results indicated a rapid migration of carvacrol towards the yolk, suggesting that in ovo EO applications into the amniotic fluid can deliver active lipophilic compounds into the gastrointestinal tract, potentially influencing gut development during the peri-hatching phase (Meijer et al., 2024a).

The in ovo immunomodulatory effects of EO in broilers have also been assessed by several groups. For example, rosemary, black seed oil and thyme modulated immune parameters, including increased bursa weights and elevated serum levels of IgG, IgM, and IgY (Saki and Salary, 2015; El-Kholy et al., 2021). Meijer et al. (2024b, 2025a) explored the effects of in ovo delivery of carvacrol on immune development and immune activation in the jejunum, bursa of Fabricius and yolk sac from embryonic E19.5 to d14 post-hatch. Overall, the research offered a strategy to enhance early post-hatch disease resilience in broiler production. However, there is published evidence indicating a narrow margin between the beneficial and toxic effects of in ovo EO applications. For instance, Niknafs et al. (2024a) reported embryotoxicity and reduced hatchability of oregano EO when applied above 10µL/egg. It has been suggested that the combinations of EO with other functional additives, such as organic acids may have a positive value worth further investigation (Toosi et al., 2016; Wishna-Kadawarage et al., 2024).

Khaskheli et al. (2025) studied the in ovo application of 27 EO selected based on antimicrobial, antioxidant, appetite-enhancing, or digestive-enhancing properties (data not shown in Table 3). The results highlighted that lemon myrtle significantly increased body weight at hatch. In addition, ylang ylang, clary sage, bergamot, lemon myrtle, and black pepper upregulated the expressions of biomarkers associated with gut integrity and barrier functions, nutrients transporters, and digestive enzymes. Gut immunity biomarkers were upregulated with the injections of ylang ylang, turmeric acid, star anise, clary sage, and black pepper when compared to the saline group. In addition, spearmint, patchouli, and turmeric EO significantly reduced the expression of amino acid transporter EAAT3 compared to saline, potentially affecting the efficient transport of glutamate, a key metabolic fuel in enterocytes. On the downside of these EO applications, oregano EO decreased body weight at hatch compared to the saline injected control. The results for clove, eugenol, lemongrass, tea tree, and thyme showed also negative impacts specifically on hatchability. A potential limitation of the study was that all 27 EO were injected at the same dose which may have resulted in that the most bioactive compounds were overdosed while the least active being underdosed (Khaskheli et al., 2025).

While less commonly researched, plant extracts have also been tested in ovo as shown in Table 3. The extracts of Citrus aurantiifolia seed and grape pomace resulted in improved hatchability and performance parameters post-hatch when injected on E17.5 (Kpossou et al., 2024; Madkour et al., 2024). The in ovo application of garlic extract at the start of the incubation phase (E0) improved hatchability and reduced mortality (Mahjar and Al-Salhie, 2022). In contrast, the in ovo application of milk thistle extract resulted in a negative impact on feed intake and weight gain up to 42 days post-hatch (Morovat et al., 2016).

Overall, the in ovo delivery of EO and plant extracts represents a viable strategy to improve bird health and resilience. The potential benefits as well as doses, cost/benefit outcomes and safety (or toxicity) are highly specific to each plant compound and should not be extrapolated from one another. This, in turn, demands a high investment in additional research before potential industry adoption.

In ovo applications of pro-, pre, and syn-biotics

Several additional non-nutrient functional supplements have been tested in ovo. While the list is longer than what is presented in this review, the objective of this section was to highlight the main functional compounds that show promising results (or the most significant impact) for future commercial adoption. Other examples of in ovo applications, including post-biotics, have been left out of this review because there were very few publications available, if any. Thus, it is anticipated that some of these additional applications will arise in the field soon, given the renewed high interest in the in ovo technology.

The in ovo seeding of beneficial bacterial cultures (probiotics) is based on the principle that the seeded bacteria are ingested by the embryo in late stages (i.e., E19 or later) after the chick breaks the inner shell membrane at the start of the hatching process (Slawinska et al., 2016). In ovo probiotics are used aiming at two main objectives: (1) decrease Salmonella contamination (in eggs and meat); (2) improve the development of a healthy microbiota in the young chick during very early stages. On the first objective, the in ovo application of mixed bacterial cultures successfully reduced the prevalence and impacts of Salmonella typhimurium and enteritidis (Cox et al., 1992; De Oliveira et al., 2014). In contrast, the in ovo inoculum of Lactobacillus spp. (including L. acidophilus, L. fermentum and salivarius) in the air cell failed to prevent Salmonella enteritidis colonization (Yamawaki et al., 2013). On the second objective for probiotics, in ovo injection of Bacillus subtilis, Enterococcus faecium, and Pediococcus acidilactici increased the population of lactic acid bacteria and decreased the population of Escherichia coli in the intestine early post-hatching (Majidi-Mosleh et al., 2017). Similarly, the in ovo injection of Lactobacillus plantarum also shifted the microbiota to benefit the Lactobacillus population in the chick post hatch (Guo et al., 2023). In addition, poultry-specific lactobacilli provided signals such as B-cell activating factor and receptor, and IFN-γ and IL-12, promoting bursal development hence B and T helper cell activation (Sharma et al., 2024). Bifidobacterium longum and Bifidobacterium bifidum injected into the air cell of the fertile egg improved body weight gain, feed conversion ratio (FCR), villi height and villi height/crypt depth ratio in broiler chickens at hatch (El-Moneim et al., 2020). However, the study also revealed that the in ovo injection of bifidobacteria strains may significantly reduce hatchability. Indeed, in ovo application of probiotics has also been associated with negative effects in other studies, which flags the need for caution in strain selection and dose optimisation. Bacillus subtilis significantly reduced hatchability and increased ileal Mucin 2 (MUC2) gene expression one week post hatch (Majidi-Mosleh et al., 2017; Triplett et al., 2018). Moreover, in ovo injection of Lactobacillus animalis or rhamnosus resulted in negative impacts on embryonic development. The first lowered number of pipped eggs and caused negative alterations on gut health (i.e., gut integrity) and growth performance, while the second was associated with exacerbation of coccidial infection and bacterial imbalance compared to the control groups (Beck et al., 2019; Huang et al., 2024). On a slightly different approached, isolates from adult hens for in ovo administration to protect against E. coli horizontal infection have successfully tested showing improved body weight gain and reduced mortality at d7 post-hatch (Arreguin-Nava et al., 2020). Moreover, Pedroso et al. (2016) showed that the in ovo inoculation of adult microbiota allowed the establishment of transient colonies which decreased the abundance of undesirable bacterial species.

Several of the probiotics used in ovo belong to the lactic acid bacteria genera (e.g., Enteroccoccus, Lactobacillus and Pediococcus). Lactic acid bacteria (and potentially other probiotics) result in a high production of short chain fatty acids (SCFA) acetic, propionic and butyric (Zhang et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2023). The SCFA are considered key factors signalling to the immune system with anti-inflammatory effect. In addition, the SCFAs, especially butyrate, are the primary fuel source for intestinal epithelial cells helping maintain the structure and function mainly in colon and ceca (Van Immerseel et al., 2006). In addition, SCFA can decrease pH and may affect bacterial growth of common enteric pathogens (Sun and O’Riordan, 2013). The potential implications of SCFA on pH dynamics in egg compartments are discussed in the next section of this review.

Alternative to the in ovo inoculum, probiotics have also been sprayed on fertile eggs during incubation (Gao et al., 2024). Spray application of probiotics in ovo have been explored with Lactobacillus and Enterococcus spp. with improvements in gut microbiota composition and several parameters, including hatchability, chick quality and post-hatch growth impacting breast and leg weights (Muyyarikkandy et al., 2023; Villumsen et al., 2023).

Prebiotics are feed ingredients not digested by the endogenous enzymatic system in chickens, resulting in fermentation patterns in the hindgut that selectively favour beneficial bacteria which, in turn, produce short-chain fatty acids that improve gut development and immune responses. Most common prebiotics used in poultry nutrition are based on inulin, fructo-, galacto-, mannan-, or xylo-oligosaccharides (Khomayezi and Adewole, 2021; Castro et al., 2024). Prebiotics are suitable for in ovo applications given that they consist mainly on small water-soluble compounds, which can easily penetrate compartmental membranes accessing the developing intestinal tract and stimulating an early innate microbiota (Slawinska et al., 2016). The impact on microbiota seemed to be a common mechanism of action of in ovo prebiotics across different types of oligosaccharides. In addition, more specific impacts have been associated to galacto- and chito- oligosaccharides showing impact on gut gene expression affecting gut histomorphology and health (Slawinska et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2020). Xylo- and mannan-oligosaccharides, in tuns, improved plasma immunoglobulin levels, general immune function biomarkers and gut health (Das et al., 2024; Beldowska et al., 2024). Sobolewska et al. (2017) observed a positive impact of the in ovo inoculum of a seaweed extract (Laminaria spp) on embryonic duodenal development. Other applications included improvements in post-hatch performance, meat quality, and protection against coccidiosis (Tavaniello et al., 2018; Angwech et al., 2019).

Recently, significant attention has been granted to the combination of selected pro- and pre-biotics, also referred to as synbiotics (Gibson and Roberfroid, 1995). The use of probiotics and prebiotics together, as synbiotic products, seem to provide higher efficacy than the use of each component separately. The in ovo application of synbiotics have been gaining attention in recent years (Madej et al., 2025). The in ovo administration of probiotics together with an adequate nutrient source (i.e., prebiotics) favours the development of the probiotic microorganism while avoiding competition for nutrients required by the developing embryo (Triplett et al., 2018). In ovo administration of a combination of nondigestive carbohydrate (i.e., inulin) with Lactoccocus lactis resulted in significant improvements in performance parameters at hatch including body weight (Miśta et al., 2017). A synbiotic combination of inulin with an Enterococcus faecium solution injected in the amniotic fluid on day E17.5 combined with a post-hatching dietary synbiotic treatments improved hatchability and post-hatching feed conversion ratio, intestinal integrity and cecal beneficial bacteria populations (Calik et al., 2017). In addition, in ovo synbiotics can influence gut health measured as improved composition of the microbiota, immunological traits and intestinal development (Siwek et al., 2018; Madej et al., 2016; Stefaniak et al., 2019, 2020). In addition, the in ovo treatment with a synbiotic administration together with choline resulted in a stimulation of the spleen across two generations (Madej et al., 2025). This, in turn, suggested that in ovo supplementations may involve epigenetic mechanisms, a concept further discussed in the section below (“Epigenetic mechanisms in chickens (intra-, inter-, and trans-generational)”).

In ovo application of organic acids and pH dynamics

Embryonic development in fertile eggs incurs in a high demand for energy and other nutrients. Sugars, fats, and amino acids are oxidised to produce energy (i.e., ATP) and release CO2 and water. The elevated CO2 production is neutralized by the bicarbonate buffer system forming bicarbonate (H2CO3) in equilibrium with bicarbonate ions (HCO3-) and hydrogen ions (H+), which can lead to the acidification of body fluids (Burger, 1979). The partial pressure of CO2 (pCO2) in blood increases with the concentration of bicarbonate ions at late stages of the chicken embryo development with blood experiencing a decrease in pH (Bruggeman et al., 2007; Branum et al., 2022). In a first phase, the albumen appears to be H+ sink with an alkaline pH decreasing over time as the embryo develops. Fig. 3 highlights the changes in pH in egg compartments during embryonic development. After a rapid increase in the first two days of incubation, the albumen pH gradually declines from 9.21 on day 0 (E0) to 7.18 on day 17.5 (E17.5), by which time most albumen has been consumed by the embryo. In final stages of embryonic development (after E15) yolk seems to buffer potential H+ excess. Yolk pH initially rises during the first 15 days of embryonic development, from 6.67 at E0 to 8.046 at E15, before decreasing to 6.88 by E19.5. The remaining yolk retained in the abdomen of the hatchling is marginally acidic (Dawes, 1975).

Dynamic changes in the pH of albumen, allantois, amnion, and yolk during embryonic development in fertile eggs in broiler chickens (compiled and adapted from Romanoff and Romanoff, 1929; Dawes, 1975; Bruggeman et al., 2007; Branum et al., 2022).

The tight control of the pH of egg compartments is critical to maintain physiological functions such as gas exchange, eggshell thickness and enzyme activities (Wideman and Buss, 1985; Escribano et al., 1988). The acidification of albumen and yolk contents in the later phases of embryonic development is associated with an overall increase in nutrient availability (Escribano et al., 1988; Ikeno and Ikeno, 1991). Two primary mechanisms may contribute to the accelerated release of these nutrients. First, the catabolic activity of enzymes responsible for nutrient mobilization is pH dependent. For example, chicken aminopeptidase N and sucrase-isomaltase exhibit optimal activity at pH 6.0 (Mizuno et al., 1982; Mane et al., 2010). Aminopeptidase N is essential for breaking albumen and yolk proteins into peptides and amino acids (Yadgary et al., 2011). As embryonic development progresses, the activity of these enzymes increases, facilitating the absorption of nutrients from albumen and yolk. Secondly, acidifying the pH increases the solubility of nutrients such as proteins and minerals (e.g., calcium, phosphorus, magnesium) and the emulsification of lipids (Nangsuay et al., 2011; Deeming, 2002). This increased solubility facilitates access to catabolic enzymes, promoting the efficient absorption of nutrients during the critical phase of rapid embryonic growth, which includes bone formation and skeletal development (Duan et al., 2013).

The role of the pH in the nutrient availability for embryonic development underscores the potential relevance of the in ovo application of organic acids. There has been little research published on the matter. The two main organic acids tested in ovo were butyric or citric acids or their salts (Table 4). However, SCFA may have also been indirectly tested by the in ovo injection of probiotics (e.g., lactic acid bacteria) as briefly discussed in the previous section. The SCFA being the main metabolites of bacterial development their release in ovo could result in additional functional effects. For example, the in ovo injection of Lactobacillus plantarum at E14 increased acetic acid in the gastrointestinal tract at E20 along with acetic and butyric acid in cecum of 1-day-old (Guo et al., 2023). In addition, the in ovo injection of chito-oligosaccharides at E12.5 resulted in higher level of propionic acid in the chick gut immediately after hatch (Zhang et al., 2020). The pKa value of SCFA (4.76 for acetate, 4.82 for butyrate, and 4.87 for propionate) is higher than the pH of albumen or yolk at any time during embryo development, thus, potentially contributing to acidify the in ovo environment. Thus, the SCFA released by in ovo probiotics may contribute to decrease pH and affect bacterial growth of common enteric pathogens (Sun and O’Riordan, 2013). Hypothetically, favouring microbial fermentation inside the egg may result in acidification of egg compartments when applied in ovo, where the decrease in pH in albumen and yolk may result in preventing pathogenic bacteria from thriving at hatch while increasing nutrient mobilisation as a potential benefit for the developing embryo (Escribano et al., 1988; Ikeno and Ikeno, 1991).

Butyric acid has been associated with improvement of the intestinal epithelial function though cell proliferation, morphological structure, absorptive function and gut flora in chickens (Zhonghong and Yuming, 2006). The in ovo butyric acid (or sodium butyrate) administered at E17.5 improved embryo development, growth and villi surface area (Tako and Ferket, 2004; Salahi et al.;, 2017; Krisnan et al., 2024). When administered at E12 no effect on post-hatch performance parameters were observed. However, significant increases in intestinal epithelia crypt depth, changes in the intestinal microbiota and changes in the expression of genes related to the immune response (including some cytokines) and the intestinal barrier (including tight junction proteins and mucins) were reported (Beldowska et al., 2024).

Citric acid is a key metabolite in cellular metabolism. In the one hand is one of the key metabolites intermediate in the TCA or citric acid cycle, hence, of ATP production in any eukaryotic cell. On the other hand, citric acid levels control glycolytic regulatory enzymes releasing pyruvate and acetyl-CoA, entering the citric acid cycle. The citric acid cycle, in turn, is central in the metabolism of carbohydrates, fatty acids, and amino acids within each living aerobic cell (Cherix et al., 2020). In addition, citric acid is a triprotic acid (pKa values of 3.13, 4.76, and 6.40) offering high buffer capacity between pH 2 and pH 8 in living systems (Goldberg et al., 2002). The in ovo application of citric acid in yolk decreased the pH and resulted in a faster release of nutrients resulting in heavier chicks at hatch (results published only as a conference abstract Khaskheli et al., 2023).

Epigenetic mechanisms in chickens (intra-, inter-, and trans-generational)

The adaptation to environmental challenges (including nutrition) involves behavioural and/or metabolic changes which, in turn, may require up or down regulation of gene expression and translation. For example, the domestication of chickens led to evolutionary alterations associated to genomic variations incompatible with classical genetic selection involving gene mutations. Tameness, a key desirable domestication behaviour, was associated with divergent epigenetic patterns which evolved within only five generations (Belteky et al., 2018). . Epigenetic programming has been defined as the changes affecting cellular differentiation and function that allow an individual to adapt to environmental stressors without changing the DNA sequence (Bednarczyk, 2021; Dunislawska et al., 2022). In chickens, as in mammals, epigenetic modifications consist mainly of DNA methylation, histone modification (i.e., acetylation and/or methylation), or microRNA (Dunislawska et al., 2022). Epigenetic changes optimise the genetic potential of an undifferentiated stem cell by using the differentiation process as an adaptive response to environmental challenges. Transgenerational epigenetic mechanisms affect germ cells and may persist for one or more generations but can also be reversed when the causative environmental challenges disappear (Guerrero-Bosagna et al., 2018)2.

Stem cells are present in many organs at any developmental stage from the embryo to the adult and may include germ cells in the embryo or in the adult. Germ cell epigenetic modifications may occur during embryonic development affecting the precursors of reproductive cells (primordial germ cells) or in the post-hatching period during gonadal differentiation (Bednarczyck et al., 2021). These mechanisms affecting germ cells may persist for several generations (referred as transgenerational) but can also be reversed when the causative environmental challenges are alleviated (Guerrero-Bosagna et al., 2018; He et al., 2018). The epigenetic transgenerational inheritance in chickens occurs primarily during the migration of primordial germ cells to the gonads during embryonic development (Bednarczyk et al., 2021; Dunislawska et al., 2022). Stress in broiler breeders causes suppressed corticosterone responsiveness affecting the regulation of stress-related genes (e.g., epithelial growth receptor 1 and corticotropin releasing hormone -CRH- receptor 1) which are passed on to next generations during the gonad formation (Goerlich et al., 2012).

The nutritional environment in the mother hen including caloric density, protein, fatty acids, mineral and vitamin contents, is one of the main environmental factors affecting epigenomic mechanisms which may have a profound effect on the pre-and post-hatching chicken life (Hynd et al., 2016). The transgenerational adaptation to potential nutrient shortages is particularly relevant to this review. In the wild, dietary protein may become limiting for granivore species, such as the fowl, usually exposed to low protein, low-density diets (Klasing, 1998). Thus, adaptive mechanisms to compensate for potential shortages in the availability of high-quality protein sources are crucial. In addition, in broiler commercial production systems, there is a mismatch between maternal and offspring diets formulated for different dietary protein contents (i.e., 40 % higher in broiler starter compared to breeder hen diets) (Corzo and Silva, 2020). This mismatch between generations may result in altered metabolic phenotypes interfering with broiler growth, such as increased fat deposition (Van der Waaij et al., 2011). Thus, understanding the right match in crude protein level between maternal and progeny is an important factor affecting the performance and health status of commercial broiler chickens, which requires further investigation. Some of these mechanisms affect the efficient use of dietary protein and amino acids in chickens (Kidd, 2003). Prehatching development programming through feeding low dietary crude protein (CP) diets during the laying phase in broiler breeders has the potential to improve embryonic development, growth rate, and FCR of the broiler progeny, particularly under dietary protein constraints (Lesuisse et al., 2018). Low-density broiler breeder diets, in turn, resulted in improved offspring growth rates, reduced mortality, together with changes in immune responses, depending on breeder age and egg weight (Enting et al., 2007). However, Heijmans et al. (2022a, b) reported contradictory findings, suggesting that the underlying mechanisms are not well understood. Dietary fish oil in broiler breeders had also a positive impact on embryonic development (Kim et al., 2025). In addition, Zn supplementation in broiler breeders improved the protection of the embryos against heat stress (Zhu et al., 2017). Furthermore, embryonic heat conditioning induces thermal and immunological resilience across generations associated to changes in gene expression in the hypothalamus with DNA methylation (Rosenberg et al., 2022).

Complementary to the pluripotency of embryonic stem cells, many tissues after birth contain adult stem cells which have the potential to differentiate only into the specific cell types of the tissue they are placed in. Thus, epigenetic reprogramming affecting adult stem cells can impact tissue specific cell differentiation (e.g., muscle, nerves, epithelia, etc.) affecting only the life of the individual “within a generation” (hence intra-generational) not transmissible across generations unless the environmental stressor persists (becoming inter-generational). Thus, intra- or inter-generational epigenetics have been referred as context-dependent (Perez and Lehner, 2019). On a side note, most current literature uses the term inter-generational to include both within and across generational epigenetic changes (Bednarczyk, 2021). This author prefers the term intra-generational (as opposed to inter-generational) to refer to adult stem cell differentiation mechanisms. Several examples have been reported on intragenerational epigenetic mechanisms. For example, high temperature and CO2 levels during incubation affected cardiac muscle development with long-term implications in chicken performance and development of related metabolic disorders associated to epigenetic changes which did not pass to the next generation (Molenaar et al., 2011a,b; Corbett et al., 2020). Genetic selection for growth rates has also been associated with epigenetic mechanisms responsible for postnatal muscle development and repair (Gu et al., 2024). In addition, in ovo interventions through vaccination (e.g., Marek’s disease vaccine) shape selective gene expression through epigenetic mechanisms within the same but not across generations. Moreover, age has also been identified as a factor influencing epigenetic modifications when adapting to environmental challenges including body temperature maintenance through changes in feather follicles (Chen et al., 2024).

Importantly in the context of this poultry nutrition review is the effect of nutrient availability on non-germinal stem cell fate in the epithelia of the gastrointestinal tract. Altered metabolic states such as low crude protein diets contributed to the development of intra- or inter-generational epigenetic modifications resulting in dietary adaptations of the parental (breeders) and subsequent generations (Lesuisse et al., 2018). Chen et al. (2021) described how the in ovo injection of l-methionine (10 mg/egg) injected in ovo on E9 improved intestinal development (organ weight, villus height and crypt depths) and gut integrity (e.g., tight junctions, and electrical resistance in jejunum) but the involvement of epigenetic mechanisms in this work were not targeted (Chen et al., 2021). One of the metabolic roles of l-methionine is as methyl donor. The DNA methylation process involved in epigenetic mechanisms, requires methyl donors and cofactors from the external environment. Dietary or in ovo supplementation with methyl-donors or methylcatalysts including l-methionine, choline, betaine, among others significantly modified specific DNA methylations in fertile eggs (reviewed by Dunislawska et al., 2022). The in ovo injection of betaine (a metabolite with three methyl groups) significantly increased hepatic content and serum concentration of cholesterol associated with epigenetic mechanisms including DNA and histone methylations (Hu et al., 2015). Similarly, dietary supplementation with betaine resulted in the upregulation of lipogenesis genes in abdominal fat pads (Xing et al., 2011). Choline, another biochemical compound with methyl donor capability, resulted in inter- and trans-generational epigenetic changes affecting the cecal mucosa transcriptome when administered in ovo (day E12) in combination with a synbiotic product (Ibrahim et al., 2025a). Other maternal dietary nutrients that can be transferred to the egg and may influence epigenetic mechanisms include selenium, vitamin D, and vitamin A (Chango et al., 2015).

In brief, while largely unknown in chickens, intra- (or inter-) and trans-generational epigenetic mechanisms have the potential to explain major environmental adaptive mechanisms relevant to nutrition and health constraints endured by the breeder, the developing embryo, or the adult (post-hatch) chicken. It is anticipated that a better understanding of epigenetic mechanisms and the role in development programming will have a fundamental impact on common practices on broiler breeder and broiler nutrition in the future.

Conclusions

Fig. 4 illustrates the main findings of this literature review highlighting nutritional pathways influencing embryonic development and developmental programming in chickens including transgenerational nutrition and in ovo intervention strategies. Improving the robustness of day-old chicks is a complex challenge that requires a multifactorial approach with only 21 days of embryonic development. A deeper understanding of how parental dietary profiles (i.e., broiler breeders) impact embryonic development and programming appears to offer a feasible strategy such as in protein and amino acid nutrition. A highlight in developmental programming is around the transfer of plant VOCs to the fertile egg and how these may shape innate preferences in day-old chicks. The concept of sensorial matching (iso-sensorial) between maternal and progeny diets is emerging as a relevant part of the solution. The in ovo technique was developed initially to provide immune protection (against Marek’s disease). More recently, in ovo supplementation (in ovo feeding -IOF-) has provided value in supplying potentially limiting nutrients. However, industry adoption of IOF has been scarce in part due to lack of consistency in the outcomes. More recently, additional applications of in ovo interventions have been arising that merit attention. One of the highlights of these novel aspects involves in ovo applications of functional plant compounds, such as EO, which have shown promising outcomes in enhancing the GIT and the gut-associated immune system. In addition, strategies to improve post-hatching robustness must consider the often forgotten in ovo compartmental metabolic dynamics, particularly those related to nutrient mobilization. Controlling the acid-base balance and the pH of egg compartments with organic acids has the potential to become a cornerstone in orchestrating embryonic development in broiler chickens. Epigenetic mechanisms underpinning several of these metabolic mechanisms shaping developmental programming in chickens warrant further investigation.

Nutritional pathways influencing embryonic development and developmental programming in chickens. Chemosensory (through organic volatiles –VOCs-) and nutritional mechanisms influence the fertile egg composition including potential epigenetic modifications transferred to the next generation (inter- or trans-generational modifications). Incubation and in ovo interventions early in embryonic development have the capacity to influence primordial germ cells resulting in transgenerational modifications. In ovo interventions on or after embryonic day E12 are less likely to result in embryonic mortality and have the potential to improve the development of the gastrointestinal and immune systems. These include essential oils, organic acids, and pre- pro- syn-biotics, amongst others. Egg compartmental changes in pH influence nutrient availability and embryonic growth. Epigenetic changes in late embryonic stages or post-hatching are not likely to affect germ cells keeping the effect on local stem cells within a tissue (intra-generational epigenetic changes).
