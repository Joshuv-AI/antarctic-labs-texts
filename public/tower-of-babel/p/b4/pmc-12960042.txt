Development of motion recognition-based interactive training content to enhance motor functions in older adults
Authors: Yun-Hee Sung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The global aging population is rapidly increasing, with associated declines in motor and cognitive function leading to higher fall risk and reduced independence. Exercise interventions improve strength, balance, and cognition, but accessibility barriers remain. While technology assistance programs are promising, they often lack clinical perspectives, are difficult to adapt, and lack features that encourage participation. This study aimed to develop and evaluate a motion recognition-based interactive training program designed by a physical therapist to enhance motor function and physical fitness in older adults. Twenty-eight community-dwelling older adults (≥65 years) were randomly assigned to an experimental group (n=13) or control group (n=15). The experimental group completed three weekly sessions of motion recognition exercises with adaptive feedback for 4 weeks, while the control group performed stretching. Outcomes included task performance metrics (attempts, corrections, accuracy) automatically recorded by the program and fitness measures from the senior fitness test. The experimental group showed significant increases in attempts and corrections across all body parts, with notable accuracy improvement in head movements (P<0.05). Flexibility significantly improved between groups, while both groups improved in lower extremity strength and endurance. No significant changes were observed in upper limb strength or agility. A clinically designed, motion recognition-based interactive program enhanced motor performance and flexibility in older adults. Incorporating adaptive difficulty and real-time feedback, it shows potential as a scalable, cost-effective digital intervention for promoting active aging.

INTRODUCTION

According to recent projections on aging and health by the World Health Organization (2024), the global population aged 60 years and older is expected to rise from approximately 1 billion in 2020 to 2.1 billion by 2050. Notably, the number of individuals aged 80 and older is anticipated to triple during the same period, reaching 426 million. This dramatic demographic shift underscores the growing importance of addressing the physical decline associated with aging. Falls are a major concern among older adults, with over one-quarter of individuals aged 65 and older experiencing at least one fall annually, and nearly 10% of these falls resulting in injuries (Alfaro Hudak et al., 2023). In particular, age-related deterioration in lower limb proprioception has been shown to negatively impact postural stability, thereby increasing fall risk (Martínez-Amat et al., 2013). Additionally, the involuntary loss of muscle mass and strength—commonly known as sarcopenia—can lead to not only decreased physical capability but also impaired gait, loss of independence, and overall health deterioration (Walston, 2012). These physiological changes are often accompanied by reductions in balance and motor control, which further elevate the risk of falls. Cognitive decline, another common consequence of aging, can exacerbate these problems by diminishing quality of life and contributing to broader personal and societal challenges.

In response to these multifaceted issues, various exercise interventions incorporating strength, balance, and aerobic training have been implemented, and have been shown to reduce fall risk by approximately 15%–20% in healthy older adults (Gillespie et al., 2012). Moreover, high levels of physical activity and fitness are strongly correlated with improved cognitive performance (Brown et al., 2012) and a lower risk of developing dementia in later life (Hamer and Chida, 2009; Middleton et al., 2010). Gerards et al. (2017) emphasized the effectiveness of perturbation-based balance training as a task-specific strategy to enhance postural control, while Tarumi et al. (2022) reported that improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness were associated with increased cognitive function and cortical thickness.

Despite their proven benefits, these interventions often rely heavily on direct supervision by physical therapists or other professionals, making them less accessible due to time, spatial, and financial constraints. To address these limitations, researchers have recently begun exploring technology-assisted interventions using motion recognition systems and interactive content. For instance, a meta-analysis by Yen and Chiu (2021) demonstrated that virtual reality (VR) exergames positively affect cognitive function, memory, and depression in older adults, suggesting their potential as a viable strategy for promoting active aging and mental well-being. In a similar vein, Kim et al. (2024) developed a personalized exercise application tailored to the functional capabilities of the older adults, and Konara Mudiyanselage et al. (2025) proposed a fall detection system to enhance safety among older populations. Ortiz-Mallasén et al. (2024) further reported that VR-based exercise programs improved strength, balance, and gait, while also reducing fall risk and achieving high levels of user satisfaction and adaptability.

As we move further into the Fourth Industrial Revolution, there is an increasing demand for innovative, technology-driven solutions that can support older adults in maintaining physical function, autonomy, and overall well-being. However, many existing studies have focused primarily on the application of emerging technologies themselves, often neglecting the unique physical and psychological characteristics of older adults. As a result, these interventions frequently lack adaptive difficulty features and fail to incorporate engaging or motivating elements. Such shortcomings may arise from differing perspectives between developers and clinical practitioners regarding how the older adults should be understood and supported.

Therefore, the present study seeks to bridge this gap by involving a physical therapist—well-versed in both the physiological and psychological needs of older adults—in the direct development of an interactive training program. This program is designed to include personalized difficulty adjustments and motivational elements that can enhance user engagement and adherence. By integrating clinical expertise with interactive technology, this study aims to propose a more practical and sustainable solution to improve the health and quality of life of older adults.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Software development

A motion recognition-based interactive training program was custom-developed by the author specifically to enhance motor functions in older adults (Fig. 1). This system guides users to move specific body parts toward target positions, measures the accuracy of their movements, and provides real-time feedback. The software was developed using Python 3.9. For real-time body tracking, we utilized Google MediaPipe Pose, a widely used machine-learning-based framework. Previous studies have validated the reliability and accuracy of MediaPipe Pose against gold-standard motion capture systems (e.g., Vicon), confirming its clinical utility for analyzing gait, balance, and upper limb movements (Hii et al., 2023). Specifically, it has demonstrated high correlation coefficients (intraclass correlation coefficient>0.9) in temporal kinematic parameters, supporting its use as a cost-effective tool for interactive training in older adults (Hii et al., 2023). Additionally, for game interface design, we used PyGame; for video frame processing, OpenCV; for data handling and Excel output, Pandas and openpyxl; and for coordinate computation and time tracking, NumPy.

The program utilizes a webcam to display the user’s real-time video feed on the monitor, functioning as a digital mirror. This augmented reality environment allows users to simultaneously view their own movements and the virtual targets. Each trial within a set begins with the appearance of a target object on the screen, prompting the user to move the corresponding body segment toward the target. If the user’s limb does not reach the target, they can visually perceive the discrepancy on the screen and self-correct their motion in real-time to align with the target object. Accuracy is determined based on the MediaPipe landmark coordinates of five key body segments: the right hand, left hand, right foot, left foot, and head. A response is considered correct (successful attempt) when the coordinates of the designated body part enter the target area within a radius of 50 pixels and remain for a duration of 0.5 sec. Upon successful alignment, visual feedback is provided (e.g., the target disappears or score increases), reinforcing the correct motion. The program records the number of attempts, corrections, and accuracy (%) (corrections/total attempts). The training program consists of three sequential sets. At the end of each set, the accuracy score is used to adjust the difficulty level. Difficulty was modulated by changing the speed of target movement, size of the target object, and interval between target appearances. After each set, real-time feedback, including score, accuracy, number of attempts and corrections, is displayed visually on the screen. All set data are automatically saved in an Excel file for record-keeping and analysis.

Participants

This study involved 28 community-dwelling older adults aged 65 years or older recruited from a senior welfare center in Changwon, South Korea. The characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. Inclusion criteria required participants to be aged 65 or older and to provide informed consent after receiving a detailed explanation of the study. Individuals with diagnosed orthopedic or neurological disorders, or with cognitive impairment (Korean Mini-Mental State Examination<24) were excluded. Participants were randomly assigned to either the experimental or control group using the RAND function in Excel. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Kyungnam University (IRB No. 1040460-A-2025-029).

Senior fitness test

Physical fitness was assessed using the senior fitness test developed by Rikli and Jones (1999), including measures of flexibility, muscle strength, agility, balance, and aerobic endurance (Kim et al., 2022). (a) Upper arm strength (biceps curl test): participants performed as many biceps curls as possible within 30 sec while seated. Female participants used a 2.27-kg dumbbell, and male participants used a 3.63-kg dumbbell with their dominant hand. (b) Lower leg strength (chair stand test): the number of times participants could rise from a seated position within 30 sec was recorded. (c) Agility and dynamic balance (2.44-m up-and-go test): The time taken to stand from a chair, walk 2.44 m around a cone, and return to the chair was measured. (d) Lower leg flexibility (chair sit-and-reach test): participants sat at the edge of a chair, extended their dominant leg, and reached toward their toes with both hands. The distance between fingertips and toes was recorded, with positive values indicating fingers beyond the toes, and negative values otherwise. (e) Upper body flexibility (back scratch test): while standing, participants reached one hand over the shoulder and the other up the back. Finger overlap was recorded as positive, lack of contact as negative. (f) Cardiorespiratory endurance (2-min step test): participants marched in place for 2 min. The number of times the dominant knee rose above the midpoint between the patella and iliac crest was counted.

Intervention

Participants in the experimental group (n=13) performed three sets of exercises using the developed motion recognition-based interactive content. Before the exercise session, the researcher ensured proper system setup and adjusted the distance between the participant and the webcam. A warm-up exercise was conducted prior to the main training. After each set, a 30-sec rest period allowed participants to review real-time feedback on their performance. The control group (n=15) engaged in a traditional stretching exercise program. All groups received the intervention for 15 min per session, 3 times per week, for 4 weeks.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 21.0 (IBM Corp., USA). General characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. The Shapiro-Wilk test was performed to verify data normality, and all variables were found to be normally distributed. Independent and paired t-tests were conducted to examine between-group and within-group differences, respectively, with the significance level set at P<0.05.

RESULTS

Changes in performance on a motion recognition-based interactive training program

The pre- and posttest results for the experimental group’s task performance following a 4-week motion recognition-based interactive training program are presented in Table 2. The number of attempts increased significantly across all body segments (head, right hand, left hand, right foot, and left foot) (P<0.05). Notably, the number of attempts for the head region demonstrated the most substantial improvement, with an approximately 3.8-fold increase. Similarly, the number of corrections also showed a significant increase in all body segments (P<0.05). The overall mean number of corrections increased significantly, supporting the program’s effectiveness in enhancing task performance success. In contrast, for accuracy, a statistically significant improvement was observed solely in the head region (P<0.05). Despite the increase in attempts and corrections, no significant changes in accuracy were found for the upper and lower limb segments.

Changes in fitness after the motion recognition-based interactive training program

To evaluate the effects of a motion recognition-based interactive training program on physical function in older adults, changes in physical fitness were analyzed in both the experimental and control groups. The results are summarized in Table 3. In the chair stand and 2-min step tests, both groups showed statistically significant within-group improvements (P<0.05); however, no significant between-group differences were observed. For the biceps curl and 2.44-m up-and-go tests, neither group showed significant changes, either within or between groups. In the back scratch test, while a significant difference between the groups was evident at postintervention, the change in scores from pre- to postintervention did not differ significantly between the groups. In the chair sit-and-reach test, a significant group-by-time interaction was observed (P<0.05), whereas the control group showed no significant change. These findings suggest that the motion recognition-based interactive training program has a beneficial effect on improving lower-body flexibility in older adults.

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to evaluate the effects of a motion recognition-based interactive training program on motor performance in older adults. The results showed that the program had a positive impact on task performance and certain physical fitness components, particularly flexibility. The experimental group that participated in this study’s intervention program showed a significant increase in the number of attempts and corrections across all body parts. This finding suggests that the gamified interface and real-time feedback effectively facilitated active participation and immersion among older adults. Such repetitive engagement likely served as a key mechanism for promoting neuromuscular adaptation through iterative learning (Seidler et al., 2010).

Notably, the head region demonstrated the greatest improvement in both the number of attempts and accuracy. This task involved a complex motor sequence that required perceiving the correct target among multiple moving balls and executing a precisely timed jump to achieve contact with the ball. The vertical jump task is particularly important for evaluating and mitigating age-related declines in muscle function. Aging is associated with reduced motor unit recruitment and firing rates, leading to diminished muscle strength, especially the maximal power needed for vertical jumping (Agostini et al., 2023; Argaud et al., 2017; Gervasi et al., 2017). Consequently, the vertical jump test is widely recognized as a valuable tool for screening sarcopenia and offers a practical, intuitive method for assessing lower limb strength (Rittweger et al., 2004; Singh et al., 2014). Within this context, the program’s jump task appears to effectively stimulate lower limb muscle function in older adults, serving as a meaningful intervention for enhancing motor capacity critical to preserving functional independence, as highlighted by Alvero-Cruz et al. (2021). However, with respect to accuracy, no statistically significant improvements were observed in body regions other than the head. This indicates that while the program effectively encouraged physical engagement, it may have been limited in enhancing precise whole-body motor control. To improve its efficacy, future iterations should focus on refining task difficulty calibration and enhancing the precision and granularity of the feedback system to promote improved coordination and precision across all body parts.

The effects of motion recognition-based interactive training on older adults’ fitness revealed significant within-group improvements in lower extremity strength and cardiopulmonary endurance. Additionally, upper extremity flexibility showed significant between-group differences, while lower extremity flexibility demonstrated significant improvements both within and between groups. These results imply that the motion recognition-based interactive training has a targeted and pronounced effect on enhancing flexibility in older adults. This selective improvement in flexibility can likely be attributed to the program’s design, which required participants to repeatedly reach toward spatial targets, encouraging movements that extend to their maximal range of motion. Such task-oriented reaching patterns inherently function as dynamic stretching exercises. Dynamic stretching is effective in improving flexibility, primarily due to its capacity to reduce muscle–tendon unit stiffness through active, controlled joint movements (Fletcher, 2010; Opplert and Babault, 2018).

This mechanism helps explain multiple findings from the current study. First, it supports the observed improvement in flexibility within the experimental group, aligning with prior evidence that dynamic stretching enhances joint range of motion (Amiri-Khorasani et al., 2011; Opplert and Babault, 2018). Second, it also accounts for the significant postintervention improvement in jump performance. Prior research has consistently demonstrated that dynamic stretching enhances muscular power and jump height, which corroborates our findings (Hough et al., 2009; Ryan et al., 2014). Therefore, it is plausible that the integration of dynamic movement patterns within the training program elicited favorable neuromuscular and biomechanical adaptations, contributing to simultaneous improvements in both flexibility and functional power. In contrast, no significant within-group or between-group differences were observed in upper extremity strength and agility. This may be attributed to the total exercise duration of 45 min per week over 4 weeks being insufficient to elicit meaningful changes in physical indicators such as muscular strength or agility. Additionally, since the program was designed with an emphasis on movement accuracy and coordination rather than high-intensity resistance training, it may have been limited in inducing physiological adaptations related to muscle hypertrophy or maximal strength (Fragala et al., 2019).

This study has a few limitations. First, the small sample size and recruitment from a single institution limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the absence of intensity control in the control group’s stretching exercises may have compromised the validity of group comparisons. Nevertheless, the findings support the potential of technology-based interventions to enhance motor function in older adults. By incorporating clinical expertise into the program’s design, this study offers a practical and scalable intervention model. Future studies should address these limitations by evaluating long-term outcomes, adherence rates, and the functional transfer of acquired skills. Furthermore, integrating cognitive and social elements could amplify the overall benefits of such programs.
