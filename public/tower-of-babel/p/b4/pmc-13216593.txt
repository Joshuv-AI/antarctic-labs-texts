TWEAK-induced alternative NF-κB signaling regulates Cxcl10 and Mymx to promote myoblast-to-myotube fusion and muscle regeneration
Authors: Neena Lala-Tabbert, Allan Humphrey, Diana Ratsun, Nathalie Earl, Martine St-Jean, Eric C. LaCasse, Robert G. Korneluk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myoblast fusion is essential for skeletal muscle growth and repair, yet the upstream signals regulating this process remain incompletely understood. Here, we show that the cytokine TWEAK promotes myotube formation through activation of alternative NF-κB signaling. Transcriptomic profiling of TWEAK-treated myotubes reveals upregulation of Myomixer (Mymx) and the chemokine CXCL10, both critical for fusion. Mechanistically, the NF-κB member RelB directly binds the Mymx and Cxcl10 gene promoters, enhancing their transcription. Genetic or pharmacological disruption of this pathway impairs myoblast fusion in both C2C12 and primary mouse myoblasts, while Mymx overexpression rescues fusion in TWEAK-deficient myoblasts. In mouse models, TWEAK treatment enhances myotube formation during muscle regeneration, whereas loss of TWEAK reduces fusion efficiency. These findings identify TWEAK-alternative NF-κB signaling as a key regulator of muscle cell fusion through direct transcriptional control of Mymx and Cxcl10 and defines targetable pathways to enhance repair in muscle disease.

Introduction

The maintenance and repair of adult skeletal muscle rely on molecular and cellular mechanisms that sustain tissue homeostasis during regeneration of injured myofibers. This process involves the activation, proliferation, differentiation, and fusion of resident muscle stem cells called satellite cells1,2. Myoblast fusion is a critical step during muscle regeneration that requires migration of fusion-competent cells, alignment of cell membranes, rearrangement of actin cytoskeleton at cell-cell contact sites, and membrane fusion3–5. Membrane fusion occurs in two stages. First, myoblast-myoblast fusion (referred to as “primary fusion”) occurs to produce nascent myotubes. Then, myoblasts fuse to nascent myotubes (referred to as “secondary fusion”) to produce larger myotubes with a greater number of myonuclei3,6,7.

Myoblast differentiation is a tightly regulated process involving several signaling pathways including nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB). Depending on the type of stimulus, activation of NF-κB signaling can occur through the classical (canonical) or alternative (non-canonical) pathway. In skeletal muscle, the classical pathway promotes myoblast proliferation and inhibits differentiation8–13. In contrast, alternative NF-κB signaling becomes active upon myoblast differentiation and is required for myoblast fusion and promoting an oxidative metabolic phenotype in muscle14–16. Under pathological conditions, constitutive activation of classical NF-κB signaling has been reported in various muscle pathologies including atrophy12,17,18, cachexia17,19, rhabdomyosarcoma20 and muscular dystrophies21–23. Thus, the classical and alternative NF-κB signaling pathways are critical and complex regulators of skeletal muscle biology and disease.

TNF-like weak inducer of apoptosis (TWEAK) is a membrane bound and soluble cytokine that signals through fibroblast growth factor-inducible 14 (Fn14), a highly inducible cell-surface receptor that is upstream of numerous signaling pathways including NF-κB10,14. The role of TWEAK and Fn14 in skeletal muscle regeneration and atrophy is complex and has led to some contradictory results in previous publications. Upregulation of TWEAK-Fn14 signaling is seen in several chronic atrophic muscle disorders such as Myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1)24,25, cancer cachexia26 and denervation-induced atrophy27. Furthermore, muscle-specific transgenic overexpression of TWEAK activates the ubiquitin proteasome system leading to increased protein degradation and decrease in muscle cross-sectional area suggesting that TWEAK/Fn14 signaling is detrimental to skeletal muscle10,28. In contrast, TWEAK/Fn14 signaling has been suggested to play a beneficial role during muscle regeneration by promoting myoblast proliferation and fusion14,29–32. However, these roles seem to be dependent on TWEAK levels. For example, we have previously demonstrated that low doses of TWEAK promote formation of large myotubes, whereas high TWEAK levels apparently inhibit myoblast fusion14. Overall, these studies demonstrate that the contradictory roles of TWEAK/Fn14 signaling in skeletal muscle health and disease need to be further elaborated.

In the present study, we decipher the mechanism, timing and role for TWEAK in promoting myoblast fusion during skeletal muscle regeneration via activation of the alternative NF-κB pathway and its key downstream targets. We show that TWEAK is transiently expressed and secreted by differentiating myoblasts, where it acts to enhance myoblast-to-myotube fusion. In vivo, TWEAK administration during regeneration increases myofiber size, while blockade of TWEAK impairs myoblast fusion. Transcriptomic profiling of differentiating myoblasts revealed that TWEAK induces the expression of Mymx, a key muscle fusogen, and Cxcl10, a chemokine implicated in cell migration. Both genes are direct transcriptional targets of RelB, highlighting a TWEAK-RelB axis that coordinates fusion-related gene expression. Together, these findings identify TWEAK-induced alternative NF-κB signaling as a critical regulator of myoblast fusion and muscle repair.

Results

Recombinant TWEAK enhances secondary myoblast fusion in vitro

Because of the conflicting data on the effect of TWEAK in skeletal muscle biology, we examined the effect of short-term (24 h) recombinant murine TWEAK treatment at different time points during C2C12 myoblast differentiation in culture. We examined the effect of low (10 ng/ml) and high (100 ng/ml) concentrations of TWEAK, which have been shown to stimulate fusion and induce atrophy, respectively14. TWEAK had different effects on differentiation and fusion depending on when cultures were treated (Fig. 1A–C). TWEAK at both concentrations inhibited myoblast differentiation when cells were treated early during differentiation, a period characterized by myoblast differentiation and nascent myotube formation (Fig. 1A). These findings corroborate those from previous studies29–31. We also found that both concentrations of recombinant TWEAK increased myoblast fusion and the number of nuclei per myotube when cultures were treated late in differentiation, a stage when myocytes fuse to nascent myotubes to form mature multinucleated myotube (Fig. 1B, C). We further examined the effect of TWEAK on myoblast fusion by differentiating immortalized murine C2C12 myoblasts into nascent myotubes (day 3 in DM) and treating them with 10 ng/ml or 100 ng/ml of recombinant murine TWEAK. Twenty-four hours after TWEAK treatment, myotubes were stained with antibody to myosin heavy chain (MyHC) to assess differentiation and fusion (Fig. 1D, E). Neither concentration of TWEAK influenced differentiation (Fig. 1F), but 10 ng/ml of TWEAK significantly increased fusion index (Fig. 1G). When examining the myotube size distribution, we found that low and high concentrations of TWEAK decreased the percentage of nascent myotubes (2–3 nuclei/myotube) and increased the number of larger myotubes (4–9 and 10+ nuclei/myotube) (Fig. 1H). The number of unfused MyHC+ cells was also decreased in TWEAK-treated cultures (Fig. 1I) suggesting that TWEAK stimulates myoblast-to-myotube fusion. We also examined the expression levels of markers of stemness and differentiation, specifically Pax7, Myod1, Myog, Mymx, and Mymk in TWEAK-treated cells. Interestingly, we found that TWEAK downregulated the pre-fusion markers Pax7, Myod1, and Myog, but upregulated the fusion marker Mymx (Fig. S1A).

Since TWEAK has been previously shown to induce muscle atrophy, we also examined the expression of the atrophy-related markers, MuRF1 (encoded by Trim63) and Atrogin-1 (encoded by Fbxo32), in TWEAK-treated cultures. TWEAK significantly increased Trim63 expression which has been previously reported27, but had no significant effect on Fbxo32 expression (Fig. S1A). Myotube diameter of TWEAK-treated myotubes was used to assess myotube atrophy. Interestingly, 10 ng/ml of TWEAK significantly increased myotube diameter (Fig. S1B) suggesting that TWEAK does not induce atrophy of nascent myotubes in culture.

To test if TWEAK stimulates myoblast-to-myotube fusion, we performed a cell mixing experiment in which wild-type C2C12 cells were cultured at high density and NucLight Red-positive C2C12 cells were cultured at low density in differentiation media (DM) to form nascent myotubes and myocytes, respectively. Cells were then mixed, re-plated, treated with 10 ng/ml TWEAK for 24 h, and stained for MyHC (Fig. 1J, K). Interestingly, we noticed that TWEAK significantly increased the number of NucLight Red myocytes that fused to myotubes (Fig. 1L) suggesting that TWEAK stimulates myocyte-to-myotube fusion.

To determine if TWEAK-induced fusion requires binding to its receptor, Fn14, we transfected C2C12 myoblasts with non-targeting siRNA (NTi) or pooled siRNA targeting Fn14 (siFn14), switched cells to DM for three days, and treated with TWEAK (10 ng/ml and 100 ng/ml) for 24 h. Loss of Fn14 significantly decreased the fusion index as previously reported32 (Fig. S1D). Interestingly, TWEAK could not rescue the fusion defect in Fn14-deficient cultures (Fig. S1D) suggesting that TWEAK induces fusion through its receptor, Fn14. Knockdown of Fn14 was confirmed by quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) (Fig. S1E).

We confirmed that TWEAK stimulates fusion in primary myoblasts isolated from the hindlimbs of C57BL/6 mice. As with C2C12 myoblast cultures, we found that TWEAK increased fusion at both 10 ng/ml and 100 ng/ml doses (Fig. S1G–J) and did not induce myotube atrophy (Fig. S1K).

TWEAK stimulates muscle cell fusion during muscle regeneration

To evaluate the impact of TWEAK signaling on muscle cell fusion in vivo, we injured TA muscle of C57BL/6 adult (8- to 10-week-old) mice with intramuscular injection of the snake venom, cardiotoxin, and systemically administered (intraperitoneal (IP) injection) low (5 μg/kg) or high (25 ug/kg) doses of recombinant TWEAK daily at 3 to 6 days post-injury (dpi), a time point when fusion occurs (Fig. 2A). Untreated mice were used as controls. Muscles were collected 7 and 14 dpi, sectioned and stained for laminin (Fig. 2B, F). Low dose (5 ug/kg) TWEAK had no effect on fiber size at 7 dpi; however, we saw a significant decrease in fiber size in TA muscle isolated from 25 ug/kg TWEAK-treated mice (Fig. 2C, D). We noticed that regenerated fibers from TWEAK-treated mice had a higher proportion of fibers with two or more centrally located nuclei compared to control mice. To examine if the increase in myoblast fusion at 7 dpi leads to larger fibers, we examined fiber size of TA muscles from TWEAK-treated mice at 14 dpi. Interestingly, we saw a significant increase in fiber size of regenerated muscle in TWEAK-treated (5 ug/kg and 25 ug/kg) mice compared to control mice (Fig. 2F, G). When fiber size was plotted as a distribution, it was noted that there was a significant shift of the distribution away from smaller fiber sizes and towards larger fiber sizes in muscle of TWEAK-treated mice as compared to control mice (Fig. 2H). TWEAK-treated mice also had significantly more fibers with more than two centrally located nuclei (Fig. 2I).

Since high doses of TWEAK has been shown to induce atrophy in vivo, we also examined the effect of TWEAK on the contralateral uninjured muscle. We found that transient TWEAK administration did not decrease body weight, TA weight or TA fiber size at 7 or 14 dpi (Fig. S2A–F). These results suggest that TWEAK promotes myoblast fusion and growth during regeneration without inducing atrophy in healthy muscle, even at high doses.

TWEAK secretion was observed to increase at days 3–5 of differentiation, which corresponds with the initiation of fusion, decreased Pax7 expression, and elevated MyHC levels (Fig. 3A–B). To determine the physiological role of TWEAK during myoblast differentiation and fusion, we transfected C2C12 myoblasts with non-targeting siRNA (NTi) or pooled siRNA targeting TWEAK (siTWEAK). Although loss of TWEAK increased C2C12 differentiation (Fig. 3C, D), these myocytes did not fuse efficiently (Fig.3E–G). Knockdown of Tnfsf12 (TWEAK-encoding mRNA) was confirmed by RT-qPCR (Fig. 3H). Since we saw a decrease in myoblast fusion, we also examined the expression of myogenic and fusion genes in these cultures (Fig. 3H). We found that loss of TWEAK decreased expression of Mymx, an important muscle fusion regulator33–35 (Fig. 3H).

We confirmed these findings in mouse satellite cell-derived primary myoblasts (Fig. S3). TWEAK was not found in the supernatant of proliferating (day 0) or differentiating (day 1) myoblasts but was found in the supernatant of primary myoblast cultures at day 2–3 in DM (Fig. S3A). We also examined differentiation and fusion of primary myoblasts isolated from wild-type or TWEAK knockout (KO) mice after two days in DM. We found that loss of TWEAK had no effect on differentiation but decreased myoblast fusion and the average number of nuclei per myotube (Fig. S3B–E). Together, these results suggest that TWEAK secreted by differentiating myoblasts is required for myoblast fusion in vitro.

Previous studies have shown that TWEAK is upregulated in muscle five days after CTX-induced muscle injury and in atrophic conditions27,31,36. We sought to examine TWEAK expression at different time points during CTX-induced muscle regeneration. TWEAK ELISA performed on muscle homogenates revealed that TWEAK is upregulated during the inflammation and satellite cell proliferation stage (3 dpi) and remains elevated until mature myotubes are formed (14 dpi) (Fig. 3I). Since TWEAK is dynamically expressed following injury, we evaluated the activity of TWEAK signaling during muscle regeneration by administering a TWEAK neutralizing antibody (αTWEAK) at 3–6 dpi. TA muscles were collected 14 dpi, sectioned and stained with laminin to outline the myofibers (Fig. 3J). Treatment with anti-TWEAK antibody during muscle regeneration resulted in significantly smaller fibers (Fig. 3K). Furthermore, anti-TWEAK decreased myonuclear accretion in newly formed fibers as demonstrated by the significant decrease in the number of fibers with two or more centrally located nuclei (Fig. 3L).

To confirm the role of TWEAK in myoblast fusion during muscle regeneration, TA muscle of adult wild-type (C57BL/6) and TWEAK KO mice were injured by CTX. Two days after, mice were given IP injections of the nucleoside DNA incorporation tag, EdU (4 μg/g), and TA muscle was isolated 12 days later and stained for EdU and laminin (Fig. 3M). Interestingly, loss of TWEAK resulted in smaller fibers (Fig. 3N) and a decrease in the number of centrally located EdU-positive nuclei per fibers (Fig. 3O). These observations indicate that TWEAK signaling regulates myoblast fusion during muscle regeneration in vivo.

TWEAK regulates genes related to cellular movement and cell-to-cell signaling and interaction

To further define the molecular mechanisms regulated by TWEAK during myoblast-to-myotube fusion, we treated C2C12 myotubes (DM day 3) with 10 ng/ml TWEAK for 12 h. Using RNA-sequencing, we were able to define the effect of TWEAK administration on global gene expression. TWEAK treatment resulted in the differential expression of 1084 genes by 1.5-fold compared to control cells. Of the 1084 genes, 638 genes were upregulated, and 446 genes were downregulated (Fig. 4A). We used Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) to reveal the pathways affected by TWEAK signaling. Interestingly, we found that “Cellular Movement” and “Cell-to-Cell Signaling and Interaction” were the top two regulated pathways when ranked by Z-score (Fig. 4B). We then compared the list of genes in these two pathways to mouse genes that are annotated as regulators of cell-to-cell fusion (GO: 0140253) (Fig. 4C). We identified eight fusion-related genes that are differentially expressed in TWEAK-treated myotubes compared to control myotubes (Fig. 4D). Interestingly, we identified Mymx, an important muscle fusogen33–35,37, among the upregulated genes in TWEAK-treated myotubes. We validated the expression of the upregulated candidate genes in C2C12 myotubes treated with TWEAK for 6 h by RT-qPCR (Fig. 4E). We focused on Mymx, a known fusogen, and Cxcl10, whose expression was strongly upregulated, as well, in TWEAK-treated cells. To confirm that these genes are regulated by TWEAK in vivo, we performed RT-qPCR analysis on CTX-injured TA from wild-type and TWEAK KO mice. We found that Cxcl10 and Mymx expression was significantly decreased in regenerating muscle (7dpi) from TWEAK KO mice compared to wild-type mice (Fig. 4F). TWEAK stimulates myoblast migration and fusion through the CXCL10/CXCR3 axis

Given the strong induction of Cxcl10 transcription in TWEAK-treated myotubes (Fig. 4E), we next asked whether this mRNA is translated and secreted. We measured CXCL10 levels in the culture media and found that TWEAK-treated C2C12 myotubes began secreting CXCL10 as early as 12 h post-treatment (Fig. 5A). Because little is known of CXCL10’s role in myoblast fusion, we investigated whether its secretion is necessary for TWEAK-induced fusion. To this end, C2C12 nascent myotubes were treated with 10 μg/ml CXCL10 neutralizing or control antibody, with or without 10 ng/ml TWEAK. MyHC staining was performed 24 h later to assess fusion (Fig. 5B). TWEAK-induced fusion was blocked by the presence of the CXCL10 neutralizing antibody (Fig. 5B, C) suggesting that CXCL10 activity is required for TWEAK to enhance myoblast fusion. CXCL10 signals through the G-protein-coupled receptor CXCR3 to promote proliferation and chemotaxis. To test whether this interaction is necessary for TWEAK-induced fusion, we treated C2C12 nascent myotubes with 100 nM of the CXCR3 antagonist AMG487 or DMSO, with or without 10 ng/ml TWEAK. MyHC staining 24 h later showed that AMG487 blocked TWEAK-induced fusion, indicating that the CXCL10/CXCR3 axis is required (Fig. 5D, E). Myomixer and CXCL10 are direct downstream effectors of TWEAK-NF-κB signaling

TWEAK can activate both the classical and alternative NF-κB pathways in skeletal muscle, so we examined which pathway drives TWEAK-induced myoblast-to-myotube fusion. Nascent myotubes (DM day 3) were treated with 10 ng/ml TWEAK, and samples were collected over 24 h. Western blotting was used to assess NF-κB members of classical (p65 Ser536 phosphorylation, p105 processing) and alternative (p100 processing, RelB) pathway activation (Fig. S4A). Contrary to previous studies, p65 phosphorylation was not significantly induced by TWEAK (Fig. S4A, B); however, p105 processing to p50 was upregulated at 6 h post treatment (Fig. S4A, C). Notably, RelB expression increased significantly at 6 and 12 h, and p100 processing to p52 was upregulated between 3 and 12 h after treatment (Fig. S4A, D, E), indicating strong involvement of the alternative pathway. To confirm that NF-κB was transcriptionally active, we transduced C2C12 myoblasts with a lentiviral NF-κB luciferase reporter and a control Renilla luciferase reporter. After three days of differentiation, nascent myotubes were treated with 10 ng/ml TWEAK for 6 h. TWEAK increased NF-κB reporter activity over 18-fold (Fig. S4F).

To elucidate which NF-κB transcription factor is most likely responsible for the increased NF-κB reporter activity, we assessed the DNA binding capability of four NF-κB family members (two classical p65, p50, and two alternative p52, RelB) in nuclear extracts from C2C12 myotubes treated with TWEAK for 6 h. Of the four transcription factors assessed, p65 and p50 were undetectable suggesting that they are unlikely to play a major role in TWEAK-stimulated fusion (Fig. 6A). In contrast, recruitment of p52 (NF-κB2) and RelB to DNA binding elements were significantly elevated in nuclear extracts from TWEAK-treated myotubes (Fig. 6A) suggesting that TWEAK stimulates the transcriptional activity of the alternative NF-κB pathway. To confirm that TWEAK engages alternative NF-κB signaling in vivo, we assessed RelB nuclear localization in TA muscle 7 days after CTX injury. To assess the role of the alternative NF-κB pathway in TWEAK-induced fusion, we transfected C2C12s with non-targeting siRNA (NTi) or pooled siRNA targeting NF-κB2 (siNfkb2) or RelB (siRelb). Transfected cells were immediately transferred to DM for three days, followed by treatment with 10 ng/ml TWEAK for 24 h. Knockdown of Nfkb2 and Relb was verified using RT-qPCR (Fig. 6D, E). MyHC staining indicated that NF-κB2 deficiency resulted in a fusion defect, which became more apparent after the addition of TWEAK (Fig. 6F, G). TWEAK failed to enhance fusion in RelB-deficient myoblasts, suggesting RelB is needed for this effect. Additionally, TWEAK increased nuclei per myotube in NTi cultures but not in NF-κB2- or RelB-deficient myoblasts (Fig. 6H). Together, these results demonstrate that activation of the alternative NF-κB pathway is required for TWEAK-induced fusion.

Given that the alternative NF-κB pathway is necessary for TWEAK-induced myoblast fusion, and that Mymx and Cxcl10 expression are upregulated in TWEAK-treated myotubes, we investigated whether their expression is regulated via the alternative NF-κB pathway. RT-qPCR analysis revealed that TWEAK-induced expression of Mymx required both p52 (NF-κB2) and RelB; whereas Cxcl10 only required RelB (Fig. 6H, I). In silico analysis of the Mymx and Cxcl10 regulatory region identified one putative NF-κB-binding motif in the regulatory region of Mymx and two putative NF-κB-binding motifs in the regulatory region of Cxcl10 (Fig. 6K). Thus, we examined whether the alternative NF-κB transcription factor, RelB, occupies the regulatory regions of Mymx and Cxcl10 in TWEAK-treated myotubes by chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP). The classical NF-κB transcription factor, p65, was used as a control to confirm that the transcriptional regulation of these two genes is specific to the alternative NF-κB pathway. Indeed, p65 did not bind the Mymx or Cxcl10 regulatory region in control or TWEAK-treated myotubes (Fig. 6L, M). However, ChIP analysis revealed that RelB occupies the Mymx and Cxcl10 regulatory region in TWEAK-treated C2C12 myotubes (Fig. 6L, M), suggesting that TWEAK regulates Mymx and Cxcl10 gene expression through RelB-mediated transcription.

Discussion

This study uncovers a previously unrecognized role for TWEAK in promoting myoblast-to-myotube fusion by (1) discovering new muscle targets of alternative NF-κB that promote cell migration and fusion and (2) identifying a muscle source of endogenous TWEAK expression during differentiation that drives alternative NF-κB signaling. While TWEAK/Fn14 signaling has been shown to support myoblast proliferation following muscle injury, via macrophage-derived TWEAK, our findings reveal an autologous source of TWEAK during muscle differentiation and regeneration. We demonstrate that differentiating myoblasts themselves secrete TWEAK, which in turn promotes fusion through activation of the alternative NF-κB pathway. Mechanistically, TWEAK induces RelB-dependent transcription of the fusogenic protein Myomixer and the chemokine CXCL10, establishing a new link between alternative NF-κB signaling and the regulation of myoblast fusion.

The role of TWEAK signaling in skeletal muscle biology has been controversial, with studies reporting both beneficial and detrimental effects. In the present study, we aimed to dissect the physiological role of TWEAK during skeletal muscle regeneration and myogenic differentiation. Initially, TWEAK/Fn14 signaling was characterized as a potent inhibitor of myogenesis, shown to reduce myotube diameter in vitro and decrease myofiber cross-sectional area in vivo10,28,31. In contrast, we have previously shown that low dose TWEAK (10 ng/ml) can promote myoblast fusion in vitro14. Recently, Fn14 was also shown to be required for myoblast fusion during skeletal muscle regeneration32. While past reviews have attributed these conflicting outcomes to differences in TWEAK dose, our findings suggest that the timing of TWEAK administration, rather than its concentration, is the critical determinant. We show that both low and high doses of TWEAK enhance fusion when administered after myoblasts have initiated differentiation, leading to larger myotubes in vitro and increased myofiber size in vivo. In contrast, TWEAK inhibits differentiation when applied at early stages, consistent with prior reports14,28,31. Notably, we find that TWEAK is secreted by differentiating myoblasts during the fusion phase, indicating an autocrine and/or juxtacrine role in promoting secondary fusion of nascent myotubes. These findings clarify the physiological context in which TWEAK supports muscle regeneration and highlight the importance of temporal regulation in the signaling effects of this cytokine.

In this study, we investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying TWEAK-induced myoblast fusion by performing unbiased global RNA-sequencing. We found that the top two pathways regulated by TWEAK were “Cellular Movement” and “Cell-to-Cell Signaling and Interaction”, two pathways that are essential for myoblast fusion3,4. When intersecting these TWEAK-regulated genes with mouse genes annotated for roles in cell-cell fusion, we identified two key targets: Myomixer and CXCL10. Strikingly, Myomixer, also known as Myomerger and Minion, emerged as one of the most differentially expressed genes between control and TWEAK-treated myotubes. Myomixer is well established as an essential fusogen in both embryonic muscle development and postnatal muscle regeneration34,35,37. We demonstrate here that Mymx expression is reduced in TWEAK-deficient myotubes and that Myomixer overexpression rescues the associated fusion defect. Mechanistically, we show that Mymx is a direct transcriptional target of RelB downstream of TWEAK-induced alternative NF-κB signaling. Given its indispensable role in fusion, downregulation of Mymx and the formation of smaller myotubes in TWEAK-deficient cells further support the conclusion that TWEAK is required for efficient myoblast-to-myotube fusion.

In addition to Mymx, we found that Cxcl10 was upregulated by TWEAK in fusing myoblasts. Chemokine C-X-C motif ligand 10 (CXCL10), also known as interferon-γ-inducible protein 10 (IP-10), is a chemokine that binds to the G-protein-coupled receptor CXCR3. The role of CXCL10 in muscle is not well defined, although it has been found to be elevated in chronic inflammatory muscle diseases such as Duchenne muscular dystrophy and idiopathic inflammatory myopathies38–40. Furthermore, the expression of CXCL10 increases following contraction-induced damage. However, its contribution to postnatal muscle regeneration remains unclear41,42. For example, CXCL10 was identified in an RNAi screen as a negative regulator of myoblast differentiation43, but recent studies have shown that CXCL10 is secreted by interferon gamma (IFN-γ) responsive macrophages to promote the proliferation and differentiation of CXCR3-positive muscle stem cells44. Based on these observations, we investigated whether CXCL10 mediates the pro-fusion effects of TWEAK. We found that TWEAK induces CXCL10 secretion from myotubes and that this chemokine was required for TWEAK-induced fusion since neutralization of CXCL10 or pharmacological inhibition of CXCR3 with AMG487 significantly attenuated fusion in response to TWEAK.

Previous work has established that CXCL10 can act as chemoattractant for CXCR3-positive cells45,46. We propose that TWEAK-induced CXCL10 secretion may create a chemotactic gradient for CXCR3-expressing myoblasts toward nascent myotubes, facilitating fusion. Supporting this, we observed that TWEAK enhances myoblast migration in a CXCR3-dependent manner. These findings suggest a model in which TWEAK promotes myoblast fusion not only by inducing Mymx expression but also by enhancing myoblast migration via CXCL10-CXCR3 signaling.

Previously, PGC-1β (Ppargc1b) was the only target of alternative NF-κB signaling identified in skeletal myotubes16; however, in our study, we have identified two new targets of the alternative NF-κB in skeletal muscle. Like Ppargc1b, the NF-κB binding sites in Mymx and Cxcl10, conform to the NF-κB consensus sequence and therefore, should be competent to bind both the classical and alternative NF-κB complexes. Certainly, Cxcl10 has been shown to be regulated by classical NF-κB complexes in epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells47–50.

In conclusion, our identification of TWEAK-induced alternative NF-κB signaling as a regulator of myoblast fusion provides new molecular insight into this essential process and how it may be disrupted in neuromuscular disease. Our findings indicate that TWEAK selectively activates the alternative NF-κB pathway to induce Myomixer and CXCL10, which are key pro-fusion genes, without activating the classical NF-κB pathway associated with atrophy. This suggests a functional distinction between these pathways. Dissecting the distinct roles and transcriptional targets of classical versus alternative NF-κB signaling will be critical for developing targeted therapies that preserve or enhance muscle regeneration while minimizing deleterious inflammatory effects.

Methods

Mice

We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. Mice were bred and handled as recommended by the guidelines established by the University of Ottawa Animal Care Veterinary Service (protocol approval #CHEO-3757) and the Canadian Council on Animal Care. C57BL/6 mice (JAX000664) aged 8 weeks were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. TWEAK knockout mice were generously provided by Biogen Idec Inc. (Cambridge, MA)31,51. Male and female mice aged 8- to 10-weeks were used in this study. All animals were housed in a controlled facility (22 °C with 30% relative humidity on a 12-h light/dark cycle) and provided with food and water ad libitum. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane prior to being euthanized by CO2 exposure.

Skeletal muscle injury

Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and the hind limbs were shaved before the procedure. Mice were injected with 30 μl of 10 uM CTX (217503, Millipore Sigma) into the left tibialis anterior (TA) muscle and sacrificed at 7 or 14 dpi. To study myoblast fusion in vivo, mice were given a single intraperitoneal (IP) injection of 4 ug/g EdU (A10044, ThermoFisher Scientific) two days after CTX injection, and the TA muscle was isolated 12 days after that. Carrier-free Recombinant murine TWEAK (1237-TW-025/CF, R&D Systems) was reconstituted in sterile PBS to a concentration of 5 μg/mL and was administered by IP injection at 5 μg/kg or 25 μg/kg. LEAFTM purified anti-mouse CD255 (TWEAK) antibody (120008, BioLegend) was administered by IP injection at 1 mg/ml. Harvested TA muscles were flash frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA, protein, and ELISA analysis. For histological analysis, TA muscles were embedded in Tissue-Tek® OCT compound, frozen in liquid nitrogen-cooled isopentane and sectioned (10 um thick) for immunofluorescent staining.

C2C12 and primary myoblast culture

C2C12 myoblasts (CRL-1722, ATCC) were cultured and maintained in growth media (GM) containing HyClone Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) with high glucose (SH30022, Cytiva) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS, F1051, Sigma) and non-essential amino acids (11140050, Life Technologies). To induce differentiation, C2C12 myoblasts were switched to differentiation media (DM) containing high glucose DMEM with 2% heat-inactivated horse serum (HS; H1138, Sigma).

Primary myoblasts were isolated as described previously52. Briefly, hindlimb muscles of C57BL/6 wild-type and TWEAK KO were dissected and digested in a 0.2% Collagenase Type 2 solution (LS004176, Worthington Biochemical). Cells were washed with serum-free high glucose DMEM and then enriched for myoblasts by magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS). Primary myoblasts were grown on Matrigel (CB-40234, ThermoFisher Scientific)-coated plates in high glucose DMEM containing 20% FBS, 10% HS, and supplemented with 10 ng/ml basic fibroblast growth factor (100-18B, PeproTech Inc) and 2 ng/ml human growth factor (100-39, PeproTech Inc). Differentiation was induced by changing the media of confluent myoblasts to high glucose DMEM containing 2% FBS and 10% HS. Low passage (fewer than five passages) primary myoblasts were used for differentiation culture assays.

Cell treatments, transfections and transductions

C2C12 and primary myoblasts were switched to differentiation media for 3 days or 24 h, respectively, to produce nascent myotubes. Nascent myotubes were treated with 10 ng/ml or 100 ng/ml recombinant murine TWEAK (1237-TW-025, R&D Systems) for 24 h. For siRNA experiments, C2C12 myoblasts were transfected with non-targeting siRNA or siRNA targeting murine TWEAK, Fn14, NF-κB2 or RelB using lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For Mymx expression vector (pCMV6-Mymx), C2C12s were transfected using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen) following manufacturer’s instructions. For NF-κB reporter assay, C2C12 myoblasts were lentivirally transduced with a NF-κB luciferase reporter lentivirus (79564, BPS Biosciences) containing a firefly luciferase gene driven by four copies of the NF-κB response element and a Renilla luciferase lentivirus (79565-G, BPS Biosciences) that constitutively express Renilla luciferase under a CMV promoter. NucLight Red-labeled C2C12 myoblasts were made by lentivirally transducing myoblasts with Incucyte® Nuclight Red Lentivirus 2.0 (Sartorius).

Immunofluorescence staining, image acquisition and analysis

C2C12 and primary myotubes were fixed with ice-cold methanol for 10 min, followed by permeabilization with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.5% Triton X-100. Myotubes were incubated with primary antibody (MF-20, DSHB) in PBS overnight at 4 °C followed by three washes with PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100. The secondary antibody used donkey anti-mouse Alexa Flour 488 (A-21202, ThermoFisher Scientific). Stained cells were imaged on the EVOS® FL Auto Imaging System (ThermoScientific) under ×10 magnification.

Muscle sections were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1 M Glycine and 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS and blocked with 5% normal goat serum, 2% BSA (A9418, Millipore Sigma) in PBS. Subsequently, the sections were incubated overnight with primary antibodies. The primary antibodies were visualized with Alexa Fluor antibodies (Invitrogen) for one hour. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (0.5 ug/ml) prior to being mounted with DAKO fluorescent mounting medium (S3023, Agilent Technologies). The Click-iTTM Plus EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (C10640, ThermoFisher Scientific) was performed following the manufacturer’s protocol to assess EdU incorporation in regenerating myofibers. Fluorescently stained muscle sections were imaged on a Zeiss Imager.Z2 equipped with an Axiocam Mono 712 camera using the Zeiss Zen imaging software. Images of stained myoblasts were acquired using the EVOS® FL Auto Imaging System (Life Technologies) and analyzed using ImageJ. We analyzed a minimum of three images per tissue to determine the minimum Feret diameter (the closest possible distance between the two parallel tangents of the muscle fiber) of each muscle fiber.

Scratch wound migration assay

NucLight Red-labeled C2C12s were differentiated for three days to produce nascent myotubes. The monolayer of cells was scratched in a straight line, washed with PBS a few times and incubated in DM with or without TWEAK and DMSO or AMG487. Cells were placed in an incubator outfitted with an Incucyte® S3 Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius). Three fields at 4x magnification were recorded every 20 min for 48 h. The total number of cells in the scar was quantified 24 h after treatment.

Western blot analysis

Whole cell extracts were obtained with RIPA buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1% (v/v) Nonidet P-40, 0.25% (v/v) Na-deoxycholate) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (PPC1010, Sigma), resolved on a 10 or 12% SDS-PAGE gel, and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. Total protein staining was performed using REVERTTM total protein stain (926-11021, LI-COR) following manufacturer’s protocol. Membranes were blocked in 5% milk or 5% BSA in 0.1% TWEEN in TBS for 30 min prior to primary antibody incubation overnight at 4°C. IRDye® 680LT and 800CW (LI-COR) were used to detect the primary antibodies and fluorescent signals were detected using the Odyssey Infrared Imagining System (LI-COR). Quantification of protein expression was performed in Image Studio Ver5.2 (LI-COR). A detailed list of the antibodies used in this study can be found in Table S1.

ELISA

The ELISA kits used in this study were as follows: mouse TWEAK/TNFSF12 DuoSet ELISA (DY1237, R&D Systems) and mouse CXCL10/IP-10/CRG-2 DuoSet ELISA (DY466, R&D Systems). All ELISAs were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell culture supernatants were concentrated using 3 kDa or 10 kDa Amicon® Ultra Centrifugal Filters (UFC8003, UFC8010, Millipore Sigma) for CXCL10 ELISA and TWEAK ELISA, respectively. TA muscle tissue was homogenized in tissue extraction buffer (100 mM Tris pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% Sodium deoxycholate) using the gentleMACS Octo Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec). For all ELISA assays, the absorbance at 450 nm was recorded using the BioTek Synergy HTX Multimode Reader (Agilent) and the wavelength was corrected for optical imperfections by subtracting the reading at 540 nm. Final concentrations were calculated by interpolations from the standard curve.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from primary myotubes and C2C12 myotubes using RNAzol® RT (R4533, Millipore Sigma) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was generated using iScript gDNA Clear cDNA Synthesis Kit (1725035, Bio-Rad Laboratories) following manufacturer’s protocol. RT-qPCRs were performed using SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR® Green Supermix (1725271, Bio-Rad Laboratories) on a QuantStudioTM 6 Pro Real-Time PCR System (ThermoFisher Scientific). Primers are reported in Table S2. All samples were run in duplicate, and transcripts were normalized to the geometric mean of three housekeeping genes (Rpl13a, Csnk2a2, Ap3d1). After normalization, the ΔΔCt method was used to determine relative fold induction between control and experimental groups.

RNA-sequencing and data analysis

Total RNA from untreated (control) and 10 ng/ml TWEAK-treated C2C12 myotubes was extracted using RNAzol® RT (R4533, Millipore Sigma) according to manufacturer’s protocol. The mRNA library was prepared using poly (A)-tailed enriched mRNA (NEBNext). The NovaSeq 6000 was used to produce 100 bp pair-end RNA-sequencing data at an average read depth of 20–30 million reads per sample. The mRNA library, RNA-sequencing and data analysis was all performed at the Center for Applied Genomics at The Hospital for Sick Children, Toronto, Canada. The quality of the FASTQ sequencing data was assessed using FastQX v.0.11.9. Adapters were trimmed using Trim Galore v.0.5.0 and reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (release m27, GRCm39) using the STAR aligner, v2.6.0c. The STAR alignments were then processed to extract raw read counts for genes using htseq-count v.0.6.1p2. Two-condition differential expression was performed with the R package DESeq2 v.1.22.2. A volcano plot of all genes identified by the RNA-seq was made using the GraphPad Prism Software. Canonical pathways representing key genes were identified using the curated IPA® database and compared to annotated cell-to-cell fusion genes (GO: 0140253). Overlapping genes were selected for heatmap expression plots. The datasets generated and analyzed in this paper have been deposited to GEO and can be accessed with GEO accession number GSE268411.

Luciferase and TransAm DNA-binding assay

Luciferase reporter activity was quantified following cell lysis using the TWO-Step Luciferase (Firefly and Renilla) Assay System (60683, BPS Bioscience) following manufacturer’s protocol. Luciferase activity was detected using the BioTek Synergy HTX Multimode Reader (Agilent). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to the Renilla luciferase activity and was presented as a fold-change between (untreated) control and TWEAK-treated samples.

Nuclear extracts from C2C12 myotubes were prepared using the Nuclear Extract Kit (40010, Active Motif) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. NF-κB activation in nuclear extracts was measured using TransAM® NF-κB Family Activation Assay (43296, Active Motif). Nuclear extracts (5 μg) from untreated (control) and 10 ng/ml TWEAK-treated C2C12 myotubes were added to wells containing NF-κB consensus binding sites and the plate was incubated according to manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance at 450 nm and 655 nm (reference wavelength) was read on the BioTek Synergy HTX Multimode Reader (Agilent).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

ChIP was performed using the SimpleChIP® Plus Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (9005, Cell Signaling) following manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, C2C12 myotubes (DM day 3) treated with or without 10 ng/ml TWEAK for 6 h were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde in cell culture media for 10 min at room temperature, quenched with 1X glycine for 5 min and washed twice with PBS. Nuclei were prepared, and chromatin was incubated with micrococcal nuclease for 30 min at 37 °C followed by sonication with a VCX130 probe sonicator (Sonics and Materials, Inc.) for three pulses of 20 s at 20% power and 30 s between pulses. Chromatin was incubated with anti-RelB (10544, Cell Signaling), anti-p65 (8242, Cell Signaling), or Normal Rabbit IgG (2729, Cell Signaling) overnight at 4°C with rotation. The enriched DNA was subjected to RT-qPCR using iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (1725271, Bio-Rad Laboratories) on a QuantStudioTM 6 Pro Real-Time PCR System (ThermoFisher Scientific). Primers are reported in Table S2. All samples were run in duplicate, and the amount of immunoprecipitated DNA in each sample is represented as a percentage of total amount of input chromatin.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis was performed using the GraphPad Prism Software. A two-tailed student’s t test was performed when comparing a single experimental condition to the control condition. A single factor ANOVA followed by an uncorrected Fisher’s LSD comparison test was used to determine significance between three or more experimental conditions. Finally, a two-way ANOVA followed by uncorrected Fisher’s LSD comparison test was used when comparing the effect of two independent categorical variables (e.g. TWEAK and inhibitors) on a continuous dependent variable (e.g. fusion index). Significance is indicated as a p value of *< 0.05, **< 0.01, ***<0.001, or ****< 0.0001. All experiments are representative of a minimum of three biological replicates and are presented as the mean ± standard error mean (SEM). Individual data points are represented as closed circles on bar graphs.
