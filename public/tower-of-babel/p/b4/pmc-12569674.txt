Expanding the Chemical Space of Mechanically Interlocked Peptides (MIPs) via Rational Design and Metabolite Profiling
Authors: Anquan Li, Honghua Zhang, Noratika Binte Nazri, Mingxin Wan, Chong Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Mechanically interlocked peptides (MIPs) possess exceptional biological stability, making them promising scaffolds for therapeutic development. However, despite advances in biological and chemical synthesis, their accessible chemical space remains limited. In particular, practical methodologies and rational design principles for mechanically interlocking and stabilizing conventional linear peptides are still underdeveloped. Here, we present a robust strategy for peptide interlocking and stabilization using active-template Cu­(I)-catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (AT-CuAAC). Through systematic exploration of amino acid side chains, macrocycle size, peptide length, and reaction conditions, we established design guidelines and constructed a diverse library of interlocked short peptides. This approach was further applied to longer peptide sequence, [Y]6-AngII, using both convergent and iterative assembly routes, significantly broadening its applicability. Further biological experiment demonstrated that mechanical interlocking confers exceptional stability, with interlocked peptides maintaining >95% integrity in plasma after 48 h and 20% in whole blood after 24 h, far outperforming noninterlocked counterparts. Metabolite profiling reveals the mechanical bond protects ≥8 contiguous residues, with terminal hydrolysis dominating degradation while core regions remain shielded. Collectively, this work provides a robust and broadly applicable approach for MIP construction, offering key insights into the mechanistic basis of mechanical stabilization, and expands the toolkit for designing robust peptide therapeutics.

Introduction

Peptide drugs, with the advantages of both biomacromolecules and small molecules, are highly valuable in pharmaceuticals. Their ability to act as privileged scaffolds against traditionally “undruggable” targets, such as protein–protein interactions, has long attracted the interest of chemists, biologists, and pharmaceutical developers. − In 2024, the global market for peptide drugs was valued at USD 46.43 billion and is projected to reach USD 66.41 billion by 2030, with an annual growth of 6.1%. Despite this potential, the rapid degradation of peptide remains a major hurdle for therapeutic applications. − To address this, various strategies such as terminal modification, backbone modification, − peptide stapling, − PEGylation, , and lipidation , have been explored (Figure A). Although these methods are often effective, they have drawbacks. For instance, terminal modifications may reduce potency, , and PEG modification can trigger immunogenic responses, necessitating individual testing for each modified peptide. Therefore, developing new approaches to stabilize therapeutic peptides remains an area of great interest.

Inspired by the remarkable stability of lasso peptides, mechanically interlocked peptides (MIPs) offer a promising approach to enhancing peptides’ durability and functionality (Figure B). − However, the chemical space available for MIPs is currently constrained by their structural complexity and limited synthetic methodologies. Existing approaches, including bioengineering, − peptide grafting, − and template-assisted assembly, − typically suffer from low yields and narrow peptide scopes (Figure C).

Recent advances have begun addressing these traditional limitations, providing promising strategies to expand the chemical space of MIPs. For instance, Link and co-workers developed a one-pot protocol in 2021 for synthesizing combinatorial libraries of disulfide-bonded MIPs using dynamic covalent chemistry (Figure D). More recently, Schiefelbein et al. integrated flow synthesis with enzymatic maturation, enabling the incorporation of noncanonical amino acids and backbone modifications into lasso peptides (Figure D). Despite pioneering efforts centered on natural lasso peptides, the key challenge in advancing MIPs lies in constructing mechanically interlocked structures from conventional, nonlasso peptides. Generalizable strategies for interlocking such unstructured linear peptides remain underdeveloped, and no design framework currently exists for enhancing peptide stability through mechanical bonding.

In this work, we provide a practical guideline for the interlocking and stabilization of conventional peptides using an active template Cu­(I)-catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (AT-CuAAC) strategy (Figure E). Although AT-CuAAC is well-established for synthesizing small-molecule rotaxanes, − adapting it to peptides introduces unique challenges: (1) steric hindrance from α-carbon substituents can interfere with reactions within the macrocycle cavity, and (2) intramolecular interactions within peptides may exacerbate steric congestion and disrupt copper coordinationboth factors that can diminish interlocking efficiency. Through systematic evaluations of side chain effects and macrocycle designs, we constructed a library of mechanically interlocked short peptides and developed two complementary strategies that efficiently interlock peptides with extended sequences. Furthermore, we demonstrate that mechanical interlocking significantly enhances peptide stability in both plasma and whole blood, altering the degradation pathways. Cell viability assays confirm the biocompatibility of MIPs, and molecular docking studies offer insights into how mechanical bonds affect receptor interactions.

Overall, this study delivers: (1) a versatile synthetic method compatible with standard peptide techniques, (2) a comprehensive set of design principles to optimize peptide interlocking efficiency, and (3) rigorous experimental validation of stability enhancement, accompanied by practical guidelines for stabilizing peptides via mechanical interlocking. Our approach effectively addresses longstanding challenges in MIP synthesis and provides a blueprint for exploring new MIP candidates and their applications in mechanistic investigations and drug discovery campaigns.

Results and Discussion

Scope and Limitations

We began by evaluating the influence of amino acid side chains on the efficiency of peptide interlocking via AT-CuAAC. Propargyl- and azido-functionalized amino acids were prepared and subjected to interlocking reactions in the presence of bipyridine-based macrocycles to generate a library of MIPs.

As shown in Table , we first investigated the effect of side chains at the alkyne-bearing end on the rotaxane formation. Various propargyl amides were reacted with primary azide 2a under standard interlocking condition. Different alkyne structures 1a–1e consistently afforded high rotaxane yields, ranging from 82% to 91% (Table , entries 1–5). These results indicate that the AT-CuAAC reaction exhibits excellent tolerance toward diverse side chains on the alkyne-bearing end, with minimal impact on the interlocking efficiency.

Next, we investigated the impact of substituent bulk on the azide component by pairing different α-azido amides 2b–2j with an unsubstituted alkyne substrate 1f. Azides 2b–2h produced rotaxanes in yields comparable to those of primary azides (Table , entries 6–12), demonstrating good tolerance toward variations in side chains. In contrast, the cysteine-derived azide 2i and the isoleucine-derived azide 2j led to marked reductions in yield, affording rotaxane 4m in 24% and rotaxane 4n in 51% yield, respectively (Table , entries 13–14). These results indicate that with macrocycle 3a, bulky substituents in close proximity to the azide group (e.g., at the β-carbon) can hinder rotaxane formation, even when the alkyne itself is unsubstituted.

Further screening confirmed that when both the alkyne and azide bear side chains, steric bulk has an even more pronounced effect on reaction yields. For example, when the tyrosine-derived alkyne 1g was reacted with azides 2b and 2f, rotaxanes 4o and 4p were obtained at 89% and 86% yields, respectively (Table , entries 15–16). However, reactions with valine-derived azide 2k and isoleucine-derived azide 2j led to substantial yield reductions, resulting in only 10% of rotaxane 4q (Table , entry 17) and trace amounts of 4s (Table , entry 19). Similarly, the reaction of isoleucine-derived alkyne 1a with phenylalanine-derived azide 2f yielded only 28% of rotaxane 4r (Table , entry 18). These findings suggest that with macrocycle 3a, when both the alkyne and azide contain side chains, bulky substituents in close proximity to the alkyne or azide group (e.g., at the β-carbon of the alkyne or azide) significantly reduce the yield of rotaxane formation.

Strategies to Improve Interlocking Efficiency

The observed sensitivity to side-chain bulk likely arises from steric effects in the transition state. Leigh and co-workers have proposed a pathway involving monopyridine macrocycles. Based on their work, we can infer that bulky groups on the azide substrate create a more sterically crowded transition state compared to bulky groups on the alkyne. This increased steric congestion raises the energy barrier and consequently reduces yield. Since the AT-CuAAC reaction is kinetically driven, strategies that lower the transition state energy could improve yields. To explore this, we screened various reaction conditions, including reducing the reaction temperature and using polar solvents that might stabilize the transition state. Encouragingly, conducting the reaction at 0 °C in THF led to an improved yield (Table , entries 17–19, condition b).

Another approach to lower the transition state energy and improve the rotaxane yield was to enlarge the macrocycle cavity. We synthesized bipyridine macrocycles with longer aliphatic chains (3b and 3c) to provide a larger cavity. Using these macrocycles led to moderate improvements in yield under standard conditions compared to smaller macrocycle 3a, particularly for reactions involving bulkier azides (Table , entries 19–21, condition a). This finding contradicts a previous study conducted by Goldup and co-workers, which discovered that AT-CuAAC with smaller macrocycles leads to higher yields of rotaxane formation. This discrepancy underscores the unique characteristics of peptide mechanical interlocking via AT-CuAAC, which may not follow trends established in small-molecule systems.

Characterization of Interlocked Structures

The interlocked structures of all synthesized [2]­rotaxanes were confirmed by high-resolution mass spectrometry and 1H NMR analysis. Figure A shows the stacked 1H NMR spectra of macrocycle 3c, rotaxane 4u, and the respective free axle, highlighting key differences that confirm interlocking. Macrocycle protons (Ha–h) appear as single signals in noninterlocked 3c but are split into two or more sets in the interlocked product 4u due to the loss of the macrocycle’s bilateral symmetry. Chemical shift changes and the NOE spectral analysis of rotaxane 4u indicated that the macrocycle is positioned on the amide side closer to the 3,5-ditert-butylbenzene stopper. This conclusion is supported by key observations: (1) a downfield shift of amide hydrogen H9 (Δδ = 2.7 or 2.5 ppm), indicating intercomponent interactions between the macrocycle and the amide group, and (2) NOE correlations, which reveal that the macrocycle hydrogens (Hd–e) are spatially close to the hydrogen H12 of the 3,5-ditert-butylbenzene stopper (Figures S137–S138).

Analysis of rotaxane 4u by 1H NMR and the Cu­(I)-rotaxane 4k complex by X-ray diffraction: (A) Partial 1H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3, 298 K) of macrocycle 3c (top), rotaxane 4u (middle) and the corresponding unthreaded axle (bottom); (B) Side view of the 4k-Cu complex (H atoms omitted), demonstrating the tetrahedral coordination geometry around the Cu center; (C) Stick representation of 4k-Cu complex, demonstrating the steric hindrance around the cavity (H atoms omitted); (D) Space-filling representation of 4k-Cu complex, demonstrating the steric hindrance around the cavity.

Single crystals of the Cu­(I)-rotaxane 4k complex were obtained by slowly evaporating a deuterated chloroform solution. As shown in Figure B–D, the Cu­(I) center adopts a distorted tetrahedral geometry. Cu­(I) is coordinated by three nitrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. The Cu–O bond (Cu–O3: 2.195 Å) is longer than the Cu–N bonds (Cu–N1: 2.046 Å, Cu–N2: 2.015 Å, Cu–N4: 1.909 Å). The N1–Cu–N2 bond angle in the complex is 83.42°, which is the smallest angle due to the constraints imposed by the bipyridine ligand. The crowded environment observed in the solid-state structure (Figure C and D) further explains the reduced efficiency of rotaxane formation for substrates with bulkier side chains.

Interestingly, the macrocycle is positioned on the amide in rotaxane 4u, whereas in the Cu­(I)-rotaxane 4k complex it locates around the triazole moiety. This difference in macrocycle positioning can be attributed to the following factors: (1) in the Cu­(I)-rotaxane 4k complex, the coordination of Cu­(I) stabilizes the macrocycle’s position on the triazole moiety; (2) in rotaxane 4u, the absence of Cu­(I) coordination eliminates this stabilization. Moreover, rotaxane 4u features a larger macrocycle, which enables a shift from the triazole to the amide group. This shift is driven by a reduction in the steric clash between components and a stronger binding affinity with the amide group. Such a transition provides potential for developing stimulus-responsive, peptide-based molecular switch.

Interlocking Longer Peptide Sequences

Next, we turned our attention to the more ambitious goal of creating a longer interlocked peptide sequence using the AT-CuAAC approach (Scheme ). As a proof of concept, we selected an octapeptide analog of [Y]6-AngII (hereafter referred to as 8-axle; Figure A), developed by the Tzakos group. This octapeptide contains a triazole group, which serves as a bioisostere of the amide group. The triazole group in the octapeptide acted as a critical functional handle, enabling the successful transformation of 8-axle into an MIP through the AT-CuAAC approach.

To construct the octapeptide-based rotaxane, we explored two strategies (Scheme A). Given that the triazole is positioned in the middle of the peptide, the most straightforward method is a convergent pathway, in which the mechanical interlocking reaction is performed using two tetrapeptides bearing an alkyne and azide group, respectively. Alternatively, an iterative strategy can be employed, where the interlocking reaction is carried out using shorter peptide sequences such as a tetrapeptide and a dipeptide. The resulting interlocked hexapeptide can then undergo two successive amide coupling reactions to yield the desired octapeptide rotaxane. While the first strategy is efficient and direct, the second offers a modular route suitable for divergent synthesis, allowing further sequence variation and optimization. Therefore, we evaluated the compatibility of AT-CuAAC with both strategies to assess its adaptability and effectiveness in constructing complex interlocked peptide architectures.

Convergent Synthesis

The synthesis of tetrapeptides 14 and 18, as well as dipeptide 12, was carried out via liquid-phase synthesis (see SI for details). Initially, we attempted convergent rotaxane formation via the AT-CuAAC reaction between tetrapeptides 14 and 18 in the presence of macrocycle 3a. However, under optimized conditions, either at room temperature or 0 °C, only trace amounts of the desired interlocked product were obtained (Table S1, entry 1–3). Given the success of the Goldup group with the AT-CuAAC strategy for nucleotide rotaxane formation, we applied their condition into peptide rotaxane synthesis. Unfortunately, no product was observed (Table S1, entry 5). Considering that the longer peptide sequence might increase steric hindrance around the macrocycle cavity, we replaced macrocycle 3a with larger macrocycle 3c. This substitution resulted in a moderate yield (38%) of the desired interlocked peptide (Table S1, entry 6). To minimize potential steric hindrance from intramolecular interactions, we used a solution of 1:2 EtOH/DMSO to disrupt noncovalent interactions. This adjustment significantly improved the yield from 38% to 65%, a favorable outcome for this challenging synthesis (Table S1, entry 7).

Iterative Synthesis

Building on these results, we proceeded with an iterative strategy. Under our standard condition, dipeptide 12 reacted with tetrapeptide 18 in the presence of macrocycle 3a, producing the interlocked hexapeptide with an isolated yield of 18% (Table S1, entry 4). Replacing 3a with the larger macrocycle 3c and conducting the reaction in a 1:2 EtOH/DMSO solvent mixture increased the yield to 84% (Table S1, entry 8). Following the successful synthesis of 5-rot, two successive rounds of deprotection and amide coupling yielded the octapeptide rotaxane, 7-rot (Scheme B). The success of both the convergent and iterative strategies validates this interlocking approach as a robust and adaptable method for synthesizing mechanically interlocked peptides.

After synthesizing the octapeptide-derived [2]­rotaxane 7-rot, we removed its protecting groups. As shown in Scheme S4, treating [2]­rotaxane 7-rot with TFA in CH2Cl2 removed both the Boc and the Pbf groups. Then, adding a mixture of TFA/TFMSA/thioanisole (v/v/v = 8/1.2/0.8) and stirring at 0 °C for 4 h yielded 8-rot, with an isolated yield of 51% after workup and HPLC purification (Figure S8). Although the presence of stereoisomers and conformers complicated the 1H NMR analysis of 8-rot, further spectral comparison between [2]­rotaxane 8-rot and the corresponding noninterlocked peptide 8-axle revealed characteristic signals from both the octapeptide and the macrocycle (Figures S17–S21), confirming the successful synthesis of the octapeptide [2]­rotaxane. The sequence fidelity of the obtained peptides was confirmed by using tandem mass spectrometry (Scheme C and D). The central positioning of the macrocycle in 8-rot was verified by the presence of fragments y5 and b5. A comparison of the MS/MS results for 8-rot and 8-axle suggests enhanced stability of MIP. 8-axle underwent efficient fragmentation at a collision energy of 50 V. In contrast, 8-rot exhibits significantly reduced fragmentation even at a higher collision energy of 60 V, demonstrating the stabilizing effect conferred by mechanical interlocking.

Stability and Degradation of MIP in Blood

To further investigate the impact of the interlocked structure on the peptide stability, we evaluated the behavior of peptides 8-rot and 8-axle in human plasma. LC-MS/MS was used to monitor peptide decomposition (Figures S23–S24). As shown in Figure B, both the noninterlocked 8-axle and interlocked 8-rot remained highly stable in human plasma, with over 95% remaining after 48 h of incubation. This high stability is likely due to chemical derivatization of the enzyme-recognition segment in both compounds 8-axle and 8-rot.

We next assessed the stability of the peptides in whole blood. Unlike plasma, whole blood contains red and white blood cells, allowing for the evaluation of peptide stability in an environment that better mimics in vivo conditions. LC-MS/MS analysis revealed that 8-axle was rapidly degraded, whereas 8-rot exhibited exceptional resistance, with approximately 20% remaining after 24 h of incubation (Figure C). This result highlights the ability of mechanical interlocking to enhance peptide stability in a more physiologically relevant setting.

To better understand the fate of the interlocked peptide under enzymatic degradation, we profiled the metabolites of 8-rot generated in whole mouse whole blood. After 24 h, a total of 16 metabolites were detected (Table S3 and S4). The major metabolites were the amide-hydrolyzed species M1341 and M1456, while minor species such as M1488 and M1184 were also identified (Figure D). Metabolite analysis revealed that degradation was initiated at both termini of the peptide sequence. Specifically, M1456 results from C-terminal cleavage of phenylalanine, and M1488 from N-terminal cleavage of aspartic acid. The higher relative abundance of M1456 compared to M1488 suggests that C-terminal cleavage is more favorable than N-terminal cleavage. Both M1456 and M1488 can be further hydrolyzed by the removal of an additional amino acid residue from the opposite terminus to generate M1341. M1341 can then be hydrolyzed further by removal of arginine to yield M1184. This final step appears to occur more slowly than the earlier hydrolytic events, as the relative abundance of M1184 remains low at both 6 and 24 h. Degradation appears to stall at M1184 within the experimental time frame, as no shorter peptide fragments were detected at 24 h. Comparison of the relative abundances of 8-rot and its metabolites at 6 and 24 h indicates a progressive decline in 8-rot with a corresponding increase in its degradation products. This degradation profile contrasts with previous reports on AngII, where both the peptide and its metabolites display very short half-lives in plasma and whole blood. These results indicate that the macrocycle provides complete protection for approximately 2–3 amino acids on either side of its location along the peptide axle. In addition, residues located 3–4 positions away from the macrocycle also show substantially reduced degradation rates. Overall, this study demonstrates that mechanical interlocking can protect a continuous stretch of at least eight amino acid residues within a linear peptide. These findings provide key insights into how the mechanical bond enhances resistance to enzymatic degradation in biological environments.

Biocompatibility and Receptor Binding of MIP

Additionally, to evaluate the biocompatibility of the introduced components, cytotoxicity tests were conducted on 8-rot, 8-axle, and macrocycle 3c (Figure A–C). The MTT assay revealed that MIP 8-rot showed slightly higher inhibition of cell growth compared to the free linear peptide 8-axle. However, all three compounds3c, 8-axle, and 8-rotdisplayed no observable cytotoxicity within the tested concentration range.

8-axle has previously been reported to display high affinity and subtype selectivity for AT2R. To assess the potential binding affinity of MIP, the relevant compoundsAngII, 8-axle, and peptide [2]­rotaxane (8-rot)were docked into the Angiotensin II Type 2 Receptor (AT2R) crystal structure (PDB code: 5UNF). The predicted binding modes of these compounds in the AT2R binding site are shown in Figure D–G. The binding mode of AngII with AT2R was very similar to that of 8-axle with AT2R. Both compounds penetrated deeply into the binding pocket of AT2R (Figure D–E), with their ligand-binding regions playing a crucial role in receptor interactions. In contrast, 8-rot did not adopt the same binding mode (Figure F–G). The interlocked macrocyclelocated between either Tyr4 and Ile5 or Ile5 and Tyr6prevented 8-rot from penetrating the AT2R binding site. Furthermore, the presence of the macrocycle may hinder several critical interactions observed between octapeptide 8-axle and AT2R, such as the π–π interaction between 8-axle’s triazole and Tyr108 and the hydrogen bonding between Try6 and Arg182 (Figure H). These findings suggest that the macrocycle may effectively cage the bioactivity of the peptide, attenuating receptor binding. Recent studies by the Goldup group have demonstrated stimulus-responsive techniques for cleaving bipyridine macrocycle in mechanically interlocked molecules, including responses to enzyme, chemical, or light. In the future, introducing stimulus-responsive macrocycles into bioactive peptide-based rotaxanes could enable the controlled release and smart delivery of peptide drugs.

Practical Guidelines and Design Recommendations for Peptide Interlocking and Stabilization

To support the rational development of MIPs, we present a structured framework that integrates key principles for peptide design, macrocycle selection, and reaction optimization using the AT-CuAAC strategy (Figure ).

Rational design framework for constructing MIPs via AT-CuAAC.

The process begins with identifying a peptide of interest that exhibits limited intrinsic stability, followed by incorporation of a triazole handle into the sequence. Based on the peptide’s length and project aims, either a convergent or iterative assembly strategy could be selected. Convergent assembly is generally faster and more efficient, while iterative assembly offers flexibility for post modification. For both strategies, evaluating the steric bulk of residues near the reactive site is crucial. When side chains are small to moderate, standard AT-CuAAC conditions often suffice. However, when bulky groups are present or standard conditions fail to produce interlocked products, a series of stepwise optimizations can be implemented. These include lowering the reaction temperature and using polar solvents to stabilize the transition state (referred to here as “optimized conditions”), selecting larger macrocycles (e.g., 3c) to accommodate steric hindrance, and employing DMSO/EtOH mixtures to disrupt intramolecular hydrogen bonding that might otherwise interfere with interlocking. If interlocking remains unsuccessful despite these adjustments, then an iterative assembly strategy may be more appropriate. This approach involves breaking down longer peptides into shorter fragments (such as di- or tetrapeptides), interlocking them at an earlier stage and subsequently extending the sequence. This approach is particularly useful for overcoming steric congestion, conformational constraints, and solubility issues that often hinder the efficient synthesis of interlocked long peptides.

Building upon these guidelines, we can further enhance the applicability of this interlocking strategy. Achieving optimal complementarity between the macrocycle and the peptide axle is crucial to ensure both efficient synthesis and stable interlockingwhile minimizing the risk of macrocycle slippage. Furthermore, since the mechanical bond provides a broad protective region, positioning the macrocycle within segments of the peptide that are less involved in target recognition may allow the system to exert its stabilizing effect without compromising biological function. Computational tools, such as molecular docking, can support rational design and assist in optimizing macrocycle placement. Looking ahead, incorporating stimuli-responsive elements may enable controlled activation of bioactivity in response to specific environmental triggers, paving the way for smart, condition-dependent delivery of therapeutic peptides.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study establishes an AT-CuAAC-based strategy for constructing MIPs, addressing a long-standing synthetic challenge and expanding the accessible chemical space of MIPs. Through the assembly of a representative MIP library and the development of both convergent and iterative synthesis routes, we derived a practical set of design principles that enable efficient interlocking across a broad range of peptide architectures.

Biological evaluation confirms the stabilizing effect of mechanical interlocking, while detailed metabolite profiling offers mechanistic insights into its protective scopeproviding a concrete framework for applying mechanical stabilization to therapeutic peptides. Beyond improving synthetic access and enhancing stability, this work sets the stage for the development of condition-responsive systems. We are currently exploring the application of this strategy for smart and programmable delivery of an interlocked peptide therapeutic.
