Dynamic behavior and failure characteristics of granite with different shapes of holes
Authors: Hongzhi Liu, Lianjun Chen, Ke Ma, Fuqiang Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuring mining, dynamic loads significantly affect the instability of goafs. Therefore, investigating the mechanical behavior of granite specimens with holes under dynamic loading is essential. This study conducted SHPB tests on granite specimens with different shaped holes: circular (C), rectangular (R), short-axis elliptical (SE), and long-axis elliptical (LE). After reaching peak stress, the strain rebound of C and LE specimens is about 0.8%, and the strain rebound of R and SE specimens is 0.2%. The peak stress ranks as follows: LE, R, C, and SE. As the strain rate increases, the debris in the sample shifts from block debris to long strip debris. When the gas pressure rises from 0.14 MPa to 0.23 MPa, both the peak stress and energy input of each specimen increase with every 1% increase in strain rate. The crack propagation and force chain evolution also were explored using PFC2D. The crack initiation stress to damage stress ratio is 1:2. Upon failure, specimens primarily develop penetrating cracks aligned with the impact direction and symmetrical cracks at the upper and lower edges of the holes. Compression force chains concentrate at the upper and lower edges of the specimen, while tension chains relate to macroscopic cracks. Finally, dimensionless equation is derived to analysis the influence factors of dynamic strength.Keywords: Granite, SHPB, Hole shape effect, Energy dissipation, PFC2DSubject terms: Civil engineering, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

Goaf refers to the cave created when underground ore extraction is not backfilled correctly1. As mining progresses, surrounding rock becomes unstable due to external stress, often causing surface subsidence or collapse2. Underground mining also creates stopes, chambers, and roadways3, compromising miner and equipment safety and posing challenges to mine development and urbanization4. Dynamic loads from rockbursts and blasting significantly impact goaf instability and failure5. Stress waves from these loads propagate through the rock, creating localized stress concentrations and inducing transient rock failure6,7. When local defects are subjected to dynamic load, new cracks will initiate and further extend until macroscopic damage occurs, which will bring potential safety hazards to the overall project. According to incomplete statistics, there are more than 300 mine accidents in China in 2024, resulting in more than 400 casualties and equipment losses of 300 million yuan. Therefore, it is of great academic significance and engineering practicability to explore the failure mode and crack development law of rock with holes under dynamic load.

Rocks with holes have gained significant scholarly attention for their mining and engineering applications8–10. Research indicates that hole shapes (HS) significantly influence rocks’ mechanical properties and failure mechanisms11. Under dynamic loading, HS is vital for determining rock strength12,13, as it affects stress concentration and crack propagation behaviors14. Stress concentration around the void is the main factor in rock failure15. Curved boundaries typically lead to tensile cracks aligned with the load, while linear apertures induce shear cracks at the corners16,17. Crack propagation rates depend on pore size; shear cracks correlate positively, while tensile cracks show an inverse rule18. In elliptical apertures, crack growth rates are determined by the major and minor axes ratio and the inclination angle19.

Researchers have conducted uniaxial compression tests on granite samples featuring three non-coplanar holes, identifying four distinct crack modes. By analyzing the force and displacement fields, they have uncovered the crack evolution mechanism surrounding the prefabricated hole in the granite sample20. Additionally, cyclic loading-unloading tests were performed on red sandstone with eight different configurations of holes. The results indicated that the compressive strength diminished as the number of holes increased, initially decreasing and then increasing again as the angle α between the hole arrangement and the loading direction varied21. In the examination of prism specimens with inverted U-shaped holes, it was observed that both the number and arrangement of holes influence the failure mode. Specifically, a single hole and two holes arranged at a 45° angle resulted in shear failure, while two holes arranged horizontally and vertically led to a mixed failure of tensile and shear types22.

Consequently, current studies examining the impact of HS on rock failure primarily concentrate on uniaxial23,24, triaxial, and various mechanical tests, with HS typically being circular or triangular in shape. There is a notable gap in research involving SHPB experiments (dynamic loading) that consider gas pressure (GP) as a variable and define the HS as a circular boundary. This type of research aligns more closely with engineering applications and can offer theoretical backing for real-world engineering scenarios.

In addition to HS, the distance between the borehole center and the contact boundary surface of the specimen and the incident bar (H) represents a significant variable in the analysis. Alterations in H modify the pathway of stress transfer, thereby affecting the distribution of stress and the propagation of cracks25,26. Under dynamic loading conditions, the interplay between the position of the hole and the direction of external loading results in intricate effects27, manifesting as nonlinear patterns in compressive strength and deformation behavior. Furthermore, increased GP intensifies the severity of cracks. It contributes to the failure of the rock material, leading to a transition in fracture characteristics from tensile to shear failure modes28,29. Scholars have studied the stress distribution of rock mass with complex shapes and positions by using complex variable function theory. For six HS in actual rock engineering, their mapping functions are determined, and their external analytical stress distribution is further given14. However, most of the existing studies focus on the HS, and there are few studies on the failure mode and energy dissipation of H on rock.

In terms of energy dissipation, rock deformation and failure correspond to the processes of energy input, dissipation, and release, respectively. Rocks with voids are more likely to sustain damage or fracture under dynamic loads30. During impact, energy is primarily dissipated through crack formation and the movement of internal particles. This fracture process directly correlates dynamic strength and fragmentation with energy dissipation density31,32. The energy absorbed by rock under dynamic loading increases with rising GP, though it does not exceed 50% of the total incident energy33. The energy dissipation per unit volume and the energy dissipation rate of granite under impact loading exhibit distinct strain rate effects. The energy dissipation per unit volume increases approximately linearly, while the dissipation rate follows a nonlinear trend. Furthermore, energy dissipation rates are influenced by specimen dimensions; specimens with larger aspect ratios demonstrate lower dissipation rates34,35.

Numerical simulation technology has emerged as a critical instrument for analyzing and predicting material behavior. The integration of numerical simulations with experimental studies facilitates a thorough understanding of the influence of defects on the mechanical properties of rock. Techniques such as the discrete element and finite element methods are adopted to simulate the intricate behavior of rock subjected to dynamic loading36,37. For instance, Chen38 utilized PFC2D to conduct a numerical simulation analysis on both intact rocks and those featuring three different HS (circular, square, and triangular). The findings indicate that the strength of the rock is affected by HS, with circular holes having the least impact and triangular holes having the most. Lin39employed the discrete element method to examine the mechanical properties of jointed rock masses with two circular holes subjected to uniaxial loading. The study concluded that the peak strength and elastic modulus of the specimen initially decrease and then increase as the joint inclination angle α changes, highlighting the crack propagation mechanism influenced by the interaction between the hole and the joint under uniaxial stress. Despite the extensive research conducted on rocks with defects under dynamic loading, there is a notable scarcity of comparative analyses that specifically address the variations in HS, H, and GP.

In conclusion, while there is a wealth of research on the failure modes and energy dissipation of rock with holes, there is limited investigation into how granite with varying HS and H responds to dynamic loading. This study conducted SHPB experiments on granite featuring four different HS (circular (C), rectangular (R), long elliptical (LE), and short elliptical (SE)) and H (20 mm, 30 mm, 40 mm, and 50 mm). A total of 64 granite samples were prepared with four different GP (0.14 MPa, 0.17 MPa, 0.2 MPa, and 0.23 MPa), and their mechanical characteristics, fragmentation behavior, and energy dissipation were examined. Additionally, PFC2D simulations were employed to investigate the evolution mechanism of force chains at a microscopic level, providing a scientific foundation and reference for real-world engineering applications.

Materials and methods

Material

The granite for this study was sourced from the Qidashan Iron Mine in Anshan City, Liaoning Province. It was processed into prismatic specimens measuring 40 mm in width and 100 mm in height. Using a water jet cutting technique, four HS were created with H of 20 mm, 30 mm, 40 mm, and 50 mm (Fig. 1). X-ray diffraction analysis showed the granite primarily consists of albite (46.85%), microcline (31.37%), quartz (20.58%), and biotite (1.2%). Uniaxial compression tests on standard cylindrical specimens (Φ50 mm) yielded a compressive strength of 135.9 MPa and a tensile strength of 12 MPa. Experimental setup

The SHPB device (Fig. 2) used in this study consists of incident, transmission, and absorbing bar, all made of 50 Cr steel with a diameter of 50 mm40. The material has a Young’s modulus of 240 GPa, 7800 kg/m³ density, a longitudinal wave velocity of 5580 m/s, and a wave impedance of 4.35 × 107MPa/s. Strain measurements were conducted using an LK 2109 A ultra-dynamic strain gauge and an LK 2400 high-speed data acquisition system. For the SHPB tests, four GP (0.14, 0.17, 0.2, 0.23 MPa) were applied, resulting in a total of 64 effective impact tests. The strain rate for each specimen was calculated, and specimens were labeled as ‘HS-H-GP (strain rate)’ such as ‘R-20-0.14.14 (52.91s⁻¹)’. Before the test, Vaseline was applied to the contact surfaces between the specimen and the incident and transmission bars to minimize end effects on the experimental results.

Initially, the study analyzes the variations in dynamic stress-strain curves and energy dissipation characteristics. Subsequently, the characteristics of rock fragments resulting from impact are assessed. Finally, the numerical simulations are conducted using PFC2D to investigate the microscopic mechanisms within granite specimens by analyzing the evolution of crack initiation stress, damage stress, and force chains during crack propagation. Dynamic stress equilibrium verification

The SHPB testing methodology is based on two primary assumptions: one-dimensional stress waves within the compression bar and the uniform stress distribution across the specimen41. In this investigation, the compression bar has a length-to-diameter ratio of 50. It is made from aluminum alloy, adhering to the aforementioned assumptions regarding the specimen’s one-dimensional stress wave propagation and uniform stress distribution. During the impact testing procedure, variations in wave impedance occur due to the differing materials between the specimen and the bar, resulting in the reflection and transmission of the stress wave at the interface during its propagation. The process of stress wave propagation is illustrated in Fig. 4a. Upon impact, the incident wave undergoes multiple reflections within the pressure bar system, ultimately leading to a state of equilibrium in the stress at both ends of the specimen after several reflections. The loading pulse is characterized by a half-sine waveform, as depicted in Fig. 4b, which is particularly advantageous for testing granite specimens containing holes. Experimental results

Dynamic stress-strain curve

A total of 64 dynamic stress-strain curves were generated from the dynamic tests that were conducted. When H = 30 mm and GP = 0.2 MPa, the stress-strain curves for the R, C, SE, and LE specimens are illustrated in Fig. 5a. These curves can be classified into two distinct types, each comprising four stages. The initial two stages are nearly identical across both types. In Stage I, stress increases linearly with strain, indicating elastic deformation. Stage II shows significant yield behavior, marking the yield stage. In Stage III, after peak stress, macroscopic cracks form near the hole, leading to failure, which occurs at approximately 4% strain for type I curves (Fig. 5b) and around 3% strain for type II curves (Fig. 5c). In Stage IV, following high-speed impact damage, strain decreases and rebounds, with type I curves strain rebounding over 0.8% and type II curves less than 0.2%. The C and LE specimens are type I modes, while the R and SE specimens are type II modes. Here, strain rebound refers to the gradual decrease in strain value after the specimen fails.

(a) Dynamic stress-strain curves of granite with different HS (b-c) the stage division of two typical curves.

All specimens’ peak stresses and strain rates are summarized in Table 2, with peak stress curves plotted against GP and H as variables. Figure 6a shows that as GP increases, both the specimens’ strain rate and peak stress rise linearly. For H = 20 mm, the GP of the R specimen increases from 0.14 MPa to 0.23 MPa, corresponding to a peak stress rise from 145.92 MPa to 203.85 MPa. The increase in peak stress across the four specimens is influenced by both the strain rate and variable H. When GP increases from 0.14 MPa to 0.23 MPa, a 1% increase in strain rate results in a peak stress rise of 0.84% for the R specimen at H = 20 mm and 0.4% for H between 30 and 50 mm. For C specimen, peak stress increases by 0.67%, 0.44%, 0.24%, and 0.81% for H values from 20 mm to 50 mm, respectively. The SE specimen shows increases of 1.02% for H values of 20–30 mm, 0.71% at H = 40 mm, and 0.32% at H = 50 mm, while the average increase for LE specimens is 0.51%. Notably, the peak stress of the LE specimen exhibits the most excellent stability in response to strain rate variations, indicating a significant strain rate effect. It is important to note that our calculation method is somewhat arbitrary, and the resulting values are averages, so their accuracy requires further research by scholars for validation.

Fragmentation characteristics

To investigate the properties of the debris generated, all specimens subjected to impact are collected for analysis. The debris produced under varying conditions is systematically sorted and weighed to assess its mass. In this research, the debris is classified into four distinct categories based on size (unit is mm): [0, 1], [1, 2], [2, 5], and [5, ∞]. The sorted debris is measured using an electronic scale. The variables HS, H, and GP are employed to analyze the crushing behavior of granite. Four specimen types’ crushing effects are analyzed with H at 20 mm and GP at 0.2 MPa. Figure 7a shows that the LE specimen produces the most debris particles, while the SE specimen generates the least. This indicates that LE specimen absorbs more energy and sustain greater damage, resulting in larger debris volumes. The debris from LE specimens mainly consists of elongated strips, while SE specimens yield shorter, square fragments. Within the same GP, an increase in H correlates with greater specimen fragmentation. The number of fragments smaller than 5 mm increases, while the visible volume of debris decreases (Fig. 7b). When H is constant, a higher strain rate transforms debris from block-like shapes to elongated strips, reducing their size and significantly increasing the total number of fragments. This suggests that a higher strain rate enhances energy absorption, leading to increased damage (Fig. 7c).

Differences in rock debris characteristics of granite (a) HS (b) H and (c) GP.

After screening granite debris, the mass of rock fragments is measured with a high-precision electronic scale, and the data are recorded. Figure 8 displays the mass percentage of debris for the R specimen. The debris from the R specimen, across different strain rates and H values, mainly consists of particles larger than 5 mm. However, as H increases, the proportion of larger debris decreases, while smaller debris increases, indicating that greater distances lead to more damage. Consequently, the energy within the specimen, contributing to its failure, also rises, along with energy expenditure. Furthermore, an increased strain rate correlates with a decrease in larger debris, suggesting enhanced fragmentation and higher energy consumption. This pattern is also seen in other specimen types.

Energy dissipation character

Effective dynamic tests require establishing a dynamic stress equilibrium before failure. This can be assessed by analyzing the dynamic stress history at both ends of the specimen. Figure 9a displays the stress balance curves of four HS samples at GP = 0.2 MPa, H = 20 mm. Initially, the dynamic stress on the incident side is zero. As impact time progresses, the EI approaches the cumulative value of the other three energy components. On the transmission side, dynamic stress arises from the transmission wave. After a minor fluctuation during the initial loading phase, the dynamic stress at both ends of the rock specimen becomes nearly identical throughout the dynamic loading process, especially near the peak stress. This suggests that the rock specimen reaches dynamic stress equilibrium during the SHPB test. Once this equilibrium is established, the dynamic stress-strain curve of the specimen can be determined.

(a) The stress balance diagram and (b) energy time-history curves.

Figure 9b depicts the energy-time history curve for specimen C-20-0.2.2. Initially (0–50 µs), the energies of EI, ER, ET, and EA are negligible. At around 60 µs, a significant increase in these components suggests energy absorption as primary cracks close. EI is notably higher than the others, with ER to ET at approximately 2:1. EA, while generally increasing, remains the smallest proportion. Between 50 µs and 80 µs, EA surpasses ET due to crack initiation, leading to increased energy absorption. From 0 to 250 µs, EI and ER rise rapidly with stress wave propagation, while EA continues to increase. At about 210 µs, EA reaches the peak, indicating impending failure, after which energy stabilizes.

The values of EA, EI, Ka for each specimen is listed in Table 3. The findings indicate a quantifiable correlation between EI and the strain rate. Specifically, as GS rises from 0.14 MPa to 0.23 MPa, when the strain rate experiences a 1% increase from its baseline measurement, for the R specimen, EI exhibits a 6.21% increase at H of 20 mm, and a 2.23% increase at H ranging from 30 to 50 mm. In the case of C specimen, there is a 1.94% increase at H between 20 and 40 mm, and a 3.98% increase at a H of 50 mm. The SE specimen shows a 3.71% increase at H of 20 to 30 mm, and a 1.37% increase at H of 40 to 50 mm. The average increase for the LE specimens is recorded at 1.88%. These results suggest that the pattern of EI variation in relation to strain rate and stress is consistent across all specimens, thereby illustrating a pronounced strain rate effect.

Numerical simulation of the dynamic failure process

Numerical model

FFC2D can simulate the mechanical behavior of particle-based materials based on Newton’s second law. It captures rock’s microscopic properties, addressing conventional laboratory tests’ limitations. Within the PFC2D framework, models of the incident bar, transmission bar, bullet, and granite specimen with holes are created for the SHPB system, with the granite specimen measuring 100 mm × 40 mm. The granite sample’s particle radius ranges from 0.4 × 10⁻³ m to 0.6 × 10⁻³ m, while the rod and bullet particles range from 1.2 × 10⁻³ m to 2.0 × 10⁻³ m. Particle interactions are modeled using parallel bond contact, with each SHPB impact model containing 19,000 particles. Various hole geometries are formed by removing specific particles (Fig. 11). After model generation, the specimen’s surrounding walls are removed, and the SHPB impact test is simulated under different impact pressures by varying the initial velocities of the bullet system particles. Figure 11 also shows the other three models, from left to right in turn is C, SE, LE, R.

Dynamic crack initiation and damage stresses

In this study, the parameters σci and σcd are assessed by tracking the number of cracks throughout the simulation process. The crack initiation and damage stress for each specimen are listed in Table 5, and the ratios of σci and σcd to the peak stress are calculated to evaluate the effects of GP, H, and HS. Figure 13a illustrates that the ratio of σci to σcd is approximately 1:2. As the GP (strain rate) increases, the trends in σci for each specimen exhibit a similar pattern to that of the peak stress, demonstrating a linear increase. The values of σci and σcd for the LE specimen are markedly greater than those observed in the other three pore geometries. This suggests that, under identical dynamic loading conditions, the initiation of new cracks in the LE specimen occurs later, and the phase of stable crack propagation is prolonged. Consequently, this results in an elevated dynamic compressive strength, which is consistent with the experimental findings.

When the HS is identical, the trends in σci and σcd generally align with the experimental data. The overall magnitude of these two stress parameters follows the hierarchy: LE > R > C > SE, indicating that under impact loading conditions, the SE specimen configuration is more susceptible to crack initiation and experiences a shorter duration of stable crack propagation. Conversely, the LE specimen configuration exhibits delayed crack initiation and an extended stable propagation phase. The elevated σci value suggests that the processes of crack initiation and propagation in LE are more challenging, thereby contributing to its superior strength. Furthermore, as H increases, the trend in σci corresponds with the variation in peak stress (Fig. 13b). Thus, the trend in the crack initiation stress ratio can serve as an indicator of the peak stress trend of the specimen to a certain extent.

Crack propagation

Figure 14a illustrates the simulated crack patterns and contact force chain images for four granite specimens subjected to an H of 30 mm and a GP of 0.17 MPa. The PFC simulation indicates that tensile cracking is the primary failure mechanism. The C and SE specimens show nearly parallel cracks to the impact direction, while the R and LE specimens have cracks extending outward. The simulated specimen is homogeneous, possibly contributing to slight discrepancies between the simulation and the physical test. The contact force chain image shows black lines for the pressure chain and red lines for the tension chain, with line thickness and color intensity indicating force magnitude. At the failure point, pressure chains concentrate around the hole, particularly in the R, C, and LE specimens, while tension chains form around them and at the macroscopic crack. The SE specimen has a significantly lower concentration of force chains, attributed to reduced contact forces between particles during impact, leading to particle movement away from the crack.

Numerical results of granite with (a) different HS, (b) long axis elliptical holes at different H.

The results in Fig. 14b show that at GP = 0.14 MPa, cracks in the LE specimens are mainly parallel to the holes and occur primarily away from them. For H = 20 mm and H = 50 mm, increased pressure is observed near the impact side, indicated by thicker force chains. At H = 40 mm, compressive stress is higher on the side opposite the impact. If the H is 30 mm, force chains are dense only around the hole, suggesting uniform stress wave transmission and particle forces. The stress concentration around the hole is the key factor for the highest dynamic strength.

To explore the microscopic process of crack propagation, granite specimens with different HS at H = 30 mm, GP = 0.2 MPa, and LE specimens with different H when GP = 0.2 MPa are taken as examples, as shown in Fig. 15. The marks a, b, c, and d correspond to the crack initiation, the unstable crack propagation, the peak stress, and the post-peak rebound points, respectively.

Crack propagation under (a) different HS and (b) different H of elliptical long axis holes.

Figure 15a shows that at point a, all specimens initiate new cracks along the center of the hole, extending to both ends. As stress is applied, the crack enters a stable expansion stage at point b. The C specimen forms a symmetrical crack at approximately 45° to the impact direction, with another parallel crack on the middle and right sides. The LE specimen develops short cracks at the upper right and lower left of the hole, with parallel cracks on the middle and right sides. The R and SE specimens generate new cracks at both shaft ends, perpendicular to the impact direction, aligning with mid-span cracks. The SE specimen’s right-side cracks connect with those around the hole, while the new cracks on the right side of the R specimen are shorter.

The LE specimen exhibits significantly fewer cracks than the other three, indicating that crack initiation during stable propagation is more difficult for LE, which suggests a higher bearing capacity. This is consistent with LE’s greater dynamic strength. As stress reaches the peak point c, the cracks increase substantially. In the C specimen, cracks propagate along existing ones, splitting at a 45° angle in the specimen, while SE and LE specimens develop vertical cracks in an ‘II’ shape. The C specimen’s crack opens outward, forming a ‘Y’-shaped crack. At point d, the specimens reach macroscopic failure, with cracks peaking and extending. Figure 15b shows that the microscopic crack propagation process is similar for specimens with the same hole shape and is less affected by H.

Discussion

The peak stress of granite specimens with holes decreases in LE, R, C, and SE. Under dynamic loading, stress concentration causes instability and failure44. Linear boundaries are more vulnerable to stress concentration than circular arc boundaries. The LE specimen’s longer circular arc boundary, aligned with the impact direction, deforms into a shorter arc under external forces45, indicating a certain strength level. This behavior resembles the C specimen, resulting in a peak stress greater than R. The SE specimen’s shorter circular arc edge experiences less deformation, while its linear boundary is more compression-prone. Consequently, SE is more likely to develop cracks and stress concentration than R46, leading to the lowest peak stress among the specimens analyzed.

The dynamic strength of rock with holes under impact load may depend on various physical properties, such as elastic modulus (E), tensile strength (σt), cohesion (C), internal friction angle (Φ), stiffness ratio (K), specimen width (W), H, and the area of holes (S). However, there is no clear quantitative relationship between these factors and the dynamic strength of granite with holes. Conclusions

In this paper, SHPB tests were conducted on four-hole granite specimens (HS) under varying gas pressure (GP) and different distances between the borehole center and the contact boundary surface of the specimen and the incident bar (H). The granite’s mechanical properties, rock debris characteristics, and energy dissipation behaviors were analyzed. The main conclusions are as follows:

(1) After reaching the peak stress, The strain rebound of C and LE specimens is greater than that of R and SE. When the hole is positioned closer to the center of the specimen, the peak stress increases. The peak stress ranking is LE > C > R > SE. The influence of the three factors on peak stress is ranked as follows: HS > GS > H. As the strain rate and H increase, debris from the specimen transitions from block-shaped fragments to elongated strips. The LE specimen generates the most debris at the same GP and H, whereas the SE specimen produces the least.

(2) As GP increases from 0.14 MPa to 0.23 MPa, each specimen’s peak stress and energy input increase with a 1% rise in strain rate. The LE specimen shows the most stable changes, clearly demonstrating a strain rate effect and a quantitative relationship with the strain rate.

(3) The absorbed energy of specimens with different HS ranks as LE, C, R, and SE in descending order. The energy utilization rate of the specimens (LE, C, and R) shows a positive correlation with strain rate, while SE exhibits a negative correlation. Additionally, the energy absorption density of C, R, and LE increases with strain rate, whereas SE first decreases and then increases.

(4) The ratio of crack initiation stress to damage stress for granites with different HS is about 1:2. Main cracks include a penetrating crack parallel to the impact direction and symmetrical cracks at the hole’s edges. Compressive chain concentration occurs at the edges, while tensile chain concentration is at the position of macro cracks. The LE specimen shows lighter force chains, indicating lower stress concentration.

The results of this study have significant implications for understanding the dynamic fracture process of defective rock masses and guiding related geotechnical engineering design. In practical engineering, it can be used to accurately guide the design of support systems like bolts, especially in areas with high stress concentration, to optimize safety and efficiency.
