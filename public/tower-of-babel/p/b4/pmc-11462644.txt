The Influence of Structural Brain Changes on Cognition in the Context of Healthy Aging: Exploring Mediation Effects Through gBAT—The Graphical Brain Association Tool
Authors: Ida Rangus, Alex Teghipco, Sarah Newman‐Norlund, Roger Newman‐Norlund, Chris Rorden, Nicholas Riccardi, Sarah Wilson, Natalie Busby, Janina Wilmskoetter, Samaneh Nemati, Lumi Bakos, Julius Fridriksson, Leonardo Bonilha
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe contribution of age‐related structural brain changes to the well‐established link between aging and cognitive decline is not fully defined. While both age‐related regional brain atrophy and cognitive decline have been extensively studied, the specific mediating role of age‐related regional brain atrophy on cognitive functions is unclear. This study introduces an open‐source software tool with a graphical user interface that streamlines advanced whole‐brain mediation analyses, enabling researchers to systematically explore how the brain acts as a mediator in relationships between various behavioral and health outcomes. The tool is showcased by investigating regional brain volume as a mediator to determine the contribution of age‐related brain volume loss toward cognition in healthy aging. We analyzed regional brain volumes and cognitive testing data (Montreal Cognitive Assessment [MoCA]) from a cohort of 131 neurologically healthy adult participants (mean age 50 ± 20.8 years, range 20–79, 73% females) drawn from the Aging Brain Cohort Study at the University of South Carolina. Using our open‐source tool developed for evaluating brain‐behavior associations across the brain and optimized for exploring mediation effects, we conducted a series of mediation analyses using participant age as the predictor variable, total MoCA and MoCA subtest scores as the outcome variables, and regional brain volume as potential mediators. Age‐related atrophy within specific anatomical networks was found to mediate the relationship between age and cognition across multiple cognitive domains. Specifically, atrophy in bilateral frontal, parietal, and occipital areas, along with widespread subcortical regions mediated the effect of age on total MoCA scores. Various MoCA subscores were influenced by age through atrophy in distinct brain regions. These involved prefrontal regions, sensorimotor cortex, and parieto‐occipital areas for executive function subscores, prefrontal and temporo‐occipital regions, along with the caudate nucleus for attention and concentration subscores, frontal and parieto‐occipital areas, alongside connecting subcortical areas such as the optic tract for visuospatial subscores and frontoparietal areas for language subscores. Brain‐based mediation analysis offers a causal framework for evaluating the mediating role of brain structure on the relationship between age and cognition and provides a more nuanced understanding of cognitive aging than previously possible. By validating the applicability and effectiveness of this approach and making it openly available to the scientific community, we facilitate the exploration of causal mechanisms between variables mediated by the brain.

Introduction

The innate capacity of the human brain to correctly perform complex cognitive tasks diminishes with aging (Drag and Bieliauskas 2010; Harada, Natelson Love, and Triebel 2013). Age‐related cognitive decline occurs across multiple cognitive domains including episodic memory, processing speed, attention, and executive functions (Harada, Natelson Love, and Triebel 2013). Conversely, areas of cognition reliant on accumulated knowledge and experience, such as vocabulary and general information, are believed to remain relatively stable or even improve until at least the sixth decade (Murman 2015; Salthouse 2019). Factors such as education, general intelligence, and physical fitness are recognized as potential mitigators of age‐related cognitive decline (Mondini et al. 2022; Szcześniak et al. 2022).

Even within the confines of “normal brain aging,” a gradual decline in cognitive performance can disrupt daily functioning and diminish overall quality of life (Christiansen et al. 2019; Pan et al. 2015). Therefore, understanding factors that can mediate the known relationship between aging and cognition is important. While many studies have demonstrated associations between aging, brain atrophy, and lowered cognitive abilities (Drag and Bieliauskas 2010; Fletcher et al. 2018), they mostly take a correlational approach without examining the underlying mechanisms. Voxel‐based morphometry (VBM) has been instrumental in quantifying structural brain changes associated with aging and relating them to cognitive functions (Ashburner and Friston 2000; Good et al. 2001). For example, brain aging has been generally linked to whole‐brain reduction in gray matter volume, with frontoparietal, insular, and cerebellar areas showing the most pronounced volume loss (Abe et al. 2008; Good et al. 2001; Raz and Rodrigue 2006; Smith et al. 2020; Tisserand 2004). Furthermore, the decline in gray matter density in frontal, medial temporal, and parietal areas is associated with general cognitive decline in the elderly (Tisserand 2004). Moreover, executive dysfunction is associated with atrophy in prefrontal regions (Raz and Rodrigue 2006), and reduced performance in episodic memory is linked to atrophy in the medial temporal regions (Jessen et al. 2006).

Nonetheless, traditional neuroimaging approaches, such as VBM often examine correlations between brain structure and behavioral outcomes but fail to capture the mechanisms through which independent variables influence behavioral outcomes. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have demonstrated the formal statistical association between aging and cognition, mediated by brain structure, through brain‐wide regional‐level mediation analyses. Mediation analysis offers the possibility of moving beyond correlations described in previous research, enabling researchers to examine the mechanism through which one variable influences another (Bolin 2014; MacKinnon, Fairchild, and Fritz 2007). Recent neuroimaging studies have capitalized on this technique to reveal novel pathways, including brain–protein interactions impinging on memory in Alzheimer disease (Zhao, Li, and Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative. 2022), and brain–genetic links influencing psychiatric disorders (Bi et al. 2017). However, neuroimaging data present unique challenges for brain‐based mediation studies. Typically, the data are multimodal and high‐dimensional, marked by complex interrelationships between brain regions, and analyzed using sophisticated approaches and experimental designs, ultimately necessitating specialized modifications to conventional mediation analysis pipelines and methods (Lett et al. 2017; Li et al. 2022; Vuorre and Bolger 2018; Woo et al. 2015; Zhao, Li, and Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative. 2022). The difficulties of applying mediation analysis to brain data may, in some cases, lead researchers to focus on a limited set of brain regions or simplified whole‐brain measures in brain‐based mediation studies (Brugulat‐Serrat et al. 2020; Dougherty et al. 2020; He et al. 2021).

In this study, we leveraged a dataset comprising standardized cognitive assessments and neuroimaging to investigate the possibility that regional variation in brain volume mediates the known effect between aging and cognition in neurologically healthy adults. To achieve this, we present the graphical Brain Association Tool (gBAT), which simplifies exploring brain‐behavior associations across the brain. The gBAT introduces a graphical user interface (GUI) designed to help researchers import brain data, bin it by regions within a whole‐brain atlas to reduce the number of comparisons for testing, match this brain data with available behavioral variables, and perform association testing. The emphasis of gBAT is on mediation analysis; however, it provides tools for evaluating both linear and nonlinear statistical dependencies between brain and behavioral variables that can be used independently. This approach allows for not only the formal test of mediation effects between variables through their relationship with brain structure but also the examination of the method's effectiveness in discerning fine‐grained regional anatomical distribution of these effects.

Methods

Participants

Data from 131 participants from the ongoing ABC Study at the University of South Carolina (ABC@USC) (Newman‐Norlund et al. 2021) were included in this analysis. Individuals aged between 20 and 80 years without a prior history of neurodegenerative diseases or stroke were eligible for enrollment in the ABC@USC study. All participants who completed MRI and cognitive testing were included in the current analysis. The institutional review board at the USC approved the study, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant at enrollment. See Table 1 for demographic information.

Cognitive Assessment

Cognitive assessment was performed using the MoCA test (Nasreddine et al. 2005). MoCA is a screening tool sensitive to mild cognitive impairment, providing an overview of an individual's general cognitive level while also capturing impairments within specific cognitive domains. We derived the following six subscores, as proposed in prior research (Kim et al. 2021; Mueller and Cammermeyer 2023): (1) memory subscore: the sum of the words recalled with no clue multiplied by three, words recalled with category cue multiplied by two and words recalled with multiple choice cue multiplied by one (range: 0–15 points); (2) executive subscore: the sum of the trail making test, clock drawing, digit span, letter A tapping task, serial sevens subtraction, language fluency task, and abstraction task (range: 0–13 points); (3) attention and concentration subscore: the sum of the total words recalled in the first and second trial out of 10 words, digit span, letter A tapping test, serial sevens subtraction and sentence repetition (range: 0–18 points); (4) language subscore: the sum of the naming task, sentence repetition and verbal fluency task (range: 0–6 points); (5) visuospatial subscore: the sum of cube drawing, clock drawing and naming (0–7 points); and (6) orientation subscore: total points achieved in the orientation task (0–6).

Imaging Data Collection and Preprocessing

MRI data were obtained using a Siemens Prisma 3 T scanner, with a 20‐channel head coil at the McCausland Center for Brain Imaging (MCBI) located at Prisma Health Hospital, Columbia, SC. A complete MRI scanning protocol for the ABC Study was published previously (Newman‐Norlund et al. 2021). For the current analysis, we utilized the T1‐weighted (T1w) MP‐RAGE images with following acquisition parameters: 1 mm isotropic voxels, 256 × 256 matrix size, 9° flip angle, and 192‐slice sequence with repetition time = 2250 ms, inversion time = 925 ms, and echo time = 4.11 ms. T1w images of each participant were skull stripped and normalized to the MNI152 template using our publicly available nii_preprocess pipeline (Rorden, EmilieMcKinnon, and Hanayik 2020), relying on nonlinear registration implemented in SPM12 (Ashburner et al. 2021). Segmentation of T1w images was performed with the Computational Anatomy Toolbox 12 toolbox for VBM and default settings were used to generate independent whole‐brain volumetric estimates for gray matter, white matter, and cerebrospinal fluid (Gaser et al. 2024). These maps were subsequently smoothed using an 8‐mm full‐width at half‐maximum (FWHM) kernel. Volumes were collapsed over voxels within each of 189 regions of interest (ROI) in the Johns Hopkins University (JHU) neuroanatomical atlas, which combines cortical and subcortical regions including white matter tracts (Faria et al. 2012). White matter structures aggregated information from the white matter tissue map, whereas other regions were based on information in the gray matter map. This procedure yielded a single average volumetric value for each ROI, in each participant.

To ensure high data quality and integrity throughout the brain image preprocessing pipeline, we implemented several quality control measures. Structural scans were inspected in real‐time by scanning personnel, with any scans exhibiting severe artifacts repeated. For preprocessing, we used our openly available pipeline, nii_preprocess (accessible on GitHub at https://github.com/neurolabusc/nii_preprocess). This pipeline automatically generates graphical representations of the final output files, which are reviewed by experienced researchers. Through this visual inspection, any significant inconsistencies introduced during normalization are promptly identified. Collectively, these quality control procedures enhanced the reliability of our neuroimaging data, ensuring a robust foundation for subsequent analyses.

Mediation Analysis Using Volumetric Data

Mediation analysis is a powerful tool that can help clarify whether the effect of an independent variable on the dependent variable is transmitted through a third variable—a mediator (Bolin 2014; MacKinnon, Fairchild, and Fritz 2007). We carried out a series of mediation analyses to investigate the intermediary role of regional brain volume as a potential mediator in the relationship between age and MoCA scores. That is, independent mediation models were constructed for each ROI, with age used as the predictor and MoCA as the outcome. While we were particularly interested in the total MoCA scores that previous work has linked to mild cognitive impairment (Nasreddine et al. 2005), our analysis was also carried out for each MoCA subscore, representing different cognitive domains along which impairment could manifest (see Figure 1 for schematic overview). The total score and each subscore were used to generate a separate whole‐brain statistical map reflecting regional mediation of the effect of age on MoCA.

gBAT

We developed the gBAT, a novel MATLAB‐based software package designed to perform various brain‐wide association analyses using brain images and behavioral variables. In this study, we specifically evaluated gBAT for mediation analyses using structural brain images. However, the toolbox also supports a range of other methods, such as correlation, partial correlation, and distance correlation, applicable across various neuroimaging modalities.

gBAT integrates custom MATLAB wrappers with a user‐friendly GUI, making complex neuroimaging analyses more accessible to researchers across various expertise levels. This tool uses CANlab's Mediation Toolbox (https://github.com/canlab/MediationToolbox (Wager et al. 2008)), which provides robust statistical frameworks for building and evaluating mediation models. The GUI leverages MATLAB's Image Processing Toolbox for efficient loading and processing of neuroimaging data.

We have made gBAT openly available at https://github.com/alexteghipco/gBAT, encouraging collaborative use and further development within the neuroscience community. The tool's intuitive interface guides users through the entire workflow, from data input to results visualization, facilitating brain‐wide association analyses without requiring extensive programming knowledge. Furthermore, we provide a user manual with this manuscript and offer a comprehensive step‐by‐step guide in our online documentation to assist users in effectively navigating the software.

To perform analyses using gBAT, neuroimaging data and behavioral variables are required as inputs. Imaging data can be provided as NIFTI files or extracted from MATLAB's own .mat file structure if the NIFTI's have already been organized into a matrix and saved in this format. Behavioral data can be supplied in typical spreadsheet formats (e.g., .csv, .xlsx, .txt, .dat, or extracted from a .mat file that contains these data), accompanied by participant identifiers. These identifiers are used to search for related brain imaging data, by finding identical lowercase string embedded anywhere within the file name of the imaging data. The gBAT provides intermediary files with meta‐data at different steps of the analysis pipeline, including after behavioral and brain data are matched. The GUI pauses the analysis, providing users opportunity to check or correct any ambiguous information (e.g., multiple NIFTI files matching a particular participant identifier). A NIFTI atlas file can be loaded into gBAT for binning neuroimaging data by regions, where all voxels belonging to a particular region (i.e., whole integer in the atlas file) are averaged together. In this study, we used the JHU cortical–subcortical brain atlas (Faria et al. 2012), which comprises 189 distinct regions at a resolution of 1.5 × 1.5 × 1.5 mm and dimensions of 113 × 137 × 113 mm. As the focus of our analysis was on gray and white matter volumes, regions within the ventricles were excluded, leaving 178 brain regions. Furthermore, the substantia nigra, an exceedingly small region of the atlas (covering only 106 voxels bilaterally), was removed as no individual‐specific gray matter volume map extended that far subcortically. This resulted in 176 brain regions. The JHU atlas is readily available within the gBAT package, along with several alternative atlases. Alternatives include the Schaeffer multiresolution atlas, which may be more appropriate for studies interested in functional brain units that capture both local and global connectivity patterns, or which require a more specific number of regions for analysis (Schaefer et al. 2018). If a more traditional parcellation of functional connectivity data is required, AICHA or the homotopic functional connectivity atlas is provided (Joliot et al. 2015). Alternative structure‐based atlases include the Harvard‐Oxford atlas based on macroscopic anatomical landmarks (Makris et al. 2023), the Julich‐Brain histological atlas based on microstructural properties (Amunts et al. 2020), and the XTRACT Human Connectome Project probabilistic tractography atlas (Warrington et al. 2020). Additionally, gBAT offers users the flexibility to incorporate their preferred atlases by simply uploading a custom atlas along with an optional text file detailing the names and tissue categorizations of ROIs. This feature facilitates tailored analyses while maintaining the tool's versatility across various research contexts, for example, enabling native space analysis.

Certain structural atlases contain regions classified as white matter and gray matter and it is possible for a neuroimaging preprocessing method to output different images for gray and white matter (e.g., as in VBM, where tissue types may be allowed to overlap). The gBAT permits two sets of NIFTI files to be loaded in to determine which set should be used for the regional average. Masks can be loaded in to exclude certain portions of the imaging data from analysis, or thresholds can be supplied to apply to the group‐averaged NIFTI image (i.e., removing voxels under a certain mean value or removing voxels for which there is no data or 0 s for a certain number of participants).

While the gBAT emphasizes mediation analysis, other statistical methods are implemented to supplement mediation results. For example, linear correlations (Pearson or Spearman) can be performed, including partial correlations, using MATLAB's Statistics and machine learning toolbox. The gBAT also permits capturing both linear and nonlinear statistical dependencies using distance correlation (Székely and Rizzo 2009, 2013). Nonlinear relationships can mask or distort true mediation effects and linear models may lead to incorrect conclusions about the mediation relationship. We implement our own version of partial distance correlation (Székely and Rizzo 2014) as well, permitting the evaluation of nonlinear relationships adjusting for the effects of other variables. To the extent of our knowledge, there is no other code in MATLAB publicly available for performing partial distance correlation. Our code uses the recursive formula, calculating the partial distance correlation by conditioning on one variable at a time, recursively. Using a slightly different methodology, it achieves identical results to an existing Python package for partial distance correlation that conditions on all variables simultaneously: https://pypi.org/project/dcor/. Finally, gBAT allows the user to perform either bias‐corrected or nonbias‐corrected bootstrapping for mediation or correlation analyses, as the latter may be preferable in some cases (Tibbe and Montoya 2022). Users can correct significance for multiple comparisons using false discovery rate (FDR) (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995), or in the case of voxel‐wise analyses, cluster‐based permutation testing. Finally, our tool enables investigators to directly visualize mediation results on the brain figures.

Robust ordinary least squares regression was used to estimate mediation models while diminishing the influence of any potential outliers and addressing the bimodal distribution of the predictor (age; see Figure 2A). In addition, path significance was evaluated nonparametrically with bias‐corrected bootstrapping (1000 bootstrap samples). Total intracranial volume was used as a nuisance variable within each mediation model. The resulting p values for all model paths were adjusted for multiple comparisons using FDR correction (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). Mediation analyses were thresholded using a uniform 0.01 FDR threshold.

Our analyses addressed the specific hypothesis that aging is associated with reductions in brain volume, which mediate the effect of age on cognitive function. That is, to the extent that there were relationships in our data, we anticipated them to be inverse when relating our predictor (age) to mediators (brain volume within ROIs) or the outcome variable (MoCA). Consequently, we expected higher brain volume to positively impact cognitive outcome variables, so mediation analysis would estimate negative mediation effect path estimates. Thus, although our analysis was two‐tailed, we masked out effects in the inverse direction, based on the previous literature relating reduced cognition with age (Drag and Bieliauskas 2010; Harada, Natelson Love, and Triebel 2013), reduced brain volume with age (Abe et al. 2008; Good et al. 2001; Raz and Rodrigue 2006; Smith et al. 2020), and increased brain volume with better cognitive performance (Nave et al. 2019; Royle et al. 2013).

Results

MoCA Results and Direct Effects of Age on MoCA and Brain Volume

Consistent with prior work (Tucker‐Drob 2011), we observed significant Spearman's correlations between scores on MoCA subtests and the total MoCA score measure (Figure 2A). The only subscore that did not significantly correlate with the total score was orientation (p > 0.05), which had markedly low variability in our cohort (Table 1) and demonstrated few significant associations with other variables (Figure 2A). Despite an overall strong correlation with total MoCA scores, not all subscores were intercorrelated. The memory subscore showed the most specific relationships and was significantly correlated with attention and concentration as well as executive subscores (p < 0.01; see Figure 2 for specific information on p values and coefficients). In contrast, the executive subscore showed a significant correlation with all other subscores (p < 0.05; Figure 2A), indicating that executive function may be commonly recruited among the MoCA tasks. The attention and concentration subscore was significantly associated with all subscores except orientation (p < 0.01; Figure 2A). Visuospatial and language subscores were significantly associated with all other subscores except memory and orientation (p < 0.01; Figure 2A).

While the total MoCA scores significantly correlated with age (p < 0.0001), some subscores did not (i.e., executive, language, and orientation subscores; p > 0.05). The relationships between all MoCA scores considered in our mediation analyses are presented in Figure 2A. Furthermore, age was not correlated with our nuisance variable, total intracranial volume (p > 0.05). Intracranial volume was associated with the total MoCA score (p < 0.01), as well as executive (p < 0.01) and language (p < 0.01) subscores (Figure 2A). At the same time, age was associated with total gray matter volume, r (129) = −0.6, p < 0.000001, total white matter volume, r (129) = −0.28, p < 0.01, and total cerebrospinal fluid volume, r (129) = 0.6, p < 0.000001.

Critically, nonparametric approaches to mediation analysis, such as the one we have implemented here, empirically estimate the distribution of the indirect effect and relax many assumptions traditionally associated with mediation models (e.g., addressing bias and skewness of the indirect effect estimates). Consequently, they are more sensitive to the influence exerted by a predictor through the mediator, making them more robust for identifying subtle mediation effects—for example, when there are no direct effects of the predictor on the outcome variable (c path) or if other path effects are insignificant when considered in isolation (Bolin 2014).

Given the observed relationships between age and gross measures of tissue volume, we anticipated age would significantly predict volume across many ROIs. This is illustrated in Figure 2B, which shows the a path across all of our mediation analyses, or the parameters derived from robust linear regression, where age is used to predict variations in regional brain volume. Older age was associated with larger decreases in volume within medial white matter, followed by dorsal brain regions, particularly sensorimotor cortex and adjacent frontoparietal regions. We observed a stable relationship between age and regional brain volumes, with no difference in a path parameter estimates as a function of changing the dependent variable. However, here too, the uncertainty around estimates differed depending on the dependent variable, as this altered the overall model's error structure. Consequently, we present effects that were significant across all subscores (Figure 2B; FDR < 0.01), and note that the implicated regions were highly similar across subscores. Brain tissue reductions in widespread brain regions were identified as key mediators in the relationship between age and executive subscore (p (FDR) < 0.01). Particularly robust effects were evident in bilateral prefrontal regions extending dorsally into the sensorimotor cortex and parieto‐occipital areas. The effect of age on attention and concentration subscore was mediated by atrophy in bilateral frontal and temporo‐occipital regions, but the strongest effect was observed in the caudate nucleus, followed by dorsal anterior cingulate gyrus and pars triangularis of the inferior frontal gyrus. Lower parameter estimates were observed for temporal regions (p (FDR) < 0.01). Intriguingly, the superior temporal gyrus, including the auditory cortex, did not show any effect. These findings are visually presented in Figure 4B.

The effect of age on the visuospatial subscore was mediated by regional volumes within bilateral frontal and parieto‐occipital areas. Parietal areas exhibitied the strongest effects overall, particularly superior parietal gyrus, followed by frontal regions, mainly posterior middle frontal gyrus as well as the optic tract, responsible for transmitting visual input information to the occipital areas. Temporal regions also showed significant effects at lower parameter estimates, p (FDR) < 0.01. Notably, sensorimotor cortex did not show any significant effect, visualized in Figure 4C.

The effect of age on the language subscore was mediated by regional brain volume in posterior frontal and inferior parietal areas. The most prominent mediating influence was discerned in the right middle frontal gyrus, but left inferior frontal gyrus (pars opercularis in particular) and precentral gyrus also showed an effect (p (FDR) < 0.01). Additional significant mediation was observed in bilateral parietal areas, encompassing the angular gyrus as well as cingulate gyrus, and precuneus. Language subscore results are visually represented in Figure 4D.

The effect of age on the memory subscore was not mediated by any regions when correcting for multiple comparisons at p (FDR) < 0.01. However, we report in Table 4 that a lower threshold of p (FDR) < 0.05 yields a single mediator—the left uncinate fasciculus, a white matter tract connecting anterior temporal lobe, including the hippocampus, to prefrontal cortex. Given the low variability in orientation subscores, with most participants reaching maximum scores, we found no mediation effects for this subscore.

Discussion

This study demonstrated the utility of our novel brain‐based mediation tool, gBAT, by showing that age‐related regional brain atrophy mediates the relationship between age and cognition in neurologically healthy adults. This innovative neuroimaging software streamlines the process for investigators to test the formal statistical mediation role of brain ROIs between continuous variables, such as age and cognitive performance. By automating complex analyses and providing intuitive interface, gBAT enables researchers to efficiently explore intricate brain‐behavior relationships across various domains of neuroscience.

Our findings not only validate the tool's effectiveness but also contribute to the growing body of knowledge on cognitive aging. We observed a direct influence of age on various cognitive domains and an inverse correlation between age and regional brain volume, particularly in the medial subcortical matter, followed by sensorimotor, prefrontal, and parietal regions. These results align with prior research indicating that the most pronounced brain volume loss due to aging occurs in a network of medial subcortical structures, with substantial volume reductions also centered around the sensorimotor cortex (Hafkemeijer et al. 2014; Liu et al. 2017). The mediation effects of age‐related brain volume loss on the relationship between age and cognition exhibited a nuanced spatial distribution, with robust mediation effects consistently observed in bilateral prefrontal regions, underscoring their known vulnerability to aging and their critical role in cognitive functions (Albinet et al. 2012; Bosnes et al. 2022).

Key mediators of total MoCA scores, including regional brain volumes in the left pars opercularis of the inferior frontal gyrus and in the angular gyrus, align with established literature emphasizing their pivotal roles as key hubs within several large‐scale brain networks such as the default mode network, central executive network, and semantic processing network, which are vital for cognitive control, information retention and manipulation as well as reasoning and problem‐solving (Higo et al. 2011; Nani et al. 2021; Seghier 2013). Notably, while medial temporal regions demonstrated consistent mediation effects across MoCA subscores, these effects were relatively lower compared to frontoparietal regions. This pattern suggests a potential differential vulnerability to age‐related cognitive decline, with medial temporal regions demonstrating greater resilience to neurocognitive aging within physiological confines. Such relative resilience could be attributed to the fact that significant medial temporal atrophy is more characteristic of pathological neurodegenerative processes rather than physiological brain aging (Celsis 2000). In line with this assumption, memory functions facilitated by the medial temporal regions were not significantly impacted by age‐related brain atrophy in our study.

Several MoCA subscores were influenced by age through the mediation of spatially distinct brain regions known to be involved in the cognitive subdomains that those subscores represent. For example, executive function and attention/concentration subtests exhibited involvement of widespread brain regions, including prefrontal, parietal, temporal, and occipital regions, highlighting the intricate network interactions central to these cognitive processes. Unlike most other cognitive domains, language, especially vocabulary, tends to remain unaffected by aging and may even improve with age (Kemper et al. 2010; Salthouse 2019). Our findings corroborated this observation, as we did not find any significant direct effect of age on language abilities as measured by the MoCA language subtest. Nonetheless, age influenced language subtest through the mediation effect of critical brain regions associated with language processing concentrated around bilateral posterior frontal and parietal regions.

The main contribution of this study is methodological. While our findings align with established knowledge on age‐related regional brain atrophy and cognitive decline, they also underscore the effectiveness and relevance of testing statistical mediation across the brain. We introduce a tool that streamlines the mediation analysis process for researchers working with neuroimaging data. By establishing the link between well‐known age‐related brain changes and cognitive decline, we have demonstrated the utility of our brain‐based association analysis tool and its potential for broader applications in neuroimaging and cognitive neuroscience. Our successful mapping of the impact of age‐related brain atrophy on cognitive function lays the groundwork for future studies to further validate the tool's robustness and reproducibility across diverse, independent samples. Such validation will reinforce the tool's value in advancing our understanding of brain‐behavior relationship across various contexts and populations.

Traditional VBM analyses typically employ mass‐univariate statistical methods, examining the direct association between brain structure and cognitive performance (Ashburner and Friston 2000; Chételat et al. 2005; Tisserand 2004). In contrast, our mediation analysis approach offers a more nuanced understanding of the interplay between age, brain structure, and cognition. By formally testing the mediating role of regional brain volumes, we can elucidate the indirect effects of age on cognitive performance, mediated through specific brain regions. This approach has several advantages over traditional VBM analyses. First, the mediation approach allows for the identification of brain regions that statistically mediate the age–cognition relationship, providing insights into the underlying neural mechanisms of cognitive aging, unlike VBM‐based analyses that identify associations. Second, it enables the quantification of relative contributions of differential brain regions to cognitive decline, potentially guiding targeted interventions or diagnostic strategies. Third, the brain mediation analysis framework can be extended beyond the age–cognition relationship, facilitating investigations into the mediating roles of brain structure in various neuropsychiatric conditions or cognitive processes. Here, we tested the relationship between age, probabilistic maps of brain volume and cognition. However, these variables could be replaced by other continuous variables and the brain maps could be any voxel‐wise scalar map in standard space.

To facilitate the adoption and implementation of this program, we have made the GUI‐based program available as freeware to the scientific community. This open‐source distribution not only enhances the transparency and reproducibility of our research but also encourages other research groups to adopt and further develop this method for their specific investigations.

It is crucial to acknowledge the limitations of this study, such as the cross‐sectional design and the potential influence of confounding variables. Future longitudinal studies could enhance our understanding of the dynamic influence of regional brain volume on the relationship between age and cognition. While acknowledging the practicality of MoCA as a cognitive screening tool, it is essential to recognize that it does not provide detailed information about cognitive functions. Additionally, despite efforts to exclude participants with a history of neurodegenerative diseases, the potential presence of undiagnosed cognitive deficits in the participants cannot be ruled out, challenging the characterization of the study population as “neurologically healthy.” Nonetheless, we believe that the study adequately captures the average community‐dwelling population, where individuals unaware of any underlying neurodegenerative brain disease may also be found. We also acknowledge the limitations of the mediation tool that we have introduced, recognizing the work that continues to be done to adapt mediation to neuroimaging data using more sophisticated pipelines. Cutting‐edge methods now exist to map multivariate mediation relationships and manage the high‐dimensionality of neuroimaging data in mediation analysis more effectively (Lett et al. 2017; Li et al. 2022; Vuorre and Bolger 2018; Woo et al. 2015; Zhao, Li, and Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative. 2022). Our tool uses a more conventional mediation approach, aligning with our goal of making this type of analysis more accessible for researchers with neuroimaging data. However, we appreciate the importance of these recent advancements and anticipate enhancing our tool to support more complex modeling scenarios in the future.

In conclusion, this study provides evidence for the utility and efficacy of brain‐based mediation analysis approach in elucidating mechanisms underlying complex phenomena such as age‐related cognitive decline. The availability of the GUI program as freeware further enhances the impact and accessibility of this research, encouraging widespread adoption and future advancements in the field.
