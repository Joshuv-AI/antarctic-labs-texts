An HDAC4-specific PROTAC degrader achieves radiation sensitization by enhancing ferroptosis in lung cancer
Authors: Chun Cheng, Longhua Sun, Jiaxue Yang, Zhiliang Liu, Fan Zhang, Lingyan Qiu, Meng Yu, Ting Wang, Jianxin Huang, Li Wang, Wei-Ping Min, Tingxiu Xiang, Kaishun Hu, Yi Sang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPost-translational modifications (PTMs) play a critical role in cancer radioresistance, yet how they regulate ferroptosis to influence radiotherapy response remains poorly understood. Here, we show that HDAC4 promotes radiation resistance in lung cancer by inhibiting ferroptosis. Through a CRISPR screen in patient-derived organoids, we identify HDAC4 as a key mediator. Mechanistically, HDAC4 acts as an E3 SUMO ligase that SUMOylates MBD1, preventing its ubiquitination and degradation. Stabilized MBD1 represses TP53 and CYP1A1 transcription, thereby suppressing lipid reactive oxygen species formation and ferroptosis. To target HDAC4, we develop a proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC), TP1, based on tasquinimod, which binds and degrades HDAC4 specifically, as confirmed by surface plasmon resonance and proteomics. TP1 exhibits stronger radiosensitizing effects than tasquinimod in lung cancer organoids and xenograft models. Our findings uncover HDAC4 as a suppressor of ferroptosis in radioresistance and present a PROTAC-based strategy to enhance radiotherapy efficacy.Subject terms: Non-small-cell lung cancer, Radiotherapy, Sumoylation

Introduction

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide, and the 5-year survival rate is still less than 16%1. Radiation therapy (RT) is an important part of a multimodal treatment approach for lung cancer and provides both curative and palliative strategies for lung cancer management2. Resistance to conventional RT is involved in RT failure, metastasis, cancer relapse, and poor prognosis in lung cancer patients3. A main factor related to radioresistance is the deregulation of the posttranslational modifications of proteins inside tumor cells that are responsible for the activation of DNA repair pathways and the inhibition of apoptosis4. For example, MRE11 ubiquitination by the E3 ligase RNF126 activates the DNA damage response and confers resistance to radiotherapy in triple-negative breast cancer5. Importantly, radioresistance remains a challenge.

In addition to DNA damage, radiation also produces reactive oxygen species, which can lead to the oxidation of biomolecules, such as lipids6. While this effect has largely remained unexplored, ferroptosis, an iron-dependent and lipid peroxidation-driven cell death cascade, has recently been identified7,8. Increasing evidence suggests that ferroptosis plays an important role in various biological and disease processes9. Lipid peroxidation is a process in which oxidants such as free radicals or nonradical species attack lipids containing carbon–carbon double bond(s) on cell membranes, especially polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs); the process involves hydrogen abstraction from a carbon, followed by oxygen insertion, resulting in lipid peroxyl radicals and hydroperoxides10. The selenium-containing enzyme GPX4 is currently recognized as a key repressor of ferroptosis, and its activity depends on the production of glutathione from the activation of the cystine-glutamate antiporter SLC7A1111. Ionizing radiation (IR) can not only promote ferroptosis by inducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production but also induce the upregulation of SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression, resulting in negative feedback, ultimately leading to radiation resistance, and the combination of ferroptosis inducers significantly increases the killing effect of IR on tumor cells12. Given the considerable degree of mystery surrounding this process, we are eager to investigate whether protein posttranslational modifications are involved in the resistance mechanism to IR-induced ferroptosis, an exploration that promises to be intriguing.

Histone deacetylase 4 (HDAC4) is a member of the class IIa HDACs, which play a role in the tumorigenesis of multiple cancers13–15. Despite having minimal deacetylase enzymatic activity towards acetyl-lysine16, HDAC4 can function as a platform to coordinate the activity of class I HDACs16. Interestingly, tasquinimod, a specific inhibitor of HDAC4, has been shown to effectively inhibit the growth and metastasis of tumor cells both in vitro and in vivo15. Additionally, HDAC4 belongs to the SUMO E3 ligase family and can directly SUMOylate IκBα, AR, LXRα, and MEF217–20. However, no investigation has been conducted to elucidate the relationship between the E3 SUMO activity of HDAC4 and radioresistance in lung cancer or other tumors.

Proteolysis targeting chimeras (PROTACs) are an approach used to induce protein degradation via the ubiquitin proteasome system (UPS). PROTACs comprise an E3 ligase receptor protein and its binding molecules, along with target protein ligands and specially designed “linker” structures. Compared with traditional occupancy-based inhibitors, PROTACs offer several advantages with respect to dosing precision, side effect management, drug resistance prevention, and the modulation of ‘undruggable’ targets21,22. Multiple PROTACs have been developed to overcome drug resistance in NSCLC23, but further research is needed to determine whether PROTACs can serve as sensitizers for lung cancer radiotherapy.

In this work, we combined a CRISPR library and lung cancer organoids to screen and identify key posttranslational modification-related proteins involved in radioresistance. We reveal that HDAC4 promotes resistance to radiotherapy by promoting ferroptosis resistance. Mechanistically, HDAC4 SUMOylates and stabilizes MBD1 to inhibit p53 and CYP1A1 expression, leading to the inhibition of lipid ROS formation. Additionally, we design and synthesiz anHDAC4-targeted PROTAC that promotes radiosensitization by enhancing ferroptosis in lung cancer.

Results

Surgically resected primary lung adenocarcinoma tissues from patients were processed to establish organoids

Compared with traditional cell lines, patient-derived organoids provide a more physiologically relevant model for the discovery of therapeutic targets24,25. Seven surgically resected lung adenocarcinomas (LUADs), which were collected from Jiangxi Cancer Center, were processed for organoid establishment (Supplementary Fig. 1a). We grew organoids in advanced DMEM/F12 basal medium with additional supplements, as reported by Ming-Sound Tsao et al.25(Supplementary Table 1). In terms of our success rates, 86% (6/7) of our dissociated LUAD tissue successfully initiated organoid culture, which was defined as organoid formation upon plating at passage zero (Supplementary Fig. 1b). To compare the morphological and histological features of lung cancer organoids (LCOs) with those of their original lung cancer tissues, we performed hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis. Like their parental cancer tissue, LCOs derived from adenocarcinoma tissue produced acinar or large glandular patterns and retained the expression of the lung adenocarcinoma markers thyroid transcription factor 1 (TTF-1) and cytokeratin 7 (CK7). Ki67, a tumor proliferation marker, was also evaluated, and the results revealed that at least 30% of the organoids were Ki67 positive (Supplementary Fig. 1b–d). We also established normal bronchial organoids (NBOs), which were evaluated via IHC. The results demonstrated that the NBOs expressed the markers TP63 and KRT5, which are markers of normal lung basal cells (Supplementary Fig. 1e). Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was used to evaluate the genome profile concordance of LCO-1-6 organoids to their source, and the WES data revealed that the spectrum of mutations was highly concordant between LCO-1-6 organoids and their matched patient tumors (Supplementary Fig. 1f, g and Supplementary Data 1), indicating that the culture conditions did not destabilize the cancer genomes. Next, to compare whether the radiation resistance of these six lung cancer organoids differed, we treated the organoids with 9 Gy of IR and then stained them with SYTOX Green, which was used to distinguish dead cells from live cells. The results showed that LCO-4 organoids had potentially stronger radiation resistance (Fig. 1a, b). Overall, our data indicate that our panel of LUAD organoids closely recapitulated the histology and genomics of patient tumors and could be used to discover or validate potential radioresistance targets.

A CRISPR screen revealed that HDAC4 serves as a critical driver of radioresistance in lung cancer

CRISPR-based screens provide a robust platform for unbiased screening and functional genomics in various fields26, including radiobiology. In this context, the typical outcomes of such screens consist of ranked gene lists that confer sensitivity or resistance to radiation therapy. To elucidate the mechanisms that enable cancer cells to resist radiation in lung cancer, we performed a pooled CRISPR screen (n = 1) to analyze the influence of 903 posttranslational modification-related regulatory proteins (Supplementary Data 2) in LCO-1 and LCO-4 organoids. Two organoid populations were not irradiated or were irradiated at 9 Gy and were cultured in vitro for 10 days. Then, genomic DNA was acquired to determine the sgRNA representation via deep sequencing (Fig. 1c, d). In our screening, among the top 20 downregulated genes, HDAC4, SUV39H1 and KCNB2 were common to both organoids (Fig. 1e). Notably, the reduced levels of sgRNAs targeting HDAC4 attracted our interest because of the ambiguous link between HDAC4 and radiosensitivity. Tissue arrays revealed a notable increase—2.14-fold—in the protein level of HDAC4 in lung cancer tissues compared with that in normal lung cells (4.29 ± 2.15 vs. 2.00 ± 1.24, respectively) (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c). Surprisingly, HDAC4 was expressed at higher levels in the LCO-1 and LCO-4 organoids than in the other organoids (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Further investigations were conducted to determine whether ionizing radiation affects the expression pattern of HDAC4. Lung cancer organoids were exposed to a dose equivalent to 9 Gy and then assessed via immunofluorescence (IF) staining 24 h after treatment. The results revealed a significant increase in the expression level of HDAC4 in 5 of the 6 organoids following exposure to irradiation (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 2e). Furthermore, immunohistochemical analysis revealed that in 2 out of 3 paired clinical samples obtained from individuals who underwent radiotherapeutic intervention, HDAC4 levels significantly increased following radiotherapeutic intervention (Supplementary Fig. 2f, g). These observations collectively imply an influential role for HDAC4 as an orchestrator that mediates resistance to radiation therapy.

We thus explored the physiological function of HDAC4 in the presence of IR. First, HDAC4 was silenced in LCO-1 and LCO-4 organoids by sgRNA (Supplementary Fig. 2h), and then, after treatment with different doses of IR, we calculated the number of organoids and found that silencing HDAC4 decreased the number of organoids (Fig. 1g, h and Supplementary Fig. 2i). Moreover, SYTOX Green staining revealed that silencing HDAC4 resulted in increased cell death (Fig. 1i, j, and Supplementary Fig. 2j), suggesting that silencing HDAC4 in LCO-1 and LCO-4 organoids significantly promoted radiotherapy sensitization. To strengthen our conclusion, we generated an HDAC4 knockout (KO) subclone from A549 cells with a single guide RNA through CRISPR/Cas9-based KO technology (Supplementary Fig. 2k, l). As expected, the HDAC4 KO subclones presented greater radiosensitization in the clonogenic survival assays (Supplementary Fig. 2m, n).

We aimed to determine whether the E3 SUMOylation ligase activity or the deacetylase activity of HDAC4 regulates radioresistance. First, on the basis of the endogenous expression of HDAC4 in six LCOs (Supplementary Fig. 2d), we generated LCO-3 organoids overexpressing wild-type (WT) HDAC4, mutant HDAC4 (H803A, D840N) without histone deacetylase activity27 or mutant HDAC4 (C292S) without SUMOylation activity17 (Fig. 1k). After irradiation, there were more organoids overexpressing WT HDAC4 and mutant HDAC4 (H803A, D840N) than mutant HDAC4 (C292S), as determined from data from organoid clonogenic survival assays (Fig. 1l, m). Taken together, these results indicate that the deacetylase activity of HDAC4 contributes only partially to radioresistance in lung cancer cells, which is predominantly driven by its E3 SUMO ligase activity.

HDAC4 promotes radioresistance by inhibiting ferroptosis through its E3 SUMO ligase activity

It has been reported that HDAC4 can regulate the DNA damage response to facilitate cell survival28. However, whether HDAC4 also governs cell survival after IR treatment through other mechanisms remains unclear. Therefore, we investigated whether HDAC4 regulates ferroptosis resulting from the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by radiation, which leads to lipid oxidation and subsequently induces ferroptosis29. As shown in Fig. 2a–e and Supplementary Fig. 3a–d, IR treatment led to a significant increase in the content of cellular lipid ROS, as determined by flow cytometry using the fluorescent probe C11-BODIPY, a membrane-targeted lipid sensor dye, and knocking down HDAC4 resulted in an increase in the lipid ROS content, indicating that under IR treatment, HDAC4 knockdown substantially increased the IR-induced accumulation of lipid ROS. In addition, we monitored morphological changes in mitochondria, another ferroptotic event, through transmission electron microscopy. Consistent with the lipid ROS results, we observed shrunken mitochondria, increased membrane density, and ruptured outer mitochondrial membrane following HDAC4 knockdown, which are characteristic of ferroptosis30(Fig. 2f). To further evaluate the role of mitochondrial damage in lipid ROS production resulting from HDAC4 knockdown, we employed a mitochondrion-targeted antioxidant (Mito-TEMPO) and found that Mito-TEMPO alleviated lipid ROS production caused by HDAC4 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 3e–h). These data suggest that HDAC4 knockdown may facilitate lipid ROS production induced by mitochondrial damage. Moreover, we treated lung cancer cells with ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), a specific inhibitor of ferroptosis31. Notably, Fer-1 treatment completely reversed HDAC4-induced lipid ROS accumulation both with and without IR treatment (Fig. 2g–j). Thus, under IR treatment, HDAC4 knockdown markedly amplified IR-induced lipid ROS.

To strengthen our conclusion, we detected lipid ROS in both the HDAC4 wild-type (WT) and KO subclones of A549 cells. As expected, the HDAC4 KO subclones presented greater IR-induced lipid ROS accumulation than did the WT subclone (Supplementary Fig. 3i, j). Next, we investigated whether ectopic expression of the HDAC4 protein in cells could diminish IR-induced ferroptosis. The SUMOylation and deacetylation of HDAC4 are associated with tumor progression20,32. We thus analyzed the contribution of SUMOylation and deacetylation to the HDAC4-mediated suppression of ferroptosis by overexpressing WT HDAC4, mutant HDAC4 (H803A, D840N) without histone deacetylase activity or mutant HDAC4 (C292S) without SUMOylation activity. In addition, base synonymous mutation of the PAM sequences of these three recombinant plasmids was performed to ensure that the sgRNA could not recognize these three plasmids. Although the ectopic WT and mutant (H803A, D840N) HDAC4 proteins decreased lipid ROS accumulation and cell death compared with those in the control group, we did not observe any changes in lipid ROS accumulation or cell death caused by IR during SUMOylation-induced enzymatic inactivation (Fig. 2k–n). These data suggest that the E3 SUMO ligase activity of HDAC4 is essential for inhibiting ferroptosis.

HDAC4 catalyses the SUMOylation of MBD1

To identify the direct targets of HDAC4-mediated inhibition of ferroptosis, owing to the lack of DNA binding ability of HDAC4, we performed tandem affinity purification using A549 cells stably expressing a triple epitope (an S protein tag, a Flag tag, and a streptavidin-binding peptide tag)-tagged HDAC4 (SFB-HDAC4) (n = 1) (Fig. 3a). Mass spectrometry analysis revealed a potential HDAC4-associated protein, methyl-CpG binding domain 1 (MBD1) (n = 1) (Supplementary Data 3), which primarily binds to methylated CpG islands and acts as a transcriptional repressor33. MBD1 association with class IIa HDACs has not yet been documented. To independently validate this single-purification hit, we performed coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP). SFB-tagged HDAC4 was cotransfected with HA-tagged MBD1, and reciprocal co-IP was performed using S-beads or HA-beads. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4a, b, using western blotting, a complex containing these two proteins was detected in the cell lysates. Moreover, the immunoprecipitation of endogenous HDAC4 resulted in the co-IP of MBD1 (Fig. 3b), indicating that HDAC4 and MBD1 exist in a complex in lung cancer cells. In addition, IF analysis revealed that they can both be found in the nucleus as expected (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). A previous study indicated that IR can induce HDAC4 nuclear (foci) accumulation34. Therefore, we employed a proximity ligation assay (PLA) to examine the interaction between HDAC4 and MBD1 after 1 h of exposure to 0 or 10 Gy of IR. Consistent with these findings, the IF results demonstrated a significant increase in the HDAC4–MBD1 interaction postirradiation, which was predominantly localized within the nucleus (Fig. 3c, d). To further validate this finding, A549 cells were transfected with flag-MBD1 for 24 hours, subjected to IR, and then analyzed 1 h later. Nuclear‑cytoplasmic separation combined with co‑immunoprecipitation assays revealed that the interaction between HDAC4 and MBD1 was maintained after IR, with an increased amount of the complex observed (Fig. 3e). These data demonstrated that IR enhances the interaction between HDAC4 and MBD1 within the nucleus. To identify the binding domains required for the interaction between HDAC4 and MBD1, a series of deletion mutants of HDAC4 and MBD1 was generated. 293 T cells were cotransfected with the SFB-HDAC4 and HA-MBD1 deletion mutants, and immunoprecipitation was performed to map the binding domains of these two proteins. Our results revealed that the catalytic domain of HDAC4 (Supplementary Fig. 4e) and the TRD domain of MBD1 (Supplementary Fig. 4f) are required for the interaction between HDAC4 and MBD1. Taken together, these data indicate that the catalytic domain of HDAC4 binds to the TRD domain of MBD1.

To test whether HDAC4 regulates the SUMOylation and acetylation of MBD1, first, HDAC4 and flag-MBD1 were cotransfected into A549 cells, and then the MBD1 acetylation levels were analyzed. The overexpression of HDAC4 had no effect on the acetylation level of MBD1 (Supplementary Fig. 4g). HA-SUMO1/2/3 were subsequently cotransfected with Flag-MBD1 in A549 cells, and co-IP assays were subsequently performed with Flag beads. The results revealed that Flag-MBD1 primarily conjugated with SUMO3 (Fig. 3f). Moreover, the SUMOylation of MBD1 was validated in A549 cells via HA-SUMO1/2/3 assays, and the results revealed that the binding of SUMO3 to MBD1 was significantly greater than that of SUMO1/2 (Fig. 3g). The knockdown of HDAC4 markedly diminished the SUMO3 modification level of MBD1 (Fig. 3h, i). Conversely, the overexpression of both WT HDAC4 and a SUMO ligase-inactive variant demonstrated that WT HDAC4 increased the SUMO3 modification level of MBD1, whereas the inactive variant did not (Fig. 3j). Moreover, we found that IR enhanced the HDAC4-mediated SUMO3 modification of MBD1 (Fig. 3k). These findings unequivocally establish that HDAC4 facilitates the SUMO3 modification of MBD1 and that IR can strengthen this effect.

The SUMOylation of MBD1 at K366 increases its stability

SUMO protects substrates from degradation via the ubiquitination pathway by competitively binding with ubiquitin to the substrate lysine35. We therefore investigated whether HDAC4 affects the protein levels of MBD1 via SUMOylation. As shown in Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 5a, b, the overexpression or knockdown of HDAC4 increased or inhibited the MBD1 protein level but not the MBD1 mRNA level. Lysine residues that undergo SUMOylation are typically found within the SUMO modification consensus motif ψKXE/D, in which ψ is a large hydrophobic residue and X is any residue36. Prediction via GPS-SUMO downloaded from (http://sumosp.biocuckoo.org/) revealed eight lysine residues of MBD1, K23, K65, K117, K277, K366, K384, K428 and K467 (Supplementary Fig. 5c), as potential SUMOylation sites. Accordingly, we constructed eight MBD1 mutants, K23R, K65R, K117R, K277R, K366R, K384R, K428R and K467R (Supplementary Fig. 5d), and examined their effects on MBD1 stability through cotransfection with or without HDAC4. In contrast with that of wild-type MBD1, the protein level of the MBD1 K366R mutant notably did not change after HDAC4 was overexpressed (Fig. 4b). This finding was further corroborated even when HDAC4 expression was augmented by increasing the plasmid transfection dose (Supplementary Fig. 5e). We also found that HDAC4 overexpression significantly increased the SUMOylation of wild-type MBD1, but not the K366R mutant (Fig. 4c). Consistently, even under basal conditions, wild-type MBD1 exhibited a higher SUMOylation level than the K366R mutant (Fig. 4c). These findings indicate that MBD1 was mainly SUMOylated at K366 by HDAC4. Next, we compared the half-life of wild-type MBD1 with that of the K366R mutant by transfecting wild-type MBD1 or the K366R mutant into 293 T cells. The protein levels were assessed by western blot, which revealed that the K366R mutant had a shorter half-life than the wild-type MBD1 did (Supplementary Fig. 5f). HDAC4 knockdown by shRNA resulted in a shorter half-life for WT MBD1 but not for the K366R mutant (Fig. 4d, e). Moreover, HDAC4 (C292S) did not affect the half-life of MBD1 (Fig. 4f, g), suggesting that the E3 SUMO ligase activity of HDAC4 promoted MBD1 stability. These data further support the critical role of HDAC4 in regulating the stability of MBD1 through SUMOylation.

An in vivo ubiquitination assay was subsequently performed. As shown in Fig. 4h, in the presence of ectopically expressed HDAC4 and MG132 (a specific proteasome inhibitor), the MBD1 WT protein was more heavily ubiquitinated in the control group than in the HDAC4 overexpression group. In contrast, HDAC4 knockdown increased the polyubiquitination of MBD1 (Fig. 4i); however, the polyubiquitination of the K366R mutant did not differ between the control group and the HDAC4-overexpressing group or between the control and HDAC4-knockdown groups (Fig. 4j, k). Furthermore, when the E3 SUMO ligase activity of HDAC4 was absent, HDAC4 had no effect on the ubiquitination of MBD1 (Fig. 4l). Further investigations were conducted to determine whether ionizing radiation affects the expression pattern of HDAC4. Lung cancer organoids were exposed to a dose equivalent to 9 Gy and then assessed via western blot 4 h and 24 h later. The results revealed a significant increase in the expression levels of HDAC4 and MBD1 in 5 of the 6 organoids following exposure to irradiation (Supplementary Fig. 5g). Finally, in multiple kinds of cancer tissues, there was a positive correlation between the protein levels of HDAC4 and MBD1 (P = 0.002, χ2 tests; Fig. 4m–o), demonstrating that HDAC4 may positively regulate the stabilization of the MBD1 protein in cancer tissues. Collectively, our results strongly indicate that HDAC4 promotes MBD1 protein stability via SUMOylation at K366, which reduces the polyubiquitination of MBD1 and prevents its subsequent degradation.

HDAC4 suppresses ferroptosis and sensitivity to radiation via MBD1

Considering that MBD1 has the capacity to recruit repressive proteins to methylated DNA, thereby facilitating transcriptional suppression33, and acknowledging the ambiguous relationship between MBD1 and ferroptosis, we hypothesized that HDAC4 may modulate the ferroptosis pathway by stabilizing MBD1. To explore MBD1-related ferroptosis targets, we performed Cut&Tag sequencing using an MBD1 antibody in LCO-4 organoids (n = 1) (Fig. 5a). The results revealed numerous potential MBD1 binding regions (Fig. 5b), particularly in well-known ferroptosis-related genes (Supplementary Data 4). We then investigated the impact of HDAC4 overexpression on these ferroptosis-related genes. qRT‒PCR revealed that only TP53 and CYP1A1 were significantly downregulated when HDAC4 was overexpressed. (Supplementary Fig. 6a, Supplementary Data 6). p53 directly inhibits the transcription of SLC7A11, a key transporter of cysteine that imports extracellular cystine into cells37. CYP1A1 increases ROS levels to promote lipid ROS formation38. We investigated whether HDAC4–MBD1 contributes to promoter CpG island methylation-mediated suppression of TP53 and CYP1A1 but does not regulate WRAP53 (Fig. 5c). Relative quantification analysis was conducted after LCO-4 organoids were transfected with shMBD1 or shHDAC4; shRNA-NC was used as a control. Compared with the control, HDAC4 or MBD1 knockdown significantly increased the mRNA and protein levels of p53 and CYP1A1 (Fig. 5d–f). However, the level of WRAP53 did not change (Fig. 5d, e). ChIP assays were also performed using HDAC4 WT or KO cells to confirm the role of MBD1 as a methyl-CpG reader in the epigenetic regulation of TP53 and CYP1A1 expression. The KO of HDAC4 significantly decreased MBD1 binding to the promoters of TP53 and CYP1A1 in lung cancer cells (Fig. 5g, h), indicating that MBD1 may play a strong role in their epigenetic silencing. Moreover, the knockdown of MBD1 dramatically abrogated the inhibitory effect of HDAC4 on the basal expression of p53 and CYP1A1 (Fig. 5i). Concurrently, the overexpression of MBD1 reversed the upregulation of TP53 and CYP1A1 induced by HDAC4 knockdown under stress conditions (Supplementary Fig. 6b, c). On the basis of the above results, we speculated that p53 and CYP1A1 may serve as key downstream effectors of HDAC4-induced ferroptosis in lung cancer cells (Fig. 5j).

Next, we addressed whether the knockdown of MBD1 alleviates HDAC4-mediated radioresistance. A549-HDAC4 stable cell lines were transfected with or without MBD1 siRNA. Lipid ROS assays revealed that cotransfection of A549-HDAC4 cells with MBD1 siRNA or overexpressing CYP1A1 reversed the increase in lipid ROS levels observed in A549-HDAC4 cells transfected with siRNA-NC (Fig. 5k and Supplementary Fig. 6d). Furthermore, we knocked down HDAC4 and then overexpressed MBD1-WT and the SUMO mutant K366R in H460 cells (Fig. 5l). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that MBD1-WT could effectively alleviate the lipid-ROS induced by HDAC4 knockdown, whereas K366R did not (Fig. 5m, n) after IR. Moreover, MBD1-WT reversed the cell death induced by HDAC4 knockdown after IR, whereas the K366R mutant did not (Fig. 5o, p). Additionally, we analyzed the contribution of MBD1 to HDAC4-mediated radioresistance. As expected, the siRNA-mediated knockdown of MBD1 abolished HDAC4-mediated radioresistance (Supplementary Fig. 6e–g). These findings collectively demonstrate that HDAC4 inhibits ferroptosis to increase radioresistance through SUMOylated MBD1 to suppress p53 and CYP1A1 expression.

Tasquinimod-based PROTAC TP1 induces HDAC4 degradation

Tasquinimod is a small-molecule compound that binds to HDAC4 and is believed to inhibit the transcription of downstream target genes by inhibiting the formation of HDAC3/N-CoR complexes39. We therefore sought to treat tumor cells with tasquinimod, which does not inhibit the interaction between HDAC4 and MBD1 (Supplementary Fig. 7a) but effectively disrupts the formation of HDAC4/HDAC3/N-CoR complexes (Supplementary Fig. 7b). Furthermore, tasquinimod was unable to reverse the inhibitory effects of HDAC4 on p53 and CYP1A1 expression (Supplementary Fig. 7c, d). Consequently, developing a novel strategy aimed at targeting the HDAC4–MBD1 signaling pathway is imperative. Proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) offer a powerful strategy for antitumor drug discovery40. A growing number of PROTACs that target cancers have been successfully developed, and ARV-471 has entered the new drug application (NDA) submission phase with the FDA. To develop a potential selective compound to target HDAC4, we used tasquinimod as a bait molecule and designed and synthesized HDAC4-targeting PROTACs. The composition and length of linkers can affect the efficiency of degraders40,41. Flexible linkers, dominated by alkyl chains and PEG, are widely used42. On the basis of these considerations, we synthesized two distinct PROTAC molecules: TP1 (Supplementary Figs. 10–13), which features an alkyl chain linker, and TP2, which incorporates a PEG chain linker (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 7e). Next, on the basis of CRBN expression (Supplementary Fig. 7f), we treated A549 and H460 cells with high CRBN expression with TP1 and TP2, respectively. The results showed that TP1 effectively reduced the HDAC4 protein concentration but that TP2 did not significantly reduce the HDAC4 protein concentration (Supplementary Fig. 7g, h). Time-course studies revealed that 10 μM TP1 achieved optimal degradation within 24 h (Fig. 6b), but low-dose TP1 achieved optimal degradation within 48 h (Supplementary Fig. 7i). To test whether TP1 can inhibit HDAC4 expression in lung cancer organoids, LCO-1 and LCO-4 organoids were treated with TP1; as expected, TP1 decreased the protein levels of HDAC4 and MBD1 (Fig. 6c–e and Supplementary Fig. 7j), suggesting that TP1 effectively promoted HDAC4 degradation in clinical lung cancer tissues.

To further explore the mechanism by which TP1 promotes HDAC4 degradation, we employed surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analyses with Biacore T200, an optical and label-free technique used to measure molecular interactions in real time. As shown in Fig. 6f, g, compounds TP1 and tasquinimod had very strong affinities for the HDAC4 protein, and the KD values were 88.24 nM and 36.45 nM, respectively. Since TP1 contains an IMiD moiety that interacts with the E3 ligase receptor CRBN43,44, we analyzed ternary complex formation (HDAC4-PROTAC-CRBN) in 293 T cells via separation of phase-based protein interaction reporter (SPPIER) assays. We used H3 tag-HDAC4 and H6 tag-CRBN complexes to examine the formation of HDAC4-TP1-CRBN ternary complexes. TP1, but not tasquinimod, induced the generation of yellow fluorescent droplets (Fig. 6h). We also used the BIFC assay to assess the PPI relationship between HDAC4 and CRBN by constructing HDAC4-VN173 and CRBN-VC155 vectors, transfecting the vectors into cells, and treating the cells with or without TP1. The fluorescence results revealed that HDAC4-VN173 and CRBN-VC155 were localized in the cytoplasm (Fig. 6i). To determine whether the CRBN protein is required for TP1-mediated HDAC4 degradation and cellular effects. We synthesized the corresponding negative control compound, negTP1 (containing a methylated CRBN ligand). The modification of the E3 ligands prevents the recruitment of their respective E3 ligases by the PROTAC, thereby allowing the assessment of the activity of the PROTAC in the absence of the E3 ligase45,46. We then treated H460 and A549 cells with 10 μM negTP1 for 24 h. The protein level of HDAC4 remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 7k), indicating that interfering with the binding to CRBN blocks HDAC4 degradation. Moreover, we used CRISPR–Cas9 gene editing to produce A549 cells with stable disruption of the CRBN gene. Western blotting of lysates prepared from control and CRBN-edited A549 cells revealed the loss of CRBN expression in CRBN-edited cells, with retained expression in control-edited cells (Supplementary Fig. 7l). Western blotting of lysates prepared from control and CRBN-KO A549 cells treated with TP1 or DMSO revealed that TP1 suppressed HDAC4 and MBD1 expression; the same effect was not observed in CRBN-edited cells (Supplementary Fig. 7l). Thus, CRBN is required for TP1-mediated HDAC4 degradation.

To comprehensively characterize the specificity of TP1 for HDAC4, we analyzed the effects of DMSO or TP1 via data-independent acquisition (DIA)-based quantitative proteomics after A549 cells were treated with TP1 or DMSO, and the results indicated that TP1 displays highly specific degradation activity towards HDAC4 (Fig. 6j and Supplementary Data 5). We then investigated whether TP1 promotes the ubiquitination of HDAC4 and induces its degradation. As shown in Fig. 6k, TP1 induced the polyubiquitination of HDAC4; furthermore, the degradation activity of TP1 was blocked by MG132 (Fig. 6l and Supplementary Fig. 7m, n), which is a proteasome inhibitor, indicating that TP1 promotes HDAC4 degradation through the ubiquitin protease pathway. These data indicate that TP1 functions by binding to HDAC4, which leads to the CRBN-dependent proteasomal degradation of HDAC4.

TP1 outperforms tasquinimod in enhancing ferroptosis

To characterize the biological effects of the TP1 PROTAC, we next treated lung cancer cells with an equivalent volume of DMSO or TP1 for 48 h and performed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis. We observed that many genes were regulated by TP1 treatment. In TP1-treated cells, the expression of the ferroptosis regulator p53 was upregulated, and the expression of the ferroptosis mediator SLC7A11 was downregulated (Fig. 7a–d); additionally, CYP1A1 expression was increased, a finding that was consistent with the effects of the shRNA-mediated knockdown of HDAC4 (Fig. 5d, f). To investigate the role of TP1 in the ferroptosis of lung cancer cells, we treated lung cancer cells with TP1, tasquinimod, or vehicle control. Forty-eight hours after treatment, TP1 significantly promoted the accumulation of lipid ROS (Fig. 7e, f). Consistent with the lipid ROS results, transmission electron microscopy revealed shrunken mitochondria with increased membrane density in TP1-treated cells (Fig. 7g). However, tasquinimod had no effect on ferroptosis.

Next, we tested the effects of TP1 on cell viability. In A549 and H460 cells, TP1 inhibited the growth of A549 and H460 cells, with IC50 values of 1.27 μM and 0.86 μM (Supplementary Fig. 8a), respectively. We compared TP1 with tasquinimod in terms of its ability to inhibit cell growth, and the results revealed that, in H460 and A549 cells, TP1 more strongly inhibited cell growth and colony formation than did tasquinimod and suppressed the cell cycle (Supplementary Fig. 8b–i). These results show that the targeted degradation of HDAC4 by the PROTAC TP1 is more effective than the parental molecule tasquinimod in treating tumors in vitro. Next, we tested the effects of TP1 and tasquinimod in a murine xenograft model (Fig. 7h). In mice treated with TP1 (three days; 10 mg/kg), we did not observe significant body weight loss (Supplementary Fig. 8j). We performed histopathological analysis of the liver, kidney, spleen, lung, intestine and heart of the mice that were exposed to TP1, and compared with the control treatment, exposure to TP1 did not cause pathological changes, suggesting that TP1 has minimal target toxicity (Supplementary Fig. 8k). Importantly, compared with tasquinimod, TP1 markedly inhibited tumor growth in the xenograft at the same concentrations (Fig. 7i, j). IHC staining indicated that TP1 successfully induced the degradation of HDAC4 in vivo (Supplementary Fig, 8l). Moreover, MBD1 and SLC7A11 also decreased after treatment with TP1 but not with tasquinimod (Supplementary Fig. 8l). We explored whether TP1 could serve as a radiosensitizer by promoting cell death under IR treatment in vivo, and our results indicated that TP1 treatment had a similar effect as that of HDAC4 knockdown, both of which enhanced radiotherapy sensitivity more than tasquinimod did (Fig. 7k, l). Thus, through targeted degradation, the PROTAC TP1 was successfully able to outperform tasquinimod in terms of antitumor effects in vitro and in vivo.

TP1 promotes the radiosensitivity of patient-derived organoids of lung adenocarcinoma

Patient-derived tumor organoids could predict treatment response to targeted therapy to provide guidance for personalized cancer therapy47. The issues of whether TP1 responds to targeted therapies need to be addressed. To assess the sensitivity of LCOs and normal lung organoids with different HDAC4 expression levels to TP1, we generated dose‒response curves for several drugs and calculated the half-maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50 values). We tested the IC50 of TP1 on these LCOs, and the results revealed that LCO-1 and LCO-4 organoids with higher HDAC4 expression had a lower IC50, whereas LCO-2 and LCO-5 organoids with lower HDAC4 expression had a higher IC50 (Fig. 8a). Tasquinimod produced similar results (Fig. 8b). Notably, normal organoids were not sensitive to these two compounds (Fig. 8a, b). These findings were further supported by analyses of downstream signaling molecules of the HDAC4-MBD1 pathway via IHC staining of LCOs after LCOs were treated with DMSO or TP1. In LCO-1, -2, -3, -4 and -6 organoids, the levels of mediators of HDAC4 signaling, including HDAC4, MBD1, and SLC7A11, decreased after TP1 treatment, whereas p53 and CYP1A1 protein levels increased (Fig. 8c and Supplementary Fig. 9a–d). These data demonstrated that our LCOs could be used for predicting TP1 responses in vitro, as well as for proof-of-concept studies of targeted drugs based on HDAC4 expression. In addition, we combined TP1 with IR to treat lung cancer organoids. As shown in Fig. 8d–g, compared with the tasquinimod group, TP1 had a stronger ability to sensitize resistant LCO-4 organoids to radiation therapy, as determined by counting the organoids and measuring the SYTOX Green-stained areas. These findings demonstrate that TP1 can serve as a radiosensitizer and promote ferroptosis in lung cancer.

Discussion

PTMs are pivotal in conferring radioresistance in cancer, orchestrating the repair of cellular DNA damage through the modulation of protein stability, interaction patterns, enzyme activity, and other mechanisms. Nevertheless, the involvement of PTMs in mediating radioresistance associated with ferroptosis remains elusive. In this study, we employed lung cancer organoids in conjunction with CRISPR screening to reveal that IR upregulates HDAC4 expression, which impedes lung cancer ferroptosis via its E3 SUMO activity and consequently enhances resistance to radiation therapy. Mechanistically, our findings demonstrate that HDAC4 facilitates the SUMOylation and stabilization of MBD1 while suppressing the expression of CYP1A1 and p53 to attenuate lipid ROS formation and promote lipid ROS degradation, respectively. To sensitize lung cancer to radiotherapy by targeting HDAC4, we developed a small-molecule PROTAC compound based on tasquinimod that effectively degrades HDAC4 and promotes lung cancer ferroptosis as well as sensitization to radiation therapy (Fig. 8h).

Cancer organoids, which maintain the physiological structure and function of their source tissue, are widely used in research on tumor pathogenesis, drug development, treatment resistance, and other fields48,49. In this study, we tested the mutations of organoids to ensure that the organoids effectively reflected the pathological features of the original sample, and we also found that LCO-4 organoids had the strongest resistance to radiation therapy. CRISPR facilitates high-throughput and unbiased screening. Our team used CRISPR technology to identify key resistance genes for lung cancer radiotherapy in the A549 cell line50. Furthermore, we used CRISPR screening to identify resistance genes in lung cancer organoids and found that HDAC4 was among the top 20 downregulated genes in LCO-1 and LCO-4 organoids. Furthermore, functional experiments revealed that knocking down HDAC4 enhanced radiotherapy sensitivity, whereas overexpressing HDAC4 enhanced radiotherapy resistance, which further confirmed the rationality and reliability of using lung cancer organoids for screening.

Lipid peroxidation is controlled by integrated oxidation and antioxidant systems. The iron-containing enzyme lipoxygenase serves as the primary driver of ferroptosis through the generation of lipid hydroperoxides, and its function is dependent on the activation of ACSL4-dependent lipid biosynthesis11. In contrast, the selenium-containing enzyme GPX4 is widely acknowledged as a central suppressor of ferroptosis, a process that is dependent on glutathione production facilitated by the cystine-glutamate antiporter SLC7A1111. There are four principal signaling pathways involved in lipid peroxide scavenging: GPX4-GSH, FSP1-CoQH2, GCH1-BH4, and DHODH-CoQH27,51. IR promotes lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis by inducing ROS production and upregulating ACSL4 expression; however, it also upregulates the expression of the ferroptosis-inhibiting proteins SLC7A11 and GPX4 to counteract ferroptosis12. The inhibition of SLC7A11 and GPX4 can promote ferroptosis and sensitize radiation-resistant cells to IR12. Nevertheless, the molecular mechanism underlying the IR-induced upregulation of SLC7A11 expression remains unclear12. Our research demonstrated that IR-induced HDAC4 upregulation leads to the increased expression of SLC7A11, which inhibits ferroptosis and sensitization to radiation; this finding partially explains why there is an increase in SLC7A11 expression following IR treatment.

A recent study suggested that CYP1A1 acts as a monooxygenase that generates reactive oxygen species, leading to increased lipid peroxidation and the subsequent induction of ferroptosis38; however, its specific role in the response to radiation remains elusive. We observed that the overexpression of CYP1A1 in HDAC4 overexpression cells resulted in increased lipid peroxidation, indicating that the HDAC4-mediated inhibition of ferroptosis may require p53 and CYP1A1 to prevent lipid peroxidation. Regarding the role of HDAC4 in ferroptosis, a previous report indicated that the deacetylation of HDAC4 has an inhibitory effect on mouse muscle cell-based models52. Our study further revealed that the E3 SUMO ligase activity associated with HDAC4 plays a pivotal regulatory role in IR-induced ferroptotic responses, which indicates that the contribution of HDAC4 to ferroptosis in different diseases seems to be context-dependent.

SUMOylation is a reversible posttranslational modification that has emerged as a crucial molecular regulatory mechanism involved in the regulation of stability, subcellular localization, and protein‒protein interactions of target proteins53. SUMOylation regulates numerous biological processes, including DNA damage repair, immune responses, carcinogenesis, cell cycle progression, and apoptosis54. Humans express four SUMO genes: SUMO1, SUMO2, SUMO3, and SUMO455,56. SUMO1 modifies the functions of many proteins related to the occurrence, development, and metastasis of cancer, suggesting that the SUMO1 gene is closely related to the occurrence and development of cancer54. Moreover, SUMO2/3 mainly affects stress proteins57,58. In recent years, several studies have shown that SUMOylation plays a key role in ferroptosis; for example, SUMOylation of SLC7A11 inhibits ferroptosis, thereby regulating proliferation and metastasis in breast cancer59. However, the role of SUMO in regulating radiotherapy sensitivity through ferroptosis remains unclear. Our results clearly revealed that MBD1 SUMOylation by HDAC4 regulates p53 and CYP1A1 expression to control IR-induced ferroptosis. These findings demonstrated that the regulation of PTMs of proteins within tumor cells caused tumor radioresistance by regulating ferroptosis.

Radiosensitizers are considered one of the strategies to improve the sensitivity of lung cancer to radiotherapy. However, thus far, very few drugs are available for the sensitization of lung cancer to radiotherapy. For example, in clinical practice, the use of PARPis has yielded certain therapeutic effects, but the efficacy seems to be limited60. TKIs targeting specific mutations in NSCLC may play important roles in radiotherapy and chemotherapy61,62, but they may also increase pulmonary toxicity, with an efficacy rate of less than 50%63,64. Although immunotherapy is currently the standard treatment for NSCLC, adjuvant immunotherapy can induce certain pulmonary toxicity after radiotherapy and chemotherapy65. Therefore, it is necessary to use targets that are more suitable for lung cancer as radiotherapy sensitizers. Targeting ferroptosis may be a strategy to improve radiotherapy sensitivity7. Our study established targeted HDAC4 degradation as a promising therapeutic strategy to overcome radioresistance in lung cancer. TasQ is believed to have the ability to specifically bind to the Zn2+ domain of HDAC4 and inhibit its interaction with HDAC3 to block the downstream target genes regulated by HDAC4. Our experiments revealed that TasQ cannot effectively block HDAC4 E3 SUMO ligase activity, resulting in the inability to block the inhibitory effect of HDAC4 on ferroptosis. Previously, the HDAC4 degraders designed by Macabuag et al. employed two panclass IIa HDAC inhibitors—hydroxamic acid (HA) 1 and trifluoromethyl oxadiazole (TFMO) 2—as warheads, which demonstrated remarkable selectivity and activity towards HDAC4, enabling efficient degradation of the target protein66. This study revealed that HDAC4 can be degraded by PROTACs. On this basis, we synthesized a small-molecule PROTAC using TasQ. PROTACs require high affinity to target proteins and the recruitment of E3 ubiquitin ligases to promote target protein ubiquitination, leading to proteasomal degradation. Through SPR experiments, we found that our small-molecule PROTAC had a high affinity for HDAC4, reaching 88.24 nM. Moreover, through SPPIER experiments, it was confirmed that a triple complex formed. Our results showed that the synthesized small-molecule compounds efficiently degrade the HDAC4 protein and reduce MBD1 protein levels. While TP1 demonstrates selective HDAC4 degradation, its requirement of 10 μM treatment within 24 h to achieve effective degradation and its structural ancestry from the polypharmacological scaffold of tasquinimod67 will necessitate further structure-activity relationship studies and orthogonal target engagement validation to enhance its degradation activity in ongoing studies. We also evaluated the ability of this small-molecule compound to promote ferroptosis and radiation sensitization in lung cancer. Importantly, PROTAC treatment and HDAC4 knockdown yielded similar results. By establishing normal lung-like organs, we found that normal lung organoids were not sensitive to TP1, indicating its low toxicity. Moreover, preliminary evaluation in an animal laboratory revealed that this small-molecule compound has low toxicity in vivo. The above results demonstrated that TP1 can effectively increase the radiosensitivity of lung cancer, suggesting that TP1 has potential as a lung cancer therapy. Recent studies have shown that adding folate groups to small-molecule compounds of PROTACs can increase their tumor-targeting ability68. This strategy should be further explored in the future. These findings suggest that small-molecule compounds can be used in clinical research. In addition, our findings are primarily derived from cell lines, patient-derived organoids, and immunodeficient xenograft models, which do not completely recapitulate the complex tumor microenvironment and immune responses that influence radiotherapy efficacy in patients. Future studies employing immunocompetent models are warranted to validate and extend our observations.

In summary, our study provides evidence that HDAC4 promotes radiation resistance by stabilizing MBD1 to suppress ferroptosis in lung cancer and has led to the development of an inducer of ferroptosis, which, in combination with radiation therapy, represents a potential therapeutic strategy.

Methods

Study approval

The use of WES data from patient-derived tumor tissues and matched organoids (LCOs) in this study has been approved by the Human Genetic Resources Administration of the Ministry of Science and Technology (MOST) of China (approval number: 2025BAT00973). The use of human lung cancer tissues was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University (approval number: KY2022042) and the Medical Ethics Committee of Jiangxi Cancer Hospital (approval number: 2024ky210). All samples were acquired as part of routine clinical diagnosis and treatment. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. A total of 42 tumor specimens were collected from patients with lung cancer (median age, 56 years; age range, 40–79 years; male: female ratio, 2:1), which were resected between November 2018 and July 2023. Paired tumor samples collected before and after radiotherapy from 3 patients were retrospectively collected from a paraffin-embedded tissue archive. Tumor response was assessed according to RECIST version 1.1 within 3 months post-radiotherapy, with tumors exhibiting radiographically stable disease (SD) or progressive disease (PD) classified as radiation-resistant. A human lung cancer tissue microarray was purchased from Outdo Biotech (Shanghai, China). A total of 120 samples were obtained from adjacent normal lobar bronchial tissues, and written informed consent was obtained from the patients (median age, 64 years; age range, 34–84 years; male: female ratio, 7:3). All the animal studies were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Experimental Animal Science Center of Nanchang University (approval number: NCULAE-20230712001). Animals were euthanized when the tumor burden exceeded 1500 mm³, in accordance with the Animal Care Guidelines.

Cells and reagents

A549 (CCL-185), H460 (HTB-177), H522 (CRL-5810), H1155 (CRL-5818), H292 (CRL-1848), HCC827 (CRL-2868), and 293 T (CRL-3216) were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA) and were authenticated via short tandem repeat profiling, tested for mycoplasma contamination, and cultured for less than two months. All of the cell lines were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.) and maintained at 37 °C in an incubator containing 5% CO2. Ferrostatin-1 (S7243) was obtained from Selleck. Tasquinimod (HY-10528) was obtained from MCE. Puromycin (A1113803) was obtained from Gibco.

Tumor tissue processing and organoid establishment

The collection of surgically resected primary tumors from patients with early-stage NSCLC and the development of organoids was approved by the Jiangxi Cancer Hospital Ethics Board. Informed written consent was obtained from all patients. All studies were performed in accordance with the TRI-Council Policy Statement: Ethical Conduct for Research Involving Humans. The clinical diagnosis of NSCLC subtypes was validated via pathologic review. The protocol for establishing NSCLC organoids was previously described25,69. Briefly, tumors and adjacent samples were quickly placed in a tube containing PBS, and the tissues were processed into 4-mm-diameter pieces and washed with ice-cold PBS. The tumor pieces were dissociated into single cells in Advanced DMEM-F12 (GIBCO) with LiberaseTM (Sigma) for 1 h, followed by a 10-min incubation with TrypLE Express (Invitrogen) at 37 °C with gentle shaking. The cells were counted and resuspended in 100% growth factor–reduced Matrigel (VWR), seeded in 24-well tissue culture plates as Matrigel domes, and maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2 with medium overlaying the Matrigel dome. Refer to the Supplementary Table 1 for a list of medium components. Organoid growth was monitored weekly to assess organoid initiation, and the organoids were maintained at the same passage for no longer than four weeks. Organoids were authenticated by whole-exome sequencing (WES) analysis and matched to patient tissue. Organoid cultures were tested routinely for Mycoplasma. Additional methods can be found in the Supplementary Materials and Methods.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and histological evaluation

IHC analysis was performed as described previously50,70. Briefly, samples were fixed in 10% formalin for 10 h at room temperature and embedded in paraffin. Sections (3 µm in thickness) were prepared and mounted on positively charged glass slides. For staining, the sections were incubated in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) and boiled in a microwave oven for 15 min. The sections were incubated with primary antibodies against HDAC4 (1:100), MBD1 (1:100), p53 (1:300), SLC7A11 (1:300), and CYP1A1 (1:300) at 4 °C overnight in a humidified container. IHC staining was evaluated by two independent pathologists specializing in lung cancer. The expression levels of HDAC4, MBD1, p53, SLC7A11, and CYP1A1 were scored using the semiquantitative immunoreactive score (IRS) scale described by Remmele and Stegner71. Briefly, HDAC4, MBD1, p53, SLC7A11, and CYP1A1 signals were detected in the cytoplasm and nucleus. The staining intensity (SI) for HDAC4, MBD1, p53, SLC7A11, and CYP1A1 in lung cancer tissue was classified as follows: 0, absent; 1, weak; 2, moderate; and 3, strong. The percentage of stained cells was categorized as follows: 0, no stained cells; 1, 1–10% stained cells; 2, 11–50% stained cells; 3, 51–80% stained cells; and 4, 81–100% stained cells. The intensity and proportion scores were then multiplied to obtain a final score.

CRISPR screening

The pooled sgRNA library contained 903 posttranslational modification-regulated proteins (Supplementary Data 2), and each gene contained 10–12 sgRNAs. The sgRNA was cloned into the lentiviral lentiCRISPR v2 vector (Addgene #52961), which is designed to co-express the sgRNA and wild-type Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 (SpCas9) from a single plasmid. For subgroup pooled CRISPR screening, 3 × 106 NSCLC organoid cells were transfected with the pooled lentiviral library at a multiplicity of infection of 0.5. After 3 days of selection with 1 μg/ml puromycin, the surviving organoids were divided into 2 parts, each of which was treated with or without 9 Gy of IR. After 10 days, the organoids were harvested and subjected to genomic DNA extraction with a tissue DNA kit (Omega, D3396-01). PCR was performed on the genomic DNA to construct sequencing libraries. This PCR generated libraries that were sequenced on the Illumina Xplus platform to obtain Fastq files. Each library was sequenced to achieve over 500× average coverage, and the data were analyzed by MAGeCK72 and MAGeCKFlute73. The sgRNAs were ranked according to beta score.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis of TP1 or tasquinimod binding to the HDAC4 protein

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) technology-based binding assays were carried out with a Biacore T200 instrument (GE Healthcare). The HDAC4 protein was immobilized on a CM5 sensor chip using a standard amide coupling procedure in 10 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.0). The coupling level of the HDAC4 protein was immobilized to 9000 response units on flow cells 1 and 2 of a Biacore series S CM5 sensor chip (GE Healthcare). TP1 and tasquinimod were diluted in running buffer (1× PBS, 0.05% Tween 20, 5% DMSO, pH 7.4) (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd.; Shanghai) to the required concentrations, and multicycle kinetic detection was carried out. The injection time was set to 90 s, the dissociation time was set to 200 s, and 50 mM Gly-HCl (pH = 3) was used as the regeneration buffer. The KD values were evaluated via Biacore T200 evaluation software 3.0 (GE Healthcare)74,75.

CUT&Tag

CUT&Tag assays were carried out using a NovoNGS CUT&Tag High Sensitivity Kit (Novoprotein, N259-YH01) according to the manufacturer’s instructions76. Briefly, 100000 individual digestive organoids were incubated with ConA magnetic beads for 10 min at room temperature, followed by incubation with a primary antibody (anti-MBD1, HPA068850, Atlas Antibodies) overnight at 4 °C. The samples were subsequently incubated with a secondary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature. The ChiTag transposome was incubated for 1 h at room temperature, after which Tn5 tagmentation was performed for 1 h at 37 °C. DNA was extracted, amplified, and purified. Constructed libraries were sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

CUT&Tag data analysis

CUT&Tag raw data (raw reads) in fastq format were first processed through in-house Perl scripts, and clean data were obtained by removing the adaptor sequences. The clean reads were then aligned to reference genome sequences using the BWA program. The.bam file generated from the unique mapped reads was used as an input file, and macs2 software was used to call peaks with a cutoff q value < 0.05. HOMER’s find Motifs Genome.pl tool was used for motif analysis. Peaks were annotated by using HOMER’s annotatePeaks.pl. The annotation results were counted, and the distribution results were plotted via R.

Analysis of lipid ROS production

The day before the experiment, 150,000 cells/well were seeded in six-well dishes. The day of the experiment, the cells were treated with the test compounds for the indicated times, harvested via trypsinization, resuspended in 500 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 2 μM C11-BODIPY (581/591) (#D3861, Invitrogen), and incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in a tissue culture incubator. The cells were then resuspended in 500 μL of fresh PBS, strained through a 40 μm cell strainer, and analyzed using a flow cytometer (FACSuite, BD Biosciences) equipped with a 488 nm laser for excitation. Data were collected from the FL1 channel (527 nm). A minimum of 10,000 cells were analyzed per condition. Data analysis was performed by using FlowJo Version 10.8.1 software.

Transmission electron microscopy

A549-shRNA-Con, H522-shRNA-Con, A549-shRNA-HDAC4, or H522-shRNA-HDAC4 cells were seeded at 600,000 cells/dish in 100 mm tissue culture dishes. To examine the impact of TP1 on ferroptosis, A549 cells were seeded at 600,000 cells/dish in 100 mm tissue culture dishes, and then treated with DMSO, TasQ, or TP1 (7.5 μM) for 48 h. The cells were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 mM phosphate buffer, followed by incubation with 1% OsO4 for 2 h. After dehydration, the cells were embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections were prepared using an ultramicrotome and then stained with lead citrate and uranyl acetate, and the distribution of mitochondria was observed via transmission electron microscopy (Philips Electronic Instruments, Mahwah, NJ, USA).

SPPIER assay

To detect PROTAC-induced ternary complex formation, EGFP-based SPPIER assays77,78 with DF-SPPIER were optimized with mCherry to label HDAC4 (HDAC4-mCherry-HOTag3) and EGFP to label CRBN (CRBN-EGFP-HOTag6). HEK293T cells in 35 mm glass-bottom dishes (801002, NEST) were transiently cotransfected with 1.0 µg of HDAC4-mCherry-HOTag3 and 1.0 µg of CRBN-EGFP-HOTag6 in combination for 12 hours and supplied with DMSO or TP1 (5 μM) for 3 h. The cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with Hoechst 33342 (62249, Thermo) for immunofluorescence analysis. Images were acquired with an Olympus system (FV1000, Japan). Olympus software (FV1000, Japan) was used to process the images, including yellow droplet images. The profile of red and green fluorescence intensity was analyzed using the plot profile function in ImageJ. The sum of yellow droplet pixel fluorescence intensity was analyzed using the analyze particle function in ImageJ.

Animal experiments

The mice were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at a temperature of 20–25 °C and 50–70% humidity with a light/dark cycle of 12 h, and had free access to water and chow (Jiangsu Synergy Pharmaceutical Bioengineering Co., Ltd, Cat. no. XTI01FZ-011). A total of 42 male athymic nude mice at 4 weeks of age were obtained from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China); thus, sex-disaggregated data were not applicable. A total of 2 × 106 cells were mixed with 0.2 ml of PBS (pH 7.4) and 30% (v/v) Matrigel matrix (BD Biosciences). Suspensions were injected subcutaneously into the flanks of randomly assigned nude mice, which were monitored over 3 weeks. An intraperitoneal injection of TP1 or TasQ (10 mg/kg per week for 2 weeks) was administered every 3 days; the control group received 200 μl of 0.1% DMSO.

DIA proteomics analysis

Lung cancer cell samples in each group were processed for DIA proteomics quantification. In brief, the sample was mixed with lysis buffer (8 M urea/100 mM Tris-HCl, pH=8.5) and then sonicated. After ultrasonication, the resulting mixture was centrifuged at 12000 × g for 5 min, and the protein concentration of the supernatant was determined via the BCA method. Protein reduction and alkylation were conducted with TCEP (tris(2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride) and CAA (2-chloroacetamide) at 37 °C for 1 h. Urea was diluted below 2 M in 100 mM Tris-HCl, followed by overnight trypsin digestion. The digested peptides were subjected to peptide purification via a custom-made SDB-RPS desalting column. The purified peptides were loaded onto a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics) coupled with an UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano system (Thermo) and analyzed in diaPASEF mode. The raw DIA data were searched against the human protein sequence database (2023-06-19, 20423 entries) downloaded from UniProt and analyzed with DIA-NN (DIA-NN 1.8.2 beta 11) in library-free mode. Search parameters were set as follows: in silico-predicted spectral library generation with Trypsin/P specificity (maximum 2 missed cleavages); carbamidomethylation (C) as fixed modification; oxidation (M) and protein N-terminal acetylation as variable modifications; MS1 mass accuracy, 15 ppm; MBR enabled; heuristic protein inference enabled; precursor FDR ≤ 1%. Protein intensities were normalized using the MaxLFQ algorithm.

Statistics and Reproducibility

Data from independent experiments are presented as means ± SDs. Statistical analysis between two groups was performed using Student’s t test (two-tailed), and statistical analysis among multiple groups was conducted via one-way or two-way ANOVA with GraphPad Prism 8.0 software (GraphPad Software). In addition, a χ2 test was performed to analyze the correlation between HDAC4 and MBD1 expression in clinical lung cancer samples. All in vitro experiments were performed at least three times independently, and the mean values of three experiments are shown. In the statistical analysis, p < 0.05 is deemed statistically significant. All representative blots are derived from at least three independent experiments with similar results.
