Complementary and alternative medicine use among patients with type-2 diabetes mellitus attending a suburban tertiary healthcare centre in Sri Lanka
Authors: Maheshi Thilakarathna, Kawmini Appuhami, Nuwan Darshana, Jennifer Perera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundType 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) represents a major global health problem with significant complications and high economic and social burdens, leading many patients to explore complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) for obtaining relief. This study was conducted to explore the use of CAM by patients with T2DM and factors influencing it.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted at a suburban tertiary care hospital in Sri Lanka using 280 randomly selected patients diagnosed with T2DM. Data were collected using an interviewer-administered questionnaire adapted using published instruments. The questionnaire included open-ended questions on types of CAM used, a five-point Likert scale that assessed attitudes and beliefs and questions assessing knowledge and perceptions. Ethics approval was received from the Faculty of Medicine, Wayamba University of Sri Lanka (PW/2024/02/01). The data analysis used the chi-square and independent sample t- tests at a 0.05 significance level.ResultsThe majority were females (87.5%), and 64.6% were > 60 years of age. The prevalence of CAM use was 68.6%, and 35.5% used it as a treatment for controlling diabetes concurrently with conventional medicine, while 4.5% used CAM concurrently with conventional medicine for improving their well-being. Orally consumed herbal remedies were the commonest CAM used (30.4%), followed by spiritual healing (19.1%) and music therapy (13.1%). Some used more than one type of CAM. Bitter gourd fruit (Momordica charantia), canerreed leaves (Costus speciosus) and fenugreek seeds (Trigonella foenum) were the most common herbs used. 52.1% demonstrated a lack of specific knowledge that would be required to enquire about the safety, efficacy and possible interactions with medications used concurrently. No significant association was observed between CAM use and sociodemographic characteristics or disease-related factors.ConclusionsThere was widespread use of CAM by patients with T2DM. Future research with larger and diverse populations is recommended to explore CAM usage in more depth. Controlled trials for commonly used CAM therapies with or without simultaneous use of conventional medications are needed to assess effectiveness, safety and possible drug interactions. There is a need to work towards formally integrating CAM and conventional medicine for improved patient satisfaction while ensuring safety and therapeutic benefits.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-05077-5.Keywords: Beliefs, Complementary and alternative medicine, Knowledge, Perceptions, Prevalence, Type-2 diabetes mellitus

Introduction

Current status of complementary and alternative medicine use

Complementary, alternative and integrative medicine (CAM), encompassing therapeutic and diagnostic approaches, is being increasingly used alongside conventional treatments, i.e., Western medicine, to enhance overall well-being [1]. The National Centre for Complementary and Integrative Health in the United States describes CAM as “a group of medical and health care systems, practices, and products that are not presently considered to be part of conventional medicine” [2]. This operational definition provides a foundation for developing research efforts that will assist in the understanding of this field and knowledge translation [3]. This shift aligns with the CAM principles that emphasise addressing underlying causes of illness rather than just treating symptoms [1].

The regulation and integration of CAM vary widely across countries. In some European nations, CAM is offered privately and remains largely unregulated, while in others, regulatory frameworks govern practitioner training and service delivery [4]. Certain countries have integrated CAM into national or regional public healthcare systems, with some treatments even reimbursed by public health insurance. Despite these developments, regulation remains inconsistent, and many CAM therapies lack high-quality scientific evidence, posing challenges for safety and clinical acceptance.

In Sri Lanka, conventional medicine coexists with a rich array of CAM healing systems, including Ayurveda (of North Indian origin), traditional Sinhala medicine, Unani (of Arabic country origin), Siddha (of South Indian origin), homoeopathy, and spiritual healing. Both public and private sectors offer CAM and conventional medicine, with Ayurveda holding a prominent, state-supported role with regard to CAM [5]. Government funding extends to Ayurveda alongside conventional medicine, while the private sector features a broader mix of CAM practices with varied practitioner qualifications and regulatory standards. Oversight of CAM systems is by the Ministry of Health via the Department of Ayurveda, which administers public CAM services [6]. Sri Lanka’s indigenous systems rely heavily on herbal and herbomineral remedies, often passed down through apprenticeships. Scientific validation for many of these practices remains limited, with treatments frequently grounded in practitioner knowledge and cultural traditions. Religious and ritual elements, particularly those involving Hindu deities, are often embedded in CAM practices, despite the country’s Buddhist majority [6]. However, the lack of standardised regulations and robust evidence remains a continuing challenge associated with the use of CAM [7].

Complementary and alternative medicine use in diabetes

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), a common metabolic disorder characterised by persistently high blood glucose levels due to impaired insulin function, accounts for over 90% of diabetes cases and is a leading cause of global morbidity and mortality [8, 9]. The disease is associated with numerous acute and chronic complications and imposes a significant psychological and social burden, including higher rates of anxiety, depression, and stigmatisation [10]. Given these challenges, many patients with T2DM turn to CAM due to lower costs, perceived safety, and possible dissatisfaction with conventional treatments [11–13]. While current diabetes treatments have significantly improved life expectancy, they are often associated with inevitable side effects. Patients having T2DM are increasingly becoming aware of these side effects and exploring alternative therapies such as CAM for managing their diabetes. This trend is reinforced by the growing global interest in CAM as an option for many non-communicable diseases, including diabetes [14].

Recent studies have reported high prevalence rates of CAM use among diabetic patients in the region. A systematic review found CAM usage among people living with diabetes ranging widely from 17 to 73%, with an average prevalence exceeding 47% [15]. In Sri Lanka, approximately 76% of diabetic patients admitted to using CAM therapies to control blood glucose levels [16]. CAM is perceived to offer potential benefits with a lower risk of adverse effects and is often culturally accepted, particularly within the Asian populations. CAM continues to play a significant role in healthcare across the Southeast Asian countries, particularly in the management of chronic conditions such as diabetes [15, 17].

The CAM practices relevant to T2DM management can be broadly categorised into five major groups: whole medical systems, mind-body techniques, biologically based therapies, manipulative and body-based practices, and energy therapies [18]. Whole medical systems such as Ayurveda and homoeopathy offer comprehensive treatment philosophies. Ayurveda, with its emphasis on herbal supplements and lifestyle modifications, is gaining recognition for improving diabetes outcomes [19, 20], while individualised homoeopathic treatment has shown enhanced glycaemic control when used alongside conventional care [21]. Among mind-body approaches, music therapy has demonstrated benefits in reducing psychological distress and has been used to support the management of diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) [22, 23]. Biologically based therapies include herbal medicine, where plants like bitter gourd and fenugreek are widely used despite limited scientific validation and the potential for interactions with conventional medications [16, 24]. Manipulative and body-based practices, such as massage therapy including Swedish and Chinese techniques, have been reported to reduce blood glucose levels and alleviate muscle tension in diabetic individuals [25]. Finally, acupuncture, a practice that overlaps both energy and manipulative therapies, is commonly used to enhance insulin sensitivity and assist in pain management [26]. Grouping these therapies by category reflects the conceptual frameworks used by different types of CAM. These CAM practices are increasingly explored by diabetes patients globally, highlighting the need for further research on their effectiveness and safety [12].

Challenges in integrating CAM into conventional medicine

Despite positive attitudes towards CAM, many T2DM patients have limited awareness and often rely on informal sources for information [3, 27]. More importantly, both physicians and T2DM patients do not have a clear idea of the term complementary and alternative medicine [28]. This highlights the need for better education of both healthcare providers and T2DM patients for successful integration of CAM into diabetes management strategies [13]. The integration of CAM into conventional care is also hindered by a lack of comprehensive education on CAM during medical training. Many educational institutions that offer degrees in conventional medicine briefly mention CAM and do not provide exposure to CAM treatment settings to enable students to adequately understand the principles of CAM treatment and its cultural background [29]. This underscores the need for further research and enhanced awareness to better understand and utilise CAM in managing T2DM.

While various CAM modalities are described positively, there is limited critical appraisal of the current evidence supporting their use [30]. There are notable drawbacks, including the lack of high-quality evidence supporting their safety and efficacy [31]. Some herbal remedies, for example, may interact with prescription medications, and standardised dosing is often lacking compared to conventional therapies [32]. Furthermore, while the general use of CAM does not appear to increase the risk of hypoglycaemia, the use of Costus speciosus leaves has been notably linked to a higher occurrence of hypoglycaemic episodes, highlighting the need for further investigation into its safety and effects [16]. Moreover, many CAM interventions are adopted widely before being rigorously studied, resulting in challenges related to practitioner regulation and scientific oversight [33]. Additionally, CAM practitioners are not always held to the same regulatory standards as conventional healthcare providers, which may impact patient safety and treatment outcomes [34].

In certain regions of the world, research on CAM is limited and often poorly documented. Unlike in some Western and Asian countries such as China, India and Korea, where government collaboration with both conventional and complementary medicine is active towards integration [35], Sri Lanka has minimal integration of CAM into conventional treatment. CAM initiatives in Sri Lanka, although present, typically operate as a parallel system without any formal collaboration between the two groups of practitioners. Therefore, this study aims to assess the CAM use among patients with T2DM attending the Teaching Hospital Kuliyapitiya, a semi-urban tertiary care centre in Sri Lanka, to inform future efforts towards integrating CAM into conventional diabetes care.

The Teaching Hospital Kuliyapitiya was selected as the study setting due to several compelling reasons. Firstly, there is a notable absence of published research on the use of CAM among T2DM patients in this region, highlighting a significant gap in the local literature. Secondly, the hospital serves a demographically diverse population, encompassing both rural and urban communities, which allows for a more representative understanding of CAM practices in different sociocultural contexts.

In Sri Lanka, CAM practices span between the arms of Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha and traditional Sri Lankan medicine [36]. Additionally, a variety of spiritual healing practices is deeply embedded in cultural beliefs and health-seeking behaviours. Furthermore, the co-existence of state-funded conventional and Ayurvedic medical services within the national healthcare system adds a unique dimension to how diabetes patients choose and combine treatment options. As non-communicable diseases, including diabetes, show a rising prevalence across the country, it becomes increasingly important to explore how CAM is utilised at the community level. Understanding these patterns in a suburban healthcare setting can contribute valuable insights to the national discourse on integrating safe and evidence-based CAM practices into conventional diabetes care.

Methods

Study design and population

A cross-sectional study was conducted among 280 randomly selected patients diagnosed with T2DM after obtaining approval from the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Wayamba University of Sri Lanka (PW/2024/02/01). The sample size was calculated to be 280 T2DM patients, based on the formula by Lwanga and Lemeshow (1991), using a 95% confidence level, an estimated prevalence of 76% for CAM use, and a 5% margin of error [37]. The T2DM patients aged 18 years and older who consented to participate and were diagnosed by a specialist physician as having T2DM using laboratory investigations were included in the study. Patients with T2DM were recruited using systematic random sampling with a skip interval of three. The clinic attendance register served as the sampling frame. A random starting point was selected by generating a number between 1 and 3, with patient number 2 chosen as the first participant. From this starting point, every third patient on the register was selected until the required sample size of 280 participants was achieved. An interviewer-administered questionnaire was used for collecting data.

Study instrument and data collection

The objectives were to assess the prevalence of CAM use by T2DM patients, their knowledge and perceptions of CAM use and their associations with sociodemographic characteristics and disease-related factors. An interviewer-administered questionnaire was developed using existing literature [11, 38] and pretested among ten T2DM patients, as there were no validated instruments [39]. The questionnaire consisted of three parts: part one addressed the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics; part two assessed the use of various CAM therapies, including frequency, sources of information, and perceived benefits or adverse effects; and part three evaluated T2DM patients’ beliefs and perceptions towards CAM using a 5-point Likert scale. Permission for data collection was obtained from the hospital administration and respective physicians caring for the T2DM patients. Participants were first provided with an information sheet, and upon agreeing to participate, a consent form was completed by the study participants. Data collection continued until the target sample size was reached. Data were collected over a 3-month period from July to September 2024. Diabetes control status was assessed based on clinic records. All questionnaires were completed with no missing responses.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using SPSS (25th version). Categorical data were presented using frequency distribution tables, and mean (SD), median (IQR), and range were calculated for numerical variables as appropriate. As HbA1C testing is not freely available in state-funded hospitals, the blood glucose control status was assessed using fasting blood glucose (FBG). A FBG level of < 110 mg/dL was considered as the normal FBG value for the analysis as per standards of the Anti-Diabetic Federation [40]. The BMI data of T2DM patients were obtained from clinic records, where values are routinely documented by nursing officers during follow-up visits at the clinic. Following the standard protocol, height and weight are measured using a wall-mounted stadiometer and a calibrated digital weighing scale, respectively, with T2DM patients removing footwear and heavy clothing. BMI was calculated using the standard formula: weight (kg) divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²). Associated factors for CAM were identified using the chi-square test. Sociodemographic factors (gender, age, religion, employment status, and educational level) and disease-related factors (BMI status, physical well-being, duration of the T2DM, and fasting blood glucose status) were considered as independent variables in the analysis. Perceptions of individual patients were evaluated using a scoring system (strongly agree to strongly disagree; 5 to 1) applied to 14 probing statements on CAM use. Total perception scores were calculated for individual patients with a possible lowest score of 14 and a maximum expected of 70. The scores between CAM users and non-users were compared using an independent sample t-test after normality assessment using Anderson-Darling test. The level of significance was considered as 0.05. Imputation methods were not required during data analysis as there was no missing data.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

280 patients diagnosed with T2DM participated in the study. The majority were females (87.5%) and above 60 years (n = 181, 64.6%) of age. (Table 1). The observed female predominance in the study sample corresponds with the actual patient demographics at the diabetic clinic where data collection was conducted. Monthly clinic attendance records indicate that nearly 800 patients seek care at the diabetic clinic, of whom approximately 80% are female. This pattern has remained consistent over the years, with poor attendance of male patients in government clinics. Nearly 40% of T2DM patients had some degree of diabetic neuropathy (n = 120, 42.9%) and musculoskeletal problems (n = 105, 41.1%) based on clinical records.

Complementary and alternative medicine use

Of the total sample, 68.6% (n = 192) reported using CAM. Among the CAM users (n = 192), 7.3% (n = 14) used it as a preventive measure even prior to being diagnosed as having diabetes, while 24.5% (n = 47) used it as a specific treatment option for controlling their FBG status. The remaining T2DM patients had used CAM for maintaining their general health status (n = 53, 27.6%), while 9.3% (n = 18) had used it for reducing general stress. Interestingly, 31.2% (n = 60) had used CAM for multiple reasons: as a preventive measure for diabetes, as a specific treatment for diabetes, to improve general health status and to reduce stress level, etc.

However, prescribed treatment from an Ayurvedic practitioner was received by only 22.9% (n = 44). The majority of the T2DM patients were on CAM as a result of the influence of relatives (n = 129, 67.1%), media (n = 99, 51.6%), and peers (n = 95, 49.5%).

Among T2DM patients who were on CAM, herbal remedies were identified as the most common type (n = 146, 76.0%), followed by spiritual healing (n = 92, 47.9%) and music therapy (n = 63, 32.8%), as shown in Fig. 1. 48% (n = 93) used more than one type of CAM modality. On inquiry it was noted that none of the T2DM patients had experienced adverse events following CAM use.

Types of herbs consumed by T2DM patients (n = 146)

Raw herbal preparations

The study revealed that patients with T2DM commonly utilised various plant-based remedies, either as individual herbs or in different combinations. Gurmar (Gymnema sylvestre), Canereed (Costus speciosus), Ivy gourd (Coccinia grandis), Jackfruit (Artocarpus heterophyllus) and Neem (Azadirachta indica) were primarily ingested raw, usually incorporated into salads. These preparations were consumed infrequently, often no more than once per month. In addition, dragon fruit was consumed raw as a fresh fruit

Use of herbs as decoctions and extracts

Some T2DM patients drank extracts made by boiling garlic, cinnamon, and fenugreek before going to bed at night.

Culinary integration

Cinnamon bark, fenugreek seeds, and garlic pods were commonly incorporated into day-to-day meals intentionally as spices in preparing curries. Garlic was also occasionally prepared as a standalone curry, while bitter gourd was consumed in both raw and cooked forms, often with the addition of spices.

These practices highlight the diverse and integrated use of traditional plant-based remedies in their day-to-day life in managing diabetes.

Discussion

Socio-demographic characteristics and CAM use

This hospital-based cross-sectional study primarily focuses on the use of CAM among T2DM patients. A review published in 2007 reported that the global rate of conventional medicine use among diabetic patients ranges from 17 to 72.8%, which aligns with our findings that 68.6% of individuals with T2DM reported using CAM [15].

Although no sociodemographic or clinical factors were found to be significantly associated with CAM use in the present study, this finding is consistent with prior literature. An Iranian study found no significant differences in CAM usage based on body mass index or education level [41]. Similarly, a study conducted in Australia reported no association between CAM use and variables such as female gender, wealth, or higher education [42]. A study from Turkey noted that factors like gender, average age, age at diabetes onset, and education level did not significantly influence the use of alternative medicine [12]. A study from Saudi Arabia [43] has reported no significant associations between CAM use and patients’ gender or age, which is consistent with the findings of our study [44]. A South African study has explicitly stated that there were no significant predictors for CAM usage among the variables gender, education level, and age [45]. Collectively, these findings suggest that the lack of statistically significant associations observed in the current study was expected and aligns with this evidence base.

Conversely, some studies have reported significant associations. A study from Pakistan identified female gender, older age, unemployment, longer diabetes duration, diabetes-related complications, and poor glycaemic control as significantly linked to CAM use [46]. Similarly, a Sri Lankan study conducted previously reported that CAM use was more common among women, although age and diabetes duration were not correlated [16]. Research from Malaysia and Bahrain also indicated a higher likelihood of CAM use among females [11]. Other studies have associated older age (≥ 65 years) and higher educational attainment with increased CAM usage [47]. Additionally, a study from Taiwan reported that prior CAM use, positive attitudes toward CAM, belief in its efficacy, and longer duration of diabetes were predictors of CAM use [11]. A study from India showed that higher education and socio-economic status correlated with greater CAM knowledge and utilisation, while religion and family history of diabetes did not significantly affect CAM-related knowledge or practice, aligning with our study’s findings [13]. These variable results likely reflect cultural and regional differences among study populations. Therefore, these contradictory results emanating from these studies indicate that these associations may need more in-depth qualitative studies to arrive at more definitive conclusions.

Presence of diabetes-related complications and CAM use

In our study the presence or history of diabetes-related conditions such as retinopathy, nephropathy, or diabetic foot did not significantly influence CAM usage. This suggests that the progression, severity, or complications of diabetes are not critical factors in T2DM patients’ decisions to pursue alternative therapies, as reported previously [12]. A study conducted previously in Sri Lanka too concluded that CAM use is not associated with the presence of comorbidities or diabetes-related complications [16].

Concomitant use of conventional medicines and CAM

An earlier study reported that 45.6% of participants used CAM alongside conventional treatments [14]. In our study, a comparable 40% reported using CAM in parallel with conventional medicine, reflecting potential discontent with the mainstream conventional healthcare system. These findings indicate that T2DM patients are more inclined to use CAM as a supplementary treatment rather than a replacement for conventional care.

Integration of traditional medical systems in Sri Lanka

In Sri Lanka, the CAM sector plays an officially recognised role within the national healthcare system, which predominantly comprises Ayurveda, Unani and Siddha medicine. The Department of Health oversees the CAM healthcare programme, which provides both inmpatient and outpatient care for a variety of diseases within hospitals. A separate group of community physicians in the CAM sector focus on disease prevention and health promotion. However, these CAM systems and conventional medical systems are not integrated into the health infrastructure. The practitioners of both sectors provide parallel services in the respective government hospitals and community clinics dedicated to these two systems. In addition, there are state-registered local traditional medical practitioners who practice systems of medicine that were practised in Sri Lanka over many centuries preceding the period of colonisation by the Portuguese, the Dutch and the British. These systems have been handed down over generations, and this indigenous knowledge is mostly confined to these families. Understanding this context is essential for interpreting the role of healthcare providers in influencing the CAM use in this setting [48].

External factors influencing CAM use

Another significant finding relates to the sources of information that T2DM patients rely on regarding alternative treatments. Relatives had the greatest influence on CAM use among T2DM patients. Family and neighbours were identified as the primary sources of CAM knowledge in other studies. It was reported that 64.2% of participants were encouraged to use CAM for diabetes management by people in their social circles [14], supporting the findings of our study where a notably high percentage (67.1%) were influenced by relatives. Furthermore, friends and neighbours were identified as two primary sources of information about CAM [13]. Our study also noted that neighbours often played a significant role in discouraging conventional treatment while promoting the use of CAM.

Similarly, media too play a notable role, which was also reported by another study where many diabetic patients stated that they primarily learnt about these therapies through media outlets such as television, radio, and newspapers [12]. This indicates a trend towards being influenced by external information sources and a tendency to trust media-related information when making health-related decisions. Only 8.7% of participants in a previous study cited physicians as a source of CAM information [14]. In contrast, our findings show that 20.3% of respondents were influenced by advice from Ayurvedic medical officers, highlighting the role of practitioners in promoting CAM usage.

Beliefs and perceptions on CAM use

A significant number of participants (86.8%) believed that using CAM would offer additional relief [13]. Our study findings support this notion of seeking extra benefit through use of CAM, as the majority of participants in this study reported using CAM to maintain general well-being (40.5%). However, other studies have reported that expectations of diabetic patients were not met by the use of CAM with regard to therapeutic benefits [13].

Nevertheless, the belief that CAM promotes the body’s natural healing processes and spiritual well-being further supports the idea that satisfaction with CAM is tied not only to physical outcomes but also to emotional and spiritual fulfilment. Findings from other countries align with these insights. In Malaysia, perceptions of added health benefits (53.5%) and safety (63.2%) were key reasons for the use of CAM among patients with diabetes [49]. Similarly, a study in Indonesia found that beliefs in the effectiveness and safety of CAM and its potential in enhancing overall health were significant predictors of CAM use [50]. Qualitative research in Saudi Arabia also highlighted that patients were drawn to CAM due to perceived benefits and cultural compatibility, although many were unaware of potential adverse effects [51].

These cross-national findings underscore how perceptions of efficacy reinforce routine CAM use, concerns about safety can cause hesitation, and satisfaction, particularly in terms of holistic health, encourages continued use. Collectively, these dimensions help explain how diverse beliefs shape CAM-related behaviours in various sociocultural contexts. In our study, nearly all CAM users expressed positive perceptions toward the use of CAM, further supporting the role of belief systems in influencing CAM adoption.

Types of CAM used by diabetic patients

Similar to findings from a study in Indonesia, the use of herbal treatments was notably high among CAM users [50]. In a previous Sri Lankan study, 67% of participants preferred herbal remedies [16], while our study reported an even higher rate, with 76% of T2DM patients using herbal medicines. Current trends show that bitter gourd (Momordica charantia) and cinnamon are among the most commonly used herbal remedies worldwide, although the specific herbal products used by T2DM patients vary significantly between countries [12]. Similarly, our study identified bitter gourd as the most frequently used herb, followed by canereed leaves (Costus speciosus) and fenugreek (Trigonella foenum), further demonstrating the variations in herb preferences across different geographical regions. An Iraqi study found that garlic was the most commonly used herb among diabetic patients. Similarly, a study conducted in Malaysia also identified garlic as the most frequently used herb, followed by bitter gourd [52].

Egede (2002) reported that spiritual healing was the second most frequently used form of CAM among individuals with diabetes, with 21% of participants following this path [47]. Similarly, our study found that spiritual healing was also the second most common CAM modality, with 47.9% of participants engaging in it.

In a study conducted in India among diabetes patients, only about 11% of participants reported using Ayurveda, despite the country’s strong foundation in Ayurvedic medicine [13]. Similarly, only 22.9% of respondents in our study explored Ayurveda medicine as a form of CAM, although Sri Lanka too has a strong Ayurveda system of medication running parallel to the conventional medical services.

A national survey conducted in the US highlighted that the belief in CAM’s ability to maintain physical health is a key predictor of CAM usage among patients with T2DM [53]. Their findings align with the notion that T2DM patients utilise CAM not only for managing illnesses but also for promoting overall well-being. CAM has also been reported as effective in managing stress. Similarly, our study revealed that most participants used CAM primarily to maintain general health, while others utilised it for managing diabetes, stress reduction, and disease prevention. This suggests that the appeal of CAM extends beyond specific therapeutic purposes to encompass holistic health maintenance.

Research has shown that many patients with T2DM lack awareness of CAM modalities, and treatment is mostly self-prescribed without any physician guidance [27]. In this study, about half of the respondents (52.1%) expressed uncertainty regarding the usefulness of CAM. This contrasts sharply with findings from a previous study conducted over 15 years ago, which indicated that a significant majority (91.3%) were aware of CAM and maintained a positive attitude toward its use [14]. This may be due to regional variations of study populations but underscores the need for enhanced public education and awareness about appropriate CAM use among diabetic patients in the current context.

Implications for clinical practice

Our findings highlight the importance of healthcare professionals acknowledging and understanding the use of CAM by patients, particularly in diabetes management. Clinicians in the conventional medical sector should initiate open, non-judgemental conversations about CAM during consultations to identify potential risks such as drug-herb interactions, promote patient safety, and support treatment adherence. Incorporating culturally sensitive communication about CAM can enhance patient trust and facilitate shared decision-making. To provide holistic, patient-centered care, healthcare providers must be equipped with knowledge about common CAM practices through targeted training programmes that improve awareness and communication skills.

Limitations and recommendations

This study has some limitations: the majority of the study participants were over 60 years of age, so there may have been a tendency towards the use of CAM compared to the younger diabetics. Older T2DM individuals are more likely to explore CAM options due to factors such as living alone, cost concerns, and travelling difficulties to seek care from conventional healthcare providers, as reported previously [11, 14]. As the study was conducted in a single tertiary care hospital in a semi-urban setting, the findings may not be generalisable to all regions or healthcare settings in Sri Lanka. As the information on CAM use was derived from patient self-reports, the findings may be influenced by recall inaccuracies or a tendency to provide socially desirable responses. These limitations suggest that the extent to which CAM is integrated into the daily lives of T2DM patients may be over- or underestimated. Therefore, further community-based qualitative research using individuals sampled from different geographical areas of the country is necessary to gain a more comprehensive understanding of CAM use among patients with T2DM.

With regard to recommendations, the study emphasises the importance of establishing nationwide education programmes to increase public awareness of CAM therapies, focusing on their usefulness and limitations. It also highlights the need for general physicians to initiate discussions with CAM practitioners in incorporating CAM into T2DM patient care and document its usage in medical reports. This approach can ensure that T2DM patients are informed about potential side effects and possible interactions with modern medications while helping to build the research evidence on related CAM practices that may be beneficial holistically. Additionally, the study calls for the development of policies and guidelines on theintegration of CAM practices that align with the cultural context.

Future directions

Future research should investigate in greater depth the underlying factors influencing decisions to use CAM through qualitative methodologies such as in-depth interviews and focus group discussions. Given the high prevalence of CAM use among individuals with T2DM, it is imperative that subsequent studies incorporate qualitative components to elucidate the decision-making processes, belief systems, and cultural determinants that contribute to use of CAM by patients. Additionally, well-designed intervention studies are essential to evaluate the safety, efficacy, and potential interactions of commonly used CAM therapies with conventional diabetes treatments. Such evidence is critical to inform clinical practice, optimise therapeutic outcomes, and ensure patient safety in the holistic management of T2DM.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this hospital-based cross-sectional study from a semi-urban setting in Sri Lanka highlights the high prevalence of CAM use among individuals having T2DM, with 68.6% of participants reporting CAM use. The results align with findings of global research, showcasing diverse patterns of CAM use across different countries. While some studies indicated a correlation between CAM usage and factors like gender, age, and socio-economic status, our study found no significant associations with socio-demographic factors. Herbal remedies and spiritual healing were the most popular CAM modalities, with bitter gourd being the preferred herbal product. This study also underscores the pivotal role of family, friends, and media in influencing CAM usage while highlighting the limited influence of healthcare professionals.

Additionally, the study revealed a considerable lack of awareness and understanding of CAM therapies among T2DM patients, underscoring the urgent need for improved public health education and more proactive involvement from healthcare providers. As reliance on CAM continues to grow globally, the development of evidence-based guidelines that are culturally sensitive and context-specific is essential to promote the safe and informed use of these alternative therapies.
