Machine learning-based cross-sectional exploration of depression-chronic pain associations in chronic respiratory disease patients
Authors: Hua Man Wu, Ying Yang, Zhi Ping Deng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic respiratory diseases (CRD) are a leading global cause of death, with high comorbidity rates of depression and chronic pain forming a bidirectional relationship that is associated with worse prognosis. Existing research lacks systematic analysis of their association in CRD patients, and traditional statistical methods are limited in identifying complex relationships, highlighting the need for machine learning-based exploratory analysis.MethodsThis cross-sectional study used data from the 2015 wave of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), including 1,702 eligible CRD patients. Depression was assessed by the CESD-10 scale, and chronic pain was defined as self-reported long-term pain. Six machine learning algorithms (XGBoost, SVM, MLP, LightGBM, RF, LR) were employed, with model performance evaluated by AUROC, accuracy, and other metrics. LASSO regression selected key features, and subgroup and restricted cubic spline analyses explored heterogeneity and dose-response associations.ResultsAmong participants, 43.2% had chronic pain. CESD-10 score showed the strongest association with chronic pain (AUC = 0.762), with a significant dose-response relationship: the highest CESD-10 tertile had a 7.18-fold higher risk of chronic pain (95% CI: 2.45–4.78, P < 0.001) after full adjustment. The XGBoost model achieved the best predictive performance among the six tested models (test set AUROC = 0.830). Subgroup analysis showed a stronger depression-pain association in urban than rural residents (P for interaction = 0.016). Key predictive factors include self-rated health status, activities of daily living (ADL), and gender, among others.ConclusionDepression severity is robustly associated with chronic pain in CRD patients in a dose-dependent manner. The XGBoost model provides reliable early identification tools. These findings support routine depression assessment and geographically tailored bio-psycho-social interventions to disrupt the depression-pain cycle and improve patient outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07855-3.

Introduction

Chronic respiratory diseases (CRD) represent a group of conditions characterized by persistent airway inflammation, airflow limitation, and impaired respiratory function, primarily encompassing chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), asthma, and interstitial lung diseases [1]. According to the World Health Organization, CRDs have become the third leading cause of death globally, affecting over one billion people worldwide [2]. These diseases are characterized by prolonged disease progression, recurrent exacerbations, and progressive deterioration, with patients commonly experiencing dyspnea, chronic cough, and limited physical activity, which substantially impair activities of daily living and social functioning.

Depression is a highly prevalent psychiatric comorbidity among patients with CRDs, especially COPD, with reported prevalence rates ranging from about 23% to over 49%, significantly exceeding rates in the general population [3, 4]. Depression not only severely compromises treatment adherence and self-management capabilities but also exacerbates negative emotions such as anxiety and helplessness, delays respiratory function recovery, and increases the risk of acute exacerbations and hospitalizations, imposing substantial burdens on patients, families, and healthcare systems [5, 6].

The comorbidity of depression and chronic pain is particularly prominent among patients with CRDs, representing a critical factor influencing prognosis. Studies demonstrate that the prevalence of chronic pain in patients with CRDs ranges from 32% to 85%, primarily manifesting as chest pain, musculoskeletal pain, and headache [7, 8]. Patients with CRDs who have comorbid depression exhibit higher rates, greater severity, and longer duration of chronic pain, while persistent chronic pain is significantly associated with higher depressive symptoms, establishing a bidirectional relationship between depression and pain [9]. This comorbid condition is associated with more severe functional impairment, longer hospital stays, higher readmission rates, and increased healthcare costs. Given the characteristic disease course of CRDs marked by recurrent exacerbations and progressive deterioration, the reciprocal interactions between depression and pain are more prone to forming vicious cycles, severely compromising patients’ physical and mental health and quality of life [10].

Extensive epidemiological and longitudinal studies provide robust evidence supporting the close association between depression and pain [11–13]. An analysis encompassing approximately 237,952 individuals across 47 low- and middle-income countries revealed that individuals with depression had nearly a 4-fold higher odds ratio for developing severe pain compared to those without depression [14]. In large-scale elderly cohorts from the United Kingdom and China, individuals with baseline depressive symptoms demonstrated approximately 71% higher odds of reporting pain subsequently [15]. Although the bidirectional comorbidity between depression and chronic pain has been extensively studied in general populations, critical knowledge gaps remain in patients with CRD, who have unique pathophysiological and clinical features that may substantially modify this association. Existing studies have mostly excluded CRD patients or included them only as a small subset, lacking dedicated analyses of the depression-pain link and its risk heterogeneity in this high-risk population. Furthermore, traditional statistical methods have limited capacity to capture complex non-linear associations and high-order interactions, while the advantages of machine learning in exploring multifactorial comorbidity patterns have not been fully utilized in this field.

This cross-sectional study employed machine learning algorithms to systematically explore the association patterns between depression and chronic pain in patients with CRDs, identifying key associated factors and heterogeneity characteristics of the association to provide scientific evidence for developing targeted integrated biopsychosocial interventions strategies. The findings are anticipated to provide a reference for joint assessment of depression and chronic pain, thereby providing insights into the bidirectional relationship between these conditions, alleviating disability associated with psychosomatic symptoms, and improving patients’ quality of life. Additionally, this research aims to offer novel insights for holistic management of CRDs while reducing healthcare burden.

Methods

Study participants

Data for this study were obtained from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) database (http://charls.pku.edu.cn), an ongoing prospective, nationally representative community-based cohort of middle-aged and elderly Chinese adults. The database received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015). Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and this study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines [16].

In this study, the 2015 wave of CHARLS served as the baseline data source for model construction. Baseline data were used to identify patients with CRD and collect relevant covariate information. Among the 21,097 participants enrolled in the 2015 CHARLS study, individuals who were not diagnosed with CRD and those with missing values for the pain outcome variable were excluded (n = 18,537). Additionally, the minimum age for all participants is 60, participants aged < 60 years or with missing age data were excluded (n = 858). Ultimately, 1,702 eligible CRD patients were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Definition of chronic respiratory disease

The diagnosis of CRD was based on self-reported physician diagnosis. Researchers asked participants: “Have you ever been told by a doctor that you have chronic respiratory diseases such as chronic bronchitis, emphysema, or cor pulmonale (excluding tumors or cancers)?” and “Have you ever been told by a doctor that you have asthma?” Participants who answered affirmatively to either question were classified as having CRD.

Definition of depression scale

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CESD-10), which has shown good reliability and validity for detecting depressive symptoms in Chinese adults. The CESD-10 consists of 10 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (“rarely or none of the time”) to 3 (“most or all of the time”), reflecting symptom frequency over the past week. Total scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms [17].

Definition of chronic pain

Chronic pain was defined as the presence of long-term symptoms, including headache, shoulder pain, arm pain, wrist pain, finger pain, chest pain, abdominal pain, back pain, lower back pain, hip pain, leg pain, knee pain, ankle pain, toe pain, or neck pain. Participants were classified as having chronic pain if they answered “yes” to at least one of the aforementioned options [18–20].

Covariates in CHARLS

Drawing on existing literature, our candidate variables included: (1) demographic characteristics, including age, gender, marital status, residence, education, total household income, and retirement; (2) lifestyle factors, such as smoking history drinking history, physical activity, social activities, sleep time, and body mass index (BMI); (3) health status indicators, including self-rated health (Srh), activities of daily living (ADL score), instrumental activities of daily living (IADL score), peak expiratory flow rate (PEF), depression scale score (CESD-10), cognitive score, disability status, fall history, audiovisual Impairment (DSI), and various chronic diseases, including hypertension, diabetes, cancer, heart disease, stroke, mental disorder, arthritis, dyslipidemia, liver disease, kidney disease, digestive disease, memory disorder, etc., all defined as self-reported physician-diagnosed conditions.

Age is recorded as the participant’s age at the time of the survey. Gender is categorized as male or female. Marital status is classified as “married” or “unmarried” (including never married, divorced, separated, or widowed). Residence is categorized as “urban” or “rural” based on the participant’s current living location. Education level is classified into four categories: “illiterate,” “primary school,” “middle school,” and “high school or beyond.” Retirement status is categorized as “retired” or “not retired” based on the participant’s current employment status. Self-perceived health status is categorized as “Good,” “Fair,” or “Poor” based on participants’ subjective assessment of their overall health condition. BMI is calculated based on measured height and weight using the standard formula (weight in kg/height in m²). Blood pressure measurements follow standardized protocols, with participants resting for at least 5 min before measurement. The average of three readings for both systolic and diastolic pressures is recorded using validated sphygmomanometers. ADL are assessed using a standardized scale evaluating six basic functions: bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, continence, and feeding. Each item is scored as 0 (independent) or 1 (dependent), with total scores ranging from 0 to 6, where higher scores indicate greater functional dependence. IADL are evaluated using a scale assessing more complex daily functions including shopping, cooking, housework, medication management, and financial management. Each item is scored as 0 (independent) or 1 (dependent), with total scores ranging from 0 to 5, where higher scores indicate greater functional impairment. Cognitive function is comprehensively evaluated through multiple domains including visual-spatial skills, memory, orientation, and attention [21–23]. Visual-spatial skills are assessed by asking participants to draw two overlapping pentagons; one point is given for a correct drawing that shows proper overlap and maintains the pentagonal shapes, and zero for an incorrect one. Memory is assessed through both immediate and delayed recall of 10 Chinese words presented to participants; the score represents the average of immediate recall (tested immediately after presentation) and delayed recall (tested after a 5-minute interval), with one point granted for each word correctly remembered. Attention and orientation are evaluated using a modified version of the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TIC-10), which derives scores from responses to questions regarding temporal orientation (year, month, day, week, and season) and attention tasks (repeatedly subtracting 7 from 100 up to five times). One point is granted for each correct response, with the total score ranging from 0 to 10. The aggregate of the aforementioned dimensions forms the overall cognitive function score, ranging from 0 to 21, with higher scores indicating enhanced cognitive function. Smoking status is categorized as “never smoker” or “current smoker” based on self-reported smoking history. Drinking status is assessed as current alcohol consumption (yes/no). Physical activity is evaluated based on participants’ engagement in regular exercise or physical activities. Sleep time is recorded as the average number of hours of sleep per night as reported by participants. Chronic diseases including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cancer, heart disease, stroke, mental disorders, arthritis, dyslipidemia, liver disease, kidney disease, digestive diseases, and memory disorders are all defined based on self-reported physician diagnosis. Participants are asked: “Have you ever been told by a doctor that you have [specific condition]?” Disability status is assessed based on participants’ self-reported limitations in daily activities or physical disabilities due to health conditions. Fall history is defined as having experienced at least one fall in the previous two years. Audiovisual impairment is assessed through self-reported difficulties with hearing or vision that interfere with daily activities. Household income is recorded as total annual household income and categorized into quartiles for analysis. Social activities participation is assessed as engagement in community activities, social gatherings, or volunteer work. Life satisfaction is evaluated on a three-point scale as “poor,” “fair,” or “good” based on participants’ overall satisfaction with their current life situation.

Statistical analysis

In descriptive analyses, continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (with range) or median (with interquartile range) and compared using Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and compared using chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.

Data processing

Missing data were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) with 5 iterations, with the proportion of missing variables not exceeding 20%. Detailed missingness rates for all variables are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Missing pattern was MAR. Interaction terms were selected based on clinical relevance and prior evidence. As a secondary descriptive analysis, univariate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the independent discriminatory ability of each candidate variable. Importantly, this analysis was purely descriptive and played no role in the feature selection process. The entire dataset was randomly split into a training set and a test set at a ratio of 7:3 using stratified sampling. Important features were selected through the Lasso algorithm to construct a subset of covariates for the final model, and SHAP was used to interpret the risk measurements of variables. LASSO was performed only in the training set to avoid data leakage. The test set was completely held out and isolated throughout the entire feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, and model training process, and no information from the test set was used to inform any preprocessing or analytical steps. Patients with CRDs were subjected to logistic regression analysis based on CESD-10 score tertiles, with stratified subgroup analysis conducted to assess heterogeneity in chronic pain occurrence across different populations. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were applied to validate risk trends.

Algorithm model construction

Six machine learning algorithms were employed: Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Random Forest (RF). The optimal hyperparameter configurations for each model were determined through grid search optimization. On the training set, we employed 10-fold cross-validation and resampling methods for model training. The detailed hyperparameter search grids and optimal values for all six machine learning models are presented in Supplementary Table 3. The Z-score algorithm was applied to standardize input values, thereby achieving consistency in attribute evaluation and preventing model overfitting.

Model evaluation metrics

The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) served as the primary indicator for model performance evaluation. Additionally, various performance metrics were examined, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1 score. The area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) was further evaluated. Calibration was assessed using calibration curves and Brier scores to evaluate the model’s calibration performance and the difference between predicted results and actual events, with decision curve analysis (DCA) supplementing the clinical utility assessment.

Statistical analysis and data visualization were performed using SPSS 27.0.1 and Python (version 3.12). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Baseline characteristics of the study population

The final analysis included 1,702 participants with CRD, of whom 735 (43.2%) experienced chronic pain. Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2 present the baseline characteristics stratified by chronic pain status. Comparison of clinical characteristics between chronic pain group and non-pain group

Functional impairments were more prevalent among participants with chronic pain, as evidenced by higher IADL scores [1.0 (0.0–2.0) vs. 0.0 (0.0–1.0), P < 0.001] and shorter sleep duration [5.0 (4.0–7.0) vs. 6.5 (5.0–8.0) hours, P < 0.001]. The chronic pain group demonstrated a higher burden of comorbidities, including arthritis (72.5% vs. 49.0%, P < 0.001), digestive diseases (51.8% vs. 34.5%, P < 0.001), heart disease (40.5% vs. 28.9%, P < 0.001), and kidney disease (21.9% vs. 12.8%, P < 0.001). Additionally, participants with chronic pain had higher rates of disability (59.3% vs. 45.8%, P < 0.001) and fall history (34.6% vs. 17.1%, P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in age, BMI, or smoking pack-years between the two groups.

In the crude model (Model 1), participants in the second CESD-10 tertile showed a significantly higher odds of chronic pain compared to the lowest tertile (OR = 2.27, 95% CI: 1.73–2.99, P < 0.001). The association was even more pronounced in the highest depression tertile, with a nearly 10-fold increased odds of chronic pain (OR = 9.92, 95% CI: 7.49–13.14, P < 0.001).

After adjusting for basic demographic characteristics including gender, marital status, rural residence, retirement status, education level, BMI, and age (Model 2), the associations remained robust. The second tertile maintained significant association (OR = 2.17, 95% CI: 1.64–2.87, P < 0.001), while the highest tertile showed slightly attenuated but still strong association (OR = 8.52, 95% CI: 6.39–11.36, P < 0.001).

In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), which included comprehensive covariates encompassing health conditions, functional status, lifestyle factors, and social determinants, the association persisted with statistical significance. Participants in the second CESD-10 tertile had 105% higher odds of chronic pain (OR = 2.05, 95% CI: 1.54–2.73, P = 0.018), while those in the highest tertile demonstrated over three-fold increased odds (OR = 7.18, 95% CI: 5.34–9.66, P < 0.001), indicating a dose-response relationship between depression severity and chronic pain occurrence in this population.

Subgroup analysis

To examine the heterogeneity of the depression-chronic pain association across different patient subgroups, we conducted comprehensive subgroup analyses stratified by key demographic and clinical characteristics (Table 3). In the total study population, each one-unit increase in CESD-10 score was associated with a 17% increased odds of chronic pain (OR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.15–1.19, P < 0.001). The most significant finding was geographic disparity, with urban residents demonstrating substantially stronger depression-pain associations (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.17–1.25) compared to rural counterparts (OR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.13–1.17, P for interaction = 0.016). This urban-rural difference likely reflects multifactorial influences including healthcare accessibility, environmental stressors, and psychosocial resilience patterns. Gender, Age and cardiac comorbidity subgroups showed comparable association strengths without significant interactions (P = 0.059, P = 0.236 and P = 0.392, respectively).

Dose-response relationship analysis

Figure 2 presents restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis examining the dose-response relationship between CESD-10 depression scores and chronic pain occurrence among CRD patients. The crude model demonstrated a significant non-linear association with chronic pain odds ratio increasing progressively with higher depression scores (P for overall < 0.001, P for non-linearity = 0.011). The risk curve showed a relatively stable baseline at lower CESD-10 scores (0–8), followed by a consistent upward trend beginning around score 10, with accelerating increases at higher depression levels, reaching odds ratios exceeding 8 at CESD-10 scores above 25.

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between CESD-10 scores and chronic pain occurrence in patients with CRD. Results demonstrated a potential dose-response relationship between CESD-10 scores and chronic pain prevalence in this population. These findings suggest that depression and chronic pain in CRD patients are closely interconnected rather than independent comorbidities, and their co-occurrence is associated with amplified disease burden and worse prognosis. Key strengths of this research include: (1) demonstration of the CESD-10 score-chronic pain association in CRD patients; (2) robust sample size utilizing a large Chinese database (n = 1,702); and (3) superior model performance achieved through machine learning algorithms, with XGBoost demonstrating the best predictive performance among the six models.

CRD patients typically exhibit systemic low-grade inflammation. αPrevious studies have suggested that inflammatory mediators including IL-6, TNF-α, and CRP may be involved in the pathogenesis of both depression and chronic pain, potentially explaining their co-occurrence in CRD patients. However, these mechanisms were not directly measured in this study and require further validation in future research [24–27]. Concurrently, these inflammatory mediators activate peripheral nociceptors and upregulate spinal dorsal horn release of substance P and calcitonin gene-related peptides, inducing pain sensitization and reducing pain thresholds—establishing the inflammatory foundation underlying these comorbidities [28, 29]. Both depression and chronic pain involve monoamine neurotransmitter dysregulation, particularly serotonin and norepinephrine [30, 31]. In CRD patients, chronic hypoxia from persistent airway obstruction, sleep fragmentation, and medication-induced metabolic disturbances collectively impair neurotransmitter synthesis, release, and reuptake. This dysfunction compromises the midbrain-periaqueductal gray-spinal dorsal horn descending pain inhibitory pathway, amplifying physical discomfort and establishing a pathological cycle of depression-neurotransmitter imbalance-pain sensitization [32–34]. Chronic hypoxia and sleep apnea demonstrate synergistic pathogenic effects: prolonged hypoxia triggers hippocampal neuronal apoptosis and white matter damage via oxidative stress, increasing depression risk while disrupting pain regulation [32]. Sleep disorders fragment normal sleep architecture, compromising pain compensation mechanisms and intensifying negative affect, thereby reinforcing the depression-pain association. Pharmacological factors further contribute to this relationship. Chronic glucocorticoid therapy disrupts HPA axis function, precipitating mood disorders [35]. Beta-2 agonist bronchodilators induce adverse effects including palpitations and tremor, exacerbating subjective discomfort [36]. Additionally, common CRD comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease and osteoporosis directly generate chronic pain while increasing psychological burden through treatment complexity [37, 38].

Subgroup analysis revealed significant geographic disparities in the depression-chronic pain association among CRD patients, with residential location serving as a critical effect modifier (P for interaction = 0.016). Urban residents exhibited stronger depression-pain associations (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.17–1.25) compared to rural residents (OR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.13–1.17), representing a 5% greater effect magnitude per unit increase in CESD-10 scores. These findings corroborate previous research by Wu et al., who analyzed 16,039 middle-aged and elderly individuals and demonstrated that depressed participants experienced greater pain severity and more pain sites than non-depressed counterparts, with urban residents showing heavier pain burden [39]. Similarly, Song et al.‘s longitudinal study identified depressive symptoms as independent pain predictors, revealing significant interactions between functional impairments and pain risk, particularly in urban populations [40]. Healthcare accessibility patterns may also contribute, as urban patients typically experience greater medical intervention exposure, potentially increasing somatic awareness and pain reporting behaviors [41]. These findings underscore the need for geographically-tailored interventions addressing depression-pain comorbidity in CRD populations, with urban patients potentially requiring more intensive psychosocial support and integrated pain management approaches.

Analysis revealed multiple significant risk factors for chronic pain beyond CESD-10 scores, including low self-rated health, impaired ADL, female gender, coexisting arthritis, fall history, insufficient sleep, non-retired status, and absence of tooth loss. Self-rated health demonstrated the highest predictive importance, suggesting that subjective health perception plays a pivotal role in chronic pain development. According to the Health Belief Model, poor self-health ratings reinforce negative cognition, intensify pain catastrophizing, reduce self-efficacy, and diminish rehabilitation engagement while potentially activating descending pain facilitation systems, thereby increasing pain risk [42]. ADL impairment exhibits bidirectional causality with chronic pain, functioning both as a risk factor and potential early manifestation, with the resulting functional decline-pain fear cycle emphasizing the clinical importance of functional assessment and rehabilitation interventions [43]. The elevated chronic pain risk among women reflects estrogen-mediated pain pathway modulation and neuroendocrine gender differences, consistent with higher female prevalence in conditions such as fibromyalgia and migraine [44]. Arthritis contributes to pain through local inflammatory mediator release, joint structural damage, and central sensitization mechanisms [45]. Fall history increases pain risk via trauma-induced inflammatory responses, fear-avoidance behaviors, and associated balance dysfunction or muscle weakness, highlighting fall prevention as an integral component of comprehensive pain management [46]. Insufficient sleep and chronic pain form a bidirectional negative feedback loop, as sleep deprivation enhances pain sensitivity through HPA axis activation, impaired central descending pain inhibition, and compromised emotional regulation, making sleep management a critical intervention target [47]. Non-retired status may confer pain risk through occupational strain, work-related psychological stress, and interference with sleep and rehabilitation processes, underscoring occupational health management’s importance in chronic pain prevention [48]. Conversely, patients with tooth loss may experience reduced pain through dietary adaptations favoring softer foods, decreased reliance on chewing function, and consequent reduction in orofacial pain manifestations [49].

It should be acknowledged that machine learning algorithms cannot overcome the inherent limitation of cross-sectional data in establishing temporal causality. However, compared with traditional regression models, machine learning offers unique strengths in processing high-dimensional data, automatically identifying latent association patterns, and quantifying the relative importance of different risk factors. These features make it particularly suitable for exploring the complex, multifactorial nature of depression-chronic pain comorbidity in CRD patients, which is the primary methodological contribution of the present study.

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, the CHARLS database lacks certain potential predictive factors, including blood gas analysis, pulmonary function test results (e.g., FEV₁, FVC), and specific medication histories, which may limit comprehensive risk assessment. Second, our analysis was confined to the Chinese elderly population, necessitating validation through larger sample sizes and external cohorts to determine generalizability to other geographic regions and populations. Third, CRD diagnosis relied on self-reported data, potentially leading to underestimation of true disease prevalence and introducing recall bias. Fourth, the CHARLS database does not include information such as pain duration and severity, which may lead to misclassification bias in the definition of chronic pain. Fifth, the specific mechanisms underlying urban-rural disparities remain unclear due to the lack of detailed data on environmental exposures, social support, and psychological status in this study. Finally, this is a cross-sectional study, which cannot establish causal relationships between depression and chronic pain. Longitudinal studies are needed to validate the temporal sequence and causal direction of this association in the future. These methodological constraints may compromise sample representativeness and limit the broader applicability of our findings.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates a robust, dose-response association between depression severity (CESD-10 scores) and chronic pain occurrence in patients with CRDs. The XGBoost machine learning model achieved better predictive performance compared to traditional statistical approaches. Future large-scale longitudinal studies incorporating objective respiratory function measures and biomarkers are needed to validate the temporal sequence and causal direction of this association. The results provide clinicians with a theoretical and data-driven reference for joint assessment and integrated management of depression-pain comorbidity in CRD patients, supporting routine depression assessment and bio-psycho-social intervention strategies to improve patient outcomes and reduce healthcare burden.
