Radiomics-based unsupervised clustering and deep learning nomogram for response prediction after neoadjuvant chemotherapy in locally advanced laryngeal cancer
Authors: Zemao Li, Huazheng Dong, Zilong Ma, Zhuo Shen, Shuang Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo develop a nomogram model for individualized prediction of neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) response in locally advanced laryngeal cancer (LALC).MethodsA total of 175 patients who underwent CT examinations before NAC from two hospitals were retrospectively enrolled and divided into training (n = 112) and test (n = 63) sets. Significant radiomics and clinical features were selected sequentially, and consensus clustering was used to identify tumor subtypes. A nomogram model for predicting remission status was constructed by fusing clinical signature, radiomics signature, cluster result and deep learning signature. The predictive performance of junior and senior doctors for NAC response was evaluated with and without model assistance.ResultsTwo radiomics subtypes (Subtype A and B) were identified. Compared with Subtype A, Subtype B had higher frequencies of heterogeneous tumor density (all P < 0.001) and marked venous-phase enhancement (all P < 0.05) in both sets. The nomogram-derived remission score showed good predictive performance for NAC response, with AUCs of 0.935 (training set) and 0.912 (test set). Survival analysis revealed that patients with high remission scores had better overall survival (OS) and locoregional control than those with low scores (all P < 0.05) in both sets. Model assistance improved the predictive performance of junior (AUC: 0.799 vs. 0.915) and senior doctors (AUC: 0.849 vs. 0.919, all P < 0.05).ConclusionThe nomogram model based on two-center databases achieved good performance in predicting NAC response and survival in LALC patients, which may contribute to the personalized treatment of LALC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12880-026-02505-x.

Introduction

Locally advanced laryngeal cancer (LALC), corresponding to stage T3–T4, N0–N3, M0 in the TNM staging system, accounts for a substantial proportion of head and neck malignancies [1–3]. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) is standard treatment for patients with LALC, particularly for those in whom organ preservation is a primary objective [4–6]. Tumor response following NAC is a surrogate marker for overall survival (OS) [7]. However, approximately 15%-46% of LALC cases are not sensitive to NAC, resulting in disease progression during the treatment [8, 9]. Early prediction of tumor response can assist clinicians in adjusting therapy to avoid toxic adverse effects and improve survival. Thus, accurately predicting remission status at the early stages of NAC is crucial for optimizing therapeutic strategies.

The histopathology result obtained after surgical resection is the gold standard of efficacy evaluation, which is invasive. Meanwhile, this approach is inherently limited by its reliance on surgical intervention, which delays the evaluation of chemotherapy sensitivity and hampers timely treatment adjustments [10]. Currently, the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1 remains the core standard for assessing the efficacy of NAC for solid tumors via imaging-based evaluation in clinical practice, especially for locally advanced malignant tumors [11].

Recent advances in radiomics, deep learning (DL) and their application to clinical research have provided approaches for the prediction of neoadjuvant therapy response in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [12, 13]. However, most radiomic studies are binary classification studies based on supervised methods [14, 15]. Guo et al. [16] have proposed an unsupervised method that integrates radiomics data to generate radiomic subtypes, which has been successfully applied to predict prognosis in patients with clear cell renal cell carcinoma. A data-driven technique known as consensus clustering can be used to stratify patient populations by the optimal number of clusters (k). However, it remains unclear whether this method is valuable for predicting response to NAC and prognosis in patients with LALC. Therefore, further studies are required to validate its reliability and facilitate its clinical translation.

This study aims to analyze the value of unsupervised clustering methods in evaluating the NAC response of patients with LALC, as well as the imaging signs of different radiomics subtypes. We further explored a nomogram model for estimating the remission status after NAC in LALC and for risk stratification. Moreover, we verified whether the predictive performance of doctors could be improved with the aid of the nomogram model.

Materials and methods

The procedures followed in this study conformed to the guidelines of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Board of the Tianjin First Central Hospital (2026-42) and Tianjin Medical University Cancer Hospital (bc20260472) approved this retrospective multicohort study but waived the requirement for written informed consent due to the retrospective nature of this study.

Patient selection

Patients with LALC at Tianjin First Central Hospital (training set, n = 112) and Tianjin Medical University Cancer Hospital (test set, n = 63) between 2018 and 2023 were reviewed. The inclusion criteria included the following: (1) pathologically confirmed laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma, (2) patients who received two or three courses of NAC, (3) underwent contrast-enhanced neck CT scans within 2 weeks prior to NAC initiation and again after treatment, and (4) complete baseline clinical data, pre-treatment clinical stage T3–4N0–3M0. Exclusion criteria: (1) history of other malignancies or prior anticancer treatment, (2) incomplete imaging data, (3) inadequate contrast-enhanced CT quality, and (4) lack of follow-up records. Treatment assessment and follow-up

Tumor response after NAC was classified into complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), and progressive disease (PD) based on the RECIST criteria (version 1.1) [17]. In this study, patients with CR or PR were classified into the remission group, while those with PD or SD were categorized into the non-remission group. Details about the treatment of LALC patients and specific classification guidelines are provided in Appendix S1 and Figure S1.

Follow-up assessments were performed at intervals of 1–3 months for the initial 2 years, 6 months between years 3 and 5, and annually thereafter [18]. The follow-up period concluded on June 30, 2025, with a median duration of 22 months. The primary outcome measure was OS, defined as the time from the date of NAC treatment to the date of death due to any cause or last follow-up [19]. The secondary outcome measures were Locoregional control (LRC). LRC duration was defined as the interval from diagnosis to the date of laryngeal cancer recurrence [20]. Local recurrences were confirmed through direct laryngoscopy and biopsy [21].

CT image acquisition, segmentation, and radiomics feature extraction

The detailed parameters of the CT scanner are displayed in Appendix S2. The CT images were first resampled to 1 × 1 × 1 mm3 to address voxel spacing inconsistencies, eliminating variations caused by different scanner resolutions. Images from the venous phase at pretreatment contrast enhanced CT were used.

All CT images obtained from the picture archiving and communication system in DICOM format were exported to ITK-SANP software (V.3.8, www.itksnap.org). The annotation process involved two radiologists, both of whom had boasted over 4 years (junior radiologist) and 16 years (senior radiologist) of experience in head and neck imaging. The detailed tumor segmentation is provided in Appendix S2.

Radiomics features of tumor regions of interest (ROIs) were extracted from the CT images using the PyRadiomic package (version 3.0.1). We extracted 107 initial radiomic features comprising 14 shape-based, 18 first-order statistical, and 75 textural features. Subsequently, image transformations, including wavelet decomposition and Laplacian of Gaussian (LoG) filtering, were applied to the texture and first-order features to derive additional features. As a result, a total of 1834 features were obtained, the detailed content is provided in Appendix S2.

Unsupervised clustering analysis

For the 1834 radiomic features, data were scaled using the “scale” function in R. For the training cohort, consensus clustering was performed using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” R package with the K-means method (Euclidean distance), repeated 1500 times to ensure classification stability (pItem = 0.8 and pFeature = 1). The optimal number of clusters was determined by the relative change in the area under the cumulative distribution function curve, and principal component analysis (PCA) was used to validate the distinct radiomic cluster components.

Development of models

A brief workflow of this research is shown in Fig. 2. The fusion model was established using a stacking strategy that incorporated the average of the predicted probabilities for remission with the LR classifier, built by seven machine-learning classifiers. To predict remission status after NAC, five models were developed: (a) a radiomics-based unsupervised clustering model, (b) a fusion clinical signature model, (c) a fusion radiomics signature model, (d) a DL signature model and (e) a remission score model. At its core, the remission score represents the predicted probability of the nomogram. Additionally, we constructed an open-source version of the remission score model, providing a web application for use by clinicians (https://remissionscoremodel.shinyapps.io/make_web/). Model-aided evaluation and clinical application

Based on CT images and the corresponding clinical data from the study sets, the junior (Z.L, 4 years of experience) and senior (H.Z, 16 years of experience) doctors were invited to assess the treatment response, and classify patients into remission group and non-remission group according to the evaluation method (Appendix S4). Each doctor was blinded to the clinical treatment results of the patient. After a 1-month washout phase, the doctors repeated the evaluation process, and their predictive accuracy was assessed with support from the remission score [18].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 27.0 software, Python (version 3.7.9) and R (version 4.3.3). The cutoff value of all machine-learning algorithms for predicting remission status to NAC was determined as 0.5. OS curves were constructed using the Kaplan–Meier curves with Log-rank test. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. More details of statistical analysis are given in Appendix S5.

Two radiomic subtypes of LALC

Unsupervised consensus clustering of 1834 radiomic features robustly stratified the discovery cohort into two subtypes when K = 2 based on the decline in cumulative distribution function area under the curve (Fig. 3A; Figure S2). PCA revealed significant differences between these clusters (Fig. 3B). Further re-clustering of 107 original features highlighted distinct patterns (Fig. 3C): Subtype A is now described as relatively homogeneous, while Subtype B is described as relatively heterogeneous (Fig. 3D). Meanwhile, there is a certain correlation between subtype A and subtype B for remission and non-remission in the training and test sets, respectively (Cramer’s V = 0.643, P < 0.001; Cramer’s V = 0.435, P < 0.001) (Figure S3 A and B). Additionally, regarding the association between subtypes and blood supply, radiomics subtypes were significantly correlated with enhancement patterns in the training and test sets (Cramer’s V = 0.291, P = 0.002; Cramer’s V = 0.259, P = 0.039) (Figure S3C and D), with Subtype B showing a higher proportion of marked venous-phase enhancement in the training and test sets (84.4%, 82.1%). Radiomics feature selection and development of a Rad-score

All feature selection steps were conducted exclusively within the training cohort. In the Mann–Whitney U test analysis, 177 features showed statistically significant differences between the remission and non-remission groups in the training set, and 11 features were selected by Boruta test (Figure S4). Finally, the LASSO regression with an optimal λ based on the minimum criteria identified 3 features with non-zero coefficients that were used to develop the Rad-score (Figure S5). The Rad-score, calculated using these 3 features weighted by their respective coefficients (Table S2), was used to construct the fusion radiomics signature.

Development and performance of the nomogram model

The construction of all prediction models in this study adhered to the 10 events per variable (EPV) rule [22]. To validate the effectiveness of the fusion algorithm and promote clinical use, a nomogram model was depicted, and the cluster, fusion clinical signature, fusion radiomics signature and DL signature were selected (Fig. 4A). The remission score demonstrated robust diagnostic performance for remission status in study sets, as confirmed by the Mann–Whitney U-test (Fig. 4B), with a cut-off value of 0.5 for the remission score. Improvement of the performance of the nomogram model

In the training set, the nomogram model could correct 75.00% of non-remission and 72.00% of remission cases in patients who were incorrectly diagnosed by the fusion clinical signature model (Fig. 5E). Similarly, for those incorrectly predicted by the fusion radiomics signature model, the nomogram model could correct 58.82% of remission cases, but showed no corrective effect for non-remission cases (Fig. 5F). For those incorrectly predicted by the DL signature model, the nomogram model could correct 40.00% of non-remission and 50.00% of remission cases (Fig. 5G). In the test set, the nomogram model could correct 76.92% of non-remission and 66.67% of remission cases in patients who were incorrectly diagnosed by the fusion clinical signature model (Fig. 5H). Similarly, for those incorrectly predicted by the fusion radiomics signature model, the nomogram model could correct 100.00% of non-remission and 86.67% of remission cases (Fig. 5I). For those incorrectly predicted by the DL signature model, the nomogram model could correct 40.00% of non-remission and 80.00% of remission cases (Fig. 5J).

Survival risk stratification based on remission score

In the training set, 67 non-remission patients (59.82% of the cohort; 93.06% of non-remission) were classified into the low remission score group, while 33 remission patients (29.46%; 82.50% of remission) were assigned to the high remission score group. In the test set, 33 non-remission patients (52.38%; 89.19% of non-remission) clustered in the low remission score group, whereas 23 remission patients (36.51%; 88.46% of remission) were categorized into the high remission score group (Fig. 6B). Kaplan–Meier survival analysis showed that patients with low remission score group had a significantly lower OS that those of high remission score group in the training set (log-rank test: P < 0.0001) (Fig. 6C, E) and test set (log-rank test: P < 0.0001) (Fig. 6D, F). Similarly, the model achieved significant LRC risk stratification in training and test sets (all P < 0.05; Fig. 6G, H).

Comparison between doctors and the remission score for NAC response assessment

In the clinical evaluation, we included all patients in the study sets, the AUC for predicting tumor response was 0.799 (0.737–0.861) for junior doctor and 0.849 (0.793–0.905) for senior doctor, both of which were lower than the AUC for the remission score (all P < 0.05). Additionally, with the aid of the remission score, the AUC improved from 0.799 (0.737–0.861) to 0.915 (0.871–0.959) for the junior doctor and 0.849 (0.793–0.905) to 0.919 (0.876–0.963) for the senior doctor (all P < 0.05). Discussion

There is currently no noninvasive and convenient method to accurately predict remission status after NAC in patients with LALC. This study presents three important new findings that could benefit the treatment and survival of patients with LALC. First, this study established radiomics subtypes based on pretreatment enhanced CT that not only depict tumor vascular characteristics but also serve as indicators of treatment response. Second, a nomogram model combining the radiomics subtypes, the fusion clinical signature, the fusion radiomics signature, and the DL signature was constructed. This remission score demonstrated favorable predictive performance across study sets. Additionally, further analysis demonstrated that the survival of LALC patients could be stratified by the remission score. Third, the nomogram model improved the predictive performance of both junior and senior radiologists, effectively reducing the performance gap between junior and senior radiologists (P = 0.79). These results suggest that the nomogram model could be an applicable and generalized tool for assessing NAC response and survival in patients with LALC.

Medical imaging plays a significant role in the clinical management and treatment response of laryngeal cancer. However, due to the susceptibility of swallowing motions to cause motion artifacts in laryngeal MRI, the application of this technique is somewhat limited. Therefore, CT with higher spatial and temporal resolution remains the preferred imaging modality. Our study employs venous-phase contrast-enhanced CT images to extract radiomic features that predict therapeutic efficacy. Venous-phase CT imaging was selected because it more comprehensively reflects the blood supply and heterogeneity of head and neck tumors [23].

This study utilizing unsupervised clustering analysis has established a radiomic subtype classification system demonstrating significant associations with remission status. Radiomic subtypes could offer a non-invasive imaging approach capable of comprehensively capturing tumor heterogeneity through 3D volumetric analysis, thereby providing a more holistic representation of tumor biology [24]. To enhance the generalizability of the clustering model, this study first determined the optimal cluster number using 1834 radiomic features and then reassigned each patient to a specific cluster based on 107 features extracted from the original images. In the training set, subtype B accounted for 35% of remission cases vs. 94.4% of non-remission cases (P < 0.001); in the test set, it accounted for 50% of remission cases vs. 89.2% of non-remission cases (P < 0.001). Our cluster model achieved AUC values above 0.69 and AUPRC values above 0.7 in both the training and test sets. Our findings indicated that the cluster model based on the primary tumor was a reasonable approach for predicting treatment response in LALC. Meanwhile, in both the training and test sets, Subtype B showed a higher proportion of cases with obvious enhancement in the venous phase compared to the arterial phase (all P < 0.05). Tumors with obvious enhancement in the venous phase may have higher malignancy, with faster tumor cell proliferation and more abnormal vascular structures, leading to increased vascular permeability [25–27]. This results in a longer retention time of the contrast agent, thereby maintaining significant enhancement during the venous phase, and the abnormal blood vessels precisely impede the effective delivery of chemotherapeutic drugs. This could be one of the reasons why Subtype B accounts for a higher proportion in the non-remission group.

Given the important clinical significance of predicting tumor response, many published works have focused on this topic. For example, much clinical information and conventional imaging signs have been proven to be independent predictive factors for remission status [28–30]. In line with the staging criteria for laryngeal and hypopharyngeal cancer, the determination of cartilage invasion and paraglottic/preepiglottic space invasion are important indicators for clinical prognosis [31]. Thus, cartilage invasion and paraglottic/preepiglottic space invasion as predictors were incorporated into the final predictive model. In our study, multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated that cartilage invasion and paraglottic/preepiglottic space invasion were independent predictors of remission. A fusion clinical signature model was therefore developed, achieving predictive performance with AUCs of 0.728 and 0.722 in the training and test sets, respectively. Previous investigations have also utilized cartilage invasion and paraglottic/preepiglottic space invasion based on CT imaging to construct a clinical model for predicting occult lymph-node metastasis in laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma, attaining comparable predictive performance [32]. Using radiomics and DL to predict treatment response is promising. Radiomics is a quantitative analysis method that uses a large number of algorithms to transform ROIs in medical images into high-dimensional features [33–35]. The study employed 1834 radiomics features extracted from segmented images. After dimensionality reduction, 3 radiomics features were selected for modeling, comprising 1 GLSZM feature, and 2 first-order features. GLSZM features reflect the heterogeneity of tumor density by describing the distinctive texture features of the tumor. First-order features reflect the heterogeneity of tumor density by describing voxel intensity distribution and the degree of internal variability. DL, a branch of machine learning, can automatically extract features that are highly suitable for research tasks [36]. DL allows efficient and objective models to be built without large datasets, leveraging advanced computer-vision techniques to exhaustively mine and evaluate the biological characteristics of tumors. Recently, ResNet-101 has become the dominant backbone for transfer-learning–based medical-image classification and its stability has been confirmed in several studies [37–39]. We used the ResNet-101 architecture to develop a DL model that predicts the response of laryngeal cancer after NAC. The model achieved an AUC of 0.924 in the training set and 0.868 in the test set. To enhance clinical interpretability, we applied Grad-CAM to generate heatmaps that reveal how the model identifies key regions. Different colors indicate the degree of attention the convolutional neural network assigns to each region, thereby clarifying the relationship between DL features and remission status, partially mitigating the “black-box” nature of deep learning. Additionally, given the nomogram model outperformed the other models, a further investigation was conducted in this study to confirm whether the nomogram model could contribute to predicting survival, which plays a key role in the clinical endpoint of the patients. In the present study, the risk stratification analysis demonstrated that the low remission score group was associated with poorer OS in the training set (hazard ratio, 4.092 [95% CI: 2.440, 6.862], P < 0.001) and test set (hazard ratio, 5.360 [95% CI: 2.667, 10.773], P < 0.001), and correlated with poorer LRC in the training set (hazard ratio, 1.746 [95% CI: 1.125, 2.708], P = 0.013) and test set (hazard ratio, 3.769 [95% CI: 1.952, 7.276], P < 0.001).

Simultaneously, the performance of the nomogram model was compared with that of doctors in predicting the NAC response of the patients. The results showed that the nomogram model significantly outperformed junior and senior doctors (P < 0.05). Additionally, the doctors’ performance significantly improved when the nomogram model was used, with predictive performance increasing from 0.799 to 0.915 for the junior doctor and 0.849 to 0.919 for the senior doctor (all P < 0.05), corroborating the previously documented benefits of AI-augmented radiologic assessments. The improved performance of doctors with the aid of the nomogram model is also suggestive of its value in clinical practice. Therefore, our findings support a possible clinical role of the nomogram model tool for predicting the NAC response in patients with LALC, which might contribute to clinical decision making.

The limitations of this study should be considered. First, this is a retrospective study with a small sample size, this study enrolled only two independent hospitals. The two cohorts had a significant difference in alcohol consumption and cartilage invasion; thus, other validation cohorts would encourage widespread use of this nomogram. Second, tumor ROIs were manually segmented in the present study, a process that inevitably introduced some subjective variations. In the future, a semiautomatic or automatic segmentation method should be developed. Third, in the clinical evaluation section, only junior and senior clinicians were currently assessed on the study sets, and repeated evaluation of identical cases after washout may still cause recall bias. Therefore, more clinicians and a larger test set should be included in the future. Finally, future studies will integrate additional biological, metabolic and genomic data to comprehensively explore the multidimensional predictive performance of the nomogram model for treatment response and prognosis of locally advanced laryngeal cancer.

In conclusion, this study integrated conventional imaging signs, the fusion radiomics signature, the unsupervised clustering of radiomic features, and DL signature to develop a nomogram model with good performance for predicting remission status to NAC in patients with LALC. This model has the potential to serve as a non-invasive and clinically applicable tool for identifying patients who are more likely to benefit from NAC and achieve a better outcome, which could promote individualized treatment and optimize the management of the patients.
