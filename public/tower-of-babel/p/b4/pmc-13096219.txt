Longitudinal changes in cardiorespiratory fitness and risk of depressive and anxiety disorders in a nationwide cohort of 7 million participants
Authors: Jin-Hyun Park, Seokjin Kong, Yohwan Lim, Yunhwan Oh, Porwarat Chawanid, Batuhan Gökbulut, Ju-Wan Kim, Sun Jae Park, Hye Jun Kim, Sangwook Cheon, Eun Seok Kang, Seohui Jang, Jeongin Lee, Minjeong Kang, Taeho Kwak, Yihyun Kim, Jihun Song, Jaewon Khil, Su Kyoung Lee, Jae-Min Kim, Seogsong Jeong, Hwamin Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
While baseline cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF) is inversely associated with mental health disorders, the association of longitudinal CRF changes on depression and anxiety risk remains unclear in large-scale populations. This study investigated the association between changes in estimated CRF (eCRF) and subsequent risk of depressive and anxiety disorders in Korean adults. This nationwide cohort study analysed 7,007,488 Korean adults aged 19 to 64 years using National Health Insurance Service data (2011–2022). An eCRF prediction equation was developed using an external dataset and applied to estimate individual CRF from consecutive health examinations (2011–2012 and 2013–2014). eCRF percentage change was categorized into seven predefined groups. Participants were followed from January 1, 2015, through January 31, 2022, and those with prior depressive or anxiety disorders were excluded. Incident depressive (ICD-10: F32–F33) and anxiety (F40–F41) disorders were identified from claims data. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models assessed associations between eCRF changes and incident depressive and anxiety disorders. Marginal structural models with inverse probability of treatment weighting addressed time-varying confounding. Among the participants, mean age was 47.7 ± 10.0 years and 3,011,020 (42.9%) were female. During follow-up, 452,464 depressive and 712,377 anxiety disorders occurred. Compared with maintained eCRF, > 5% decrease was associated with increased risk of depressive disorders (HR [hazard ratio] = 1.168, 95% CI [confidence interval] 1.155–1.182) and anxiety disorders (HR = 1.119, 95% CI 1.108–1.129). Conversely, ≥ 5% increase was associated with reduced risk of depressive disorder (HR = 0.928, 95% CI 0.912–0.945) and anxiety disorder (HR = 0.860, 95% CI 0.848–0.873). Declining CRF was associated with increased risk of depressive and anxiety disorders, while improving CRF was associated with lower risk. Associations remained robust in analyses accounting for time-varying confounding. These findings suggest CRF changes as a potential indicator of mental health risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41932-2.

Introduction

Cardiorespiratory fitness (CRF), commonly assessed by maximal oxygen uptake (VO2max), is widely recognized as the gold standard for evaluating aerobic endurance and as a critical indicator of overall physiological health1–3. The clinical relevance of CRF extends beyond physical performance measures, with substantial epidemiological evidence demonstrating that higher CRF levels are associated with reduced risks of cardiovascular disease4 and all-cause mortality5,6, underscoring its relevance as a population health indicator. Beyond its established links with physical health, recent research has also emphasized the association between CRF and mental health conditions. Depressive and anxiety disorders represent a substantial global health challenge, contributing significantly to disability-adjusted life years7 and imposing a considerable societal and economic burden8. Recent meta-analyses reported that lower levels of CRF are associated with increased risk of developing depressive and anxiety disorders9,10.

While baseline CRF levels have been consistently inversely associated with the risk of depressive and anxiety disorders, the impact of longitudinal changes in CRF over time remains insufficiently explored in large-scale epidemiological studies. A large-scale prospective cohort study (n = 131,431) by Wiklund et al.11 examined the association between changes in VO2max from late adolescence to adulthood and subsequent risk of depressive and anxiety disorders. However, this study was restricted to male participants and focused on transitions from late adolescence to adulthood rather than examining transitions within the general adult period. Furthermore, large-scale studies investigating VO2max changes and depressive and anxiety disorders in general adult Asian populations have not been reported. This scarcity likely stems from practical difficulties in directly measuring and longitudinally tracking VO2max in large population datasets12.

While previous research has developed prediction equations for VO2max using non-exercise parameters13,14, these models have relied on variables not readily available in large-scale population datasets. Therefore, this study aimed to develop a prediction model for estimating cardiorespiratory fitness using an external cohort and variables available in the Korean National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) database. The resulting equation predicts VO₂max from routinely collected health examination data, and the predicted values were used as estimated CRF (eCRF) in the NHIS cohort of approximately 7 million adults. We then assessed the association between two-year changes in eCRF and incident depressive and anxiety disorders.

Methods

Study design and population

Figure 1 provides an overview of the study design and participant selection process. This nationwide, population-based cohort study employed a two-phase analytical framework to investigate the association between longitudinal changes in eCRF and the incidence risk of depressive and anxiety disorders. In the first phase, an eCRF prediction model was developed using data from the Korean National Fitness Award (NFA) program; detailed procedures for model development and validation are described in a separate subsection of the Methods. In the second phase, the validated eCRF equation was applied to the NHIS cohort to examine the relationship between two-year changes in eCRF and subsequent development of depressive and anxiety disorders.

The NFA represents a nationwide governmental program designed to enhance population-level physical fitness through systematic fitness evaluations. Participation was restricted to individuals who answered “No” to all items on the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q)15, which screens for symptoms or conditions such as chest pain, dizziness, heart disease, or musculoskeletal limitations that would require medical clearance before exercise testing. From the initial dataset of 87,843 participants with submaximal treadmill-measured VO2max, we applied exclusion criteria as detailed in Fig. 1a. Specifically, exclusions were applied sequentially to remove: (1) implausible VO₂max values (< 10 or > 70 mL/kg/min), (2) individuals for whom the NFA dataset did not provide valid VO₂max measurements because values were only available for participants aged 19–64 years, (3) records with missing predictor variables required for model development, (4) invalid or non-interpretable values (e.g., negative numbers, non-numeric entries), and (5) duplicate observations. Duplicate records occurred when individuals participated in the NFA program multiple times across different years; in such cases, only the most recent examination was retained. Following these exclusions, a final analytic sample of 48,799 participants was retained for the development and internal validation of the eCRF prediction model.

The NHIS provides mandatory universal health coverage for approximately 97% of South Korea’s population16,17. The NHIS administrative database contains comprehensive sociodemographic information, complete healthcare utilization records, and International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnostic codes. Additionally, the NHIS conducts mandatory biennial health screenings for all insured individuals18–21. This study analyzed data from over 10 million adults aged 19 years or older who underwent both the 2011–2012 and 2013–2014 national health screening examinations. Among those, we identified 7,761,623 individuals aged 19–64 years with complete data required to eCRF. Participants were excluded if they had physiologically implausible eCRF values (< 10 or > 70 mL/kg/min), died before follow-up (January 1, 2015), had prior diagnoses of depressive or anxiety disorders, or had missing values for key covariates. The final analytic cohort comprised 7,007,488 individuals (Fig. 1b).

Development of the eCRF prediction model

The eCRF prediction model was developed using 48,799 participants from the NFA program who had submaximal treadmill–measured VO₂max. Predictors were selected to match variables available in the NHIS health examinations and included age (years), sex, BMI (kg/m²), waist circumference (cm), systolic, diastolic, and mean blood pressure (mmHg). Continuous predictors were standardized using a robust scaler, and sex was included without transformation. A multiple linear regression model was trained using 5-fold cross-validation after hyperparameter tuning via random search. After model selection, the final model was refit using all five development batches and evaluated in a separate internal test subset that was not used during cross-validation. Coefficients were then back-transformed from standardized units to their original measurement scales to produce an interpretable eCRF prediction equation suitable for application to the NHIS cohort.

Exposure definition

Changes in eCRF were calculated using standardized values from two consecutive national health examinations (2011–2012 and 2013–2014), approximately two years apart. eCRF was calculated using the VO2max estimation equation developed from the NFA dataset. The changes in eCRF were defined as the percentage difference: (eCRF at 2013–2014) - (eCRF at 2011–2012) divided by eCRF at 2011–2012, multiplied by 100. Participants were categorized into seven groups: >5% decrease, 3–5% decrease, 1–3% decrease, maintained (-1% to < 1%), 1–3% increase, 3–5% increase, and ≥ 5% increase (Fig. 1c).

Definitions of covariates and comorbidities

Sex was categorized as male or female by self-report. Age was calculated from year of birth in the NHIS eligibility database. Household income, based on insurance premium contributions as a proxy for socioeconomic status, was categorized into quartiles (Q1-Q4), with low income defined as the lowest quartile (Q1). Smoking status was categorized as never, former, or current. Alcohol consumption was classified by self-reported drinking frequency per week as nondrinker (0), light (1–2 times), moderate (3–4 times), and heavy (≥ 5 times). Physical activity was evaluated by weekly frequency of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) and categorized into four groups: 0, 1–2, 3–4, and ≥ 5 times per week. Anthropometric and laboratory variables included body mass index (BMI) categorized as < 18.5, 18.5–22.9, 23.0–24.9, 25.0–29.9, or ≥ 30.0 kg/m2, waist circumference (cm), systolic and diastolic blood pressure (mmHg), total cholesterol (mg/dL), and fasting serum glucose (mg/dL). Height and weight were measured by trained personnel during the standardized NHIS health examination, and BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. Waist circumference was measured at the midpoint between the lower rib margin and the iliac crest. Blood pressure was measured using an automated sphygmomanometer after a seated rest period according to NHIS screening protocols.

Comorbid conditions were defined using clinical measurements, ICD-10 diagnostic codes, and prescription records22. Hypertension was identified by systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, or ICD-10 codes I10–I13 or I15 with antihypertensive prescription. Diabetes mellitus was defined as fasting plasma glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL or ICD-10 codes E11–E14 with antidiabetic medication. Dyslipidemia was defined as total cholesterol ≥ 240 mg/dL or ICD-10 code E78 with lipid-lowering therapy. Overall comorbidity burden was quantified using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) from ICD-10 codes.

Outcome definition

Participants were followed from January 1, 2015, until the first occurrence of depressive disorder, anxiety disorder, death, or end of follow-up on January 31, 2022. The primary outcomes were incident diagnoses of depressive disorder (ICD-10 codes F32–F33) and anxiety disorder (ICD-10 codes F40–F41), ascertained based on the first occurrence of each code in the claims database23. In the NHIS claims system, all psychiatric diagnoses were required to be certified by board-certified psychiatrists using standardized diagnostic protocols21,24.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized as means and standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables and counts with percentages for categorical variables. For eCRF prediction model development, multiple linear regression was applied using Python 3.11.5 and scikit-learn 1.4.0. Continuous variables were scaled using robust standardization, and categorical variables encoded as male = 0 and female = 1. Performance was evaluated using coefficient of determination (R²), mean absolute error (MAE), and root mean squared error (RMSE). Final coefficients were converted back to the original scale for NHIS dataset application.

Using the developed eCRF prediction equation, we calculated individual eCRF values for the NHIS cohort and examined longitudinal changes over a two-year period. Associations between eCRF changes and risk of depressive or anxiety disorder were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models, with the maintained eCRF group (− 1% to < 1%) as reference. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using scaled Schoenfeld residuals. Two models were constructed: the first adjusted only for the eCRF at 2011–2012; the fully adjusted model additionally included household income, lifestyle behaviors (smoking, alcohol consumption, MVPA), weight status, and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, CCI score). Variables used to construct the eCRF prediction equation (BMI, waist circumference, blood pressure, and sex) were excluded from the adjustment models because they are intrinsic components of the exposure and adjusting for them would remove part of the exposure variation. Results are reported as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), with absolute risk differences calculated using observed events and person-years. To address potential time-varying confounding, we additionally performed marginal structural model analyses using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW)25,26. Stabilized weights were estimated via multinomial logistic regression with baseline eCRF and covariates, truncated at 1st/99th percentiles. Weighted Cox models with robust standard errors estimated marginal HRs and absolute risk differences using weighted events and person-years.

Dose-response relationships were examined using restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses27. Splines were fitted with four knots at the 3rd, 33rd, 67th, and 95th percentiles of the eCRF change distribution. Reference points were established at median eCRF change values, with sex-specific medians used in stratified analyses to account for differences in the distribution of eCRF changes. Analyses were conducted for the overall cohort and stratified by sex. Stratified analyses assessed effect modification by sex, age group (< 40, ≥ 40 years), CCI score (< 1, ≥ 1), smoking status (never, past, and current), MVPA (< 2, ≥ 2 times/week), household income, and alcohol consumption. Potential interactions were tested using the Wald test on product terms in multivariable adjusted models. These factors were selected because they are established determinants of both cardiorespiratory fitness and susceptibility to depressive and anxiety disorders. Sensitivity analyses excluded participants developing depressive or anxiety disorders within the first year to minimize reverse causation bias28. Additional analyses evaluated weight change associations within eCRF change categories. Participants were grouped into three eCRF categories: decreased (<-1%), maintained (-1% to < 1%), and increased (≥ 1%), then stratified by weight change (> 3% loss, maintenance [± 3%], > 3% gain) with weight maintenance as reference.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

eCRF prediction equation and study baseline characteristics

The eCRF prediction equation developed through Fig. 1a was: eCRF = 73.493 - (age × 0.159) - (body mass index [BMI] × 0.413) - (waist circumference × 0.133) + (systolic blood pressure × 21.974) + (diastolic blood pressure × 43.972) - (mean blood pressure × 65.978) - (sex [0: male, 1: female] × 8.494). The performance of the developed equation in 5-fold cross-validation (CV) and the test dataset is presented in Table S1.

Participant characteristics across eCRF change groups are summarized in Table 1. Among 7,007,488 participants, mean (SD) age was 47.7 (10.0) years and 42.9% were female. Participants with greater eCRF increases were older and had higher comorbidity burdens. Those with ≥ 5% eCRF increase had the highest mean (SD) age 49.3 (10.3) years and greatest prevalence of hypertension (41.0%), diabetes (17.3%), and dyslipidemia (35.4%). This group also had the highest physical activity levels, with 26.7% reporting ≥ 5 times per week of MVPA. Conversely, participants with decreasing eCRF had higher adiposity and lower activity. The > 5% decrease group had the lowest proportion engaging in MVPA ≥ 5 times per week (18.0%). Comparative characteristics of NHIS and NFA datasets used for eCRF estimation are presented in Table S2.

Changes in eCRF and risk of depressive and anxiety disorders

During 47,779,576 person-years of follow-up for depressive disorder and 46,758,801 person-years for anxiety disorder, 452,464 and 712,377 cases were newly diagnosed, respectively. Adjusted hazard ratios (HRs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and absolute risk differences by eCRF change group are presented in Fig. 2 and Table S3. Dose-response relationships were observed: participants with declining eCRF had progressively higher risks, while those with increasing eCRF had lower risks versus maintained eCRF.

For depressive disorder, the > 5% decrease group had the highest risk (HR = 1.168, 95% CI 1.155–1.182), followed by 3–5% decrease (HR = 1.077, 95% CI 1.067–1.087) and 1–3% decrease (HR = 1.028, 95% CI 1.020–1.036). Conversely, eCRF increases were associated with reduced risk: ≥5% increase (HR = 0.928, 95% CI 0.912–0.945), 3–5% increase (HR = 0.971, 95% CI 0.957–0.984), and 1–3% increase (HR = 0.988, 95% CI 0.978–0.997). For anxiety disorder, patterns were similar with more pronounced inverse associations for eCRF improvements. The > 5% decrease group had the highest risk (HR = 1.119, 95% CI 1.108–1.129), while the ≥ 5% increase group showed strongest protection (HR = 0.860, 95% CI 0.848–0.873). All trends were statistically significant (P for trend < 0.001). Results from models adjusted only for eCRF at 2011–2012 are in Table S4. Marginal structural models using IPTW to account for time-varying confounding yielded consistent results (Table S5).

RCS analyses demonstrated smooth, nonlinear dose-response relationships across eCRF changes in overall and sex-stratified analyses (Fig. 3). Analyses using absolute eCRF differences (mL/kg/min) yielded consistent results (Fig. S1).

Subgroup analyses

In stratified analyses, the associations between eCRF changes and the risk of depressive and anxiety disorders varied according to sex, age, comorbidity burden, smoking status, MVPA, household income, and alcohol consumption (Tables S6–S12). Females exhibited stronger inverse associations with eCRF improvement than males. In the ≥ 5% increase group, the risk of depressive disorder was lower in females (HR = 0.899, 95% CI 0.879–0.918) than in males (HR = 0.951, 95% CI 0.919–0.984), and similar patterns were observed for anxiety disorder (females: HR = 0.819, 95% CI 0.805–0.833; males: HR = 0.842, 95% CI 0.819–0.867).

Risk reductions associated with eCRF improvement were also more pronounced among adults aged ≥ 40 years than among younger individuals. Participants with baseline comorbidities (CCI ≥ 1) experienced greater benefit from improving eCRF than those without. Current smokers showed the highest risk with declining eCRF and attenuated benefit from improvements, whereas never smokers demonstrated the most pronounced inverse associations. Regular exercise amplified these associations, with more frequent physical activity (≥ 2 times/week) linked to greater reductions in risk. Patterns by household income and alcohol consumption were generally consistent across subgroups, with modest differences in effect size.

Sensitivity analyses

Two prespecified sensitivity analyses assessed the robustness of findings. First, to mitigate reverse causation, we excluded participants developing depressive or anxiety disorders within the first year (Table 2). For depressive disorder, participants with > 5% eCRF decrease maintained increased risk (HR = 1.164, 95% CI 1.149–1.179), while those with ≥ 5% increase continued showing reduced risk (HR = 0.923, 95% CI 0.905–0.942). Similar trends occurred across all eCRF change groups for both outcomes.

Discussion

This study represents the first population-based longitudinal investigation examining the relationship between CRF changes and subsequent risk of depressive and anxiety disorders. In this nationally representative cohort of 7 million young and middle-aged Korean adults, individuals who experienced CRF decreases over a two-year period had significantly higher risks of developing depressive and anxiety disorders, whereas those who improved CRF had lower risks. Participants with > 5% CRF decrease exhibited 17% and 12% higher risks of depressive and anxiety disorders, respectively, while those with ≥ 5% increase showed 7% and 14% lower risks. These associations were independent of baseline CRF level and multiple confounders, and they remained robust in sensitivity analyses.

Our results extend existing literature on CRF and mental health. Previous studies demonstrated that higher baseline CRF reduces risk of subsequent depressive and anxiety disorders29,30. Meta-analytic evidence demonstrated that individuals with poor and moderate cardiorespiratory fitness levels exhibit elevated risks of mental health disorders by 47% and 23%, respectively, when compared to those with high fitness levels, with depression risk specifically increased by 64% and 31%10. However, previous investigations have predominantly relied on a single time point and have been limited to non-Asian populations. This study addresses these gaps by examining longitudinal CRF changes in a large-scale Asian cohort. Our findings are consistent with the only other longitudinal CRF study: in a Swedish male cohort, CRF deterioration was associated with 24–25% higher risks of depressive and anxiety disorders, and conversely, increasing CRF was associated with lower risk11. Our study extends these findings to both sexes and a wider age range, reinforcing the robustness of the association between CRF trajectories and depressive and anxiety disorders. In prespecified subgroup analyses, the overall pattern of associations between eCRF changes and depressive and anxiety disorders was largely consistent across sex, age, comorbidity burden, behavioral risk factors, and socioeconomic status. Although several interactions reached statistical significance, decreases in eCRF were uniformly associated with higher psychiatric risk and increases in eCRF were associated with lower risk in most subgroups. The slightly stronger gradients observed in men, in individuals with multimorbidity or a history of smoking, and in those with lower levels of habitual physical activity or lower income are clinically plausible, given the greater cardiometabolic vulnerability and psychosocial stress load in these groups. These findings support the concept that maintaining or improving cardiorespiratory fitness is broadly beneficial for mental health, while also highlighting high-risk subpopulations in whom fitness declines may be particularly detrimental.

Before considering potential mechanisms, it is important to interpret the characteristics of individuals who showed the largest increases in eCRF. Notably, participants in the ≥ 5% eCRF increase group were older and had a higher burden of comorbidities, a pattern that may initially appear counterintuitive given that CRF typically declines with age and chronic illness. Several explanations are possible. First, individuals with lower baseline eCRF may exhibit greater proportional gains even with modest increases in physical activity, consistent with regression to the mean. Second, some participants may have undertaken lifestyle changes after receiving new diagnoses or abnormal health screening results, leading to measurable improvements in CRF over the two-year interval. Third, physiological and measurement variability in eCRF estimation may contribute to apparent increases, particularly among those with initially low CRF. Finally, selection effects may operate if relatively healthier individuals within older or comorbid groups are more likely to participate in repeated health examinations. These factors may help explain the observed distribution of CRF changes across baseline characteristics.

There are several plausible mechanisms that may explain how changes in CRF influence the development of depressive and anxiety disorders. Improved CRF typically reflects sustained engagement in aerobic physical activity, which is thought to be associated with neurobiological adaptations relevant to mental health. Exercise training is known to elevate brain-derived neurotrophic factor levels, promote hippocampal neurogenesis, and enhance synaptic plasticity, changes that have been linked to greater resilience against depression31–33. Additionally, enhanced CRF is associated with systemic anti-inflammatory effects and improved stress physiology. Individuals with higher CRF typically exhibit reduced levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines34,35 and demonstrate more regulated hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis responses to psychological stressors36,37. In addition, behavioral and psychosocial pathways may also contribute to the observed associations. Engaging in regular physical activity (and thereby improving fitness) can improve mood38, reduce stress39, and foster self-esteem40, all of which are protective against depressive and anxiety disorders. Conversely, CRF decline might reflect reduced activity, social withdrawal, or emerging health problems, contributing to mental health deterioration. While some bidirectionality is plausible (incipient psychiatric disorders may lead to inactivity and fitness losses), the overall evidence supports a hypothesis wherein maintaining higher CRF provides neurobiological and behavioral benefits protecting against depressive and anxiety disorders.

Clinical implications

The present findings carry important public health and clinical implications. They highlight CRF as a potential vital sign of mental health, as routine surveillance of non-exercise estimated VO2max could help flag individuals at higher risk for depressive and anxiety disorders. In practical terms, a noticeable decrease in an adult’s CRF may help clinicians identify individuals who might benefit from interventions aimed at maintaining or improving fitness. Likewise, maintaining or improving fitness appears to be associated with lower risk of common mental disorders. This underscores the importance of widespread physical activity promotion and prevention of fitness loss across the population. Even modest improvements in activity and fitness can be meaningful, as epidemiological evidence suggests that as little as 1 h of exercise per week is associated with lower mental disease incidence38,41. Health authorities and clinicians could leverage these insights by integrating exercise counseling, fitness programs, and follow-up fitness evaluations into routine care. For instance, employers and community health programs might implement initiatives to encourage regular aerobic activity, thereby helping individuals preserve CRF as they age.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, CRF was estimated using a VO2max prediction equation rather than direct cardiopulmonary exercise testing. Although non-exercise-based equations are validated and practical for large-scale epidemiological use, they may introduce measurement error. In addition, the structure of the eCRF model introduces several methodological limitations. Because the equation was optimized for prediction, not causal interpretation, the inclusion of correlated blood pressure variables and the large back-transformed coefficients should not be viewed as physiological effects. Any imprecision in eCRF estimation is likely nondifferential with respect to subsequent depressive or anxiety disorders and would attenuate, rather than exaggerate, the observed associations. Although the model was developed in the NFA cohort, its application to the NHIS assumes transportability of predictor relationships, and some degree of transport error cannot be excluded. Furthermore, because participation in the NFA cohort required screening negative on all PAR-Q items, the eCRF prediction model was developed in a relatively healthy population, which may introduce healthy volunteer bias and limit its transportability to individuals with lower functional capacity or chronic disease.

Second, psychiatric disorders were identified through diagnostic codes and treatment records in the NHIS42, potentially missing milder cases or those not seeking medical care. However, outcome misclassification would likely dilute rather than create spurious associations. We also lacked detailed clinical characteristics of disorders (e.g., subtypes, or recurrence of depressive and anxiety disorders), which could be explored in future research43. In addition, differential access to mental healthcare may influence the likelihood of receiving a formal psychiatric diagnosis in the NHIS system. Because diagnoses require evaluation by a board-certified psychiatrist, individuals with lower income or limited healthcare-seeking behavior may be less likely to obtain clinical assessment, leading to under-ascertainment of milder or untreated cases in these groups. Such differential misclassification could attenuate the observed associations across income strata, particularly if individuals with lower socioeconomic status experience symptoms but do not seek or receive psychiatric evaluation. Therefore, the true magnitude of associations in lower-income groups may be underestimated.

Third, despite extensive covariate adjustment, residual confounding remains possible. Unmeasured factors such as dietary habits, sleep quality, personality traits, or genetic predispositions might influence both fitness maintenance and mental health vulnerability44,45. Health-conscious or stress-resilient individuals may be more likely to improve fitness and less prone to mental disorders for reasons not fully captured in our models. In addition, lifestyle and psychological factors such as diet quality, chronic stress, and sleep patterns were not available in the NHIS data and therefore could not be incorporated, which may have resulted in residual confounding. Group sizes varied across eCRF change categories, which may influence the precision of estimates in smaller groups; however, the observed dose-response relationship remained consistent across the full range of eCRF changes. Moreover, CRF has a substantial heritable component, and recent large-scale evidence indicates that shared familial factors can partly account for the inverse association between CRF and depression46. Thus, unmeasured genetic and family-level influences may contribute to the associations observed in our study, and residual confounding from these factors cannot be fully excluded.

Fourth, reverse causation remains a potential limitation. Declines in CRF may reflect early or subclinical manifestations of depressive or anxiety disorders, such as reductions in motivation, energy, or physical activity, that occur well before these conditions receive clinical recognition47,48. Although our 1-year exclusion window reduces the likelihood of this bias, it cannot fully account for longer prodromal periods during which emerging symptoms may influence CRF trajectories. A key methodological challenge in longitudinal exposure studies is time-varying confounding, wherein baseline CRF affects both subsequent CRF changes and psychiatric risk, creating complex confounding structures that standard regression cannot adequately address. We mitigated this by implementing marginal structural models with IPTW, which demonstrated consistency with our primary findings and strengthened the robustness of the observed associations. Nonetheless, unmeasured confounders including genetic susceptibility and early subclinical symptoms remain potential sources of bias. Future research incorporating Mendelian randomization or more granular longitudinal assessments may further elucidate causal pathways.

Finally, our Korean population with regular health screening may limit generalizability. CRF levels and incidence of depressive and anxiety disorders might differ in other countries or populations with less healthcare access. However, similar inverse associations between CRF and depression have been reported in North America and Europe9, and the consistency of our findings with those from a Swedish cohort11 suggests that the linkage between maintaining CRF and better mental health is likely a generalizable phenomenon. In addition, Korea’s exceptionally high participation in national health screening programs and the population’s distinct BMI and CRF distributions may limit the extrapolation of our findings to settings with different demographic profiles or healthcare utilization patterns. Differences in cultural, behavioral, or healthcare system factors may also influence both CRF trajectories and mental health diagnosis rates, and thus caution is warranted when generalizing these results to non-Korean populations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this large cohort study provides concrete evidence that dynamic changes in CRF are linked to subsequent risk of depressive and anxiety disorders. A decrease in CRF was associated with a higher incidence of mental disorders, whereas maintaining or improving CRF was associated with a lower incidence. These findings suggest that CRF may serve as an indicator related to psychological well-being. Maintaining or improving CRF levels might be relevant for identifying potential targets in mental health promotion efforts. Future studies should explore causal mechanisms in depth and assess whether interventions specifically targeting fitness, such as structured exercise programs or behavioral counseling, can effectively reduce the incidence of depressive and anxiety disorders. Integrating CRF monitoring and promotion into clinical practice and public health strategies may offer a complementary approach to traditional mental health prevention frameworks.
