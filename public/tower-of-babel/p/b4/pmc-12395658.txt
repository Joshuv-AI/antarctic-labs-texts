APOE ε2 is associated with reduced risk of early post-stroke cognitive impairment but not with long-term functional outcome
Authors: Yangbo Hou, Shuyun Hua, Zhi Guo, Jiwei Cheng, Guoyi Li, Liangqi Wang, Qian Xiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe associations between APOE genotype and early cognitive impairment and long-term functional prognosis after acute ischemic stroke (AIS) are uncertain.ObjectiveTo investigate the associations between APOE genotype and early cognitive impairment and long-term functional prognosis after AIS.MethodsOur study was a single-center, prospective cohort study, that included 109 patients with AIS. At baseline, APOE genotype, cognition and white matter hyperintensities (WMH) were assessed within 2 weeks of stroke onset. At 3 months and 18 months, the modified Rankin scale (mRS) was used to assess the functional outcome after stroke.Resultsε2 carriers had better cognitive performance than ε2 noncarriers did in the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA, p = 0.003) and the Trail Making Test (TMT, p = 0.038). Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that ε2 was an independent protective factor for early post-stroke cognitive impairment (OR 0.213, 95% CI 0.055–0.820), whereas ε4 was not associated with early post-stroke cognitive impairment (OR 2.582, 95% CI 0.314–21.219). ε2 had no significant effect on WMH, whereas ε4 aggravated WMH, especially in the deep white matter (DWM). No significant interaction effect between the Fazekas score and ε2 on early post-stroke cognitive impairment was found. Female sex (OR 7.081, 95% CI 2.531–19.813), admission NIHSS score (OR 1.265, 95% CI 1.062–1.507), and DWM Fazekas score (OR 2.224, 95% CI 1.106–4.469) were independent risk factors for long-term unfavorable prognosis after ischemic stroke.Conclusionε2 was associated with reduced risk of early post-stroke cognitive impairment, but not with the favorable long-term functional prognosis after stroke.

Introduction

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) is the most common type of stroke, with high prevalence, disability and mortality [1]. Post-stroke cognitive impairment is prevalent and cognitive deficits were observed in approximately 50% of the first-ever lacunar stroke patients [2]. In mild ischemic stroke, early cognitive impairment was observed in 63% of acute stroke patients [3]. Notably, cumulating evidences showed that early cognitive impairment was associated with long-term cognitive performance and functional outcome [3–6], highlighting the importance of early screening of cognitive deficits after ischemic stroke (IS).

Apolipoprotein E (APOE), which is involved in cholesterol metabolism, is the main apolipoprotein in cerebrospinal fluid. There are three alleles ε2, ε3 and ε4, forming six possible genotypes: ε2/ε2, ε2/ε3, ε2/ε4, ε3/ε3, ε3/ε4 and ε4/ε4. It is generally accepted that ε4 could increase the risk of Alzheimer’s disease (AD), whereas ε2 could protect against AD [7]. However, the associations between APOE genotype and early post-stroke cognitive impairment are still under debate. A study in Norwegian patients suggested that ε4 was an independent risk factor for early cognitive impairment after stroke [8], while a study in Chinese patients indicated that ε4 was not a biomarker for diagnosing early cognitive impairment after IS [9]. Since IS and AD have been reported to share common pathophysiological features [10], it is valuable to investigate whether ε4 carriers are at higher risk of more severe post-stroke cognitive impairment and unfavorable functional outcome.

White matter hyperintensities (WMHs) and cerebral microbleeds (CMBs), which are associated with APOE gene polymorphisms, have also been linked to functional decline, poor prognosis, bleeding risks and increased mortality in stroke patients [11–17]. Whether WMHs and CMBs mediate the effects of APOE genotype on early cognitive impairment and long-term functional outcomes after AIS remains unclear.

Our study is a single-center, prospective cohort study, aimed at investigating: (1) whether APOE genotype is associated with early cognitive impairment after AIS; (2) whether APOE genotype is associated with long-term functional outcome after AIS; and (3) whether WMHs and CMBs mediate the effects of APOE genotype on early cognitive impairment and long-term functional outcome after stroke.

Methods

Patients

The study enrolled inpatients who were diagnosed with AIS in the Department of Neurology at our hospital consecutively from March 2021 to March 2022. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) in compliance with the criteria of Chinese guidelines for the diagnosis and treatment of AIS; (2) could cooperate with brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examination; (3) brain MRI showed WMHs; (4) willing to accept APOE genotyping; and (5) could cooperate with regular follow-up and secondary prevention of IS; with no contraindications to antiplatelet drugs. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with cardioembolic stroke according to the Trial of Org10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST); (2) patients with severe heart disease, cardiac insufficiency, hepatic insufficiency, renal insufficiency, respiratory failure, malignant tumors, severe peptic ulcers, active bleeding, severe mental illness, and other confirmed neurodegenerative diseases, that could obviously affect the functionality and life expectancy of patients; (3) patients with alcohol or drug abuse; (4) patients diagnosed with dementia before stroke onset; and (5) patients who were participating in other clinical trials. WMHs were graded on T2 and fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) MRI images, typically located in the proximity of the cerebral ventricles or within the semioval center. All enrolled patients signed an informed consent form.

Clinical and radiographic assessments

We collected data on demographic characteristics, medical history, stroke severity and laboratory tests including APOE genotype, serum lipid levels, glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), homocysteine (Hcy), and liver and kidney function. Stroke etiology was classified according to the TOAST subtypes. Two independent investigators (YH and JC) performed the classification, with any discrepancies resolved through discussion or consultation with other authors (QX and GL) until consensus was reached.

Cognitive function was assessed within 2 weeks of stroke onset using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), and Trail Making Test (TMT). Stroke severity was evaluated at admission and discharge using the National Institute of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS), while functional outcomes were measured at 90 days and 18 months post-stroke using the modified Rankin Scale (mRS).

At baseline, all enrolled patients underwent 3.0-T brain MRI and susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI). WMHs were graded using the Fazekas Rating Scale, with separate assessments for periventricular white matter (PVWM) and deep white matter (DWM). Additionally, the number and distribution of CMBs were evaluated by qualified radiologists.

All the clinical and radiographic assessments were performed independently by two trained observers who were completely blinded to APOE genotype. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or consultation with other authors until a consensus was reached.

The Fazekas scale was as follows: 0, absent; 1, punctate; 2, beginning confluent; and 3, confluent [18]. Fazekas 0–1 was defined as no or mild LA; Fazekas 2–3 was defined as moderate to severe LA. The PVWM lesions were graded as follows: 0, no lesions; 1, “caps” or pencil-thin lining; 2, smooth “halo”; and 3, irregular periventricular hyperintensity, extending into the deep white matter. The DWM lesions were graded as follows: 0, no lesions; 1, punctate foci; 2, beginning confluence of foci; and 3, large confluent areas [19]. CMBs were defined as homogeneous round hypointense lesions with diameters ≤ 5 mm on SWI by two trained radiologists. The Microbleed Anatomical Rating Scale (MARS) was used to categorize the location of CMBs.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 25.0. Quantitative data with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD) and compared by the Student’s t test. Quantitative data inconsistent with normal distribution were expressed as the median and interquartile range M(IQR), and compared by nonparametric tests (Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis H test). Count data were compared by Pearson Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Pearson correlation analysis was used for normally distributed data and Spearman correlation analysis was used for nonnormally distributed data or rank variables. Multivariate stepwise logistic regression was conducted for variables with p < 0.1 in the univariate analysis. An analysis of covariance was conducted to examine the interaction effect of APOE genotype and WMHs on the MoCA scores. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the enrolled patients

A total of 110 patients were enrolled in the study: ε2/2: 0.9%, ε2/3:15.5%, ε2/4: 0.9%, ε3/3 70.9%, and ε3/4 11.8%. The ε2/4 carriers were excluded from the statistical analysis because of the possibly opposing effects of ε2 and ε4. The patients were divided into three groups: E2 (ε2/2 and ε2/3), E3 (ε3/3), and E4 (ε3/4). According to the status of ε2 or ε4, patients were also divided into ε2 carriers (ε2+) and ε2 noncarriers (ε2-), ε4 carriers (ε4+) and ε4 noncarriers (ε4-). The TOAST subtypes were as follows: large-artery atherosclerosis (LAA, n = 28, 25.7%), small-artery occlusion (SAO, n = 71, 65.1%), and undetermined etiology (UC, n = 10, 9.2%). The vascular distributions of stroke were as follows: anterior circulation (n = 71, 65.1%), posterior circulation (n = 30, 27.5%) and combined anterior-posterior circulation (n = 8, 7.3%). The cognitive function of different APOE phenotypes

Among the E2, E3 and E4 groups, there was a significant difference in the MoCA scores (F = 4.810, p = 0.011), with a downward trend from E2 to E4. The results of the MoCA and TMT revealed that ε2 carriers had better cognitive performance than ε2 noncarriers did (MoCA: p = 0.003; TMT: p = 0.038). The MMSE results also showed a similar trend (p = 0.066). In the language domain of the MMSE (naming, repetition and command comprehension), ε2 carriers had significantly higher scores than ε2 noncarriers did (Z=-2.407, p = 0.016). Risk factors for unfavorable long-term functional outcome

Spearman correlation analysis revealed that the early MoCA score was negatively correlated with the mRS score at 90 days (rs=-0.283, p=0.009), but not with the mRS score at 18 months (rs=-0.021, p=0.849). The partial correlation analysis revealed a correlation between the MoCA score and the 90-day mRS score, independent of the ε2 allele (ε2 carriers: rs=-0.268,p=0.014; ε2 noncarriers: rs=-0.290, p=0.007) and WMHs severity (mild/moderate WMHs: rs=-0.290, p=0.007; severe WMHs: rs=-0.301, p=0.006).

With unfavorable functional outcome (mRS ≥ 3) at 18 months as the dependent variable, univariate regression analysis revealed that female sex (OR 6.974, 95% CI 2.684-18.126, p=0.000), previous history of ischemic stroke (OR 2.476, 95% CI 1.013-6.052, p=0.047), admission NIHSS score (OR 1.207, 95% CI 1.028-1.416, p=0.022), and DWM score (OR 1.880, 95% CI 1.066-3.315, p=0.029) were risk factors for unfavorable prognosis. Although in the univariate analysis, smoking history seemed to be a protective factor (OR 0.294, 95% CI 0.108-0.800, p=0.017), the effect did not exist after adjustment for sex in the multivariate analysis. The multivariate analysis revealed that female sex (OR 7.081, 95% CI 2.531-19.813, p=0.000), admission NIHSS score (OR 1.265, 95% CI 1.062-1.507, p=0.008), and DWM score (OR 2.224, 95% CI 1.106-4.469, p=0.025) were independent risk factors for long-term unfavorable prognosis after ischemic stroke. Neither the APOE genotype nor early post-stroke cognitive impairment was an independent risk factor for unfavorable long-term functional outcome (Table 6). Discussion

Our results demonstrated that (1) ε2 carriers were superior to ε2 noncarriers in terms of overall MoCA performance and specific cognitive domains of the MoCA, including visuospatial/executive function, naming and abstraction. Although ε4 carriers were not significantly inferior to ε4 noncarriers in overall cognitive function, ε4 carriers performed worse than ε4 noncarriers in the visuospatial/executive and naming domains of the MoCA. (2) ε2 had no significant effect on WMHs severity, whereas ε4 aggravated WMHs severity, especially the DWM. (3) Multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed that ε2 was an independent protective factor for early post-stroke cognitive impairment, whereas ε4 was not significantly associated with early post-stroke cognitive impairment. (4) There was no significant interaction effect between the Fazekas score and ε2 on early post-stroke cognitive impairment. (5) Female sex, NIHSS score at admission, and DWM score were independent risk factors for long-term unfavorable prognosis after ischemic stroke. APOE genotype was not associated with the long-term functional outcome.

Previous longitudinal studies reported inconsistent results regarding the association between APOE genotype and post-stroke cognitive impairment. Sachdev et al. followed 183 patients with stroke/transient ischemic attack (TIA) for 3 years and reported that for patients without a subsequent stroke/TIA, ε4 was a risk factor for greater global cognitive and verbal memory decline [20]. Ballard et al. followed 137 stroke survivors older than 75 years without dementia for 12 months, and reported that ε4 was associated with greater progression of cognitive decline [21]. Werden et al. reported that ε4 was associated with delayed recovery of verbal memory and reduced entorhinal cortex volumes in the first year after ischemic stroke [22]. In the study of Khlif et al., patients with the ε4 allele exhibited greater cortical thinning over 3 years of follow-up after stroke [23]. Another study by Wagle et al. reported that ε4 was an independent risk factor for cognitive impairment at 13 months after stroke [24]. However, according to the study of Bour et al., cognitive decline during 24 months after stroke was not associated with APOE ε4 allele [25]. The Framingham Study supported that ε4 did not increase the risk of cognitive decline in stroke patients [26]. A population-based cohort study, which followed 92,728 patients with stroke/TIA for up to 5 years, held that ε4 homozygosity, not ε4 heterozygosity was associated with both pre-event and post-event dementia [27]. Another community cohort of 1301 Swedish subjects with 3 years of follow-up indicated that ε4 increased the risk of dementia without stroke, but not dementia with stroke [28]. With regard to early post-stroke cognitive impairment, studies are more limited, and the results are controversial [8, 9]. We speculate that the discrepancy could be partly due to differences in ethnicity and sex, different diagnostic criteria for cognitive impairment, and whether the brain imaging variables were taken into account. According to our literature search, there are few prospective studies on APOE gene polymorphisms and early post-stroke cognitive impairment, especially in the Chinese population. Our results showed that ε2 was an independent protective factor for early post-stroke cognitive impairment in the Chinese population. Although ε4 carriers performed worse than ε4 noncarriers did in specific cognitive domains, ε4 was not an independent risk factor for early post-stroke cognitive impairment, after adjusting for multiple confounders.

With respect to the association of APOE genotype with post-stroke functional outcome, previous studies have yielded inconsistent results. A meta-analysis of cohorts revealed that ε4 was not associated with functional prognosis at 3 months after ischemic stroke, whereas ε2 was associated with poor prognosis at 3 months in men, not in women [29]. A prospective study of 496 patients with a 1-year follow-up revealed that APOE genotype was not associated with the 1-year functional outcome after AIS [30]. A study by Gromadzka et al. indicated that in the whole cohort, there was no association between the APOE genotype and the 1-year outcome, but in men, ε4 may be an independent risk factor for mortality within 1 year of stroke [31]. A meta-analysis that included 1453 patients with ischemic stroke supported that APOE genotype was not associated with functional prognosis several months after ischemic stroke [32]. Two meta-analyses suggested that ε4 increased the risk of poor prognosis in hemorrhagic stroke, not in ischemic stroke [33, 34]. However, the study of Rong et al., which retrospectively included 1929 Chinese patients, suggested that ε4 increased the risk of poor prognosis at 3 months after AIS [35]. Broderick et al. reported that ε4 had no effect on the 3-month outcome, but ε2 improved the efficacy of tissue plasminogen activator (t-PA) in the treatment of AIS [36].

In our study, neither ε2 nor ε4 had a significant effect on the 18-month functional outcome after stroke. Instead, female sex, the NIHSS score at admission, and the DWM score were found to be independent risk factors for poor long-term prognosis after AIS. Although ε2 was an independent protective factor for early cognitive impairment after stroke, it did not affect long-term functional prognosis. We also found that early cognitive function was correlated with the 90-day mRS score, but not with the 18-month mRS score, suggesting that long-term prognosis may be affected by more complex factors and that the APOE genotype might not be sufficient to influence long-term prognosis. In contrast to our results, Zietemann et al. reported that early MoCA scores predicted the long-term functional outcome [6]. However, this study did not include an analysis of WMHs, which might be a confounder that would affect functional outcomes. WMHs are closely related to a variety of cerebrovascular risk factors. Significant correlations between white matter alterations and cognitive impairment were even found in asymptomatic metabolic syndrome (MetSd) patients, highlighting the importance of cerebrovascular risk control [37].

In our study, we found that the DWM score, rather than the early MoCA score, was associated with the long-term functional outcome. The different distributions of WMH might reflect the heterogeneity of the mechanisms involved. Previous studies have shown that the main pathologies of DWM hyperintensities (DWMHs) are demyelination, axonal cleavage and gliosis, whereas the main pathologies of PVWM hyperintensities (PWMHs) are endothelial destruction and interstitial edema [38]. Cai et al. reported that PWMHs were mainly attributed to dysfunction of the glymphatic pathway, whereas DWMHs could be affected by both dysfunction of the glymphatic pathway and ischemia-hypoperfusion [38]. Hannawi et al. reported that the PWMH volume was more strongly associated with vascular properties reflecting arterial stiffness and cardiovascular remodeling than DWMH [39]. In a study by Qu et al., a larger DWMH volume was significantly correlated with deficit behavioral changes at 3 months after stroke [40]. Nakamizo et al.. reported that DWMH Fazekas scores were associated with cognitive function [41]. Keller et al. followed 3077 community-dwelling older adults and reported that both higher PWMH and DWMH volumes were associated with long-term dementia risk [42]. The reason why DWM, not PVWM, was associated with the long-term functional outcome in our study remains to be elucidated. These findings need to be confirmed in future prospective studies with larger sample sizes.

The TOAST subtypes of our cohort are as following: LAA (n = 28, 25.7%), SAO (n = 71, 65.1%), and undetermined etiology (n = 10, 9.2%). The TOAST subtype distribution did not reveal significant difference among APOE genotypes. Patients with LAA showed a higher proportion of cognitive dysfunction (MoCA ≤ 25) than those with SAO (92.9% in LAO vs. 78.9% in SAO).However, the difference did not reach statistical significance, potentially due to insufficient sample size. While the prior study report cognitive deficits in approximately 50% of first-ever lacunar stroke patients [2], our cohort revealed an even higher prevalence (78.9% of small artery occlusion [SAO] cases with MoCA ≤ 25), highlighting that lacunar infarction is not a benign disorder, and requires more adequate and rigorous management and follow-up. Given the distinct pathophysiological mechanisms, clinical presentation, and prognostic profile of small vessel disease compared to other ischemic stroke subtypes [43, 44], future large-scale prospective studies specifically focusing on lacunar infarction are warranted to further characterize these observations and optimize patient care strategies.

Our study has the following strengths. First, it was a longitudinal and prospective study. Second, few prospective studies have investigated the associations of APOE gene polymorphisms with early cognitive impairment and long-term functional outcome, especially in the Chinese population. Our results expand the understanding of APOE genotype and stroke outcomes in the Chinese population. Third, we found that the DWM score, rather than the APOE genotype, influenced the long-term prognosis after stroke, suggesting that in AIS patients the DWM score might be more convenient and economical in predicting stroke outcome than the APOE genotype. However, for patients with a known APOE genotype, attention should still be paid to stroke prevention, since the ε2 allele does not prevent the carriers from having a poor long-term prognosis.

Our study also has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small. Owing to the scarcity of ε2 carriers, we did not perform the sex-stratified analysis. Second, the median NIHSS score in our study was 3 points, so the results may be more applicable to minor stroke, and future studies are needed for moderate and severe stroke. Third, at the end of follow-up, some patients were unable to receive cognitive assessments in the hospital because of severe disability and functional dependence. Therefore, we did not evaluate the effect of APOE genotype on the long-term cognitive impairment after stroke.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated that ε2 was an independent protective factor for early post-stroke cognitive impairment, but not for long-term functional outcome. ε4 had no significant effect on either the early cognitive impairment or long-term function after ischemic stroke.

Future studies could focus on the following aspects: (1) the longitudinal trajectories of cognitive dysfunction across different APOE genotypes and stroke subtypes in stroke patients; (2) employment of more advanced imaging methods such as diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) or quantitative high-resolution imaging to follow up the white matter changes longitudinally and investigate the association with cognition across different APOE genotypes; (3) integrated analyses incorporating vascular territory, collateral circulation and comprehensive neuropsychological profiles to better clarify genotype effects on post-stroke prognosis.
