Epigenetic landscape of hormone-independent sexual dimorphism and characterization of canine XIST
Authors: Seong-Ju Oh, Dayeon Kang, Subin Jang, Tae-Seok Kim, Chae-Yeon Hong, Yong-Ho Choe, Chan-Hee Jo, Are-Sun You, Yoon Jung Do, Gyu-Jin Rho, Jaemin Kim, Sung-Lim Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSexual dimorphism in dogs (Canis lupus familiaris) manifests through pronounced differences in morphology, physiology, and disease susceptibility. Despite early neutering, the persistence of sex-specific differences highlights the need to investigate factors beyond sex hormones that contribute to these characteristics. We collected whole blood tissue from spayed female (n = 4) and castrated male (n = 4) beagles and performed whole genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) and RNA seq. To investigate hormone-independent sex dimorphism of DNA methylation in neutered dogs, we investigated differentially methylated genes (DMGs) between sexes and candidate molecular pathways. Furthermore, we analyzed sex-related correlations between gene expression and methylation levels. Sex-related differentially methylated genes, independent of hormone influence, are associated with oncogenic signaling and neuronal pathways. Differences in methylation status between the sexes were significantly associated with alterations in gene expression, indicating that methylation plays a regulatory role in gene transcription. Identification of canine XIST, previously annotated as LOC102156855, suggests a conserved mechanism of X-chromosome inactivation across species and a sex-specific epigenetic imprint on the genome, which is maintained independent of sex hormones. These findings enrich the understanding of sex-specific biology in dogs and highlight the intricate interplay between epigenetic modifications and gene expression in determining sex-specific phenotypes and disease susceptibilities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19398-5.Keywords: Dog, Hormone-independent sexual dimorphism, DNA methylation, Gene expression, Canine XISTSubject terms: Epigenomics, Gene expression

Introduction

Sexual dimorphism in dogs is an important research field with clinical and biological implications, including disease development, physiological variations, and behavioral differences1–4. Investigating the molecular mechanisms responsible for sex-related variations linked to clinical characteristics is important for understanding disease morbidity and physiological differences considering sexual dimorphism. In humans, genetic and epigenetic disparities between males and females play a crucial role in shaping these differences and are associated with various diseases5–7. However, only a limited number of studies have investigated the molecular mechanisms underlying sexual dimorphism by analyzing epigenetic modification in dogs8–10.

Sex hormones, also known as gonadal steroid hormones, have the greatest influence on sexual dimorphism. Elimination of sex hormones affects various physiological processes, including cell proliferation, immunity, behavior, and body composition in both males and females. These hormonal changes may alter the manifestations of diseases known for their sexually dimorphic characteristics, leading to different presentations in the absence of these hormones11–14. In fact, for dogs with no plans for breeding, elimination of the effects of sex hormones is favored to improve their quality of life and overall health. Gonadectomy, commonly referred to as neutering, is a widespread procedure in many countries, that is used to control reproduction in companion dogs and reduce the number of abandoned dogs15. It is also used to alter undesirable behavioral characteristics (such as aggression and roaming)16–18 and reduce the incidence of reproductive disorders19–21. However, despite early neutering and no exposure to sex hormones, sexual dimorphism is still recognized between sexes22. Therefore, the investigation of sexual dimorphism in neutered dogs is essential for exploring factors, beyond sex hormones, that contribute to sexually dimorphic characteristics.

This study aimed to explore hormone-independent sexual dimorphism in epigenetic modifications on the autosomes in neutered dogs. To control for cases attributable to non-genetic factors, healthy 4-year-old dogs (four females and four males) exposed to the same husbandry environment and supervised at the National Institute of Animal Science, Rural Development Administration, were selected based on clinical examinations. The methylome and transcriptome data from whole blood of age-matched neutered female and male beagle dogs were generated using whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) and analyzed. Our research sheds light on the genetic variations driving sexual dimorphism in dogs, enhancing our comprehension of epigenetic mechanisms tied to these differences. This study offers valuable insights into disease susceptibility and management, contributing significantly to the field of veterinary genetics and epigenetics.

Methods

Experimental design and sample collection

This study was conducted according to the guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the National Institute of Animal Science (NIAS 2022 − 586). Eight beagle dogs (four males and four females) were investigated to explore hormone-independent sexual dimorphism in DNA methylation and Gene expression. All dogs were 5 years old, had been neutered prior to 1 year of age, and were maintained under uniform environmental and husbandry conditions at the National Institute of Animal Science (NIAS), Republic of Korea. The dogs were individually housed in controlled environments (1.7 m × 2.1 m, 22–24 °C; 12 h light/dark cycle) and received daily group-based outdoor activity for at least three hours to ensure social interaction and minimize isolation-related stress. They were fed a commercial diet twice daily based on maintenance energy requirements, and water was provided ad libitum. To support animal health, dogs received regular vaccinations (rabies, distemper, adenovirus, parvovirus, parainfluenza, Bordetella bronchiseptica) and prophylactic treatments (monthly heartworm medication and biannual deworming). Routine health monitoring was conducted, including biannual complete blood counts and serum biochemistry panels.

Only clinically healthy and behaviorally normal animals, confirmed through veterinary health checks and behavioral observation, were selected for inclusion. To minimize confounding variables and ensure the reliability of epigenetic analyses, no experimental treatments were performed, and blood sampling was conducted at a single time point. The sample size (n = 4 per group) was determined based on ethical considerations and animal availability, with each dog considered an experimental unit. The study is compliant with the ARRIVE guideline 2.0.

All animal care, sample management, and blood collection procedures were conducted on-site at the National Institute of Animal Science (NIAS), Korea. Blood collection was performed on the same day for all animals. Whole blood was collected from the jugular vein using a 21-gauge needle and 5 mL syringe. Blood samples were allocated into EDTA tubes and SST vacutainers (BD Biosciences, USA) for hematological and biochemical analysis, and into PAXgene Blood RNA Tubes (PreAnalytiX GmbH, Switzerland) for WGBS and transcriptome analysis. Samples for RNA and DNA extraction were stored at − 80 °C until processing.

Hematological and biochemical analysis

Blood samples preserved with EDTA-2 K were immediately analyzed using an automatic hematology analyzer (IDEXX ProCyte Dx Hematology Analyzer; IDEXX Laboratories, Westbrook, ME, USA). The parameters analyzed included red blood cell count, hematocrit, hemoglobin, mean cell volume, mean corpuscular hemoglobin, mean cell hemoglobin concentration, red blood cell distribution width, reticulocyte count, reticulocyte hemoglobin, white blood cell count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, eosinophil count, basophil count, platelet count, mean platelet volume, platelet distribution width, and PCT level.

For biochemical analysis, blood samples collected in Vacutainer serum separator SST tubes were centrifuged at 3,500 rpm for 15 min. Serum samples were analyzed using an automatic analyzer (IDEXX Catalyst Dx Serum Chemistry Analyzer; IDEXX Laboratories, ME, USA). The serum biochemical parameters analyzed were glucose, creatinine (CREA), blood urine nitrogen (BUN), BUN: CREA ratio, phosphorus, calcium, total protein, albumin (ALB), globulin (GLOB), ALB: GLOB ratio, aminotransferase, alkaline phosphate, gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase, total bilirubin, cholesterol, amylase, and lipase. Significant differences in the data between females and males were analyzed using GraphPad Prism v.8.

DNA extraction and WGBS analysis

DNA was extracted from whole blood using an Exgene Blood SV mini kit (GeneAll Biotechnology, Seoul, Republic of Korea) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quality and quantity were analyzed using a Qubit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and a microplate reader (Infinite F200; Tecan, Mannedorf, Switzerland), and by electrophoresis. Libraries were prepared using the xGen Methyl-Seq Library Prep Kit (Integrated DNA Technologies, Coralville, IA, USA). The input DNA concentration was analyzed using Qubit. A total of 100 ng Genomic DNA was fragmented to an estimated size of 350 bp using the S2 Focused Ultrasonicator system (Covaris, Woburn, MA, USA). Bisulfite conversion was performed using an EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). Bisulfite-converted single-stranded DNA fragments were end-repaired and adapter-ligated. Adapter-ligated DNA was amplified with an indexed primer to complete the WGBS library with a full-length adapter, and then purified using AMPureXP beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA). The libraries were qualified with High Sensitivity D1000 ScreenTape & Reagents using TapeStation4200 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). The WGBS library was quantified using a KAPA library quantification kit (Kapa Biosystems, Wilmington, MA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Following cluster amplification of the denatured templates, paired-end sequencing (2 × 151 bp) was performed using an Illumina NovaSeq6000 system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Images were analyzed using NovaSeq6000 control software v.1.7.5 (Illumina), and the output base calling data were demultiplexed using bcl2fastq v.2.20.0.422. The raw data were deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database under accession number E-MTAB-15,165.

General quality control of DNA methylation data was performed using FastQC23and low-quality reads were trimmed using Trim Galore v.0.6.724 and aligned to the reference dog genome ROS_Cfam_1.0 using Bismark v.0.22.325. Aligned reads were processed using the Picard Tools package26 with default parameters. The aligned reads were sorted using Picard SortSam v.2.23.9 and duplicates were removed using Picard MarkDuplicates v.2.26.2. MethylDackel (https://github.com/dpryan79/MethylDackel) was used to determine the numbers of unmethylated and methylated cytosine sites. The methylation ratio was calculated as 0–1.

Analysis of sex-related DMRs

Metilene v.0.2–527 was used as a calling tool to identify DMRs between males (n = 4) and females (n = 4). The significance of sex differences was assessed using p-values from an independent Mann-Whitney U test, converted to FDR, with a threshold of < 0.01. Gene annotation datasets, including the GTF file of ROS_Cfam_1.0 from the Ensembl database28 (https://asia.ensembl.org/index.html), were used to characterize the genomic distribution of DMRs. These datasets included three functional genomic region types: intergenic regions (UP1K, DW1K), intragenic regions (5’UTR, STOP CODON, START CODON, CDS, EXON, 3’UTR), and promoter regions. The Gene body was defined as the region spanning from 1 kb upstream of the transcription start site (TSS) to 1 kb downstream of the transcription end site (TES), thereby encompassing both Genic and proximal flanking elements. The promoter was defined as the region extending from 2 kb upstream to 100 bp downstream of the TSS. DMGs were identified by mapping DMRs to gene regions using the annotation datasets.

DMRs with an average methylation difference > 1 in each group were analyzed to compare methylation differences between autosomes and the X chromosome. The complete dataset was background-adjusted, and regions with an average methylation difference ≥ 0.3 were highlighted to examine sex-specific DMR patterns. To further investigate hormone-independent sex-related methylation differences, we focused on autosomal DMRs with relatively high methylation difference. For subsequent analyses, including candidate Gene selection and pathway enrichment, only autosomal DMGs were considered. The line graph for candidate Genes displays the delta beta value, representing the average methylation difference at all cytosine sites within 15 kb flanking the DMRs between the sexes.

RNA extraction and preprocessing, and analysis of sex-related DEGs

RNA was extracted from whole blood using a PAXgene Blood RNA Kit (762174; QIAGEN, Germantown, MD, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were prepared for 150 bp paired-end sequencing using a TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina). A total of 500 ng of RNA was fragmented, and single-stranded cDNA was synthesized using random hexamers. Double-stranded cDNA was synthesized using this single-stranded cDNA as the template. After sequential end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation, the cDNA libraries were amplified. The quality of these libraries was estimated using a TapeStation 4200 and a High Sensitivity D1000 ScreenTape System (Agilent Technologies). They were quantified using a KAPA library quantification kit (Kapa Biosystems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following cluster amplification of the denatured templates, paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was performed using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina). The raw reads were deposited in the NCBI SRA database under accession number E-MTAB-14,232.

Differential expression analysis was performed using RNA-seq data from males (n = 4) and females (n = 4). The quality of the RNA-seq data was evaluated using FastQC v.0.11.923, followed by the removal of low-quality reads using Trimmomatic v.0.3929. Using the GTF and FASTA files provided by the Ensembl database28 (https://asia.ensembl.org/index.html), the data was mapped to the Canis lupus familiaris reference genome ROS_Cfam_1.0 using STAR tool v.2.7.3a30. The number of RNA-seq reads mapped to the genes was calculated from the BAM and GTF files using FeatureCounts v.2.0.131. PCA was performed to detect sample outliers using read count values transformed by variance-stabilizing transformation and processed with the DESeq2 v.1.36.032 package in R v.4.2.233. Read counts were normalized using the DESeq2 package, and DEGs on autosomes were identified between the sexes. The normalization method was divided by sample-specific size factors that were determined as the ratio of the median per gene to the geometric mean per gene, calculated using DESeq34. In this study, genes meeting the significance criteria of p < 0.05 and | log2 fold change | > 1 were considered to be differentially expressed, with males serving as the reference for the control. P-values were assessed using the Wald test. DEGs were annotated using the reference genome ROS_Cfam_1.0.

Functional annotation and enrichment analysis

To elucidate the functions of DEGs (with |log2 fold change| > 1, p < 0.05) and DMGs (with FDR < 0.01), the annotated upregulated DEGs and hyper-DMGs in males and females for GO and KEGG35,36 pathway analyses using ShinyGO v.0.7737 (http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/) and the Enrichr web tool38 (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) were applied. The top 20 KEGG pathways meeting the criterion of p < 0.05 were selected for each DEG and DMG set using Enrichr. Terms with FDR < 0.05 identified using ShinyGO were considered significantly overrepresented biological functions in each DEGs and DMGs set.

Identification of the orthologous region of human XIST in dogs

The NCBI database was used to compare the biological sequences between human and dog genes. The GRCh38.p14 human reference genome and ROS_Cfam_1.0 dog reference genome were used. For extensive analysis of the annotated genome, information on the nucleotide collection (NC_000023.11; chrX:73,820,651 − 73,852,753) of human XIST (X-inactive specific transcript) was collected using Genome Data Viewer (GDV)39. A fast alignment algorithm, BLASTN40was then applied to identify highly similar regions between the sequences of different species. Statistical significance was established by annotating sequences that satisfied an E-value ≤ 0, based on the GDV database.

The Eukaryotic Promoter Database (EPD)41 and UCSC genome browser42 were used to profile the orthologous promoter region of human XIST in beagle dogs. First, information on the XIST promoter (XIST_1; chrX:73,851,610 − 73,851,669) from the human non-coding promoter database of EPD was verified. After genome reference assembly from GRch38 to CanFam3.1 using UCSC LiftOver, the coordinates were converted to ROS_Cfam_1.0 genome assembly using chain alignment data (GCF_014441545.1). The human–dog XIST orthologous region was visualized through whole-genome cross-species alignment using NCBI Comparative Genome Viewer. The similarity between the human–dog XIST orthologous region identified in dogs and human XIST was examined. GDV of NCBI was used to gather information on the XIST-like gene, and it was added to an existing GTF file. The newly generated GTF file and beagle’s FASTA file were used to quantify the expression of added lncRNAs in terms of TPM using the rsem-calculate-expression of RSEM43. Publicly available data [PRJNA629466, Tissue: Whole Blood (female, n = 12; male, n = 6); PRJNA690861, Testis (male, n = 48)] were downloaded from the NCBI database and quantified in terms of TPM using rsem-calculate-expression of RSEM for comparing the expression of XIST-like gene in beagles and other dog breeds. Differences in the expression patterns of the XIST-like gene between females and males were visualized using a boxplot, and a DEG analysis was conducted to determine statistical significance. Read counts were calculated using FeatureCounts from the newly generated GTF and BAM files using the STAR tool. After normalizing the read counts using DESeq2 in R, the Wald test was conducted to determine whether the XIST-like gene was differentially expressed between sexes.

Results

The overall flow of the study

A flowchart that summarizes the overall flow and the aim of the study is provided in Fig. 1. Whole blood samples were collected from four male and four female beagle dogs, all of which were 5 years old and neutered. Clinical data were collected from each dog sample through hematological and serum biochemical analyses. RNA-seq and WGBS data were generated to quantify gene expression and DNA methylation levels and to compare these levels between sexes. Specifically, to investigate sexual dimorphism in beagle dogs, sex-differential epigenetic pattern analysis and X-chromosome inactivation analysis were conducted separately for autosomes and the X chromosome, respectively.

Sex-related differences in clinical phenotypes

Physical assessments along with hematological and serum biochemical analyses, were performed in clinically healthy and age-matched male and female dogs to investigate sex-related clinical differences. No significant differences in body size or weight were observed between the sexes (Table S1). All hematological and biochemical parameters were within the reference intervals for both sexes (Tables S2 and S3). No significant differences were observed in most parameters between sexes. Plateletcrit (PCT), the concentration of platelets in the bloodstream, was significantly higher in males than in females (p < 0.05) (Table S2). Serum creatinine and albumin levels were significantly higher in females than in males (p < 0.05) (Table S3).

Sex-related DNA methylation patterns

From approximately 40 million cytosine methylation sites analyzed across the Genome in whole blood samples from male and female dogs, we identified 36,128 significant sex-related differentially methylated regions (DMRs) (FDR < 0.01). The sex-related DMRs include distinct sets of specifically hyper-methylated in either males or females. We annotated significant regions according to their Genomic locations to investigate whether these DMRs exhibited distinct Genomic distribution patterns related to functional regulatory roles. All DMRs were annotated into 10 functional genomic region types, among which enrichment was observed in gene body and promoter, supporting their major roles in DNA methylation regulation among various genomic features44,45.

Among these annotated sex-related DMRs, the numbers of hyper-methylated regions within the gene body and promoter on autosomes were similar between males and females (Fig. 2A). In contrast, notable sex-specific differences in the number of hyper-methylated regions emerged on the X chromosome, particularly within the gene body and promoter (Fig. 2B). Specifically, hyper-methylated DMRs (hyper-DMRs) annotated to the gene body on the X chromosome were primarily observed in males, whereas those in the promoter were primarily found in females. This sex-specific enrichment on the X chromosome was consistently observed across other genomic annotations as well (Fig. S1). To further explore these sex-specific methylation patterns, we quantified the magnitude of methylation differences within these regions. In autosomes, the level of methylation differences in hyper-DMRs were similar between sexes. In contrast, 0.9% of male hyper-DMRs on the X chromosome showed average methylation differences larger than 0.3, whereas 20.9% of female hyper-DMRs exceeded an average methylation difference of 0.3 (Fig. 2C, D). These results provide epigenetic evidence of X-chromosome inactivation (XCI), which occurs exclusively in females to equalize the expression of X-encoded genes between the sexes in mammalian species7,46–49.

Distinguishing whether the observed methylation differences on the X chromosome result from inherent sex differences or X-chromosome inactivation (XCI) remains challenging. To reduce this ambiguity, we primarily analyzed differentially methylated genes (DMGs) on autosomes. DMGs were defined as hyper-DMRs on autosomes overlapping with Gene body and promoter regions, identifying 2,764 hyper-DMGs in females and 2,743 in males (FDR < 0.01). Among these, we selected the top 10 hyper-methylated DMGs (hyper-DMGs) for each sex (Table S4). In males, the hyper-DMG with the highest methylation difference was MX1 (MX dynamin-like GTPase 1; methylation difference = 0.463; FDR = 7.83E-12) located on chromosome 31 (Fig. 2E). In females, ING3 (inhibitor of growth family member 3; methylation difference = 0.461; FDR = 1.62E-11) on chromosome 14 exhibited the highest methylation difference (Fig. 2F). Methylation patterns surrounding these DMGs were assessed across all cytosine sites within ± 10-kb regions. While methylation levels at surrounding positions were similar between sexes, pronounced differences were clearly evident at the central hyper-DMRs.

Functional enrichment analysis of sex-related DMGs

To further investigate the biological functional differences associated with these DMGs, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). Twenty enriched pathways (p < 0.05) were selected from the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis. Female hypermethylated DMGs were predominantly involved in pathways regulating cellular structure and signal transduction, such as actin cytoskeleton regulation and PI3K-Akt and Rap1 signaling, which may reflect epigenetic modulation of cell morphology and communication (Fig. 3A). In contrast, male hypermethylated DMGs were enriched in oncogenic and inflammatory pathways, including MAPK and calcium signaling, as well as cancer-associated pathways such as gastric and breast cancer, suggesting a potential male-biased epigenetic influence on tumorigenic and immune-related processes (Fig. 3C).

(C, D) Functional enrichment analysis for the KEGG pathway (C) and GO terms (D) of male hyper-DMGs. The 20 top significant KEGG pathways (p < 0.05) in both sexes are categorized into six and colored. The top 20 significant GO Biological Processes (FDR < 0.05) were selected and sorted by average rank (FDR and fold change).

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that most of the 20 top enriched biological processes (FDR < 0.05) in female hyper-DMGs were associated with nervous system-related morphogenesis and developmental processes (specifically neurons and axons) (Fig. 3B). Most of the 20 top biological processes (FDR < 0.05) enriched in male hyper-DMGs were associated with nervous system-related morphogenesis and developmental processes. They are also related to synaptic transmission (such as biosynthetic processing and modulation of chemical synaptic transmission) (Fig. 3D).

Although directly immune-related pathways and terms were not present among the top 20 results, analysis of all significantly enriched KEGG pathways (adjusted p < 0.05) and GO terms (FDR < 1 × 10−5) identified sex-specific enrichment in immune-related categories (Table S5-S8). Female hyper-DMGs were associated with T and B cell signaling and leukocyte-related processes, while male hyper-DMGs were enriched in Fc receptor pathways and immune system development.

Sex-related gene expression patterns

Differences in gene expression between the sexes were analyzed using transcriptome data generated by RNA-Seq to determine whether sex-specific differences in methylation were significantly associated with changes in gene expression. Principal component analysis (PCA) of gene expression showed clear segregation into two distinct clusters based on sex, with PC1 and PC2 accounting for 56% and 13% of the total variance, respectively (Fig. 4A). We identified 207 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between sexes (p < 0.05; | log2 fold change | > 1), with 141 DEGs upregulated in females, which was more than twice the number of 66 DEGs upregulated in males (Fig. 4B, C). Additionally, 137 of the 207 DEGs were annotated. We selected the top 10 upregulated DEGs in females and the top 10 upregulated DEGs in males as key candidate genes. These DEGs showed distinct expression patterns that were clearly distinguishable between sexes (Table S9, Fig. 4D). Among the sex-related DEGs, SMIM2 (Small Integral Membrane Protein 2; log2 fold change = 1.05; p = 5.18E-06) was the most significantly upregulated DEG in females and was located on the chromosome 22 (Fig. 4E). The most significantly upregulated DEG in males was SNRPD3 (small nuclear ribonucleoprotein D3 polypeptide; log2 fold change = −2.73, p = 6.26E-25) located on chromosome 26 (Fig. 4F).

Functional enrichment analysis in sex-related DEGs

GSEA was conducted to further investigate the function of the DEGs in sexual dimorphism. We identified 5 significant KEGG pathways (p < 0.05) associated with female upregulated DEGs. Among these, 3 metabolism-related pathways were identified, with glycine, serine, and threonine being the most significantly enriched. Additionally, pathways related to the regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes and malaria were significantly enriched (Fig. S2A). For upregulated DEGs in males, 4 KEGG pathways (p < 0.05) were identified, with the Complement and coagulation cascades being the most significant. These pathways included signaling pathways regulating pluripotency of stem cells, fatty acid elongation, and the TGF-beta signaling pathway (Fig. S2B).

GO analysis revealed that the upregulated DEGs in females were associated with 13 significant GO terms (FDR < 0.05). Most of these genes were enriched in cell adhesion-related processes, while other processes, including thermoregulation and actin filament organization, were also represented (Fig. S3). No significant GO terms were identified for upregulated DEGs in males.

Correlation analysis between methylation and expression of sex-related genes

A correlation analysis was performed between methylation and expression of sex-related DMGs on autosomes to identify how changes in methylation due to sexual dimorphism affect gene expression patterns. The analysis showed no meaningful global correlation between the methylation levels of sex-related DMGs and gene expression (p = 0.0084; R = −0.048) (Fig. S4). This suggests that DNA methylation alone is not a primary determinant of gene expression at a global level, as gene expression patterns are influenced by multiple regulatory mechanisms50,51.

Therefore, we examined overlapping Genes from the DMG and DEG analyses to assess whether differential methylation is more strongly linked to changes in Gene expression. We identified 79 overlapping genes that met the threshold criteria in both DMG (FDR < 0.01) and DEG (p < 0.05, |log2 fold change| > 1) analyses. Among them, 9 and 31 Genes were hyper-upregulated and hyper-downregulated in males, respectively, while 29 and 10 were hyper-upregulated and hyper-downregulated in females (Fig. 5A, and Table S10). ABCC8 (ATP-binding cassette subfamily C member 8; R = 0.74, p = 0.035), NALCN (sodium leak channel, non-selective; R = −0.79, p = 0.028), PALLD (palladin, cytoskeletal associated protein; R = 0.79, p = 0.028), and PROB1 (proline rich basic protein 1; R = −0.88, p = 0.0072) exhibited strong correlations within their respective classifications (Fig. 5B, C).

Uncovering the human XIST orthologous region in dogs

We hypothesized that the significant differences in methylation levels on the X chromosome between sexes were likely due to XCI. To test this hypothesis, we analyzed both the methylation and expression patterns of XIST, a key regulator of XCI. Despite the understanding of genetic mechanisms underlying XCI in females, information on the promoter region of XIST in dogs is lacking. XIST is a long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) gene, which plays a crucial role in XCI52,53. Nucleotide sequence alignment (BLASTN) indicated that the genomic region identified as an ortholog of human XIST aligned to the X chromosome of dogs, specifically to the lncRNA LOC102156855, and showed significant sequence similarity. In this comparison, the total number of matches of the five pairwise alignments and nucleotide similarity levels were high at 73.39% (Fig. 6A).

The extent of assembly alignment is shown in green. (B) Mechanism of regulating XIST expression through methylation of the XIST promoter. (C) Bar plot showing the methylation level of the XIST orthologous promoter region and cytosine sites up to 10 kb upstream/downstream between sexes. The intermediate methylation level (0.5) is shown in each graph. (D) Boxplot showing the expression of LOC102156855 for each sex. The x-axis represents sex, and the y-axis represents TPM values. The graph on the left shows sex-specific expression in the beagle data, and the graph on the right displays sex-specific expression in other tissues (whole blood and testis) of dogs in the NCBI database.

The methylation profiles of human–dog XIST orthologous promoter region were investigated to understand the mechanism of sex-dependent regulation of LOC102156855 expression in the dog dataset. A human model explained the mechanism of transcriptional regulation of XIST by methylation of the XIST promoter within the X chromosome during development (Fig. 6B). Cross-species alignment indicated 66 cytosine sites in the human–dog XIST orthologous promoter region. The human–dog XIST orthologous promoter was approximately 15 kb from the first exon of LOC102156855 (Fig. 6A). Within this promoter region, four cytosine sites overlapped between males and females. The average beta value of methylation was ascertained for each sex, including the target cytosine region and upstream/downstream 10 kb of cytosine sites, to analyze trends in the surrounding genetic regions (Fig. 6C). In the human–dog XIST orthologous promoter region, a clear difference in methylation level was noticed with 0.47 in females and 0.98 in males. The XIST orthologous promoter and all cytosine sites within approximately 2.5 kb exhibited significant differences in methylation patterns between sexes; however, later ranges showed similarities. Hemi-methylation in females and complete methylation in males indicates Xa (active X chromosome)-specific methylation patterns54,55.

Next, we investigated whether the mechanism of transcriptional regulation of LOC102156855 is explained by methylation of the human–dog XIST orthologous promoter region. Quantification of LOC102156855 expression in transcripts per million (TPM) revealed that in the dog dataset, all males (n = 4) had TPM values of 0, whereas those for females (n = 4) ranged from 3.24 to 12.39. The Wald test for statistical significance showed that it emerged as the most significantly upregulated DEG (p = 3.26E-55) in females (Fig. 6D). This suggests that sex-specific methylation levels in the human–dog XIST orthologous promoter region influenced the transcriptional regulation of LOC102156855, and this gene was expected to be XIST. To ascertain whether the expression pattern of LOC102156855 was consistent in the dog dataset and across other canine tissues, the expression of LOC102156855 was measured in terms of TPM using publicly available data from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. In whole blood tissue, female samples (n = 12) exhibited TPM values of 18.7–51.3, whereas TPM values were 0 in male samples (n = 6). In testis tissue, TPM values for males (n = 48) ranged between 0 and 4.31, indicating slight expression. RNA-seq data of whole blood tissue according to sex aligned with the expression pattern of LOC102156855. The results of testis tissue analysis showed that XIST was detectable only in the germ cells of the testis in males (Fig. 6D)56. Therefore, these results support the hypothesis that LOC102156855 is an XIST in dogs and that the differences in the methylation of the X chromosome are influenced by XCI.

Discussion

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of sexual dimorphism in dogs, focusing on the interplay between differential DNA methylation and its role in shaping sex-specific biological traits. While one aspect of analyses centered on autosomal DEGs and DMGs to investigate hormone-independent sexual dimorphism, we also explored whether the canine genome harbors an XIST ortholog. As XIST is the key regulator of XCI in mammals, its presence and methylation status in dogs provides essential context for understanding sex-related epigenetic regulation57–60. Identifying candidate regions of canine XIST and confirming sex-specific methylation differences further support the influence of XCI on DNA methylation. This study advances our understanding of epigenetic modifications underlying sex-specific gene regulation in dogs, a topic largely unexplored in the context of neutered individuals.

Sexual dimorphism in dogs is typically influenced by sex hormones and is reflected in physical traits such as size, weight, and coat characteristics61. However, in this study, neutered dogs showed no significant differences in these traits between sexes, suggesting that hormonal influences, rather than genetic factors, primarily drive these physical differences. Despite the absence of external disparities, hematological and serum biochemical analyses revealed sex-related differences in plateletcrit (PCT), serum creatinine, and albumin levels, indicating underlying physiological differences beyond physical appearance.

Our methylation analysis revealed that a greater number of DMRs in females exhibited an average methylation difference exceeding 0.3, whereas the majority of DMRs in males had an average methylation difference of 0.3 or lower. This finding can be explained by the XCI observed in mammals54. Therefore, subsequent analyses focused on autosomal regions, where we identified 36,128 DMGs (FDR < 0.01) associated with sexual dimorphism, each strongly linked to specific genes. Functional enrichment analyses using GO and KEGG pathways revealed that these DMGs were involved in distinct biological processes and signaling cascades potentially influenced by epigenetic sex differences. In females, hypermethylated DMGs were enriched in signal transduction pathways, particularly those related to the cardiovascular system, suggesting a potential link to sex-specific risk of cardiomyopathy. Male hyper-DMGs were enriched in pathways related to cell signaling and cancer, suggesting distinct biological susceptibilities between the sexes. GO analysis further revealed sex-specific differences in processes related to synaptic transmission and nervous system development, potentially contributing to cognitive or neurological differences. While these results do not fully elucidate the mechanisms underlying sexual dimorphism, they highlight key biological areas where sex-based methylation differences may manifest.

In addition to neurological and cardiovascular processes, extended pathway analysis revealed sex-specific differences in immune-related DNA methylation. Female hyper-DMGs were enriched in immune pathways such as T cell receptor signaling, B cell receptor signaling, and leukocyte activation, whereas male hyper-DMGs showed enrichment in Fc receptor signaling and immune system development. To further explore this observation, we examined the top 10 hyper-DMGs in each group and identified several genes with known immune-related roles. In females, these included HERC3, TLX1, and ING3, which are involved in inflammatory signaling, T cell development, and immune transcriptional regulation62–64. In males, MX1 and ZBTB46 were notable, contributing to antiviral defense and dendritic cell differentiation65,66. These gene-level observations reinforce that sex-specific immune regulation may be epigenetically mediated, even in the absence of gonadal hormones, and suggest that DNA methylation differences may contribute to functional divergence in immune responses between sexes through hormone-independent mechanisms67,68.

Epigenetic sex differences have been extensively reported in humans, and a previous study with a design similar to ours provided a publicly available dataset of sex-specific differentially methylated positions (DMP)7. In that study, 396 sex-specific DMPs were identified, and among the Genes annotated to those DMPs, 19 in females and 5 in males overlapped with our findings (Table S11). Notably, SHANK2, a synaptic scaffolding protein, was hypermethylated in females in both dogs and humans. SHANK2 has been implicated in autism spectrum disorder (ASD), a neurodevelopmental condition more prevalent in males69. Interestingly, its expression has been shown to be regulated by sex hormones70suggesting that sex differences in SHANK2 methylation and expression may arise during early development. These early hormonal effects may persist even after hormone levels decline, indicating a potential long-term epigenetic imprinting effect. The identification of conserved sex-specific DMGs between dogs and humans suggests an evolutionary component to epigenetic sex regulation. However, the larger number of species-specific DMGs highlights substantial divergence in sex-related DNA methylation patterns. These differences may reflect evolutionary divergence in epigenetic regulation or, in the case of the human dataset, residual hormonal influences that could not be fully controlled.

DNA methylation is intricately associated with gene expression. Methylation within promoters, exons, and enhancers generally promotes gene silencing, whereas methylation in the gene body is occasionally associated with elevated gene expression71. In this study, 79 genes showed sex-specific DNA methylation accompanied by differential expression. In particular, the expression of ABCC8 (exhibiting a high correlation among hyper-up genes in males), which affects ATP-sensitive potassium channels and insulin secretion, increases through promoter activation by SRY (sex-determining region Y)72,73. SRY on the Y chromosome of numerous mammals including humans plays a central role in the formation of biological sex differences74. Loss of ABCC8 significantly protects male mice from post-traumatic brain atrophy, a process that may be mediated by SRY on the Y chromosome73. Interestingly, in this study, hypermethylation of the gene body of ABCC8 in male dogs was observed alongside its upregulation. This suggests that the increase in ABCC8 expression in males could be influenced by activation of its promoter by SRY, which is essential for male sex determination, and by the potential role of hypermethylation in the gene body.

The sex-related DEGs and DMGs identified in this study were associated with clinical phenotypes reflecting sex differences. Von Willebrand factor (VWF), a hyper-upregulated gene in females, plays a key role in the formation of platelet plugs and the promotion of platelet aggregation and adhesion to damaged vessel walls—processes essential to the initiation of blood coagulation75–77. Under normal physiological conditions, platelet levels are maintained through a balance between production and clearance78. Therefore, VWF overexpression may increase coagulability, leading to greater platelet consumption and a consequent reduction in PCT. Although increased VWF expression and reduced serum albumin are often considered indicators of vascular damage79our findings present a contrasting scenario, suggesting the need for a more nuanced interpretation of sex-specific pathophysiology. In addition, CUBN, another hyper-upregulated gene in females, is involved in protein reabsorption in the kidneys, particularly the uptake of albumin, and is considered an important marker of renal function80–82. In our study, females exhibited significantly higher levels of serum albumin, which may reflect enhanced albumin reabsorption mediated by CUBN upregulation. Moreover, serum creatinine levels were also elevated in females, raising the possibility that CUBN expression may affect glomerular filtration rate (GFR). Previous studies suggest that CUBN variants can influence GFR83–85and the overexpression of CUBN may be linked to reduced GFR, potentially explaining the simultaneous increase in both creatinine and albumin levels in females. However, further investigation is needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying these sex-related differences.

Nevertheless, our study has several limitations. To minimize variability, we used neutered beagle dogs of the same age and raised under identical environmental conditions; however, the sample size was relatively small. Therefore, the generalizability of our findings to other breeds or age groups may be limited. In addition, the analysis was based on whole blood samples, and tissue-specific epigenetic modifications were not assessed.

Despite these limitations, our study provides meaningful insight into hormone-independent sex differences in DNA methylation and gene expression in dogs. It offers a valuable foundation for understanding sex-biased epigenetic regulation in mammals. Further studies involving larger, more diverse populations and multiple tissue types are needed to validate and expand upon these findings.

Although one of our focuses was on autosomal methylation and expression differences, we investigated whether an XIST ortholog exists in dogs, given the central role of XIST in mediating XCI across mammals. XCI is an essential process in mammals, that ensures dosage compensation for X-linked genes in females86. XIST, which is central to XCI, produces a non-coding RNA that coats the inactive X chromosome, leading to its silencing87. This silencing is mediated by the recruitment of chromatin-modifying complexes such as Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2), which establishes a repressive chromatin environment to equalize gene dosage between sexes86. XIST has been extensively characterized in humans and mice, revealing its complex structure, function, and epigenetic regulation88,89. However, understanding of its analogs in other mammals, including domestic animals such as dogs, remains limited. Comparative genomic studies have revealed that the mechanism of XCI is conserved across mammals; however, variations in the initiation, spread, and maintenance of XCI reflect evolutionary divergence among species86,90. Based on these insights and ortholog analysis, LOC102156855 was discovered and hypothesized to be the canine equivalent of XIST.

In this study, the human–dog XIST orthologous promoter region was identified and similarities in methylation and gene expression between human XIST and human–dog XIST orthologous promoters were observed, underscoring evolutionary conservation and similarities in the mechanisms of XCI in humans and dogs. These findings were further supported by the observation that LOC102156855 was expressed in male testis tissue and that its expression was predominantly higher in female dogs than in male dogs. This expression pattern aligns with the tissue expression patterns of XIST in mammals. LOC102156855 expression observed in the testes of male dogs, despite its absence in other male tissues, parallels the finding that in males, XIST can only be detected in the germ cells of the testis. This observation provides compelling evidence to support the hypothesis that LOC102156855 serves as the counterpart of XIST.

Existing reports on LOC102156855 have offered a unique perspective. In a study focusing on bladder cancer in dogs, urine samples collected from normal females and cancer-afflicted males were used to culture bladder organoids for analysis, which revealed significant upregulation of LOC102156855 in bladder organoids cultured from normal female urine, suggesting that this gene is a potential biomarker for cancer in dogs91. While previously interpreted as cancer-associated, our results suggest that this differential expression likely reflects sex differences rather than disease-specific alterations. This highlights the importance of accounting for sex as a critical biological variable in genetic and epigenetic research.

The present analysis indirectly suggests that high methylation levels on the X chromosome in females result from the influence of XCI on XIST. These findings contribute to the understanding of the genetic basis of XCI in dogs and provide compelling evidence for the existence of a gene presumed to be XIST, which has not been previously identified in dogs. Further characterization of the functional role of LOC102156855, investigation of its relevance to canine development and disease, and extension of these investigations to other mammalian species is necessary for elucidating the universality and diversity of the mechanisms underlying XCI.

Conclusion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to clearly delineate differences in DNA methylation according to sex, excluding the influence of sex hormones in dogs. Genes that show distinct sex-specific patterns at both the expression and methylation levels may serve as novel biomarkers for distinguishing sex in dogs, thereby highlighting the complex genetic and epigenetic landscape that contributes to biological distinctions. This study marks the first identification of the XIST promoter region in dogs. These findings enhance our understanding of canine genetics and establish a foundational platform for future studies aimed at exploring sex-related genetic variations. This study can be extended to veterinary and breeding practices to develop a genetic framework for investigating sex-related health issues and traits in dogs.
