Postoperative pain improvement in obese patients is not associated with long-term weight reduction after lumbar fusion surgery
Authors: Claudius Jelgersma, Anne Schreiber, Anton Früh, Robert Mertens, Kiarash Ferdowssian, Nils Hecht, Peter Vajkoczy, Lars Wessels
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionObese patients are often advised to lose weight before spinal surgery to improve postoperative outcomes. However, pain-related functional limitations make preoperative weight reduction unfeasible. In this context, it would be expected that postoperative pain relief facilitates subsequent weight reduction. Thus, the objective of this study was to examine whether a positive clinical response to mono- or bisegmental lumbar fusion for degenerative indications is associated with body-weight change during long-term follow-up.MethodsIn this retrospective study, 53 obese patients (BMI ≥30) who underwent spinal fusion surgery between 2020 and 2022 were analyzed. A positive response was defined as a reduction of ≥2 points on the visual analog scale (VAS) for back pain. Patients were divided into responders (R; n = 33) and non-responders (NR; n = 20).ResultsBaseline characteristics showed no significant difference. Mean follow-up was 3.5 years. Overall, BMI decreased from 34 ± 4 to 33 ± 5 (p = 0.002), corresponding to a mean weight reduction from 100 ± 18 kg to 97 ± 21 kg (p = 0.003). In subgroup analyses, a significant BMI decrease of 7% was observed in the NR group (p = 0.004), whereas no statistically significant BMI change was observed in the R group.ConclusionPostoperative pain relief did not appear to be associated with long-term weight loss in this cohort. These findings suggest that key factors other than pain relief may influence physical activity and weight change in obese patients following spinal fusion.Keywords: Spinal fusion, Weight loss, Obesity, degenerative

Introduction

The prevalence of obesity continues to increase (Collaborators GBDAB, 2025). This trend is accompanied by a growing incidence of degenerative spinal changes and obesity-related back pain, both of which significantly impair daily functioning (de Mello et al., 2019; Parenteau et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2021). Furthermore, excess body weight can exacerbate pre-existing spinal pathologies (Schuller et al., 2011). While technical advances, such as improved intraoperative imaging (including CT- and cone-beam CT–based techniques) as well as minimally invasive percutaneous approaches have been made to facilitate safe instrumentation even in obese patients (Kendlbacher et al., 2022; Jelgersma et al., 2026), obesity itself remains an independent risk factor for perioperative morbidity and adverse surgical outcomes (Jain et al., 2021). This includes prolonged operative time, greater intraoperative blood loss, higher infection rates, and increased reoperation rates (Adindu et al., 2025). Therefore, in routine clinical practice, preoperative weight reduction is generally recommended for overweight patients; however, this is often not achievable due to pain and reduced mobility. Conversely, it raises the question of whether postoperative pain relief and functional recovery may promote subsequent weight loss. In this context, it may also be assumed that caregivers expect successful pain reduction to facilitate postoperative weight control. To date, the postoperative change of weight and its association with clinical outcomes have not been fully investigated. A few studies have reported postoperative weight loss in patients with higher BMI following spinal procedures compared to non-obese individuals (Vorster et al., 2023). Nevertheless, the association between clinical response, specifically pain reduction after surgery, and subsequent weight loss has been described only marginally and within relatively short follow-up periods of approximately one year (Joseph et al., 2017).

The objective of the present study was to analyze postoperative weight changes in obese patients undergoing short-segment spinal fusion surgery and to evaluate the relationship between clinical improvement and weight change during long-term follow-up.

Methods

The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the local ethics committee. This retrospective, monocentric study included patients who underwent surgery between 2020 and 2022. Inclusion criteria were degenerative spinal disease without prior instrumentation, age ≥18 years, a minimum body mass index (BMI) of 30, and complete follow-up data on both pain scores and self-reported body weight with a minimum follow-up duration of one year. Degenerative indications for surgery included neuroforaminal or central spinal canal stenosis, spondylolisthesis, and combined degenerative pathologies requiring mono- or bisegmental fusion. Patients with fractures or spinal infections were excluded. Based on postoperative back pain reduction assessed using the visual analog scale (VAS; 0–10), patients were stratified into responder (R; n = 33) and non-responder (NR; n = 20) groups. Responders were defined as those who achieved a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of ≥2 points on the VAS(Copay et al., 2008).

Statistical analyses and graphical visualizations were performed using IBM® SPSS® Statistics (version 31.0.0.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.6.1). Standard statistical tests were applied to compare baseline patient characteristics between groups, as reported in the corresponding table. Changes in back and leg pain were described descriptively, as back pain reduction served as the group-defining parameter. To compare weight trajectories, paired t-tests were used. A two-sided significance level of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The groups showed no significant differences in baseline characteristics (Table 1). The mean age was 66 ± 13 years in the NR group and 62 ± 11 years in the R group. Mean baseline BMI was 34 ± 3 in the NR group and 35 ± 4 in the R group. There were no differences in comorbidities, particularly those related to metabolic syndrome (arterial hypertension, diabetes, OSAS), or in cofactors potentially associated with back pain (depression, nicotine use, osteoporosis, previous spinal surgery without instrumentation). Postoperative pain intensity

Mean follow-up was 41 ± 13 months in the NR group and 42 ± 12 months in the R group (range 12-61 months). Patients in the NR group had a baseline back pain of VAS 6 ± 4, which remained unchanged at postoperative follow-up (VAS 6 ± 3), while leg pain decreased from VAS 5 ± 4 to 3 ± 4. As defined by group allocation, the R group demonstrated a clinically meaningful reduction in back pain of more than two points, with an average decrease from VAS 8 ± 2 to 2 ± 3. Additionally, leg pain decreased from VAS 5 ± 4 to 2 ± 3.

Weight trajectory

Regarding the overall weight trajectory, independent of group, 59% of patients decreased weight, 30% gained weight, and 11% remained unchanged. This resulted in a BMI reduction from 34 ± 4 to 33 ± 5 (paired t-test p = 0.0019), corresponding to a change in weight from 100 ± 18 kg to 97 ± 21 kg (paired t-test p = 0.0025) (Fig. 1A). No significant differences were found between female and male patients. In within-group analyses, a significant weight reduction of 7% was observed in the NR group (paired t-test: BMI 34 ± 3 to 31 ± 4, p = 0.004), whereas the decrease in the R group was not statistically significant (BMI reduction of 2%; paired t-test, p = 0.134) (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, in between-group analyses, the mean reduction in BMI points showed a statistically significant difference (p = 0.040; independent t-test; mean BMI reduction in NR: −2.3 ± 3.1; R: −0.6 ± 2.5). The prevalence of weight change is shown in detail in Fig. 1B. The prevalence of weight loss was 75% in the NR group and 49% in the R group. Among patients who lost weight, the magnitude of weight loss was similar between groups. Patients in the NR group exhibited a mean weight loss of 8 ± 5 kg (median 7 kg; IQR 4–12 kg) while patients in the R group showed a weight loss of 9 ± 5 kg (median 6 kg; IQR 2–10 kg). An increase in weight was observed in 15% of patients in the NR group, whereas it was higher in the R group (39%).

Discussion

The present study investigates the impact of postoperative pain improvement following mono- and bisegmental fusion surgery on long-term postoperative weight trajectories in obese patients. This study adds to the existing literature, as weight changes after spinal surgery in obese patients are generally described, but have not been correlated with postoperative clinical response.

The two largest available studies addressing postoperative weight changes after spinal surgery report divergent findings (Vorster et al., 2023; Anderson et al., 2010). Anderson et al. found no evidence of postoperative weight loss in overweight patients in their systematic review, whereas Vorster et al. reported a higher likelihood of clinically relevant weight reduction in a meta-analysis of eight studies (Vorster et al., 2023; Anderson et al., 2010). However, based on the analyzed studies, they were unable to demonstrate a difference in absolute body weight.

From a clinical perspective, it would be reasonable to assume that overweight patients who benefit from surgery would experience weight loss as a result of reduced pain-related mobility limitations. Therefore, to analyze postoperative weight loss in relation to pain response, the cohort of obese patients was stratified into responders and non-responders based on the MCID for back pain. Response was defined as a reduction of at least two points on the visual analog scale, in accordance with commonly reported MCID values in the literature (Copay et al., 2008). Although the overall cohort of obese patients demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in body weight over the study period, the clinical relevance of this finding must be interpreted with caution, as the mean reduction amounted to only 1.3 BMI points. To achieve meaningful health benefits across different domains, a weight reduction of at least 5–10% of baseline body weight is generally recommended for overweight individuals (Zahid et al., 2025).

In the present study, responders did not show a significant reduction in body weight, whereas non-responders showed a statistically significant mean weight loss of 6.7% of baseline body weight in within-group analyses, thereby meeting the lower threshold of the recommended range. As clinically relevant weight loss was only observed in the non-responder group, the hypothesis that postoperative pain reduction is associated with subsequent weight loss through improved mobility was not supported.

These findings are consistent with a previous study that did not directly compare responders and non-responders, but stratified patients based on a predefined MCID of 15% improvement in the Oswestry Disability Index. In that study, despite functional improvement, no weight change was observed at one year postoperatively in patients with a BMI ≥30 (Joseph et al., 2017).

Given that recovery of activity levels, including return to work, often reaches a plateau between 6 and 12 months, with reported return-to-work rates of only 69–82% at one year (Lauren et al., 2025; Singh et al., 2024), a minimum follow-up of one year was required for inclusion. This addresses the potential concern that a follow-up within one year may be insufficient to evaluate weight trajectory, which likely depends in part on daily activity levels. Interestingly, even with an extended follow-up, resulting in a mean of 3.5 years in this study, obese patients who experienced postoperative pain reduction did not demonstrate meaningful weight loss.

No clear explanation for the observed weight reduction in the non-responder group was identified, as baseline characteristics revealed no relevant differences between groups with respect to general demographic variables, obesity-associated parameters, or preoperative symptom duration and pain medication. A hypothetical assumption is that non-responder patients were more likely to participate in physiotherapy programs to address residual pain or to try to reduce weight, as a modifiable factor influencing their mobility and pain, whereas responders returned to their regular habits. However, this could not be objectively assessed using the retrospective dataset.

Limitation

The retrospective single-center design limits causal inference. The sample size was small, primarily because patients without follow-up data for both pain scores and body weight were excluded. The statistical analysis showed significant differences in within-group and between-group analysis. Nevertheless, differences should be interpreted with caution, as their magnitude may not reflect clinically relevant differences. Finally, mobility at baseline and postoperatively as well as potentially relevant factors influencing postoperative weight change, such as physical activity, dietary behavior, participation in physiotherapy or rehabilitation, or use of weight-reducing medication and programs, were not prospectively or systematically recorded. Additionally, only one self-reported weight from the last follow-up for each patient was included in the analysis, not accounting for possible fluctuations in each individual over time. Functional outcome measures, including ODI and quality-of-life questionnaires, were documented inconsistently throughout the follow-up period, precluding their reliable inclusion in the analysis. These factors may represent relevant confounders influencing postoperative weight changes and clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

In this cohort of obese patients undergoing short-segment lumbar fusion, patients whose postoperative pain improved did not lose weight, while those without pain improvement did. This suggests that pain relief alone does not reliably predict long-term mobility-related weight loss.
