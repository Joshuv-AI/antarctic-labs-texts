Dual mechanisms of supporting cell regeneration in the neonatal mouse cochlea
Authors: Julia M. Abitbol, Maggie S. Matern, Sara E. Billings, Jacqueline Yao, Sung-Won Choi, Tian Wang, Stefan Heller, Alan G. Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCochlear supporting cells are glia-like cells critical for development, homeostasis, and regeneration. In the neonatal mouse cochlea, inner phalangeal cells (IPhCs), a supporting cell subtype surrounding inner hair cells, are uniquely regenerated by greater epithelial ridge (GER) cells. Here, we used fate-mapping and single-cell transcriptomic analysis to show that GER cells both mitotically and non-mitotically regenerate IPhCs. Mechanisms of regeneration are spatially distinct, with non-mitotic regeneration found throughout the cochlear spiral and mitotic regeneration restricted to the apical and middle turns. By profiling the regenerating cochlea at four time points, we revealed a damage-induced loss of medial-to-lateral specialization of GER cells. Moreover, in silico analysis predicted distinct lineages and associated pathways where mitotic and non-mitotic IPhC progenitors emerge in the lateral GER. Finally, inhibiting proliferation eliminated mitotic IPhC regeneration while sparing non-mitotic regeneration in vitro. Together, our study reveals dual mechanisms and molecular targets governing supporting cell regeneration.

Introduction

Hearing loss is the most common sensory disorder, caused by the irreversible loss and/or dysfunction of cochlear hair cells and adjacent supporting cells.1 Current treatments are limited to hearing aids and cochlear implants, although promising molecular therapies are emerging to reverse genetic hearing loss in preclinical models and early clinical trials.2 In the non-mammalian cochlea, supporting cells can proliferate, with daughter cells regenerating lost hair cells while maintaining the supporting cell population;3,4,5 however, this phenomenon is not observed in the mature mammalian cochlea.6,7 Currently, the mechanisms underlying this lack of proliferation and regeneration in the mature mammalian cochlea are not fully understood.

Cochlear supporting cells play critical roles in development, function, and regeneration of the inner ear. Supporting cells can act as hair cell precursors after damage in the neonatal cochlea,7 and also provide the cochlear epithelium with direct structural and mechanical support for the proper functioning of hair cells.8,9 In the developing cochlea, supporting cells display spontaneous calcium transients that are essential for the maturation of the sensory epithelium, recycle potassium ions that are necessary for generating the positive endocochlear potential required for cochlear function, and contribute to the supply of nutrients and energy to the cochlea.10,11,12,13 In the mature cochlea, supporting cells also serve to remove dead cells after damage and produce a “scar-like” epithelium after hair cell damage, thereby preserving cochlear integrity.14,15 Furthermore, mutations in supporting cells such as GJB2 and GJB6 disrupt the gap junctions and lead to hearing loss in both mice and humans.16,17,18 Finally, although numerous studies focused on inducing supporting cells to regenerate lost hair cells,19,20,21,22 our understanding of supporting cell regeneration itself remains inadequately defined.

The mammalian cochlea contains supporting cell subtypes defined by distinct cytomorphology, spatial locations, and transcriptomes.23 When supporting cells are specifically ablated in the neonatal mouse cochlea, lateral supporting cells fail to regenerate,24,25 whereas medial supporting cells regenerate fully in two distinct damage models.24,26 Lineage tracing has shown that cells in the greater epithelial ridge (GER) can regenerate inner phalangeal cells (IPhCs) via both mitotic and non-mitotic mechanisms when damage is extensive,26 whereas only non-mitotic responses occur after localized damage,24 suggesting dual mechanisms of supporting cell regeneration. Previously, we demonstrated that damage induced upregulation of stress responses,26 but the mechanisms underlying mitotic and non-mitotic regeneration remain unclear.

In the current study, we ablated IPhCs in the neonatal cochlea and found that GER cells regenerated IPhCs in all three cochlear turns. In the apical and middle turns, both mitotic and non-mitotic regeneration occurred, while in the basal turn, regeneration was primarily non-mitotic, driven by direct transdifferentiation from GER progenitors. By profiling early and late regenerative stages, we identified and validated dynamic gene expression changes in GER cells following supporting cell ablation, which included a loss of polarized gene expression along the medial-lateral axis, the emergence of non-mitotic progenitors, and spatially restricted mitotic progenitors. Single-cell RNA transcriptomic data revealed unique differentially expressed genes in the damaged/regenerating cochlea at four different time points, with distinct lineages and dynamic gene expression where lateral GER cells non-mitotically and mitotically regenerate IPhCs. In cochlear explants treated with the DNA polymerase inhibitor aphidicolin, mitotic regeneration was significantly reduced in the apical and middle turns, whereas non-mitotic IPhC regeneration remained unchanged throughout the cochlea. Together, our study provides a comprehensive characterization of both mitotic and non-mitotic progenitor populations capable of regenerating supporting cells in the mammalian cochlea, along with associated temporal and spatial transcriptomic changes.

Results

Mitotic and non-mitotic GER cells contribute to inner phalangeal cell regeneration

To ablate cochlear supporting cells, we used Lgr5DTR/+ knock-in mice in which the human diphtheria toxin receptor (DTR) and EGFP are driven by endogenous Lgr5 expression.27 In the neonatal cochlea, Lgr5 is expressed in IPhCs, inner pillar cells, the third row of Deiters’ cells, and the lateral GER.28 DT administration induces rapid degeneration of Lgr5+ cells in both the neonatal and adult Lgr5DTR/+ mice.26,29 Three days after DT injection to postnatal day 1 (P1) Lgr5DTR/+ mice, most Fabp7+ IPhCs throughout the cochlea have degenerated (∼66%, 74%, and 70% IPhC loss in apical, middle, and basal turns, respectively, Figures 1A and 1B). At P7, IPhCs robustly regenerated in all three cochlear turns (Figures 1A and 1B). We previously showed that outer hair cells and supporting cells in the lateral compartment (Deiters’ and pillar cells) also degenerated by P4 but did not regenerate by P726, suggesting a spatiotemporal pattern of regenerating supporting cells. In the P7 Lgr5DTR/+ cochlea, regenerated IPhCs expressed GLAST and Cx26, both of which are present in native IPhCs in undamaged cochleae at this age, indicating at least partial maturation of the regenerated IPhCs (Figures S1A–S1D). To delineate the time course of damage-induced proliferation and IPhC loss, we immunostained for Ki67 in control and Lgr5DTR/+ (damaged) cochlea between P2 and P7. In the apical and middle turns, significantly fewer IPhCs were observed in the damaged cochlea starting at P4, followed by a gradual regeneration of IPhCs that was complete by P7 (Figures S2A and S2C). In contrast, IPhCs were mostly restored in the base by P5 (Figures S2A and S2C). As expected, Ki67+ cells were rarely detected in control cochleae at each time point (Figures S2A and S2B). In the damaged cochlea, however, many Ki67+ cells were detected, particularly in the lateral GER of the apical and middle turns at P3 and P4 (Figures S2A and S2B), while almost none were found at other time points, nor in the basal turn (Figures S2A and S2B). Interestingly, proliferation was first observed and peaked at P3, just before the maximal loss of IPhCs at P4 (Figures S2A and S2B). These results suggest that proliferation in the GER is spatiotemporally restricted.

To fate-map the proliferative GER cells, we next administered EdU in vivo (once daily from P3-5) to Lgr5DTR/+; GLASTCreERT/+; R26RtdTomato/+ mice (DT and tamoxifen at P1) and examined the P7 cochlea (Figure 1C). Under these conditions, the tdTomato reporter labels approximately 70%–80% of GER cells in each cochlear turn at P7 (Figures S3A and S3B). As expected, no EdU+ cells were observed in the undamaged control cochleae (Figure 1D). In contrast, damaged (Lgr5DTR/+; GLASTCreERT/+; R26RtdTomato/+) cochleae displayed many tdTomato+, EdU+ IPhCs and lateral GER cells, which were most abundant in the apical and middle turns (Figure 1D). We observed that a subset of regenerated IPhCs (Fabp7+, Sox2+) were tdTomato+ EdU+, indicating that regeneration by GER cells occurred through both mitotic (EdU+) and non-mitotic (EdU-negative) mechanisms. In the apical and middle turns at P7, the proportions of EdU+ and EdU-negative IPhCs were similar, whereas the basal turn had a significantly higher proportion of EdU-negative IPhCs, suggesting that basal GER cells regenerate IPhCs non-mitotically (Figures 1E and 1F). These results indicate that GER cells regenerate IPhCs via mitotic and non-mitotic mechanisms in a spatially distinct manner.

To verify damage-induced proliferation and mitotic regeneration, we examined Ki67CreERT2/+; Lgr5DTR/+; R26RtdTomato/+ mice and used Ki67CreERT2/+; R26RtdTomato/+ as undamaged controls (Figure 2A). Using this approach, we observed Ki67-tdTomato labeling in approximately 18%, 15%, and 8% of GER cells in the apical, middle, and basal turns, respectively, at P7 (Figures S3C and S3D). Since Ki67 expression is most robust at P3 and P4, tamoxifen was administered at P3 to trace Ki67+ cells (Figures S2A and S2B). Following DT and tamoxifen injections (at P1 and P3, respectively), we examined the cochleae from P7 Ki67CreERT2/+; Lgr5DTR/+; R26RtdTomato/+ mice. At P7, many IPhCs and lateral GER cells in the apical and middle turns were Ki67-tdTomato+ (Figures 2B and 2C), with tdTomato+ and tdTomato-negative IPhCs comprising similar proportions (Figure 2D). In contrast, IPhCs in the basal turn were predominantly (84 ± 21%) tdTomato-negative (Figure 2D). Single-cell RNA-seq reveals dynamic gene expression in the greater epithelial ridge

To further elucidate the mechanisms of IPhC regeneration, we used single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to examine the temporal transcriptomic changes in IPhC and GER cells after damage. After DT and tamoxifen injections (at P1 and P3, respectively), cochleae from P4, P5, P6, and P7 Ki67CreERT2/+; Lgr5DTR/+; R26RtdTomato/+ (damage) and Ki67CreERT2/+; R26RtdTomato/+ (control) mice were profiled. Using the expression of distinct marker genes, we computationally isolated Epcam+ epithelial cells from both groups (damage and control) at each time point and integrated them into a single dataset (Figures S4 and S5; Tables S2 and S3; Figure 3A). We examined the contribution of P4-P7 damage and control conditions to each cluster and found a small population of cells (cluster 17) that were observed mainly in the damage group at P4 (Figures 3C and 3D). Using gene ontology (GO) and KEGG Pathway analysis, we observed in cluster 17 high expression of genes associated with cell cycle progression, such as Mki67, Cdk1, Ccnd1 and Top2a (Figures 3E and 3F; Figures S6 and S7A–S7C). Based on these results, we presumed cluster 17 to be proliferative GER cells that emerged following Lgr5+ cell ablation (Figure 1D). In addition to cluster 17, we also observed a cluster of cells only present in the damage condition at all 4 time points (cluster 8, Figures 3C and 3D). Differential gene expression analysis of cluster 8 compared to all other clusters revealed increased expression of tdTomato, suggesting that these cells also exhibited a proliferative response to Lgr5+ cell ablation (Figure 3E), as well as the DC/IPC marker Fabp3 (Figure 3E; Table S3). GO and KEGG Pathway analysis of cluster 8 further revealed enrichment of genes associated with nuclear division and cell cycle, as well as neurodegeneration (Figure 3F; Figures S6D, S7D, and S7E). Consistent with the expression of these enriched genes, we have previously shown that DC/IPCs proliferated after ablation of Lgr5+ cells in vivo, and that proliferative cells subsequently degenerated.26 Based on these results, we conclude that cluster 8 represents DC/IPCs responding to Lgr5+ cell ablation.

To better understand the GER cells’ response to Lgr5+ cell ablation, we next examined differentially expressed genes in GER and IPhCs between control and damaged cochleae at each time point, excluding cluster 17 (proliferating cells) (Figure 3G). At P4, we identified 695 upregulated and 491 downregulated genes in the damaged compared to control GER (Figure 3G). Upregulated genes included the canonical medial GER markers Calb1, Crabp1, and Lgals1 (Figures 3B and 3G; Table S3). Consistent with IPhC loss at P4 (Figure 1D), downregulated genes included the IPhC markers Fabp7 and Ednrb, as well as the lateral GER and supporting cell markers Jag1 and Lgr5 (Figure 3G). At P5, we continued to observe downregulation of supporting cell markers in the damaged GER (2,267 total genes), including Hes5, Jag1, Lfng, and Lgr5, along with upregulation of medial GER genes (205 total genes) such as Lgals1 and Cryab (Figure 3G). Interestingly, by P6, IPhC gene expression (Fabp7 and Ednrb) is no longer included among the 189 significantly downregulated genes identified in the damaged cochlea (Figure 3G; Table S3), likely due to IPhC regeneration. Additionally, we observed that some lateral GER genes, such as Calb1 and Crabp1, were no longer among the 182 upregulated genes in the P6 damaged GER (Figure 3G). By the time IPhCs were fully regenerated at P7, 624 genes remained differentially downregulated in the damaged condition, including Sox2 and Lgr5 (Figure 3G). However, we found that both medial and lateral GER markers (Lgals1 and Jag1, respectively) are upregulated in the damaged condition, suggesting a possible loss of the medial-to-lateral specialization of the GER (Figure 3E; Figures S8 and S9; Table S3).

Since in silico analysis of the single-cell transcriptome suggests that damage disrupted specialization of the GER cells, we next validated the differentially expressed genes that mark the medial and lateral GER domains (Figures S8 and S9). In control cochlea, we confirmed the expression of the medial GER markers Galectin1 (Lgals1) and Calb1 at P6 and P7 (Figure 3H). By contrast, in the damaged cochlea, we noted broader expression of Calb1 and Galectin1 that included the central/intermediate GER region (Figure 3H; Figure S8). Similarly, expression of the medial GER marker Crabp1 increased and extended to the central GER in all turns of the damaged cochlea (Figures 3I and 3K). This result validates the scRNA-seq data, which showed Crabp1 to be significantly upregulated in the damaged compared to control P4 cochleae (Figure 3G). By P7, expression of Crabp1 decreased compared to P4, but expression remained broader than the control (Figure 3I), suggesting a sustained loss of specialization in the GER.

Our data also showed that Jag1 was decreased at P4/P5 before being upregulated at P7 in the damaged cochleae (Figure 3E; Figure S9). Immunostaining for Jag1 revealed decreased expression in the lateral GER in all three turns of the P4 damaged cochlea compared to the control (Figure 3J). Conversely, at P7, Jag1 was increased in the lateral GER of the damaged cochlea relative to the control (Figure 3J). Taken together, these data demonstrate validated transcriptomic changes in the GER and IPhCs after supporting cell loss, including the emergence of mitotic progenitors, loss of medial-to-lateral specialization of the GER, and initial loss and subsequent regeneration of IPhCs.

Trajectory analysis reveals two distinct mechanisms of IPhC regeneration

To define the dynamic changes in gene expression that occur during mitotic and non-mitotic IPhC regeneration, we employed the Slingshot algorithm30 to identify cell-cell transitions during IPhC regeneration in the damaged cochleae (P4-P7). For this analysis, we isolated and reclustered lateral GER cells (cluster 5 only), IPhCs (cluster 7), and proliferating cells (cluster 17) from the damaged cochlea (Figure 3A). Lateral GER cluster 6 was excluded from the trajectory analysis as it is mostly composed of cells from the P6 and P7 timepoints, suggesting that this cluster is developmentally regulated (Figures 3C and 3D). Additionally, cluster 5 but not cluster 6 showed enriched expression of Ki67-tdTomato (Figure 3E; Table S3). In this manner, we focus solely on the process of IPhC regeneration. Subclustering analysis resulted in six new clusters, with contributions from each time point (Figure 4A). Proliferating cells (cluster 5) were identified based on high expression of the cell cycle genes Mki67 and Top2a; lateral GER cells (clusters 0, 1, 2, 4) were defined by Jag1 and Cyp26b1 expression; and IPhCs (cluster 3) were identified by increased Fabp7, Ednrb, Erbb4, and Lgr5 expression, among other markers (Figure 4B; Table S3). Cluster 2 is distinct from other lateral GER clusters in that it is mainly composed of cells from P7. Based on this subclustering, a trajectory analysis using Slingshot31 further revealed three lineages, all of which initiated in lateral GER cluster 0: lineage 1, which extends to the regenerated IPhCs; lineage 2, which extends to the proliferating cells; and lineage 3, which extends to P7 lateral GER (cluster 2, Figures 4A–4D). Because lateral GER cluster 2 is mainly composed of cells from P7, we presumed that Lineage 3 represents maturation of the lateral GER after damage (Figure S10). GO term analysis of the genes differentially expressed along lineage 1 (non-mitotic lineage) pseudotime revealed the early expression of genes involved in ribosome assembly and translation (Rplp0 and Rps20), which were subsequently downregulated (Figure 4E; Table S3). As pseudotime progressed, genes associated with cell proliferation and cell death (Ccnd1 and Bax) were also transiently upregulated, possibly reflecting the response of lateral GER cells to Lgr5+ cell loss. This process was followed by a transient upregulation of genes associated with mesenchymal cell proliferation, cell morphogenesis and neurogenesis; although this lineage does not progress to the Mki67+ cells in cluster 2. This stage of the trajectory may therefore represent the decision point between mitotic and non-mitotic regeneration in the apical and middle turns. Following the downregulation of proliferative genes, cells along lineage 1 upregulated genes associated with establishing cell polarity (Prickle2 and Wdpcp), and finally cell adhesion and neurogenesis genes are upregulated as the cells transition to regenerated IPhCs (Ednrb and Lgr5) (Figure 4E; Table S3).

As expected, the GO terms for the differentially regulated genes along lineage 2 (mitotic lineage) pseudotime resemble those in the first half of lineage 1 pseudotime. GO term analysis of these genes indicated early downregulation of transepithelial transport genes (Gjb2 and Cldn9), followed by transient upregulation of genes involved in ribosome assembly (Rplp1, Rps29). As the lineage progressed, genes involved in head development, neurogenesis and cell adhesion were upregulated (Ascl4, Egr1, Nrcam), followed by robust expression of cell cycle genes as cells transitioned to a proliferative state (Bub3, Top2a) (Figure 4F). Although not revealed by this trajectory, it is likely that the proliferating cells (cluster 5) transition back into lateral GER cells (cluster 4) following completion of the cell cycle. These cells then contribute to the overall pool of lateral GER cells (cluster 4) that continue along either lineage 1 to become IPhC or lineage 3 to become mature lateral GER cells (Figure 4D). Overall, this pseudotime trajectory analysis revealed three branching lineages, one in which lateral GER cells directly replace lost IPhCs after Lgr5+ cell ablation (lineage 1), one in which lateral GER cells undergo a period of proliferation before regenerating lost IPhCs (lineage 2), and one in which early lateral GER cells maintain their identity and mature up to P7 (lineage 3, Figure S10).

To identify pathways capable of controlling the non-mitotic and mitotic lineages, we next investigated differential gene expression between all three lineages at their branchpoint in cluster 1 lateral GER cells. For this, the lineages were embedded with fix points along pseudotime called knots, and the branchpoint of all lineages was defined as knot 3 (Figure 4G). We next investigated differential gene expression along pseudotime specifically between knots 3 and 4, where all three lineages diverge, to determine early factors that may influence a cell’s decision to follow each lineage. GO analysis of the genes differentially expressed (p value <0.05) between lineage 1 (non-mitotic) and lineage 2 (mitotic) showed an enrichment of genes associated with cell cycle progression (Figure 4H; Table S3), further revealing the early activation of pro-proliferative genes in a subset of cluster 1 and 4 lateral GER cells. KEGG Pathway analysis of this set of genes further showed enrichment of p53 signaling and ECM-receptor interactions (Figure 4I; Table S3), as well as Wnt signaling (Table S3). Paired with the results of the GO analysis, these data suggest these pathways and processes may be implicated in differentiating mitotic and non-mitotic regeneration of IPhCs in response to damage.

We next examined differential gene expression across cell clusters 0 through 5 in relation to pseudotime, followed by ingenuity pathway analysis (IPA)32 to further assess pathway activation and repression. Focusing specifically on the intermediate (cluster 1 vs. cluster 4) and latter portions of lineage 1 (cluster 4 vs. cluster 3) and lineage 2 (cluster 4 vs. cluster 5), our analysis showed an activation of extracellular matrix organization (Z score = 1.387), epithelial adherens junction signaling (Z score = 1.732) and gap junctions signaling (Z score = 3.153), as well as WNT/β-catenin signaling (Z score = 0.632) and IGF-1 signaling (Z score = 0.447) as lateral GER cells from the damaged condition transitioned from cluster 1 to cluster 4. Furthermore, following lineage 1, we identified subsequent repression of extracellular matrix (Z score = −3.606) and cell junction organization (Z score = −2.333) processes, as well as IGF-1 signaling (Z score = −0.447) and WNT/β-catenin signaling (Z score = −0.333) as cluster 4 cells then transitioned to cluster 3 IPhCs (Table S4). Alternatively following lineage 2, this analysis also identified activation of cell cycle pathways such as synthesis of DNA (Z score = 6.332) and mitotic G1 phase and G1/S transition (Z score = 4.596), and repression of the senescence pathway (Z score = −1.886) as cluster 4 cells transitioned to a proliferative state (cluster 5, Table S4). Paired with the results of the GO analysis, these data identify pathways and processes that may be implicated in differentiating mitotic and non-mitotic regeneration of IPhCs in response to damage.

Inhibition of proliferation reduces supporting cell regeneration

To investigate the IPhC ablation model in vitro, we next established cochlear cultures from P2 Lgr5DTR/+ mice that had received DT one day prior (Figure 5A). To ensure that aphidicolin inhibits proliferation, we quantified the number of EdU+Sox2+ cells in the GER. Discussion

Regeneration in the sensory organs naturally occurs in non-mammalian species such as birds and fish.33,34,35 Supporting cells proliferate and produce daughter cells, which subsequently generate new hair cells while preserving the population of supporting cells. However, the mature mammalian cochlea lacks the capacity to proliferate and regenerate hair cells.6,36 In the neonatal cochlea, in contrast, a modest degree of hair cell regeneration and proliferation is observed, but these damage responses are significantly diminished after the first postnatal week.7,37,38,39

Glia, the supporting cells of the brain, regenerate upon central nervous system damage through proliferation in both mice and humans.40 Microglia in the brain promote the remyelination of neurons by facilitating the proliferation and differentiation of oligodendrocyte progenitor cells.40 In the inner ear, two previous studies show that after supporting cell ablation, the supporting cell subtype IPhCs completely regenerate in the neonatal cochlea, with cells in the GER serving as the source of regeneration.24,26 Here, through in vivo fate-mapping, scRNA-seq, and pharmacologic inhibition of cochlear explants, we have identified two independent mechanisms of regeneration following IPhC loss: one which requires the mitosis of adjacent cells (in the apical and middle turns) and the second is the direct transdifferentiation of GER cells into new IPhCs across all three cochlear regions. Through computational analysis and validation of scRNA sequencing data from the damaged and regenerating GER and IPhCs, we found that damaged GER cells lose specialization, proliferate by upregulating DNA replication genes while downregulating cell cycle inhibitors, and upregulate lateral GER and IPhC genes, leading to IPhC regeneration. Lastly, we showed that the inhibition of proliferation only partially reduced IPhC regeneration. Together, our data provide the first extensive scRNA-seq dataset on the dual mechanisms of supporting cell regeneration in the mammalian cochlea.

Spatially distinct mechanisms of inner phalangeal cell regeneration in the neonatal mouse cochlea

During development of the neonatal mammalian cochlea, hair cells display a gradient of maturity, with the hair cells at the base differentiating and maturing earlier than the apex. In the adult cochlea, hair cells in the base of the cochlea are more structurally and functionally mature compared to the apex.41 Similarly, supporting cells in the organ of Corti from the apical turns are more regenerative and responsive to drug manipulation than their basal counterparts.7,20,42,43,44 Here we show that GER cells in the neonatal cochlea regenerate IPhCs through two spatially distinct mechanisms: both mitotic regeneration which occurs in the apical and middle regions and non-mitotic regeneration (direct transdifferentiation) which is the primary mechanism of regeneration in the base. A similar spatially segregated mechanism of regeneration has also been observed in the chicken basilar papilla, where mitotic hair cell regeneration is the primary process in the neural region, and direct transdifferentiation occurs in the abneural region.4,33,45,46,47,48,49,50 In the undamaged basilar papilla, two transcriptionally distinct groups of supporting cells have been identified in the neural and abneural regions, which correlate with the different regenerative properties of these cell groups.49

In the chicken basilar papilla, direct transdifferentiation occurs first (within 24 h after hair cell damage), followed a few days later by mitotic regeneration.33,51 Similarly, we observed that IPhC regeneration occurs earlier in the base compared to the apical and middle turns of the damaged cochlea (Figure S2). These findings highlight the similarities in the spatial and temporal dynamics of regeneration between avian species and neonatal mice.

Mitotic regeneration of inner phalangeal cells in the neonatal mammalian cochlea

In a previous study where IPhCs were ablated using Plp1-DTA transgenic mice, IPhC regeneration occurred without any evidence of proliferation.24 This contrasts with our damage model which shows mitotic regeneration in the apical and middle cochlear turns.26 As Lgr5 is expressed in other cell types other than the IPhCs (inner border cells, lateral GER, inner pillar cells, and the third row of Deiter’s cells), we postulate that DT treatment in our damage model causes more severe damage than the Plp1-DTA model, leading to the robust cell proliferation observed in the lateral GER (Figure 1C).26 Indeed, we previously demonstrated a dose-dependent relationship between DT concentration and the degree of damage and proliferation,26 further corroborating that the greater degree of damage observed in our model likely contributes to the proliferation observed.

Interestingly, although we observed proliferation in the lateral GER in damaged mice in vitro, we did not observe mitotically regenerated (EdU+) IPhCs as were seen in vivo. We also did not find any significant increase in IPhCs from 2DIV compared to 3DIV in the basal turn of the damaged cochlea, suggesting that the basal turn did not fully regenerate IPhCs in vitro (Figure 5D). However, inhibiting proliferation in vitro significantly impaired IPhC regeneration in the apex and middle turns (Figure 5), suggesting that the dual mechanisms of IPhC regeneration were present both in vivo and in vitro. In fate-mapping experiments, EdU was administered once daily between P3-P5 to label mitotic cells at the time of injection. Previous studies have found the half-life of EdU in mice to be 24–60 min after oral, intravenous, or intraperitoneal administration.52,53 Therefore, it is possible that non-mitotically regenerated IPhCs were underestimated in the current study. As we fate-mapped Ki67 cells and found a similar proportion of mitotically to non-mitotically regenerated IPhCs (Figure 2), such an underestimation is deemed unlikely.

Lineage analysis of our scRNA-seq dataset suggests that the lateral GER cells in the damaged cochlea follow one of three distinct lineages, with lineage 2 identifying mitotic progenitors uniquely upregulating genes associated with the regulation of transepithelial transport, neuron differentiation and neurogenesis, and genes involved in the mitotic cell cycle. Lineage 1 depicts dynamic expression of candidate factors mediating non-mitotic regeneration of IPhCs. For instance, Fgf10, which is upregulated in both lineages, is required for vestibular morphogenesis and cochlear outgrowth, and its expression continues in the GER in the postnatal cochlea.54 In the developing brain, gain- and loss-of-function experiments have shown that a transient expression of Fgf10 is required for proper differentiation of radial glial cells, which serve as neural progenitors.55 Fgf10 treatment enhanced axonal growth and regeneration and improved functional recovery in rats following peripheral nerve injury.56 Furthermore, Fgf10 is essential for adult stem cell maintenance in the lung and promotes lung epithelial regeneration after various stresses.57 In our model, upregulation of Fgf10 in the GER after damage may implicate its role in differentiation of regenerating IPhCs.

Other genes upregulated in the lineage of direct transdifferentiation (non-mitotic regeneration) included ribosome assembly and translation, neuron differentiation and neurogenesis, and cell adhesion. Among the highly expressed genes in groups 3 and 4 of the trajectory analysis were Neuropilin-1 (Nrp1) and Sema3a (Figure 4E). Neuropilin-1 is a co-receptor for signaling molecules and Sema3A, both crucial to guiding cell migration, axon pathfinding and angiogenesis58,59 In the embryonic and neonatal mouse cochlea, Nrp1 and Sema3a are expressed in the cochlear duct and spiral ganglion neurons, with deletion of either gene causing aberrant innervation of hair cells.60,61 Their upregulation in lineage 1 suggests that they may play a role in non-mitotic regeneration of IPhCs, possibly by regulating cell migration. Lastly, some lateral GER cells did not participate in IPhC regeneration. Moreover, treatment with aphidicolin reduced IPhC regeneration in the apical and middle turns while non-mitotic regeneration appeared preserved. These findings suggest that the dual mechanisms of IPhC regeneration operate independently, and that proliferation is required, at least in part, for IPhC regeneration and cannot be replaced by a non-mitotic, regenerative process.

Our study underscores a difference in damage response between the apical and basal GER cells, specifically in the neonatal cochlea. In addition, supporting cells in the organ of Corti proliferated and appeared to reorganize after damage despite a lack of regeneration.26 An alternative approach to delineating this spatial difference could be to separately analyze the two regions via spatial transcriptomics. Moreover, whether such a spatial difference in the damage response exists in the mature cochlea is unknown and warrants further investigation.

Damage disrupts the medial-to-lateral axis of the greater epithelial ridge

The GER is a transient columnar epithelial structure in the neonatal mouse cochlea and is remodeled into the inner sulcus after the first postnatal week.62 Transcriptomes of the undamaged neonatal cochlea have been reported and demonstrate the specialization of GER cells into lateral and medial subgroups.23,63,64 We confirmed this GER specialization by analyzing our single-cell data and identified and validated several known medial (Calb1, Crabp1, and Lgals1) and lateral (Fabp7 and Jag1) GER markers. Remarkably, in the damaged group at all time points, we observed a disruption in the medial-to-lateral GER axis where medial GER markers were found to be more diffusely expressed and less segregated (Figures 3G–3I; Figure S8). As damage is limited to the Lgr5+ lateral GER, these changes in expression suggest that damage results in cell signaling to the medial GER. Considering the remarkable regenerative capability of the medial and lateral GER,65 these changes raise the possibility of a dedifferentiation and/or regenerative response. One hypothesis suggested by this finding is that the medial GER cells can detect changes in the lateral GER cells and act as supporting cell progenitors that respond to IPhC damage. These cells can then migrate laterally to replenish the lost IPhC population. A similar phenomenon has been observed in other species and organs. In the zebrafish lateral line, distinct supporting cell populations are differentially regulated in response to damage.66 Moreover, mantle cells located in the periphery of the neuromast serve as a reserve progenitor cell pool that responds to severe damage.67 In the dermis and hair follicle, injuries such as hypoxia induce the activation of multiple classes of progenitors via the IFN homolog interleukin-24 (IL-24)/IL-24 receptor signaling/STAT3 loop. IL-24 then acts in both an autocrine and paracrine manner to coordinate re-epithelialization, re-vascularization, and dermal fibroblast proliferation to regenerate damaged tissue.68 In the intestine, regeneration occurs as progenitor cells undergo de-differentiation prior to hyperproliferation and regeneration.69,70 The GER of the neonatal mammalian cochlea may act similarly, where the lateral GER is more proliferative and retains the regenerative capacity of adjacent IPhCs, whereas the medial GER functions as a reserve progenitor pool.

In summary, our work elucidates two distinct mechanisms by which GER cells regenerate supporting cells through proliferation and direct transdifferentiation, with these dual processes being spatially restricted in the neonatal mammalian cochlea. The comprehensive transcriptomic datasets of the quiescent and regenerative cochleae should enable the discovery of new candidate targets involved in regenerating supporting cells in the damaged inner ear.

Limitations of the study

One main limitation of this study is that time points immediately after damage were not interrogated, thus limiting our insights into the early transcriptomic changes in the GER. Thus, it remains unclear how spatially distinct (apical versus basal or medial versus lateral) GER cells respond and become progenitors. Secondly, several candidate regulators of regeneration predicted in silico warrant validation through additional gain- or loss-of-function experiments.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice

The following mouse strains were used: Lgr5-DTR-EGFP (gift from F. de Sauvage, Genentech),27 GLAST-CreERT (Slc1a3-Cre-ERT, Stock #12586, Jackson Laboratory),73 Rosa26R-tdTomato (Ail4, Stock #7914, Jackson Laboratory),74 and Ki67-CreERT2 (Stock #029803, Jackson Laboratory).75 Both sexes of mice were used. Four developmental time points were assessed (P4, P5, P6, and P7). To ablate Lgr5+ cochlear supporting cells, 4 ng/g diphtheria toxin (DT, Millipore) was administered to P1 Lgr5DTR-EGFP/+ mice through intraperitoneal (IP) injections. Wildtype littermates injected with DT served as controls.

To activate Cre recombinase, tamoxifen was dissolved in corn oil and injected IP. 0.2 mg/g tamoxifen was injected into GLASTCreERT/+ mice at P1, 4 hours after DT injection. For Ki67CreERT2/+ mice, 0.25 mg/g tamoxifen was injected at P3. EdU (25 μg/g, IP, Invitrogen) was injected once daily (i.e. every 24 hr) between P3-P5 to label proliferative cells in a subset of experiments. Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Stanford University School of Medicine approved all procedures in accordance with NIH guidelines (protocol #18606 and 26956).

Method details

Genotyping

Genomic DNA was isolated from collected tail tip samples of mice by adding 180 μl of 50 μM NaOH and incubating for 1 hr at 98°C, followed by the addition of 20 μl of 1 M TrisHCl. Conventional PCR was performed for genotyping using primers listed in Table S1.

Organotypic cochlear cultures

Organotypic cochlear explants were prepared as previously described31 from cochleae of P2 Lgr5DTR/+ mice after DT injection at P1. Pups were euthanized by cervical dislocation and surface sterilized by immersion in 70% ethanol for 10 minutes prior to dissection. The cochlear epithelium was dissected and plated on CellTaq-coated glass coverslips (Ted Pella). DMEM/F12 media supplemented with B-27, N2, EGF, bFGF, IGF, heparin sulfate, and 100 μg/ml ampicillin was used (Sigma and Invitrogen) and the media was changed daily.76 Cultures were incubated at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 48 hr or 72 hr, with a subset supplemented with 5 μM EdU and 10 μM aphidicolin.

Immunofluorescence

Cochleae were harvested at defined time points and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in phosphate buffered solution (PBS), pH 7.4 Electron Microscopy Services (Fisher) for 1 hr at room temperature (RT). Organotypic cochlear cultures were fixed in 4% PFA for 45 minutes at RT. Tissues were permeabilized with 0.5% TritonX-100 (in PBS) for 45-60 min at RT. Tissues were then blocked with 5% donkey serum, 0.1% TritonX-100, and 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA), (dissolved in PBS) for 1 hr at RT. This was followed by incubation with primary antibodies dissolved in the blocking solution overnight at 4°C. The following day, tissues were washed three times with PBS (10-minute washes each) and then incubated with secondary antibodies diluted in PBS containing 0.1% TritonX-100, and 1% BSA for 2 hr at RT. Tissues were washed another three times with PBS at 10-minute intervals and mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen) then coverslipped.

The following primary antibodies were used: goat anti-Sox2 (1:200; R&D Systems), rabbit anti-fatty acid binding protein 7 (FABP7, 1:200, Abcam), mouse anti-Connexin 26 (1:400; Thermo Fisher), rabbit anti-EAAT1/GLAST (1:500; Abcam), rat anti-ki67 (1:100; Thermo Fisher), rabbit anti-cellular retinoic acid binding protein 1 (CRABP1, 1:200, VWR-Proteintech), goat anti-Galectin-1 (1:50, R&D), rabbit anti-calbindin (Calb1, 1:200, Cell Signaling Technologies), goat anti-Jagged1 (1:100, Santa Cruz). Alexa Fluor secondary antibodies (488, 546, 1:500, Invitrogen) were used. DAPI (1:10,000, Invitrogen) and the Click-iT EdU Alexa Fluor 647 Imaging kit (Invitrogen) were used as per manufacturer’s instructions.

Imaging and cell quantification

Whole mount cochleae and cryosections were captured as z-stack images using an LSM880 confocal microscope (Zeiss). Cells were quantified using Fiji from z-stack confocal images (1 μm intervals) of the sensory epithelia. Representative images are shown as single-plane images of the apical, middle, and basal turns of the cochlea unless otherwise stated.

Single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and computational analysis

Whole sensory epithelia of cochlea were dissected in cold, sterile HBSS (Cytiva, Cat# 18777-008) and tissues from one to two mice were pooled together per sequencing run. The genotypes used were Ki67CreERT/+; Lgr5DTR/+; Rosa26RtdTomato/+ (damaged group) and Ki67CreERT/+; Rosa26RtdTomato/+ (undamaged controls) mice. After dissection, the organs were pooled together into one sylgard coated dish for each genotype, incubated in thermolysin (0.5 mg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, T7902) at 37°C for 45 minutes. The organs were then transferred to 300 μL of Accutase (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat# 00-4555-56) in a 1.5 mL LoBind eppendorf tube and incubated at 37°C for another 20 minutes. The tube was turned upside down and 300 μL of DMEM/F12 with 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Fisher) was added to the epithelium.77 Trituration was performed using 60x pipetting at 500 μL volume to dissociate into single cells. The volume was then transferred to another 1.5 mL LoBind Eppendorf tube with a 40 μm filter mini strainer (pluriSelect). Cells were spun down in a 4°C centrifuge for 5 min at 300 rcf. Supernatant was aspirated and the cell pellet was then resuspended in 60 μL DMEM/F12 with 5% FBS. Cell counting and viability was assessed prior to sequencing. For each genotype and time point, two individual sequencing runs were performed.

Cells were captured for sequencing at the Stanford Functional Genomics Facility using 10x Genomics Chromium Single Cell 3' Reagents v3.1 kit (10x Genomics) and resulting libraries were sequenced on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina) using a 150 bp paired-end read configuration. Reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome mm39 with a supplemental TdTomato-WPRE sequence using 10x Genomics Cell Ranger v6.0.0 with default parameters.71 Filtered digital gene expression matrices were used for downstream data analysis using Seurat v5.1.0.78 Briefly, cell profiles were filtered based on the capture of sufficient unique counts and features, as well as a maximum of 10% mitochondrial gene content. Doublets were removed using DoubletFinder,79 and cells of interest from each time point and genotype were isolated based on the expression of known markers. Cell profiles from different time points and conditions were integrated using Harmony and visualized on UMAP plots generated with scCustomize.80,81 Gene ontology and KEGG analyses of differentially expressed genes in clusters 8 and 17 were performed using clusterProfiler. Trajectory analysis was performed with Slingshot using default settings.30 Significantly changed genes along the identified lineages (p-value < 0.05, Log2 fold change > 0.25 or < -0.25) were calculated using the tradeSeq function associationTest with lineages = TRUE.72 Gene ontology analyses of pseudotime gene groups were performed using ShinyGo v0.80.82 Detection of genes with differential gene expression at lineage branchpoints was performed using the tradeSeq function earlyDETest, using the default number of knots (6) and knots of interest = c(3, 4) and pairwise = TRUE. Gene ontology and KEGG analyses of differentially expressed genes at the branchpoint of lineages 1 and 2 were performed using clusterProfiler. Data were further analyzed with the use of QIAGEN IPA (QIAGEN Inc., https://digitalinsights.qiagen.com/IPA).32 Datasets are deposited and available for additional analysis on the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, accession number GSE291228).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (v10). ANOVAs followed by post-hoc analyses are specified in figure legends. All experiments (except for sc-RNA sequencing) were done in triplicates or more. N represents either the number of mice or cochlea examined. Data shown are mean ± SD. Statistical analysis tests used are mentioned in figure legends. Significance was denoted as the following: ∗p<0.05, ∗∗p<0.01, ∗∗∗p<0.001, ∗∗∗∗p<0.0001.
