To me, or not to me: Effects of mRNA adenine methylation on biotic stress responses
Authors: Raul Sanchez-Muñoz
Published: 2023

Plants have evolved mechanisms to regulate transcription by the interplay of intricate and dynamic biological networks. Among these, DNA and RNA modification by methylation have been demonstrated to be powerful—and complex—regulatory mechanisms (Sharma et al. 2021; Long et al. 2008). Posttranscriptional modification of RNA by the methylation of adenine bases at the N6 position, producing N6-methyladenosine (m6A), is the most common modification of coding and noncoding RNAs. In this issue, Wil Prall, Arsheed H. Sheikh, and colleagues (Prall et al. 2023) explore the dynamics of adenine methylation and its effects on plant immunity during biotic stress.

Comparing a set of Arabidopsis (A. thaliana) mutants partially deficient in the m6A writer machinery or overexpressing the m6A eraser AlkH10B, the authors provide an exhaustive map of the m6A dynamism before and after Pseudomonas syringae DC3000 (Pst DC3000) infection (see Fig. panel A). All these mutants and the 35S:ALKBH10B overexpression line showed a significant decrease in m6A levels compared with the Col-0 background (control conditions). When challenged with a Pst DC3000 infection, bacterial multiplication in the defective m6A mutants was significantly lower than in control conditions. Concordantly, both early responses (measured as ROS levels) and late responses (monitoring growth, weight, and hormonal levels) to biotic stress were more pronounced and faster when the levels of m6A were reduced, leading to an overall better performance under biotic stress.

The authors identified transcripts differentially methylated in control versus stress conditions using RNA-seq with m6A-RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing. The detection of hypomethylated genes in the defective mutants compared with Col-0 samples suggested that in basal conditions, m6A deposition in genes related to plant immunity helps to maintain their low levels of expression. On the other hand, after pathogen exposure, a decrease in m6A deposition was measured particularly in immunity-related genes, contrasting with an increase in the methylation levels of growth- and development-related genes. Characterization of the hypomethylated mutants revealed a key role of m6A deposition in the trade-off between immunity and growth and development. In addition, the loss of methylation in the m6A-deficient mutants seems to act as a stress-priming event, allowing these lines to be better prepared for potential future infections (see Fig. panel B).

Going a step further to provide unambiguous evidence for the mechanistic role of m6A deposition, a genome-wide Mapping of Uncapped and Cleaved Transcripts sequencing was performed to explore the effects of methylation on mRNA stability. As previously described (Anderson et al. 2018), a correlation between the levels of m6A and transcript stability was found. A significant increase in transcript stability was detected in the Col-0 background after but not before the pathogen stress, which was not found in the m6A-defective mutants. This suggests a direct yet context-specific connection between m6A and mRNA stability. Nevertheless, the relationship between m6A and gene expression seems to be more complex because m6A accumulation was related to both positively and negatively regulated genes. These results suggest that specific regions along the mRNA accumulating m6A may influence transcript abundance, and therefore it is both a region- and condition-specific process.

The authors performed different comparisons between all the sequenced datasets—RNA-seq, RNA-seq with m6A-RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing, and genome-wide Mapping of Uncapped and Cleaved Transcripts sequencing —obtaining a comprehensible and exhaustive map of the m6A dynamics under biotic stress responses. The results show that m6A modifications play a vital role in the trade-off between immune responses and growth and development. m6A abundance can be viewed as regulating gene expression by modulating the delicate equilibrium between mRNA stability and abundance. By unravelling mRNA methylation mechanisms and their biological relevance, the work of Prall et al. (2023) brings us one step closer to fully understanding the intricate relationship between plants and their environment. To methylate, or not to methylate; that is the question.
