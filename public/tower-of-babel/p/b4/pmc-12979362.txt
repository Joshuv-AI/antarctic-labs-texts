Hospital Air and Healthcare Personnel as Reservoirs of Drug-Resistant Bacteria in Tertiary Care Settings in Pakistan
Authors: Nabeel Ahmad, Muhammad Faizan Imran, Rana Talal Ahmed, Mirza Muhammad Suleman, Madiha Riaz, Ahsan Sattar Sheikh, Ming-Hsien Chiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe aim of this cross-sectional study was to evaluate the environmental–human interface of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) by investigating drug-resistant bacteria in hospital air and the nasal microbiota of healthcare personnel across four tertiary care hospitals in Pakistan.Material and MethodsA total of 80 samples (40 aerosol samples and 40 nasal swabs) were collected and analyzed using culture-based microbiological identification. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed using the Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institute-guided disc diffusion method, and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was used to screen representative β-lactam–resistant isolates for key resistance genes, including blaZ, blaI, CTX-M, and SHV.ResultsBacterial isolates were categorized into three ecological groups: environment-exclusive strains (eg, Escherichia coli detected only in aerosols and viridans streptococci detected only in nasal samples), co-existing strains prevalent in both niches (notably Staphylococcus aureus and Staphylococcus epidermidis), and strains showing marked differential prevalence between environments (eg, Serratia marcescens predominant in aerosols and Enterococcus spp. in nasal samples). A high proportion of isolates exhibited resistance to multiple antibiotic classes, consistent with multidrug-resistant (MDR) phenotypes, while resistance patterns suggestive of extensive drug resistance were observed in selected isolates. Molecular analysis confirmed the presence of clinically relevant β-lactamase genes, including blaZ/blaI in aerosol-derived S. aureus and CTX-M/SHV in nasal Klebsiella pneumoniae.ConclusionThese findings demonstrate that hospital air acts as not only a reservoir for environmental pathogens but also a potential conduit for microbial exchange with healthcare personnel. Therefore, integrated infection control strategies incorporating air quality management and personnel surveillance are essential. As the interpretation of the findings is limited by the cross-sectional design and reliance on culture-based methods, future studies should include longitudinal and molecular-based approaches.

Introduction

Hospital environmental monitoring, coupled with healthcare personnel surveillance, is fundamental to understanding the spread of multidrug-resistant pathogens, particularly in heavily populated areas such as Lahore, Pakistan. Tertiary care hospitals in this region operate under conditions characterized by high patient turnover, crowded clinical spaces, and variable implementation of infection prevention and control measures.1 In many healthcare facilities in Pakistan, routine airborne monitoring and nasal screening of healthcare personnel are not systematically implemented, limiting early detection of environmental contamination and asymptomatic carriage of resistant microorganisms.2 Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has emerged as a pressing global health concern, threatening the effectiveness of existing treatments and leading to severe public health outcomes.3 AMR occurs when microorganisms, such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites, evolve mechanisms to resist the effects of antimicrobial agents such as antibiotics, antivirals, and antifungals.4 Resistance mechanisms may arise through genetic mutations, natural selection, or resistance gene acquisition via horizontal gene transfer.5 Once these microorganisms acquire antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs), they survive and thrive in environments containing antimicrobial agents. Drug resistance complicates the treatment of infections, often leading to prolonged hospital stays and increased morbidity, and even death. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), AMR microorganisms are responsible for approximately 700,000 deaths annually, with projections indicating that this figure could increase to 10 million by 2050 if no effective measures are implemented.3

Hospitals where several immunocompromised or at-risk patients are admitted represent a dangerous setting for the spread of AMR pathogens.6 The emergence of AMR microorganisms is worrisome because they can aerosolize and spread throughout hospital settings.7 Patients showing colonization of bacteria, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, and extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Escherichia coli, may transmit pathogens through droplets that are generated when coughing, sneezing, or talking.8 Such droplets can either be airborne or form droplet membranes and splashes that can be deposited on surfaces, thereby posing a threat to other patients, healthcare workers (HCWs), or visitors, and subsequently disseminating resistant pathogens. This risk is amplified in high-dependency areas such as intensive care units (ICUs) and emergency departments, where ventilation may be suboptimal and patient density is high.9

The spread of AMR airborne pathogens (APs) across hospitals is a threat to not only patients, but also healthcare providers who work closely with patients and handle medical devices.10 Hospital employees, particularly those threatened by communication with patients or their surroundings, are the most vulnerable to AMR pathogen infection themselves, promoting the spread AMR microorganisms in hospital settings. Healthcare workers may be asymptomatic carriers of AMR pathogens; therefore, they can act as disseminating agents of AMR microorganisms to other patients, further burdening the healthcare system.11 In resource-limited settings, where systematic surveillance programs are often lacking, healthcare personnel may inadvertently serve as mobile reservoirs linking environmental and human microbial niches. Consequently, integrated infection control strategies that incorporate environmental monitoring, personnel screening, and targeted surveillance of airborne transmission pathways are increasingly recognized as essential components of AMR pathogen containment.8

Data available on the ecological overlap between hospital air and healthcare personnel as interconnected reservoirs of AMR microorganisms, particularly in resource-limited healthcare settings, are limited. In this study, we assessed the presence of AMR pathogens in hospital air and among healthcare personnel in tertiary care hospitals in Pakistan. Specifically, we aimed to identify the most prevalent AMR pathogens using microbiological identification in high-risk areas of hospitals, such as ICUs, emergency departments, and general wards, and to evaluate the mechanisms by which these pathogens are transmitted through air. Molecular techniques, including polymerase chain reaction (PCR), were employed to detect selected β-lactam resistance genes in representative aerosol- and nasal-derived bacterial isolates. By integrating environmental and human surveillance, this study sought to provide evidence to inform context-specific infection prevention and control strategies in tertiary healthcare settings.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection

Four tertiary care hospitals were selected because of their extensive patient volumes, city settings, and identical medical service delivery, including intensive care and general medical divisions. The findings showed general validity for related healthcare settings throughout the area because the methodology involved testing multiple key locations. Forty aerosol samples were collected from multiple critical locations within each ward, including those directly adjacent to patient beds, central corridors, and near ward entrances, to differentiate between patient-related aerosol dissemination and general environmental contamination. The samples were collected via passive (non-volumetric) air sampling using the settle plate technique, with agar and chocolate plates exposed for a 30-min period to capture airborne particles. As we employed a non-volumetric approach, sampled air volume was not quantified. The non-volumetric approach was selected as it is a practical and widely used method for environmental AMR pathogen surveillance in resource-limited hospital settings. Nasal swab samples were obtained from 40 healthcare workers, including doctors, nurses, lab technicians, pharmacists, and security personnel. Each swab was carefully collected from both nostrils using sterile culture swabs to minimize contamination. All samples were transported immediately under sterile conditions and processed promptly; no long-term storage was performed prior to culture-based analysis. Samples were carefully labelled and processed following the standard biological procedures. The sample size was determined based on feasibility, consistency with prior environmental AMR pathogen surveillance studies, and the need to capture representative microbial diversity across high-risk hospital areas.

Ethics Approval Statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of The University of Lahore (Approval No. 24/300, IMBB).

Patient Consent Statement

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to study commencement.

Microbiological Examination and Identification in Collected Samples

Microbiological examinations and identification in both the nasal and aerosol samples were performed under stringent conditions to prevent contamination. The nasal swabs were immediately placed in sterile transport containers and promptly processed to preserve the microbial mass. The swabs were immersed in 5 mL of nutrient broth and vortexed to release microbial organisms; the broth was incubated at 37°C for 24 h. Subsequently, the incubated suspension was cultured on selective differential media, such as mannitol salt agar (MSA) for the growth of gram-positive organisms, blood agar for hemolysis assessment, chocolate agar for fastidious organisms, MacConkey agar for gram-negative organisms, and EMB agar for E. coli. Finally, after 24 h of incubation, the type of hemolysis, pigmentation, and metabolic overlays were examined. The most common bacteria in all samples were isolated on nutrient agar to obtain pure colonies for further biochemical analysis.

Simultaneously, the aerosol samples collected from hospital environments were transported under sterile conditions, plated on nutrient and chocolate agar plates, and incubated at 37°C. After incubation, colony morphologies were recorded and common pathogens were isolated. The isolated colonies were subjected to a series of biochemical tests, including Gram staining and catalase, DNase, urease, triple sugar iron (TSI), indole production, motility, and Simon citrate tests, to confirm the identity of the organisms. These tests, along with culture on selective and differential media and analysis of colony morphology, facilitated the identification of microbial species. Advanced automated identification systems (eg, VITEK and MALDI-TOF) were not available for use in this study.

AST

AST was performed following the Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) protocol to assess the resistance profiles of isolated bacterial strains. Bacterial suspensions were prepared using 0.5% McFarland standard and streaked on Mueller–Hinton agar plates. After applying antibiotic discs, the plates were incubated at 37°C for 16–18 h. The antibiotic susceptibility profiles were evaluated to assess non-susceptibility across multiple antimicrobial classes. MDR was defined as resistance to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial classes. Resistance patterns suggestive of extensive drug resistance (XDR) were observed in selected isolates; however, formal XDR classification was not systematically applied across all isolates owing to limited testing across all antimicrobial categories.12

Molecular Identification

Bacterial DNA was extracted, and its yield and quality were determined; a conventional PCR assay was performed using a set of designed primers (PrimerQuest - design qPCR assays | IDT) (Table 1) to detect β-lactamase–resistance genes. The PCR conditions were as follows: 5°C initial hot-start activation at 94°C and amplification for 30 cycles with denaturation at 94°C for 30s, annealing at 50°C–60°C for 30s, and extension at 72°C for 1 min with a 7-min terminal extension at 72°C. The obtained PCR products were further analyzed using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, revealing key resistant markers, which indicated that the genes detected corresponded to β-lactamase genes responsible for resistance in K. pneumoniae and S. aureus. Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics are primarily used to summarize the prevalence and distribution of bacterial isolates, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and resistance genes.13 Categorical variables are expressed as frequency and percentage. Where appropriate, inferential statistical analyses were conducted to compare the prevalence of bacterial isolates between aerosol and nasal samples using the Chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test, with Fisher’s exact test applied when expected cell counts were <5. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1; R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Results with p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant when inferential testing was applicable.

Results

Bacteria were isolated from aerosols and nasal swabs collected from healthcare professionals. The identification process involved the analysis of colony morphology, Gram staining, and a series of biochemical tests. Gram-positive cocci, S. aureus, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and Enterococcus spp., were identified using catalase and DNase tests. Gram-negative organisms, including E. coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, K. pneumoniae, Acinetobacter sp., Serratia marcescens, and Haemophilus influenzae, were identified using biochemical tests such as TSI agar, urease, oxidase, motility, citrate utilization, and indole production tests. Additionally, viridans streptococci and Streptococcus pneumoniae were identified among the gram-positive species. These biochemical characteristics, along with morphological differences, enabled the identification of various bacterial species present in the nasal and aerosol samples. The distribution of the identified pathogens, their AST patterns, and the PCR-based identification of resistance genes are described in the following sections.

Prevalence and Distribution of Bacterial Isolates in Hospital Environments

A total of 80 bacterial isolates, comprising 40 from aerosol samples and 40 from nasal swabs of healthcare professionals, were identified and characterized (Figure 1). The analysis revealed distinct distribution patterns, allowing for the classification of isolates into three ecological groups. Group A consisted of environment-exclusive strains. Notably, V. streptococci (90% prevalence) and H. influenzae (75%) were exclusively detected in nasal samples, consistent with their role as common inhabitants of the human upper respiratory tract. Conversely, Bacillus spp. (40%) and E. coli (25%) were detected only in aerosol samples, suggesting that their primary reservoir is the inanimate hospital environment. Group B included strains co-existing in both environments with similar prevalence rates. This group was dominated by common opportunistic pathogens, such as S. epidermidis (80% in aerosol and 95% in nasal samples) and S. aureus (70% in aerosol and 85% in nasal samples), indicating their ubiquitous presence and potential for cross-contamination between personnel and the air. Other co-existing species included Streptococcus spp. (60% vs 50%), K. pneumoniae (40% vs 45%), and P. aeruginosa (25% vs 15%). Group C comprised strains with a significant differential prevalence, indicating a strong preference for one environment. Serratia marcescens (75% vs 40%) and A. baumannii (50% vs 20%) were significantly more prevalent in aerosol samples than in nasal samples. In contrast, Enterococcus spp. showed a higher prevalence in nasal samples (30%) than in aerosol samples (10%). These differences indicate a descriptive trend toward environmental preference; however, there were no significant differences in the prevalence of these species between the aerosol and nasal samples (p > 0.05). Overall, these findings highlight a complex interplay between the microbial communities of the hospital air and those of the healthcare personnel, characterized by both shared microbiota and niche-specific bacterial populations. Detection of β-Lactamase Genes

To investigate the molecular basis of the observed β-lactam resistance, conventional PCR was performed on representative phenotypically β-lactam–resistant S. aureus and K. pneumoniae isolates. Among aerosol-derived S. aureus isolates (n = 28), blaZ (375 bp) and blaI (250 bp) were detected in 25 isolates (87.5%), indicating the widespread presence of penicillin-resistance determinants (Figure 2a and b). Among nasal K. pneumoniae isolates (n = 18), CTX-M (593 bp) was detected in 13 isolates (72.5%), while SHV (858 bp) was detected in 7 isolates (37.5%), supporting the genetic basis of ESBL phenotypes observed in these strains (Figure 2c and d). These molecular findings provide direct genetic evidence supporting the phenotypic β-lactam–resistance patterns identified in hospital-associated bacterial isolates. Discussion

This study presents compelling evidence for a paradigm shift in our understanding of AMR dynamics within healthcare facilities, moving beyond traditional surface-focused infection control models toward a comprehensive ecological framework that recognizes the hospital environment as an interconnected microbial ecosystem.14,15 Our findings demonstrate that hospital air and the nose of healthcare personnel (HCP) constitute synergistic reservoirs that may contribute to the circulation of MDR and XDR bacteria through bidirectional transmission pathways.

The substantial prevalence of key opportunistic pathogens shared between aerosol and nasal niches—S. aureus detected in 70% of aerosol samples and 85% of nasal samples, and S. epidermidis present in 80% of aerosol samples and 95% of nasal specimens—reveals a concerning failure of conventional infection prevention strategies to interrupt microbial dissemination in high-density healthcare environments.16 These findings align with those of recent meta-analyses, which demonstrated that traditional surface decontamination protocols inadequately address the three-dimensional nature of pathogen transmission, particularly in facilities with suboptimal ventilation systems.17

The development of our tripartite ecological classification system represents a considerable methodological advancement in healthcare epidemiology, enabling the stratification of bacterial isolates into: (A) environment-exclusive strains, (B) co-existing strains, and (C) strains exhibiting significant differential prevalence.9 This analytical framework transcends conventional binary presence–absence reporting to provide mechanistic insights into transmission dynamics that are essential for developing targeted, evidence-based interventions.18

Previous studies in hospital epidemiology emphasize that the most sophisticated infection control programs now employ similar ecological approaches to identify “superspreader” organisms and high-risk transmission nodes.19 Prior research has demonstrated that effective AMR pathogen containment requires understanding of the specific ecological niches and transmission preferences of individual pathogen species, rather than applying uniform control measures across all microorganisms.15 The significantly high nasal carriage rate of S. aureus (85%) among HCP in our study substantially exceeds global benchmarks, with recent systematic reviews reporting pooled MRSA carriage rates of 17.20% in Pakistan, 5.65% in India, and 8.83% in Nepal.20 This disparity suggests that Pakistani healthcare facilities represent particularly high-risk environments for staphylococcal transmission, potentially reflecting inadequate implementation of evidence-based infection prevention protocols or structural deficiencies in healthcare infrastructure.21

The concept of HCP as “mobile reservoirs” has gained considerable traction among leading healthcare epidemiologists, who recognize that traditional hand hygiene interventions, while necessary, are insufficient to address the complex dynamics of respiratory-mediated transmission.22 Contemporary research demonstrates that healthcare workers continuously aerosolize their nasal microbiota through routine respiratory activities, creating persistent bioaerosol clouds that can remain suspended for extended periods and travel considerable distances within healthcare facilities.23 The predominant presence of E. coli in aerosol samples (60%) compared to minimal nasal colonization (5%) observed in this study exemplifies environment-specific pathogen ecology that requires targeted environmental management strategies.24 This finding is consistent with the results of emerging research, demonstrating that gram-negative bacteria, particularly Enterobacteriaceae spp., exhibit enhanced environmental persistence and can establish self-sustaining populations in hospital air handling systems, particularly in the presence of moisture and organic matter.25

The detection of key β-lactamase–resistance genes (blaZ and CTX-M) in both environmental and nasal isolates provides crucial molecular evidence linking phenotypic resistance patterns to specific genetic determinants.26 This genetic concordance supports our ecological model by demonstrating that resistance genes circulate freely between environmental and human reservoirs, likely facilitated by horizontal gene transfer mechanisms operating within the hospital ecosystem.27,28 Contemporary experts in molecular epidemiology recognize that successful AMR pathogen surveillance programs must integrate phenotypic and genotypic characterization to identify emerging resistance mechanisms and track their dissemination patterns.17 The most sophisticated surveillance systems now employ whole-genome sequencing to map transmission networks and identify previously unrecognized epidemiological links between seemingly unrelated isolates.29

Our findings necessitate a fundamental reconsideration of infection prevention strategies, moving from compartmentalized approaches toward integrated, ecosystem-based interventions.30 The most effective programs now combine enhanced environmental controls (including advanced air filtration and ultraviolet disinfection systems) with comprehensive HCP screening and decolonization protocols.31,32 Leading infection prevention specialists emphasize that future hospital designs must incorporate principles of microbial ecology, including optimized air flow patterns, antimicrobial surface technologies, and real-time environmental monitoring capabilities.33 The integration of artificial intelligence and machine learning approaches for predictive modeling of transmission risk represents an emerging frontier that promises to revolutionize infection prevention practice.34

While our study provides valuable insights into AMR ecology in Pakistani healthcare facilities, certain limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precluded assessment of temporal dynamics and seasonal variations in pathogen prevalence. Second, the reliance on culture-based detection methods may underestimate the true diversity of the hospital microbiome, as some fastidious organisms require specialized culture conditions or may exist in viable but non-culturable states.35 In addition, demographic characteristics such as age and sex of healthcare personnel were not recorded, as the primary objective was to investigate environmental–human ecological overlap rather than individual-level risk profiling, which limited stratified epidemiological analyses. Future investigations should incorporate demographic data to enable more detailed assessment of host-related factors. Furthermore, future studies employing metagenomic approaches could provide a more comprehensive characterization of hospital microbiomes and help identify previously unrecognized transmission pathways.36 Longitudinal studies integrating environmental surveillance with clinical outcome data will be essential to validate the clinical relevance of this ecological framework and to evaluate the effectiveness of targeted infection control interventions.

Conclusions

We propose an ecological framework for understanding AMR pathogen transmission within healthcare facilities, highlighting hospitals as interconnected microbial environments in which environmental air and healthcare personnel act as overlapping reservoirs. The tripartite classification system introduced here offers a structured approach to distinguish environment-exclusive, co-existing, and differential prevalence, which may help identify high-risk interfaces for targeted infection prevention strategies. The observed burden of MDR phenotypes, together with resistance patterns suggestive of extensive drug resistance in selected isolates, underscores the importance of integrated, ecosystem-informed approaches to infection control that consider airborne pathways alongside conventional surface-based interventions. While the cross-sectional design of this study precludes inference of direct transmission pathways or temporal dynamics, the findings support the value of combining environmental air monitoring with healthcare personnel screening. Collectively, this ecological perspective may inform future infection prevention efforts by guiding targeted interventions, including improved air quality management, focused surveillance of high-risk wards, and risk-based antimicrobial resistance monitoring. Longitudinal and genomics-based studies will be essential to validate these observations and translate this framework into evidence-based infection control strategies.
