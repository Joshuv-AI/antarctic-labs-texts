Clinical efficacy of modified laminoplasty in the treatment of cervical spondylotic myelopathy
Authors: Zhiyu Huang, Tianzuo Zhan, Shaodong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLaminoplasty complications, particularly axial symptoms and C5 nerve root palsy, significantly impair patients’ quality of life. To address these challenges, we used a modified surgical technique that preserves muscular attachments to the C3 and C7 spinous processes while incorporating C4/C5 foraminotomy, aiming to reduce the incidence of these complications.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 93 patients who underwent laminoplasty for cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM) at our institution between January 2016 and June 2022. The cohort comprised 42 patients receiving modified laminoplasty (modified group) and 51 patients undergoing traditional laminoplasty (traditional group). Demographic and surgical parameters, including gender, age, operative duration, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative hospital stay, and complications, were systematically collected. Radiographic parameters including C0-C2 Cobb angle, C2-C7 Cobb angle, T1 slope (T1S), and C2-C7 sagittal vertical axis (SVA) were measured preoperatively and at follow-up on X-ray films. Functional outcomes were evaluated using Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) and Neck Disability Index (NDI) scores.ResultsThe mean follow-up duration was 55.2 months. No significant differences were observed in terms of gender, age, or operation time between the two groups. Modified group exhibited significantly lower intraoperative blood loss and shorter postoperative hospital stays compared to traditional group (p = 0.013 and p = 0.024, respectively). At follow-up, the C2-C7 Cobb angle and C2-C7 SVA in the modified group, as well as the C2-C7 Cobb angle in the traditional group, demonstrated significant reductions (17.02 ± 10.49° vs. 20.76 ± 10.55°; 24.00 ± 9.18 mm vs. 26.81 ± 9.22 mm; 14.94 ± 12.31° vs. 23.04 ± 10.58°, p < 0.05). Furthermore, the decrease in the C2-C7 Cobb Angle was more pronounced in the traditional group compared to the modified group (-8.10 ± 9.24° vs. -3.74 ± 8.52°, p < 0.05). The NDI score improved more significantly in the modified group than in the traditional group (-13.90 ± 7.59 vs. -10.16 ± 8.74, P = 0.032), whereas the improvement in the JOA score was similar between the two groups (p = 0.344). Additionally, the increase in the postoperative NDI score was positively correlated with increases in T1S and C2-C7 SVA (r = 0.181, p < 0.05; r = 0.208, p < 0.01).ConclusionCompared with traditional laminoplasty, the modified laminoplasty provided equivalent neurological decompression efficacy while demonstrating superior clinical advantages, including reduced surgical invasiveness, preserved cervical stability, and a lower incidence of postoperative axial symptoms, though it showed no significant difference in preventing postoperative C5 palsy.

Background

Laminoplasty is one of the common surgical approaches for treating cervical spondylotic myelopathy (CSM). The underlying principle involves enlargement of the spinal canal to induce posterior migration of the spinal cord, thereby achieving direct decompression of posterior neural structures while simultaneously producing indirect decompression of anterior compressive elements [1]. However, clinical observations following the application of traditional laminoplasty have revealed several limitations: (1) Insufficient attention to nerve root decompression, coupled with the predominant clinical manifestation of mixed spinal cord-nerve root type cervical spondylopathy, leading to suboptimal postoperative outcomes; (2) High incidence (9-80%) of postoperative axial symptoms (AS) [2, 3]; (3) Elevated risk (4.2-24.1%) of C5 palsy (C5P) [4, 5]; (4) Potential instability caused by laminar opening at C2 or C7 levels [6–9].

The cervical segments most frequently affected clinically are C5-C6, followed by C4-C5 and C6-C7. Decompression of the three most common segments can be completed by opening the C4, C5 and C6 laminae and resecting the upper half of the C7 lamina. Therefor, we used a modified surgical approach that preserves muscular attachments to both C2 and C7 spinous processes. This technique incorporates selective C4/C5 foraminotomy based on preoperative symptom assessment, aiming to maintain cervical lordosis while reducing postoperative axial symptoms and C5 nerve root palsy incidence.

Methods

Study population

This study conducted a retrospective analysis of patients who underwent posterior cervical surgery for CSM at our institution between January 2016 and June 2022. The present study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongda Hospital affiliated to Southeast University (Clinical trial number: 2017ZDSYLL001-P01). The inclusion criteria were: (1) Diagnosis of CSM with clinical manifestations of cervical spinal cord impairment, primarily characterized by motor, sensory, and reflex disturbances in all four limbs; (2) Imaging studies confirming cervical spinal cord compression consistent with clinical symptoms. The exclusion criteria included: (1) Incomplete medical records; (2) Concurrent cervical spine infection, fracture, tumor, rheumatic diseases (such as ankylosing spondylitis), or neurological disorders (such as amyotrophic lateral sclerosis); (3) Previous cervical spine surgery. Based on the aforementioned criteria, the 93 patients were enrolled into two groups: the modified laminoplasty group consisting of 42 cases (36 males and 6 females), and the traditional laminoplasty group comprising 51 cases (40 males and 11 females; Table 1).

Surgical management

The standard posterior cervical approach was employed, with the side exhibiting more severe spinal cord compression selected as the opening side. In the modified group: For C2-C3 spinal cord compression on preoperative MRI: C3 lamina and inferior C2/C3 ligamentum flavum were excised, with partial C2 dome undercutting as needed; otherwise, partial C3 laminectomy was performed. For C6-C7 compression: The proximal 1/3 to 1/2 of C7 lamina was resected using a drill or ultrasonic osteotome, preserving C7 spinous process and C6/C7 interspinous ligament; otherwise, full C7 lamina was retained. C4-C6 laminae were elevated toward the hinged side, confirming dural sac expansion and adequate decompression. Appropriately sized titanium plates were fixed between the opened laminae and lateral masses to maintain expansion. Patients with C4/5 foraminal stenosis or severe spinal cord compression where significant posterior cord shift is anticipated, keyhole decompression of the C5 nerve root is performed at the C4/C5 foramen on the open-door side. Symptomatic foraminal stenosis confirmed radiologically warranted corresponding segment keyhole decompression until nerve root release. Radiographic analysis

The C0-C2 Cobb angle was defined as the angle between the McGregor’s line and the line connecting the lower endplate of C2; cervical lordosis (CL) was typically measured using the C2-C7 Cobb angle, formed by the intersection of lines along the lower endplates of C2 and C7; T1 slope (T1S) represented the angle between the tangent line of the T1 superior endplate and the horizontal line; and the C2-C7 sagittal vertical axis (C2-C7 SVA) was quantified as the horizontal distance from the posterosuperior corner of the C7 vertebral body to a vertical plumb line passing through the geometric center of the C2 vertebral body. All radiological parameters were measured on lateral radiographs by two independent researchers, with the average values selected for analysis. The detailed measurements are shown in Fig. 2.

Clinical evaluation

The measured surgical outcomes included operative time, intraoperative blood loss and postoperative hospital stay. Neurological function was evaluated preoperatively and during follow-up using the Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score, with recovery rate (%) calculated as (postoperative JOA − preoperative JOA)/(17 − preoperative JOA). Functional cervical status was assessed via the Neck Disability Index (NDI). Complications were systematically documented, including AS, C5P, cerebrospinal fluid leakage, neurological deterioration, and surgical site infection.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS 25.0 software. Categorical variables between the modified and traditional groups were compared using χ2 tests, while continuous data were analyzed with paired 2-tailed t test or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Preoperative and postoperative follow-up outcomes were evaluated using repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA). Use linear regression analysis and Pearson’s coefficients to assess correlations between the variables. A two-tailed α level of 0.05 was set as the statistical significance threshold, with P < 0.05 indicating statistically significant differences.

Results

Demographic and clinical data

With a mean follow-up duration of 55.2 months, both groups demonstrated no statistically significant differences in gender distribution, age, or operative time. Modified group exhibited significantly reduced intraoperative blood loss and shorter postoperative hospitalization periods compared to traditional group (p = 0.013 and p = 0.024; Table 1). Regarding postoperative complications, modified group demonstrated 6 cases of axial symptoms, 4 cases of C5 nerve palsy, 1 case of neurological deterioration, 1 case of surgical site infection, and no instances of cerebrospinal fluid leakage. In contrast, raditional group exhibited 19 cases of axial symptoms, 3 cases of C5 nerve palsy, 2 cases of neurological deterioration, 2 cases of surgical site infection, and 4 cases of cerebrospinal fluid leakage. A statistically significant difference in the incidence of axial symptoms was observed between the two groups (p > 0.05; Table 2). Within the modified group, 20 patients underwent prophylactic foraminotomy, with one developing postoperative C5 palsy. Among 22 patients without prophylactic foraminotomy, three developed C5 palsy, showing no statistically significant difference (p > 0.05; Table 3).

Radiological parameter assessment

No significant intergroup differences were observed in preoperative and postoperative sagittal parameters between the two groups (p > 0.05; Table 4). In the modified group, C0-C2 Cobb angle, C2-C7 Cobb angle, and T1 slope decreased from22.76 ± 10.86°, 20.76 ± 10.55°, and 26.81 ± 9.22°, respectively, preoperatively, to 21.69 ± 13.54°, 17.02 ± 10.49°, and 24.00 ± 9.18°, respectively, at the final follow-up, while the C2-C7 SVA increased from 15.87 ± 15.07 mm preoperatively to 17.76 ± 13.06 mm at final follow-up. In the traditional group, C0-C2 Cobb angle, T1 slope, and C2-C7 SVA increased from 21.16 ± 10.79°, 26.24 ± 9.29°, and 18.37 ± 11.40 mm, respectively, preoperatively, to 22.45 ± 10.69°, 26.33 ± 9.44°, and 19.33 ± 14.75 mm, respectively, at the final follow-up, while the C2-C7 Cobb angle decreased from 23.04 ± 10.58° preoperatively to 17.76 ± 14.94 ± 12.31° at final follow-up. Compared to preoperative measurements, the modified group demonstrated significant reductions in both the C2-C7 Cobb angle and C2-C7 SVA, while the traditional group showed a significant decrease in the C2-C7 Cobb angle (p < 0.05). Furthermore, the traditional group exhibited a greater reduction in the C2-C7 Cobb angle compared to the modified group (−3.74 ± 8.52° vs. −8.10 ± 9.24°, p < 0.05).

Clinical outcome assessment

Both groups demonstrated no significant differences in preoperative JOA and NDI scores (p = 0.317 and p = 0.720, respectively; Table 5). Postoperatively, significant improvements were observed in both groups: JOA scores increased from 10.02 ± 3.02 and 10.61 ± 2.59 preoperatively to 14.10 ± 3.05 and 13.86 ± 2.46 at follow-up (P < 0.05), while NDI scores decreased from 22.52 ± 6.31 and 22.02 ± 7.06 preoperatively to 8.62 ± 7.98 and 11.86 ± 7.85 at follow-up (p < 0.05). No statistically significant difference was observed in JOA score recovery rates between groups: 58.30% ± 36.79% in the modified group versus 50.00% ± 45.62% in the traditional group (p > 0.05). However, the modified group demonstrated significantly greater reduction in Neck Disability Index (NDI) scores compared to the traditional group (−13.90 ± 7.59 vs. −10.16 ± 8.74, P = 0.032).

Correlation analysis

The results of the correlation analysis are given in Table 6. Postoperative increases in NDI scores were positively associated with elevated T1S (r = 0.181, p < 0.05) and C2-C7 SVA (r = 0.208, p < 0.01). In contrast, postoperative JOA scores showed no significant correlations with C0-C2 Cobb angle, C2-C7 Cobb angle, T1 slope, or C2-C7 SVA. Analysis of imaging parameters revealed significant correlations: the C0-C2 Cobb angle demonstrated a significant negative correlation with the C2-C7 Cobb angle (r = −0.392, p < 0.01) but a positive correlation with the C2-C7 SVA (r = 0.532, p < 0.01). The C2-C7 Cobb angle exhibited a positive correlation with the T1S (r = 0.387, p < 0.01) and a negative correlation with the C2-C7 SVA (r = −0.292, p < 0.01). Furthermore, the T1S showed a significant positive correlation with the C2-C7 SVA (r = 0.464, p < 0.01).

Discussion

Axial symptoms is a prevalent complication following posterior cervical surgery, with reported incidence rates ranging from 9 to 80% in previous studies [2, 3]. Axial symptoms is currently believed to arise from multiple etiological factors: disruption of the posterior cervical muscle-ligament complex (MLC), muscle detachment from the C2 and/or C7 spinous processes, loss of cervical stability, reduced range of motion, diminished neck muscle strength, and prolonged cervical collar immobilization [3]. The preservation of muscular attachments to the C2 and C7 spinous processes may play a more critical role than deep cervical extensor muscle protection in preventing axial symptoms [10, 11]. These muscles provide the majority of extension torque, and their disruption compromises the mechanical contribution of extensor musculature in maintaining cervical lordosis and sagittal alignment. This leads to progressive loss of cervical curvature and mobility. To compensate and maintain horizontal gaze, intact extensor muscles undergo prolonged hypercontraction, ultimately resulting in chronic cervicoscapular pain, stiffness, and even muscle spasms.

The semispinalis cervicis muscle attached to the C2 spinous process serves as a primary contributor to cervical extension, generating 37% of the total extension torque within the posterior cervical musculature during contraction, as demonstrated in biomechanical studies of neck muscles [6]. Shimizu et al. [7] further emphasized its critical role in maintaining cervical stability and preserving physiological lordosis. Conventional laminoplasty involving C3-C7 decompression typically requires resection of the distal C2 spinous process to access the C3 lamina, thereby disrupting the semispinalis-C2 attachment [12]. C3 laminectomy, instead of C3 laminoplasty, achieves spinal cord decompression while preserving the muscular attachments to the C2 spinous process. This approach achieves spinal cord decompression while preserving the muscular attachments to the C2 spinous process, improving postoperative cervical mobility and reducing axial symptom incidence from 81–47.5% [13–16].

Postoperative interspinous encroachment following C3 laminoplasty has been identified as a significant contributor to axial pain and restricted cervical mobility [17]. Lee et al. [17]demonstrated that C3 laminoplasty resulted in reduced C2-C3 interlaminar spacing, with a 42.2% incidence of C2-C3-C4 laminar fusion at 3-year follow-up, directly impairing cervical range of motion. In contrast, C3 laminectomy effectively preserves muscular attachments to the C2 spinous process, eliminates impingement/stimulation of C2-inserted musculature by laminar flap displacement, and facilitates extended decompression via straightforward C2 distal decompression when required, thereby obviating the need for direct C2 laminotomy.

The muscular attachments to the C7 spinous process include the trapezius, rhomboid minor, serratus posterior superior, splenius capitis, and nuchal ligament. The nuchal ligament, while connecting the scapular girdle muscles, directly anchors to the osseous surfaces of the C6 and C7 vertebrae, redistributing excessive mechanical loads from the mid-to-upper cervical spine and playing a pivotal role in maintaining cervical stability and lordosis [8]. Takeuchi et al. [18] introduced a superior C7 hemilaminectomy with dome resection, which achieved adequate decompression while preserving the muscular attachments to the C7 spinous process. This technique not only resulted in favorable neurological recovery but also significantly reduced postoperative axial symptom incidence. The modified surgical approach, incorporating C3 laminectomy, C4-C6 laminoplasty, and C7 dome resection, preserved muscular attachments to the C2 and C7 spinous processes. Postoperatively, the modified group demonstrated a significantly lower incidence of axial symptoms compared to conventional methods.

C5 nerve root palsy represents another common complication of posterior cervical surgery, with reported incidence rates ranging from 4.2 to 24.1% in the literature, predominantly occurring on the opening side [4, 5]. Most cases manifest within the first postoperative week, while a minority develop symptoms 2–4 weeks postoperatively [19]. Preoperative C4/C5 foraminal stenosis is recognized as a risk factor for C5 palsy (C5P). When C4/C5 foraminal stenosis is absent (diameter ≥ 4.1 mm), correction angles exceeding 20° constitute the critical risk factor for palsy onset. Conversely, when the C4/C5 foraminal diameter measures < 2.7 mm, the risk increases significantly regardless of correction status [20, 21].

Sasai et al. [22]demonstrated that combining foraminotomy with laminoplasty reduced the incidence of C5 palsy (C5P) from 8.1 to 0%. Two additional studies corroborated this approach, reporting reductions in C5P rates from 6.4 to 1.4% and from 4.0 to 0.6%, respectively [23, 24]. However, some studies have not shown efficacy of prophylactic foraminotomy in mitigating C5P risk, while cautioning that resection exceeding 50% of the facet joint during decompression may induce segmental instability [25, 26]. Nakajima et al. [27]further noted that asymptomatic C4/C5 foraminal stenosis frequently coexists in patients without C5P, suggesting prophylactic foraminotomy constitutes an unnecessary invasive procedure for preventing radicular dysfunction. A recent multicenter retrospective study [28]demonstrated that C5 palsy (C5P) is significantly associated with the number of laminoplasty segments (odds ratio [OR] = 2.0), suggesting that limited and selective laminoplasty may effectively prevent this complication.

In this study, foraminotomy decompression failed to effectively prevent C5 nerve root palsy (C5P), likely attributable to the multifactorial pathology of C5P. The heterogeneous clinical manifestations reflect that underlying C5 nerve root compromise may involve distinct pathomechanisms, thereby elucidating the persistent challenges in identifying isolated risk factors [4].

Current evidence remains insufficient to establish a significant association between secondary kyphosis following laminoplasty and diminished clinical outcomes. However, in patients with preoperative loss of cervical lordosis, postoperative disappearance of physiological curvature and subsequent kyphotic deformity increase anterior mechanical stress on the spinal cord, potentially compromising surgical decompression efficacy and ultimately leading to poor prognosis [29]. Posterior cervical surgery inevitably disrupts the musculoligamentous complex (MLC), risking lordosis loss. This triggers adaptive increases in T1 slope (T1S) to maintain cephalic balance and horizontal gaze, while compensatory cervical lordosis develops. Final cervical alignment depends on the equilibrium between postoperative kyphotic progression and compensatory lordotic adaptation [30, 31]. Patients with low T1S exhibit greater compensatory capacity to preserve sagittal balance, whereas those with elevated T1S demonstrate limited adaptability. Furthermore, studies have identified significantly positive correlations between C2-C7 sagittal vertical axis (SVA) and regional cervical dysfunction, global health scores, and myelopathy severity [32, 33], consistent with our findings. Some scholars also put forward different opinions. Kim et al. [34] believed that changes in radiological parameters had nothing to do with clinical results, and that sufficient decompression and strong fixation were the basis for good surgical results.

This investigation has several limitations requiring clarification. As a single-center retrospective study, the data encompass surgical cases from January 2016 to June 2022, with follow-up assessments consolidated into a single session in September 2024. Prolonged postoperative follow-up intervals (extending up to 8 years for some patients) introduce substantial risks of long-term recall bias. Furthermore, surgical procedures were performed by surgeons with varying levels of experience over a 6.5-year period. Despite adherence to standardized surgical protocols, potential confounding effects on outcomes may arise from accumulated surgical experience and phased technical refinements among operators.

Conclusion

The modified laminoplasty preserved muscular attachments to the C2 and C7 spinous processes while incorporating selective C4/C5 foraminotomy based on preoperative symptomatology. Compared with traditional laminoplasty, the modified laminoplasty provided equivalent neurological decompression efficacy while demonstrating superior clinical advantages, including reduced surgical invasiveness, preserved cervical stability, and a lower incidence of postoperative axial symptoms. However, no significant difference in C5 nerve root palsy (C5P) rates was observed, and the specific therapeutic efficacy of this modification warrants further investigation.
