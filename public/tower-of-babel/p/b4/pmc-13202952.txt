Synthesis, characterization, and antitumor potency of Pd2+, ZrO2+, and VO2+ complexes with tridentate Schiff base: in vitro biological and in-silico insights
Authors: Shimaa M. Abdel-Fatah, Maram T. Basha, Ahlam I. Al-Sulami, Mohamed R. Shehata, Laila H. Abdel-Rahman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Schiff base complexes of ZrO2+, VO2+, and Pd2+ have been produced and characterized by IR, TGA, UV/Vis, and 1 H NMR methods, as well as powder X-ray diffraction (XRD), elemental (CHN) analysis, mass spectrometry, conductivity, and magnetic characteristics. Density Functional Theory (DFT) computations applying the Gaussian 09 software were employed to estimate the equilibrium geometry of the ligand and its complexes at the B3LYP level of theory. The 6-311G++(d, p) basis set was used for the C, H, O, N and Br atoms, and lanl2dz for the Pd, V, and Zr atoms. ZrO2+ and Pd2+ complexes demonstrate diamagnetic behavior, but magnetic susceptibility investigations verify the paramagnetism of the VO2+ complex. Based on spectroscopic and magnetic susceptibility studies, the ZrO2+and VO2+ complexes have square pyramidal geometries, and Pd2+ ZrO2+ complex has a square planar geometry. All complexes demonstrate strong antimicrobial potency against various bacterial and fungal species. Pd2+ > ZrO2+ > VO2+ > H2B is the order of antimicrobial effectiveness, revealing the biggest inhibition zone against Gram-negative bacteria by the Pd2+ complex. The order of the rise in antifungal capability against A. flavus is Pd2+ > ZrO2+ > VO2+> H2B. Additionally, the complexes exhibit notable cytotoxicity against cancer cell lines, such as HCT-116, in contrast to Vinblastine. Moreover, these compounds also demonstrate great potential as free radical scavengers. Finally, possible binding mechanisms at the active sites of bacterial and fungal receptors were explored through docking studies.

Introduction

Schiff bases are ligands with biological activity. They form metal complexes that possess enhanced biological properties [1, 2]. Zirconium shows significant coordinating ability with ligands containing oxygen, nitrogen, and sulfur. These complexes are used in many biomedical applications, such as prosthetic joints, dental implants, and adjuvants for antibody production [3]. Because zirconium and Schiff bases can create stable metal–organic complexes with cytotoxic [4–7], antibacterial, and catalytic properties, these complexes serve as excellent models for certain metalloenzymes. Biological activity of Schiff base complexes depends on metal’s nature, geometry, and electronic propereties. Among other applications, Schiff bases themselves have been employed as chemical synthesis intermediates [8–11].

Palladium (II) complexes are used extensively in biological applications because of their strong bactericidal and anticancer effects. Several studies have shown that palladium (II) complexes have strong antibacterial effects through mechanisms like reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, membrane interruption, and DNA interaction via intercalation [12]. Furthermore, this element can be combined with biologically active ligands to modify the complexes’ characteristics and increase their antibacterial activity by utilizing the kinetic effects of complexation. Recent research has demonstrated that vanadium complexes offer several physiological and pharmacological benefits, including insulin-mimetic, diuretic activity, antihypertension, anti-hyperlipidemia, anti-obesity, and an increase in hemoglobin and myoglobin’s affinity for oxygen [13–17]. Therefore, the cytotoxic properties of these substances were investigated in animals with tumors and carcinogenesis. Furthermore, compared to healthy cells and tissues, malignant cells and tissues accumulate more vanadium-based substances [18–21].

Despite extensive research on metal complexes of Schiff bases, there is a lack of diverse metal ion exploration, comparative analyses, and detailed mechanistic insights. Therefore, in this work, we synthesize and thoroughly characterize a series of novel transition metal complexes, specifically ZrO2+, VO2+, and Pd2+ imine complexes, using a variety of analytical techniques. Transition metal complexes, specifically ZrO2+, VO2+, and Pd2+ were studied due to their promising biological features.We evaluate these complexes’ biological activities, focusing on their antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anticancer potentials. To learn more about the geometry, computational investigations are employed as well, such as DFT simulations and molecular docking was employed for studying interaction mechanisms of the complexes with biological targets as PDB ID: 1R4U and PDB ID: 6KQ9. The ultimate goal of this research is to aid in developing novel metal complexes with improved therapeutic qualities.

Synthesis of imine ligand (H2B) and its metal complexes

2-amino-3-hydroxypyridine (1.0 mmol, 0.11 g, 20 ml EtOH) was mixed with 5-bromosalicylaldehyde (1.0 mmol, 0.20 g, 20 ml EtOH). The mixture of reaction was refluxed for two hours in air at 65 °C, then, filtered and rinsed with ethyl alcohol (H2B); Yellow powder; yield, (71%); mp-225 ◦C; FTIR (cm− 1): 3671 (-OH), 1620 (C=N), 1271 (C-O phenol). UV/ vis (λmax): 266, 425, 516 nm. 1H NMR (δ, ppm): 7.25–8.08 (m, 6 H-Ar), 9.48(s, 1H, CH=N), 10.22 (s, 1H, OH), 6.93 (s, 1H, OH); Mass (EI-MS): 292.93 (Calculated: 292.90) for C12H9O2N2Br; Elemental analysis: C 48.95; H 3.22; N 9.56 (Calculated: C, 49.16; H, 3.07; N, 9.55).

The final product was filterated, rinsed with ethanol, and desiccated to produce an orange powder with an 81% yield and a melting point of over 300 °C. Physical characterization

The Gallenkamp device (UK) was used to determine the melting point. Employing a Shimadzu FTIR spectrophotometer{model 8101}, infrared spectra were captured in range 4000 –400 cm− 1. In acetonitrile, UV-visible spectra were obtained employing a PG spectrophotometer {model T + 80}. The 1H and 13C NMR spectra were acquired using a Bruker instrument running at 400 MHz, with DMSO-d6 as the solvent and TMS as the internal standard. A Jenway conductivity meter {model 4320} was applied to assess the conductivity in DMF. A Shimadzu 60 H analyzer was utilized to conduct thermogravimetric analysis under nitrogen at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. At Cairo University, elemental analysis was performed using a Perkin-Elmer elemental analyzer {model 240c}. Magnetic properties were estimated using Gouy’s balance. Mass spectra were generated using EI at 70 eV with a Hewlett-Packard MS-5988 GC-MS instrument. pH was measured at 298 K using a HANNA 211 pH meter. The crystal structure was investigated with an X-ray diffractometer (PANalytical X’Pert³-Powder) using monochromatized Cu Kα radiation in range 5–80ο. Anticancer activity was assessed with an ELISA microplate reader (Sunrise, TECAN, Inc., USA), measuring absorbance at 590 nanometers at the Regional Center for Mycology and Biotechnology; Al-Azhar University.

Antimicrobial potency

The disc route was employed to assess the antimicrobial potency of the Schiff base and its metal complexes (with a concentration of 20 (µg/mL) in DMSO) against E. coli (-ve, ATCC25922), M. luteus (+ ve, ATCC 10240), A. flavus (RCMB 002002), and G. canidum ( RCMB 041001). The plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C for the bacteria and 72 h at 35 °C for fungi.The measurements were carried out three times.It was established what the minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) was [22–23]. The first tube without turbidity was evaluated as the MIC. The antifungal agent was fluconazole, and the antibacterial agent was ofloxacin.

Anticancer potency

HCT-116 colon cancer cells were employed in the antitumor potency test. For antitumor assays, the tumor cell lines were suspended in medium, then incubated for 24 h. The tested compounds were then added into 96-well plates (three replicates) to achieve six concentrations for each tested compound. After incubating for 24 h, the numbers of viable cells were determined by the MTT test. Then, the optical density was measured at 590 nm to determine the number of viable cells and the percentage of viability was calculated as [(ODt/ODc)]x100% where ODt is the mean optical density of wells treated with the tested sample and ODc is the mean optical density of untreated cells. The 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50) was estimated from graphic plots of the dose response curve for each conc. using Graphpad Prism software. After the end of the treatment at tested concentration, the plates were inverted to remove the medium, the wells were washed three times with 300 µl of phosphate buffered saline (pH 7.2) and then the cells were fixed to the plate with 10% formalin for 15 min at room temperature. The fixed cells were then stained with 100 µl of 0.25% crystal violet for 20 min. The stain was removed and the plates were rinsed using deionized water to remove the excess stain then allowed to dry. The cellular morphology was observed using an inverted microscope (CKX41; Olympus, Japan) equipped with the digital microscopy camera to capture the images representing the morphological changes compared to control cells. The cytopathic effects (morphological alterations) were microscopically observed at 200x [24].

Free radical scavenging potency

The free radical scavenging experiment using {2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH)} was carried out to spectrophotometrically evaluate the antioxidant efficacy of the H2B ligand and its complexes. A DPPH solution was prepared using {0.004% (w/v)} methanol and stored at 10 °C in the darkness. Compounds were prepared in various concentrations {1280, 640, 320, 160, 80, 40, 20, 10, and 0 µg/mL} with methanol. After mixing 1 mL of each sample with 3 mL of DPPH solution, the optical density was measured using spectrophotometry. Absorbance decrease was monitored from one to sixteen minutes at 515 nm. The percentage inhibition (PI) of DPPH radicals was calculated using the formula: PI = [(Acontrol - Acomplex)/Acontrol] * 100%, with ascorbic acid serving as the reference [25].

Computational aspects

The Gaussian 09 software was utilized for all computations at the B3LYP/GENECP level of theory. The ligand and desired complex geometries were computed by considering the atoms of C, H, N, O, and Cl at 6- 311 G++ (d, p) basis set; Pd, V, and Zr at lanl2dz basis set [26]. Density functional theory (DFT) frequency calculations were performed after geometry optimization to verify that all molecular structures are true local minima on the potential energy surface, rather than transition states. All vibrational frequencies are positive and real, indicating no imaginary frequencies.

Molecular docking

To get the probable binding modes at the receptors’ most active locations for Aspergillus flavus (PDB ID: 1R4U) [27] and Micrococcus luteus (PDB ID: 6KQ9) [28], were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank, for further analysis molecular docking was carried out utilizing MOA2022 software [29]. After loading the DNA structure into the MOE program, energy optimization was carried out. The preparation of the receptor was performed automatically by the program MOE. The generated model, using default settings in the SiteFinder tool included in the MOE software to find the most active site, allowed for a systematic conformational study with an RMS gradient of 0.01 kCal/mol. The docking validation procedures used by removing the co-crystallized ligand, docking it back into the protein, and calculating the RMSD between the docked and experimental pose and found to be less than 2 Å, indicates a reliable protocol.

Results and discussion

Physicochemical properties

Commonly, all the metal complexes dissolve in organic solvents like DMSO. The VO2+ complex is paramagnetic and has square pyramidal geometry, according to magnetic assessments, but the ZrO2+ and Pd2+ complexes are diamagnetic. Pd2+ complex has square planar geometry and ZrO2+ complex has square pyramidal geometry. According to conductivity measurements, none of the complexes have electrolyte properties. According to various analyses, the cations prefer a metal: ligand ratio of 1:1. The Schiff base’s production is confirmed by the ¹H NMR spectra [30]. No decomposition was observed during melting point determination. Based on what we get from elemental analyses, molar conductance, magnetic measurements, infrared and electronic spectra, we know the suggested structures of the prepared complexes. It is shown that H2B ligand behaves as dibasic tridentate ligand with ONO sites and coordinate with metal ions via two protonated phenolic-OH and azomethine-N.

Infrared spectroscopy

In the IR spectra obtained for the H2B ligand, the azomethine group is responsible for a sharp band at 1620 cm− 1. The spectrum also demonstrates a distinguishing band at 1271 cm− 1, implying the occurrence of a phenolic (C–O) group. Coordination with the azomethine (H–C=N) group is expected to result in a sharp band at 1594–1629 cm− 1 in all the metal complexes, confirming the complexation of the imine with metal ions. All the complexes exhibit prominent IR bands between 842 and 889 cm-1, indicating coordinated water molecules. The (M–N) and (M–O) stretching vibrations are attributed to the infrared bands found at 451–483 cm⁻¹ and 533–542 cm⁻¹, respectively, confirming the presence of metal ions in these complexes. Additionally, the IR bands observed at 906–979 cm− 1 are assigned to (Zr = O) and (V = O) vibrations respectively (Fig. 1(a-d)) [31–32].

1HNMR and 13CNMR of the prepared imine ligand and its metal complexes

The imine ligand’s 1H-NMR spectrum shows signals for the proton of the azomethine at 9.48 (s) δ and aromatic ring protons at 6.82–8.17 (m) δ, which confirms the formation of imine [33–34]. The peaks at (10.22 (s), 13.72 (s) δ) resulted from –OH groups. The imine ligand’s ¹³C-NMR spectra display a signal for the azomethine carbon at 160.26 ppm and signals at 111.16-150.44 ppm for aromatic carbons. The ¹H-NMR spectra of the ZrO2+ and Pd2+complexes show signals at 9.22 and 9.19 (s, δ), respectively, for the protons of the azomethine group as shown in Fig. 2(a-b). The hydroxyl group signal’s disappearance and the azomethine proton’s change in signal position verify that the ligand and metal ions are coordinating.

Electronic spectra

In the imine’s electronic spectra (H2B) ligand, the absorption band at about 266 nm is caused by the π→π* transition, while the absorption band at about 425 nm is caused by the n→π* transition. Because of the coordination between the nitrogen atom and the metal ions, the metal complexes reveal absorbance bands around 354–455 nm, illustrating the movement of electrons from the ligand to the metal ion. The coordination between the (H2B) ligand and the metal ions in the produced complexes is supported by the notable differences in optical density for the complexes as compared to the ligand. Furthermore, the measured paramagnetic properties of the VO2+ complex correlate well with its electronic spectra (Fig. 3) [35]. The zirconium and palladium complexes reveal diamagnetic properties.

Thermal analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis was conducted out at a heating rate of 10 °C min-1 in a nitrogen atmosphere. There are several phases of thermal breakdown, involving the elimination of coordination and hydrated water molecules as well as the eventual loss of the organic component. Weight losses of 8.01%, 8.69%, and 4.09% were observed in the ZrO2+, VO2+, and Pd2+ complexes, respectively. These weight losses are correlated with the loss of water molecules. The weight declines of 47.71–17.06% for the ZrO2+ complex, occurring between 196 and 788 °C, correlate with the exclusion of the residual damaged organic portion. For the VO2+ complex, weight losses of 33.64–37.58% between 174 and 779 °C indicate the removal of the residual thermally decomposed fraction. The Pd2+ complex showed weight losses of 25.55–42.06% between 211 and 791 °C, correlating to the exclusion of the residual damaged organic portion (Table 1) [20]. Mass spectra

The elemental analysis findings were in excellent agreement with the detected molecular ion peaks, according to the mass spectra that were collected. The stoichiometry of the H2B ligand is confirmed by the molecular ion peak at 292.93 m/z, which matches the calculated molecular weight of C₁₂H₉O₂N₂Br. The stoichiometry of the ZrO2+ complex is supported by the molecular ion peak at 452.20 m/z, which corresponds to the predicted molecular weight of C₁₂H13O6N₂BrZr. The stoichiometry of the VO2+ complex is confirmed by the molecular ion peak at 411.03 m/z, which matches the calculated molecular weight of C₁₂H₁₃O₆N₂BrV. The stoichiometry of the Pd2+ complex is supported by the molecular ion peak at 433.23 m/z, which corresponds to the calculated molecular weight of C₁₂H₁₁O₄N₂BrPd (Fig. 4(a-d)) [36]. The suggested molecular weight and stoichiometry were confirmed by the mass spectrum of the produced complexes, which displayed the expected molecular ion peak.

PXRD analysis

PXRD was used to confirm the crystalline nature.The spectra of the VO2+ complex displayed no clearly defined crystalline peaks, demonstrating that the VO2+ complex is amorphous. With lattice constants of a = 11.50000 Å, b = 4.85000 Å, and c = 12.60000 Å, the unit cell of the (H2B) ligand has a volume of V = 681.89 Å3. A requirement for a monoclinic structure, as a ≠ b ≠ c and α = γ ≠ β, was examined in light of these cell features, as shown in Fig. 5(a-d) [37]. The ZrO2+ complex’s unit cell has a volume of V = 4276.32 Å³ with lattice constants of a = 14.55500 Å, b = 18.53600 Å, and c = 12.66600 Å. These characteristics, which include a ≠ b ≠ c and α = γ ≠ β, point to a monoclinic structure. Lattice constants for the Pd2+ complex’s unit cell were a = 16.59000 Å, b = 13.96000 Å, c = 25.22000 Å, and V = 4657.47 Å³. These characteristics, which include a ≠ b ≠ c and α = γ ≠ β, point to a monoclinic structure. Using the Scherrer equation, the particle sizes of H2B, ZrO2+, and Pd2+ were determined to be 65 nm, 44 nm, and 35 nm, respectively. This indicates that the prepared compounds are in the nanoparticle range.

Antimicrobial activity

The inhibition zones (mm) and minimum inhibitory concentrations (µg/ml), presented in Tables 3 and 4, were measured to assess the antimicrobial activity. For the antibacterial and antifungal tests, respectively, ofloxacin and fluconazole served as references. We found that the metal complexes’ antibacterial potency went as follows: Pd2+ > ZrO2+ > VO2+ > H2B. The Pd2+ complex, which was most effective against Gram-negative bacteria, has the biggest inhibitory zone (28 mm). This behavior can be elucidated by the chelation theory and Overton’s idea. Electron delocalization occurs throughout the entire chelated ring when the donor atoms in the ligand share some of the metal ion’s positive charge, which improves the permeability of the bacterial membrane’s lipid layers.

The order of increasing antimicrobial activity against fungi (A. flavus) was Pd2+ > ZrO2+ > VO2+> H2B, as shown in Fig. 7(a-b) [38–39]. The Pd2+ complex had the maximum activity index (96.55%) and was the most efficient against Escherichia coli, as displayed in Table 5. Additionally, the Pd2+ complex had the maximum activity index (95.83%) and was the most efficient against Geotrichum candidum.

Anticancer potency

The cytotoxic potential of metal-imine complexes was evaluated by testing them against cancer cells in a concentration range of 0 to 500 µg/ml. The estimated IC50 values for each compound are presented in Figs. 8, 9 and 10, and 11. It was found that the metal-imine complexes had bigger potencies than the free imine ligand. The type of metal and the various coordination sites influence the biological potency. Based on Tweedy’s chelation theory, the metal atom involved in the complexes’ anticancer activity may play a crucial role. The findings suggest that each metal-imine complex (IC50 = 12.66–24.79 µg/ml) exhibited significant cytotoxicity against HCT-116 cancer cells compared to the ligand (IC50 = 62.06 µg/ml). Furthermore, by making the protons in the imine more acidic and forming stronger hydrogen bonds, the metal’s positive charge may enhance the anticancer efficacy and biological potency [41]. Among the three complexes, PdB demonstrated the highest potency, exhibiting low IC50 value of 12.66, comparable to the standard drug, vinblastine (IC50 = 2.65 µg/ml). In contrast, ZrOB and VOB exhibited moderate cytotoxicity with IC50 values of 20.96 and 24.79, respectively.

Apoptosis mediated by caspases is one way that metal-imine complexes partly stop the growth of HCT-116 cancer cells. Apoptosis is a key process for preventing cancer from advancing. Caspases trigger apoptosis, as shown by the comparison between Figures (8, 9, 10, and 11 C) (cells treated with the highest concentration of 500 µg/ml) and Fig. 12 (control cells without treatment, showing 100% cell survival). The results also relate to the standard drug vinblastine (IC50 = 2.65 µg/ml). The coordination of metal ions with imine ligands makes the complexes more lipophilic, helping them enter cancer cells. Once inside, these complexes can interact with cellular biomolecules to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), which promote apoptosis and cause oxidative stress. Additionally, the different shapes of each metal complex may affect how they interact with cellular targets like enzymes and receptors involved in cell growth and survival. The specific binding ability of these metal complexes to targets can interfere with important signaling pathways, further slowing cancer cell growth.

Free radical scavenging activity

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are highly reactive and potentially harmful chemical agents generated during metabolic processes in the body. Examples of ROS include hydrogen peroxide, hydroxyl radical, and superoxide anion. These molecules are known to cause oxidative damage to proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids, which can lead to various diseases such as cancer, diabetes, and Alzheimer’s disease. Antioxidant-rich medications that can slow down or prevent oxidative stress are crucial for protecting the body from this damage. As a control, ascorbic acid was used to generate the DPPH radical in evaluating the antioxidant activity of all compounds produced. The antioxidant activity of both the compounds and the standard increased dose-dependently. The dose-response curves illustrating the compounds’ ability to scavenge DPPH radicals are shown in Fig. 13. All compounds exhibited strong scavenging activity with the lowest IC50 values. The order of IC50 values for antioxidant activity was PdB < VOB < ZrOB < H2B < Ascorbic acid. This trend is explained by each sample’s ability to donate a hydrogen atom, breaking the chain reaction of free radicals. The results indicate that the PdB complex is the most effective, with the lowest IC50 value compared to the ligand, showing potential as a treatment for oxidative stress-related degenerative diseases. Therefore, each molecule possesses potent antioxidant properties, making these substances promising free radical scavengers [42].

Conclusions

Using a variety of analytical techniques, a novel Schiff base, H2B, and its ZrO2+, VO2+, and Pd2+ imine complexes were synthesized and thoroughly characterized in this work. NMR spectroscopy, FT-IR, elemental analysis, and mass spectrometry were employed to verify the compounds’ composition and structure. Based on conductivity assessments, none of the complexes are electrolytes. The ZrO2+ and Pd2+ complexes demonstrated diamagnetic features, but magnetic susceptibility measurements validated the paramagnetism of the VO2+complex. In line with a number of evaluations, divalent cations prefer a metal: ligand ratio of 1:1. According to biological activity testing, all metal complexes displayed exceptional inhibitory power against the investigated bacterial and fungal strains related to the free ligand. Specifically, the Pd2+ complex was most effective against A. flavus, showing the largest inhibitory zone against Gram-negative bacteria. Furthermore, these complexes demonstrated a high level of cytotoxicity (IC50 values greater than the free ligand) against colon cancer cells. These substances also hold significant promise as scavengers of free radicals. Using the Gaussian 09 program, To clarify the equilibrium geometry of the ligand and complexes, computations utilizing DFT were conducted. The electronic structure and stability of the produced complexes were demonstrated by these computer investigations. Furthermore, putative binding modes in the active areas of bacterial and fungal receptors were discovered by molecular docking analyses, suggesting potential biological roles for these targets. The findings of this investigation show that the synthesized metal-imine complexes hold considerable promise as antibacterial, antioxidant, and anticancer agents. Their enhanced lipophilicity and interaction with cellular targets likely contribute to their improved biological efficacy when coordinating with metal ions. The results reflect the importance of metal coordination in the progress of promising therapeutic agents.
