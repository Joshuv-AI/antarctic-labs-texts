Progress and application of biopolymer-based packaging systems integrated with Zataria multiflora Boiss. (Shirazi thyme) essential oil
Authors: Abbas Najafi Tabrizi, Yousef Ramezan, Amir Kamkari, Aida Gholamazad, Mohaddeseh Shahnazary, Fateme Fatahi, Setareh Haghighat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Active packaging systems have gained prominence as an effective alternative to conventional synthetic materials. Such systems aim to not only preserve product quality and extend shelf life but also to enhance the functional properties of the product. The essential oil (EO) of Zataria multiflora boiss. (Z. multiflora), rich in phenolic compounds, can serve as a valuable functional additive. This study reviews the incorporation of Z. multiflora essential oil (ZEO) into bio-based films/coatings via conventional and advanced methods, including nanoemulsions and Pickering emulsions. The present review aims to assess ZEO effects on the mechanical, barrier, optical, and functional properties of bio-based matrices, such as polysaccharides, proteins, and composites. However, further research is required to optimize ZEO stability, achieve the optimal controlled release of its active substances (e.g., carvacrol), and overcome challenges in industrial scalability and regulatory compliance.

Introduction

Active packaging systems incorporating functional agents have been recognized as a viable strategy to extend the shelf life and ensure the safety of food products. As a substantial natural compound, plant-derived EOs are notable for their broad inhibitory effects against spoilage and pathogenic microorganisms, as well as their capability to mitigate oxidative degradation (Moradinezhad et al., 2023). Belonging to the Lamiaceae family, Z. multiflora (also known as Shirazi thyme) is an aromatic and medicinal herb. The EO of this plant is rich in phenolic terpenoids like thymol and carvacrol. Such compounds deliver considerable antimicrobial and antioxidant properties, causing enzymatic malfunctions and fatally disrupting microbial cell membranes (Gago et al., 2025; Imran et al., 2022). ZEO effectively inhibits major foodborne pathogens (e.g., Escherichia coli, Listeria monocytogenes, and especially Staphylococcus aureus). Unlike the Gram-positive bacteria, Gram-negative ones are more resistant to ZEO function due to their less permeable outer membrane. Significantly, lower concentrations are required to inhibit fungal growth due to the greater susceptibility of molds and yeasts to ZEO, representing a distinct advantage for preventing such spoilage (Mohammadi, Ahari, & Yousefi, 2023; Mohammadi, Yekta, et al., 2023a). Such attributes introduce ZEO as a promising natural preservative for active food packaging systems. Accordingly, numerous studies have integrated ZEO into biopolymer matrices to create functional films/coatings. In a carboxymethyl cellulose film, for instance, integration of 1–3% ZEO provided strong antibacterial and antioxidant functionality, with a direct increase in efficacy based on its concentration (Dashipour et al., 2015). κ-Carrageenan, as another film matrix, with 3% ZEO and nanoclay, exhibited ∼78% lower water vapor permeability (WVP) and potent inhibition of several foodborne pathogens (Nouri et al., 2020). Similarly, zein film with up to 10% ZEO showed a ∼ 2–3 log CFU/g reduction in L. monocytogenes and E. coli populations. However, such incorporation can also affect the mechanical properties, which can be addressed by incorporating other materials, such as sodium bentonite nanoclay (Moradi et al., 2016). Collectively, these practical examples demonstrate ZEO's capacity to impart significant functionality to diverse edible packaging, while also revealing formulation trade-offs. Moreover, such films can retain ZEO's antioxidant capacity, potentially slowing oxidative spoilage (Kashiri et al., 2017). Recently, advanced delivery systems for ZEO have been explored to address formulation challenges. The direct dispersion of EOs into hydrophilic polymers often results in issues such as phase separation or quick volatilization. Therefore, to address such challenges, researchers have turned to nanoencapsulation techniques for ZEO, which enhance stability and enable controlled release (Rasteh et al., 2024; Salehi et al., 2020). In this regard, basil-seed gum films containing nanoemulsified ZEO demonstrated more homogeneous dispersion and potent antibacterial effects than films containing free EO (Gahruie et al., 2017). Likewise, encapsulating ZEO in electrospun polyvinyl alcohol nanofibers enhanced its retention and gradual release, effectively prolonging the preservation of fresh fruits (Moradinezhad et al., 2023). In addition to reducing the rapid loss of the volatile components, encapsulation also enables masking the intense aroma and flavor. Such approaches ensure a sustained antimicrobial effect by slowing the diffusion of ZEO's active constituents. Significantly, the implementation of these active packaging strategies has yielded quantifiable shelf-life extensions, with ZEO-infused films/coatings prolonging the acceptable storage period of fresh perishable products (Karami-Osboo et al., 2023). Despite these advances, significant gaps remain. The chemical variability of ZEO (depending on plant source and extraction) can affect its consistent performance. Additionally, the ZEO's exact release kinetics and function in packaging have not been fully elucidated. Debate persists on the relative importance of vapor-phase diffusion versus direct contact-mediated effects. High volatility and an intense aroma pose additional challenges since ZEO can evaporate quickly or impart off-flavors without proper encapsulation or optimal dosing (Dejene, 2025). For instance, increasing the ZEO concentration in a sodium caseinate coating enhances antimicrobial activity and also produces a prominent herbal taste. Achieving the right concentration that balances efficacy and sensory acceptability is therefore critical (Jackson-Davis et al., 2023). ZEO can make polymer matrices more brittle or more permeable at higher loads. Contradictory trends in barrier performance have also been observed, where ZEO reduced WVP in carrageenan films, yet in zein ones, an excessive ZEO content led to increased permeability and water sensitivity (Moradi et al., 2016; Nouri et al., 2020). Some studies suggest that combining ZEO (a membrane-active phytochemical) with other hurdles (e.g., nisin) could synergistically enhance antimicrobial efficacy through multiple mechanisms. However, such strategies remain underexplored (Dejene, 2025; Karnwal et al., 2025). Ongoing research is addressing these issues by optimizing encapsulation techniques and composite formulations, as well as investigating the scalability and regulatory compliance of ZEO-infused packaging. By tackling these challenges, the full preservative potential of ZEO in sustainable food packaging can be realized, paving the way for next-generation active packaging solutions to fulfill the growing demand for safe, clean-label food preservation strategies.

ZEO; an overview

Sources and availability

Z. multiflora, a perennial aromatic subshrub of the Lamiaceae family, is mainly cultivated in Iran, Pakistan, and Afghanistan. This southwestern Asia endemic plant is widely distributed across arid and semi-arid Iranian provinces, including Fars, Kerman, Hormozgan, and Khorasan, where it is known as “Avishan-e-Shirazi” (Shirazi thyme) (Niczad et al., 2019). Closely related to thyme and oregano (and occasionally referred to as “zaatar”), it carries considerable cultural significance, used as a seasoning in yogurt, soups, and meat dishes, as well as its importance in traditional medicine for treating gastrointestinal and respiratory conditions (Shomali & Mosleh, 2019). Historical sources, including Avicenna's Canon of Medicine, document its therapeutic applications as an antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, analgesic, spasmolytic, and carminative agent, reflecting a long-standing familiarity with its health benefits and flavor (Mahdizadeh et al., 2015). Z. multiflora is both harvested from the wild and cultivated, supported by an abundant natural distribution that ensures a steady biomass supply. Its EO can be extracted from its aerial parts—leaves and flowering tops— typically via hydrodistillation or steam distillation. The fresh or dried herb is readily available in the local markets, and both the plant and its oil are traded through informal and formal channels, including pharmaceutical companies. This level of accessibility, along with relatively high oil yields of approximately 2.3–3.6% on a dry-weight basis, supports its economic feasibility as both a medicinal and culinary herb in producing countries (Karimi et al., 2020). Although less common in global trade than common thyme or oregano, ZEO is gaining international recognition for its potent bioactivity, particularly as a natural preservative, with its market value influenced by harvest yields and regional demand (Niczad et al., 2019). Ecotype surveys conducted in Estahban (1767 m), Neyriz (1600 m), Fasa (1450 m), and Larestan (668 m) of the Fars province, recorded environmental parameters, such as altitude, temperature, precipitation, soil pH, electrical conductivity, and macronutrient content, alongside yield and compositional data to evaluate chemotypic variation (Sadeghi et al., 2015). The yield and composition of this plant are significantly affected by altitude and climate, where cooler and elevated sites (e.g., Neyriz and Estahban) deliver higher yields (∼3.25–3.58%) and greater thymol content, whereas warmer and lower-altitude sites (e.g., Fasa and Larestan) yield less (∼2.27–2.39%) with higher carvacrol proportions. Comparable yields (∼3.1%) have been obtained from ecotypes originating in Kerman province under similar environmental conditions. These chemotypic differences can be strategically leveraged to tailor batches for specific flavor or antimicrobial properties without modifying processing protocols (Meamari et al., 2021; Shomali & Mosleh, 2019).

Harvesting is typically performed at full flowering to maximize oil content, with shade-drying for approximately two weeks at 25 ± 2 °C to preserve volatile compounds before grinding (Nazari et al., 2024). Hydrodistillation using a Clevenger apparatus for ∼3 h is the standard extraction method. Although supercritical CO₂ extraction improves the retention of oxygenated monoterpene, it entails higher capital and operational costs. Chemical composition is routinely analyzed by GC-FID and GC–MS (HP-5 or HP-5MS columns, oven 60–240 °C at 3 °C/min, helium carrier gas, EI at 70 eV), with compound identification based on retention indices and spectral libraries. Methanolic extracts are evaluated for total phenolic content (Folin–Ciocalteu method) and antioxidant activity (DPPH assay) (Jiyanpour & Yavari, 2022; Pourhosseini et al., 2020).Bioactivity assessment involves in vitro antimicrobial assays against S. aureus, S. epidermidis, E. coli, and Candida albicans following CLSI protocols, as well as in vivo antiparasitic trials against Toxoplasma gondii in NMRI mice (Mahmoudvand et al., 2020; Shomali & Mosleh, 2019). The combination of well-characterized ecotypes, stable yields, and minimal infrastructure requirements for hydrodistillation supports the commercial-scale production of consistent, quality-controlled ZEO. Such attributes and properties, along with its ethnobotanical heritage and growing global interest, introduce ZEO as a substantial bio-based compound and valuable economic alternative with significant potential in food preservation (Kumar et al., 2023).

Chemical composition

Chemically, ZEO is distinguished by its high proportion of monoterpenoid phenolics, predominantly thymol and carvacrol, which drive its thyme-like aroma and bioactivity. GC–MS analysis of Fars province ecotypes identified thymol (34.41–54.35%) and carvacrol (5.35–15.34%), along with their biosynthetic precursors p-cymene (9.49–19.85%) and γ-terpinene (7.34–16.70%), and α-pinene (1.63–5.25%) as the main monoterpene hydrocarbons. Analyzing the EO from Kerman province exhibited thymol at 45.42%, carvacrol at 25.96%, and p-cymene at 10.65%, achieving a total identification of 98.81% under comparable analytical conditions (GC–MS, HP-5MS, EI 70 eV) (Pourhosseini et al., 2020; Rahimi et al., 2019).

Biosynthetically, geranyl diphosphate undergoes cyclization to γ-terpinene, which is aromatized to p-cymene and subsequently hydroxylated to produce thymol or rearranged to yield carvacrol. This pathway describes the consistent co-occurrence of p-cymene and γ-terpinene with the phenolic endpoints. (Frey et al., 2025). The thymol-to-carvacrol ratio varies considerably across regions and chemotypes, with some EOs dominated by thymol (e.g., ∼44–54%) and others by carvacrol (∼30–50%), constituting mainly 30–86% of the total oil composition jointly. Such variation signifies the impact of genotypic factors and environmental conditions—particularly temperature and altitude—on monoterpene cyclase and hydroxylase activity (Llorens-Molina et al., 2022). Higher-altitude, cooler sites may yield thymol-rich profiles, whereas lower-altitude, warmer sites lead to carvacrol-dominant ones, enabling targeted selection for specific sensory or antimicrobial applications (Khoshbakht et al., 2020).

ZEO also contains a diverse range of other terpenes and aromatic compounds, including β-myrcene, α-thujene, limonene, linalool, and sesquiterpenes, such as β-caryophyllene and its oxide. Representative compositional data include γ-terpinene (∼16.1%), p-cymene (∼9.1%), β-myrcene (∼6.5%), and α-thujene (∼3.7%) alongside thymol, or carvacrol (∼41.2%), thymol (∼27.4%), “O-isopropyltoluene” (∼4.5%), linalool (∼3.4%), and γ-terpinene (∼4.1%) (Abbasi et al., 2024).

Antioxidant and antibacterial properties

The antioxidant potential of ZEO is evidenced by its significant total phenolic content in methanolic extracts (approximately 235–302 mg GAE g−1 DW) and robust DPPH free-radical-scavenging capacity (half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) approximately 349–454 μg mL−1), suggesting effective radical quenching through hydrogen or electron donation (Niczad et al., 2019). The EO fraction is characterized by the dominance of thymol and carvacrol, which can potentially donate single electrons or hydrogen atoms to address cellular oxidative stress, form resonance-stabilized phenoxyl radicals, and potentially contributing to lipid-phase antioxidant protection in food systems (de Sousa et al., 2023). The phenolic compounds present in ZEO, along with its hydrophobic behavior, exert inhibitory effects on pathogenic microbial growth. The hydrophobic nature of EOs allows them to interact with and penetrate the microbial cell membrane via lipid components. As a result of such penetration, the microbial cell would be structurally damaged, which lethally heightens its membrane permeability (Osanloo et al., 2023). Assessing the antimicrobial activity according to National Committee for Clinical Laboratory Standards (NCCLS)/CLSI protocols against S. aureus, S. epidermidis, E. coli, and Candida albicans demonstrated its robust activity against all tested microorganisms. Differences in the proportions of thymol and carvacrol among ecotypes indicate that the source region influences functional potency. Thymol-rich EOs from higher altitudes and carvacrol-rich EOs from lower altitudes provide a versatile resource for targeting both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria (Khwaza & Aderibigbe, 2025; Pilevar et al., 2024). In vivo, the oral pre-administration of ZEO (0.2- or 0.4-mL kg1 daily for 14 days) to male NMRI mice prior to Toxoplasma gondii RH tachyzoite challenge resulted in extended survival (days 8–9 compared to day 5 in controls) and a reduction in peritoneal parasite burden, with no significant alterations in clinical chemistry or hematology. This protozoal model, although distinct from bacterial endpoints, exhibits systemic bioactivity and tolerability, thereby reinforcing ZEO's potential for translation as a food-relevant antimicrobial/antioxidant at doses significantly lower than those utilized in the animal study (Hossein et al., 2020; Mahmoudvand et al., 2020). Applications in the food industry

The ZEO's broad antimicrobial and antioxidant activities highlight its considerable potential as a natural preservative in food. Its functional versatility derives from the dominance of phenolic monoterpenes—primarily thymol and carvacrol—with relative proportions varying among chemotypes according to temperature-altitude gradients. This variability enables sourcing or blending thymol-rich (herbal, milder) or carvacrol-rich (more pungent) EOs, balancing the antimicrobial spectrum and sensory acceptability (Karimi, 2022). Elevated total phenolic content in methanolic extracts, combined with the EO's phenolic-monoterpene profile, underpins dual-function benefits in food systems: lipid and membrane stabilization via antioxidant activity and direct inhibition of spoilage and pathogenic microorganisms. These properties have been harnessed in numerous food industry applications aligned with the “clean label” movement (Hashemi et al., 2017; Shomali & Mosleh, 2019).

Significantly, ZEO-containing packaging systems have been developed for perishable products. Biodegradable matrices based on proteins (e.g., caseinate, gelatin), polysaccharides (e.g., chitosan, starch, carboxymethyl cellulose), and gums (e.g., κ-carrageenan, sage seed gum) have been enriched with ZEO to provide antimicrobial protection and oxidative stability in perishable and fresh products like meats, fruits, and vegetables (Mohammadi, Ahari, & Yousefi, 2023). Advanced preservation approaches integrate ZEO with other hurdles. Encapsulation in carbohydrate matrices or emulsification enhances the stability and dispersibility in high-moisture foods, while controlled atmosphere packaging (MAP)—both direct and plasma-treated gas configurations—has been shown to extend shelf life by delaying spoilage and microbial growth (Rezagholizade-shirvan et al., 2024). Such findings confirm that ZEO is an efficient, adaptable, and technically feasible natural preservative for diverse food systems. Its successful use at pilot scale underscores the importance of establishing matrix-specific efficacy and sensory thresholds through standardized microbiological testing, ensuring both product safety and consumer acceptance.

Safety

Toxicological assessments, along with the long-standing culinary and medicinal use in Southwest Asia, confirm the safety profile of ZEO. Traditionally consumed as an herbal tea, spice, or medicinal preparation with no significant reports of toxicity, ZEO benefits from an empirical record of safe human exposure (Aali et al., 2025; Chandrasekara & Shahidi, 2018). Additionally, regulatory frameworks further corroborate the safety of ZEO. Many thymol- and carvacrol-rich EOs, including thyme and oregano, are classified as “Generally Recognized as Safe” (GRAS) for edible purposes in the United States. Significantly, ZEO is included within these classifications, indicating its potential for safe use in food applications at controlled concentrations (Jackson-Davis et al., 2023; Yuseri et al., 2025). Experimental evidence reinforces this toxicological profile. A sub-acute oral study in male NMRI mice, administering ZEO at 0.2- or 0.4-mL kg−1 daily for 14 days, reported no mortality or significant alterations in liver enzymes (AST, ALT, ALP), renal indices (creatinine, blood urea nitrogen), bilirubin, or hematological parameters (RBC, WBC, platelets, hemoglobin, hematocrit) (Mahmoudvand et al., 2020). When prophylactically administered before Toxoplasma gondii challenge, the same dosing regimen significantly extended survival (mortality on days 8–9 versus day 5 in infected controls) and reduced peritoneal tachyzoite counts, demonstrating in vivo bioactivity at exposure levels without measurable toxicity. Chemical characterization revealed a phenolic monoterpene-rich profile —primarily thymol (45.42%), carvacrol (25.96%), and p-cymene (10.65%)—thymol (45.42%), carvacrol (25.96%), and p-cymene (10.65%)—consistent with compositions associated with antimicrobial and antioxidant functionality (Mahmoudvand et al., 2020). Typical inclusion rates in edible films/coatings are low, resulting in minimal food migration. At these levels, studies have reported no adverse effects in consumers or sensorial attributes, as long as the flavor intensity is properly managed. The EO's strong, herbal, and pungent notes naturally restrict its use; higher concentrations can impart overly intense flavors, while lower amounts achieve preservation goals without compromising taste. This sensory threshold ensures that ZEO intake remains well below toxicological limits, sustaining its general safety in food applications (Gammage & Marangoni, 2025).

Incorporation of ZEO into biopolymer-based food packaging systems

As a functional additive, ZEO can be incorporated into edible films/coatings, which are thin protective layers, typically measuring only a few millimeters in thickness, designed to preserve the food products and inhibit deterioration. While films are produced independently through well-established manufacturing methods (e.g., casting) before being applied to food products, coatings are formed directly on the product's surface using techniques such as dipping (Ribeiro et al., 2021; Sharma et al., 2021) (Fig. 2).Fig. 2The direct incorporation of ZEO into edible packaging and its impacts on the biopolymer-based films/coatings.Fig. 2

Direct and conventional methods for incorporating ZEO into biopolymeric films/coatings

Solvent casting method

The casting method is a widely employed technique for preparing biopolymer films in a three-stage process, commencing with the solubilization of the biopolymer or polymer mixture in a solvent. The ZEO can then be incorporated into the resulting homogenous solution. The achieved emulsion would then be cast, where it's poured into a designated mold or flat substrate to dry. During drying, the solvent evaporates, delivering a thin, cohesive polymer layer adhered to the mold. This cost-effective method requires no specialized equipment and operates at relatively lower temperatures, making it appropriate for most food-grade materials that are sensitive to heat during the process. However, it primarily yields simple geometries, such as sheets or tubular structures. Moreover, its lengthy drying phase, which should be controlled to promote intermolecular interactions among polymer chains and to ensure the formation of a uniform and well-organized film microstructure, makes it less suitable for large-scale industrial applications (Suhag et al., 2020).

Electrospinning

Electrospinning is a versatile fabrication technique that involves subjecting a film-forming solution to a high-intensity electric field to produce mats composed of nanofibers characterized by a high surface-to-volume ratio, porous morphology, and low weight. Instrumentally, the electrospinning mainly consists of a high-voltage power supply, a syringe pump for precise fluid delivery, and a collector plate. During the process, when the biopolymer/ZEO solution loaded into the syringe is subjected to an applied electrical potential, the liquid droplet at the needle tip deforms. Once the electrostatic stress exceeds the surface tension of the solution, a charged polymer jet is ejected. As this jet traverses the electric field, it undergoes extensive stretching and rapid solvent evaporation, leading to the formation of nanofibers that are deposited onto the collector. These nanofibers subsequently assemble into a coherent fibrous mat characterized by a more uniform diameter distribution and an enhanced surface-area-to-volume ratio. The fabrication of electrospun mats at ambient temperature mitigates the thermal deterioration of biopolymers and bioactive additives. The fibrous structure of these mats can enhance the stabilization and controlled release of ZEO, as well as heightened responsiveness to variations in temperature and moisture due to their elevated surface area. However, electrospun mats demonstrate limited mechanical strength, necessitating their integration onto a supportive substrate to enable practical application in food packaging (Bahrami et al., 2023; Wu et al., 2024).

Dip coating

The dip-coating method can effectively deposit functional coatings directly onto diverse substrates. Initially, the substrate is fully immersed in a coating formulation containing ZEO, formulated to achieve the desired film characteristics. During this process, the coating solution uniformly wets and deposits onto the substrate surface due to interfacial interactions and controlled withdrawal parameters. Subsequently, the solvent component within the deposited coating evaporates under predefined conditions, promoting consolidation of the coating matrix. This process results in the formation of a homogeneous, thin, and adherent protective layer on the substrate. Solvent removal can occur passively at ambient temperature or actively through controlled thermal treatment, ensuring the development of a solid and durable coating. While this cost-effective method enables uniform coatings of products with complex geometries, it is generally inappropriate for large substrates and coatings with high viscosity (Dubey & Dubey, 2020).

Spray coating

Spray coating is a widely employed and highly efficient technique for applying semi-permeable membranes directly onto surfaces. This process is initiated by atomizing the coating solution into minute droplets, which are then uniformly deposited across the targeted area to ensure complete coverage. The characteristics of the resultant membrane are closely dictated by a synergy of factors, including the fundamental properties of the polymeric solution, such as its viscosity, density, and surface tension; operational settings like the flow rate and air pressure; and system design elements, notably the nozzle geometry and spray angle. This method is highly favored due to its minimal solvent requirements, precise control over film thickness, and easy adaptability to large-scale applications (Ribeiro et al., 2021).

The impacts of ZEO on the film-forming biopolymers

The direct incorporation of ZEO into edible biopolymer films/coatings through the methods above can profoundly modify the mechanical, physical, and functional properties of the packaging systems. Typically, ZEO is integrated into the polymer matrix, such as polysaccharides and proteins, through direct dissolution or dispersion in the polymer solution to functionalize the resulting edible films. The interaction of ZEO with the polymeric matrix affects its thickness, tensile strength (TS), and elongation at break (EB), and, in some cases, improves the antioxidant and antimicrobial activities of the films/coatings (Sayadi et al., 2025) (Fig. 2).

Polysaccharides

Generally, the biodegradability, abundance, and biocompatibility of polysaccharides position them among the beneficial materials for food packaging. The direct incorporation of ZEO can modify the structural and functional properties of polysaccharide-based matrices. For instance, the ZEO integration into a sage seed gum (SSG) film can enhance its thickness and total solid content. This effect can be attributed to the entrapment of ZEO droplets within the polymeric network of the SSG, which subsequently leads to an increase in the film's overall thickness (Mohammadi, Yekta, et al., 2023b). A similar impact is observed in carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) films, in which ZEO inclusion can reduce both their TS and EB. This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the hydrophobic nature of the EO disrupting the hydrophilic CMC polymer matrix, fundamentally weakening its intermolecular cohesion. This structural weakening results from the accumulation of oil domains within the inter-polymeric spaces. This accumulation creates weak interactions between the polymer chains and the oil droplets. The reduced internal density lowers the tension capacity required for chain movement, facilitating the motion of polymer chains under stress. As a result, a noticeable decrease occurs in the film's overall mechanical properties (TS and EB) (Andacheh et al., 2020). The incorporation of ZEO into films/coatings primarily reduces the WVP and alters the Oxygen Transmission Rate (OTR). WVP reduction is attributed to ZEO increasing the film's overall hydrophobicity, thereby inhibiting the passage of water molecules through the polymer matrix. For OTR, low concentrations enhance the barrier property by a physical filling effect. In this impact, ZEO components occupy the free volume within the polymer structure, physically hindering O2 permeation. However, this barrier effect is highly dependent on concentration; low amounts are beneficial, while excessive levels can aggregate. The high concentrations, therefore, detrimentally increase both WVP and OTR by potentially lowering the film's surface energy and enhancing the solubility and absorption of diffusing gases (Mohammadi, Yekta, et al., 2023b). ZEO/micro chitosan-based films can diminish porosity and uniformize the dispersion of components. This structural effect is achieved by ZEO, facilitating a homogeneous dispersion within the polymer matrix. As a result, a more compact structure is formed that enhances the film's overall stability and functional properties (Ebadi et al., 2022).

Moreover, ZEO can significantly improve the antimicrobial and antioxidant properties of zedo gum and alginate-based coatings. This remarkable functional enhancement is aligned with the high concentration of phenolic oxygenated monoterpenes, such as thymol and carvacrol, within the ZEO composition. This composition delivers a substantial antimicrobial effect against key spoilage pathogens, including E. coli and S. aureus in Zedo Gum coatings, and Enterobacteriaceae in Alginate systems. Furthermore, the EO's antioxidant capability effectively delays the chemical deterioration of fresh products, thereby improving the shelf life of perishable goods (Saffari Samani et al., 2023b).

Proteins

Despite the appropriate film-forming capacity of proteins, they develop rigid networks due to their strong intermolecular interactions, resulting in films/coatings with insufficient mechanical and functional properties, which highlights the necessity of applying a plasticizer. Interestingly, ZEO can act as a plasticizer in protein-based packaging to decrease the TS and Young's modulus, which ultimately increases the film's flexibility. This plasticizing effect is fundamentally achieved by ZEO components disrupting the strong intermolecular hydrogen bonds within the protein matrix, thereby facilitating chain mobility. This mechanical shift is evident in films like zein and gliadin nanofibers, where the incorporation of ZEO leads to a reduction in the film's stiffness. Furthermore, the incorporation of ZEO into the zein matrix leads to an increase in the film's Water Solubility (WS). This unexpected effect is primarily because the EO creates new protein-polyphenol interactions. These interactions weaken the stabilizing forces within the cohesive protein network, which makes the overall structure more easily dissolved when it comes into contact with water (Bahrami et al., 2023; Rigueto et al., 2024). The inclusion of ZEO into protein-based systems, such as soy-gelatin films and gliadin nanofibers, can significantly enhance their thermal resistance and glass transition temperature. This strengthening effect is fundamentally attributed to the interactions of the EO's phenolic compounds with the protein backbone. This molecular engagement improves the structural integrity of the resultant materials and requires greater energy input for thermal decomposition (Bahrami et al., 2023; Raeisi et al., 2021).

Blended composites

ZEO has demonstrated the potential to functionalize composite films and blends, thereby improving their mechanical and physical properties. For instance, composites composed of polylactic acid (PLA) and tragacanth gum (TG) can be incorporated by ZEO to show more thickness and TS, as well as low WVP due to the hydrophobic nature of ZEO that interacts with the hydrophilic components of the matrix, improving barrier properties of the film through lengthening the water vapor diffusion pathway. Moreover, ZEO's carvacrol can impart packaging with antibacterial, antifungal, and antioxidant properties (Bargh et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2020). The presence of ZEO can affect the morphology, optical features, and barrier properties in polysaccharide-protein blended matrices. While this EO can improve the morphology and antimicrobial activity of the blends, its impact on barrier and optical behavior significantly depends on its distribution and the polymer compatibility. The hydrophobic nature of ZEO leads to a notable reduction in WS in blended packaging systems, such as pullulan/soy-based films. The presence of EO effectively minimizing the quantity of water molecules positioned between the polymer chains that are typically stabilized by hydrogen bonding. This action results in the film matrix being less accessible to water, thereby limiting dissolution and enhancing its water resistance. Additionally, this same hydrophobic characteristic of ZEO further tends to significantly decline the film's WVP, contributing to enhanced moisture barrier performance. (Bargh et al., 2025; Saffari Samani et al., 2023b). However, WVP can be increased in certain blends, such as chitosan/whey-based films, due to the structural defects induced in the polymer structure by the EO. This includes a negative influence on the intermolecular bonding, which subsequently generates free volume between the polymer chains. This negative effect is exacerbated by phase separation, a result of the inherent incompatibility between the EO and the matrix. These structural flaws ultimately create pathways for water molecules, significantly accelerating their transport across the composite film and consequently increasing its WVP. ZEO incorporation can result in a slight yellow hue in the blend and reduce its brightness and transparency, that is primarily attributed to their aggregation within the polymer matrix. These resulting clusters become sufficiently large to scatter and block light transmission, thereby decreasing the overall optical clarity of the film. (Gohargani et al., 2020). Furthermore, the presence of ZEO in blended composites, such as gelatin/nano-chitosan films, provides substantial antimicrobial performance against foodborne pathogens, like L. monocytogenes and Salmonella Typhimurium. This critical effect is achieved by the oil's high content of phenolic components, such as thymol and carvacrol, which increase the permeability of the microbial cell membrane, leading to the leakage of essential intracellular contents and, consequently, cell death (Hematizad et al., 2021b).

Other and indirect methods for incorporating ZEO into biopolymeric films/coatings

Nanoencapsulation and nanoemulsion

Integrating ZEO into the emulsions of very small droplets provides the nanoemulsions. Applying such EO-loaded nanoemulsions into active packaging systems can increase the ZEO efficacy. This increment stems from the improved dispersion of EO droplets into the biopolymer matrix that enhances the specific surface area of the active molecules (Almasi et al., 2021). This uniform distribution of hydrophobic ZEO imparts the packaging systems with an effective barrier against water vapor diffusion. In the realm of hydrocolloidal packaging, particularly in the case of SSG films, the incorporation of ZEO nanoemulsions fosters a dense network. This occurs through the interaction between the hydrophobic ZEO droplets and the hydrophilic domains of SSG. As a result of such an interaction, a robust barrier would be formed that significantly inhibits water vapor transfer. Significantly, the presence of lipidic components to decrease the hydrophilic/lipophilic ratio can lead to a lower moisture loss. This impact shows the significant role of such EOs to enhance the WVP of the enriched films/coatings (Erfanifar et al., 2023). Beyond moisture transfer, this colloidal delivery system also affects the resistance and TS of enriched packaging (Hosseini et al., 2025). Such a mechanical alteration occurs when ZEO-loaded nanoemulsion weakens the homogeneous structure and network cohesion of SSG films by substituting the polymer-EO interaction with intermolecular polymer chain interactions. Additionally, the polymer chain mobility would be increased by the presence of ZEO. The plasticizing feature of this EO results in increased flexibility and elasticity (Azadbakht et al., 2018; Li et al., 2018). Due to the EOs' biocompatibility, these compounds can be combined and co-encapsulated with other beneficial substances. This novel approach can enhance the synergetic effects of packaged products and improve their durability (Panahi & Mohsenzadeh, 2022). For instance, ZEO can be co-encapsulated with bacteriocins (e.g., nisin) to enrich biopolymeric matrices with an inhibitory effect on a wider array of microorganisms. A ZEO-nisin encapsulated mixture would be physically entrapped into a chitosan-based nanogel (Hosseini et al., 2024). Nisin and ZEO interact within the chitosan nanogel network through a combination of hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding. The hydrophobic surface of ZEO interacts with the nonpolar regions of nisin. On the other hand, the amino and hydroxyl groups of chitosan form hydrogen bonds with both compounds. This dual mode of interaction results in the formation of stable nanostructure matrices and cross-linked networks (Torres et al., 2020). Furthermore, this configuration facilitates the immobilization and controlled release of both bioactive compounds during storage. Such structural integration enhances the compatibility between the EO and the biopolymer, ultimately improving mechanical resistance and antimicrobial efficacy while reducing the volatility of ZEO (Zhang et al., 2023). Moreover, the antimicrobial activity of an alginate-based coating can be enhanced significantly through the addition of ZEO encapsulated within solid-lipid nanoparticles (SLNs). Both ZEO and SLNs encapsulated and compounded with alginate-based coatings improve the antimicrobial properties of active packaging systems (Laein et al., 2024). In this process, ZEO is dispersed within the molten lipid phase, followed sequentially by emulsification and solidification steps. As a result, stable SNLs are formed, which effectively encapsulate and protect the EO from oxidation and evaporation (Hosseini et al., 2024). ZEO-loaded SLNs interact with the alginate matrix through intermolecular interactions between the carboxyl and hydroxyl groups of alginate and the polar head groups of the lipid nanoparticles. This, for instance, hydrogen bonding and electrostatic attraction, causes a homogeneous distribution of the SLNs in the biopolymeric matrix. Therefore, a compound barrier of hydrophobic–hydrophilic would be created. The resulting alginate coatings demonstrate enhanced water vapor resistance and mechanical stability, and the controlled release of ZEO from the SLN–alginate system prolonged antimicrobial activity and spoilage in food products (Karimi-Khorrami et al., 2022; Li et al., 2021). The incorporation of ZEO nanoemulsions into starch-based matrices enhances the antimicrobial performance and barrier properties of biopolymeric films. In particular, the dispersion of ZEO nanodroplets within cornstarch matrices, especially when combined with cinnamaldehyde, results in synergistic antibacterial activity against L. monocytogenes (Abbasi et al., 2021). The enhanced antimicrobial performance is linked to the increased interfacial area of nanoemulsified ZEO. This increment facilitates more effective interaction with microbial cells, enabling the controlled and gradual release of active compounds (Forgi et al., 2025). Additionally, the incorporation of ZEO nanoemulsions into basil seed gum (BSG)-based films has demonstrated antimicrobial activity against B. cereus and E. coli. The hydrophobic nature of the dispersed nanodroplets provides effective surface resistance against moisture. Meanwhile, the flexibility, mechanical adaptability, and partial plasticization of the polymer chains are improved. These effects collectively enhance the functional and antimicrobial properties of the starch-based biopolymer films, making them well-suited for applications in active food packaging (Gahruie et al., 2017; Kong et al., 2022). Pickering emulsions

Pickering emulsions, stabilized by solid particles adsorbed at the oil–water interface, enhance the stability of dispersed droplets and represent an effective strategy for incorporating ZEO into polymer matrices (Zhao et al., 2022). The solid particles create a physical steric barrier that prevents droplet coalescence and improves the thermodynamic stability of the emulsion system under varying humidity and temperature conditions. The synergistic integration of these emulsions and bioactive substances forms continuous layers within the film matrix. Such dense layers can then promote the moisture barrier properties of the film and significantly reduce its WVP (Zhang et al., 2025; Zomorodian et al., 2023). Such an enhancement is attributed to the adsorption of solid particles, such as cellulose nanocrystals, chitosan nanoparticles, or starch granules, at the oil–water interface. The process forms an interfacial film encapsulating the ZEO droplets, creating an oil-particle matrix that effectively improves the film's barrier properties by restricting the mobility of the dispersed phase and reducing the diffusion pathways for water vapor molecules (Rigg et al., 2022). Additionally, hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions between the stabilizing particles and polymer chains contribute to the formation of a unique internal structure, which further enhances the mechanical integrity and deformation stability of the film (Phan et al., 2023). Notably, excessive incorporation of ZEO disrupts this structural balance. At high ZEO concentrations, phase separation induces droplet aggregation and microchannel formation. Such alterations can subsequently increase WVP and facilitate vapor transmission. This phenomenon arises from the insufficiency of interfacial coverage by solid particles to stabilize all droplets. Such a lack results in coalescence and the development of structural discontinuities within the film matrix. Thus, precise optimization of the ZEO-to-particle ratio is essential to maximize emulsion stability, achieve a densely packed structure, and enhance barrier performance. This strategy offers a controlled encapsulation mechanism that not only enhances the dispersion and stability of ZEO but also facilitates its gradual release. Hence, such factors enhance antimicrobial efficacy, moisture resistance, and mechanical stability of the film (Akhter et al., 2019). Chemical grafting/crosslinking

Along with the incorporation of inorganic or bioactive fillers, chemical crosslinking can also reinforce the polymer matrix through the formation of covalent and hydrogen bonds. These modifications can significantly enhance the film's structural integrity and mechanical strength.

The interactions between active functional groups of the polymer (e.g., hydroxyl and carboxyl groups) and crosslinking agents can result in a 3D network capable of resisting deformation to improve the tensile properties of the films. Notably, the addition of nanoparticles (e.g., TiO2) or bioactive compounds (e.g., rosemary extract) can further promote intermolecular interactions and strengthen interfacial adhesion within the network. For instance, using ZEO in SSG coatings combined with rosemary extract significantly increases the TS of the coating from 0.85 to 1.29 MPa (Hosseini et al., 2025). These improvements can also be observed in starch–pectin and BSG films containing microencapsulated or nanoemulsified forms of ZEO. These films demonstrate notable enhancement in both TS and elasticity. Such a behavior can be attributed to the homogeneous dispersion of nanodropets and the reinforcement of polymeric interactions (Sani et al., 2021). The intermolecular bonding and filler–polymer interactions contribute to the formation of a cohesive and compact structure, which restricts molecular mobility and increases TS and stability. Furthermore, a well-organized polymeric matrix can prevent excessive or heterogeneous dispersion, reducing localized stress levels (Guchait et al., 2022; Yadav et al., 2024).

Application of ZEO in food packaging films/coatings

Food packaging primarily aims to preserve the quality of food products, ensuring their safety during storage by protecting them against mechanical, chemical, and biological threats (Yang et al., 2025). As stated, among the various synthetic and natural substances used or studied to enhance packaging systems to confront such hazards, ZEO has demonstrated significant potential as a functional additive in food packaging, mainly due to its potent antimicrobial and antioxidant properties. ZEO can be incorporated directly or indirectly into a range of natural-based edible films/coatings, and significantly enhance their performance in extending the shelf-life of perishable food products (Chen et al., 2025; Mobininejad et al., 2021). The potent antimicrobial capability of ZEO, which makes this EO a promising option to be employed in packaging systems, is primarily rooted in the well-known hydrophobicity of EOs that enables their penetration into the bacterial membrane through its available lipid components. Such an interaction causes structural damage to the cell, increasing its membrane permeability and leading to the leakage of vital cellular contents of the microorganism (Langroodi et al., 2018; Osanloo et al., 2023). Hence, the lipophilic nature and phenolic compounds of ZEO, especially its carvacrol, deliver its inhibiting impact on the viability of pathogenic bacteria, such as E. coli, S. enteritidis, S. aureus, and B. cereus. Therefore, possessing such a capability turns ZEO-containing active packaging into a desirable choice for perishable products, such as meats. For instance, wrapping meat products in bio-based films enriched with ZEO can effectively reduce their microbial loads, including psychrotrophic and lactic acid bacteria, as well as Enterobacteriaceae, which is known as a hygienic index in meats, such as poultry (Hematizad et al., 2021a). In this regard, carvacrol and its acidic hydroxyl group, which is capable of forming hydrogen bonds, alter the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria morphologically, releasing the lipopolysaccharides, and consequently expand the permeability of the cytoplasmic membrane. Gram-negative bacteria exhibit lipophilic impermeability to such compounds, making this mechanism less effective. In contrast, Gram-positive bacteria present higher sensitivity to antibacterial compounds due to the absence of an outer lipophilic membrane (Kachur & Suntres, 2020). Beyond delivering antibacterial active ingredients, the barrier properties of this packaging also suppress fungal growth. Such suppression arises from absorbing the moisture resulting from the respiration of the packaged fruits or vegetables, restricting the fungi and yeast's access to their necessary moisture (Hematizad et al., 2021a; Khajeh et al., 2024; Moradinezhad et al., 2023). However, such a potent antibacterial effect may also impact the probiotic bacteria. For instance, ZEO-enriched films/coatings can not only inhibit harmful pathogens but also beneficial Lactobacillus species, including L. acidophilus, L. rhamnosus, L. plantarum, and L. casei. This non-selective antimicrobial activity, as a notable limitation, highlights the importance of using such packaging primarily for non-probiotic products, confirming the significance of using certain concentrations for intended application (Mehdizadeh et al., 2018).

In addition, the ZEO-containing films/coatings can act as antioxidants to prevent or postpone the lipid oxidation, which can crucially decline the quality and organoleptic properties of fresh products through several factors, including exposure to light and oxygen (Mehdizadeh & Langroodi, 2019). Cellular metabolism regularly contains the formation of free radicals or reactive oxygen intermediates, whereas the overproduction of such compounds—including superoxide and hydroxyl radicals, as well as hydrogen peroxide— can damage the cell and cause its malfunction (Gholami-Ahangaran et al., 2022). The antioxidation impact of ZEO-containing films/coatings stems from the radical-scavenging activity of phenolic compounds. Thymol, in particular, can donate electrons or hydrogen atoms to neutralize free radicals and reduce lipid oxidation, as indicated by the lower peroxide value (PV). Therefore, such antioxidant films/coatings with a combined inhibitory effect on psychrophilic and mesophilic bacteria (that oxidize saturated fatty acids) can effectively preserve lipid-rich products, like meats (Ahmadi-Dastgerdi et al., 2022; Amiri et al., 2019; Domínguez et al., 2019; Mohammadpourfard et al., 2021). For instance, coating chicken meat with alginate nanoparticles containing ZEO can mitigate the endogenous alkalinizing reactions and sufficiently inhibit the microbial reproductivity (Ranjbar et al., 2025). As a result, enzymatic and bacterial autolytic and proteolytic activities are halted, preventing the subsequent uncontrolled pH rise caused by the formation of basic nitrogenous substances, such as ammonia and biogenic amine. Consequently, in addition to pH, ZEO can also affect the total volatile basic nitrogen (TVBN), which represents the level of volatile amines in the product due to the available microbial viability and activities (Baek et al., 2021; Sayyari et al., 2021).

ZEO can also confront the enzymatic alterations in agricultural products. Interestingly, the antioxidant activity of ZEO-based films/coatings depends on the packaged product composition. For instance, fresh-cut products with inherent antioxidant compounds exhibit varied rates of phenolic compound formation, due to their damaged tissue and stress, which can elevate phenolic accumulation through the phenylpropanoid pathway (Hashemi et al., 2021; Khajeh et al., 2024). Therefore, coating such phenolic-rich products prone to oxidation with ZEO-containing packaging can confront the decline in the antioxidant activity of the product. Post-harvest enzymatic browning is a major issue in many crops, primarily caused by polyphenol oxidase (PPO). Such an undesirable enzymatic reaction occurs when phenolic compounds are exposed to oxygen due to membrane damage from tissue injury or textural changes. The activity of this enzyme eventually decreases the total phenolic content (TPC) of the product, reducing the quality of the stored products. Hence, ZEO can enhance phenylalanine ammonia lyase activity, a key enzyme in biosynthesis and accumulation of phenols, and increase the production of phenolic compounds (Hashemi et al., 2021; Zhang, 2023). For instance, incorporating ZEO into zedo gum-based packaging can preserve the coated potato strips by controlling the enzymatic browning pathway and limiting the PPO activity (Khajeh et al., 2024). The retained antioxidant activity is primarily due to the absence of oxygen, which restricts the enzymatic oxidation of phenolic compounds. This effect results from the packaging's barrier properties that reduce oxygen availability and respiration rates, thereby decreasing the enzymatic oxidation of phenolics (Dong & Wang, 2018; Qiao et al., 2021). Such barrier properties can also hinder the weight loss, which is critical in fresh-cut products (especially fruits) due to their higher surface-to-volume ratio that promotes the moisture release (Rad et al., 2016a). Thus, the ZEO-containing films/coatings retain the textural firmness and increase the product acceptance by preventing such release (Babapour et al., 2022; Basaglia et al., 2021). Such a firmness retention and shelf-life extension can also be observed in button mushrooms coated with ZEO-containing tragacanth gum (Nasiri et al., 2017). Generally, mushrooms soften due to reduced cellular rigidity during aging and maturation. The presence of microorganisms (e.g., Pseudomonas species) also accelerates textural degradation by degrading the intracellular matrix and diminishing the central vacuole, contributing to cell collapse. Therefore, such an active coating can delay ripening by mitigating contamination and respiration rate through hindering microbe-driven softening and confronting the enzymatic degradation of cell walls (Luo et al., 2024). Practically, however, the described effects of ZEO require its usage at relatively high concentrations, which can adversely affect the sensory attributes of the packaged product due to the intense aroma and flavor profile of this EO (Raeisi et al., 2015). Importantly, encapsulating these functional compounds within appropriate bio-based and biocompatible polymers shows a significant potential to address this substantial issue by improving their stability and efficiency. However, the encapsulation technique would be selected through several factors, including the film-forming material properties (Aydogdu et al., 2019; Rasteh et al., 2024). For instance, incorporating ZEO into polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) fibers via electrospinning produces a core-shell structure that enables controlled release of ZEO within the PVA-based film matrix. This extends the antifungal activity of ZEO, considerably inhibiting the growth of fungi and yeasts, including Aspergillus fumigatus and Candida albicans, positively affecting the fruit's shelf life (Wen et al., 2017). Also, nanoemulsion, as a nanoencapsulated system, has shown considerable capability to increase EOs application through delivering a wide dispersibility of such ingredients and controlling their release, especially for ZEO in products with sensitive sensorial properties (Maurya et al., 2021; Taghizadeh et al., 2025). For instance, coating chicken meat with a corn starch-based packaging containing ZEO nanoemulsion can enhance the antimicrobial efficacy, confronting major pathogens, particularly L. monocytogenes (Abbasi et al., 2021). The compatibility of ZEO with other bioactive compounds enables synergistic effects with agents such as cinnamaldehyde. This aromatic aldehyde can disrupt glucose metabolism and energy production. Such a synergy promotes the preservative effectiveness of the resulting films/coatings for perishable products (Wang et al., 2025). However, it should be noted that the concentration of such other compounds is also critical in the ZEO function. For instance, the efficacy of ZEO in a chitosan-based packaging enriched with sumac extract depends on the sumac extract concentration. Interestingly, at higher levels, sumac extract can reduce the functionality of ZEO. Sumac extract itself is a natural antimicrobial and antioxidant agent rich in flavonoids and phenolic compounds (e.g., tannins and gallic acid) (Farazandehnia et al., 2024; Langroodi et al., 2018). Overall, ZEO can enrich edible packaging systems mainly by its significant antimicrobial and antioxidant properties. Nevertheless, the application of this EO can be limited due to its dependence on the concentration used and the core film/coating matrix to deliver the favorable bioactive function. Therefore, in addition to applying novel and practical methods, the concentration and the materials used to form the film/coating should be carefully selected to achieve the desirable functional application of such a beneficial compound in packaging perishable food products, such as fruits and meats.

Challenges and prospects

Despite its multiple promising and beneficial aspects, incorporating ZEO into edible packaging systems presents several technological challenges. For instance, the extraction and isolation of bio-based compounds often require costly procedures and may also feature batch-to-batch quality fluctuations. The possible undesirable organoleptic properties of biopolymers due to their natural sources can also diminish the acceptability and commercialization of the products (Gupta et al., 2024). Moreover, while employing methods such as encapsulation techniques addresses the controlled release and stability of EOs, these approaches can impose relatively high costs at an industrial scale. Many of the wall materials and required agents may not be compatible with the ZEO and the substrate matrix (Sousa et al., 2022). Future studies should therefore prioritize the translation of these films/coatings from laboratory-scale development to industrial production and commercial application, contributing to the reduction of plastic waste and its consequential environmental impact.

Conclusion

ZEO-enriched edible packaging represents a multifunctional and biodegradable alternative to conventional petroleum-based materials, advancing sustainable active packaging systems through the use of bio-based additives. The incorporation of ZEO significantly enhances the mechanical and barrier properties, antioxidant capacity, and antimicrobial activity of biopolymer-based films/coatings derived from polysaccharides, proteins, and biocomposites. Moreover, the biocompatibility of this EO enables it to act synergistically with other active components present in the packaging matrix. Technically, ZEO can be integrated into these matrices via conventional methods (e.g., solution dispersion) as well as advanced approaches (e.g., nanoemulsification), which improve its stability and release kinetics. The functional benefits of ZEO are primarily attributed to its phenolic terpenoids, such as carvacrol and thymol. Such compounds can effectively inhibit chemical degradation and microbial decay. Thus, ZEO-enriched functional films/coatings are promising substitutes for preserving nutritional value and extending the durability of perishable food products, such as meats and fruits. However, the limited availability of empirical evidence constrains the generalization of this review. This lack of data negatively impacts the successful industrial implementation and consumer adoption. The gap between the laboratory scale and industrial setting requires further interdisciplinary research to address health considerations, cost-effectiveness, and scalable processing.
