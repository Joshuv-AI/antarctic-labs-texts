Mental healthcare act and electroconvulsive therapy: Making a case for review
Authors: Jagadisha Thirthalli, Deepak S. Ghadigaonkar, B N Gangadhar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Mental Healthcare Act of 2017 has introduced some restraints on the practice of electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) in India. It has prohibited the administration of unmodified ECT and ECT to minors without permission from the mental health review board. While intended as safeguards for the patients, blanket application of these restraints has practical concerns. ECT is known to be one of the most effective and rapidly acting treatments in some psychiatric emergencies. The unavailability of anesthesiologists or required infrastructure may limit access to modified ECT in resource-poor settings. In such situations, the patient may consent to the unmodified ECT. If the patient lacks the capacity to provide consent, the nominated representative may provide it. Such intervention can be life-saving at times and mitigate complications. This article makes the case for reconsidering the current legislature. The unmodified ECT may be considered in certain exceptional situations. Relevant authorities should consider amending the Mental Healthcare Act of 2017 in this regard.

Electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) has remained a potent and safe intervention in the management of severe mental disorders for more than 80 years. It was introduced much before the introduction of effective psychopharmacological medicines. The benefits of the latter overshadowed the effects of ECT seen earlier. The application of electricity to live humans, though as a therapeutic intervention, was likened to the then prevailing inhuman torture of war criminals and Holocaust victims. This stigmatized ECT. ECT had come in for therapeutic use before the advent of anesthetic modification. The dramatic visuals of a convulsion in the recipient during the ECT procedure, therefore, only added to the stigma. The adverse effects of the ECT were viewed through this convex lens of negative image and stigma. The adverse effects were feared to be irreversible. Research has confirmed the benign and transient nature of these adverse effects.[1] The efficacy of ECT was also doubted in the light of double blind randomized controlled trials that the medicines were subjected to. ECT, therefore, was examined with the best efforts using this gold standard design in the latter half of the last century. These studies only proved the therapeutic effects of ECT in a range of severe mental disorders: depression, mania, and schizophrenia. Professional bodies endorsed this in consensus conferences. This led to the development of clinical practice guidelines on ECT published version after version by professional bodies of different countries.[234]

Being viewed with the same prejudicial lenses of stigma and feared adverse effects brought some dividends. Researchers advanced several findings that helped refine ECT progressively. The introduction of anesthetic modification in the ECT procedure was the first one. The stimulus dose was the next to be refined, along with the development of safe electrical stimulus instruments. The role of the seizure for the therapeutic effect of ECT was confirmed in experimental research. Researchers also examined the potential mechanisms in the therapeutic action of ECT. Yet the endorsement of the use of ECT prefixed some caution and conditions.[234] ECT is commonly recommended when the conventional medicines fail and must be used along with the ongoing medical treatment. It is also recommended when there is a need for rapid improvement in the context of harm to self or others.

The prejudices and concerns also influenced the lawmakers. Some have banned the use of ECT (e.g., in Slovenia[5]). Others imposed certain mandatory conditions for the use of ECT. The recent one is from our own country, the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 (MHCA). The first condition is that ECT be used only with anesthetic modification. The second condition is that ECT should be avoided in children and adolescents younger than 18 years. In this age group, ECT should be used only after obtaining prior permission from the Mental Health Review Board. The third condition is that ECT cannot be administered as an emergency treatment. It is pertinent to re-examine these conditions in the light of accessibility, cost, and individual choices. It may be noted that the MHCA 2017 is heavily in favor of respecting human rights and freedom of choice of treatment and aftercare for the affected individual.

In this regard, the condition provided in the MHCA mandating the use of anesthesia can be a serious concern. The sight of the dramatic convulsion during ECT can be disturbing to some. Anesthetic modification, along with muscular paralysis, masks this convulsion and hence the sight is less disturbing. The individual having the convulsion will not experience the same as the convulsion itself follows unconsciousness. Yet, the intention of anesthesia is to mitigate the discomfort experienced by the patient during the ECT, the shock of electrical stimulus and the convulsion. To date, however, there have been no reports of patients complaining of the discomfort of being aware of the electrical shock or experiencing convulsions during the ECT procedure. The containment of the muscular contractions during the convulsion has other benefits. Frail persons and younger people with strong muscles can be at risk of fractures. The fracture, mostly in the body of the vertebral bone, is neither common nor symptomatic when it occurs.[6] There are statements by patients who fear the injection (of the anesthesia) more than the ECT itself. Although a few older studies have directly compared modified and unmodified ECT, contemporary head-to-head evidence comparing their therapeutic efficacy and adverse-effect profiles remains limited.[78] The advantage of modified ECT is therefore not definitively supported. However, modified ECT enjoys circumstantial preference. There are also authors who have endorsed the role of unmodified ECT, including professional bodies.[9] Anesthesia for ECT demands the participation by one more specialist, the anesthesiologist. It also mandates several medical instruments including the Boyle’s apparatus and other physiological monitoring. Therefore, the use of ECT becomes limited to some advanced or higher-level centers. It is known that there is a scarcity of anesthesiologists in the country. This is the reason that the regulatory authority, the then Medical Council of India, relaxed conditions to help admit more students in anesthesia for postgraduate training. Unavailability of anesthetists has been one of the concerns against shifting to modified ECT. Therefore, modified ECT limits accessibility.[10] Additional professional fees, as well as additional medical instrumentation, add to the cost of ECT, thus making it less affordable to some.

There is hence a case for retaining unmodified ECT on the following grounds. It is as effective as the modified ECT. There are no serious concerns about the lasting adverse effects. It is accessible and more affordable. It is not known how extensive the consultations with stakeholders were, both the patients and psychiatrists, when these conditions on the use of ECT were introduced. Therefore, the condition in the MHCA forbidding unmodified ECT can be reconsidered. It should be noted that no patient will receive ECT without their own consent or consent from their nominated representatives. Therefore, the consent procedure should include providing objective information on the pros and cons of both ECT procedures. The patient should have an informed choice of accepting or consenting to the anesthetic modification. The patient may weigh the factors of accessibility and affordability against any other concerns about unmodified ECT. Such an informed choice made by the individuals or their nominated representatives to receive ECT should be respected. This also supports the ethos and the spirit of the MHCA 2017.

Thus, given the available scientific data, unmodified ECT deserves to be retained for clinical use in patients with severe mental disorders. However, the choice of modified or unmodified ECT should be the left to the patient. The treating doctor should retain the right of refusal to administer unmodified ECT, if the patient prefers the same.

In summary, modified ECT is the default procedure to be commonly mandated. However, ECT in uncommon situations[9] and in situations when a patient consents or even prefers the unmodified ECT, the same should be allowed instead of totally forbidding it. If the choice for patients is no ECT vs unmodified ECT and they prefer the latter, the choice must be honored. However, if the clinician or a clinic’s policy to use only modified ECT, the same should be explicitly disclosed.
