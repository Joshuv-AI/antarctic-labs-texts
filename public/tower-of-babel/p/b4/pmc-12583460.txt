AI-powered knowledge organization: a next-generation approach to library classification using DeepSeek-R1
Authors: Na Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the advancement of information technology, libraries have shifted from traditional physical services to an integrated “offline + online” model, becoming digital hubs in the national public cultural service system. However, current book classification still relies primarily on manual efforts, which suffers from inefficiency and inconsistent standards, making it difficult to meet the growing demand for processing massive volumes of books. Leveraging the latest developments in artificial intelligence, this paper proposes an automatic book classification algorithm based on the DeepSeek-R1-Distill model to improve classification accuracy and efficiency. Experimental results demonstrate that the algorithm achieves an average F1-score of over 87% in a 21-category Chinese book classification task, validating its effectiveness. Future work could explore the integration of more advanced large language models and domain-adaptive pre-training strategies to further advance classification capabilities. This research contributes a novel technical pathway toward intelligent book classification and underscores the significant potential of large language models in library and information science, offering both theoretical insights and practical value for the evolution of next-generation knowledge organization systems.Keywords: Book classification, DeepSeek-R1-Distill model, Large language models, Domain-adaptive pre-trainingSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Reading transcends mere knowledge acquisition, serving as a vital pathway for developing systematic thinking, independent judgment, and metacognitive awareness. As an intellectual practice, it enables individuals to overcome spatiotemporal constraints and engage deeply with ideas. In an era of pervasive uncertainty, reading provides epistemological orientation, fostering intellectual resilience, broadened perspectives, and cognitive composure. Given these benefits, nationwide reading initiatives have become foundational to national cultural development, civic literacy, and sustainable innovation. Crucially, reading and innovation are intrinsically linked–reading cultivates the cognitive processes underlying creativity, including knowledge synthesis, problem identification, and inspiration generation1.

The digital transformation of public cultural infrastructure, as outlined in China’s 14th Five-Year Plan and Long-Range Objectives Through 2035, has positioned libraries at the forefront of technological innovation. Since the 1990s, Chinese libraries have evolved from traditional in-person services to an integrated online-to-offline (O2O) model, offering multi-platform accessibility (e.g., PCs, mobile devices, smart TVs) and diversified services such as digital retrieval systems, webinars, and virtual exhibitions. By leveraging social media (WeChat, Weibo), libraries have dismantled barriers to resource dissemination, significantly expanding their digital reach. By 2019, China’s library network included over 3,000 county-level libraries, with digital repositories exceeding 21,000 TB (including 858 million electronic documents) and annual web visits surpassing 2.1 billion2. This transformation has solidified libraries’ role as digital hubs in national public cultural services. The Chinese Library Classification (CLC), established in 1975 and revised in 20103, remains the dominant bibliographic standard in China.

Currently, library book classification primarily relies on manual categorization. This approach requires professionals to review and classify each book individually, making the process tedious and time-consuming, especially when handling a large volume of books. The efficiency of manual labeling is far lower than the rate at which information grows. Additionally, differences in understanding among annotators can lead to inconsistent classification standards, resulting in inconsistent labeling outcomes and subjective biases, which undermine the accuracy and reliability of the data. With the rapid increase in the number of books, the workload of manual classification has expanded dramatically, posing a significant challenge to library management. The book classification task categorizes books based on their textual field information and is a specific application of text classification technology. Its core lies in analyzing the semantic content of the text to allocate the most appropriate category label to the input data. Since the original text is unstructured data, text representation techniques are needed to transform it into structured features that can be processed and computed by computers4.

This review covers the development of text classification techniques, starting from traditional representation learning models to the current use of pre-trained NLP models. Throughout this process, the expressive power of these methods has shown a significant upward trend. Traditional approaches relied on statistical text representation methods including Bag-of-Words (BoW)5, TF-IDF6, Chi-square feature selection7 and Word2Vec embeddings8. These methods, combined with conventional machine learning algorithms like Support Vector Machines, achieved moderate success but faced significant limitations in feature engineering requirements and domain adaptability9. Unlike traditional machine learning methods, the current mainstream pre-training NLP models are mainly divided into two categories: one is the feature-based approach represented by ELMo10, which transfers knowledge by extracting fixed language feature vectors; the other is the fine-tuning approach represented by BERT11, which adapts to downstream tasks by adding task-specific classification layers on top of the model and jointly optimizing the entire model’s parameters.

Among them, a series of deep learning networks have also been applied to enhance the model’s expressive capabilities. It can use layer-by-layer nonlinear transformations to gradually abstract data into higher-level feature representations, thereby demonstrating excellent performance in complex tasks. Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) introduced sequential processing capabilities12, while Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) excelled at capturing local text patterns13. The Transformer model14 established new benchmarks through self-attention mechanisms and bidirectional context modeling. Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) further enhanced classification by modeling document relationships through graph structures15.

Despite these advancements, several challenges remain in effectively adapting these technologies to library classification tasks: (i) the limited representational capacity of conventional text encoding methods and (ii) earlier NLP pre-trained models (ELMo and BERT) often suffer from a lack of broad, generalizable knowledge features. In recent years, researchers have found that by increasing the number of parameters and the amount of data used in pre-training language models, large language models (LLMs) can achieve significant performance improvements while demonstrating special capabilities that smaller models do not possess, such as contextual learning ability and step-by-step reasoning. Among these, DeepSeek has demonstrated exceptional natural language processing capabilities and has become one of the most popular large models among Chinese users16. Therefore, we propose a book classification algorithm based on a large pre-trained model (DeepSeek-R1-Distill), introducing a new technical approach to book classification, which highlights the significant potential of LLMs in library and information science.

Methods

As illustrated in Fig. 1, this study proposes a DeepSeek-R1-Distill-based multimodal feature fusion model that includes three steps: Embedding Encoding Layer, Multi-granularity Feature Extraction Module, and Feature Fusion And Integrated Classification Unit. Among these, the system architecture’s Multi-granularity Feature Extraction Module comprises two key components: Local Semantic Perception Module and Global Dependency Modeling Module.

The Chinese Book Classification Algorithm Based on DeepSeek-R1-Distill follows a three-step framework: Step 1 (Data Processing): Book metadata (title, author, publisher, abstract) is preprocessed via word segmentation, stop-word removal, and vectorization. Step 2 (Semantic Representation Learning): The vectors are enhanced by a High-level Semantic Module and then passed into two complementary components: (i) Local Semantic Perception Module (convolution, activation, pooling) for local feature extraction, yielding ; (ii) Global Dependency Modeling Module (position encoding, multi-head attention, feed-forward networks) for global context modeling, yielding . Step 3 (Ensemble Learning): Local and global embeddings are fused into and classified via a linear-softmax layer to produce final category predictions.

Embedding encoding

First, data preprocessing was performed by extracting key bibliographic metadata for each book, including title, author, publisher, keywords, and abstract. Non-informative elements (e.g., stopwords like “the” and “Ah”, punctuation marks such as “...”, and special characters like “/ &”) that contribute minimally to textual analysis were removed. Chinese text segmentation was then accurately executed using the Jieba tokenizer, followed by frequency-based filtering to eliminate low-frequency noise words that could distort analytical results.

DeepSeek currently stands among the performant open-source AI models, demonstrating superior capabilities across multiple benchmark evaluations. As an inference-optimized model, its architecture is specifically designed to enhance logical reasoning capabilities. Specifically, R1 employs reinforcement learning (RL) techniques to optimize large-scale training datasets, enabling efficient processing of complex reasoning tasks. Unlike conventional supervised fine-tuning (SFT), R1 eliminates this step and instead adopts the more advanced GRPO algorithm, which enhances reasoning capabilities through self-evolution mechanisms (e.g., reflection and long-chain reasoning). Furthermore, R1 incorporates a dynamic gating mechanism to optimize expert routing, allowing more precise activation of relevant experts during reasoning tasks and thereby improving computational efficiency.

The DeepSeek-R1-Distill model is fine-tuned based on open-source models such as the Qwen 2.5 and Llama 3 series, and further optimized using inference data generated by DeepSeek-R1. It covers multiple scales ranging from 1.5 billion to 70 billion parameters. Evaluation results show that the distilled small-scale dense models demonstrate excellent performance across various benchmark tests. Building on this progress, we constructed a text semantic encoder using the DeepSeek-R1-Distill 8B model and implemented localized deployment through the Ollama framework. During the embedding encoding process, this model can map book text information into a low-dimensional semantic space, generating highly discriminative dense vector representations. These vectors not only accurately capture the semantic information of the text but also effectively represent the semantic relationships between texts, thereby laying a solid semantic foundation for subsequent text classification tasks.

Multi-granularity feature extraction

The feature extraction framework initially employs a high-order semantic enhancement module to derive deep semantic representations of the input text. Subsequently, a parallel architecture integrating both local semantic perception and global dependency modeling modules is adopted to perform hierarchical feature extraction from micro- and macro-perspectives, respectively. This dual-path collaborative mechanism enables simultaneous capture of fine-grained semantic patterns while effectively modeling long-range contextual dependencies within the textual data.

High-order semantic enhancement module

This step adopts a fully connected neural network architecture to hierarchically capture high-order semantic representations of bibliographic texts. Specifically, a cascaded LayerNorm-ReLU-Dropout processing unit is implemented between the input and hidden layers: layer normalization is first applied to adapt the data distribution, followed by nonlinear transformation via ReLU activation, and finally, dropout regularization is employed to enhance the discriminative power of the feature space.

Results

Data description and experimental environment

In this study, the dataset was sourced from Chinese book resources on the Duxiu academic platform18. Considering that Class Z (General Books) primarily includes special publication types such as dictionaries, encyclopedias, and yearbooks, its sample size is relatively limited and suffers from frequent field-missing issues. Therefore, we ultimately selected 21 major book categories (A–X), covering complete metadata for 132,803 books, including key fields such as book titles, subject terms, abstracts, and Chinese Library Classification (CLC) codes (as shown in Fig. 2).

To ensure the reliability and reproducibility of experimental results, this study employs a stratified sampling strategy for dataset partitioning. Specifically, the StratifiedShuffleSplit method was utilized to divide the dataset into training, validation, and test sets at a ratio of 0.8:0.1:0.1, respectively. This approach effectively maintains consistent class distribution across all data subsets compared to the original dataset, thereby preventing potential class bias issues that may arise from random partitioning.

This study employs Adam as the optimizer and cross-entropy loss as the objective function, with dynamic learning rate adjustment implemented through the ReduceLROnPlateau strategy. All experiments were conducted on a workstation equipped with an Intel® Xeon(R) Gold 6248R CPU@3.00GHz48 processor and NVIDIA Corporation GA102GL[RTX A6000] graphics cards, running on the PyTorch framework under Ubuntu 22.04.6 LTS. The model was trained from scratch for 10 epochs with an initial learning rate of and a batch size of 256, incorporating early stopping. The complete training process required approximately 30 minutes. Four neural network architectures were selected as baseline models: LSTM19, TextCNN, DPCNN20, and Transformer21. The specific configurations for each model are detailed below:

LSTM

The Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network represents an optimized architecture derived from conventional recurrent neural networks, specifically designed to address the vanishing and exploding gradient problems inherent in processing lengthy sequences. This model’s fundamental innovation lies in its gating mechanism, comprising input gates, forget gates, and output gates. Through this selective information retention and discarding system, the architecture achieves substantial enhancement in modeling long-range dependencies. LSTM facilitates the transmission of crucial information via cell states, enabling effective learning of long-term temporal relationships, making it particularly suitable for sequential data modeling applications including machine translation, speech recognition, and financial forecasting. Despite demonstrating superior performance in sequence modeling tasks, LSTM’s serial computation paradigm results in comparatively slower training speeds and remains constrained in processing extremely long sequences.

TextCNN

TextCNN represents a seminal application of convolutional neural networks to text classification tasks, fundamentally employing multi-scale convolutional kernels to extract local textual features. Departing from conventional fully-connected neural architectures, the model implements a four-layer structure consisting of sequential input, convolutional, pooling, and fully-connected layers. The input layer transforms lexical items into low-dimensional vector representations through embedding techniques such as Word2Vec, TF-IDF, or GloVe. The subsequent convolutional layer utilizes kernels of fixed width (corresponding to word vector dimensionality) yet variable length to capture n-gram features across multiple granularities via a sliding-window mechanism. The pooling layer performs dimensionality reduction while preserving critical features through max-pooling operations, while the final fully-connected layer synthesizes global information for classification. By adapting image processing convolution operations to textual data through localized connectivity and parameter sharing, the model achieves effective local semantic feature extraction alongside substantial parameter efficiency, demonstrating exceptional performance and computational efficacy in text classification applications.

DPCNN

The Deep Pyramid Convolutional Neural Network (DPCNN) represents an efficient architecture specifically designed for text modeling, whose core innovation lies in its hierarchical and progressive approach to capturing textual features. The model initially employs a text region embedding layer to transform traditional word-level representations into semantic units covering multi-word fragments, effectively enhancing local context modeling capabilities. The architecture features a distinctive pyramid structure that alternately stacks convolutional blocks (each containing two convolutional layers with pre-activated residual connections) and stride-2 max-pooling layers. This design maintains a constant number of feature maps while progressively expanding the receptive field, thereby establishing long-range semantic relationships in a computationally efficient manner. Notably, the structure achieves two critical advantages: (i) computational complexity remains at a constant level through gradual downsampling, and (ii) residual connections ensure stable training of deep networks. Furthermore, the model incorporates unsupervised pre-trained embeddings (e.g., Word2Vec, TF-IDF, GloVe) to further improve feature extraction accuracy. DPCNN’s technical combination achieves an optimal balance between global semantic understanding and computational efficiency in text classification tasks, as demonstrated by its performance benchmarks.

Transformer

The Transformer architecture represents a groundbreaking advancement in sequence modeling through its innovative self-attention mechanism. Departing fundamentally from recurrent architectures like RNNs and LSTMs, this model eschews sequential processing entirely, instead leveraging parallelized attention mechanisms to capture global dependencies with significantly enhanced computational efficiency. The architecture’s key innovations comprise three principal components: (i) multi-head attention mechanisms that concurrently model semantic relationships across disparate positional contexts, substantially improving feature representation; (ii) positional encoding schemes that compensate for the absence of inherent recurrence by explicitly encoding sequential order information; and (iii) position-wise feed-forward networks that apply nonlinear transformations to augment the model’s representational capacity. These architectural breakthroughs have established the Transformer as a foundational framework in both natural language processing and computer vision, demonstrating unprecedented performance in complex tasks including machine translation and generative text modeling.

Evaluation metrics

For text multi-class classification tasks, we employ a comprehensive evaluation metric system that assesses model performance from multiple perspectives to ensure objective assessment. The confusion matrix (also referred to as an error matrix or contingency table) serves as a fundamental tool for evaluating classification model performance (as shown in Table 1). The matrix comprises four key components: True Positives (TP), denoting instances where samples belonging to the positive class are correctly predicted as positive; False Negatives (FN), representing positive-class samples incorrectly predicted as negative; False Positives (FP), indicating negative-class samples erroneously predicted as positive; and True Negatives (TN), corresponding to negative-class samples accurately predicted as negative. This matrix not only provides intuitive visualization of binary classification outcomes but can also be extended to multi-class scenarios through row and column expansion. Diagonal elements quantify correct classifications per category, while off-diagonal elements reveal misclassification patterns between categories.

Precision

Precision, also referred to as positive predictive value, quantifies the proportion of true positive instances among all samples predicted as positive by the model. Generally, higher precision values indicate lower false positive rates and consequently superior classification performance.

Recall

Recall, alternatively termed sensitivity or true positive rate, measures a model’s capability to identify actual positive instances from all truly positive samples in the dataset. Higher recall values generally indicate fewer false negative occurrences, reflecting more comprehensive detection performance in classification tasks.

F1-score

The F1-score serves as a composite evaluation metric that harmonizes precision and recall through their harmonic mean. This balanced measure provides a singular performance indicator where higher values reflect more optimal equilibrium between classification accuracy and coverage, indicative of superior overall model capability.

Micro-average, Macro-average and Weighted-average

In multi-class classification tasks, three distinct averaging strategies are commonly employed for metric computation - micro-average, macro-average, and weighted-average - each exhibiting unique characteristics suitable for different data distribution scenarios. Micro-average computes global evaluation metrics by aggregating all classes’ TP, FN and FP counts, effectively treating predictions across all categories as a unified whole, which inherently biases toward majority classes. Macro-average independently calculates evaluation metrics per class before taking the arithmetic mean, equally weighting all categories regardless of sample size differences, thus demonstrating greater sensitivity to minority classes. Weighted-average computes class-weighted means using each category’s sample count as weighting factors, simultaneously considering class importance while accounting for inherent distribution imbalances.

Conclusions

This paper proposes a DeepSeek-R1-Distill-based book classification algorithm, which employs an ensemble heterogeneous network architecture to achieve multi-level semantic representation, significantly improving the accuracy. The proposed method follows a three-stage processing pipeline: First, the DeepSeek-R1-Distill model is utilized to construct a text semantic encoder, mapping book metadata (e.g., title, author, abstract) into low-dimensional dense vectors. Second, a parallel multi-granularity feature extraction module is designed, wherein a local semantic perception module adopts a multi-scale 1D convolutional network to capture n-gram features, while a global dependency modeling module establishes long-range semantic relationships via a Transformer encoder. Finally, a dual-stage feature fusion strategy is introduced, where local and global features are concatenated before prediction is performed using a weighted voting-based ensemble classification mechanism.

Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed approach achieves an average F1-score exceeding 87% on a 21-category Chinese book classification task, confirming its effectiveness. The main innovations of this work include: (i) an efficient semantic encoder constructed using open-source LLMs; (ii) a heterogeneous network architecture for collaborative feature extraction; and (iii) a excellent classification strategy based on ensemble learning. Nevertheless, this study also has certain limitations. First, due to computational constraints, only the 8B version of DeepSeek-R1-Distill was employed, while larger variants (14B, 32B, and 70B) remain unexplored. Further investigation of these larger-scale models may yield additional improvements in reasoning ability and classification accuracy. Second, although the proposed framework demonstrates promising performance, its current design still leaves room for optimization, particularly in terms of more sophisticated feature fusion strategies and adaptive module interactions. Finally, the exploration of different LLM architectures and fine-tuning strategies specific to book classification has not been sufficiently addressed, which represents an important direction for future research. By addressing these limitations through deeper model exploration and framework refinement, it is expected that even higher performance and broader applicability can be achieved in the task of Chinese book classification.
