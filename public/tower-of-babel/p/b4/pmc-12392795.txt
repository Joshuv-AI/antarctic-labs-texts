Disease burden of seasonal influenza in China from 2010 to 2019: A systematic review and meta-analysis based on multiple indicators
Authors: Hanyu Zhang, Zu Gong, Nan Peng, Meijiao Wan, Jialong Tan, Chen Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThrough systematic review and meta-analysis, the average annual disease burden of seasonal influenza in China from 2010 to 2019 was estimated, and the differences between the northern and the southern regions were compared. A systematic review was conducted to collect articles from 2010 to 2019 on the disease burden of seasonal influenza in China. Study quality was assessed using the cross-sectional appraisal tool from the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality. Relevant data were extracted, and a random-effects model was used to produce age-stratified estimates for each metric. From these, the average annual number of influenza cases and excess deaths were calculated. A subgroup analysis compared northern and southern regions to account for possible regional differences. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software. A total of 24 articles were included. The annual average number of influenza cases is 111,429,687 (95%CI: 82,372,516–154,937,609). The burden of influenza, in terms of incidence rates, outpatient rates, and hospitalization rates, was heaviest among children under 5 years old. The burden of excess mortality was the heaviest among the elderly ≥65years old. Except for the incidence rates and hospitalization rates of individual age groups, there were no significant north-south differences in most epidemiological indicators. The study obtains the average annual disease burden of seasonal influenza. The disease burden of children <5years old and the elderly is relatively heavier. The study provides valuable evidence for the prevention and control of seasonal influenza.KEYWORDS: seasonal influenza, disease burden, age, incidence rates, outpatient rates, hospitalization rates, excess mortality rates

Introduction

Influenza is a common respiratory infectious disease caused by the influenza virus. It can be effectively transmitted among people through aerosols, droplets and contact, and often leads to seasonal epidemics and even pandemics.1 Influenza usually presents typical symptoms such as fever, chills and cough after a 1–2 days’ incubation period, which may lead to hospitalization or even death.2 Furthermore, workers and students may be absent from work or classes due to contracting the flu. There are approximately one billion seasonal influenza cases globally each year, and it causes 290,000–650,000 respiratory deaths.3 People of all age groups are generally affected by it, especially high-risk groups such as pregnant women, children under 5 years of age, older people, individuals with chronic medical conditions, individuals with immunosuppressive conditions and health and care workers.3 The prevalence of influenza can bring a huge disease burden to the entire society. Timely and accurate assessment of the burden of influenza has important public health significance.

In 2023, 12,780,291 cases of influenza were reported in China, accounting for 68.33% of the reported cases of legally notifiable infectious diseases across the country, with an incidence rate of 906.56 per 100,000 persons.4 However, the national statutory infectious disease reporting system significantly underestimates the true prevalence of influenza due to underreporting and cases that do not seek medical care; estimates from community and sentinel hospital data are closer to reality.5,6 Furthermore, at present, the assessment of the burden of influenza in China is mostly concentrated in a single province or city, or focuses on specific age groups and individual evaluation indicators (such as hospitalization rates, excess mortality rates, etc.). There are relatively few overall assessment studies on the burden of seasonal influenza at the national level.7–10

Therefore, this study will conduct a systematic review and meta-analysis of the literature related to the disease burden of seasonal influenza in China from 2010 to 2019, integrating a multi-level analytical framework of incidence rates, outpatient rates, hospitalization rates and excess mortality rates. The study will exclude the influence of the H1N1 pandemic in 2009 and COVID-19. Additionally, the disease burden of influenza varies with the seasons, and the prevalence fluctuates annually.11 Thus, using the aggregated results over a ten-year period can better balance the seasonal variations, and the annual average burden of seasonal influenza in China can be obtained. The estimation of the disease burden of influenza is of great significance for helping to improve vaccination decisions, formulate influenza prevention strategies and measures, and optimize the allocation of social health resources.

Methods

Key indicators

This study focused on four key indicators related to influenza: (1) the prevalence of influenza in the overall population and across age groups; (2) the outpatient rate in the overall population and by age group; (3) the hospitalization rate in the overall population and by age group; and (4) excess mortality. Influenza-associated excess mortality was estimated using deaths attributed to respiratory and circulatory (R&C) causes or all-cause mortality. In epidemiological and public health contexts, excess mortality refers to the number of deaths during a specific period that exceed the expected baseline under normal conditions.12

Search strategy

The articles from January 1, 2010 to October 30, 2024 were retrieved in the six databases, including PubMed, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wan Fang, China Biomedical Literature Service System and Chongqing VIP. The search strings were related to seasonal influenza in the Chinese population and included “Influenza”; “flu”; “Incidence rate”; “prevalence”; “morbidity”; “surveillance data”; “Outpatient rate”; “Inpatient rate”; “mortality rate”; “China”; “age distribution”; “Epidemiology”; “public health”; “annual report.” For specific search formulas, please refer to Annex 1. As we used different data resources, we conducted dual deduplication of the articles through software and manual methods in order to avoid the problem of duplicate articles, and we ensured that each case was counted only once.

Study selection criteria

The inclusion criteria include: (1) The study content includes at least one of the influenza incidence rates, outpatient rates, hospitalization rates and excess mortality rates by age group from 2010–2019 in China; (2) The study includes at least one dataset with positive influenza cases, covering the relevant age group for analysis within a specific geographic area or indicator. (3)The article is written in either Chinese or English.

The exclusion criteria include: (1) Studies of research types such as systematic reviews, meta-analyses, master’s theses, conference proceedings, reviews, editorials and letters; (2) The research content is avian influenza or a specific type of influenza; (3)Studies for which the full text cannot be obtained; (4) Research with incomplete original data or unclear data sources.

Data extraction

All the potentially relevant information included in the study was independently recorded by the two authors using a pre-designed form. When there was a dispute over whether to select articles for analysis, the final decision was made by a third author. We extracted the following information from each study: the name of the first author, publication year, study region, survey year, survey subjects, statistical model, epidemiological indicators (incidence rates, outpatient rates, hospitalization rates, excess mortality rates) and their confidence intervals. We use excess mortality of influenza in the respiratory and circulatory systems (R&C) categories of causes of death or all causes of death to estimate burden of influenza-associated excess mortality.13

Quality assessment

The quality of the included literature was evaluated using the cross-sectional study rating tool recommended by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ). The AHRQ standard was chosen because it is widely used to evaluate the methodological rigor of cross-sectional studies and can systematically identify the risks of bias.14 We included 8 items of this tool and added a new dimension, covering key aspectssuch as research design, sample selection, measurement methods, confounding control and statistical analysis. Each item was rated as ’ Yes (1 point) ’ or ’ No (0 points)’ with a total score ranging from 0 to 9 points. The higher the score, the better the quality of the research. Two authors independently scored the included articles, and the differences were agreed upon through group discussions.

Statistical analysis

In this study, we conducted a meta-analysis to pool four age-stratified indicators of seasonal influenza disease burden,15 including the incidence rates, outpatient rates, hospitalization rates caused by influenza and excess mortality rates of influenza estimated using R&C causes of death or all causes of death, and reported the combined estimates and their 95% confidence intervals.

Specifically, in this study, a single-group meta-analysis was employed, integrating the individual data from multiple studies to calculate a comprehensive estimate and its variance.

Previous studies have shown that assuming that the sampling distribution of the individual study effect size estimates is approximately normal is the theoretical basis for single-group rate meta-analysis.16 Only in this way can the distribution of the weighted combined total effect size be calculated through normal approximation, thereby obtaining reasonable combined results and confidence intervals.16 However, the rate values extracted from the original articles are close to zero and the data are discrete. Therefore, these rate values and their confidence intervals need to be log-transformed first to make them more close to a normal distribution,16 and then the standard error of the log-transformed rate values can be calculated for weighting (the formula is as follows).

The value 1.96 is the critical value from the standard normal distribution corresponding to a 95% confidence level. In the formula, we use 2 × 1.96 because the full width of a 95% confidence interval spans approximately ±1.96 standard errors on either side of the estimate. This rearrangement allows us to derive the standard error from the log-transformed confidence interval bounds, under the assumption of a normal distribution on the log scale.

We anticipate that there will be a certain degree of heterogeneity due to the observational nature of the included studies. Therefore, the rma function in the R’s “metafor” package was used in the study to combine the effect sizes based on the random-effects model, and the restricted maximum likelihood method (REML) was adopted to estimate the between-study variance (τ2), and heterogeneity was further assessed using standard statistics such as I2 and Q. Then the combined effect size and its confidence interval on the logarithmic scale are obtained. Finally, they are indexed to convert back to the original rate for display.

During the logarithmic transformation process before the combined analysis of the hospitalization rates, zero values appeared. We corrected these zero values by adding 10−5. The main consideration for choosing the replacement value of 10−5 is that this value should be very close to the rate size in our dataset and the statistical software should be able to successfully return valid results after the replacement. Theoretically, this replacement can avoid the undefined problem.17 The confidence interval was conservatively adjusted to ensure the validity of the interval. The lower limit was taken as max [corrected hospitalization rates × 0.8, 10−5 * 0.1]. The upper limit is adjusted to max[corrected lower limit × 1.2, 10−5 * 2].

When we conducting a combined analysis of excess mortality rates, negative values exist in the data extracted from each age group under 60 years old. Offset correction is required for the negative values. The offset is defined as the absolute value of the minimum negative number plus 0.000005. Then, offset correction is applied to add offsets to all extracted data. Subsequently, logarithmic transformation and standard error calculation are carried out. Finally, the inverse transformation function is defined. After indexation conversion back to the original rate and subtraction the offset, subsequent analysis is conducted.

All statistical analyses were performed using R4.4.1. The “metafor” package was used to generate combined estimates and 95% confidence intervals, conduct subgroup analyses, and draw forest plots.

Age group stratification and other treatments

In addition to analyzing the overall population, age-specific analyses were conducted by dividing the population into five groups: <5 years, 5–14 years, 15–24 years, 25–59 years, and ≥60 years. The estimation of influenza-associated excess mortality also included the ≥65 years age group to reflect the higher risk in older adults. This approach enhances the policy relevance of the findings. Moreover, as most studies adopted similar age groupings, this classification allowed us to include as many relevant studies as possible for each group.

If the article only mentions the <15 years old age group, then the data of <15 years old will be used for both <5 years old and 5–14 years old; if only the 15–59 years old age group is mentioned, then the data of 15–59 years old will be used for both 15–24 years old and 25–59 years old. The estimation of influenza-associated excess mortality further included the ≥65 years old age group.

For some age groups with only one eligible study, meta-analysis could not be performed, and the original data from that study were used instead.

Results

Document retrieval

The study conducted article search in accordance with the Guidelines for Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020. After the initial search, a total of 2023 relevant articles were obtained, including 1,032 Chinese articles and 991 English articles. Excluding 817 duplicate articles, there were 1,206 remaining articles. After reading the titles and abstracts of the articles, 1071 articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded. The remaining 135 articles were read in full. 111 articles were excluded and finally 24 articles were included. Among them, 5 articles were related to the incidence rates of influenza, 4 articles were related to the outpatient rates, 8 articles were related to the hospitalization rates, and 9 articles were related to the excess mortality rates. Research basic characteristics

Among the 24 studies included, the average AHRQ score is 6.54, indicating an overall quality at the upper-middle level. The primary shortcomings are predominantly related to selective reporting bias and the lack of blinding among outcome assessors.

The 24 studies encompassed in the analysis spanned the influenza seasons from 2010/11 to 2018/19, with the majority of the research periods concentrated between 2010 and 2015.

In terms of geographical regions, two articles took the mainland of China as the research area (one of which covered 30 provinces excluding Tibet Autonomous Region), nine articles studied the northern region, mainly in Beijing, and 13 articles studied the southern region, mainly in central and eastern provinces or cities.

In terms of statistical research methods, the multiplication model is mostly used in the study of incidence rates, while the negative binomial regression model is more frequently employed in the study of excess mortality rates. The characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1.

Meta-analysis results

The combined results of the random effects model show that in terms of incidence rates, the estimated average incidence rates of seasonal influenza in the entire population are 6,149 (95%CI: 5,169–7,314) per 100,000 persons, among which the incidence rates is the highest in the age group <5 years old. It reached 28,917 (95%CI: 19210–43,528) per 100,000 persons (Figure 2a). The group with the highest outpatient rates is also <5 years old, 2,288(95%CI: 996–5,728) per 100,000 persons (Figure 2b). Regarding the hospitalization rates, the overall hospitalization rates related to influenza in the population is at 66 (95%CI: 46–96) per 100,000 persons. The age group <5 years old has the highest rates, at 570 (95%CI: 268–1,210) per 100,000 persons, followed by the age group ≥60 years old, at 93 (95%CI: 68–128) per 100,000 persons. (Figure 2c) Regarding the excess mortality rates, the majority of studies included in this review focused on influenza-associated excess mortality among older adults aged 65 years and older. Notably, only one study examined excess mortality in the 60-and-older population, yielding a sample size too small for meta-analysis; however, multiple studies are available for the ≥65 age group. Consequently, this study was limited to performing a pooled analysis of the effect estimates for excess mortality specifically in the ≥65 age group. The data results of the age group of ≥60 years old in Figure 2d are the original data from the only one study. The excess mortality rates in the age group of ≥65 years old is much higher than that in other age groups, reaching 97.2 (95%CI: 72.8–129.7) per 100,000 persons (Figure 2d).

Discussion

Our research establishes a multi-level influenza disease burden estimation framework covering multiple age groups and multiple indicators.

Analysis by age group reveals that the average annual incidence rate of seasonal influenza among children <5 years old is 28.92%, which falls within the 20%-30% incidence rates range for children announced by the World Health Organization. The incidence rate among all age groups aged 15 and above is approximately between 2.68% and 8.24%, which is basically on par with the 5%-10% adult incidence rates announced by the World Health Organization.40 The outpatient burden related to influenza among children <5 years old is the heaviest, and hospitalizations mainly occur among children <5 years old and the elderly, which is consistent with the conclusions of previous studies.40–43 The burden of influenza-associated outpatient and hospitalization for children and the elderly is relatively high, possibly due to their relatively weak immune function, making them more prone to infection and having a higher risk of complications.44 It is important to note that influenza incidence among older adults is likely to be underestimated. This underestimation may be attributed to several factors, including atypical clinical presentations in the elderly, lower healthcare-seeking behavior among this age group, limited virological testing, and the reduced sensitivity of case definitions such as SARI in this age group. Additionally, influenza-related deaths in older adults may be misclassified due to comorbidities, further contributing to the underestimation.45,46 Studies have found that the burden of excess mortality among people ≥65 years old is much higher than that among people aged 0–64. This may be because the elderly usually have underlying diseases and are prone to severe complications after contracting influenza, making them have a higher risk of death.35,37,47 These findings suggest that the disease burden of influenza is relatively higher among children under 5 years of age and older adults. Treating them as key prevention and control targets is crucial for reducing the disease burden of influenza. Given limited budget and resources in China, prioritizing influenza prevention strategies for young children and older adults is essential. Treating these groups as key targets for prevention and control is crucial for reducing the overall disease burden of influenza.

Influenza vaccination is the most effective strategy to prevent infection and reduce the risk of disease progression to hospitalization and death. In China, the influenza vaccine is classified as a “Category II” vaccine, indicating that it is not covered by the central government and must be paid for out-of-pocket by individuals who choose to receive it. Only in a few cities with sufficient local public health funding – such as Beijing, Ningbo, and Shenzhen – do local Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) offer free influenza vaccines to school-aged children and adults aged ≥60 or ≥65 years. Consequently, national influenza vaccine coverage remains low, particularly among older adults, with coverage rates below 20% in this population.47,48 Expanding access to free influenza vaccination for children and the elderly at the national level could significantly improve coverage, reduce the burden of seasonal influenza, and enhance health equity among vulnerable populations.

Subgroup analysis divided into the northern and southern regions shows that there are no significant regional differences in most indicators. This is different from expectations. Some studies have pointed out that the number of hospitalizations related to influenza (SARI) in the southern region is 20 times that in the northern region.49 Similarly, our findings indicate that hospitalization rates in the south are substantially higher, reaching about three times the rates in the northern region. This difference may be caused by reasons such as the demographic characteristics of different regions, the accessibility of medical services resources and the different statistical models adopted.

Our estimated flu outpatient rates among children <5 years old are lower than 11,443 per 100,000 persons in the 2022/23 flu season in the United States, but the hospitalization rates are higher than 119 per 100,000 persons in the United States. Such regional differences are common and may arise from variations in case definitions, calculation methodologies, and healthcare-seeking behaviors.43 There are 2–6 million seasonal epidemics in France each year. From 2010 to 2018, studies estimated that an average of 28 people were hospitalized per 100,000, most of whom were children <5 years old and the elderly. The age distribution is similar to that of this study.50 In terms of excess mortality rates, our study is consistent with other studies on East Asian countries. More than 85%-90% of influenza deaths occur in individuals ≥65 years old.51

There are some limitations in this study. Firstly, the number of articles used for estimating each epidemiological indicator is relatively small, and the heterogeneity of the studies is relatively high. However, such high heterogeneity is quite common in the meta-analysis of rate data.52 Multiple factors can cause this heterogeneity, including methodological differences in the studies and the years of investigation, etc. In addition, due to the small number of studies, the information provided by the classic heterogeneity test Cochran’s Q may not be sufficient to reject the null hypothesis, and there may also be biases and inaccuracies in I2 (a statistic quantifying the proportion of total variation across studies due to true heterogeneity).53,54 Therefore, we need to treat the results of the meta-analysis with caution. We did not conduct a conventional sensitivity analysis but provided the range of 95%CI. On the one hand, the number of our studies was small, and the common sensitivity analysis methods were not fully applicable. On the other hand, the group analysis reduced the number of studies, which might lead to a low I2. This is not an appropriate solution to the heterogeneity problem.52

Secondly, the scope of the available data is limited. Only two of the included studies were nationwide surveys; the remainder were clustered in central and eastern China. The northern region was represented solely by Beijing, and no data were available for the western or northeastern regions. This geographic imbalance may have led to gaps in the overall findings. As influenza burden is strongly influenced by seasonality, geographic factors, and circulating virus strains, a single national estimate may oversimplify these complex patterns.55 Therefore, the obtained combined rate should be regarded as a weighted average over a historical period/specific population, rather than an exact prediction for future seasons. The results should be used in conjunction with real-time monitoring data and model studies, and updated regularly.

Thirdly, the treatment of zero and negative values in our analysis could be further refined. Based on previous research experience, we use extremely small values for correction. This theoretically can avoid the meaningless aspects in logarithmic transformation.17 However, this approach lacks rigorous mathematical derivation and sensitivity analysis to prove its rationality. Further exploration of more scientific processing methods is needed to preserve the value of information.

Subsequently, the influence of factors such as influenza strain typing and vaccination rates on the changes of influenza disease burden in different years can be further considered. Based on the results of this study, the estimation of influenza economic burden can also be further conducted, so as to evaluate the influenza economic burden situation in China more comprehensively. At the same time, it should also be noted that there are difficulties in obtaining reliable and accurate data of influenza epidemiological indicators at present. This may be attributed to several challenges, including the significant underestimation of influenza incidence by the national notifiable disease reporting system, the limited representativeness of medical institutions included in the sentinel surveillance system, and the inherent difficulty in accurately quantifying the true disease burden.9,51,56 In the future, it is imperative to further strengthen the surveillance of influenza viruses, refine the quality of life record-keeping, establish a more comprehensive epidemiological research system, and improve the accuracy of research. These efforts will collectively provide a solid foundation for scientifically evaluating the burden of influenza – associated diseases, thereby facilitating more effective prevention, control, and management strategies.

Conclusion

The study establishes a multi-level analytical framework for the disease burden of influenza in China from 2010 to 2019, providing evidence indicating that children <5 years old and the elderly are the groups with the highest disease burden of influenza. Most epidemiological indicators show no significant regional differences between the north and the south. The comprehensive evaluation of the disease burden of seasonal influenza in China provides a valuable reference basis for the calculation of the disease burden of influenza and the further improvement of prevention strategies and measures. Going forward, further improvements to the influenza surveillance system could contribute to more precise and representative epidemiological estimates.

Biography

Chen Chen is an Associate Professor in the Department of Global Health at the School of Public Health, Wuhan University, China. She is a health economist with a PhD from the University of York, UK, and postdoctoral training at Peking University, China. Her research focuses on the economics of vaccination, behavioral economics, and health policy evaluation. Chen leads several national and international projects, including collaborations on vaccine nudging and digital health platforms in China and Brazil, and behavioral strategies to promote flu vaccination among older adults. She has published extensively in SSCI/SCI journals and contributed to guidelines on vaccine economic evaluations in China. Her methodological expertise spans randomized trials, cost-effectiveness analysis, and survey-based research. Chen also teaches undergraduate and postgraduate courses in health economics and global health methods. Her work aims to inform equitable and evidence-based health policy, particularly for aging populations and underserved communities.
