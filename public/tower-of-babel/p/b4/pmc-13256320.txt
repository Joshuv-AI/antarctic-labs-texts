Introduction

Diagnostic pathology has long relied on the manual examination of hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) stained slides under a light microscope; a workflow that has changed remarkably little since Wissozky’s first stain formulation in 1877 and that now includes certain refinements toward a global standardized protocol [1,2]. Traditionally, pathologists are trained to interpret these images and make diagnostic decisions, particularly in cancer where accurate diagnosis as well as other sophisticated evaluations (e.g. grading and staging) are crucial. Although essential for cancer diagnosis, this method is labor-intensive, prone to inter-observer variability, and limited by the physical constraints of glass slides. The advent of high-resolution whole-slide imaging (WSI) and digital pathology marks a pivotal transition, facilitating virtual microscopy, telepathology, and the creation of extensive, searchable image repositories that reconceptualize histology as a data-rich discipline [3–5].

By 2050, the global cancer incidence is projected to rise by 77%, largely due to global increases in longevity as well as changes in lifestyle. Concurrently, our understanding of the biological and clinical complexity of cancer is increasing, propelled by tumor heterogeneity and the necessity to integrate morphological, molecular, and spatial data for precision oncology. The sheer volume and multidimensionality of information like morphology, immune infiltration, stromal remodeling to spatially resolved proteomic and transcriptomic patterns within a single histologic section now surpass the interpretive capacity of human vision [6,7]. Artificial intelligence (AI), especially deep learning and foundation models trained on large-scale histopathology datasets, has emerged as a transformative approach, enabling quantitative and reproducible extraction of diagnostic, prognostic, and molecularly relevant features directly from routine H&E slides.

In the past decade, cloud-based data sharing and advancements in artificial intelligence, machine learning, and deep learning have significantly transformed medical imaging. Contemporary AI foundation models, trained on extensive histopathology image datasets, can accurately detect cancers, characterize tumor subregions and immune microenvironments, quantify biomarkers with high reproducibility, and infer molecular alterations and clinical outcomes directly from routine histology. In digital pathology, AI primarily serves to augment pathologists by automating quantitative tasks, reducing subjectivity, and improving efficiency. From a research perspective, these approaches also enable the discovery of novel and/or previously unknown tissue-level signatures linked to disease biology.

This review positions AI-enabled digital pathology not simply as a technical innovation, but as a paradigm shift in tissue interpretation. We outline the current challenges in digital pathology and discuss how AI/ML is emerging as a transformative extension of tissue interpretation in cancer diagnostics including the development of AI/ML for evaluation of digital histologic images, discussion of research on the use of AI in evaluation of H&E slides and emerging information regarding the regulatory landscape governing the application of AI in tissue evaluation, providing the most up-to-date information in the field. Only English-language, peer-reviewed publications, news articles, and policy documents were evaluated and cited as necessary. The primary keywords used for screening included ‘digital pathology,’ ‘foundational models,’ ‘artificial intelligence’ and ‘machine learning,’ and ‘adoption of AI in pathology.’ Secondary references identified within primary manuscripts were also examined and incorporated to ensure continuity of scientific context. All reviewed literature is accessible through PubMed or the specific organizational websites listed in the reference section. In the reference list, we have also highlighted the most impactful articles in the field.

Digital pathology: foundations and enablers

Digital pathology represents the convergence of imaging technology, computational infrastructure, and data science [8]. Practically, it transforms traditional glass-slide histopathology imaging into a digital, data-rich framework capable of providing insights beyond the limits of conventional microscopy. This section outlines the key advantages of digital pathology over traditional methods and highlights the foundational components that enable future integration of artificial intelligence (AI) in cancer diagnosis and evaluation.

Transition from conventional microscopy to whole-slide imaging (WSI)

Whole-slide imaging (WSI) digitizes the entire glass slide into a high-resolution image, allowing long-term, high-fidelity storage without the constraints and risks associated with preserving physical glass slides [9]. Over time, stored slides can degrade, leading to reduced staining intensity that compromises reanalysis and introduces irreproducibility [10]. Even the use of serial sections as substitutes can introduce variability, particularly in antigenicity. Bertheau et al. demonstrated that the molecular markers such as CD3, vimentin, CD45, and CD20 show significant decrease in staining intensity after just 3 months of storage compared to a freshly cut section [11]. By digitizing slides at the time of preparation, WSI enables recording tissue morphology and biomarker studies in their original state, thus improving the reinterpretation of these cases as close to the original results as possible. Additionally, through cloud-based platforms it can support remote collaboration and large-scale multi-center data sharing.

As the field is increasingly adopting WSI, several core elements must be established to ensure accessibility and scalability. Figure 1 outlines these foundational components. This includes data acquisition through analytics and translation and the associated regulatory frameworks that govern clinical applicability and ensure adherence to institutional and governmental guidelines.

A central challenge within this framework is managing the computational demands of WSI. Each slide can generate several gigabytes of data, depending on resolution and imaging depth, calling for the need of robust storage and high-performance computing resources to support uninterrupted analysis and AI/ML-driven workflows. Graphics processing units (GPUs) are essential to accelerate computationally intensive operations such as optical-density, color normalization, pixel intensity quantification and other complex image transformations [12]. To facilitate these processes, NVIDIA has discussed an optimized workflow integrating MONAI and RAPIDS aimed at enhancing real-time data readout and analysis pipelines [13]. The MONAI is an open-source platform that has a community-driven framework designed to accelerate research and clinical translation in medical imaging. RAPIDS is a suite of open-source software libraries that enable the development of GPU-accelerated analytical pipelines. Together, these tools streamline the WSI pipeline, spanning pre- and post-processing, visualization and quantitative analytics which effectively addresses the computational complexity inherent to multi-dimensional, information-rich data in digital pathology.

Digital workflow integration in clinical laboratories

Historically, clinical laboratories have a selective and close knit infrastructure that depends on segregated storage and management of data within an organization. It involves bulk physical and/or digital storage confined within its framework. Transitioning to a digital framework offers substantial gains where connected technologies streamline and automate data retrieval with real-time monitoring across both public and private settings [14]. While there are practical challenges in transitioning to a digital framework, notably costs of procurement and maintenance, there are significant benefits, including fast throughput of image evaluation and data analysis with proficient amalgamation of key clinical aspects associated with the patients. For example, AI/ML and computer aided solutions provide opportunities for collaboration among global experts via telepathology (Figure 1) [15].

Telepathology requires standardized modes of communication between data elements, especially with inter-institutional (including international) collaborations. Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) provides an international standard for storing, managing, and transmitting medical images and associated metadata [16]. While traditionally used to support inter-operability across modalities in radiology (e.g. X-ray, CT/MRI-scans) DICOM is gaining importance in digital pathology as AI/ML tools and large-scale data workflows become embedded in diagnostic practice. The DICOM standard (ISO 12052) ensures secure, standardized and interoperable data exchange across clinical and research applications [17]. In addition to the secured transaction of data, the core components of DICOM standard also encompass file format and a unique DICOM header describing the data elements. Additionally, the DICOM Network Service (DNS) defines the communication protocol between source and retrieval points. Several studies have demonstrated the feasibility and advantages of implementing DICOM standards in digital pathology; their model facilitated efficient access to both image and metadata while enabling seamless information exchange for downstream analyses [18].

Regulatory landscape

Under the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA, 1996), the protection of patient data, particularly electronic health information, remains paramount. In 2022, the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) updated their guidance to codify stringent expectations for data security, privacy, and controlled access in digital environments [19]. Multiple global regulatory bodies oversee the development, validation, and deployment of AI/ML in pathology to ensure safe and ethical use. In Europe, the European Medicines Agency (EMA) requires AI-enabled digital pathology devices to comply with the Medical Device Regulation (MDR) or the In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (IVDR) to receive a CE mark for clinical use [20]. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) leads the approval process. As of 2024, 26 AI-based analytical tools have received regulatory authorization for cancer diagnostics, including Stratipath Breast [21], PaigeProstate Detect [22], and Visiopharm Metastasis Detection [23]. These approved AI platforms support quantitative, reproducible interpretation of H&E-stained tissue sections to detect cancer progression and inform staging. However, many countries still do not have well formulated regulatory bodies to oversee such advancements. In India, the Central Drugs Standard Control Organization (CDSCO) acts as the national regulatory body that oversees such device certifications along with drugs and cosmetics used in the country [24]. To address the lack of a specialized body to oversee the advancements in AI and implementation in health care India, like many other countries, has joined the HealthAI Global Regulatory Network to enhance international supervision in AI based health care [25].

Regulatory approval of AI applications to pathology center on a set of rigorously evaluated criteria designed to ensure systemic safety, transparency and long-term reliability. Key criteria include a predefined stable AI/ML model and a predetermined plan to manage future updates. This helps maintain consistent performance across different user and laboratory settings and takes into consideration evolving clinical workflows. Transparency around the AI/ML’s intended use, decision-making frontiers and operational limitations is also essential for building trust among pathologists and clinical stakeholders. A critical parameter is the predetermined change control plan (PCCP) where the US-FDA requires a clear strategic outline that will manage subsequent modifications and updates to the system, such as devices and software. As detailed in the FDA’s 2021 publication, PCCP ensures that the alterations will not affect the system’s effectiveness or safety [26].

Overall, the integration of digital pathology tools into clinical workflows is built upon a growing but carefully governed foundation of regulatory, technical, and ethical principles. These frameworks continue to evolve as global research advances. In the following sections, we explore these components in greater depth, with particular emphasis on the expanding role of artificial intelligence in interpreting cancer tissue images and transforming diagnostic practice.

Artificial intelligence in pathology: core technologies

The use of AI to evaluate digital histologic images has rapidly evolved, shifting from classical machine-learning (ML) methods reliant on carefully articulated features to modern deeplearning (DL) systems capable of extracting complex hierarchical patterns directly from WSI(s). This evolution has enabled models to achieve high accuracy across tasks ranging from tumor detection and grading to biomarker inference and prognostic predictions that exceed the limits of human perception. In this context, the technological foundations of this transition outline the key computational paradigms shaping contemporary AI in pathology. We begin by distinguishing traditional machine-learning approaches from deep-learning methodologies, with an emphasis on how it has transformed image-based diagnostics. We then discuss the primary model architectures used in digital pathology such as convolutional neural networks (CNNs) which have long served as the backbone of medical image analysis and transformer-based models which are increasingly redefining state-of-the-art performance through their ability to structure long-range spatial dependencies in WSIs. Finally, we discuss critical aspects of the AI learning environment, in particular foundational models. These are all essential for translating algorithmic advances into safe and trustworthy diagnostic tools suited for real-world practice.

Machine learning (ML) vs. deep learning (DL): key concepts

The integration of AI into cancer diagnostics has been shaped by a decisive shift from classical ML techniques to DL–based approaches. Early applications of ML in pathology relied on algorithms such as support vector machines, random forests and logistic regression models, which operate on predefined histo-morphological features. These manually defined features range from determining nuclear size, shape and texture to glandular architecture, pleomorphism and quantification of nuclear or cytoplasmic protein expression to support tasks like cancer grading, survival modeling and prognostic risk stratification (Table 1) [32–35]. Although these approaches offer a degree of interpretability and provide valuable insights in earlier computational pathology studies, they were inherently constrained by the need for expert-driven feature design. As a result, the performance of ML systems is often not translatable across tissue types, staining protocols and cancer subtypes, limiting their ability to generalize across diverse clinical settings.

Deep learning (DL) fundamentally transformed this landscape. Instead of relying on predefined features, DL models, particularly multilayered convolutional neural networks, learn hierarchical and highly discriminative representations directly from raw WSIs [36,37]. Landmark studies such as Coudray et al. [38], applied DL to The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) lung cancer dataset to predict genetic mutations directly from histology. It was a paradigm shift and became widely recognized in positioning DL as the foundational technology for modern computational oncology and which has shown the ability to predict molecular subtype, stratify patient risk, automate tumor microenvironment profiling and integration of multimodal datasets.

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and transformer-based models for image analysis

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) remain the foundational architecture in digital pathology because they can automatically learn hierarchical spatial features from histopathology images by detecting fine details such as nuclear and cellular textures and combine them into higher-level patterns such as tumor structures. Unlike traditional methods, CNNs discover relevant features without the input of manual pre-defined features, capturing subtle visual cues for tumor detection, subtype classification and prognostic quantification. Benchmark algorithms like CAMELYON16 and CAMELYON17 have demonstrated that CNNs can achieve near-expert pathologist performance [39,40]. Similarly, CNN-based models trained on the largest multi-institutional prostate cancer dataset, PANDA, achieved pathologist-level accuracy in Gleason grading across independent cross-continental cohorts and is now being applied to Gleason grading in prospective clinical trials [41].

Recently, transformer-based architectures have gained traction in computational pathology for their ability to capture long-range contextual dependencies across gigapixel WSIs. Notably, Shao et al. introduced TransMIL, a Transformerbased correlated MIL framework that integrates global tissue architecture with local histological features. This achieved state-of-the-art performance in tumor detection and cancer subtype classification [42]. Hybrid models that combine CNNs for localized feature extraction and transformers for contextual reasoning are emerging as powerful tools with potential applications in predicting cancer biomarker status and survival outcomes [43,44].

Computational pathology foundation models

Foundation models represent a promising transformative advance in computational pathology, enabling large-scale pretraining on extensive collections of histopathology images that often include tens of thousands to hundreds of thousands of slides spanning diverse tissue types and staining protocols [45–47]. Table 2 provides a summary of recent foundation models, exemplified by architectures such as Prov-GigaPath [48] Virchow [50], CONCH [47]. These models use rich visual representations across diverse tissue types and cancer classes to extract features through self-supervised or multimodal pretraining on WSIs. Prov-GigaPath, for instance, was pretrained on over 1.3 billion image tiles spanning more than 171,000 whole slides across 31 tissue types and substantially outperformed second-best approaches on 25 out of 26 pathology tasks, highlighting the importance of large-scale training data and whole-slide context modeling in improving performance across diagnosis, subtyping, and biomarker prediction tasks.

However, many current foundation models may be less valuable in, for example, AI evaluation of rare diseases, or applications where outcomes are the essential training endpoint, as these situations necessarily limit the available material on which to train the model. Modern foundation models have been leveraging advanced architectures such as vision transformers, multimodal alignment of pathology images and model fusion. Hierarchical transformer frameworks integrate multiscale context, from cellular-level details to whole-slide tissue architecture. These models incorporate clinical data to empower AI/ML systems to link phenotypic tissue patterns with clinical outcomes [45]. Ensemble multiple foundation models have been shown to outperform individual models across various downstream tasks [52], enhancing both robustness and acceptance.

While the clinical potential of foundation models is substantial, their safe deployment demands rigorous validation, dataset harmonization and strong regulatory oversight. Training on diverse, heterogeneous datasets may confer robustness to variations in staining, scanners and tissue types. Challenges persist, including biases from underrepresented populations, batch effects and the need for transparent cross-institution evaluation [53]. As regulatory frameworks evolve, foundation models can support general-purpose pathology by reducing annotation burdens, accelerate model development and scale up clinically meaningful applications.

Model interpretability and clinical validity

For AI to gain widespread adoption in digital pathology, both interpretability and clinical validity are essential [51]. Interpretability enables pathologists and clinicians to understand why a model arrives at a given prediction, thereby providing accountability and safe integration into diagnostic workflows. To this end, visualization techniques such as class activation mapping (CAM), Grad-CAM and attention-based heatmaps have increasingly been incorporated into histopathology AI research. In breast and colorectal cancer studies, these tools have confirmed that well-trained models consistently attend to biologically meaningful regions, for example, invasive tumor fronts, necrotic cores, peritumoral stromal remodeling and lympho-vascular angiogenesis, demonstrating that the model is using meaningful biological tumor features rather than irrelevant artifacts [54]. Such insights not only validate model reasoning but also reveal potential novel morphologic correlates linked to molecular or clinical phenotypes.

Recently, a study demonstrated that the use of deep learning not only to classify histopathology images of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma but, importantly, to make the model’s decisions interpretable for pathologists. The authors show that CNNs can accurately distinguish tumor from normal tissue on H&E slides, using explainability methods such as Grad-CAM and HR-CAM to highlight the image regions driving predictions. These visual explanations reveal that the network focuses on biologically useful features agreeing with expert pathologists’ assessments. The study suggests that CNNs, particularly when paired with interpretable visual outputs, hold potential as a tool to assist pathologists in evaluating cancer histology [55].

Regulatory agencies prioritize transparency in model design, dataset composition and evaluation metrics as mandated evidence that a tool performs reliably outside the development environment before approval for clinical use. This is critical for universal adaptation of such systems, where patient safety is top priority. These expectations reflect the real-world complexities of pathology practice where variability in tissue preparation or imaging workflows can challenge even well-optimized algorithms. By ensuring interpretability and rigorous validation, AI/ML can serve as a decision-support system that augments pathologists’ expertise while maintaining accountability in patient care.

Clinical applications of AI/ML in cancer diagnostics

The application of AI/ML into oncology is rapidly transforming cancer diagnostics with the aim of increased accuracy, scalability and reproducibility in clinical decisions. Researchers are building AI-driven analytical platforms that are capable of extracting subtle, high-dimensional patterns from diverse data sources. These include histopathological images, radiological scans, genomic profiles and electronic health records to collectively form a multi-dimensional diagnostic ecosystem as demonstrated (Figure 1). As previously discussed, (section 3.1), DL algorithms use multilayered neural networks to extract discriminative features from WSI. This ability has been utilized to develop algorithms that can be proficiently used in tumor detection, grading, subtype classification and biomarker quantification [36]. Some of the aspects are discussed in the subsequent sections.

Tumor diagnosis and classification

Identification of abnormal tissue growth and distinguishing between benign and malignant forms are the primary steps in tumor detection and classification. Further subtyping based on histopathological or molecular characteristics is critical in determining treatment strategies. Using classical H&E staining a pathologist is able to recognize cancer cells and distinguish them from normal cells through a series of often subtle color, architectural and morphologic changes that illustrate cellular abnormalities, including enlarged and irregular nuclei, high nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio, loss of normal architecture and cellular inclusions and arrangements that can indicate the cancer type and state of differentiation (i.e. tumor grade). Even though relied on for over a century and despite the ubiquitous nature of H&E staining, it is well recognized that this staining can result in fairly significant variations in color, due in part to the natural derivation of the stains as well as inconsistencies in staining protocol (incubation time, temperature, humidity, batch variation, etc.) that sometimes can impede pathological evaluation, although pathologists are trained to ‘read through’ these variations [56–58]. However, as discussed more fully below, such color variation can be a major source of irreproducibility and lack of generalizability when training and using AI algorithms on digital histologic images. Despite this, there is tremendous effort to adapt AI for cancer diagnosis for good reasons; although pathologists are highly trained individuals and use specific criteria to evaluate histologic material, there is well documented interobserver variability, especially regarding tumor grading, but even in diagnosis. For example, out of 259 gynecological cancer patients, nearly 30% of cases had pathological discrepancies after undergoing second review, the majority of which were due to adjudicating subtypes and tumor grade [59]. Table 1 highlights some of the parameters utilized by pathologists from biopsy or surgical samples to diagnose and evaluate and especially grade cancer, and where AI/ML can play a critical role by evaluating these features quickly, reproducibly and precisely in thousands of cells simultaneously. There is growing evidence that utilizing AI/ML in automating and assisting in pathological analyses can result in greater reproducibility in cancer evaluation, further discussed in sections 4.2 and 5.1.

Application of AI/ML/DL in cancer grading

Cancer grade refers to the appearance of cancerous cells under a microscope and is based on the degree of tumor differentiation, that is how close or far the cancer looks compared to its normal tissue counterpart. As outlined in Table 1, a variety of parameters are used to evaluate cancer grade and which may be beneficially evaluated by AI/ML. Grading is used as a surrogate for tumor aggressiveness, where more poorly differentiated (higher grade) tumors generally behave more aggressively than more well differentiated (lower grade) tumors. Each tumor type has its own grading system, with usually at least several systems proposed. Developed over half a century ago, Gleason grading (GG) is a scoring system for prostate cancer based on architectural features of the tumor [60]. It has been scrutinized over the years but continues to be a reliable tool for prostate pathology [61]. However, there is significant interobserver variability in Gleason grading, even between experts. A study from a large consortium of pathologists demonstrated that use of AI/ML significantly improved the reproducibility of Gleason grading [62]. In the case of breast cancer, investigators have developed various algorithms to reproducibly assess parameters important in grading, such as mitotic count [63,64]. A recent review on gastric cancer summarizes examples of DL models used to detect malignancy and histological subtypes directly from H&E slides with high accuracy (often AUROC > 0.95), and demonstrates reduced review time and improved pathologist performance when DL assistance is applied. It showed that AI enhanced detection of subtle or borderline lesions that are difficult to identify under conventional microscopy [65].

Feature extraction using DL models have been constantly evolving. Several studies have examined how features learned by deep learning models from whole-slide images can be rendered interpretable and biologically meaningful. Visualization approaches such as CAM, Grad-CAM, and attention-based heatmaps are commonly used to localize regions most influential for model predictions. These regions have been shown to correspond to established histopathologic features, including invasive tumor margins, necrosis, stromal remodeling, and immune infiltration. AI for survival prediction and risk stratification

Deep learning algorithms trained on WSIs can be associated with clinical endpoints such as overall survival, progression-free survival, or recurrence risk [66,71,72]. For example, models that quantify tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and tumor-associated stroma from H&E-stained tissue can stratify patients into high- and low-risk groups, with prognostic performance comparable to or even exceeding that of conventional histopathological metrics [73]. Here, the authors developed deep learning-based tissue classification scoring algorithms and also segmentation models to quantify the spatial arrangement of tumor cells, stromal regions, and lymphocytes on digitized H&E slides. The resulting digital features were evaluated for their association with disease-specific survival in two multi-institutional cohorts. The findings demonstrated that AI-extracted histologic spatial features, particularly quantifications of lymphocyte infiltration and stromal architecture, provide independent prognostic information in the assessment of patients with triple-negative breast cancer. Furthermore, integrating these digital features with established clinical variables, such as lymph node status, enhances risk stratification and indicates the potential to refine prognostic models. In colorectal and lung cancers, image-based signatures derived via AI also show promise for predicting outcomes [74,75]. Both studies go beyond subjective interpretation by radiologists or pathologists, using computational feature extraction for predictive modeling in lung cancer classification and prognosis, and in metastatic colorectal cancer outcomes. Clinically, both AI and radiomic signatures are intended to provide actionable insights that may improve risk stratification and inform treatment decisions, particularly in populations where traditional imaging or histopathology fails to capture subtle prognostic differences. The AI lung cancer meta-analysis synthesizes a broad evidence base to characterize performance trends and statistical robustness of AI models, thereby offering a comprehensive overview of AI applications in imaging diagnosis and prognosis. The radiomics colorectal cancer study exemplifies how imaging signatures can be structured to predict patient outcomes, demonstrating translational potential beyond simple classification tasks. However, persistent heterogeneity in study designs, imaging protocols, and analytical workflows remains a significant barrier to clinical standardization, as discussed in subsequent sections.

Integration with biomarker and clinical data

AI models can further enhance prognostic accuracy by integrating histopathology images with genomic, transcriptomic and clinical datasets (Figure 2). ML has been used to correlate histological phenotypes with mutational profiles, copynumber variation or gene-expression signatures, enabling cancer diagnosis and prognosis prediction [69,76]. In a recent review article, the power of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) was interpreted in the context of functional polymorphisms which can lead to diseased outcomes [77]. Genomic data with identifiable SNPs can also augment the predictive modeling of AI in future. For example, in glioma and clear cell renal cell carcinoma, combining WSI features with genomic and transcriptomic data has been shown to outperform predictions based on histology or molecular data alone [69]. These integrative models hold the potential to guide individualized therapy selection, optimize surveillance strategies and identify patients most likely to benefit from targeted interventions.

Real-world implementation and case studies

Over the past decade, AI has been transitioning from experimental research to practical applications in digital pathology, demonstrating its potential to enhance diagnostic accuracy, efficiency, and prognostic insights. Several AI-powered platforms have received regulatory clearance from the FDA and CE mark, enabling their integration into clinical workflows, while emerging applications continue to explore predictive and prognostic capabilities in oncology. These applications highlight both the benefits and challenges of AI/ML implementation, including technical variability, dataset limitations, workflow integration, and staff training. This section presents examples of FDA/CE-approved AI tools, pilot studies of innovative AI applications, and lessons learned from practical deployment in pathology laboratories, illustrating the evolving landscape of AI in clinical practice.

Examples of FDA/CE-approved AI tools in pathology

The last decade has seen the translation of AI from research laboratories into regulated clinical environments within digital pathology. Several AI-powered platforms have received regulatory clearance from agencies such as the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Union’s CE mark, signaling a growing confidence in their clinical safety and effectiveness. Among the most prominent examples is Paige® Prostate, the first FDA-approved AI diagnostic tool for in vitro diagnostic use in pathology [22]. Developed by Paige.AI, this algorithm assists pathologists in detecting prostate cancer in digitized WSIs, helping pathologist reducing diagnostic errors and improve efficiency, resulting in up to 70% reduction in detection errors [22]. Similarly, Ibex Medical Analytics (Ibex) have achieved CE-IVD certification and have received the first FDA 510(k) clearance in leading the AI-powered cancer diagnostics [78]. Another notable platform, PathAI, has collaborated with major pharmaceutical companies and healthcare institutions to develop algorithms that quantify histopathological features and predict therapeutic responses, promoting precision pathology solutions [79]. While many of its tools remain under investigational use, the platform represents a scalable ecosystem for both clinical diagnostics and translational research. Aiforia empowers pathologists and researchers with advanced deep learning AI and cloud-based technologies, aiming to enhance the speed and consistency of analyzing complex medical images in the field of pathology [80].

Collectively, these examples illustrate the evolution of AI in pathology over the past decade, from assisting in routine screening to more advanced, data-driven applications – ranging from efficiency improvements and quality assurance to, in some cases, supporting precision medicine and cloud-based solutions that enhance scalability and workflow.

AI prediction of brain metastases in NSCLC

Brain metastasis is a common and serious complication in patients with early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [81,82]. While surgery can often remove tumors confined to the lung, a subset of patients later develop metastatic lesions in the brain, which significantly worsens prognosis [83,84]. Currently, there is a lack of reliable tools to predict which patients are at high risk, often leading to either overtreatment or insufficient monitoring [85]. To address this challenge, our group has developed a deep learning algorithm that was trained and then tested on digital images of diagnostic H&E slides from 158 patients with early stage NSCLC who underwent lung resections with curative intent and who did not receive systemic therapy either before or after primary surgery and who either suffered brain metastasis (met+) or no metastasis to any site after 10 years follow up (met−). From this cohort, the AI was trained on 118 cases, and then tested on a separate, non-overlapping cohort of 40 patients; this was repeated three times with different subsets of cases in the training and testing cohorts. The AI model achieved 87% accuracy in predicting which patients would develop brain metastases (met+) vs. predicting which patients developed no metastases after 10 years follow-up (met−), significantly outperforming four experienced pathologists, whose average accuracy was 57%. Importantly, the algorithm also demonstrated high specificity in identifying patients unlikely to experience any metastasis, highlighting its potential to reduce unnecessary treatment. The model used a complex set of histologic features including tumor cells and tumor microenvironment and generated tile-level ‘attention’ maps that demonstrate that the model focused on similar areas in both the met+ and met− patients. These attention maps suggest that the tumor microenvironment may be crucial in the prediction of met+ vs. met− patients and may in the future allow us to further dissect the biologic basis of the AI prediction [86].

Having achieved high predictive power, we wanted to better understand how AI learns. We first looked into the optimal parameters required to best train AI. Not surprisingly, we found that the highest focus resolution led to the greatest learning accuracy, and that the optimal field of view was 40 microns [87]. This last result makes sense because we had previously shown that AI learns through reading the environment of the tumor, not just on individual tumor cells. Interestingly, even at very poor resolution, the algorithm was still predictive, just less so than at high resolution. Reasoning that this was the result of the preservation of color, we next explored the impact of color on AI learning.

While we achieved high predictive power in determining met+ vs. met− patients even after several rounds of analysis, we showed that the algorithm was not generalizable; we obtained a new series (batch) of histologic slides and their corresponding digital images from the adjacent recuts of the same tissue blocks as in the study described above (158 patients with early stage NSCLC [86]), processed in the same lab but at a different time. We found that the AI trained on the first batch of slides (described above) was unable to predict patient outcome and did not improve even when the tinctorial difference corrections were made through either traditional color-tuning or stain normalization with the help of a Cycle Generative Adversarial Network (CycleGAN) process. However, when that second batch underwent training (118 cases) and testing (40 non-overlapping cases) in a similar fashion as the first batch, the predictive power could be reproduced [58], clearly indicating that while information was ‘in’ the digital images that could predict outcome, the resulting algorithm developed in training of one batch could not be effectively used on another batch of slides, despite the fact these images were from slides taken from the same tissue blocks.

This lack of generalizability of diagnostic and predictive AI algorithms has plagued the field, and we now understand that at least one of the primary reasons for this is the color/stain variations that are a normal part of the histologic tissue processing and staining, as discussed in section 4.1. This highlights the need to develop an entirely new way to process and collect consistent microscopy images from histologic slides that can be used to both train and allow for the general application of predictive AI algorithms. To that end, we have begun exploring ways to eliminate the stain process, and thus the inherent variation it produces, from the tissue processing protocols.

AI prognostic models combining digital pathology images with clinical variables

Prostate cancer is one of the most common cancers among men, and for those with high-risk or locally advanced disease, predicting how the cancer will behave is particularly challenging. Traditional pathological markers often fall short in accurately distinguishing which patients are likely to experience disease progression or succumb to their cancer [88]. To address this unmet need, Parker et al., recently developed and validated a multimodal artificial intelligence (MMAI) model that integrates digital pathology images with clinical variables [89]. This tool has been shown to predict outcomes such as distant metastasis and prostate cancer–specific mortality and to identify patients most likely to benefit from second-generation androgen receptor pathway inhibitors in the STAMPEDE trial cohort. By integrating histopathologic and clinical data, this MMAI approach represents a novel strategy for precision oncology in high-risk prostate cancer, offering more individualized prognostic insights and informing treatment decisions in a way that traditional markers alone cannot achieve.

External validation and generalizability of AI in digital pathology

Despite the growing number of AI algorithms demonstrating high performance in retrospective and single-institution studies, external validation across independent populations and laboratories remains one of the most critical challenges for clinical adoption in digital pathology [90,91]. External validation refers to the evaluation of an AI model on data generated outside the environment in which it was developed, ideally encompassing variations in patient demographics, tissue processing protocols, staining methods, slide scanners, and institutional workflows. Such validation is essential to assess the generalizability, robustness, and clinical reliability of AI systems prior to deployment.

However, accessing to large, well-annotated multi-institutional datasets is limited by data-sharing restrictions, privacy concerns, and variability in annotation standards [92]. Even when external dataset is available, AI models frequently experience performance degradation [93]. As demonstrated by multiple studies – including the example described in Section 5.2 – models trained on one batch or institution may fail to generalize even to samples derived from the same tissue blocks processed at different times, underscoring the fragility of many current AI approaches [74].

Conclusion

The integration of AI into pathology offers significant potential for enhancing diagnostic accuracy, efficiency, and workflow optimization. However, there are several technical and methodological challenges that must be addressed to ensure robust and reliable implementation. As mentioned earlier, some of the critical factors are the quality, size and diversity of training datasets [94–96]. AI model performance depends heavily on these aspects, and models trained on limited or homogeneous cohorts may not generalize well across different patient populations or staining protocols. Therefore, assembling diverse and well-annotated datasets is essential to reduce bias in clinical outcomes. Another major concern involves diagnostic accuracy, as AI algorithms can produce false positives or negatives that can influence clinical decision-making [97,98]. Validation using independent datasets and careful integration of AI as a decision-support tool, rather than a standalone diagnostic system, are vital to minimize errors and maintain clinical confidence. Moreover, a framework to demonstrate how the models perform outside it’s training environment is critical; this could be addressed through the development of a standardized method to assess performance of a proposed algorithm that can be applied across all platforms [99]. Technical factors such as color variation and slide preparation have now been demonstrated to be of crucial importance in affecting AI performance [94]. Implementing color normalization, stain augmentation and standardized imaging protocols might mitigate sensitivity to these variations [100]. But as these types of correction have not so far proved effective [58], there may be a need to establish a more fundamental correction and rethink how tissues are processed and stained to produce digital images.

Reproducibility and reliability remain central to clinical adoption. Cross-site validation, continuous performance monitoring, and quality control mechanisms are necessary to ensure consistent results across scanners, laboratories, and patient cohorts. Equally important is seamless system integration and workflow adaptation. For example, some institutions have already developed a workflow that integrates AI in tissue evaluation, which have streamlined daily operations, decreased the number of immunohistochemical tests performed and reduced turnaround times [101].

Real-world deployment of AI systems has highlighted practical challenges, including the need for staff training and addressing system integration complexities, emphasizing that successful adoption depends on careful planning and ongoing support [101]. Successful AI/ML adoption also depends on structured training and change management. The University Health Network in Toronto, Canada, employed a dual workflow model, providing simultaneous access to glass and digital slides [102]. This, combined with the use of ‘super users’ as change champions, facilitated adoption and reduced resistance among staff. Additionally, regulatory and ethical compliance is critical to ensure patient safety, data privacy, and public trust, requiring institutions to remain updated on evolving healthcare policies. Finally, continuous monitoring, audits, and user feedback are indispensable for evaluating AI performance and guiding iterative improvements. Expert opinion

Digital pathology and AI are poised to fundamentally reshape cancer diagnostics and therapeutic decision-making. However, significant challenges exist for the clinical translation of AI, including ensuring generalizability, mitigating bias, and establishing long-term clinical accountability. Multiple examples in this manuscript demonstrate that AI models often achieve high performance within their training environments but fail to generalize across institutions due to differences in staining protocols, slide preparation, scanner technologies, and patient demographics. These domain shifts can introduce systematic bias, disproportionately impacting underrepresented populations and potentially worsening healthcare inequities if models are implemented without diverse, multi-institutional validation. In addition to technical bias, there is an ethical imperative to define accountability when AI-assisted decisions affect diagnosis, prognosis, or treatment selection. Although current AI systems primarily serve as decision-support tools, their increasing predictive capabilities raise concerns regarding liability, transparency, and informed consent, especially when model logic is opaque or subject to continuous updates. Achieving long-term clinical accountability requires robust governance frameworks that include predefined change control plans, ongoing post-deployment monitoring, auditability, and clear delineation of responsibilities among developers, institutions, and clinicians. Ethical deployment must balance innovation with patient safety, ensuring that AI augments rather than replaces pathologist judgment, while maintaining trust through interpretability, regulatory oversight, and equitable performance across diverse clinical settings. The potential impacts of recent advances and research on real-world outcomes, the feasibility and barriers of implementation

Advances in digital pathology and artificial intelligence (AI) have the potential to substantially enhance diagnostic accuracy, reproducibility, and efficiency in cancer care. Quantitative analysis of histo-morphological features, such as nuclear–cytoplasmic ratio and pleomorphism, as well as tumor heterogeneity, immune contexture, and spatial biomarker distributions, can directly inform diagnosis, grading, prognosis, and therapeutic decision-making. Integrating AI-derived pathology features with molecular and clinical data may refine treatment guidelines, improve patient stratification, optimize drug utilization, and reduce inter-observer variability. From an economic perspective, AI could reduce turnaround times, minimize repeat testing, and expand pathology services amid workforce shortages. Implementation is becoming increasingly feasible, as demonstrated by the deployment of FDA- and CE Mark-approved AI tools for diagnostic and grading tasks, but adoption remains limited due to concerns regarding uniform code of acceptance across institutions as well as infrastructure costs. Generalized protocols and universal algorithms would be useful in helping to carry forward the gains of AI/ML in digital pathology.

Pathologists are likely to shift from manual assessment toward supervisory and interpretive roles – validating AI outputs, resolving complex cases, and integrating results into clinical context. Anticipated gains include improved diagnostic precision, reduced inter-observer variability, faster turnaround times, and access to prognostic and predictive insights that exceed human capability. The primary loss may be the reliance on purely subjective visual assessment as the diagnostic standard. Notably, AI is expected to serve as an adjunct, enhancing rather than diminishing the scope of inter-observer variation, and ultimately improving patient management and outcomes.

Key areas for improvement and current limitations restricting progress are examined

Algorithms trained on limited datasets frequently underperform in new geographic or institutional contexts due to staining variability, scanner differences, and population bias. Technical challenges encompass the computational demands of processing gigapixel whole-slide images and the limited infrastructure available in resource-constrained settings. Methodologically, reliance on weak labels or limited annotations continues to restrict biological interpretability. Enhancing attention maps, Grad-CAM, and biologically relevant validation is essential for building trust and achieving regulatory acceptance.

Beyond technical and methodological limitations, a key conceptual issue is the relative importance of feature–extraction–based approaches compared to emerging foundation-model–driven strategies in computational pathology. A central theme in recent literature is the evolving balance between two complementary yet often contrasting paradigms: feature-extraction–driven methods and foundation-model–based analysis. Classical feature extraction techniques, grounded in established histopathologic principles, provide high interpretability and biological transparency. By explicitly quantifying morphologic attributes such as nuclear atypia, architectural organization, and tumor microenvironment features, these methods are particularly valuable in low-data settings, rare disease contexts, and regulatory environments where explainability is essential. However, reliance on predefined features limits their capacity to capture the multidimensional complexity of tissue biology and restricts scalability across diverse datasets.

In contrast, foundation models represent a transformative shift toward large-scale, data-driven representation learning. By leveraging extensive, heterogeneous whole-slide image datasets, these models learn hierarchical, context-rich features that extend beyond human-defined descriptors, enabling superior performance across a wide range of diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive tasks. However, significant challenges persist, including limited interpretability, susceptibility to dataset bias, high computational requirements, and sensitivity to domain shifts such as staining variability and institutional heterogeneity. These issues directly affect clinical generalizability.

It is anticipated that the field will not converge on a single dominant paradigm but will instead move toward an integrative framework that combines the strengths of both approaches. Feature extraction methods are expected to remain critical for anchoring AI outputs in biologically interpretable and clinically validated parameters, while foundation models will facilitate scalability, adaptability, and cross-domain generalization. Hybrid strategies that incorporate interpretable feature engineering, foundation model embeddings, and multimodal integration are likely to define the next phase of computational pathology. This convergence will be essential for developing robust, generalizable, and clinically trustworthy AI systems that bridge the gap between technical innovation and real-world implementation.

Is there a definitive endpoint?

In the present state of research, we believe that there is no clear, definitive endpoint in sight; rather, progress is expected to be iterative and adaptive. As biological knowledge, computational methods, and clinical data streams evolve, AI systems will continuously be refined, retrained, and expanded to address new clinical questions. Continued development of foundation models, transformer-based architectures, and multimodal learning frameworks could enable robust biomarker discovery, survival prediction, and treatment response modeling. AI may uncover previously unrecognized tissue-level correlates of disease biology and enable precise, individualized prognostication.

Summary of expert opinion

Over the next 5 to 10 years, digital pathology is expected to implement fully integrated, AI-assisted workflows. Routine tasks such as tumor grading, biomarker quantification, and quality control will be largely automated, leading to faster, more consistent, and more accurate diagnostics while reducing inter-observer variability. Pathologists will take on supervisory roles, validating AI-generated results and advancing the field beyond traditional visual assessments.
