Exploring the ethical use of race in cancer research
Authors: Kersten J Pierre, Catherine Diop Chalmers, Chineme Amechi, Jedeiah Dickerson, Dagmawi Lulseged, Margie D Dixon, Rebecca D Pentz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo determine if race was used biologically, socially, or both ways in oncology cooperative group trials.BackgroundThe National Institute of Health defines race as a social construct that groups people based on physical appearance, social factors, and cultural background. While the use of race may no longer be as problematic as it once was, race is still sometimes misused in scientific research. This misuse can contribute to the belief that there is a biological difference between racial groups, warranting unequal and unjust treatment.MethodWe analyzed 32 research articles from 2002 to 2011 and 100 articles from 2016 to 2023 conducted by seven prominent cooperative cancer groups—NCORP, NRG, ECOG, Alliance, SWOG, COG, and ACRIN—to describe how race is used either as a socially, biologically, or both.ResultsEleven of the 32 early articles and 62 of the 100 later articles used race to answer the question posed or the hypothesis. 6/11(55%) early articles and 38/62(61%) later articles used race as a social construct, 2/11(18%) and 4/62(6%) used race biologically, and 3/11(27%) and 11/62(18%) used race both ways. The remaining 9(15%) of the later articles did not use race biologically or socially and were labeled Neither.Implications and ConclusionScientific articles should use race as a social construct. Although most articles used race appropriately, the few that did not or were unclear show that there needs to be a greater awareness of the impact of language about race in scientific studies. Correctly using race is crucial to ethical research.Keywords: ethics, race, social construct, oncology research, oncology cooperative groups

The fact that 45% (2002-2011) and 24% (2016-2023) of the articles analyzed used race either biologically or ambiguously as both social and biological demonstrates the need for investigators to think carefully about how they are using “race” and provide a clear definition, so that the research results are not ambiguous as to whether results attributed to race are due to ancestry, social determinants of health, or other factors, thereby avoiding an unethical use of race, which can contribute to disparities.

Introduction

A pressing issue in clinical research is the lack of standardization of the term “race.” According to Merriam-Webster, race is defined as “any one of the groups that humans are often divided into based on physical traits regarded as common among people of shared ancestry.”1 The genomic evidence from the Human Genome Project, which identified that all human beings were 99.9% identical at the DNA level, debunks the idea that race is a biological attribute.2 Rather, it is a social construct, and has sometimes been used to reify social, political, and economic hierarchies in a given society.3 The ambiguity of the idea of race is also reflected in the dynamism of racial classifications over time—particularly in the United States. Irish, Italian, and Jewish immigrants in the United States were not considered white from the late nineteenth century to the early twentieth century.4

Clinical research and medical science investigators pride themselves on their adherence to and search for objective truths and facts. Yet, they have actively contributed to medicalization and the misuse of the imprecise term “race.”5 Vyas et al. compiled a list of race corrections currently used in clinical algorithms. One example is the STONE score, which is used to predict the likelihood of a patient with flank pain developing ureteral stones.6 This is a 13-point scale, with a higher score indicating higher risk. Three points are added if a patient is non-Black, which means that Black patients often have lower STONE scores, which could mean clinicians may be less likely to use more “aggressive evaluations” and treatments for Black patients. The Vyas et al. paper draws attention to the pressing need for consensus on defining, standardizing, and utilizing race in research clearly and ethically as a social construct.

Proponents of precision medicine have shown that when we take the genome, social determinants of health (SDOH), and culture of an individual or population into account, we get a more precise explanation of how physiology, environment, and social forces interact to create racialized disparities in patient health and treatment outcomes.7,8 Even focusing on disparities by race may not be the best approach in the long run given the growing racial heterogeneity.

Conversely, proponents of considering race in medical research cite data showing that there is evidence of differences in physiology that are associated with race. This includes, for example, different responses to some treatments. It is difficult to determine whether these differences are based on ancestry or social determinants of health.9 In recognition of such examples, some scientists prefer to use the term “ancestry,” which recognizes how the “geographical origins” of one’s ancestors could explain specific molecular and physiological markers and phenotypes. While the use of ancestry-based genetic testing for cancer treatments is not widely available, there have been strides made to use genomic markers to evaluate seemingly racialized disease mortality outcomes. Using genomics is helpful as it acknowledges the genetic diversity between populations of people lumped together in one racial group. Blackman et al. investigated differences in “genetic ancestry informative markers” between study participants in an all-Black participant cohort.8 The results of the study found that Black people born in the United States were more likely to be heavy smokers and had differences in metabolization. Differences in ancestry informative markers across three varying ancestral Black population groups were found. In this study, by using ancestry and culture to identify divergences between populations within a given racial group, an avenue has been created for discourse about personalized interventions for genotoxicity associated with tobacco use and secondhand smoke exposure, informed by ancestry and not just race. race, as a blanket term in research, assumes biological homogeneity in racial groups, which is not the case.

The National Institutes of Health (NIH) requires the collection of race and ethnicity for grant-funded clinical trials, primarily to ensure that the results are generalizable.10 Although the NIH is clear that race is not a biological category, it can mistakenly be interpreted as such, leading to confusion and misunderstandings about research results.11

The goal of our study was to analyze research articles from oncology cooperative groups (NCORP, NRG, ECOG, Alliance, SWOG, COG, and ACRIN) to determine if the term “race” was used biologically, socially, or as a mixture of both biological and social use. To our knowledge, reports on the use of race have not focused on oncology research. This analysis is critical to understanding how race has been used within oncology literature so that proper assessments and interventions can be implemented for the appropriate use of race. Additionally, this review can potentially reduce the reinforcement of inaccurate ideas that position race as a biological construct.

Methods

In 2011, the Department of Health and Human Services published its Implementation Guidance on Data Collection Standards for race, Ethnicity, Sex, Primary Language, and Disability Status.12 These guidelines were updated by the Health and Human Services Office of Minority Health in 2021. To get a good perspective on the use of “race” before and after the guidelines, we analyzed articles from 2002 to 2011 and then from 2016 to 2023, halfway between the report’s publication year and the year the report was last updated. Additionally, experts at our institution suggested that analyzing cooperative group articles would make our research comprehensive and allow us to understand how race is currently used in oncologic research. We, therefore, searched PubMed for articles published by the seven oncology cooperative groups (NCORP, NRG, ECOG, Alliance, SWOG, COG, and ACRIN). The search words used were: “race,” “cancer,” and then, the abbreviation for the cooperative group For the period 2002 to 2011, 34 articles were found and for the period 2016 to 2023, 107 articles were found. See Figures 1 and 2 for the breakdown by cooperative group and by the number that used race to answer the question posed or the hypothesis, which were then carefully analyzed. The articles that were analyzed are listed in Supplementary Data S1. A double coding methodology was used with two coders analyzing the articles individually, determining if the article used “race biologically, socially, or mixed-use” (both biologically and socially) based on the article’s content. Articles that referred to the utilization of race as a proxy for observed genetic differences were determined to have utilized race biologically. For example, if an article mentioned or suggested that specific genetic differences have led to a health disparity resulting from racial differences within a population, the coders assigned the article a “biological” label. Articles that referred to social determinants of health to explain observed racial disparities were determined to have utilized race socially. For example, an article suggesting or mentioning that differences in access to healthcare or sociodemographic factors account for health disparities was assigned a “social” label by the coders. Articles observed using race both biologically and socially were labeled “Mixed use.” Any articles that did not use race either biologically or socially and assigned a “Neither” label by the coders. In the articles classified as Neither race was rarely mentioned in the article, was not defined and did not factor into the conclusion.

Results

Only 11 of the 32 early articles and 62 of the 100 later articles used race to answer either the question posed or the hypothesis and were further analyzed. Six out of the 11 early articles (55%) and 38 of the 62 later articles (61%) used race as a social construct. Two of the 11 (18%) and 4 of the 62 (6%) used race biologically, and 3 of the 11 (27%) and 11 of the 62 (18%) used race both ways. The remaining 9(15%) of the later articles did not use race biologically or socially and were labeled Neither. Table 1 and Figures 3 and 4 display these results.

The social use articles recognized the causative role of social determinants of health in racial health disparities. These articles also emphasized that race was determined through self-report measures and viewed as a sociodemographic variable. For example, one article called for investigations into social determinants of health and other demographics to explain the racial disparity in survival between non-Hispanic black and non-Hispanic white patients with uterine or ovarian carcinosarcomas. This article focused on social determinants of health and found that these social factors could explain racial disparities.

The biological use articles utilized race to approximate genetic differences and stated the possibility of racial biological differences. For example, in one study suggesting a correlation between vitamin D deficiency and pancreatic cancer, the authors report that the greater prevalence of vitamin D deficiency in black patients with pancreatic cancer is consistent with other studies, likely due to increased UVB radiation. The authors then state that pancreatic cancer is one of several cancers where an increased risk among “blacks” was not attributable to socioeconomic status, cancer stage at diagnosis, or treatment. Here race is treated as biologically meaningful, as the study links Blackness directly to melanin levels and presents Black patients as physiologically homogeneous. It then rules out social determinants of health and their proximal outcomes as explanations for the racial disparity in pancreatic cancer outcomes, without offering alternative explanations. In this context, the omission invites a biological interpretation. While not the most overt example of racial biologization, this framing illustrates how invoking “Black” as a uniform biological category, without critically engaging race as a social construct, can implicitly support a biological understanding of race.

Similarly, the mixed-use articles viewed race as a genetic proxy factor rather than seeing that race is best suited to be a proxy for social inequalities. For example, one article stated that research has shown there are differences in biology, access-to-care, socioeconomics, and treatment that explain the disparities in prostate cancer survival. It then goes on to argue that differences in the immune systems and in gene expression between Blacks and Caucasians play a part as well. This study inconsistently used race, ancestry, and ethnicity when explaining treatment outcomes. However, there was some acknowledgment of race as a social construct, so it was deemed mixed-use. Within the mixed-use articles, race, ethnicity, and ancestry were often misused interchangeably without providing any precise definition.

While race and ethnicity are determined by society and are unfixed, we would like to share appropriate definitions based on the definitions provided by the Federal Office of Management and Budget.13 Race is a socio-political construct based on physical attributes. Ethnicity is also a social construct, but instead, it focuses on shared culture and may reflect geographic origin. Lu et al explain that ancestry is based on lineage and can be categorized as genetic, geographical, or genealogical ancestry.14 Genealogical ancestry encompasses identifiable ancestors in your family tree and is what most people often think about when considering ancestry. Genetic ancestry refers to the inheritance of material composing one’s genome. Geographical ancestry refers to similarity in the genomes of continental groups. Few of the analyzed articles used ancestry as a variable, despite cases where ancestry is more appropriate than race, specifically when comparing ethnic groups.

Discussion

Overall, our findings suggest that while using race as a demographic variable can aid in evaluating health disparities between racial groups, it is essential that race be clearly defined within a study and acknowledged as a social construct. Failure to properly define race in medical and scientific literature can lead to misunderstanding, misinterpretation, and faulty assumptions regarding the author’s intended purpose. Additionally, how race is utilized in terms of biological versus social use and its value to a study should be explicitly stated. The way that race is collected should also be included in this statement. Race can be self-reported or ascertained by the researcher. A helpful approach for researchers could be asking themselves and their teams, “Why is race being utilized, and how will they define and measure race?” We suggest using DASH—Define race, state whether you will be discussing participants Ancestry, or Social Determinants of Health and finally what your Hypothesis is.

Many of the articles analyzed failed to consider the influence of social determinants of health on a person, community, or population’s health. Such social determinants of health include income level, education level, environmental racism, access to quality healthcare, housing, and food security. These determinants show us how the same social structures in which the construct of race has evolved have produced disparities in disease incidence and health outcomes. Being of a historically marginalized race often adversely affects one’s socio-economic status, which consequently limits one’s access to quality food, education, medical insurance, healthcare, and shelter. All of the social determinants of health, including low socioeconomic status, racism, lack of access to quality healthcare, safe housing and food, place Black and other non-White patients at a disadvantage when it comes to navigating the treatment of diseases like cancer. Lack of access to quality care also inhibits one from utilizing optimal early detection and preventative cancer services.

Furthermore, the use of race can also be appropriate when discussing a group disproportionately affected by social determinants. These social determinants can cause stress, leading to DNA methylation and other epigenetic changes. Using race in this manner requires authors to thoroughly discuss the factors and social constructs that ultimately contribute to epigenetic changes. Rather than using broad racial categorizations to justify certain health discrepancies between various groups, focusing on clinical factors specific to each individual can be more effective. For example, focusing on personal diagnoses of health conditions relevant to the condition of interest instead of race or ethnicity can lead to less generalization, as in the case of a pigment scale when looking at vitamin D deficiencies.

We recognize that considering factors such as social determinants of health or genetic ancestry instead of race can be both impractical and outside the scope of many trials. In fact, it is necessary that authors report on patient differences such as race. Doing so allows both the authors and readers to identify disparities found in results that stem from social inequities and racism. Therefore, in instances where access to genetic ancestry is impractical, it is crucial that authors at least utilize language that reinforces the presence of social determinants of health and social inequities when explaining their results. For instance, in a trial of drug X for disease Y, with 100 patients, of whom 20 have black skin and self-identify themselves as Black or AA, we recommend that the researcher still group and categorize the 20 patients in the way that they have self-identified, report on any differences found in the study’s results, and clearly state that “these results may indicate or be the result of social inequities present in healthcare” in one way or another. This way, the presence of social inequities is acknowledged in a way that does not lead readers or other researchers to assume that results are due to biological differences.

Race should also be avoided as a proxy for genetic ancestry if more precise measurements are available. As with race, stating how genetic ancestry will be used, measured, and defined is essential for transparency and clarity. When using genetic ancestry to analyze genetic variants, it is necessary to be specific when discussing the ancestry referenced. These methods can lead the scientific community toward a race-conscious research approach and practice.

Conclusion

Ultimately, the findings presented in this article highlight how race can be misused despite the research being well executed and published in high-quality impact journals. While most of the analyzed articles (60%) utilized race appropriately as a social construct, the articles that used race inappropriately or ambiguously demonstrate the need for clear definitions and reasoning behind including race in scientific research and literature. Additionally, more significant consideration must be given to the impact on a community when using race in scientific work. We hope that the data and our suggestions on how to use race appropriately will lead to the implementation of strict guidelines and criteria for any research that utilizes race in academic journals and inspire greater reflection on our collective use of race as a scientific community.
