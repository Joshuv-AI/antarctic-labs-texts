The role of mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo1 in knee osteoarthritis inflammation
Authors: Dingchang He, Xin Liu, Wenhao Yang, Taiyuan Guan, Guoyou Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMechanosensitive ion channel Piezo1 is known to mediate a variety of inflammatory pathways and is also involved in the occurrence and development of many orthopedic diseases. Although its role in the inflammatory mechanism of knee osteoarthritis (KOA) has been reported, a systematic explanation is yet to be seen. This article aims to summarize the role of inflammatory responses in the pathogenesis of KOA and elucidate the mechanism by which the Piezo1-mediated inflammatory response contributes to the pathogenesis of KOA, providing a theoretical basis for the prevention and treatment of knee osteoarthritis. The results indicate that in the mechanism leading to knee osteoarthritis, Piezo1 can mediate the inflammatory response through chondrocytes and synovial cells, participating in the pathological progression of KOA. Piezo1 has the potential to become a new target for the prevention and treatment of this disease. Additionally, as pain is one of the most severe manifestations in KOA patients, the inflammatory response mediated by Piezo1, which causes the release of inflammatory mediators and pro-inflammatory factors leading to pain, can be further explored.KEYWORDS: Osteoarthritis, Piezo1, inflammatory response, chondrocytes, synovial cells, pain

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a degenerative disease of the knee joint that severely affects the quality of life of patients, imposing a significant burden on both individuals and society [1]. KOA not only causes joint pain, deformity, and dysfunction [2,3], but studies have also shown that it significantly increases the risk of cardiovascular events, lower limb deep vein thrombosis, and all-cause mortality [4–7]. According to the literature, there are currently more than 300 million KOA patients worldwide [8]. KOA has had a tremendous impact on our country and the world, as it not only increases the economic burden on society but also inflicts significant pain on patients, due to the accelerated growth of the elderly population [9]. Moreover, with the continuous exacerbation of aging, there is a trend of increasing prevalence of KOA [10]. The occurrence of KOA is believed to be caused by a variety of factors through complex interactions. These factors include joint integrity, joint load, genetic susceptibility, local inflammation, and the status of the muscles and fat around the knee joint [11,12]. Currently, there are multiple explanations for the pathogenesis of KOA, including metabolic inflammation, chondrocyte apoptosis and autophagy, synovial inflammation, and cytokine theories [13–15]. Therefore, elucidating the pathogenesis of KOA can provide a solid theoretical foundation for clinical prevention and treatment of the disease, which is crucial for patients and society as a whole.

The Piezo1 mechanosensitive ion channel was first identified in 2010 in the mouse neuroblastoma cell line Neuro2A and its trimeric propeller structure was elucidated by GE [16,17] and others. The Piezo1 protein is composed of 2,521 amino acids in humans and 2,547 amino acids in mice. Under cryo-electron microscopy, the mouse Piezo1 protein forms a homotrimeric structure resembling a “three-bladed propeller,” with each monomer consisting of 38 transmembrane helices that together create 114 transmembrane domains, spatially arranging into a “nanobowl” shape. The N-terminus of each protein monomer contains 36 transmembrane helices, known as peripheral helices (PHs), which are organized into 9 repeating transmembrane helical units (THUs; also referred to as Piezo repeats) with each unit comprising 4 transmembrane segments, forming the “propeller blades” that extend outward. The remaining 2 transmembrane helices at the C-terminus of the protein monomer, the outer helix (OH) and the inner helix (IH), participate in the trimerization to form a pore for ion entry into the cell (Figure 1) [16,18,19]. Structurally, Piezo1 can be primarily divided into the N-terminal “propeller blade” module, the C-terminal central pore module, the “beam” structure that serves as a connector, and the “anchor” region. The central pore module at the C-terminus includes the OH, IH, the intracellular C-terminal domain (CTD), and the extracellular “cap”-like domain (C-terminal extracellular domain, CED; also known as Cap), which are connected in the sequence OH-Cap-IH-CTD. The “beam” structure acts as a scaffold, supporting the “propeller blades” and connecting them to the C-terminal domain. The “anchor” region inserts between THU9 (i.e. repeat A) and OH, linking the peripheral “propeller blades” to the central pore module in the sequence PHs-Anchor-OH-Cap-IH-CED [16,18–20].

Structure and regulation of Piezo1 channel.

Piezo1 can detect mechanical stimuli from the extracellular environment and convert them into biological electrical signals, thereby activating cellular signaling pathways and influencing the life cycle of cells. In recent years, progress has been made in the study of Piezo1 in bone-related research. The KOA has complex pathophysiological characteristics. Although Piezo1 is associated with both KOA and its inflammatory response, the exact mechanism by which Piezo1 contributes to the onset of inflammation in KOA is still unclear. Additionally, changes in mechanical stress are crucial for KOA, but it is uncertain whether these changes are intrinsically linked to inflammation. Therefore, we will conduct an in-depth analysis of Piezo1’s role in KOA based on the inflammatory response.

The functional role of Piezo1 in the KOA

Articular cartilage is an avascular and innervated tissue capable of withstanding a variety of loading types, including compression, tension, shear, and hydrostatic pressure. Chondrocytes, the primary cellular constituents of cartilage, maintain the dynamic equilibrium of the extracellular matrix (ECM) by responding to mechanical stresses primarily propagated through the ECM [21]. The ion channels PIEZO1, PIEZO2, and TRPV4 are responsible for the mechanosensory function of chondrocytes. In particular, PIEZO channels are implicated in the pathophysiological remodeling of cartilage in response to hyperphysiological loading, as well as in the promotion of aging and apoptosis [22]. Variations in mechanical loading can modulate the activity of PIEZO channels in chondrocytes. Studies indicate that an average indentation of 256 nm is required to induce a Ca2+ influx in chondrocytes, and this response is significantly attenuated following the knockdown of the Piezo1 gene [23]. Under cyclic tensile strains ranging from 3% to 18%, primary mouse chondrocytes exhibit an increase in PIEZO1 expression, concomitant with an enhanced Ca2+ influx [24]. Increasing substrate stiffness reduces PIEZO1 expression [25,26], and compressing porcine chondrocytes to deformations greater than 50% promotes Ca2+ influx. PIEZO1 expression increases Ca2+ transients and loosens F-actin, thereby reducing the Young’s modulus of the cells [27]. Inhibition of PIEZO1 with GsMTx4 or PIEZO1-specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) abolishes the F-actin loosening phenomenon [27]. These findings suggest that PIEZO1 facilitates the development of OA by increasing Ca2+ transients and loosening the actin cytoskeleton.

Research indicates that PIEZO1 May be associated with chondrocyte aging. Knockdown of PIEZO1-related genes reduces the expression of aging-associated genes, reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, and intracellular Ca2+ concentration [28]. Treatment with Yoda1 increases the expression of aging-associated genes, and this effect is inhibited by chelation of intracellular Ca2+, highlighting the importance of Ca2+ signaling in the progression of aging [28]. PIEZO1 influences chondrocyte apoptosis by regulating intracellular Ca2+ concentration. Studies show that urocortin-1 (UCN) mitigates cell death and Ca2+ overload induced by Yoda1 or mechanical loading via PIEZO1 activation [29,30]. Furthermore, activation of PIEZO1 can elicit an inflammatory response, with Ptgs2, Ccn2, and Rcn2 potentially acting as downstream genes [31–33].

In summary, PIEZO1 is closely associated with chondrocytes and exerts its effects through multiple processes including inflammation, aging, and apoptosis. The inflammatory response plays a key role in the pathogenesis of KOA

The inflammatory response is involved in nearly all physiological and pathological processes in the human body [34]. Inflammation is mediated by inflammatory factors, which are important components of the body’s innate immune response. These factors can be categorized based on their origin into interleukins, tumor necrosis factors, and interferons, among others. According to their roles in the inflammatory response, they can be further divided into pro-inflammatory factors and anti-inflammatory factors. The inflammatory response impacts various aspects of human life and activities, and KOA is particularly sensitive to this response. Studies have shown that inflammatory factors and pathways related to inflammation play a significant role in the physiological and pathological processes of KOA [35]. KOA is a disease that affects the entire joint, involving multiple tissues such as cartilage, subchondral bone, and synovium in its development and progression.

In KOA, the destruction of chondrocytes is the most common pathological change. Chondrocytes are the only cells present in cartilage and, under healthy conditions, they maintain the balance within the cartilage microenvironment [36]. Under various external injury stimuli, inflammation is triggered, which typically activates chondrocytes while reducing their numbers. This leads directly to an imbalance in the regulatory effects of chondrocytes. Further activation of chondrocytes promotes the production of pro-inflammatory mediators and matrix metalloproteinases [37], resulting in irreversible cartilage damage and a vicious cycle of inflammation and degradation. As classic inflammatory factors, IL-6 and IL-1β can lead to cartilage destruction through a variety of pathways. Studies have found that IL-6 and IL-1β have a multifaceted and critical impact on the occurrence and development of KOA [38,39]. They are positively correlated with the severity of KOA and the content of cartilage metalloproteins, enhancing the degradation and destruction of cartilage by metalloproteins. Tumor Necrosis Factor-α (TNF-α) is another inflammatory factor involved in the pathogenesis of KOA. Studies have shown that TNF-α is significantly upregulated in KOA [40]. Furthermore, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α can all induce the production of MMPs (matrix metalloproteinases) and ADAMTS (a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs), which can lead to further destruction of chondrocytes. Additionally, NF-κB (Nuclear Factor kappa-B) acts as a pleiotropic transcription factor, regulating the expression of genes for various inflammatory factors in the human body [41]. The NF-κB signaling pathway, as one of the primary pathways in cartilage, is crucial during the course of KOA [42]. In addition, the activation of signaling pathways such as STAT3, TLR4, and PI3K-Akt can recruit corresponding downstream molecules, thereby inducing high expression of inflammatory factors such as IL-1β and TNF-α [42]. Therefore, effectively inhibiting the inflammatory factors during the course of KOA can alleviate both local and systemic inflammatory responses, protecting articular cartilage and promoting tissue repair.

Additionally, synovitis plays a significant role in the diagnosis and treatment of KOA [43]. The homeostasis of the intra-articular environment is closely related to synovial cells [44]. As an important tissue for maintaining the inflammatory homeostasis of the knee joint, synovial cells are a crucial component of the regulatory system for the inflammatory response in the knee. When cartilage fragments fall into the joint and come into contact with the synovium, synovial cells recognize them as foreign substances and respond by producing inflammatory mediators found in the synovial fluid [45]. Simultaneously, when stimulated, the synovium proliferates and secretes synovial fluid, among other mediators. These substances can also induce synovial angiogenesis and increase the synthesis of inflammatory cytokines and matrix proteins by the synovial cells themselves. Numerous studies [46,47] have shown that the activation of fibroblast-like synovial cells can lead to osteoarthritis. NI Z [48] and others have confirmed that once activated, synovial cells can further mediate the onset of joint inflammation through exosomes. When synovial cells are damaged, they can disrupt the intra-articular environment of the knee joint, leading to further deterioration of the inflammatory response and accelerating the progression of knee osteoarthritis. Therefore, the inflammatory activation of synovial cells not only causes a disturbance of the knee joint homeostasis but also accelerates the progression of knee osteoarthritis.

The role of Piezo1-mediated inflammatory response in KOA

Piezo1 functions as a mechanosensor in the skeletal system, playing a physiological role in regulating the dynamic balance of bone [49]. Piezo1 influences the balance between osteoblasts and osteoclasts by converting mechanical forces into electrical signals, which is crucial for bone growth and development [50–52]. Normal physiological mechanical stimulation is essential for the proper functioning of bones and joints. Excessive mechanical loading on bones can lead to inflammation and degenerative changes. In osteoarthritis, Piezo1 expression in chondrocytes is upregulated under excessive mechanical stress, leading to an influx of Ca2+ and activation of inflammatory responses through pathways such as the Nk-β signaling pathway, which can further result in chondrocyte apoptosis [26]. Several studies have demonstrated that the knockdown of the Piezo1 gene in P5 chondrocytes results in a reduction of intracellular Ca2+ and reactive oxygen species (ROS) concentrations, which in turn promotes cell proliferation and decreases the expression of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) related genes, thus mitigating cellular senescence. Activation of Piezo1 with Yoda1 inhibits SASP, while concurrently increasing intracellular Ca2+ and ROS concentrations. This effect can be reversed by the intervention of BAPTA-AM, a Ca2+ chelator [28]. Furthermore, Liu, Y, et al. have further corroborated that Piezo1 activation promotes the expression of interleukin-6 (IL-6) through the p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) and nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling pathways [53].

Additionally, the Piezo1 channel can mediate downstream signaling molecules such as MAPK/ERK5 and MAPK/ERK1/2, causing excessive apoptosis of chondrocytes under high mechanical stress [54]. Studies have shown that inflammatory factors such as IL-1β play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of OA, and IL-1β can promote the expression of Piezo1 [55]. Inhibiting Piezo1 expression can effectively alleviate IL-1β-induced chondrocyte apoptosis and inhibit chondrocyte autophagy, thus slowing the progression of KOA. After chondrocyte apoptosis, cellular contents and debris can stimulate the intra-articular environment, producing a large number of reactive oxygen species and inflammatory mediators (such as IL-1β, TNFα, PE, etc.), which further mediate the inflammatory response, damage new cartilage tissue, and cartilage blood vessels. The loss of chondrocytes and increased chronic inflammation can increase joint fluid volume, applying pressure to both intra-articular and extra-articular structures, further activating mechanoreceptors and damage receptors, and forming a vicious cycle [56]. Piezo1-mediated inflammatory response and cartilage degeneration

Piezo1 is widely present in mammalian cells, and when cells are subjected to mechanical stimulation from the external environment, Piezo1 affects their proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [57]. Studies have shown that when the chondrocyte membrane receives mechanical stimulation, the expression of Piezo1 increases and mediates the formation of endogenous currents in the chondrocyte membrane, indicating the role of Piezo1 in the mechanical conduction of chondrocytes [23]. Additionally, the “OARSI Guidelines for the Non-Surgical Management of Knee Osteoarthritis” consider abnormal mechanical stress to be a high-risk factor for the onset of KOA [58]. Recently, the relationship between Piezo1 and various inflammations has also received increasing attention [59,60], and mechanical stimulation can activate innate immune cells and trigger inflammatory responses through Piezo1 [61,62]. Furthermore, inflammatory signals can also sensitize Piezo1 proteins in articular chondrocytes, making them a pathogenic feedforward mechanism in osteoarthritis [27]. Piezo1 is a mechanically sensitive, nonselective cation channel primarily permeable to Ca2+ ions. Increased expression and enhanced function of Piezo1 can raise intracellular calcium ion concentrations, sensitize chondrocytes to mechanical stress, and accelerate apoptosis. Studies by Whasil [27] and others have reported that Piezo1 expression is mainly determined by p38MAP-kinase and transcription factors such as CREBP1, HNF4, and ATF2 that directly bind to the Piezo1 promoter. It has also been reported that inflammatory factor IL-1α can enhance Piezo1 expression in chondrocytes, leading to a influx of calcium ions, which results in the thinning of F-actin proteins and reduces the mechanical rigidity of chondrocytes. However, the inhibitor GsMTx4 is capable of suppressing this process. Studies have found [63] that Piezo1 can mediate the activation of the JAK2 protein to trigger the inflammatory response, thereby accelerating cell apoptosis, and also mediate excessive apoptosis of cells through the ERK5 signaling pathway. Additionally, there are reports suggesting that in nucleus pulposus cells, Piezo1 May mediate inflammation by activating the NLRP3 inflammasome to accelerate the production and maturation of IL-1β [64]. On the other hand, Zhao [59] suggest that Piezo1 can enhance adipocyte plasticity to suppress the inflammatory response. In summary, based on these feedforward mechanisms, it is reasonable to specifically target and block Piezo1-mediated inflammation in chondrocytes to slow the progression of KOA.

Piezo1 and knee osteoarthritis synovitis

Studies have shown that synovitis can persist throughout the course of KOA. Even before significant changes are detected in imaging examinations, arthroscopic inspection has revealed a strong association between synovitis and minimal cartilage defects in the medial compartment of the joint, indicating that this condition is not confined to the late stages of the disease [65,66]. Mechanical forces are closely related to the onset and progression of osteoarthritis, with reports in the literature suggesting that mechanical stimulation can significantly promote the proliferation of synovial cells. Abnormal mechanical stress can exacerbate synovial macrophage apoptosis and accelerate the progression of knee osteoarthritis through the Piezo1 ion channel [67,68]. He S, et al. [69] discovered that mechanical stimulation interventions significantly increase the expression level of Piezo1 mRNA in synovial cells, and there is a positive correlation between the duration of mechanical stimulation and the expression level of Piezo1 mRNA in synovial cells, as well as the proliferation rate of these cells. Additionally, the study used siRNA technology to inhibit Piezo1, and the results indicated that siRNA-Piezo1 can suppress the proliferation of synovial cells. The research also confirmed that the MAPK/ERK1/2 signaling pathway is downstream of Piezo1 and is closely related to the proliferation of synovial cells and the expression of inflammatory factors. In a separate study, Qu X, et al. [70] found that overexpression of Piezo1 protein can promote apoptosis in synovial cells, and silencing Piezo1 can reduce intracellular calcium ion release. After introducing the ERK5 inhibitor BIX02188 into the same cells to inhibit ERK5 protein expression, the calcium ion content was further reduced, suggesting that Piezo1 May mediate apoptosis in synovial cells through the MAPK/ERK5 signaling pathway. The correlation between the duration and magnitude of mechanical loading and the expression of Piezo1 in chondrocytes has been investigated. Studies by Savadipour A et al. [71] have indicated that the interaction between Piezo1 and Piezo2 is dependent on the amplitude of mechanical loading. Under atomic force microscopy (AFM) loads of 100, 300, and 500 nN, differences in Piezo1 expression were observed. Concurrently, research by Yang et al. [72] has shown that under a loading frequency of 0.5 Hz and a cell elongation of 20%, the expression of Piezo1 varies following interventions of 0 h, 2 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h, with the highest expression observed at 24 h. In the study by He S, a loading intervention was applied with a frequency of 6 cycles per second, an amplitude of 20%, and an intensity of 50 Hz for 24 and 48 h, which may be associated with the observed promotion of synovial cell proliferation rates due to mechanical stimulation. However, it is also possible that other factors are involved, necessitating further in-depth research.

Piezo1-mediated inflammatory response and knee osteoarthritis pain

KOA is clinically characterized by common symptoms such as knee joint pain, limited mobility, functional impairment, joint deformity, and even disability. Among these, pain is the most intense and in need of relief, and it is also the primary reason for the decline in patients’ quality of life [73]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines produced by lesions in the articular cartilage, subchondral bone, and synovium are one of the main causes of KOA pain; moreover, changes in mechanical load within the joints of KOA patients can stimulate nociceptors. This long-term stimulation leading to nociceptive sensitization can also result in pain [74]. Philpott [75] have confirmed that the severity of pain and synovitis is directly proportional, with moderate to severe synovitis increasing the relative risk of persistent pain in KOA patients by 4.73 times. Synovitis leads to pain primarily through the release of biomarkers such as C1M, pro-inflammatory cytokines (especially IL-1β and IL-6), neuropeptides, and prostaglandins, which stimulate the synovial sensory nerves and induce pain [76]. Additionally, cartilage and subchondral bone can also form a self-perpetuating cycle through the release of these cytokines, stimulating and damaging the joint structure and cartilage sensory nerves, leading to inflammation around the cartilage and bone marrow edema, thus causing pain [77]. Mechanosensitive ion channels can convert mechanical stimuli into intracellular chemical signals, and recent studies have found that they can mediate nociception and participate in the transduction of noxious mechanical stimuli. Mechanosensitive ion channels can also directly stimulate sensory neurons known as nociceptors, which are associated with a variety of chronic pain conditions. Zhang M [78] observed that the ectopic expression of Piezo1 and the generation of IA currents in dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons can sensitize touch perception. Additionally, the absence of Piezo2 in neurons of mice has been found to sensitize mice to mechanical pain [78,79].

As one of the main causes of pain, inflammatory factors can stimulate the formation of new blood vessels in the cartilage and surrounding nerves, leading to pain. Recently, the role of mechanical forces in KOA has gained more attention, and it has been confirmed that mechanical stimulation mediates inflammation. However, there is a lack of sufficient evidence for the role of mechanosensitive ion channels in mediating pain mechanisms. Further research is needed to investigate how mechanosensitive ion channels mediate inflammation and subsequently cause KOA pain.

Discussion

In the study of knee osteoarthritis, research on Piezo1 has mainly focused on the mechanisms of cartilage proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis, or on the pyroptosis of synovial macrophages due to mechanical stimulation that directly leads to knee osteoarthritis [80–82]. Although there have been some reports on the inflammatory mechanisms of Piezo1 protein in the skeletal system [49,59], comprehensive articles are lacking due to the various inflammatory lesions within the knee joint that can affect the course of KOA. Currently, research on the role of Piezo1 protein in the inflammatory response phenotype of KOA is in its initial stages, and most studies have not deeply investigated the specific mechanisms involved. Moreover, the current research methods for studying the role of Piezo1-mediated mechanical stress stimulation in the pathogenesis and treatment of KOA are mainly focused on in vitro cell-based experiments and mouse animal studies, which still require further exploration through animal and clinical trials. Therefore, this review primarily summarizes the current research findings and prospectively discusses the significance of Piezo1 protein in the inflammatory response mechanisms of KOA.Interestingly, there are reports indicating that Piezo1 is not essential for normal joint development [83].

The current research on the direct correlation between Piezo1, a mechanosensitive channel protein, and KOA is relatively limited, with most studies being primarily based on basic experimental research. Clinical research in this area also requires further exploration. The research in this field is indeed in its infancy, with a scarcity of related literature, which results in certain limitations in this review. For example:1. The specific mechanisms by which Piezo1 regulates the inflammatory response in KOA are not fully understood. It is unclear how Piezo1 upregulates or suppresses the expression of specific downstream genes and proteins to exert its biological effects.2. Knee joint pain in KOA is often associated with inflammatory factors, but the role of Piezo1-mediated inflammation in KOA pain requires further research.These limitations are also the main breakthrough points and focus of future research on the inflammatory mechanisms of KOA. Additionally, due to the search strategy used for literature retrieval, which included titles, keywords, and abstracts, some valuable literature may have been missed, leading to a risk of selection bias. Furthermore, for some well-accepted definitions and conclusions, the authors have focused on citing primary literature, and some cited references may be from older publications.

Future prospects

This review highlights the scarcity of clinical research on Piezo1 in KOA and calls for further exploration by researchers in this field. Additionally, pain, one of the most severe clinical manifestations of KOA, and the inflammation mediated by mechanosensitive ion channels that contribute to KOA pain require in-depth investigation. Future research should focus on the molecular mechanisms related to inflammation in KOA, particularly those involving Piezo1. Current studies suggest that Piezo1-mediated inflammatory responses regulate chondrocytes and synovial cells, with the effects varying depending on the type, magnitude, and duration of mechanical forces, warranting further quantitative research. Furthermore, the subchondral bone is considered crucial in the progression of KOA and is closely associated with the inflammatory response, making it a potential direction for research.
