Surgery versus radiotherapy after neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy in limited-stage small cell lung cancer
Authors: Rui Zou, Ziyue Zhu, Xiao Teng, Sizhe Yu, Xiayi Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe role of surgery in limited-stage small cell lung cancer (LS-SCLC) remains controversial and has traditionally been limited to early-stage disease. This study aimed to evaluate whether neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (NIC) followed by surgery is associated with survival outcomes compared with definitive radiotherapy in patients with LS-SCLC.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study of 64 patients with stage I–IIIB LS-SCLC who received NIC between April 2020 and July 2024. Patients subsequently underwent surgery (n=23) or definitive radiotherapy (n=41) based on multidisciplinary evaluation and patient preference. The primary endpoints were overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). Survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared via log-rank tests. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression was employed to identify independent predictors of PFS.ResultsBaseline demographic, clinical, and tumor characteristics were generally comparable between groups. The objective response rate (ORR) to NIC was 91.3% in the surgery group and 85.4% in the radiotherapy group (P=0.70). Median OS was significantly longer in the surgery group (45 vs. 20 months; P=0.048; 2-year OS: 73.9% vs. 40.4%). Median PFS was also significantly prolonged after surgery (30 vs. 9.5 months; P=0.002; 2-year PFS: 55.2% vs. 23.2%). Multivariable analysis confirmed surgical treatment as an independent favorable factor for PFS [hazard ratio (HR): 0.31, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.15–0.64; P=0.001]. In exploratory subgroup analyses, the PFS benefit appeared most pronounced in patients with stage II–IIIA disease (median: 32.0 vs. 8.1 months; P=0.004).ConclusionsIn this cohort, NIC followed by surgery was associated with improved survival outcomes compared with radiotherapy. These findings suggest that surgical consolidation after effective systemic induction is a viable strategy for selected patients with LS-SCLC, particularly those with intermediate disease burden, warranting further prospective validation.Keywords: Limited-stage small cell lung cancer (LS-SCLC), neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (NIC), surgery, radiotherapy

Highlight box

Key findings

• Among patients with stage I–IIIB limited-stage small cell lung cancer (LS-SCLC) treated with neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (NIC), those undergoing surgery demonstrated longer survival than those receiving definitive radiotherapy.

What is known and what is new?

• Concurrent chemoradiotherapy has long been the standard treatment for LS-SCLC, and surgery has traditionally been restricted to very early-stage disease. The introduction of immunotherapy has improved systemic control but has not clearly defined the optimal local treatment strategy after induction therapy.

• This study compares surgery and definitive radiotherapy after a uniform NIC backbone and provides real-world evidence on local treatment selection in the immunotherapy era.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings suggest that surgery may be considered as a consolidative local treatment option after effective NIC in selected patients with LS-SCLC, warranting prospective validation.

Introduction

Limited-stage small cell lung cancer (LS-SCLC) is an aggressive malignancy characterized by rapid growth, early dissemination, and a high propensity for recurrence (1). For decades, concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CRT) has remained the standard treatment for patients with LS-SCLC who are not candidates for surgical resection, whereas surgery has traditionally been restricted to a small subset of patients with very early-stage disease (2,3). Despite initial sensitivity to cytotoxic therapy, long-term outcomes remain suboptimal, highlighting the need for improved multimodal treatment strategies (4).

Recent advances in immunotherapy have reshaped the therapeutic landscape of small cell lung cancer (SCLC) (5). The addition of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) to first-line chemotherapy has significantly improved survival in extensive-stage disease, prompting exploration of immunotherapy in earlier disease settings (6-9). More recently, the ADRIATIC trial established durvalumab consolidation following concurrent CRT as a major advance and an essential component of contemporary treatment for LS-SCLC, providing a new benchmark for survival outcomes (10,11). Nevertheless, this strategy does not alter the established local treatment paradigm, and locoregional failure remains a clinically relevant challenge.

Neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (NIC) offers a biologically distinct treatment sequence by delivering systemic therapy upfront, potentially enhancing tumor regression, eradicating micrometastatic disease, and modulating the tumor microenvironment (12). In other thoracic malignancies, this induction approach has been shown to improve resectability and deepen pathological response (13). In LS-SCLC, emerging phase II studies have demonstrated encouraging pathological response rates with perioperative immunochemotherapy, suggesting that effective systemic induction may enable surgical resection in patients previously not considered candidates for surgery (14,15). However, these studies have been limited by small sample sizes and short follow-up, and their impact on long-term survival remains uncertain (16-19).

Importantly, existing studies have largely focused on comparisons between immunochemotherapy and chemotherapy alone, rather than on the optimal choice of local consolidative therapy after induction treatment (20-23). Whether surgery confers a survival advantage over definitive radiotherapy following NIC in LS-SCLC has not been adequately addressed, particularly in patients with intermediate disease burden who fall outside traditional surgical indications.

In this context, we conducted a real-world, single-center retrospective cohort study to compare survival outcomes between surgery and definitive radiotherapy after NIC in patients with LS-SCLC. We sought to evaluate whether surgical consolidation following effective systemic induction is associated with improved long-term outcomes, thereby informing the evolving role of surgery within contemporary multimodal treatment strategies for LS-SCLC. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0959/rc).

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This single-center retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients with LS-SCLC who received NIC between April 2020 and July 2024. Limited-stage disease was defined according to the Veterans Administration Lung Study Group system and the 8th edition tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) classification (24). Eligible patients had pathologically confirmed SCLC, received NIC as initial therapy, and subsequently underwent definitive local treatment with either surgery or radiotherapy. Patients with extensive-stage disease, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status ≥2, prior systemic therapy for SCLC, or incomplete clinical data were excluded.

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. 2025 IIT No. 996). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Treatment strategy

NIC was administered every 21 days for 2–6 cycles. The chemotherapy regimen consisted of etoposide (100 mg/m2) combined with platinum-based agents, including cisplatin (75 mg/m2) or carboplatin [area under the curve (AUC) =5]. Immunotherapy included programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) or programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors, such as durvalumab, sintilimab, adebrelimab, or atezolizumab. The specific regimen and number of cycles were determined by treating physicians based on treatment response and tolerability. After completion of NIC, all patients were evaluated by a multidisciplinary tumor board. Definitive local treatment was selected based on tumor response, resectability, clinical stage, comorbidities, and patient preference, reflecting real-world clinical decision-making.

Patients assigned to surgery underwent resection with curative intent, including lobectomy or segmentectomy with systematic lymph node dissection or sampling, performed via video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) or thoracotomy. Complete resection was defined as microscopically margin-negative (R0), and perioperative variables (operative time, blood loss, hospital stay, and perioperative mortality) were recorded. Patients not selected for surgery received definitive thoracic radiotherapy using intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) or three-dimensional conformal radiotherapy (3D-CRT), with a total dose of 45–60 Gy delivered in once- or twice-daily fractions.

Outcome measures

The primary endpoints were overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). OS was defined as the time from initiation of NIC to death from any cause or last follow-up. PFS was defined as the time from NIC initiation to disease progression, recurrence, or death. Radiological response was assessed according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 (25). Patients were followed up at regular intervals (every 2–3 months in the first 2 years and every 3–6 months thereafter) with imaging and clinical evaluations, including chest computed tomography (CT), laboratory tests, and additional imaging such as brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or positron emission tomography (PET) when clinically indicated. The last follow-up date was June 2025.

Immunohistochemistry and molecular subtyping

Available pretreatment biopsy and surgical specimens were analyzed by immunohistochemistry. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections were stained for ASCL1, NEUROD1, YAP1, and POU2F3 using standard protocols. Expression was semi-quantitatively evaluated using the H-score method, with positivity defined as H-score >10. Tumors were classified according to the dominant transcription factor as SCLC-A, SCLC-N, SCLC-P, or SCLC-Y. All slides were independently reviewed by two pathologists blinded to clinical outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical variables using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test. Survival was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared by log-rank test. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to identify predictors of PFS. Variables included in the multivariable model were selected based on clinical relevance and prior clinical experience. Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0), with two-sided P<0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics, neoadjuvant treatment, and radiological response

A total of 1,211 patients with SCLC were screened (Figure 1). After screening, 64 patients with stage I–IIIB LS-SCLC who received NIC were included in the final analysis. Among them, 23 patients underwent surgery and 41 received definitive radiotherapy. Baseline characteristics, neoadjuvant treatment details, and radiological responses are summarized in Table 1. Baseline characteristics were generally comparable between groups, although the surgery cohort included more male patients and a higher prevalence of diabetes. Although stage I–II disease was more frequent in the surgery group, the overall stage distribution did not differ significantly, and most patients (75.0%) had stage III disease.

Patients received a median of four cycles of NIC, with similar treatment intensity and regimen distribution between groups. Radiological assessment according to RECIST version 1.1 demonstrated substantial tumor regression across the cohort. The median reduction in target lesion size was 61.9%, and the overall objective response rate (ORR) was 87.5%, without significant differences between treatment groups. No patients experienced disease progression during neoadjuvant therapy, and all patients proceeded to definitive local treatment.

Surgical outcomes and pathological response

Perioperative and pathological characteristics of patients undergoing surgical resection after NIC are summarized in Table 2. Among the 23 patients in the surgical cohort, lobectomy was the predominant procedure (22 patients, 95.7%), and one patient (4.3%) underwent segmentectomy. VATS was performed in 17 patients (73.9%), whereas 6 patients (26.1%) underwent open thoracotomy. R0 resection was achieved in all 23 patients (100%). The median interval between the final cycle of neoadjuvant therapy and surgery was 30 days [interquartile range (IQR), 24–35 days]. The median operative time was 141 minutes (IQR, 112–200 minutes), with a median estimated blood loss of 50 mL (IQR, 20–100 mL). The median postoperative length of hospital stay was 8 days (IQR, 6–14 days). No deaths occurred within 30 or 90 days after surgery. Pathological assessment demonstrated a complete response in 2 patients (8.7%). According to ypTNM staging, 9 patients (39.1%) achieved ypN0 status. Overall, pathological downstaging compared with the pre-treatment clinical stage was observed in 16 patients (69.6%). Final pathological staging revealed 2 patients (8.7%) with pathological complete response (pCR), 7 patients (30.4%) with stage I disease, 6 patients (26.1%) with stage II disease, and 8 patients (34.8%) with stage III disease.

Survival outcomes and prognostic factors

With a median follow-up of 39 months, survival outcomes differed between the two treatment groups (Figure 2A). The median OS was 45 months in the surgery group and 22 months in the radiotherapy group (P=0.048). The corresponding 2-year OS rates were 73.9% for patients undergoing surgery, and 40.4% for those receiving radiotherapy. PFS was also longer in the surgery group, with a median PFS of 30 months compared with 8.1 months in the radiotherapy group (P=0.002) (Figure 2B). The 2-year PFS rates were 55.2% in the surgery group and 23.2% in the radiotherapy group.

Overall survival (A) and progression-free survival (B) by local treatment modality.

To identify factors associated with disease progression, univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed for PFS (Tables 3,4). In univariable analysis, treatment modality was significantly associated with PFS [hazard ratio (HR): 0.37; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.19–0.70; P=0.002], while clinical N stage also showed a significant association. In multivariable analysis, surgical treatment remained independently associated with improved PFS (adjusted HR: 0.31; 95% CI: 0.15–0.64; P=0.001).

Exploratory subgroup analysis of PFS

Exploratory subgroup analyses were performed to assess the consistency of PFS outcomes according to disease burden, including clinical stage and nodal status (Figure 3).

Kaplan-Meier curves of progression-free survival according to local treatment modality, stratified by baseline disease burden. (A) Clinical stage II; (B) clinical stage III; (C) node-positive disease (N1–N2); (D) intermediate disease burden (stage II–IIIA). N, node.

When stratified by clinical stage, patients with stage II disease showed a numerical separation of PFS curves between treatment modalities; however, the difference did not reach statistical significance, with the median PFS not reached in the surgery group and 14.0 months in the radiotherapy group (P=0.10; Figure 3A). In contrast, among patients with stage III disease, surgical resection was associated with a longer median PFS compared with radiotherapy (14.0 vs. 7.6 months; P=0.03; Figure 3B).

Stratification by nodal status demonstrated that in patients with node-positive disease (N1–N2), surgery was associated with a longer median PFS compared with radiotherapy (17.0 vs. 7.1 months; P=0.008; Figure 3C). Given the observed patterns across clinical stage and nodal status, an exploratory analysis was further conducted in patients with intermediate disease burden, defined as stage II–IIIA disease. In this subgroup, the surgery group achieved a longer median PFS compared with the radiotherapy group (32.0 vs. 8.1 months; P=0.004; Figure 3D).

Serum biomarkers and survival outcomes

Serum neuron-specific enolase (NSE) and gastrin-releasing peptide precursor (ProGRP) levels were evaluated at baseline and after NIC to characterize biomarker dynamics and their association with survival outcomes (Figure 4). Both treatment groups demonstrated significant declines in NSE levels following neoadjuvant therapy, whereas ProGRP levels showed an overall downward trend, reaching statistical significance in patients treated with radiotherapy (Figure 4A,4B).

Patients were stratified according to established baseline biomarker thresholds. Individuals with normal baseline NSE or ProGRP levels who subsequently underwent surgery generally experienced longer PFS compared with those treated with radiotherapy, whereas elevated baseline biomarker levels were associated with shorter survival irrespective of local treatment modality (Figure S1).

An exploratory analysis using a combined biomarker classification further demonstrated that patients with normal baseline levels of both biomarkers who underwent surgery achieved the most favorable outcomes. In this subgroup, median OS and PFS were not reached, with 2-year OS and PFS rates of 88.9% and 81.8%, respectively (Figure 4C,4D). Survival in this group was longer than that observed in patients with elevated biomarkers treated with radiotherapy and in those with elevated biomarkers who underwent surgery. Outcomes were also numerically improved compared with patients with normal biomarkers treated with radiotherapy. Given the limited number of events, these findings are considered exploratory.

Molecular subtyping was performed on available tumor specimens to describe subtype distribution and changes following NIC. The analyzed samples included 8 pretreatment biopsy specimens and 23 post-treatment surgical resection specimens from the surgery group, as well as 24 pretreatment biopsy specimens from the radiotherapy group. Discussion

The standard treatment paradigm for LS-SCLC is concurrent CRT, with surgery generally restricted to a small subset of patients with very early-stage disease. In this real-world study, we observed that NIC followed by definitive surgery was associated with prolonged OS and PFS compared with definitive radiotherapy after NIC. Exploratory subgroup analyses further suggested that this association was most evident among patients with intermediate disease burden, particularly those with stage II–IIIA disease.

Surgery following NIC was performed with acceptable perioperative safety. Complete resection was achieved in all surgical patients, most procedures were performed using minimally invasive approaches, and no 30- or 90-day postoperative deaths occurred. These findings support the technical feasibility and safety of surgical consolidation after NIC.

Prior clinical trials provide important context for interpreting these findings. In the CONVERT trial, definitive CRT achieved a median OS of approximately 25.4–30 months, with 2-year OS rates of 51–56%, despite optimization of radiation dose and fractionation (4). Subsequent incorporation of immunotherapy through consolidation strategies further improved outcomes, as demonstrated in the ADRIATIC trial, which reported 2-year OS and PFS rates of 68.0% and 46.2%, respectively (10). Perioperative immunotherapy approaches have also been explored in early-phase studies, including NeoSCI and LungMate-005, showing substantial pathological tumor regression and high rates of complete or major pathological response among patients proceeding to surgery after NIC (14,15). In the present study, patients treated with NIC achieved a median OS of 45 months and a 2-year OS rate of 73.9%, with a median PFS of 30 months, whereas patients treated with radiotherapy after immunochemotherapy demonstrated survival outcomes comparable to those reported with established non-surgical approaches. Although cross-study comparisons should be interpreted with caution, these observations suggest that patients undergoing surgery after NIC may represent a feasible treatment option for selected patients.

Beyond clinical stage and treatment modality, baseline serum tumor biomarkers were associated with survival outcomes after NIC (15,23). Patients with preoperative normal baseline levels of both NSE and ProGRP experienced the most favorable OS and PFS. By comparison, patients with elevated baseline biomarker levels demonstrated shorter survival across treatment groups. Taken together, these findings indicate an association between baseline serum tumor marker status and survival outcomes after NIC, supporting a potential role for serum biomarkers in patient stratification.

Molecular subtyping analyses provided additional biological context for the observed clinical outcomes. In post-treatment surgical specimens, ASCL1- and YAP1-dominant subtypes accounted for the majority of tumors, consistent with prior reports in limited-stage disease (26,27). Moreover, longitudinal evaluation of paired pretreatment and post-treatment specimens demonstrated frequent subtype shifts following NIC, most commonly transitions toward YAP1-dominant phenotypes. Although exploratory and based on a limited number of paired samples, these observations suggest that systemic induction may be associated with dynamic phenotypic remodeling rather than static subtype distribution. When considered alongside the serum biomarker findings, molecular subtype evolution may represent a complementary biological signal associated with treatment response and subsequent clinical outcomes.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the single-center retrospective design introduces inherent selection and information bias. Although baseline characteristics were generally comparable and multivariable analyses were performed, patients selected for surgery may have had more favorable clinical conditions, such as better performance status, cardiopulmonary function, and treatment responsiveness, which could partially account for their improved survival outcomes rather than the surgical intervention alone. Second, the sample size was relatively limited, particularly in subgroup, biomarker, and molecular analyses, and these findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. Third, heterogeneity in immunotherapy regimens and treatment cycles reflects real-world clinical practice but may have influenced the observed outcomes. In addition, molecular subtyping analyses were constrained by the limited availability of paired pre- and post-treatment specimens, restricting the robustness of conclusions regarding subtype evolution.

Therefore, prospective, multicenter studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate these findings and to better define the optimal patient population for surgery following NIC.

Conclusions

In summary, this real-world analysis suggests that NIC followed by surgery is feasible in carefully selected patients with LS-SCLC. Compared with CRT alone, surgery may offer potential benefit in a subset of patients, although larger prospective studies are needed.
