Surgical outcomes of bilateral medial rectus recession in cases of acute acquired comitant esotropia
Authors: Majid Farvardin, Hadi Farvardin, Amirhossein Rajabi, Hajar Farvardin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute Acquired Comitant Esotropia (AACE) is a form of acquired esotropia that occurs in patients without significant hyperopic refractive error. This study aims to investigate the outcomes of bilateral medial rectus muscle (BMR) recession in patients diagnosed with AACE at our strabismus referral center.MethodsSurgical profiles of all AACE cases who underwent BMR recession from 2015 to 2024 were recruited. Ocular deviation was measured using the base-out breakpoint method during alternate prism cover testing. Postoperative outcomes were evaluated six weeks (short-term) and six months to five years after the surgery (long-term). Cases that experienced postoperative diplopia or a horizontal deviation greater than eight Prism Diopters (PD) were classified as surgical failures.ResultsThis study involved 118 cases of AACE, with an average age of 7.8 years. The average visual acuity was 0.07 ± 0.09 LogMAR in the right eye and 0.08 ± 0.13 LogMAR in the left eye. The spherical equivalent of cycloplegic refraction was + 0.60 ± 1.57 diopters (D) and + 0.65 ± 1.54 D in the right and left eye, respectively. Average esotropia was 43.36 PD in near fixation and 42.61 PD in distant fixation. Bilateral medial rectus recession 4 to 7 mm (based on Park’s standard table) was performed. The overall short-term and long-term success rates were 90.7% and 81.2%, respectively. Surgical failure was more frequently observed in younger patients and those without diplopia (p-values: 0.018 and 0.003). Preoperative visual acuity, refractive error, and angle of esotropia did not affect the surgical outcome.ConclusionBMR recession was an appropriate surgical option for treating AACE cases. The base-out breakpoint method proved effective for preoperative measurement of esotropia in these cases.Keywords: Acute acquired comitant esotropia, Esotropia, Strabismus, Medial rectus

Introduction

Acute Acquired Comitant Esotropia (AACE) is characterized by a sudden onset of esotropia in patients without significant hyperopic refractive error, ocular restriction, or limitation of ocular movements [1–3]. AACE was previously considered a rare form of acquired esotropia, but ophthalmologists have recently noticed a significant increase in its occurrence [2, 4–6]. The rising number of AACE cases has drawn more attention to its possible etiology, potential risk factors, and surgical and non-surgical treatment options [1–3].

Prior research has linked the recent increase in AACE cases to the growing trend of prolonged near work, especially excessive use of digital devices and smartphones among children and adolescents [1, 7]. Treatment options when facing a patient with AACE include both surgical and non-surgical interventions [2–12]. The effectiveness of non-surgical interventions, such as near-work cessation and topical cycloplegic drops (bedtime tropicamide), has been a topic of debate in previous studies [7, 12].

The surgical management of AACE has been found to produce more precise and long-lasting results compared to botulinum toxin injection into the medial rectus muscles [8, 13–15]. While strabismus surgery has been the traditional treatment of choice for AACE, there is no consensus on the best surgical technique or dosing [1, 3, 9, 16]. This study aims to investigate the postoperative outcomes and the reoperation rate of AACE cases treated at our strabismus referral center in the last 10 years. Additionally, we aim to develop a proper surgical dosing table and identify possible factors contributing to postoperative success.

Materials and methods

This study is designed as a retrospective review of the surgically treated AACE cases. Medical and surgical data of all patients who were labeled as AACE in the last ten years (from 2015 to 2024) were recruited. We conducted a re-analysis of the medical records to ensure the diagnosis of AACE by applying the following definition. AACE referred to the sudden onset of comitant esotropia in patients over one year old who previously had aligned eyes and good binocular potential. Patients diagnosed with AACE exhibited no significant hyperopia, lacked a refractive component, and did not have any limitations in ocular ductions or versions. Exclusion criteria were:

Cycloplegic refraction exceeding + 2.50 diopters (D).

Cycloplegic refraction exceeding + 2.50 diopters (D).

Accommodative component: ≥10 Prism Diopter (PD) decrease or over 50% decrease in the angle of esotropia wearing full hyperopic correction.

Accommodative component: ≥10 Prism Diopter (PD) decrease or over 50% decrease in the angle of esotropia wearing full hyperopic correction.

Incomitance: ≥5 PD change in esotropia in the side gaze compared to the primary position.

Incomitance: ≥5 PD change in esotropia in the side gaze compared to the primary position.

Divergence insufficiency and convergence excess: ≥10 PD discrepancy between far and near esotropia.

Divergence insufficiency and convergence excess: ≥10 PD discrepancy between far and near esotropia.

Any pathological condition diagnosed in the central nervous system imaging.

Any pathological condition diagnosed in the central nervous system imaging.

History of previous strabismus surgery.

History of previous strabismus surgery.

Each patient’s basic profile, including sex, age at the time of surgery, the interval between presentation to surgery, best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) of both eyes, and the spherical equivalent (SE) of the cycloplegic refraction in both eyes, was recorded. Visual acuity was measured using the Snellen E-chart at a distance of six meters in daylight conditions. The visual acuity was recorded using decimals and then converted to logMAR for further analysis. The visual acuity of preverbal children, recorded in CSM notation (Central, Steady, Maintained), was not included in the statistical analysis. Cycloplegic refractions were measured 30 to 45 min after the instillation of two drops of cyclopentolate 1%, five minutes apart. In those patients whose cyclopentolate refraction was over + 1.00 D, atropine cycloplegic refraction was performed to unmask their maximal hyperopia. These cases further underwent spectacle trial with full hyperopic correction. To ensure exclusion of partial refractive accommodative esotropia, only cases that showed less than 10 PD reduction in the amount of esotropia with full atropine refraction were labeled as AACE and included in the study.

Ocular deviation in all patients was measured using the “base-out breakpoint” method during alternate prism cover testing, conducted by the senior strabismus consultant. An alternate prism cover test was performed with a handheld prism at both near and far fixation. The prism power was gradually increased in a stepwise manner to induce fusional movements until minimal exotropia was noticed or the patient reported experiencing crossed diplopia. The highest prism power reached before either of these points was considered the “base-out breakpoint”. The base-out breakpoint was considered the preoperative angle for performing strabismus surgery based on Park’s standard Table [17].

Preoperative and postoperative horizontal deviations, the dominance of the horizontal deviation (right dominant, left dominant, or alternate deviation), and the presence or absence of diplopia were recorded from the available notes. The type of strabismus surgery was gathered from the operation sheets, and the follow-up intervals were all recorded. All surgeries were performed under general anesthesia by a single experienced strabismus surgeon (M.F.). After nasal limbal peritomy, the medial rectus muscle (MR) was hooked and secured with a double-armed 6 − 0 Vicryl suture using a locking bite at each pole. Then the muscle was disinserted and re-sutured to the sclera with the dosage determined primarily by the preoperative angle of esotropia measured by the base-out breakpoint method.

The surgical outcomes were classified as follows:

Complete success: achieving postoperative orthotropia and resolution of diplopia.

Complete success: achieving postoperative orthotropia and resolution of diplopia.

Partial success: resolution of diplopia with a postoperative horizontal deviation of up to eight PD, which could be a residual esotropia or consecutive exotropia.

Partial success: resolution of diplopia with a postoperative horizontal deviation of up to eight PD, which could be a residual esotropia or consecutive exotropia.

Surgical failure: postoperative diplopia or a horizontal deviation of over eight PD.

Surgical failure: postoperative diplopia or a horizontal deviation of over eight PD.

The follow-up intervals and details regarding reoperation for failed cases were also noted.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, New York, USA). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were reported as numbers and percentages. Fisher’s exact and chi-square tests were applied to compare categorical variables between groups. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was performed to evaluate the normality of the distribution for continuous data, with a p-value > 0.05 considered indicative of a normal distribution. Comparisons of continuous variables between groups were conducted using the Kruskal-Wallis test, with post-hoc analysis done with the Mann-Whitney U test using Bonferroni correction.

Results

Figure 1 displays the participants included in the current study. Our study included 118 AACE cases, including 86 male and 32 female individuals with an average age of 7.88 ± 4.59 years (range 2.5 to 30 years old). The average visual acuity of the right and left eyes was 0.07 ± 0.09 and 0.08 ± 0.13 Log MAR, respectively. The spherical equivalent of cycloplegic refraction was + 0.60 ± 1.57 D in the right eye (ranging from − 6.50 to + 2.50 D), and + 0.65 ± 1.54 D in the left eye (ranging from − 6.50 to + 2.50 D). The interval from esotropia presentation to surgery was 9.26 ± 11.07 months (ranging from one month to five years). Esotropia in near and far fixation was 43.36 ± 10.54 PD (ranging from 25 to 70 PD) and 42.61 ± 10.64 PD (ranging from 25 to 70 PD), respectively. Constant diplopia was reported in 27 cases (22.9%) before surgery. Esotropia was alternate in 73 cases (61.9%), mild right-side dominant in 28 patients (23.7%), and mild left-side dominant in the other 17 cases (14.4%). The demographic characteristics of AACE cases, categorized by age, are summarized in Table 1. About half of the cases (48.3%) involved elementary school children aged six to eleven years. A notable male dominance was observed across all age groups. Patients older than 11 years exhibited more myopic refractive errors and a higher percentage of preoperative diplopia, along with a lower preoperative angle of esotropia (P-values: <0.001, < 0.001, 0.045, 0.031).

An early follow-up interval of six weeks after surgery was documented in all cases, and 45 cases had further long-term follow-ups ranging from six months to five years. The average postoperative esotropia in the early follow-up was 2.62 ± 5.51 PD in the near fixation (ranging from 18 PD exotropia (XT) to 25 PD ET) and 2.48 ± 5.30 PD in distant fixation (ranging from 18 PD XT to 20 PD ET). For the 45 patients with late follow-up, the mean esotropia at the final follow-up was 2.38 ± 9.390 PD (ranging from 25 PD XT to 35 PD ET) for near fixation and 2.07 ± 8.85 PD (ranging from 25 PD XT to 30 PD ET) for far fixation. After the operations, none of our patients developed postoperative vertical deviations or torsional effects.

In the 118 reviewed cases of AACE, complete success was achieved in 68 cases (57.6%), partial success was attained in 39 cases (33.1%), and surgery was unsuccessful in 11 cases (9.3%). Long-term follow-up in 45 cases revealed that complete and partial success was achieved in 23 (51.1%) and 14 cases (31.1%), respectively. Short-term follow-up revealed that eight patients had residual esotropia over 8 PD (ranging from 10 to 25 PD), of whom four underwent second strabismus surgery (unilateral or bilateral LR resection), and the other four cases were satisfied with their postoperative results. Three cases developed consecutive exotropia (ranging from 10 to 18 PD) due to overcorrection and therefore underwent unilateral medial rectus muscle advancement. All cases, except one, achieved favorable ocular alignment (within eight PD of horizontal deviation) after the second surgery. The overall rate of short-term and long-term surgical failure after standard BMR recession in our series was 9.3% and 17.8%, respectively. We further examined the preoperative characteristics of AACE cases across three groups: complete success, partial success, and surgical failure. Our analysis showed that patients in the failure group were younger than the other groups (P-value: 0.018), and none of them exhibited preoperative diplopia (P-value: 0.003). Additionally, there were no significant differences among the groups regarding preoperative visual acuity, refractive error, angle of deviation, dominance of deviation, and the dose of BMR recession (see Table 2).

Discussion

AACE has seen a rise in research in recent years, likely due to its increased incidence [1–10, 18–23]. There are notable differences in the demographic characteristics of AACE cases based on age [18, 22, 23] and country of origin [10, 19–23]. The current study investigated the demographic characteristics of 118 AACE cases surgically treated in our strabismus referral center. Among these cases, 39% were preschool children (under 6 years old), 48.3% were primary school children (ages 6–11), and 12.7% were adolescents (12 years and older). Refractive errors were more myopic in the adolescent group compared to the other two groups. This group also exhibited a higher prevalence of diplopia as their presenting symptom, while their preoperative esotropia was significantly lower. It is important to note that children with AACE at the preschool age typically present with hyperopic refractive errors. However, they usually have a non-significant refractive error for their age and lack a refractive component, which helps differentiate their diagnosis from that of refractive accommodative ET and partially accommodative ET cases. Our findings are consistent with previous studies [10, 18–23], including the most recent study, which indicated a negative correlation between age and spherical equivalent of the involved eyes and the angle of deviation at distance [18].

The current study reported surgical outcomes of 118 cases diagnosed with acute acquired comitant esotropia. Preoperative esotropia was measured using the base-out breakpoint method, and patients were scheduled for bilateral medial rectus recession according to Park’s standard dosage table. The mentioned surgical plan led to an overall short-term success rate of 90.7%, a long-term success rate of 82.2%, and a reoperation rate of 5.9%. Eleven cases in our series did not achieve favorable ocular alignment after their first surgery; of whom eight experienced under-correction, while three developed over-correction. Previous studies have reported success rates for surgical correction in cases of AACE ranging from 66% to 98% [10, 24–29]. The high percentage of under-correction following BMR recession in AACE cases observed in some previous studies suggests the possibility of mismeasurement of the exact preoperative angle of esotropia [25, 29, 30]. To address this issue, some authors have recommended using an augmented dosage of BMR recession for AACE patients, based on Park’s standard Tables [25, 29–31]. On the other hand, other efforts have focused on accurately determining the preoperative angle of deviation [26, 32–39].

The Prism Adaptation Test (PAT) is commonly used to measure the preoperative angle of esotropia in order to achieve better postoperative outcomes [26, 33, 34]. A comparative study involving AACE cases showed that surgery guided by the results of the PAT produced better treatment outcomes compared to simple alternate prism cover testing [33]. Various methods of PAT, including short PAT, partial PAT, and long PAT, exhibited similar effectiveness in determining the angle of esodeviation [34]. The PAT method is thought to reveal a hidden level of esotropia that is significantly greater than what is identified during the initial measurement. This allows for a more accurate evaluation of the patient’s true potential for fusion. It is important to emphasize that using this method requires cooperation from both the patient and the physician, as well as additional time and potential costs [26, 33, 34, 37].

Some authors have reported that the monocular occlusion method, which was commonly used for intermittent exotropia, can also effectively reveal latent deviations in cases of acute esotropia [39]. Additionally, a recent study demonstrated that in cases of AACE, patients who underwent augmented-dose surgery based on the single Maddox rod test achieved more favorable surgical outcomes compared to those who were assessed using the conventional alternate prism cover test, particularly when there was a difference of over 5 PD in preoperative esotropia as evaluated by the two methods [36]. Another study conducted by Dai and colleagues assessed the effectiveness of the base-out recovery point method for maximizing fusional convergence and accurately measuring the preoperative angle of deviation [32]. Their study involved 22 cases categorized as AACE, all of whom were treated based on the angle determined by the preoperative base-out recovery point. Of these cases, 19 achieved orthotropia, and three individuals experienced minimal esophoria. In the current study, we used a measurement technique for determining the preoperative angle of esotropia that closely resembled the method described by Dai and colleagues [32]. This method achieved successful postoperative outcomes in over 90% of cases. The base-out recovery point method for measuring the angle of deviation is more convenient and cost-effective than the prism adaptation test, as it can be completed quickly without requiring additional time or effort.

Our results indicated that patients with preoperative ET greater than 40 PD had an average residual esotropia of more than 5 PD (see Table 3; Fig. 2). Therefore, we recommend a slightly augmented surgical dosing for BMR recession when esotropia exceeds 40 PD. Our suggestion is as follows: For 45 PD of esotropia, a 6 mm BMR recession is recommended. For 50 PD, a 6.25 mm BMR recession is appropriate; for 55 PD, a 6.5 mm BMR recession; and for 60 PD, a 7 mm BMR recession. If esotropia exceeds 60 PD, combine the maximum BMR recession with a unilateral lateral rectus resection (Table 4).

Strabismus surgical planning for AACE largely depends on the surgeon’s experience and preference when choosing between BMR recession or unilateral recess-resect surgery [1–3]. While a few studies have suggested that unilateral recess-resect is preferable to BMR recession [16, 24], the limited number of AACE cases and the lack of direct comparisons between these two methods make it difficult to draw firm conclusions. Additionally, it’s important to consider the different techniques used to measure preoperative esotropia and the augmented surgical dosages applied in some studies when comparing these postoperative outcomes.

Our study had several limitations, primarily due to its retrospective nature, which resulted in variability in age and fusional potential of the included cases. Long-term follow-up data were available for fewer than half of the cases, which made it challenging to draw definitive conclusions. However, it is likely that patients who did not return for long-term follow-up visits had achieved stable ocular alignment and were satisfied with their surgical results. Variants such as the daily amount of near work, digital device usage, and history of prior psychosocial stress were not recorded in our medical and surgical sheets. Consequently, we were unable to categorize our cases using the traditional Burian and Miller classification. Additionally, preoperative measurements of esotropia were not compared with other alternative methods. The base-out breakpoint method for measuring esotropia could be challenging in patients with suppression, and the prism adaptation test would be a more suitable alternative in these cases [32]. And finally, there is insufficient information on the adoption and evolution of the base-out breakpoint method over time.

The current study demonstrated that younger age was associated with a higher risk of surgical failure, as indicated in two previous studies [10, 40]. Other preoperative factors did not significantly impact the surgical outcomes. We reported an overall success rate of over 90% in AACE cases when performing standard bilateral medial rectus muscle recession, using preoperative measurements of esotropia assessed by the base-out breakpoint method. Future randomized clinical studies are recommended to determine the most suitable method for measuring preoperative esotropia and the best surgical approach. These studies should explore how preoperative esotropia measurements and surgical planning would affect the surgical success of patients diagnosed with acute acquired comitant esotropia.

Conclusion

This study provides several key contributions to the management of AACE. First, it is one of the largest single-center surgical series of AACE reported to date, providing outcomes from a standardized surgical approach: BMR recession. Second, we validate the clinical utility of the base-out breakpoint method for measuring the preoperative angle of deviation. Our high success rate (90.7%) demonstrates that this method is a rapid, reliable, and cost-effective alternative to more time-consuming techniques like the PAT, particularly in a clinical setting with high patient volume. Third, our analysis provides a clear, data-driven surgical dosing recommendation. We identified that strict adherence to Park’s table for larger angles (> 40 PD) leads to a predictable under-correction. Therefore, we propose a novel, slightly augmented dosing table (Table 4) for BMR recession in AACE, which we believe can help surgeons achieve more precise outcomes for patients with higher preoperative deviations. Finally, we identified that younger age and the absence of preoperative diplopia are risk factors for surgical failure, information that is crucial for preoperative planning and setting patient expectations.
