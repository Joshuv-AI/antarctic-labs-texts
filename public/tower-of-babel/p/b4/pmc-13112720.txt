Risk factor profiles for infections in individuals with type 2 diabetes: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Han-Cheng Yu, Ji-Juan Zhang, Jin-Jin Zhang, Xiao-Bao Yu, Frank Qian, Rui Li, Zi-Xin Qiu, Kai Zhu, Ru-Yi Li, Yu-Xiang Wang, Kun Yang, Ting-Ting Geng, Oscar H Franco, An Pan, Gang Liu, Yun-Fei Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfection is a significant complication of diabetes, yet comprehensive assessments of risk factors for infections in individuals with diabetes are scarce. We aimed to identify risk factor profiles and their contributions to infections in people with type 2 diabetes (T2D).MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 24,021 participants with T2D. Twenty-eight risk factors covering domains of socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors, environmental pollution, psychological factors, clinical biomarkers, and comorbidities and complications were selected. Negative binomial regression models were used to estimate incidence rate ratios (IRRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for infections. Population attributable fractions (PAFs) were calculated to assess the contributions of individual risk factors and domains.ResultsDuring a median follow‑up of 13.2 years, 9988 infection events occurred among 6517 participants, with an overall incidence rate of 33.5 per 1000 person‑years. Nineteen risk factors were significantly associated with increased infection risks, including high Townsend deprivation index, low education level, low household income, retired or unemployed status, central obesity, unhealthy diet, current smoking, non‑moderate alcohol consumption, unhealthy sleep duration, depression, loneliness, high glycated hemoglobin, low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low estimated glomerular filtration rate, cancer, cardiovascular disease, peripheral artery disease, diabetic microvascular complications, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Central obesity was the leading individual contributor to total infections (PAF, 13.2%). Across domains, lifestyle factors contributed most to total infections (joint PAF, 32.7%), followed by socioeconomic status (joint PAF, 26.3%), comorbidities and complications (joint PAF, 25.2%), clinical biomarkers (joint PAF, 17.1%), psychological factors (joint PAF, 6.3%), and environmental pollution (joint PAF, 3.3%).ConclusionsThis study identified a broad array of risk factors for infections in adults with T2D, indicating the importance of comprehensive management strategies for preventing infections among individuals with T2D.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04775-2.Keywords: Infections, Diabetes, Risk factors, Population attributable fraction, Cohort

Background

Diabetes represents a major global health challenge, affecting over half a billion people worldwide [1]. Infections are significant complications of diabetes, contributing to increased healthcare expenditures and reduced quality of life [2, 3]. Diabetes nearly quadruples the rate of infection-related hospitalization [4], and individuals with diabetes experience a 2.39-fold higher risk of infection-related mortality compared with those without diabetes [5].

People with diabetes have unique risk factors for infections, such as hyperglycemia, as well as macrovascular and microvascular complications, which differ from those of the general population, highlighting the need for tailored infection prevention strategies [6–11]. Furthermore, the pathophysiology of infections in diabetes is complex, involving multiple risk factors that complicate effective prevention [12]. Although previous studies have suggested cancer, smoking, and an unhealthy body mass index (BMI) as additional risk factors for infections among the population with diabetes, these studies have primarily focused on a limited set of risk factors or infection types and have not quantified the relative contributions of these risk factors [10, 11]. Therefore, a critical research gap remains regarding the comprehensive identification of risk factor profiles for infections among people with diabetes, particularly concerning potentially socioeconomic, lifestyle, environmental, and psychological factors.

To fill this knowledge gap, we aimed to evaluate the associations between risk factor profiles, spanning the domains of socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors, environmental pollution, psychological factors, clinical biomarkers, and comorbidities and diabetes-related complications, with the risk of infections among individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D). Furthermore, we aimed to quantify the relative contribution of these factors and corresponding domains on the population-level by calculating the population-attributable fractions (PAFs).

Methods

Study population

The UK Biobank is a large-scale, multicenter prospective cohort study enrolling more than 500,000 individuals aged 40–69 years between 2006 and 2010 across England, Scotland, and Wales. Details on the study design and data collection have been previously described [13]. In this study, we included 24,021 participants with baseline prevalent T2D, which was identified using an algorithm that combined information on diabetes diagnosis, age at diagnosis, diabetes type, diabetes medications, and diabetes complications (Additional file 1: Fig. S1) [14].

Ascertainment of outcomes

Primary diagnoses of severe infections leading to hospitalization or deaths after recruitment were identified via linkage to hospital inpatient records and the death register using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Edition (ICD-10) codes. Hospital inpatient data were sourced from the National Health Service (NHS) Hospital Episode Statistics for England, the Patient Episode Database for Wales, and the Scottish Morbidity Records, all of which provide ICD‑10 coded diagnoses supplied by the respective national health authorities. Record linkage was performed by UK Biobank using participant identifiers to ensure accurate matching. Several studies have demonstrated that the linkage was robust and reliable, ensuring high-quality outcome ascertainment [15, 16]. The infection events were classified into 21 categories (Additional file 1: Table S1). For each category, infections repeated within a 90-day period were considered the same event, while those exceeding a 90-day interval were regarded as separate events [17]. The number of total infections of each participant during follow-up was obtained by aggregating the counts across these 21 categories of infections. The primary outcome of the study was incidence of total infections, and the secondary outcome was incidence of several high-incidence infections, including pneumonia, gastrointestinal infections, and sepsis.

Assessment of risk factors

At the assessment center, baseline information such as demographic characteristics, lifestyles, psychosocial factors, medical history, and physical measures was obtained through structured touch screen questionnaires, verbal interviews, or physical examinations. Biological samples were collected at recruitment using standardized methods and devices.

A total of 28 potential risk factors were selected based on their associations with infections, as suggested by previous studies (see Additional file 1: Table S2) [7, 11, 18–31]. These risk factors were categorized into six domains:

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and numbers (%) for categorical variables. The follow-up period of each participant was calculated from the date of recruitment to the earliest date of the following events: death, loss to follow-up, or the end of the follow-up (October 31, 2022, for England; September 24, 2021, for Scotland; and May 28, 2021, for Wales). Loss to follow‑up was identified when deaths were reported by relatives before death registry confirmation, when NHS records showed inability to maintain linkage or that participants have left the UK, when participants reported moving overseas, or when consent for future linkage was withdrawn.

Considering the discrete nature and over-dispersion observed in the distribution of infection events, multivariable negative binomial regression models (with an offset accounting for follow-up days) were employed to estimate the incidence rate ratios (IRRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of total and several high-incidence infections. Model 1 was adjusted for age (years), sex (male or female), self-reported ethnicity (White or other), duration of diabetes (years), and diabetes medication use (none, oral agents only, or insulin). Model 2 was further adjusted for the other risk factors within the same domain. Model 3 additionally included mutual adjustment for all 28 individual risk factors. We further constructed a directed acyclic graph to delineate the potential causal pathways between risk factors, covariates, and infection outcomes (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). Moreover, collinearity among the variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor, and no significant collinearity was detected (Additional file 1: Table S4). When evaluating the associations between the risk scores of different domains and the risks of infections, models were adjusted for age, sex, ethnicity, duration of diabetes, and diabetes medicine use, and the risk scores of different domains were mutually adjusted. In addition, to characterize potential non‑linear associations between clinical biomarkers and infection risks, we modelled each biomarker using restricted cubic splines (RCS) with 3 to 5 knots, selected on the basis of the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC). The spline models used the same covariates as Model 1, and the resulting associations were plotted with the median biomarker value as the reference. Values above the 99th percentile and below the 1st percentile were winsorized to the corresponding cut‑points.

Stratified analyses were performed by age (≤ 60, > 60 years), sex (male or female), and diabetes duration (≤ 3, > 3 years). We included the product term in the negative binomial regression models and used the likelihood ratio test (LRT) to evaluate multiplicative interactions.

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings. Firstly, we set the end date of the follow-up to December 31, 2019, to mitigate the impact of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. Secondly, to address potential reverse causation bias, we excluded participants with infections within ± 90 days surrounding the recruitment date. Thirdly, taking into account the potential impact of autoimmune diseases [35] or severe immunosuppressive conditions (such as organ transplantation, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and human immunodeficiency virus infection) on infections, we excluded participants diagnosed with these diseases or conditions either at baseline or during the follow-up period. Fourthly, we repeated the analyses in the complete-case dataset without missing value imputation. Fifthly, obesity was redefined using a BMI threshold of ≥ 30 kg/m2 instead of central obesity determined by waist circumference. Sixthly, we additionally constructed supplementary definitions of depression and anxiety using questionnaire‑based symptom items and repeated the analyses accordingly. Depression was assessed using two questions on the frequency of depressed mood and unenthusiasm or disinterest over the past 2 weeks, whereas anxiety was assessed using two questions on the frequency of tenseness or restlessness and tiredness or lethargy over the same period. Each item was scored from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), and a summed score of ≥ 3 for each symptom pair was used to indicate the presence of depressive or anxiety symptoms [36]. Seventhly, to account for potential clustering by region, we re‑estimated associations for environmental pollution factors using mixed‑effects negative binomial regression with a random intercept for assessment center. Eighthly, we re‑evaluated the associations between risk factors and the risk of first incident infection during follow‑up using Fine-Gray competing risk models to account for death as a competing event. Ninthly, we recalculated the population‑attributable fractions using IRRs from the minimally adjusted model (Model 1) to reduce conditioning on the adjustment of potential mediators or colliders. The direction and statistical significance of the effect estimates were used to assess the robustness of the sensitivity analyses.

Statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Missing values were imputed using the chained random forests method (R package missRanger, version 2.2.0) (Additional file 1: Table S5). All P-values were two-sided. The Bonferroni-corrected method was used to handle multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was defined at a threshold of P < 0.0017 (0.05/28) in analyses of individual risk factors or interactions and at P < 0.0083 (0.05/6) in analyses of risk scores. For other analyses, statistical significance was defined if P < 0.05.

Results

This study included 24,021 participants (62.6% men) with T2D, with a mean age of 60.1 years, and 6517 (27.1%) participants experienced one or more infection events during the follow-up period. The baseline characteristics of the participants according to the occurrence of infections are shown in Table 1. Compared with those without infections, participants who developed infections were generally older, more often men and of white ethnicity, and had a longer duration of diabetes. They were also more likely to use insulin and to exhibit socioeconomic disadvantages, adverse lifestyle behaviors, and psychological risk factors. Moreover, they had less favorable metabolic profiles and a greater burden of comorbidities, including cardiovascular disease, diabetic complications, COPD, and hypertension, with higher levels of environmental pollution exposure, except for PM10 and Lden.

The associations of low household income, retired or unemployed status, current smoking, unhealthy sleep duration, and low eGFR with the risk of total infections were significantly stronger among individuals with T2D aged < 60 years compared with those aged ≥ 60 years (all LRT-P < 0.001) (Additional file 1: Table S13). Additionally, the association between current smoking and total infections was more pronounced in women than in men (LRT-P < 0.001) (Additional file 1: Table S14). No significant interactions were observed between risk factors and diabetes duration in relation to infection risks (Additional file 1: Table S15).

In sensitivity analyses, the magnitude and direction of the associations between risk factors and infections remained largely unchanged when limiting the end of follow‑up to December 31, 2019 (Additional file 1: Table S16), excluding participants with infections within ± 90 days of the recruitment date (Additional file 1: Table S17), excluding those with autoimmune diseases or severe immunosuppressive conditions at baseline or during follow‑up (Additional file 1: Table S18), analyzing data in the complete‑case dataset (Additional file 1: Table S19), or redefining obesity using a BMI threshold of ≥ 30 kg/m2 instead of central obesity (Additional file 1: Table S20). Moreover, similar results were observed when depression and anxiety were jointly defined using questionnaire‑based information (Additional file 1: Table S21) or when environmental pollution factors were analyzed using mixed‑effects negative binomial regression with a random intercept for assessment center (Additional file 1: Table S22). In addition, when using Fine‑Gray competing‑risk models to account for the competing risk of death, the associations between risk factors and the risk of first infections during the follow-up period remained significant (Additional file 1: Table S23). The PAFs calculated using effect estimates from the non-mutually adjusted model increased, while the ranking of individual risk factors and their domains remained largely unchanged (Additional file 1: Tables S24 and S25).

Discussion

In this study of individuals with T2D, we identified 19 factors across multiple domains, including socioeconomic status, lifestyle behaviors, psychological factors, clinical biomarkers, and comorbidities and complications, which were associated with increased risks of infection. Central obesity was the leading single attributable factor for total infections, while low educational attainment contributed most to pneumonia and gastrointestinal infections, and retirement or unemployment was a major contributor to sepsis. At the domain level, lifestyle factors accounted for the largest joint population attributable fraction for total infections (32.7%), followed by socioeconomic status (26.3%), comorbidities and complications (25.2%), clinical biomarkers (17.1%), psychological factors (6.3%), and environmental pollution (3.3%). Notably, no individual environmental pollution factors remained significantly associated with infection risk after multivariable adjustment. Across several sensitivity analyses, the direction and magnitude of these associations remained largely unchanged, and the ranking of individual risk factors and domains was consistent. These findings highlight the multifactorial nature of infection susceptibility in T2D and emphasize the need for integrated prevention strategies that address lifestyle behaviors, socioeconomic conditions, and clinical risk management in combination.

In this study, the incidence rate of infection‑related hospitalization or death among people with T2D was 33.5 per 1000 person‑years, lower than the 41.7 per 1000 person‑years reported in a previous study using the Clinical Practice Research Datalink. This difference may reflect the healthier profile of UK Biobank volunteers [37]. Several studies have reported specific risk factors that were associated with increased risks of infections for people with diabetes, including higher deprivation status, previous or current smoking, higher BMI, chronic kidney disease, CVD, peripheral vascular disease [17], and poor glycemic control [7, 38]. In addition, a study in Chinese adults with T2D suggested previous or current smoking, higher BMI, macrovascular disease, diabetic neuropathy, and cancer as risk factors for infection-related hospitalizations, although failed to account for recurring events [11]. Our research corroborated several previously identified risk factors for individuals with diabetes and highlighted additional significant factors across various domains, such as central obesity, low education level, low household income, retired or unemployed, unhealthy diet, non-moderate alcohol consumption, unhealthy sleep duration, depression, and loneliness. Given the wide range of risk factors associated with infections, patients with diabetes may require comprehensive management strategies that extend beyond focusing solely on individual factors, such as glycemic control. Factors such as high BMI, central obesity, suboptimal glycemic control, and diabetic complications are characteristic of individuals with diabetes and thus may represent particularly relevant intervention targets for this population. Nevertheless, given the broad range of infection‑related risk factors, comprehensive management strategies are required beyond those specific to diabetes. Several interventions, such as lifestyle modifications, have proven effective in improving glycemic control and cardiometabolic markers in diabetes and may also lower infection risk despite limited direct evidence [39, 40]. Although low educational attainment is not easily modifiable in adulthood, its downstream consequences can be addressed. Interventions targeting health literacy, preventive care, and self‑management have demonstrated benefits in enhancing behavioral and clinical risk profiles [41, 42].

Furthermore, the RCS analyses identified several clinical biomarkers that exhibited significant non‑linear associations with infection risk, suggesting the presence of biomarker ranges associated with lower susceptibility. Similarly, prior research in individuals with T2D has reported a U‑shaped association between HbA1c levels and sepsis [6, 11]. These findings indicate that dichotomizing biomarkers using a single threshold may obscure potential risk gradients in individuals with T2D, underscoring the need for further research to define clinically meaningful biomarker ranges for infection prevention. Additionally, the domain‑specific risk scores summarize the cumulative burden of multiple factors within each domain, providing a more integrated view of their contribution to infection susceptibility. By capturing the combined influence of co‑occurring exposures, these scores reflect multidimensional risk patterns that single variables may not represent, highlighting the value of domain‑level profiling for identifying individuals at elevated infection risk.

In addition, we found that the associations of low household income, retired or unemployed status, current smoking, unhealthy sleep duration, and reduced eGFR with total infections were more pronounced among individuals aged < 60 years, while the association between current smoking and total infections was stronger among women, with significant multiplicative interactions. The stronger associations of low household income, retired or unemployed status, unhealthy sleep duration, and reduced eGFR with total infections among younger adults suggest potential opportunities for targeted infection‑prevention strategies in younger individuals with T2D, such as support for minimum income or employment stability, guidance on healthy sleep practices, and enhanced monitoring of kidney function [43]. Additionally, the more pronounced association between current smoking and total infections among women underscores the necessity for sex-specific smoking cessation programs. Tailoring public health strategies to account for these factors might enhance the effectiveness of interventions to reduce the burden of infections in populations with T2D.

Research assessing the PAFs of infection-related risk factors in population with diabetes is limited. A previous study has found that 15.7% of infection-related deaths and 16.5% of infection-related hospitalizations were attributed to HbA1c levels outside the range of 6–7% (42–53 mmol/mol) among patients with diabetes [7]. In our study of adults with T2D, we found that the primary risk factors with the top 3 PAFs for total infections were central obesity (13.2%), low education level (11.6%), and hypertension (10.2%), while high HbA1c levels [> 7% (53 mmol/mol)] ranked 13th with a PAF of 3.8%. These findings suggest that central obesity represents a particularly important modifiable determinant of infection risk in individuals with T2D, and given the high prevalence of obesity in this population, weight‑management interventions may be effective in reducing the infection burden [44]. Moreover, the magnitude of a PAF reflects both the prevalence of a risk factor and its relative risk [33]. Factors such as low education level and retired or unemployed status may yield large PAFs because they are common structural conditions, even though they are not biological determinants of infection. Low educational attainment may impair health literacy and diabetes self‑management [45], while retired or unemployed status may indicate limited resources, reduced access to care, and greater social vulnerability [46]. These social determinants make a substantial contribution to the infection burden and underscore the need to address them alongside more readily modifiable risk factors in adults with T2D. When examining the domains, we found that the lifestyle domain had the highest joint PAF for total infections at 32.7%, followed by socioeconomic status at 26.3% and comorbidities and complications at 25.2%. These findings underscore the central role of promoting healthy lifestyles in preventing infections among adults with T2D while also highlighting the importance of addressing socioeconomic inequalities, managing comorbidities and complications, and considering other relevant factors to achieve comprehensive risk control.

To our knowledge, this study is among the first to comprehensively investigate the relationships between risk factor profiles and infection risks, as well as the PAFs of specific risk factors or domains, among individuals with T2D. The prospective design, long follow-up period, and relatively large sample size increased the methodological robustness of the findings. However, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, risk factors were assessed only at baseline, limiting the ability to capture changes over time, particularly for exposures that may vary substantially during long‑term follow‑up. In addition, some self‑reported risk factors may be affected by recall bias, while infection outcomes derived from hospital inpatient records and the death register may also be influenced by regional differences. If such misclassification is non‑differential, it would likely bias the observed associations toward the null and lead to an underestimation of the true relationships. Secondly, immunization status is a potential modifier of infection risk. However, the absence of immunization data in this study may have introduced residual confounding. Furthermore, since participants in the UK Biobank are likely representative of a population of “healthy volunteers” with healthier lifestyles and fewer self-reported health conditions, there may be an inherent selection bias [37]. Given that individuals with less healthy lifestyles and more comorbidities are at higher risk of infections, this potential selection bias may contribute to an underestimation of the associations observed between risk factors and infection risks. Nonetheless, the true direction and magnitude of this bias remain uncertain, and thus, the interpretations should be made with caution. Thirdly, the calculation of PAFs was based on the assumption of causal relationships between selected risk factors and infections, which is a challenging task in observational studies. Thus, the PAFs in this study should be interpreted with caution. Given the large number of risk factors examined and the potentially selection bias, our study should be considered hypothesis-generating, identifying potential risk factors for infections in T2D rather than confirming causal relationships. In addition, some of the risk factors may be difficult to modify in practice, which highlights the need for future research using randomized controlled trials incorporating innovative and effective intervention strategies to validate these findings. Fourthly, our study was restricted to infections identified through hospital inpatient records and death registers. Consequently, mild or nonhospitalized infections were not captured, meaning that our findings primarily reflect the determinants of more severe infections. Future studies with more comprehensive data are needed to address this gap. Fifthly, because the UK Biobank cohort is predominantly composed of individuals of European ancestry and is not nationally representative, caution should be exercised when generalizing these findings to other populations. Since the PAFs depend on the underlying prevalence of each risk factor, they may not be directly applicable to populations with different distributions of exposure.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study identified a broad array of risk factors associated with risks of infections, emphasizing the need for comprehensive strategies across various domains to effectively mitigate the risk of infections in people with T2D. Further studies are warranted to confirm these findings.
