A hybrid transformer-PPO framework for multi-objective energy management in renewable-based microgrids
Authors: Yeganeh Sadeghpour, Ehsan Azad Farsani, Hamid Reza Abdolmohammadi, Iman Goroohi Sardou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper proposes a hybrid framework that combines a Transformer-based multi-output forecasting model with a proximal policy optimization (PPO) agent for intelligent energy management in renewable-based microgrids. The Transformer forecaster captures long-range temporal dependencies in load, photovoltaic (PV), wind generation, and electricity price time series, providing 24-h-ahead predictions that serve as state inputs to the control layer. The PPO agent then learns an adaptive energy management policy that jointly optimizes three conflicting objectives: maximizing economic profit, minimizing carbon emissions associated with grid electricity, and reducing dependency on external power imports. Introduction

Amid the ongoing global transition toward low-carbon and renewable-energy systems, the integration of distributed solar photovoltaic (PV) and wind generation has become a central priority for modern power networks1,2. According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), global investments in renewable energy exceeded USD 500 billion in 2023, with the share of renewables in electricity production continuing to rise across all major regions3. To meet increasing electricity demand and achieve deep decarbonization targets, microgrids that integrate distributed energy resources (DERs), energy storage systems (ESS), and controllable loads have emerged as a critical enabler of sustainable, resilient, and localized power systems4. Microgrids enhance operational flexibility, improve renewable integration, and enable localized energy trading through both islanded and grid-connected modes4. However, their growing reliance on intermittent renewable sources introduces new challenges of uncertainty, volatility, and optimal dispatch in real-time5,6.

However, the variability and intermittency of renewable generation, combined with dynamic load fluctuations and volatile electricity prices, pose critical challenges for maintaining cost-effective and reliable microgrid operation6. Addressing these challenges requires two complementary capabilities: (1) highly accurate forecasting of renewable generation, load demand, and market signals, and (2) adaptive, real-time decision-making strategies capable of balancing economic, environmental, and operational objectives under uncertainty7,8.

Conventional forecasting approaches, such as autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA), support vector regression (SVR), and recurrent neural networks (RNN, LSTM) are limited in capturing nonlinear dependencies and long-range temporal correlations within high-dimensional energy datasets6,9. In contrast, Transformer-based architectures leverage self-attention mechanisms to capture intricate temporal dependencies, achieving state-of-the-art performance in renewable forecasting and demand prediction9,10. Recent advancements, including Autoformer and FEDformer, have demonstrated strong long-term forecasting performance on benchmark datasets and competitive results compared with common deep-learning baselines (e.g., CNN/LSTM-based models)11,12.

Nevertheless, accurate forecasting alone is insufficient to ensure optimal real-world microgrid performance7. Forecast outputs must be translated into adaptive control actions that dynamically respond to fluctuating renewable availability and market conditions7. Reinforcement Learning (RL), as a model-free and data-driven optimization paradigm, has therefore emerged as a promising approach, enabling autonomous agents to learn optimal scheduling and dispatch strategies through continuous interaction with stochastic and partially observable environments without explicit system modeling13,14.

Among RL algorithms, Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) stands out for its stability, sample efficiency, and scalability, rendering it particularly suitable for continuous control tasks such as battery scheduling, renewable utilization, and energy trading in complex microgrid environments14,15. Integrating Transformer-based forecasting with PPO-driven decision optimization has the potential to enable autonomous, economically profitable, and environmentally sustainable energy management in renewable-based microgrids.

However, existing Transformer-PPO frameworks still present several limitations. Many focus solely on single-objective optimization, typically profit maximization, or employ fixed reward formulations that fail to dynamically balance conflicting objectives16,17. Unlike prior hybrid approaches that apply Transformers and PPO in loosely coupled or sequential pipelines, the present work integrates forecasting and control in a tightly coupled, end-to-end architecture where forecast embeddings directly inform the PPO state representation. Furthermore, prior works seldom investigate how tuning key reward coefficients (α, β, γ) or adjusting the Transformer input window length influences overall system stability and performance16. This lack of comprehensive sensitivity analysis limits the understanding of model robustness under diverse market conditions and renewable generation scenarios18.

To address these research gaps, this study introduces an enhanced Transformer-PPO hybrid framework for intelligent energy management in renewable-based microgrids. The proposed system employs a multi-objective reward function with tunable weighting coefficients that explicitly balance three conflicting objectives: profit maximization, carbon-emission minimization, and reduction of grid dependency16,19. In addition, a comprehensive quantitative sensitivity analysis is performed on both the reward parameters and the Transformer input sequence length to investigate how forecasting accuracy and control policy dynamics interact18. This design yields deeper insight into the synergy between predictive modeling and reinforcement learning, thereby helping to bridge the gap between data-driven forecasting and adaptive decision-making in complex energy environments7,18.

Overall, the proposed framework offers a scalable, adaptive, and environmentally sustainable solution for next-generation microgrids4,19. It demonstrates robust decision-making capability and balanced performance under uncertainty, variable renewable availability, and dynamic market conditions18.

The main contribution of this study is the development of a hybrid Transformer-PPO framework for intelligent energy management in renewable-based microgrids. The proposed approach integrates attention-based multi-output forecasting with reinforcement learning-based decision making, enabling the control agent to anticipate renewable generation variability and electricity price fluctuations while optimizing operational strategies. In addition, a multi-objective reward formulation is designed to simultaneously balance economic profitability, carbon emission reduction, and grid import minimization. The framework is further evaluated through sensitivity analyses on key model parameters, including the Transformer input sequence length and reward coefficients, to assess the robustness and stability of the proposed system.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. “Related work” reviews related work on microgrid forecasting, reinforcement learning-based energy management, and hybrid forecasting-optimization frameworks. “Methodology” presents the proposed Transformer-PPO methodology, including the forecasting module, reward design, and training procedure. “Results and discussion” reports the experimental setup and discusses the results, sensitivity analyses, and comparative benchmarks against existing methods. Finally, the Conclusion summarizes the main findings and outlines directions for future research.

Related work

Advancements in forecasting, optimization, and control have become increasingly essential for achieving intelligent and reliable microgrid energy management. Prior research in this field has primarily concentrated on three interconnected domains: (1) forecasting methodologies for renewable generation, demand, and market dynamics, (2) RL-based strategies for adaptive operational control, and (3) hybrid frameworks that integrate forecasting with optimization and decision-making processes1. This section provides a comprehensive overview of key advancements and representative studies within each domain, highlighting their contributions, limitations, and interdependencies.

Forecasting approaches in microgrids

Accurate forecasting of energy demand, solar PV and wind generation, and electricity prices is a fundamental prerequisite for predictive scheduling and proactive energy trading in microgrids6. Traditional statistical methods, such as autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) and exponential smoothing, remain widely adopted due to their simplicity and interpretability20. However, these models often fail to capture the nonlinear dynamics and meteorological effects inherent in high-frequency energy data6,9. Recent studies have explored attention-based and Transformer-based architectures to improve forecasting accuracy for renewable energy generation and load demand in microgrids18. Shallow machine learning techniques, including support vector regression (SVR) and random forest regression, provide incremental performance improvements but still struggle to model long-range dependencies and multi-scale variability6.

Traditional statistical methods, such as autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) and exponential smoothing, remain widely adopted due to their simplicity and interpretability20. However, these models often fail to capture the nonlinear dynamics and meteorological effects inherent in high-frequency energy data [6.9]. Beyond forecasting itself, recent studies have emphasized the importance of explicitly modeling uncertainty in scheduling and trading frameworks. For instance, robust kernel density estimation-based data-driven optimal scheduling has been proposed for power systems to address data errors and renewable energy uncertainties, thereby enhancing operational reliability under imperfect information21. Likewise, uncertainty-aware coordination has also been studied in integrated energy environments, where a bi-level energy sharing model between multi-energy cloud energy storage and multiple integrated energy systems was developed to improve collaborative scheduling and resource allocation under complex operational conditions22. In parallel, recent studies have explored attention-based and Transformer-based architectures to improve forecasting accuracy for renewable energy generation and load demand in microgrids18. Shallow machine learning techniques, including support vector regression (SVR) and random forest regression, provide incremental performance improvements but still struggle to model long-range dependencies and multi-scale variability6.

Recent studies have demonstrated the superiority of Transformer-based deep learning architectures for multi-output and long-horizon energy forecasting, as they effectively leverage self-attention mechanisms to capture complex temporal dependencies9,10,23. In contrast to sequential recurrent networks such as LSTM and GRU, which process time series step-by-step, Transformers compute attention scores in parallel, enabling scalability for large, high-resolution energy datasets24. Variants such as Autoformer decompose time series into seasonal and trend components, improving robustness against volatility11, while cross-variable Transformer frameworks jointly model correlated targets—such as load, wind, and PV outputs to enhance system-level accuracy10. Furthermore, multi-task Transformer architectures designed to simultaneously forecast load, renewable generation, and electricity prices have been shown to further improve decision-making performance in microgrid optimization contexts9,10.

Reinforcement learning for energy management

RL has emerged as a promising paradigm for microgrid energy management, providing an alternative to traditional optimization and rule-based control methods under uncertainty13,14. By continuously interacting with dynamic environments, RL agents learn optimal control policies without requiring explicit mathematical or physical models of system behavior13. Among various RL algorithms, PPO has gained significant attention due to its favorable trade-off between training stability and computational efficiency, making it well-suited for tasks such as battery scheduling, distributed energy coordination, and energy trading14,15. Empirical studies have demonstrated that PPO-based controllers outperform Deep Q-Learning (DQN) and other value-based algorithms in terms of cumulative reward, convergence reliability, and robustness under stochastic operating conditions25. Reinforcement learning techniques, particularly Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), have also been investigated for adaptive microgrid energy management and operational decision-making16,19.

Extending beyond single-agent architectures, Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (MARL) frameworks have been developed for decentralized energy systems, where multiple agents—representing distributed energy resources (DERs), storage units, or market participants—cooperate to achieve both local and system-level objectives, such as emission reduction and grid stability26. PPO-driven MARL architectures have demonstrated enhanced scalability, adaptability, and coordination performance, enabling efficient distributed decision-making across complex and large-scale microgrid networks26.

Hybrid forecasting-optimization frameworks

A growing body of recent research has focused on hybrid frameworks that integrate advanced forecasting models with RL-based optimization to holistically address renewable variability, demand uncertainty, and market volatility7. In such systems, Transformer-based forecasts of demand, renewable generation, and electricity prices typically serve as state inputs for PPO-driven agents, which enables predictive and adaptive real-time operational strategies. Compared with standalone forecasting or rule-based control methods, these hybrid architectures have been shown to consistently achieve lower operational costs, higher renewable utilization, and reduced carbon emissions18.

Multi-objective hybrid architectures further extend these capabilities by incorporating Pareto-based or weighted-sum optimization, allowing microgrids to balance profitability, CO₂ emission reduction, and grid independence without overemphasizing a single performance metric16,19. Such integrated approaches demonstrate strong potential for self-optimizing, economically viable, and environmentally sustainable microgrid operations, as validated through simulation studies and benchmark case analyses in recent literature19.

Recent studies have shown that utilizing forecasting and optimization frameworks can significantly aid microgrids in making informed decisions when faced with intermittent renewable energy sources and uncertain market conditions7. This is especially true when the predictive models give the controllers who use reinforcement learning information about the current state. However, the way people do this now is not always the same, especially when it comes to combining what the forecasting models say with the optimization that happens in time7. Also, people handle the trade-offs between goals in different ways, and it would be better if they could do this in a single process that includes everything16. Microgrid decision-making can be enhanced with forecasting and optimization frameworks, which are crucial for microgrids to operate effectively in the presence of renewable variability and market uncertainty18. Motivated by these gaps, this paper proposes a hybrid framework that combines a Transformer-based multi-output forecasting module with a PPO-based energy management agent to enable adaptive, data-driven operation18. The following section details the proposed methodology, including the forecasting architecture, the PPO formulation, and the integrated training/deployment workflow.

Despite these advancements, a critical gap remains in the literature: most hybrid forecasting-control frameworks treat forecasting and decision-making as separate, sequential modules, limiting the ability of the control policy to directly leverage temporal patterns captured during forecasting16. The present study addresses this gap by proposing a tightly coupled Transformer-PPO architecture in which forecast embeddings are directly integrated into the PPO state representation, enabling end-to-end learning and dynamic multi-objective optimization. This architectural innovation, combined with a flexible reward design and systematic sensitivity analysis, distinguishes the proposed framework from prior work and provides new insights into the interplay between forecasting accuracy, policy dynamics, and microgrid performance under uncertainty.

Methodology

This study introduces a hybrid framework that integrates a Transformer-based multi-output forecasting model with a PPO agent to enable adaptive and autonomous microgrid energy management24,25. The forecasting component leverages the ability of the Transformer to capture complex, multi-scale temporal dependencies11, while the PPO agent learns optimal operational policies to maximize profit, minimize carbon emissions, and reduce grid dependency14,15. The methodology is organized into three parts: the forecasting module (“Transformer-based forecasting module”), the PPO optimization agent (“PPO-based optimization agent”), and the integrated forecasting-optimization framework (“Integrated forecasting-optimization framework”).

Transformer-based forecasting module

The forecasting module employs a sequence-to-sequence Transformer architecture to generate 24-h-ahead forecasts for electricity demand, PV generation, wind power, and market prices9,10. Transformers, initially introduced for natural language processing24, have demonstrated superior performance in energy forecasting due to their capability to model long-term temporal patterns and exogenous variables9.

To ensure reproducibility, additional implementation details of the forecasting module are provided here. All input features, including load, PV generation, wind generation, and electricity price series, were normalized using z‑score statistics calculated from the training set, and the same parameters were applied to the validation and test sets. Missing values were imputed using a forward‑fill strategy followed by a backward consistency check to remove residual gaps.

The forecasting loss was implemented as a weighted Mean Squared Error, where the weighting coefficients were derived from the inverse coefficient of variation for each predicted time step and normalized to sum to one across the 24‑step horizon.

The decoder was trained using teacher forcing during the early stage of training and gradually transitioned to scheduled sampling to reduce dependency on ground‑truth inputs. The model generates the full 24‑step‑ahead forecast directly in a single forward pass rather than recursively, preventing error accumulation and reducing inference latency.

PPO-based optimization agent

The optimization layer employs PPO25, a policy-gradient algorithm based on the Actor-Critic architecture, to manage battery scheduling26, energy trading, and renewable utilization. PPO is widely used in energy systems due to its balance between training stability, robustness, and computational efficiency14,15. The actor network, parameterized by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\theta\:$$\end{document}, outputs control actions, while the critic network, parameterized by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\phi\:$$\end{document}, estimates the state value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:V\left({s}_{t}\right)$$\end{document}, guiding policy updates based on the reward feedback received from the microgrid environment25.

To ensure clarity of the optimization setting, the microgrid environment used for PPO training is fully specified here. The microgrid contains a battery energy storage system (BESS) with a capacity of 100 kWh. The state of charge (SOC) is constrained between 10% and 90% of the rated capacity to prevent deep cycling and extend battery life. The maximum charge and discharge power are limited to 25 kW, and the round‑trip efficiency is modeled as 90%, decomposed into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\eta\:}_{charge}$$\end{document} = 0.95 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\eta\:}_{discharge}$$\end{document} = 0.95.

Grid interaction is defined by two separate tariffs representing import and export prices. The import tariff corresponds to the market price series included in the dataset, while exported electricity is compensated through a fixed feed‑in tariff equal to 70% of the corresponding import price. The model enforces a no‑simultaneous‑charging‑and‑discharging constraint and applies power balance equations at each time step, ensuring that load, PV, wind, battery power, and grid exchange remain feasible.

Carbon emissions are modeled using a marginal emission factor of 0.48 kgCO₂/kWh for imported electricity. Locally generated renewable energy is treated as carbon‑free. Total daily emissions are computed as the sum of grid imports multiplied by the emission factor. These assumptions define the environment in which the PPO agent learns optimal battery scheduling, grid interaction, and renewable utilization policies.

Integrated forecasting-optimization framework

The proposed framework integrates the Transformer-based forecasting module (“Transformer-based forecasting module”) with the PPO-based optimization agent (“PPO-based optimization agent”) to form an adaptive, closed-loop architecture for intelligent microgrid energy management18. In this integrated system, the Transformer forecaster provides 24-h-ahead predictions of electricity demand, PV and wind generation, and market prices. These forecasts provide the state inputs for the PPO agent, enabling it to determine optimal real-time control actions for energy trading, battery operation, and grid interaction.

Unlike conventional hybrid frameworks that treat forecasting and optimization as independent tasks, the proposed design establishes a dynamic feedback connection between the two modules18. The control decisions derived by the PPO agent directly influence system states, while the forecasting module continuously refines its predictions based on updated operating conditions. This forecast-control coupling allows the microgrid to adapt proactively to volatile renewable availability and market prices7.

Results and discussion

The results of the proposed Transformer-PPO hybrid framework are presented and discussed in this section.

The analysis focuses on evaluating the forecasting accuracy of the Transformer model, the control performance of the PPO agent, and the overall effectiveness of their integration in optimizing microgrid operations.

Both quantitative metrics and qualitative behaviors are examined to highlight how the proposed method enhances economic efficiency, reduces carbon emissions, and improves grid independence compared to baseline and state-of-the-art approaches.

Forecasting performance evaluation

The forecasting performance of the proposed Transformer model was evaluated using a microgrid dataset obtained from Kaggle28, which contains synthetic but statistically realistic hourly load demand, PV and wind generation, and electricity price series. The model was trained on historical data and tested on unseen future intervals to assess generalization capability and temporal adaptability. For model training, the hourly series were resampled/interpreted at a 15-min resolution, resulting in 96 input time steps per day.

To ensure a fair and methodologically consistent comparison across all forecasting baselines, all models were trained and evaluated under an identical experimental protocol. Each method received the same historical input data, identical input-window length, and the same train-test temporal split. The training budget, including the number of epochs, batch size, and data preprocessing steps, was also kept fixed across all models. Although MA‑RL is not a conventional supervised forecasting model, it was included because prior microgrid‑control studies report its predictive outputs as part of their learning pipeline. For fairness, its forecasting component was restricted to the same information set and trained under the same conditions as the purely data‑driven baselines. Therefore, the reported differences in forecasting accuracy arise solely from model capability rather than unequal data availability, computational budget, or evaluation settings.

Figure 4 compares the predicted and actual values over representative days. The Transformer effectively captures both long-term trends and short-term fluctuations, accurately following the nonlinear variations in renewable generation and demand patterns.

Across these repeated trials, the variation in performance metrics remained small, with standard deviations below 2.5% for forecasting metrics (MAE, RMSE, R²) and below 3% for control-related indicators such as operating cost, carbon emissions, and battery cycling stability. These low variances indicate that the proposed hybrid framework exhibits stable convergence behavior and is not sensitive to stochasticity in weight initialization or minibatch sampling. Discussion

The results obtained in this study highlight the effectiveness of the proposed Transformer-PPO hybrid framework in achieving a balanced trade-off between forecasting accuracy, operational profit, and environmental sustainability.

Compared to the baseline methods, the Transformer-based predictor demonstrated superior capability in capturing nonlinear temporal relationships and multi-scale dependencies among load, renewable generation, and market dynamics.

This improved predictive performance directly enhanced the control stability and reward convergence of the PPO agent, confirming the value of tight forecast-control integration in microgrid energy management.

Despite these promising results, certain observations merit further discussion. The percentage reduction in CO₂ emissions, although numerically modest within the simulated dataset, can have significant real-world implications depending on the carbon intensity of regional power grids. For example, in European systems with higher renewable penetration, an equivalent relative reduction could represent a substantial absolute decrease in emissions, while in fossil-dominated grids the same improvement might yield smaller real-world gains. The dataset used in this research, obtained from Kaggle26, is synthetic but statistically realistic and was used to evaluate algorithmic robustness rather than to reproduce a specific real-world microgrid profile. Therefore, the reported results should be interpreted as conceptual performance indicators rather than absolute regional estimates.

Another point of consideration lies in the multi-objective reward design. The sensitivity analysis revealed that moderate weighting between profit (α = 1.0), emission reduction (β = 0.3), and grid dependency (γ = 0.3) yields the most stable performance. This balance reflects the importance of policy calibration in aligning economic incentives with sustainability objectives — a factor that may vary across markets with different tariff or carbon-pricing structures. Future research could extend this framework by incorporating real multi-source datasets, demand response mechanisms29,30, and region-specific renewable outputs or carbon intensity profiles, to better represent actual grid behavior.

A deeper interpretation of the reward-sensitivity analysis is warranted given that the reported differences across the tested β and γ settings were relatively small. This observation does not necessarily imply that the environmental reward components were ineffective; rather, it reflects the operational characteristics of the simulated microgrid, where renewable availability and battery constraints limited the range of feasible decisions in many intervals. Under such conditions, variations in β and γ produce only modest changes in the realized grid-import and CO₂-emission penalties because the agent’s optimal behavior is already strongly shaped by physical and operational constraints.

Nonetheless, the limited numerical spread across different reward-weight configurations indicates that the multi-objective reward may benefit from further normalization or re-scaling to ensure that each component operates within comparable magnitudes. Future work will incorporate an expanded range of weight combinations and alternative scaling strategies to more thoroughly characterize the sensitivity of the learned policy to environmental objectives.

A further point concerns the interpretation of the environmental reward components during training. Although the logged values of the grid-import and CO₂-related terms were zero in some episodes, these objectives were not inactive. Instead, this behavior indicates that under certain operating conditions, the combination of renewable generation and battery scheduling enabled the agent to satisfy demand locally, thereby eliminating both grid-import penalties and the associated carbon-emission penalties. This result should therefore be interpreted as a policy outcome under the given dataset and environment assumptions, rather than as evidence that the environmental objectives were absent from the optimization process.

Furthermore, expanding the control strategy into a multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) paradigm would enable cooperative coordination among distributed assets (e.g., EV chargers, prosumers, and storage units), enhancing scalability in larger microgrid networks.

Overall, the discussion underscores that while the present results are derived from simulated data, the methodological insights, particularly the integration of attention-based forecasting with adaptive reinforcement learning, provide a strong foundation for real-world deployment under diverse regional energy systems.

Conclusion

This study developed a hybrid Transformer-PPO framework for intelligent and adaptive energy management in renewable-based microgrids. By combining the predictive strength of Transformer architectures with the adaptive learning capability of Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), the proposed system achieved a dynamic balance between economic profitability, carbon reduction, and grid independence. The integration of multi-output forecasting and reinforcement learning enabled the agent to anticipate renewable variability and market fluctuations while maintaining stable control performance under uncertainty.

The conducted sensitivity analyses on both the reward coefficients (α, β, γ) and Transformer input sequence length revealed that:

(i)a higher forecasting horizon improves short-term decision stability but increases training variance, and(ii)balanced reward configurations (α = 1.0, β = 0.3, γ = 0.3) provide a configuration with balanced performance across objectives between financial and environmental objectives.

Despite the promising results, the current framework is evaluated on synthetic yet statistically realistic data and assumes perfect observability of state variables. Therefore, the results should be interpreted as an initial validation of the proposed approach rather than evidence of immediate practical deployment. Future extensions may include: (a) validation on real-time microgrid platforms, (b) incorporation of stochastic price modeling and weather uncertainty, and (c) exploration of multi-agent coordination among distributed microgrids. Such developments would help further assess the scalability, resilience, and applicability of Transformer-RL architectures for next-generation sustainable power networks.
