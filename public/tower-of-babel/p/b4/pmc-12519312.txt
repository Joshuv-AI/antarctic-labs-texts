The E3 ubiquitin ligase Trip12 semi-selectively attenuates Wnt signaling
Authors: Jessica Ensing, Amber D Ide, Carla Gilliland, Visakuo Tsurho, Emma Rudisel, Isabella Caza, Amber N Stratman, Nathan J Lanning, Stephanie Grainger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryWnt signaling is critical for the development and maintenance of many cell lineages, including hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs). The tight regulation of Wnt signals is essential, as overactivation can drive tumorigenesis. Numerous Wnt ligands and Frizzled (Fzd) receptors exist, and the negative regulation of particular Fzd signals is the focus of this study. We previously identified the requirement of Wnt9a-Fzd9b pairing for early HSC proliferation. However, the mechanisms controlling activation and signal termination are unclear. Here, we show that the E3 ubiquitin ligase Trip12 (thyroid hormone receptor interactor 12) targets the third intracellular loop of Fzd9b at K437, promoting lysosomal degradation by destabilizing Fzd9b membrane expression. Our data further indicates that Trip12 is semi-selective for Fzd9b. The Trip12-mediated reduction of Fzd9b surface expression dampens Wnt9a/Fzd9b signaling, affecting HSC proliferation in zebrafish. Our findings reveal a receptor-specific regulatory mechanism, with implications for targeted Wnt pathway therapies.Subject areas: Biochemistry, Cell biology

Introduction

The Wnt signaling pathway is highly conserved among metazoans and drives diverse processes in development and homeostasis, such as tissue patterning, morphogenesis, and balancing stem cell proliferation and differentiation.1,2 The intracellular events in the Wnt pathway are diverse and are typically categorized as either β-catenin-dependent or β-catenin-independent. Here, we focus on β-catenin-dependent signaling. In the β-catenin-dependent pathway, a secreted Wnt protein interacts with a seven-pass transmembrane cell surface receptor from the Frizzled (FZD) gene family, and a single-pass transmembrane receptor from the low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein family (LRP5 or LRP6). This event at the membrane ultimately leads to the dissociation of the cytoplasmic β-catenin destruction complex, enabling the translocation of β-catenin to the nucleus for target gene activation and to diverse biological outputs across a multitude of tissue types.1,2

The importance of Wnt signaling is reflected by an abundance of driver mutations in Wnt pathway genes, causing diseases such as cancer, Alzheimer’s disease, and diabetes, among others.3,4,5,6 For example, losing mechanisms to downregulate receptor availability at the cell surface can lead to cancer in numerous cell types.7,8,9 Correspondingly, tight control of signal activation is required at multiple levels, including through the availability of FZD receptors at the membrane. One mechanism to regulate membrane receptor trafficking is post-translational modifications, where amino acid side chains have covalent modifications impacting their function, such as ubiquitination.10 For example, the E3 ubiquitin ligases RNF43 and ZNRF3 target FZDs for degradation, a critical step in restricting Wnt signaling.7,8,9,10 Congruently, mutations in genes that negatively regulate the membrane localization of FZD, such as RNF43 and ZNRF3, are also common in cancers since loss of function leads to excessive proliferation.

In vertebrates, there are at least 19 Wnts and 10 Fzds, suggesting some degree of functional specificity and ligand-receptor selectivity, although this is only beginning to be uncovered. The role of Wnt signaling in hematopoietic development is complex and relies on the input of multiple Wnt ligands. We recently showed that the specific pairing of Wnt9a and Fzd9(b) is required in zebrafish and human hematopoietic stem cell development.11,12 In the absence of β-catenin-mediated Wnt signaling, developing hematopoietic stem cells fail to proliferate in the dorsal aorta before seeding the sites of secondary hematopoiesis, an effect mediated by Wnt9a and its cognate receptor Fzd9b.13 However, how the membrane availability of Fzd9b is regulated remains incompletely understood. Here, we demonstrate that Fzd9b is ubiquitinated, which is required to attenuate the Wnt9a signal. We identify that the E3 ubiquitin ligase TRIP12 is required to restrict Wnt9a/Fzd9b activity by reducing cell surface availability and increasing trafficking to the lysosome. This impact on cell surface expression is semi-selective to only a subset of Fzd receptors. Finally, we show that Trip12 plays a role in zebrafish hematopoietic stem cell development, a Wnt9a/Fzd9b dependent event.

Results

Ubiquitination of Fzd9b at K437 attenuates the Wnt9a signal

We have previously reported that Wnt9a and Fzd9b form a cognate pairing required for zebrafish hematopoietic stem cell development.11,12 Tight regulation of Wnt signals is vital to homeostasis, particularly in stem cell niches; however, the regulation of the Wnt9a/Fzd9b signal is incompletely understood. Post-translational modifications such as ubiquitination are essential regulators of receptor trafficking and degradation.10,14 Therefore, we sought to identify potential ubiquitin modifications impacting Fzd9b function.

To do so, we generated a stable human embryonic kidney (HEK)293 cell line harboring an expression construct containing zebrafish Fzd9b fused to a V5 tag15 and an AviTag16 (Fzd9b-V5-AVI) followed by a ribosomal skip sequence (P2A) and the biotinylating enzyme BirAR118G (Figures 1A and 1B)17 to improve the efficacy of precipitating Fzd9b. The Fzd9b-V5-AVI cells stably expressed Fzd9b (Figure S1A). To ensure that the Fzd9b-V5-AVI was functional, we used an established reporter assay, Super TOP Flash, which responds to Wnt/β-catenin signaling,18 and found that Fzd9b-V5-AVI was functional in response to Wnt9a, albeit at slightly lower levels (Figure S1B). Further, an exogenous dosage of 0.2 mg/L biotin increased the level of Fzd9b biotinylation (Figure S1C), without impacting on the Wnt9a/Fzd9b signal (Figure S1D).

In the absence of pulldown, we noted a faint, higher molecular weight band around 110 kDa (white arrowhead, Figure S1A) in addition to the expected size of Fzd9b (black arrowhead, Figure S1A), suggesting potential post-translational modifications with larger molecular weights such as the addition of multiple 8 kDa ubiquitin moieties and/or aggregations. To determine if Fzd9b was modified, and if Wnt9a stimulation changed the status of ubiquitination, we treated Fzd9b-V5-AVI cells with conditioned medium collected from Chinese hamster ovary (CHO) cells expressing Wnt9a, or naive CHO cells (mock) for 1-min, precipitated Fzd9b-V5-AVI using streptavidin beads, and assessed pulldown (PD) fractions using antibodies to V5 and ubiquitin (Figures 1A and 1B). We then imaged these simultaneously on the same blot using LiCor imaging (Figure 1C). We observed multiple Fzd9b/V5 bands at the predicted sizes around 70 kDa (Figure 1C, black arrowhead).11 Fzds are known to be glycosylated during maturation, and these multiple bands likely represent different states of maturation, as discussed in previous literature.11 We also noted the enrichment of smears of bands at larger molecular weights (Figure 1C, white arrowheads), which may represent aggregate dimers and trimers that multi-pass transmembrane receptors commonly form on immunoblots. We could also detect a smeared Fzd9b band near 100 kDa (Figure 1C, purple bracket). We did not note any major changes in modified or unmodified Fzd9b abundance with Wnt9a treatment (Figure 1C). To determine if these multiple Fzd9b moieties may represent a ubiquitinated receptor, we immunoblotted for total ubiquitin and found the presence of ubiquitinated smears between about 70 and 135 kDa (Figure 1C, green bracket). These smears are typical of polyubiquitinated proteins,19 and suggested that Fzd9b might be ubiquitinated. To test if longer treatments of Wnt9a led to changes in the enrichment of the ubiquitinated form of Fzd9b, we treated cells for 25 or 50 min, and found that there was a slight increase in ubiquitinated signal (Figure S1E). To confirm that there were ubiquitin moieties specifically attached to Fzd9b, we precipitated Fzd9b from untreated cells, separated these by SDS PAGE, stained with Coomassie Blue, and extracted a band around 100 kDa (Figure 1D), and used mass spectrometry to identify peptides with a signature C-terminal glycine-glycine moiety indicative of ubiquitination.20 Using this approach, we identified the peptide TEGTNTEK(GG)LEK, corresponding to a site of ubiquitination at K437 of Fzd9b (Figure 1E). We validated this using a synthetic Fzd9b peptide and found identical results (Figure S1F), indicating that Fzd9b is ubiquitinated at K437.

The ubiquitination site at K437 is in the third intracellular loop of Fzd9b (Figure 2A). There is a very high degree of conservation in the amino acid sequence surrounding K437 among vertebrates (Figure 2B), implicating the importance of this residue. We tested the impact of losing the ubiquitination site at K437 on Wnt9a/Fzd9b signaling using a Super TOP Flash assay. We compared K437R (Arginine (R) is structurally similar to Lysine (K), but cannot be ubiquitinated) to WT Fzd9b, and showed close to a 3-fold increase in Wnt activity in Fzd9bK437R (Figure 2C), indicating that this residue plays a role in limiting Wnt9a signaling activity independent of bulk protein levels of Fzd9b at the level of a plate of cells (Figure S2A). In human FZD9, K440 is the residue conserved from zebrafish Fzd9b. To test if this ubiquitination site plays a role in human WNT9A and FZD9 signaling, we tested the signaling capacity of the FZD9K440R in a Super TOP Flash assay. We found an analogous increase in signaling (Figure 2D), with no effect on protein expression (Figure S2B), suggesting that the function of this residue is conserved and supporting the notion that K437 (or K440 in humans) delimits the Wnt9a signal. We used our established Fzd9b antibody, which binds to an extracellular epitope (Figure 1B) to compare the cell surface expression of WT Fzd9b and Fzd9bK437R, and found that the loss of this ubiquitination site leads to the stabilization of Fzd9b at the cell surface (Figures 2E and S2C). Taken together, these data indicate that Fzd9b ubiquitination at K437 leads to the destabilization of cell surface expression to delimit the Wnt9a signal.

Trip12-mediated ubiquitination dampens the Wnt9a/Fzd9b signal

By modeling Wnt signals in human (e.g., HEK293) cells with zebrafish proteins, we can uncover conserved molecular mechanisms of signal regulation.11,12,13 Through APEX2-mediated proximity ligation in HEK293 cells, we previously identified human proteins enriched at the intracellular regions of zebrafish Fzd9b in response to zebrafish Wnt9a.11 From this dataset, we identified ubiquitin ligases that were associated with Fzd9b, and found that the E3 ubiquitin ligase Thyroid hormone Receptor Interacting Protein 12 (TRIP12) had the highest increase in enrichment upon Wnt9a stimulation, followed by other positive regulators of ubiquitination including MYC binding protein 2 (MYCBP2), MSL complex subunit 2 (MSL2), RB binding protein 6 (RBBP6), and UBA domain containing 2 (UBAC2) (Figure 3A). Using siRNAs targeted to each of these human mRNAs, paired with a Super TOP Flash assay, we found that the suppression of only two of these targets, TRIP12 or MYCBP2, led to an increase in Wnt9a/Fzd9b signaling (Figures 3B and S3A–S3C), suggesting that these play a role in dampening the Wnt9a/Fzd9b signal. TRIP12 was prioritized for follow-up analysis as it was the E3 ubiquitin ligase most dynamically recruited to Fzd9b in response to Wnt9a (Figure 3A), and it had the strongest impact on Wnt9a/Fzd9b activity (Figures 3B, S3B, and S3C). Knockdown of TRIP12 led to an increase in human FZD9 signaling activity (Figure 3C), supporting the conservation of this interaction. We found that the overexpression of zebrafish Trip12 nearly ablated the Wnt9a/Fzd9b signal (Figure 3D), while Trip12 did not impact Fzd9b lacking the putative ubiquitination site (Fzd9bK437R) activity, indicating that K437 is the site of Trip12 function (Figure 3E). Trip12 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase with a highly conserved catalytic cysteine at C2020 (C1959A in humans, Figure S3D), which, when substituted for by an alanine, ablates ubiquitin ligase activity.21 Consistent with this, Trip12C2020A did not impact Wnt9a/Fzd9b signaling (Figure S3E), indicating that Trip12 ubiquitin ligase activity impacts Fzd9b function. To validate our findings, we used CRISPR/Cas9 to generate HEK293 Super TOP Flash cells lacking TRIP12 (Figures S3F and 3F; hereafter referred to as TRIP12 KO cells) and found that the complete ablation of TRIP12 protein also led to an increase in Wnt9a/Fzd9b signaling, which could be rescued with the overexpression of Trip12 (Figure 3G). Altogether, these results demonstrate that Trip12 acts to limit the Wnt9a/Fzd9b activation through the ubiquitination of K437 on Fzd9b.

Fzd9b cell surface availability is limited by Trip12-mediated ubiquitination at K437

The E3 ubiquitin ligases RNF43 and ZNRF43 have been shown to broadly regulate the cell surface availability of FZD receptors by targeting them for lysosomal degradation,7,8,9 leading us to hypothesize that Trip12 may impact Fzd9b expression in the same way. To study Fzd9b expression, we used our previously established stable Fzd9b-mKate13 cells and introduced Trip12 with a C-terminal HA tag (Trip12-HA). We found that exogenous Trip12-HA led to the loss of Fzd9b expression while Trip12C2020A-HA (a catalytically inactive mutant) did not (Figures 4A and 4B). Interestingly, the impact of Trip12 seems to be variable on a cell-to-cell basis since some cells had very limited impact (Figures 4A and 4B). These data indicated that the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of Trip12 at K437 decreases Fzd9b availability for signaling.

Ubiquitination of proteins can lead to diverse intracellular outcomes, including the degradation of proteins through either the proteasomal or lysosomal pathways.22 The small molecules MG132 and BafilomycinA1 inhibit the proteasome and lysosomes, respectively.23,24 The treatment of Fzd9b-V5-AVI cells with MG132 or BafilomycinA1, followed by Wnt9a treatment, V5-tag immunoprecipitation, and subsequent immunoblotting showed an increase in higher molecular weight Fzd9b following treatment with either compound (Figure 4C, white arrowheads), suggesting that both the lysosome and proteasome normally degrade Fzd9b. In addition, there was an increase in the ubiquitinated proteins around the ∼100 kDa sized Fzd9b moieties (Figure 1C), especially in response to BafilomycinA1 compared to vehicle treatment (Figures 4C and 4D), suggesting that ubiquitinated Fzd9b is degraded primarily by the lysosome. We observed a further increase in ubiquitinated Fzd9b with Wnt9a treatment (Figures 4C and 4D), suggesting that adding Wnt9a treatment leads to Fzd9b trafficking toward the lysosome. We verified that the addition of MG132 led to an increase in modified β-catenin, as previously reported25 (Figure S4A), indicating the modest impact noted on Fzd9b accumulation was not due to poor MG132 activity.

Taken together, the data above (Figures 2C–2E, 4A, and 4B) suggested that the Trip12 ubiquitination of Fzd9b may lead to a reduction in Fzd9b cell surface levels, and targeting to the lysosome. Surprisingly, neither Wnt9a or exogenous Trip12 expression seems to impact on total Fzd9b levels in a pool of cells (Figure S4B), suggesting that Trip12 only regulates small pools of Fzd9b protein and/or cells at any given time. Using Fzd9b-mKate expressing cells, we found that cells with Trip12 lost Fzd9b expression and increased co-localization of Fzd9b with LAMP1+ vesicles, supporting targeting to the lysosome, which increased in Trip12+ cells (Figures 4E and 4F). To assess the impact of Trip12 on the cell surface expression of Fzd9b at a more global level, we transfected HEK293 Fzd9b-V5-AVI cells with either Trip12-IRES-GFP or GFP alone, and treated with Wnt9a conditioned medium after 48 h, to assess the cell surface level of Fzd9b by flow cytometry 24 h after Wnt9a treatment (Figure 4G). We then compared GFP+ populations and noted that both Wnt9a and Trip12 alone led to the slight destabilization of Fzd9b at the membrane, an effect that was roughly additive (Figure 4G). These data suggest that Trip12 can act on Fzd9b independently of Wnt9a, and that destabilizing Fzd9b at the cell surface may act as a mechanism of negative control of the signal. We observed a similar phenomenon with human FZD9 (Figure S4C, compare Trip12+ cells marked with white arrowheads to Trip12 negative cells marked with blue arrowheads). Altogether, these data support a model where Fzd9b cell surface availability is limited by Trip12-mediated ubiquitination at K437, and that Fzd9b is degraded by the lysosome.

Trip12 exhibits some functional specificity for Wnt9a/Fzd9b signaling

Loss-of-function mutations in other E3 ubiquitin ligases targeting Fzds, such as ZNRF43 and RNF3, are tumorigenic.7,9,10 In a broad survey of human cancer samples across The Cancer Genome Atlas, we identified 69 different DNA mutations leading to premature STOP codons in the TRIP12 coding sequence, all of which are predicted to ablate the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity (Figure S5A). These mutations were present in every cancer type examined, suggesting that the TRIP12 modification of FZD activity may play a broader role in cancer than previously appreciated. Consistent with this, aligning the residues of the third intracellular loop of all zebrafish Fzd proteins revealed that of the 4K residues, only K437 in zebrafish (or K440 in humans) is conserved across all human FZDs, except for FZD8 (Figure 5A) and zebrafish Fzds (Figure S5B), suggesting that TRIP12 could impact Wnt signaling broadly. Consistent with this, Wnt3a, which is known to signal through most FZD receptors,26 had nearly 6× more signaling in TRIP12 KO cells, as assessed by Super TOP Flash activity (Figure 5B). This data suggested that TRIP12 may act on multiple FZD receptors present in HEK293 cells. To identify the FZDs impacted by Trip12, we used a previously established HEK293 line lacking FZD1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 8,26 and re-expressed each of the FZD receptors in the context of Wnt3a and/or Trip12 and found that only Wnt3a/FZD1 (Figure 5C) and Wnt3a/FZD4 (Figure 5D) signaling were negatively impacted by Trip12 expression. Of note, the FZD8 signal was enhanced with Trip12 expression (Figure 5E), suggesting that there is some degree of specificity for the TRIP12-mediated ubiquitination of Fzd9b/FZD9 in modifying Wnt signals. As previously shown, FZD2, 5, and 7 signaled well with Wnt3a, but were not impacted by Trip12 expression (Figures S5C–S5E), FZD3, 6, 9, and 10 had extremely low activity with Wnt3a (Figures S5F–S5I).26 Together, these data suggest that individual FZDs have unique mechanisms of regulation by Trip12.

To better understand how Trip12 could positively influence FZD8 while negatively influencing Fzd9b/FZD9, we expressed Trip12 in our established Fzd8a-mKate cell line.13 While we found that Fzd8a and Trip12-HA were co-localized to LAMP1+ lysosomes (Figure 5F, orange arrowheads), we also found the abundant expression of Fzd8a at the cell periphery in Trip12+ cells (Figure 5F, white arrowheads), and there was no change in overall Fzd8a-mKate expression on a cell by cell basis (Figure 5F), suggesting that Fzd8a and Fzd9b are differentially regulated in response to Trip12. To assess if Trip12 differentially impacts the localization of other Fzds, we generated cell lines expressing Fzd3a-mKate, Fzd5-mKate, or Fzd7a-mKate, a selection of receptors from each Fzd subfamily (Figure S5B). Like Fzd9b and Fzd8a—Fzd3a, Fzd5 and Fzd7a were all localized to lysosomes (Figures 5G–5I, orange arrowheads). In contrast to Fzd9b—Fzd3a, Fzd5 and Fzd7a were still abundant at the cell periphery in Trip12+ cells (Figures 5G–5I, white arrowheads). Furthermore, cell by cell Fzd3a-mKate and Fzd7a-mKate expression were unaffected by Trip12 (Figures 5G and 5I). Interestingly, there was an increase in Fzd5-mKate expression in response to Trip12 (Figure 5H). Together, these data indicate different Fzds are differentially regulated by Trip12.

Trip12 is required for hematopoietic stem cell development

The pairing of Wnt9a and Fzd9b is required for hematopoietic stem cell proliferation in the aorta in zebrafish.11,12 Hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells are marked by several molecular markers, including cmyb, cd41, and gata2b. In the absence of the Wnt9a/Fzd9b signal, cmyb+ cells are decreased at 40 hours post fertilization (hpf).11,12 We therefore hypothesized that a loss of Trip12 would lead to an increase in Fzd9b receptor availability to increase Wnt9a/Fzd9b signaling, ultimately leading to an increase in cmyb+ cells at 40 hpf. Injection of a translation blocking (ATG) antisense morpholino (MO) targeting trip12 showed an increase in cmyb expression at 40 hpf (Figure 6A), suggesting an increase in hematopoietic stem cells. After hematopoietic stem cells emerge from the dorsal aorta, they proceed to seed the caudal hematopoietic tissue, where we observed a rise in cd41+ (Figures 6B and 6C)27 and gata2b+ (Figure S6A)28 hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells in MO treated animals at 72 hpf (Figure 6B, grayscale).27 The ATG MO was rescued by trip12 mRNA (Figure S6A), and we also observed an increase in gata2b+ cells in animals treated with a second trip12 MO which blocked splicing (Figure S6B), indicating specificity of the treatment. Combining fzd9b MO and trip12 MO led to a normal number of gata2b+ cells at 72 hpf (Figure 6D), further supporting that Trip12 exerts its impact through Fzd9b. To test if the E3 ubiquitin ligase activity of Trip12 was required for hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell development, we rescued trip12 MO-treated animals with either trip12 WT or trip12 C2020A mRNA. We found that mRNA encoding the enzymatically dead Trip12 was not able to rescue loss of Trip12 (Figure 6E), indicating that E3 ubiquitin ligase activity is also required for correct hematopoietic stem cell development. Altogether, these data support a role for Trip12 E3 ubiquitin ligase activity in dampening Fzd9b function during hematopoietic stem cell development.

Discussion

The tight regulation of Wnt signaling is critical during development and homeostasis, partly due to its essential role in cellular proliferation. For example, we have shown that Wnt9a and Fzd9b are essential for the developmental proliferation of hematopoietic stem cells.11,12 Here, we demonstrate that Trip12 limits the Wnt9a/Fzd9b signal during hematopoietic stem cell development through its E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. Mechanistically, Trip12 ubiquitinates Fzd9b in the third intracellular loop at K437, leading to its destabilization from the cell surface and lysosomal targeting, thus limiting pathway activation. This impact is semi-selective for different Fzd receptors and can occur in the absence of Wnt9a (Figure 7).

A model for the Trip12 regulation of Fzd9b and hematopoietic stem cell development

In the presence of Trip12, Fzd9b is ubiquitinated at K437, leading to its removal from the cell surface and targeting to the lysosome. This leads to a diminished Wnt9a/Fzd9b signal and reduced hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells in vivo, consistent with Wnt9a/Fzd9b function in driving proliferation. This function of Trip12 is independent of Wnt9a but is enhanced in the presence of Wnt9a.

The balance of ubiquitination and deubiquitination has been proposed to regulate Wnt signaling sensitivity to cells through trafficking Fzd receptors to the lysosome or the plasma membrane, as demonstrated with Fzd4.29 Other groups have shown the importance of different ubiquitin ligases in regulating Wnt signals through downregulating cell surface expression of different FZD receptors. For example, the transmembrane receptors and E3 ubiquitin ligases RNF43 and ZNRF3 are important drivers of FZD stability at the plasma membrane: RNF43 regulates FZD1, FZD5 and FZD7 cell surface expression,9 while ZNRF3 regulates FZD4, FZD5, FZD6, and FZD8,7 with a preference for FZD6.30 Mechanistically, RNF43 and ZNRF3 function to ubiquitinate cytosolic lysine residues,9 a process that is inhibited by introducing the secreted ligand R-Spondin, which acts in concert with the seven-pass transmembrane LGR4/5 receptors.7

The regulation of FZD cell surface expression by RNF43 and ZNRF3 is thought to occur through the lysosome.9,10,29 Our findings here show that TRIP12 regulates Fzd9b/FZD9 cell surface availability through the ubiquitination of K437 in the third intracellular loop, which leads to lysosomal targeting (Figure 4F). However, this mechanism is distinct from the transmembrane receptors RNF43 and ZNRF3, since TRIP12 is located intracellularly. TRIP12 is enriched to Fzd9b upon Wnt9a stimulation (Figure 3A), and there is a Wnt9a-dependent enhancement of Fzd9b regulation, though Trip12 also acts in the absence of Wnt9a stimulation (Figure 4G). This is further supported by the enhancement of ubiquitinated Fzd9b in response to Wnt9a stimulation, especially in the presence of BafilomycinA1 (Figures 4C and 4D). Interestingly, we observed that the K437 ubiquitination site is conserved across all FZDs (Figures 5A and S5B), and Wnt3a (which signals through most FZDs) signaling activity, was also impacted in TRIP12 KO cells (Figure 5B). This prompted us to determine the degree to which Trip12 impacts signaling with other FZDs, where we noted that only a subset of Wnt3a/FZDs were impacted by Trip12 (Figures 5C–5E). Furthermore, although all the Fzds we examined could be found in the lysosome (likely due to normal receptor recycling), only a subset of these seems to be removed from the cell surface in response to Trip12 overexpression (Figures 5F–5I). In fact, the Wnt3a/FZD8 signal and Fzd8a plasma membrane localization seem to be enhanced by Trip12 (Figures 5E and 5F), supporting that Trip12 has differential functions in the context of different Wnt signals. This further implies that there may be additional layers of regulation required for Fzd9b-specific ubiquitination by Trip12. For example, we previously showed that the EGFR phosphorylation of the Fzd9b C-terminal tail is necessary to initiate endocytosis and Wnt9a signaling. It is tempting to speculate that such modifications are a prerequisite for Trip12 function on FZD9/Fzd9b as well. Other Trip12-regulated proteins require similar layered control. The targets BAF57, APP-BP1, and ARF all first need a signal that frees them from their functional binding partners before TRIP12 gains access to them and ubiquitinates them.31 Finally, our APEX2 data isolated proteins within a ∼10 nm radius32; however, it is formally possible that the interaction with FZD9/Fzd9b is indirect.

We observed that Wnt9a and Trip12 do not impact overall Fzd9b abundance when examined in a pool of cells (Figures 1C, 4C, and S4B). However, the ubiquitinated pool of Fzd9b is increased with Wnt9a treatment (Figures 4C and 4D), and Fzd9b is lost in the presence of Trip 12 in selected cells (Figures 4A and 4B). We have also observed a shift in the cell surface expression of Fzd9b in response to both Trip12 and Wnt9a (Figure 4G), as well as in the Fzd9b K437R mutant, which cannot be ubiquitinated at this site (Figure 2E). These data support a model where only a portion of the Fzd9b pool is ubiquitinated at a given time, and that only certain cells are “competent” to be impacted by Trip12. This may also relate to published roles for Trip12 that are dependent on the cell cycle state, tissue type, or subcellular localization, for example.31,33,34,35,36 Despite this modest impact on the pool of Fzd9b protein and the cell surface levels of Fzd9b, loss of the ubiquitination site at K437 leads to a 3-fold increase in Wnt9a/Fzd9b signaling activity (Figures 2C and 2D), indicating that although the pool of ubiquitinated Fzd9b is limited, there is still an impact on function. It will be exciting to see in the future how these cells are made “competent” for this function, and the degree to which this impacts signaling at a single cell level. When comparing the pool of ubiquitinated Fzd9b regulated by the proteasome (MG132, Figure 4C) to the pool regulated by the lysosome (Bafilomycin, Figure 4C), it appears as though more ubiquitinated Fzd9b is processed through the lysosome than the proteasome. This also points to a model where there are different pools of Fzd9b; some may be signaling competent, while others may not. This also may reflect differences in signaling requirements across tissues and Fzd receptors. For example, in Drosophila imaginal discs, Frizzled2 is sent for degradation only after a signal-competent complex with Arrow (equivalent to LRP6) is formed.37 Additionally, there are tissue specific differences in degradation routes and timing of Drosophila Frizzled2.38 Although we did not detect other ubiquitination sites on Fzd9b in this study, this may be related to relative instability and/or abundance of peptides targeted with ubiquitination, especially given that RNF43 and ZNRF3 sites are mapped to different intracellular locations.7,9,10,30,39 The recruitment of other ubiquitin ligases in response to Wnt9a also suggests that further ubiquitination of Fzd9b occurs, which we have not yet detected. This is likely due to the relative instability of ubiquitinated proteins and the seemingly small pool of ubiquitinated Fzd9b.19

TRIP12 function is incompletely understood, although its dysregulation is implicated in multiple diseases.31 TRIP12 promotes proteolysis of at least nine substrates, including chromatin regulators,40,41 ubiquitin machinery,34,36 and transcription factors,21,35 among others.42,43,44 In addition to these bona fide substrates, TRIP12 has been noted to interact with a huge swath of proteins in the cell, including several receptors; however, targeting these interacting proteins has yet to be shown.31 Interestingly, TRIP12 is reported to be localized in the nucleus in cell lines and human tissues.33,36 Although we found a predominant nuclear signal, Trip12 was also expressed outside the nucleus (Figure 4A). Consistent with its hypothesized role in regulating diverse cellular substrates, mutations in TRIP12 are commonly identified in cancers, including those of the blood. Notably, premature STOP codons identified (Figure S5A) would all be predicted to impact ubiquitin ligase function. Our findings here show that TRIP12 impacts Wnt9a/Fzd9b signaling and now link TRIP12 function to the regulation of the WNT pathway, which is also widely implicated in cancer initiation and progression.1,45,46,47,48 It has been challenging to generate therapeutics targeting the WNT pathway due to its pleiotropic requirements in homeostasis. Identifying specific WNT pathways in vivo will be essential to derive therapies that more specifically target cancer cells. The interaction between TRIP12 and FZD9 may represent one such avenue for therapeutic intervention.

Limitations of the study

Much of the characterization was conducted using HEK293 cells, which are a suitable model for Wnt signaling biology, but do not account for all the context-dependent requirements of Wnt signaling. There may be missing or extra cell-dependent cofactors, for example, which could dictate different functions in other cell types. This may be one of the underlying causes for differences in how TRIP12 impacts FZD receptors. At the stages examined in this study, the sex of the zebrafish is unknown. A 50:50 distribution can be assumed.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell culture

All TOP Flash luciferase assays were conducted in cell lines derived from our previously established female HEK29350 cells with a stably integrated Super TOP Flash reporter.18 All cells were grown under standard conditions at 37°C and 5% CO2 with media supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. HEK293 cells used Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) as a base, while Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) cells were grown in a 50:50 mix of DMEM and F12. Cell lines were not further authenticated beyond receipt from ATCC. Mycoplasma testing was conducted monthly, and cells were always negative. HEK293 TOP Flash cells lacking TRIP12 were generated using CRISPR/Cas9 as previously described with sgRNAs 5’ AGACATTGCAGCATGGGTGA 3’ and 5’ GAACGTCGCAGTGACCCCCC 3’, which are predicted to cut out approximately 280 base pairs of genomic DNA, including the start codon and predicted upstream basal promoter. Loss of protein was verified using immunoblotting (see below) and sequencing. HEK293 TOP Flash cells lacking FZD1, 2, 4, 5, 7 and 8 were a generous gift from Michael Boutros.26

Zebrafish

Zebrafish were maintained and propagated according to Van Andel Institute and local Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee policies under animal use protocol #24-08-024. AB∗ zebrafish were used as wild-type animals in all experiments. At the developmental ages analyzed, it is not possible to identify males and females; an approximate 50:50 ratio can be assumed. The ages of analysis are indicated in figures and legends. Animals were randomly assigned to experimental groups. The Tg(cd41:eGFP)la2Tg, Tg(gata2b:KalTA4)sd32Tg, Tg(UAS:GFP)mu271 and Tg(kdrl:mCherry-CAAX)y171 lines have been previously described.28,51,52 The ATG MO for trip12 with the sequence 5’- GACATTGGCACCTCTCTCCTGAAG-3’ was acquired from GeneTools. 1-cell stage zygotes were injected with ATG-blocking trip12 MO at dosages indicated in the figures. Embryos and larvae were cultured to the ages indicated in figures in Essential 3 (E3) medium (5 mM NaCl, 0.17 mM KCl, 0.33 mM CaCl2, 0.33 mM MgSO4, 10-5 % Methylene Blue). After 24 hours, embryos were moved to E3 with 3 x 10-4 % N-Phenylthiourea (PTU, Sigma P7629) to halt pigmentation.

Method details

Generation of experimental tools

Transgenic cell lines

Antibiotic-selected clonal transgenic cell lines were generated as previously described,11 using the PiggyBAC transposase system.53 Clonal lines were validated using immunoblotting and Wnt reporter assays.

Wnt conditioned medium

Wnt protein-containing conditioned media was prepared from transgenic CHO cell lines as previously described,13 using a doxycycline-inducible promoter to temporally control expression of Wnt protein.

Plasmids

Expression constructs were generated using Gibson Assembly and/or PCR from plasmids harboring a CMV promoter, or a doxycycline-inducible promoter system. Fzd9b, Fzd8a and FZD9 plasmids were previously described.11,13 Mutations in Fzds were introduced using site-directed mutagenesis. Trip12 cDNA (NM_001347668.1), Fzd3a (NM_001042761.2), Fzd5 (NM_131134.2), and Fzd7a (NM_131139.2) were PCR amplified from zebrafish cDNA. FZD1-10 cDNAs were a generous gift from Karl Willert. AVI Tag (GLNDIFEAQKIEWHE) coding sequence was inserted via site-directed mutagenesis. BirA (R118G) was synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies. All plasmids were sequence validated by full-plasmid sequencing.

Experimental details

Super TOP flash assays

Luciferase reporter assays were performed with either transient transfection or stable lines, as indicated in the figure legends, and as previously described.13 Each experiment was performed with at minimum biological triplicate samples and reproduced at least once with a similar trend. Wnt activity was calculated by normalizing Firefly Luciferase output to Renilla Luciferase; Wnt9a/Fzd9b fold induction was set to 100. siRNA sequences used are available as a Table S1.

Fzd9 enrichment

Enrichment of Fzd9b proteins was accomplished using HEK293 cells stably expressing V5 and Avi tagged zebrafish Fzd9b, and co-expressing BirA after a P2A ribosomal skip sequence. Cells were grown in standard media as described above, supplemented with 0.2mg/L biotin (Sigma, B4639) for at least 24 hours. Cells were treated as described in the figure legends and lysed in RIPA buffer (150mM NaCl, 50mM Tris-HCl, pH7.5, 1% NP40, 0.1% SDS, 12mM deoxycholate) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, 04693132001), phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo, A32957) and 20mM N-Ethylamaleimide (Fisher, E01365G). Biotinylated proteins were enriched using Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin T1 (Fisher, 65601), essentially according to the manufacturer’s recommendations, including five 1 mL washes with ice-cold RIPA buffer. Biotinylated proteins were eluted from the beads by resuspending in Laemmli buffer,54 supplemented with 25mM biotin-D and incubating at 55°C for 15 minutes.

Immunoblotting

Immunoblots were performed according to LiCor manufacturer’s procedures (lysates subjected to blotting for Fzds were not boiled), using primary antibodies for: α-V5, [1:5,000, Cell Signaling Technologies, 13202S], α−β−actin, [1:10,000, Sigma, A2228], 1:500, α-Fzd9b,11 1:750, α-ubiquitin [BioLegend, 646302, Lot# B287176], and α-TRIP12 [1:10,000, Fortis, A301-814A, Lot# 3]. Signals were detected using goat-a-rabbit HRP [1:20,000, Southern BioTech, 4050-05, Lot #A1420-SQ21E], IRDye 800CW Streptavidin (for biotinylated proteins) [1:25,000, LiCor, 926-322230, Lot#D20920-05], goat-α-mouse IR700 [1:20,000, LiCor, 926-68070, Lot#D20419-25] and goat-α-rabbit IR800 [1:20,000, LiCor, 926-32211, Lot#D20510-25].

Flow cytometry

Flow cytometry was conducted using HEK293 cells stably expressing Fzd9b-V5 or Fzd9bK437R-V5 constructs. When noted in figure legends, cells were transfected with a total of 5 μg of plasmid DNA and treated with Wnt9a conditioned medium for 5 minutes 72 hours later. Cells were trypsinized and pellets collected by centrifugation, washed with PBS, resuspended in PBS with 2% FBS and 1mM EDTA, filtered through an 80μm filter, and stained using Fzd9b antibody11 and goat anti-rabbit AlexaFluor 647 (Southern Biotech, 4050-31, lot#E1519 S109Z). Dead cells were excluded using 2 μg/mL DAPI. Cells were sorted using a Beckman Coulter CytoFLEX S.

Mass spectrometry sample preparation

Fzd9b was eluted from streptavidin beads using 25mM biotin D, 2.5% SDS and 5% beta-mercaptoethanol at 95°C for 5 minutes, and proteins were resolved by SDS PAGE and stained using EZBlue (Fisher, G1041), according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The gel band was tryptic digested with Tryptic Digestion Kits according to the manufacturer’s recommendations (Thermo Fisher, 89871). The supernatants were dried down on SpeedVac and resuspended with 10 μl of 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid in H2O. Synthetic peptides for TEGTNTEK(GG)LEK were obtained from Biosynth (Gardner, MA).

Mass spectrometry

Samples were analyzed on an Exploris 480 (Thermo Scientific) attached to a Vanquish Neo nano UPLC (Thermo Scientific). Peptides were separated with nano HPLC column (75 μm x 20 cm, 1.7 μm C18, P/N HEB07502001718IWF, CoAnn Technologies). Mobile phase A was water with 0.1% formic acid. The LC gradient was as follows: 1% B (20% water, 80% acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) to 26% B over 51 minutes, 85% B over 5 minutes, and 98% B over 4 minutes, with a total gradient length of 60 minutes. One full MS scan was collected with a scan range of 350 to 1200 m/z, resolution of 120 K, standard maximum injection time, and Auto Gain Control (AGC). A series of MS2 scans were acquired for the most abundant ions from the MS1 scan using orbitrap analyzer. Ions were filtered with charge 2–5. An isolation window of 1.6m/z was used with quadruple isolation mode. Ions were fragmented using higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) with a collision energy of 30%. For the targeted quantitation, parallel reaction monitoring (PRM) was performed on an Exploris 480 mass spectrometer coupled with the Vanquish Neo LC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A total of 2 μg of digested peptides were separated on a nano capillary column (20 cm × 75 μm I.D., 365 μm O.D., 1.7 μm C18, CoAnn Technologies, Washington) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. Gradient conditions were identical to above. Full MS spectra (m/z 375-1200) were collected at a resolution of 120,000 (FWHM), and MS2 spectra at 30,000 resolutions (FWHM). Standard AGC targets and automatic maximum injection times were used for both full and MS2 scans. A 32% HCD collision energy was used for MS2 fragmentation. All samples were analyzed using a multiplexed PRM method with an unscheduled inclusion list containing the TEGTNTEK(GG)LEK target peptide.

Confocal imaging

Confocal imaging and analysis for Wnt9a/Fzd9b trafficking were done essentially as previously described.13 Briefly, HEK 293 Fzd9b-mKate cells on poly-L-lysine coated glass-bottom 35 mm dishes were cultured to approximately 50% confluence prior to transfection (if used), fixed on glass coverslips stained by immunofluorescence using the primary antibodies (α-V5, [Cell Signaling Technologies, 13202S], α-Fzd9b,11 α-LAMP1, [Cell Signaling Technology, cat# 15665S]), α-HA [Invitrogen, cat#26184, lot# XA337820], and secondary antibodies (1:500 for each, Goat-α-mouse Alexa 488 [cat# A11001, lot# 2189178], goat-α-rabbit Alexa 488 [cat# 4050-30, lot# e1317 NC39], goat-α-mouse Alexa 555 [cat# 1030-32, lot# E2518 ZA08E], goat-α-rabbit Alexa 647 [cat# 4050-31, lot# E1519 S109Z], Southern Biotech). Slides were imaged within one week.

Confocal z-slices were collected using an Andor Dragonfly 620-SR spinning disc mounted on a Leica DMi8 microscope, equipped with 405, 488, 561, 640 and 730 nm laser lines and 5x air, 10x air, 20x air, 40x water, 63x oil and 100x oil objectives. Scanning was sequential and images were collected at a minimum of 1900 x 1900 resolution. Representative images were produced by combining between 1-10 z-slices, as noted in figure legends.

Zebrafish mRNA rescues

The mRNA for rescue experiments was synthesized using the SP6 mMessage machine kit (LifeTech), according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The resultant mRNA was quantified by nanodrop, and dosages are indicated in figures.

qPCR

Standard methods were used to derive RNA and cDNA, and qPCR was performed using FastStart Universal SYBR Green Master Mix (Roche) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Primers used are available as a Table S2.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Mass spectrometry

Proteome Discoverer 3.1 (Thermo Scientific) is used to process raw spectra for mass spectrometry data. The search criteria are protein database; Danio rerio (Taxonomy ID: 7955). Carboxyamidomethylated (+57 Da) at cysteine residues for fixed modifications, GlyGly at lysine (+114 Da), oxidized at methionine (+16 Da) residues, and acetylation at lysine residues (+14 Da) for variable modifications, two maximum allowed missed cleavage, 10 ppm tolerance for MS and MS/MS. All PRM data analysis and integration were performed using Skyline software.55 The transitions' intensity rank order and chromatographic elution were required to match those of synthetic standards for each measured peptide.

Confocal imaging

The mKate pixel intensities were measured using ImageJ analysis of mean pixel intensities on a cell-by-cell basis from summed Z projections of the entire cell diameter. Data were normalized to mKate intensities in Trip12- cells in the same image. The data are pooled from 3 images with 8-10 cells per image measured.

To quantify LAMP1+ vesicles with Fzd9b, ImageJ Comdet v.0.5.5 was used to identify and quantify LAMP1 and Fzd9b-mKate vesicles in z-projections of entire cells using the following settings for all images. Particle size: 5 pixels and smaller; Fzd9b-mKate intensity threshold: 20; LAMP1 intensity threshold:10.

qPCR

qPCR data was analyzed using the 2-ΔΔCt method.56
