Are doctors from the complementary and alternative systems of medicine less equal than their allopathic counterparts? Public sector doctors’ experiences of recruitment from two Indian states
Authors: Bhaskar Purohit, Peter S Hill
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComplementary and alternative systems of medicine, which include Ayurveda, Yoga, and Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, Sowa Rigpa, and Homoeopathy (AYUSH), play a significant role in the Indian healthcare system. Despite many efforts to integrate and mainstream AYUSH, there are significant inequities that disadvantage AYUSH doctors compared to their allopathic counterparts. In this paper, we examine the recruitment-related experiences of contractual AYUSH doctors and make some side-by-side comparisons with those of contractual allopathic doctors from two Indian states.MethodsThis study, on which this paper reports, is set within a larger qualitative study conducted in India to examine the experiences of public sector doctors with Human Resource Management systems. We conducted semi-structured interviews with 61 participants, including 33 frontline doctors and 28 policy actors. We employed purposive sampling to select doctors from two states. Data collection occurred from February to October 2019. Thematic analysis, utilizing the Framework Approach, was applied to organize and synthesize qualitative data based on themes identified from the data. We also developed job histories from the interviews with the doctors to explore their experiences with the recruitment system. The quantitative data gathered through job histories were analysed using frequencies and triangulated with the narrative accounts provided by the doctors.ResultsThe paper reports the discontent of AYUSH and allopathic doctors with the recruitment, but this was consistently worse for AYUSH, especially in State 1, in several ways. One, there were significant discrepancies in salaries and allowances between AYUSH and allopathic doctors. Two, AYUSH doctors experienced stagnated career progressions and high job insecurity. Three, the system sabotaged AYUSH doctors’ expectations of progressing to regular recruitment in State 1. And four, AYUSH doctors perceived the system to be highly inequitable and unresponsive towards their concerns, particularly in State 1, with critical implications for health services.ConclusionThis paper highlights the extreme form of inequity perceived and experienced by the contractual AYUSH doctors. The policy and institutional environment surrounding AYUSH integration is weak, and there is a significant failure of institutions to meet the expectations of these doctors. Additionally, there are insufficient translations between policy and practice, leaving larger questions about holistic integration and the inclusion of AYUSH unresolved. Our findings suggest that the subtle nuances discussed in the paper indicate a bias toward allopathic doctors, which may further lead to the marginalization of AYUSH.Keywords: Contractual recruitment, AYUSH doctors, Human resource management, Doctors, India

Introduction

Indian healthcare practice is pluralistic, with the coexistence of traditional, complementary, and alternative medicine (TCAM), which generally refers to those primarily practising Ayurveda, Yoga, Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, Sowa Rigpa, and Homoeopathy (AYUSH) alongside allopathic medicine [1]. AYUSH is widely practised in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), such as India, and the contributions of AYUSH practitioners are significant in health system strengthening, patient care, and the delivery of primary healthcare services [2, 3]. To introduce medical pluralism into the delivery of health services and provide access to health services, Indian reforms have aimed at integrating the AYUSH systems of medicine [4]. One of the main initiatives by the national government has been the formulation and implementation of a country-wide programme, called the National Rural Health Mission (NRHM), rolled out in 2005 (the NRHM is now called the National Health Mission or NHM) aimed at mainstreaming the AYUSH. Under the NHM, a nationwide policy has been pursued to integrate AYUSH into the public health system since 2005. The AYUSH integration policy involves the co-location of AYUSH doctors and biomedical doctors at the same public health centres [5, 6]. To achieve mainstreaming and integration of the AYUSH and improve the availability of doctors in the rural and underserved areas, NHM’s main strategy has been to appoint AYUSH and allopathic doctors on annual contracts [7]. Consequently, this led many state governments to increase the establishment of AYUSH centres in their public healthcare system, and a large number of allopathic and AYUSH doctors were appointed on contracts under the NHM [8]. Since the inception of NHM in 2005, many AYUSH doctors have been recruited on contracts [9]—by April 2014, 10,933 AYUSH doctors and 5419 AYUSH paramedical staff were recruited [10]. Also, by 2014, 331 (44.3%) of the District Hospitals (DH), 1885 (36.3%) Community Health Centres (CHC), and 8461 (34.6%) Primary Health Centres (PHC) across India were co-located with AYUSH facilities [10].

Despite the concerted efforts to integrate and mainstream AYUSH, and the large number of contractual recruitments, challenges like inequitable administrative procedures and power relations have continued to impede the integration of AYUSH [5]. Other challenges, such as low budgets for AYUSH facilities, non-functional laboratories, and bureaucratic hospital norms, have impeded the mainstreaming process of AYUSH [11]. Contractual AYUSH doctors experience systemic challenges such as inadequate infrastructure and supplies, unethical interpersonal arrangements, and limited support from non-AYUSH personnel [12]. Thus, AYUSH continues to hold a subordinate place in the public health system compared to allopathic medicine, with reported power asymmetries, professional hierarchies, and inequities in the system that disadvantage the AYUSH doctors [4, 13]. Scholars argue that the limited integration has resulted in the marginalization of AYUSH [4].

Additionally, when compared to contractual allopathic doctors and other NHM staff, AYUSH doctors face significant Human Resource Management (HRM) related disparities, including inequitable salaries that preclude increments and allowances, stagnated careers, and substantial delays in contract renewals [12, 14]. Other studies from the Indian context have highlighted issues with the management of human resources as a critical factor affecting AYUSH integration [5].

One of the most significant disparities faced by NHM contractual AYUSH doctors is the lack of opportunities for civil service recruitment—a recruitment pathway that offers ongoing/permanent employment. In the Indian context, civil service recruitment is also referred to as ‘regular’ recruitment and is carried out by an autonomous body called the Public Service Commission (PSC), established in each state. The lack of opportunities for regular recruitment creates further disparities for AYUSH doctors, such as lower salaries, high job insecurity, lack of entitlement to service-related benefits, and stagnated career progression, compared to regular allopathic doctors [15].

The reforms aimed at integrating AYUSH and the periodic assurances from the health administration to recruit AYUSH into regular service have raised expectations among these doctors, but progress has been uneven across states. Because health is a state matter in India, AYUSH doctors’ interactions and experiences with the system can vary significantly between states, leading to conspicuous inequities and a breach of their expectations. Nevertheless, these issues remain largely unexplored in the HRM space.

This paper is part of a larger multi-case qualitative research study conducted in two Indian states to explore the experiences of public sector doctors with three HRM sub-systems: recruitment, initial postings, and subsequent postings and transfers (PT). Results from the broader research on initial postings and subsequent PT reveal issues of poor policy implementation and negative perceptions among doctors toward these sub-systems [16, 17]. Similarly, the recruitment component of the study examined the experiences of regular and contractual allopathic doctors. It highlights key HRM issues such as extended contractual employment and bureaucratic obstacles that limit their opportunities of becoming civil servants [18]. Along with the issues discussed in our broader research, we identified several recruitment and employment challenges faced by AYUSH doctors in one of the study states, which justifies the independent analysis presented in this paper.

Although the government has supported AYUSH with an integration policy for nearly two decades, resulting in thousands of AYUSH doctors working under the NHM, significant differences still exist between these states. Therefore, in this paper, we focus on the key recruitment and employment experiences of contractual AYUSH doctors, especially from one state, and compare how these experiences differ from those of AYUSH and allopathic doctors in the two states.

Methods

Study design and setting

This research is a part of a larger embedded, multiple-case qualitative study conducted in two Indian states to examine the experiences of public sector doctors with three HRM sub-systems (recruitment, first postings, and subsequent PT). The states were purposively selected as two distinct cases, with divergence in relation to geography, demographics, health indicators, and key health workforce indicators. The three HRM sub-systems were defined as units of analysis embedded within the two cases. Doctors working with the health department and the National Health Mission (NHM) were the main focus of the study. The employment status of doctors (regular and contract) was the sub-unit of analysis. The two states have been anonymized and referred to as State 1 and State 2. The two states were purposively selected for their demographic and socio-economic contrasts. State 1 is in the north-eastern part of India and is a small state by geographic area with a population of less than a million. The Infant Mortality Rate (IMR) of the state was 30 per 1000 live births, and the Under-5 (U-5) mortality rate was 32 per 1000 live births [19]. State 2 is located in the western part of India and is one of the largest Indian states by geographic area. The IMR of the state is 41 per 1000 live births, and the U-5 mortality rate was 51 per 1000 live births [19].

Selection of participants and data collection

For this qualitative study, we conducted semi-structured interviews with 61 participants, including 33 frontline doctors and 28 policy actors, with an average interview duration of 36 min for the frontline doctors and 32 min for the policy actors, respectively. Those who agreed to participate in the study were included after signing a written consent form. The lead author (BP) conducted the data collection from February to October 2019.

The interviews with doctors focused on understanding their experiences with recruitment in the two states. Purposive sampling with maximum variation was used to include the doctors from the two states to have a representation of: (a) AYUSH and allopathic doctors; (b) doctors working on contractual and regular recruitment; and (c) doctors with varied periods of employment. The primary inclusion criterion for selecting doctors for the study was a minimum service of at least two years with the government.

Due to the geographical, demographic, and socio-economic differences between the study states, there were some variations in sampling between the two states. Of the 33 frontline doctors, 15 were from State 1, which included four AYUSH and six allopathic doctors working on contracts, as well as five allopathic doctors working under regular employment. Considering that State 1 is small, with only four districts and a limited number of doctors, we ensured representation from all four districts among the doctors. State 1 had only a small number of AYUSH doctors, with only 12 at the time we conducted the study, all of whom were appointed on contracts. We could identify and include four participants from this cohort, which presents a fair representation of these doctors.

In contrast, State 2 is quite large, with over 30 districts. We employed purposive sampling to interview 18 doctors (four AYUSH and 14 allopathic doctors working in regular service), who represented the six districts. The six districts were selected purposively to ensure representation of doctors from areas both near and far from the state headquarters. Despite our efforts, we could not identify contractual doctors who had served for at least two years from the six selected districts in State 2.

To understand the existing strategies and systems concerning recruitment, we also interviewed 28 policy actors—16 from State 1 and 12 from State 2. We employed purposive sampling in both states to identify policy actors based on their knowledge of the research topic’s issues and the positions they held at various institutions. This ensured a broad representation of key policy actors.

All but three interviews were audio-recorded with the written consent of the participants, while detailed notes were taken for the three un-recorded interviews. Most interviews (38) were conducted in English, while the remaining 23, from State 2, were conducted in a mix of English and Hindi (the official Indian language). These interviews were transcribed verbatim and then translated into English. Subsequently, all the transcripts and interview notes were imported into the data analysis software, NVivo 12. Once imported, all transcripts were coded, and a detailed analysis was carried out using NVivo 12 [20]. The lead author (BP) conducted all the interviews with participants at their workplaces, except for six interviews with doctors, which were held at a training centre, and one interview with a policy actor, which was conducted at his residence.

Data analysis

For the larger research study, we used Framework Analysis (FA), which is a matrix-based method, to arrange and synthesize qualitative data based on the themes generated from the data [21]. FA was suited to the current research because our research used a combination of inductive and deductive analysis, which is a main feature of FA [22]. For the larger research, we identified several themes (a priori and emerging themes) that capture the interactions and experiences of doctors with HRM sub-systems. Our analysis revealed several themes that capture the recruitment and employment-related experiences of AYUSH contractual doctors. The data were organized and synthesized for the analysis, and a coding framework was developed, which was then applied to all transcripts using NVivo 12. Additionally, we constructed job histories from the doctors’ interviews to examine their experiences with the recruitment system. The quantitative data, captured through job histories, were analysed using frequencies and triangulated with the narrative accounts given by the doctors.

Conceptual framework

For this research, we drew upon and adapted a framework on HRM performance and its underlying causal chain [23]. This framework is based on the premise that a better understanding of how the HRM systems work is critical for the effective functioning of HRM. This involves understanding the difficulties and complexities of the organizational ‘black box’ [24]. The framework is explained by representing the HRM process as a linked chain—where the intended HR practices lead to the actual HR practices. These are filtered by perceived HR practices, and then result in employee reactions, and collectively, in organizational performance [23]. This framework emphasizes several vital aspects. First, it underscores the difference between rhetoric and intentions (of what organizations will do) and reality (of what managers actually do). Factors such as resource constraints, managers’ own judgments, and interests may potentially reshape the intended HR practices and affect the implementation. The framework also emphasizes that employee beliefs, attitudes, and behaviour are at the core of the HRM—to bring about desired outcomes, and therefore, these need to be positively influenced. Employee perceptions of HR practices influence employee behaviour.

Ethics

The study received ethical approval from the Human Research Ethics Office at the University of Queensland, Faculty of Medicine, Australia (approval number: 2,017,001,666/TRC-IEC No: 9/2016) and the ethics review committee at the Indian Institute of Public Health, Gandhinagar, India (approval number: TRC-IEC No. 9/16). Informed written consent was sought from all the participants before data collection, and the personal data and transcripts were anonymized before analysis.

The analysis of the qualitative data and the job histories reveals several themes below that capture the experiences of AYUSH and allopathic doctors. These themes suggest that both AYUSH and allopathic doctors had negative experiences with recruitment in the two states, but these were much worse for AYUSH, particularly in State 1.

Poor emoluments and allowances for contractual doctors

Both contractual and regular AYUSH doctors from State 1 complained about their meagre salaries and how these were significantly less than those of allopathic doctors. Two AYUSH doctors reported that, despite provisions for yearly increments, they had not received any annual increments in the last few years. This was perceived as discriminatory by the AYUSH doctors as they believed that they performed similar roles and responsibilities and worked for an equal number of hours as their allopathic counterparts.

Although we do similar work and work equally [referring to contractual allopathic doctors], we only get half the salary of what they get. So, when it comes to salaries, there is a big discrimination. (State 1, AYUSH 01)

I started with a salary of INR 27,500 per month [USD 340] with a 10% increment promised every year. But, I did not get any increments for two years. I think NHM has stopped increments for AYUSH doctors completely. (State 1, AYUSH 12)

Regular AYUSH doctors from State 2 also reported issues of lower salaries and the absence of a rural allowance compared to those received by regular allopathic doctors. All the regular AYUSH doctors from State 2 also lamented not being allowed by the state to engage in private practice, while also not receiving the Non-Practice Allowance (NPA)—an allowance paid to doctors who opt not to engage in private practice. AYUSH doctors considered this unfair, as the regular allopathic doctors from their state had the option to either choose private practice or receive NPA.

We are not allowed to do private practice, and we also do not get NPA. You see this is not right because the regular medical officers [allopathic doctors], either have a choice to do private practice or get NPA in lieu of no private practice. The whole idea of NPA is that doctors can fully focus on their service, so the scheme is good, but it would be nice if we also get this allowance. (State 2, AYUSH doctor 13)

Increased workload and rural postings for AYUSH doctors

Contractual AYUSH doctors from State 1 perceived the system to be unfair towards them in several ways. These doctors reported that the state expected them to fill the vacancies in rural postings and to work nearly around the clock, compared to their allopathic counterparts, who could choose to work in urban areas and larger hospitals with less extensive work shifts.

“We, as AYUSH doctors, don’t get what we deserve. We are usually posted at periphery areas [rural and remote locations] for 24*7 as there can be an emergency at the health centre. But if you look at other doctors [referring to allopathic regular doctors], it’s like, they opt to work for big hospitals located in cities. Not only do they get a much higher salary, but their work shifts are so relaxed, only 6 to 8 hours or so per day. After all this, they also get two days off from work. I think this is not fair” (State 1, AYUSH 01)

Comparison of durations served on contracts: significantly longer for contractual doctors

The average duration (in months) served on contracts by AYUSH and allopathic doctors from the two states varied significantly—it was longest for contractual allopathic doctors in State 1, followed by contractual AYUSH in the same state, and shortest for allopathic doctors in State 2 (see Table 3). The failure of the PSC to conduct periodic recruitment emerged as the main reason for the long durations served on contracts, as reported by the allopathic contractual doctors in State 1 and a few regular allopathic doctors in both states.

“We haven’t had PSC regular recruitment in the state for many years now so I after nine years of service, I am still working on contract” (State 1, contractual allopathic doctor, 03).

Comparison of opportunities for regular recruitment: no opportunities for AYUSH in State 1

An extreme form of inequity reported by the contractual AYUSH doctors from State 1 was the lack of opportunities for regular recruitment—this also explains the long periods these doctors served on contracts (Table 3). In contrast, contractual doctors, both allopathic and AYUSH, in State 2 had opportunities for regular recruitment. At the time of the interviews, all four AYUSH doctors from State 2 were already recruited under regular employment, after serving a contractual period between 12 and 24 months. These doctors were recruited through a competitive evaluation and selection process carried out by an agency responsible for conducting civil service recruitment for AYUSH doctors. Similarly, the six allopathic contractual doctors from State 1 had opportunities for regular recruitment; however, as discussed in the previous theme, the duration they served on contracts was high due to bureaucratic delays (delayed recruitment by the PSC due to overburdened responsibilities of recruitment) in the conduct of the regular recruitment process.

“We have no avenues for regularization. Not even a single AYUSH doctor in our state has been regularized” (State 1, contractual AYUSH doctor 14)

“As of now, we do not recruit the NHM AYUSH doctors in regular service.” (State 1 KI 10, District office: Health department)

“I was recruited under regular service after two years of serving on contract” (State 2, regular AYUSH doctor 15)

Perceptions of inequity about AYUSH in State 1

In State 1, contractual AYUSH doctors alluded to their experiences by drawing comparisons with their counterparts, which included (a) regular allopathic doctors from their state as well as other states; (b) AYUSH doctors from other Indian states; and (c) contractual allopathic doctors from their state. These comparisons highlight the negative perceptions among AYUSH contractual doctors from State 1, who believe that they are highly disadvantaged.

“There is always discrimination as I said between regular allopathic doctors and contractual doctors” (State 1, contractual AYUSH doctor 02)

AYSUH doctors from State 1 also indicated that all Indian states, barring their state, provide opportunities for regular recruitment of AYUSH contractual doctors, which is critical for job security.

Some of my friends [referring to AYUSH doctors], were okay with moving out to different states and working there because they knew that they had opportunities to get into regular jobs and most got regular jobs there. Now they are regularized, but I am not. They have that job security” (State 1, contractual AYUSH doctor 12)

“As of now, in fact, this is the only state in the entire country, where AYUSH doctors have not been regularized, not even a single AYUSH doctor. Other states conduct public service commission recruitment for AYUSH doctors” (State 1, contractual AYUSH doctor 14).

AYUSH doctors from State 1 perceived the contractual allopathic doctors from all Indian states, including their state, as highly privileged because they have predictable career trajectories and opportunities for regular recruitment.

“See allopathic doctors on contracts know that they will get promoted and at some point in their career, and will get into regular service. At least their career is streamlined, which is not the case for us” (State 1, contractual AYUSH doctor 02)

Implications of lack of opportunities for regular recruitment for contractual doctors

The study indicates that all contractual doctors wish to be recruited for regular employment, which is highly valued by the study participants. Regular recruitment is perceived as associated with better organizational commitment and greater accountability, and it is highly regarded by the doctors. Conversely, issues related to contractual appointments, such as low job security and limited HR benefits, are thought to contribute to high attrition and low organizational commitment.

“The regular doctors are answerable and accountable. If I am in regular service, I will be highly motivated and committed towards my work ” (State 1, KI 03: State office: NHM)

“In my opinion, NHM doctors face lot of uncertainties- they don’t know whether their jobs will be regularized or not. At the end of the day what they want is a regular job, a secure job. Due to these uncertainties, many of them may quit and find jobs elsewhere” (State 01, contractual allopathic doctor 03)

All four AYUSH doctors from State 1 reported very low job security and stagnated career progression compared to their allopathic counterparts. From their viewpoint, the lack of opportunities to get into regular service was highly inequitable, and it affected their career progression, promotion, and salary benefits negatively.

Frankly, I feel like my career is stuck for years. If there was an opportunity for regular recruitment, then we would have a defined salary grade and we would get periodic promotions like we become senior medical officers, and so on. I have been working here for 5 years, and I know some contractual AYUSH staff who have been working here for eleven years. But there is no difference between someone working for one year or eleven years as we are all working at the same level. (State 1, contractual AYUSH doctor, 14)

Like allopathic doctors on contract, AYUSH doctors viewed the lack of regular recruitment opportunities as a significant factor that negatively impacts their motivation and a primary cause of the high turnover rate among them. Additionally, several key policy actors recognized that the absence of recruitment opportunities for regular service for AYUSH doctors has an adverse effect on retention.

“Some of my AYUSH friends joined and then left the NHM job because of the problem of regularization”. (State 1, contractual AYUSH doctor 01)

At the end of the day what they [referring to NHM doctors] want is a regular job, a secure job. Because of uncertainties involved in regular recruitment, many of them want to quit and many leave the NHM job and go elsewhere. This affects retention and it’s something we cannot guarantee.” (State 1 KI 08, State office, Health Department)

Breach of AYUSH doctors’ expectations

AYUSH doctors believed that regularizing the small number of AYUSH doctors in the state was not difficult if the administration intended to. However, the state’s reasoning about its inability to regularize these doctors was very different—the small number of AYUSH doctors prevented the state from creating a separate cadre for the AYUSH doctors.

“I have worked for 5 years already. I think right now there are only 12 or 13 AYUSH doctors in the entire state that need to be regularized. So if the government wants, they can easily conduct SPSC for us but perhaps they don’t want to do so” (State 1, contractual AYUSH doctor 01)

“We are unable to regularize any AYSUH doctor because there are very few of them, so we are not able to form a separate cadre” (State 1, KI 03, State office, NHM)

Therefore, AYUSH doctors viewed the state's inaction regarding their regularization as a strategic manoeuver. All AYUSH doctors from State 1 stated that they have not left their positions despite many years of working under contracts, hoping for future regularization. However, the state has not fulfilled these periodic assurances and continues to fail in regularizing them.

“We’ve had a few meetings with the concerned authorities but every time we hear the same thing that we will regularize you. They have been verbally giving positive responses saying that eventually you will get regularized, and that we are working on it. But we need certainty and security else we need to move out. We are reaching our saturation point” (State 1, contractual AYUSH doctor 01)

Despite many assurances from the officials about our regularization, nothing has happened till now. I would have left long back but decided to continue as officials kept telling us that our regularization will be done” (State 1, contractual AYUSH doctor 02)

Discussion

We examined the experiences of public sector doctors with recruitment in two Indian states. The paper highlights the discontent of AYUSH and allopathic doctors with the recruitment, but how this is consistently worse for AYUSH, especially in State 1, in several ways. One, there are significant discrepancies in salaries and allowances between AYUSH and allopathic doctors. Two, AYUSH doctors experience stagnated career progression and high job insecurity. Three, the system can sabotage AYUSH doctors’ expectations of progressing to regular recruitment. And finally, AYUSH doctors perceive the system to be highly inequitable and unresponsive towards their concerns, which has implications for health services. The findings are important from the perspectives of policy, implementation, and governance because, although the Indian government established the framework for AYUSH integration, our results show that the integration operates quite differently in practice in the two study states and that HRM practices, as perceived and experienced by the contractual AYUSH doctors, reveal significant inequity against AYUSH contractual doctors.

Our findings suggest that both AYUSH and allopathic doctors complain about failures in policy implementation in both states. For allopathic doctors, it is a result of bureaucratic inefficiencies due to failing to conduct regular PSC examinations. However, for AYUSH doctors, the failures in policy implementation are reported as worse, with significant inequities. The first form of inequity perceived by regular and contractual AYUSH doctors was the meagre salaries and the discrepancies in allowances, corroborated by other Indian studies [12, 14]. Another form of inequity reported in this paper was the lack of salary revisions and annual increments for contractual AYUSH doctors in State 1, unlike regular AYUSH doctors and allopathic doctors. Some Indian studies also support these findings with considerable disparities in salaries, lack of allowances for family, health, and housing, which further disadvantage AYUSH contractual doctors [12]. One of the key findings is the perceptions of unfairness among AYUSH doctors regarding the health administration’s reliance on and expectations of AYUSH doctors in meeting the health workforce requirements for rural postings and working nearly around the clock. Other than issues of working overtime, Indian studies have reported the poor facilities and infrastructure at rural health centres that AYUSH work within [12].

The most significant form of inequity experienced by AYUSH doctors is the lack of opportunities for regular recruitment in State 1. This is a key finding as it suggests that not only are contractual AYUSH doctors highly disadvantaged compared to allopathic doctors, but they are also worse off than AYUSH doctors from State 2 and other Indian states that offer regular recruitment for these doctors. From a policy and health workforce perspective, this is an important finding because although AYUSH has been integrated into health services through various initiatives such as the NHM, the reported disparities experienced by AYUSH doctors suggest a rather underwhelming implementation of the national policy to mainstream and integrate AYUSH into the system [12]. The lack of opportunities for regular recruitment in State 1 suggests the dynamics of the labour market, allowing the state to be in a much stronger position to negotiate and determine recruitment conditions, almost unilaterally in State 1. Studies in the area of health workforce from India have reported how the administration unilaterally determines doctors’ initial postings [16].

Many HRM issues identified in this research, such as poor salaries and incentives, lack of career progression, demotivation, and job insecurity, are linked to contractual recruitment, which was a central preoccupation for doctors. Unfortunately, the contractualization of labour in India is part of an increasing trend that has been ongoing since the inception of the NHM—India has seen an increase in the proportion of contract workers from nearly one in four in 2004/2005 to one in three in 2011/12. Researchers argue that contractualization has permitted the use of labour without according them workers’ rights [25]. Evidence from our research highlights this in the treatment of AYUSH doctors under the NHM, who are paid less than their counterparts, and other studies also report lower pay compared to other health workers like nurses [26].

Our paper also highlights that periodic assurances from the administration and the health system reforms in other Indian states have raised expectations among AYUSH practitioners regarding their regularization. However, these assurances are merely rhetorical, with the reality being that State 1 has failed to regularize the services of these doctors. The system's failure to recruit AYUSH doctors can sabotage their hopes and expectations of progressing to civil service recruitment; thus, the doctors perceive these periodic assurances as manipulative—a tactic that allows the state to retain the existing doctors to meet its strategic needs.

The contractual appointment of AYUSH may be a strategic approach for the state to meet its temporary service demands, but from the doctors’ perspective, it is a tactical approach. This compounds many problems for doctors, including job insecurity and a lack of career progression, which are causes of demotivation among health workers in Sierra Leone [27], and public sector doctors and nurses in India [28–31]. Our findings suggest that State 1 continues to exploit the policy environment to determine recruitment legislation, policies, and structures, thereby enabling the state to maintain the contractual recruitment of AYUSH doctors without providing avenues for regular recruitment. Despite the high discontent and breach of expectations among AYUSH, the actions of these doctors are limited to requests, and the study participants did not report other forms of resistance. This is a striking finding considering the health system context in India, where recruitment-related issues have been challenged in courts [32] and other Indian studies have reported the use of strong resistance and power by the doctors to influence decisions related to their postings [17]. The researchers did not investigate the reasons for the lack of resistance from AYUSH doctors, but there are two likely explanations. One, the AYUSH doctors passively accept what the administration has to offer with the hope that situations would improve in the future—the periodic assurances from the administration partly support this. And two, the embedded medical hierarchies that have led to the marginalization of TCAM by the allopathic profession [33]. Thus, these doctors have little power to influence their recruitment, presenting a challenge to the effective integration of TCAM [34].

This paper also highlights the tensions between doctors’ expectations regarding the regularization of the very small number of AYUSH doctors in State 1 and the state's reasoning for its inability to do so. The core of these tensions was the absence of the AYUSH cadre and department in the state, leading to expectation gaps. The absence of legislation/policies, a separate cadre, sanctioned posts, and the department of AYUSH in State 1 is suggestive that the national policy articulations on the integration of AYUSH are not effectively translated into the state context [9]. While our study was not designed to explore the reasons for the absence of the AYUSH department in the state, literature suggests that a single department may govern all the prevalent systems of medicine, creating problems of hierarchy, inequitable representation, and marginalization of specific systems of medicine [9]. Thus, our research highlights the need for an institutional environment that effectively integrates different systems of medicine, enabling inter-system coherence in addressing the tensions and expectation gaps between AYUSH doctors and the state.

The sense of severe inequity experienced by AYUSH can negatively affect their motivation and trust, with critical implications such as poor retention and potential conflicts between doctors and the system. Thus, the issues highlighted in the paper have implications for health services that must be addressed before tensions between doctors and the administration escalate to levels where doctors resort to strong resistance and protests. Protests are reported when governments fail to address issues between health workers and the administration until the health workers resort to demonstrations [23]. A recent newspaper article from India reported that 800 AYUSH doctors protested in the state of Karnataka against discrimination in salary and allowance paid to them compared to the doctors working in the allopathy department [35]. Similarly, in LMICs like Nigeria, other health workers have alleged that the Nigerian health system is designed to favour the doctors [15]. The dominance of doctors over the years has encouraged other health workers to form a new group called the Joint Health Sector Unions in Nigeria [15].

The inequalities related to HRM, especially concerning the recruitment and employment of AYUSH doctors, highlight the complexities and realities faced by gig workers. This creates an institutional environment where the state can unilaterally set recruitment conditions, which may have serious implications for attracting and retaining health workers. Additionally, existing insurance schemes like Ayushman Bharat exclude AYUSH treatments, potentially hindering the development of AYUSH facilities. All these factors worsen the issues related to the exclusion and marginalization of AYUSH doctors and systems.

Our paper highlights how the contractual AYUSH doctors perceive themselves as highly disadvantaged. Despite these findings, the case study design and findings from State 2 offer some positive insights into how the regular recruitment of AYUSH practitioners can effectively address the primary concerns of doctors. State 2 manages to adopt a more inclusive approach towards AYUSH doctors, with initiatives to address state-level variations in the regularization of positions, and is one of the first few states in India to have initiated permanent recruitment of AYUSH doctors. Thus, the practice from State 2 suggests less systemic bias and non-inclusiveness of AYUSH, effectively bridging some of the expectations gaps identified in the study.

In summary, the study highlights a range of known factors, including some critical ones, that facilitate the effective integration, recruitment, and retention of AYUSH doctors within the health system. Since recruitment into the regular service is key to retaining doctors and is closely linked to other HRM issues discussed in the paper, we provide several key policy and implementation recommendations drawn from this research. However, given the contextual nature of health and HR issues in India, these recommendations remain broad and tentative. The conflicting tensions, expectation gaps, and lack of trust experienced by doctors in their interactions with the HRM system, along with the ad hoc solutions, worsen the HRM issues. Negative experiences and perceptions of the system perpetuate inequity, uncertainty, and the exploitation of doctors by the system. Therefore, from an employee perspective, predictable and timely opportunities for regular recruitment are essential. This can lead to more positive perceptions among AYUSH doctors regarding their fair and equitable treatment, particularly concerning issues of pay parity. This can also be crucial in addressing career progression and professional equity issues. Addressing perceptions is at the core of HRM, as the perceptions employees have about HR practices can significantly influence their behaviour and ultimately impact HR outcomes [36]. However, ensuring professional equity, fair treatment, and opportunities for AYUSH doctors may not be easy to achieve. It requires significant policy and legislative changes—specifically, the creation of the AYUSH department and a separate cadre of AYUSH doctors to ensure there are enough sanctioned positions for recruitment. Lessons can also be learned from State 2, which has been quite successful in providing professional equity and predictable career pathways for AYUSH doctors. Implementing these changes necessitates a shift from the health system's current ad hoc, short-term, and tactical approach. This requires various institutions, including the PSC, Health, Finance, AYUSH, and administrative departments, to unite and adopt a more holistic perspective on HRM. Another recommendation is to delegate recruitment roles and powers within the established structures and institutions, away from the PSC, to other bodies, as done in State 2. Such changes can occur without necessitating significant reforms or legislative amendments. This can address expectation gaps and likely contribute to improved trust and retention.

This study has several limitations. Challenges encountered in the field, including limited access to key policy actors, policy documents, and HR data from the states, as well as time and resource constraints, prevented us from conducting a comprehensive institutional and system analysis. The constraints in the sample, with a limited number of doctors interviewed from the two states, limit the generalizability of the study findings. Considering the diverse nature of states in India, and the significant differences in the two states and the employment conditions of the doctors compared in the study, we acknowledge that generalizability becomes a challenge. Nonetheless, the study findings resonate with and reflect similar issues in other Indian states, supported by the literature. Furthermore, our sample did not reveal contractual doctors from State 2. This, however, does not exclude the possibility of their presence in the service; it is an artefact of the sample. Finally, the doctors may have a recall bias in specifying the exact dates for job-related events related to recruitment.

In conclusion, this paper locates the positioning of AYUSH doctors within the HRM space. It provides insights into perceptible inequities and breaches of AYUSH doctors’ expectations and how the case of contractual AYUSH doctors represents an extreme form of inequity. The policy and institutional environment around AYUSH integration is weak, and there is a critical failure of institutions in responding to the expectations of the doctors. There are also inadequate translations between policy and practice, which leave unresolved larger questions around holistic integration and inclusion of AYUSH that ideally should place them equally with their allopathic counterparts. Most crucially, the policy and institutional environment has a strong bias towards allopathic doctors, leading to further exclusion of AYUSH [37].
