BB-EIT: A Generalized Prediction Model for Protein Adsorption on Polymer Brushes Using Augmented Chemical Embeddings
Authors: Shiwei Su, Nobuyuki Tanaka, Yoshitaka Ushiku, Koichi Takahashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Precise control of protein adsorption on polymer surfaces is essential in materials science and biomaterial design, with applications in antifouling materials, biosensors, cell culture, and drug delivery systems. However, the complex interactions between polymers and proteins and the limited availability of high-quality interaction data remain major challenges in polymer informatics. Current approaches often lack the generalizability needed to model diverse polymer–protein systems within a single unified framework, and there is a paucity of comprehensive predictive models capable of handling diverse polymer–protein interactions. To address these challenges, we introduce BB-EIT (Biointerface BERT Encoder for Interaction Translation), a novel generalized model designed to accurately predict the amount of diverse protein adsorption on polymer brushes. BB-EIT leverages the pretrained ChemBERTa large language model (LLM) architecture using SMILES strings for robust chemical representation and convenient data augmentation through SMILES enumeration. By adapting the pretrained model with an extended layer integrating a comprehensive set of physicochemical and biochemical features, including polymer thickness, water contact angle, and surface charge as well as protein isoelectric point (pI) and size, the BB-EIT showed state-of-the-art performance and strong generalizability. The model accurately predicted the adsorption behavior in previously unseen polymer and protein systems. This work represents an important step toward the data-driven design of biomaterials with tailored protein adsorption properties.

Introduction

The field of biomaterials science is moving rapidly from traditional trial-and-error methodologies to data-driven approaches leveraging machine learning and deep learning to accelerate the analysis and discovery of new materials. , Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly being applied across scientific disciplines. − Their success has led to the development of several domain-specific models, such as MatSciBERT, which focuses on text mining and information extraction from the materials science literature; PolyBERT, which was designed for polymer property prediction; and ChemBERTa, which supports various cheminformatics tasks. , These Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT)-based models utilize the bidirectional context of chemical representations such as SMILES notation to simulate atomic interactions and generate comprehensive, accurate representations of chemical structures. −

Despite recent computational advances, there are critical challenges in polymer informatics in transitioning from the prediction of intrinsic chemical and physical properties to the modeling of complex material functionalities. − A major bottleneck in this transition is the investigation of intricate physicochemical interactions, particularly protein adsorption on polymer surfaces. This phenomenon is critical in biomaterials science because numerous polymer-based applications, including biosensors, bioreactors, antifouling films, cell culture, and drug delivery systems, are contingent on the precise control of the amount of protein adsorption at the polymer–biointerface. − Modeling this behavior is difficult due to the complexity of polymer–protein interactions, which involve two distinct molecular systems. These interactions are affected by multiple factors, including the chemical composition and physical structure of the polymer as well as their physicochemical properties such as hydrophilicity and surface potential. In addition, the characteristics of proteins themselves, including their type, size, and charge, along with external environmental conditions such as temperature and pH, affect these interactions. − This multivariate complexity has contributed to the lack of high-quality, reliable data sets that would be necessary for training robust machine learning models. , Furthermore, the experimental generation of such data is both resource-intensive and time-consuming, and inconsistencies in experimental conditions across studies further limit the availability of a comprehensive public database. This sparsity of data has hindered the development of general-purpose LLM applications in this domain.

Consequently, existing predictive models rely primarily on conventional machine learning algorithms, which often produce models that are tailored too narrowly to specific protein classes or polymer chemistries. For example, Liu et al. used a support vector regression (SVR) model to design polymer materials that can prevent the adsorption of proteins, Okuyama et al. utilized linear regression and decision tree-based algorithms to understand the key properties affecting protein adsorption on zwitterionic polymer brushes, and we previously reported protein adsorption prediction using random forest models. While these studies advanced understanding of polymer–protein interactions, the conventional models employed fundamentally lacked the generalizability required to handle diverse polymer–protein systems, compounded by the scarcity of sufficient high-quality data points needed to accurately predict adsorption amounts within a single unified framework.

To address these challenges in polymer informatics, we introduce the BB-EIT (Biointerface BERT Encoder for Interaction Translation) model. BB-EIT is a novel generalized predictive model with state-of-the-art performance for protein adsorption amount on polymer brushes. BB-EIT employs a two-step approach to overcome the limitations of conventional methods, specifically their lack of generalizability and reliance on limited data points, as aforementioned. First, it builds on the pretrained LLM, ChemBERTa architecture, which already possesses deep knowledge about chemical structures from extensive pretraining. It is well suited for this task because polymer–protein interactions are strongly influenced by chemical features that ChemBERTa represents effectively. Second, to mitigate the structural complexity of polymer–protein systems and ensure that the predictive power of BB-EIT is grounded in reliable and high-quality data, this study focuses on polymer brush architectures. These materials are designed to form highly controlled interfaces, with high graft density that promotes uniform and extended polymer conformations. This structure inherently minimizes confounding factors, such as surface roughness and geometric heterogeneity. Utilizing data derived from these well-defined polymer materials can ensure the data quality necessary for BB-EIT to accurately translate complex phenomena into quantifiable measures of interaction. From the perspective of machine learning, utilizing the flexibility of the LLM-based model supports generalizability and enables the investigation of multiple classes of interactions within a single framework. Moreover, unlike conventional models that rely on precomputed chemical descriptors, BB-EIT uses SMILES strings for robust chemical representation. − Utilizing transfer learning and data augmentation via SMILES enumeration helps to overcome data scarcity and increase prediction accuracy. , A critical innovation is the integration of an external layer containing holistic physicochemical and biochemical features (Figure ) to capture the full complexity of the polymer–protein interface.

Architecture of BB-EIT (Biointerface BERT Encoder for Interaction Translation) for protein adsorption prediction. The model foundation is the pretrained ChemBERTa encoder, which generates high-dimensional vector embeddings from the polymer SMILES string. These embeddings are concatenated with a 5-dimensional vector that contains domain-specific features (pI, molecular weight, ζ-potential, water contact angle, and thickness) to form the integrated input for the prediction head (FC1 and FC2).

This comprehensive methodology establishes BB-EIT as a state-of-the-art model that can extrapolate to previously unseen polymer and protein systems, representing a major advance in data-driven biomaterials design.

Methodology

Preparation of the Data Set

The data set used for model training and testing was collected from a previous publication. It contained the raw adsorption measurements (in ng/cm2) of bovine serum albumin (BSA), lysozyme (Lys), and fibrinogen (Fib) on various polymer brush surfaces. For the polymer part, this data set comprised diverse monomer architectures, including nonionic (e.g., PHEMA, PMMA), anionic (e.g., PMS), cationic (e.g., PMTAC), and zwitterionic (e.g., PMPC) polymer brushes. For the protein part, it included 53 observations for BSA, 40 for Lys, and 8 for Fib, for a total of 101 distinct measurements. Adsorption amounts were determined using quartz crystal microbalance with dissipation monitoring, and the data set spanned a wide range of adsorption values, from approximately 0 to 103 ng/cm2, and captures interaction behaviors from protein repulsion to immobilization. Only the measurement data for which the experimental environment was stated as pH 7.4 in the reference articles were collected in our data set.

The graft density of all samples surpassed 0.1 chains/nm2, assured that the graft density was saturated to make polymer chains densely packed to form a fully extended brush structure. Each entry in this data set included chemical and physical structural information as well as relevant physicochemical properties for the polymer brushes, including the SMILES representation of the monomer, film thickness (in nm), water contact angle (in degrees), and ζ-potential (in mV). It should be noted that the contact angle and ζ-potential values were not measured experimentally for these entries. Instead, they were predicted using previously validated preconstructed machine learning models to avoid missing values and to ensure data consistency across the full data set. , A wide range of wettability from about 5° to 120° water contact angle and surface charge from around −70 to +70 mV ζ-potential was covered.

In addition, protein-specific properties including isoelectric point (pI) and molecular weight (in kDa) were included as features to characterize each protein. The pI represents the pH at which the net electrical charge of the protein is zero and serves as a measure of protein surface charge. Molecular weight, used as a proxy for protein size, is also a key factor influencing the steric interactions with polymer brushes. Data Augmentation

To enhance the robustness and generalizability of BB-EIT model and to mitigate overfitting issues associated with a limited data set, we used a data augmentation strategy based on SMILES enumeration. This approach is conceptually analogous to data augmentation techniques used in pattern recognition, such as rotating or flipping images to increase data set size without altering the underlying object. In this study, the molecular structures of monomers were encoded using SMILES notation, which is a widely adopted graph-based linear notation in cheminformatics. While a molecule has a unique canonical SMILES representation, multiple valid noncanonical arrangements can be generated by varying the atom traversal order during molecular graph encoding, as shown in Figure . By enumerating the SMILES strings, we introduced syntactic variations of the same monomer, increasing the diversity in the training data, while preserving the molecular structure. These alternative SMILES strings represent the same chemical structure but provide syntactic diversity that can improve the training of machine learning models, especially those that process sequential data, such as transformer architecture-based LLMs.

Illustration of data augmentation by SMILES enumeration using 2-methoxyethyl acrylate (MEA) as an example. By switching the atom order to generate various noncanonical SMILES strings, the method effectively increases the data set size for a single molecule without altering its target label.

For each molecule in the data set, enumerated SMILES strings were generated from the canonical representation using the MolToSmiles function with randomization enabled in the open-source Python toolkit RDKit (version 2023.03.2). To ensure the chemical validity of the augmented data, any molecules that failed to parse during this enumerating process were discarded. This strategy effectively expanded the training data set, providing a model with a richer variety of input representations for each unique chemical entity, thereby improving its ability to learn underlying patterns and generalize to previously unseen data.

To reflect the experimental errors inherent in protein adsorption data, a heteroscedastic Gaussian noise was introduced to the output labels representing the protein adsorption amounts. The rationale was that the standard deviation of experimental error scales with the magnitude of the measured adsorption due to the combination of measurement sensitivity, protein aggregation, and surface heterogeneity. For each augmented SMILES entry, Gaussian-distributed noise was added to the original adsorption amount value. The standard deviation (σ) of the noise was computed as σ=max(0,0.05×y) where y is the original protein adsorption value. This formula ensures a minimum uncertainty floor while scaling appropriately with the adsorption amount. Noise values that resulted in negative adsorption values were clipped to zero to preserve physical plausibility. The choice of a 0.05 noise scale was informed by two factors. Statistically, it approximates the experimental uncertainty typical of QCM-D measurements, where a 95% confidence interval for the adsorption amount generally falls within the ±10%. Methodologically, introducing this label noise serves as a form of regularization that prevents the model from overfitting to the specific values of the original sparse data set. By simulating the heteroscedastic nature of experimental error, the model is encouraged to prioritize the underlying structure–property relationships over exact label matching.

Besides that, the features representing polymer brush features (film thickness, water contact angle, and ζ-potential) were kept unchanged because SMILES enumeration does not alter these physically measured properties.

In total, the augmentation process increased the data set by approximately 64 times (6400 data points included the original data set), resulting in a substantially larger training data set suitable for the ensemble-based LLM. These combined strategies of SMILES enumeration and noise injection were aimed to simulate the variability of structural representation and experimental uncertainty inherent in protein adsorption measurements on polymer brush surfaces. The augmented data set is shown in the Supporting Information. The first 101 data points were the original data, and the rest were the augmented entries.

Feature Selection

The feature selection process was designed to comprehensively represent the complex polymer–protein system by integrating both chemical and physical characteristics. For the polymer components, the chemical structure of each monomer was encoded using SMILES notation, which was used as a direct input to the ChemBERTa encoder. In addition to this chemical representation, five key physical and physicochemical properties of the polymer brushes were included to provide essential context. The thickness of the polymer brushes, which can directly influence protein accessibility and binding, was included as a feature of their physical architecture. The hydrophilicity or hydrophobicity of the polymer surface was quantified using the static water contact angle, while the ζ-potential reflected the electrostatic charge at the polymer–solution interface. Although other physical structural factors such as surface roughness and graft density can affect protein adsorption amount as well, they were neglected in the model. This was due to the uniform characteristics of polymer brushes that the roughness of their surface was relatively small. Determining the graft density required multiple experiments, making it difficult to integrate with a generalized model as a feature. Furthermore, since the data set focused exclusively on high density regime (>0.1 chains/cm2), thickness can act as a reliable and easily measurable proxy for graft density.

The pI and molecular weight were selected as the characteristics of the proteins. The selection of the pI as a biochemical descriptor was based on its role as a fundamental indicator of the pH-dependent charge character of a protein. While the exact net charge at a given pH provides a more granular representation of electrostatic driving forces, the model was designed to leverage standardized features available across diverse literature sources. For the proteins used in this study, the pI acted as a consistent proxy for the net charge when the experimental environmental if fixed at pH 7.4. Under these fixed conditions, a protein with a pI < 7.4 such as BSA or Fib consistently carries a negative net charge, where a protein with a pI > 7.4 such as Lys carries a positive net charge. The molecular weights for proteins ranged from 14 to 340 kDa which were used as a proxy for protein size. By integrating these high-variance features, the model can capture a broad spectrum of the biochemical spaces, thereby enhancing the generalizability and predictive accuracy of the framework for both known and unseen polymer–protein systems.

Together, SMILES notations and these five additional features (thickness, contact angle, ζ-potential of polymer brushes, pI, and molecular weight of proteins) were systematically evaluated to construct predictive models of protein adsorption behavior.

To evaluate the relative importance of these features, a systematic ablation study was conducted, where additional models were trained using various subsets of these features to enable comparison of the predictive performance when the model was provided only partial information. This approach helped confirm the critical contribution of each feature to the overall predictive accuracy.

Furthermore, to evaluate the impact of charge representation on the performance of the model and to address potential concerns regarding charge regulation, an additional comparative model test was conducted using computed net charge at pH 7.4 as an alternative feature to pI. The computed net charge for the proteins used in this study at fixed experimental pH 7.4 is shown in Table S1. By testing the architectural sensitivity to these different charge features, it is sought to validate whether the global property-based feature pI or the specific calculated value net charge served as a more robust input as the generalizability.

Architecture of BB-EIT

The proposed BB-EIT architecture represents a novel integration of a foundation model with domain-specific features to enhance the predictive capability for protein adsorption, as shown in Figure . The model uses a hybrid architecture built on the frozen ChemBERTa encoder. It maintains its encoder structure, which includes the input embedding layer and a 6-layer encoder equipped with 12 attention heads, leveraging knowledge retained from its original pretraining configuration. The chemical structure of the monomer unit, represented by its SMILES string notation, is first fed into the ChemBERTa encoder, which processes the sequential data to generate a high-dimensional (1 × 768) vector representation.

A key methodological innovation lies in introducing a mechanism to incorporate essential domain-specific information. Specifically, instead of directly connecting the output of the ChemBERTa encoder to the final prediction layers, this high-dimensional embedding is concatenated with an external vector composed of five critical physical, chemical, and physicochemical features: film thickness, contact angle, ζ-potential of the polymer brushes, and the pI and molecular weight of proteins. This explicit concatenation creates a combined holistic feature vector that simultaneously captures the deep chemical context learned from the SMILES string and the important physical and biological contexts not intrinsically encoded within the chemical notation of the monomer.

Subsequently, this combined vector (1 × 773 dimensions) is passed through a fully connect layer (FC1), which serves to process the integrated information, generating a comprehensive representation of the polymer–protein interaction system. The output of this layer is then subjected to a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function to introduce nonlinearity. This is followed by a second fully connected layer (FC2), which acts as the prediction head, yielding a single regression value corresponding to the predicted protein adsorption amount.

This architecture can ensure that the deep chemical knowledge from the pretrained ChemBERTa foundation model is effectively merged with crucial experimentally measured physicochemical parameters to produce accurate predictions for complex interfacial phenomena.

Model Training

As shown in Figure , the augmented data set, containing a total of 6400 data points, was randomly divided into a training set (80%, 5120 data points) and a test set (20%, 1280 data points).

Scheme of the BB-EIT training and the evaluation process. The original data set from the literature was subjected to data augmentation (SMILES enumeration and Gaussian noise injection) to create a substantially larger augmented database. This was split into a training set (80%) and a test set (20%). A 5-fold cross-validation procedure was applied to generate multiple submodels, whose predictions were averaged to form a final ensemble model.

The model was optimized to minimize prediction error using the Adam optimizer and the Mean Squared Error (MSE) loss function was employed, which is standard for regression tasks, to quantify the difference between the predicted ( yi^ ) and reported (y i ) protein adsorption amounts. , MSE=∑in(yi^−yi)2n

A batch size of 32 data points was used for gradient updates. The parameters in ChemBERTa were not updated during the training, resulting in a frozen encoder layer. Model optimization was conducted using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 10–2. To ensure convergence while avoiding local minima, a StepLR scheduler reduced the learning rate by a factor of 0.5 every 40 epochs (step size), allowing the model to make larger steps initially and then progressively refine its weights as training advanced. To ensure model robustness and prevent overfitting, an early stopping mechanism was used to monitor validation loss and halt training if no improvement was observed over 15 epochs. This approach minimized both overfitting and unnecessary training time. In addition, a 5-fold cross-validation strategy was applied to the training set. This involved splitting the training set randomly into five equal subsets with one subset designated as the validation set and the remaining four subsets used to train a submodel in each of the five training iterations. This process resulted in the generation of five distinct submodels. Detailed information about the hyperparameters for BB-EIT model is provided in Table S5.

An ensemble model was then constructed by averaging the predictions from the five submodels. This ensemble approach reduced the prediction variance and improved the accuracy of the final output. The performance of the final ensemble model was evaluated with the held-out test set.

The model was trained on an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 GPU (8GB VRAM). The complete 5-fold cross-validation and ensemble training process required approximately 2 h, which demonstrates the computational efficiency of the architecture.

The model’s predictions were compared against the reported values using two key metrics suitable for regression tasks: the root-mean-square error (RMSE) and coefficient of determination (R 2). Models with superior performance were characterized by R 2 closer to 1.00, signifying a better fit to the data, and smaller RMSE, reflecting a smaller average difference between the predicted and reported protein adsorption amounts. The formulas for these metrics are as follows RMSE=MSE R2=1−SSRSST SSR=∑i(yi^−y̅)2 SST=∑i(yi−y̅)2 where y̅ is the average value, n is the total sample number, and SSR and SST represent the sum of squared regression and total variation in the data set, respectively.

BB-EIT, trained on the proposed augmented data set, showed significantly improved predictive performance compared to the baseline model trained only on the original limited data (101 entries; Figure b). Specifically, the model trained on the small original data set (101 entries) achieved a R 2 of only 0.75 on the training set and 0.73 on the test set. This confirms that the data augmentation strategy was crucial for mitigating the small data set issue.

To further validate the robustness of the data augmentation strategy, a sensitivity study was conducted by varying the heteroscedastic noise scale (0, 0.01, 0.05, 0.1, and 0.2) among different augmented data sizes. The results (Figure ) demonstrated that model performance remained remarkably stable for noise scales up to 0.05, within the R 2 showing only a marginal decline as noise was further increased to 0.20. This stability confirms that the chosen augmentation parameters provide a robust simulation of experimental uncertainty while allowing the model to effectively learn the underlying physicochemical principles of polymer–protein interactions.

Feature Importance Analysis and Model Generalizability

To provide a statistical context for the inputs of the deep learning model and to investigate potential collinearity, Pearson correlation analysis was performed on the five additional features and the output label (protein adsorption amount) using the original 101 nonaugmented data points (Figure ). The resulting correlation matrix revealed linear relationships within the system.

Pearson correlation heat map of additional features and protein adsorption. The matrix shows the linear relationship (R) between the five features and the protein adsorption amount. The correlation ranges from −1.0 to 1.0. R closer to 0 indicates a weaker correlation.

The protein-specific properties, pI (R = −0.10) and molecular weight (R = 0.02), exhibited relatively weak linear correlations with protein adsorption. Similarly, the polymer-related properties thickness (R = −0.05), contact angle (R = 0.00), and ζ-potential (R = 0.13) showed minimal linear associations with the adsorption amount. These observations suggested that complex nonlinear relationships captured by deep ChemBERTa embedding and subsequent feature fusion layers were essential for achieving the model’s high predictive accuracy (R 2 = 0.88), as simple linear correlations alone were insufficient to explain the variance in the data.

To quantify the contribution of each input component to prediction by BB-EIT, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was performed on the FC1 latent space. The analysis was designed to evaluate the impacts of six distinct components (i.e., the summed ChemBERTa embeddings and five additional features) on the prediction output (i.e., the protein adsorption amount; Figure ). The SHAP values shown in the plot were calculated by averaging the individual SHAP scores generated by each submodel within the 5-fold cross-validation ensemble. The results indicated successful synthesis of deep learning and domain-specific feature engineering.

Mean absolute SHAP values for the BB-EIT FC1 output. The bar chart quantifies the SHAP values of the five additional features and ChemBERTa embeddings (768 dimensions aggregated).

The sum of all ChemBERTa embeddings, which aggregated the chemical structural information derived from SMILES notation, was confirmed as the most important predictive component. This validated the fundamental choice of BERT architecture for encoding the underlying polymer chemistry. The protein charge and ζ-potential were the next most influential factors, showing that the polymer–protein interaction was strongly affected by the electrostatic interaction. In addition, the hydrophobic interaction, represented by the contact angle, also showed a relatively strong influence on this interaction. These results were consistent with previous studies. The comprehensive SHAP value plot confirmed that BB-EIT operated as a true hybrid model, using ChemBERTa embedding for the underlying chemical context while relying heavily on the physicochemical features of polymers and the properties of proteins for final prediction refinement.

Models that excluded surface-related properties (e.g., contact angle or ζ-potential) or physical features (e.g., thickness) generally showed a consistent and significant decrease in predictive accuracy. This was consistent with previous studies on polymer–protein interactions suggesting that surface hydrophilicity, electrostatic charge, and brush architecture are primary determinants of protein adsorption. Notably, when all three polymer-surface features (thickness, contact angle, and ζ-potential) were excluded, as in row 8 in Table , model performance dropped markedly with RMSE of 122.24 and R 2 of 0.70 on the test set. These observations indicated that chemical embedding derived solely from SMILES notation of polymers was insufficient to capture the complex interfacial phenomena governing protein adsorption. Additionally, as shown in row 5 in Table , the training performance that excluded the imputed polymer-surface features, i.e., contact angle and ζ-potential decreased to 0.83 for R 2 on the test set, which indicated the importance of the imputed physicochemical features of polymer brushes.

The importance of protein-specific features was also evident. The exclusion of both protein-related features, pI and molecular weight, resulted in a significant decline in accuracy (Table , last row). This observation underscored the importance of capturing protein-specific characteristics to differentiate adsorption behavior across various protein types.

These results were consistent with Pearson correlation analysis, which showed generally weak feature correlations, confirming that the model benefits from a diverse set of inputs describing the physicochemical characteristics of the material alongside the inherent properties of the protein.

In addition, predictive performance analysis revealed a nuanced role for the pI of proteins. As shown in Table (row 17), a model variant that excluded only the pI feature surprisingly showed a marginal improvement in the performance on the test set. This counterintuitive finding was likely a result of the model overfitting to spurious correlations within the limited data set, which contained only three protein types: BSA, lysozyme, and fibrinogen. The model may have inadvertently learned to use other features, such as molecular weight, as a proxy for the protein type, which covaried with pI in this specific set. This type of “shortcut” learning, while effective on data following the same independent and identically distributed (IID) pattern, can compromise generalization to out-of-distribution (OOD) data. , This interpretation was consistent with the results of Pearson correlation analysis, which showed a relatively strong negative linear correlation between pI and molecular weight (R = −0.43) (Figure ).

Analysis of Learned Latent Space Visualization by UMAP

The internal structure of the BB-EIT feature embedding was analyzed by applying Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) to the 256-dimensional output of the FC1 hidden layer (Figure a). The resulting projection indicates fundamental organization of the latent space by protein identity. Each point represents a specific polymer–protein interaction. As shown in the plot, these data points, colored by protein type, formed highly separated and topological distinct clusters, as well as the global coordinates of data points that contained the information on polymer–protein interactions. This clear spatial segregation, driven entirely by input features, showed that the neural network effectively compressed the heterogeneous input into a latent representation, where the primary components of the system were maximally differentiated. Furthermore, the plot confirmed the success of the nonlinear transformation and compression of the model, as this clean separation was superior to the more mixed distribution observed on UMAP of the raw concatenated layer (Figure S6), confirming that the architecture of this model successfully refined the input structure.

External Validation and Novel System Prediction

To further probe the generalizability of the model and confirm its performance on OOD data, we performed external validation across four distinct polymer–protein systems using independent data taken from literatures (Figure ). , All of the validation data was conducted under standardized physiological conditions, specifically at pH 7.4 as for all of the training set data.

Summary of the external validation across different polymer–protein adsorption systems. The lysozyme (Lys) represented a known protein type to the model, yet proteins phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK) and bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP-2) and polymer brushes poly­(sulfobetaine methacrylate) (PSBMA) and poly­(sulfopropyl methacrylate) (PSPMA) were unknown types to the model.

The first validation state involved predicting the adsorption of lysozyme and phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK, MW: 44.7, pI: 6.9) on poly­(sulfobetaine methacrylate) (PSBMA), the chemical structure of which was not included in the original IID data set. The model demonstrated high absolute accuracy for these systems, with predicted values of 68.63 ng/cm2 for lysozyme and 15.57 ng/cm2 for PGK, closely matching the reported experimental values that for lysozyme on PSBMA film with a thickness of 75 nm was 8.7 ng/cm2 and for PGK on PSBMA film 8.2 nm thick was 79.0 ng/cm2 (Table ). This external validation confirms that BB-EIT possessed a unique ability to generalize to novel polymer structures and unseen protein types, particularly globular proteins, where structural similarities to the training set were maintained.

The results of polymer-protein systems of Lys and PGK on poly­(sulfobetaine methacrylate) (PSBMA) demonstrated the precise prediction performance of BB-EIT model. Yet the model only captured the adsorption tendency of bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP-2) on poly­(sulfopropyl methacrylate) (PSPMA) surfaces.

To rigorously evaluate the architectural sensitivity to different electrostatic features, validation was performed by replacing pI with the computed net charge at pH 7.4. While the net charge-based variant showed a marginal improvement in IID data set (Figure S5), its performance degraded when applied to OOD systems like Lys and PGK that the predicted adsorption amounts were 55.4 and 15.0 ng/cm2 for Lys and PGK respectively. This performance drop indicated that utilizing pI as a global property-based feature provided superior stability and predictive precision across diverse literature data sets, reinforcing its selection as the primary biochemical features of proteins for this generalized framework.

To further probe the boundary conditions of the model, a stress test was conducted using bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP-2) on poly­(sulfopropyl methacrylate) (PSPMA). This system represents a highly specialized interaction profile that is absent from the training set. For polymer thicknesses of 10 and 30 nm, BB-EIT predicted adsorption amounts of 150 and 187 ng/cm2, respectively (Table ). While these results underpredicted the reported experimental measurements of 370 and 756 ng/cm2, the model successfully identified the thickness-dependent adsorption trend, correctly predicting that an increase in the thickness of polymer brush leads to higher protein adsorption.

The absolute discrepancy indicated the limitations of characterizing complex polymer–protein systems through global features such as pI and molecular weight to represent proteins. The homodimer BMP-2 contains localized heparin-binding domains that generate extremely high surface charge density that exhibit a high specific affinity for sulfate groups. It leads to specific, high-affinity binding with sulfonated polymers such as PSPMA that transcends standard electrostatic interactions. Because BB-EIT currently relies on global features like pI and molecular weight for proteins, it treated BMP-2 as a standard basic protein and failed to account for this kind of specific chemical binding. Consequently, while the model utilized the physical structural features (i.e., thickness) of polymer brushes to identify the correct interaction tendency, it lacked the biochemical knowledge required for absolute quantification in the specific binding systems.

Furthermore, the model’s current reliance on global features was compounded by the omission of explicit environmental input nodes. Because the training data was collected from diverse literature sources, despite the importance of ionic strength and salt concentrations in the adsorption situations, especially for zwitterionic systems, standardizing variables of them were not feasible at this early stage. Since ζ-potential and specific binding energy are sensitive to these parameters, their absence limits the model’s ability to transition from trend-based prediction to highly precise quantification in fluctuating environments. Additionally, while thickness serves as an effective proxy for the physical structure of densely packed brushes where the graft density was larger than 0.1 chains/cm2, resolving the spatial distribution of polymers more accurately will require the inclusion of features like molecular weight and grafting density in future iterations, especially if the data of low-density polymers is included in the data.

To address these limitations and move forward a comprehensive foundation model, future research will explore more sophisticated protein representation methods, including sequence-based embedding from models like ProteinBERT or ESM-2, to capture the biochemical information such as specific binding required for universal prediction. , Transitioning to such a framework will ultimately require expanded, high-quality data sets that explicitly incorporate environmental variables including temperature, pH, and ionic strength to refine the predictive precision at the biointerfaces of this model.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed BB-EIT, a machine learning model that represented a novel integration of foundation LLM ChemBERTa with an extended domain-feature layer that can accurately predict the adsorption of multiple protein types on polymer brushes with distinct structures. BB-EIT showed state-of-the-art performance, with R 2 of 0.88 on an independent test data set. External validation across diverse systems demonstrated that BB-EIT possessed a unique ability to generalize to both novel polymer architectures and new protein types. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first LLM-based approach that generalizes predictions across protein types and polymer brush structures within a single unified framework. It represents an early step toward a foundation model for polymer–protein interactions.

Our analysis showed that incorporating a comprehensive set of features, including monomer SMILES notation, polymer brush properties (film thickness, contact angle, and ζ-potential), and protein characteristics (pI and molecular weight), is essential for high predictive accuracy. These features enable the model to capture the complex interplay between protein and surface properties that governs the adsorption behavior. Besides that, the success of the SMILES enumeration and label-noise strategies was validated through a comprehensive sensitivity analysis, which confirmed the robustness of the model for handling the sparse data set typical of polymer informatics.

While limitations exist regarding absolute quantification for highly specialized binding systems or varying environmental conditions, these results establish BB-EIT as a powerful baseline architecture. Future advancements will focus on incorporating sequence-based protein embeddings and environmental variables to refine the predictive precision of the model. Overall, this study established a precedent for predicting complex physicochemical interactions at the material interface and supported the emergence of data-driven materials design in biotechnology.
