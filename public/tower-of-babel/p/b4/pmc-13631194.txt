The malignant synapse: architecture, signal integration, and therapeutic vulnerabilities in glioma
Authors: Bo Yuan, Xiaolin Zhang, Dongying Zheng, Haoran Wang, Xiran Jiang, Bin Zhang, Feng Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Emerging evidence has fundamentally reshaped the neuro-oncological paradigm, revealing that gliomas and brain metastases are not isolated cellular masses but synaptically integrated entities within the brain’s neural circuitry. This review comprehensively delineates the architecture and multi-modal signaling landscape of the “malignant synapse.” We explore how diverse pre-synaptic inputs—encompassing glutamatergic, cholinergic, and GABAergic signals—are structurally anchored by matricellular organizers (like thrombospondins) and proteolytically cleaved factors (like sNLGN3). Post-synaptically, glioma cells deploy a sophisticated array of effectors to translate these neural inputs. Through a convergence of electrochemical (AMPAR-mediated), mechanosensory (CSPG4-PIEZO1 cascade), and metabolic (CHRM3 and TrkB) axes, these diverse signals universally ignite the PI3K-mTOR signaling hub, which is further amplified across the tumor mass via the Connexin-43-coupled tumor microtube (TM) syncytium. Recognizing this profound reliance on neural inputs exposes a critical therapeutic vulnerability. We systematically evaluate pharmacological strategies to disconnect these malignant circuits, highlighting the repurposing of neuroactive drugs—including perampanel, gabapentin, and bumetanide—to effectively stall tumor progression. Finally, we address formidable translational challenges, such as blood-brain barrier penetrance and off-target neurotoxicity. We also outline future frontiers, particularly leveraging spatial multi-omics to decode how synaptic signaling orchestrates the tumor immune microenvironment. Ultimately, dismantling the neuron-glioma axis represents a transformative frontier in conquering intractable brain cancers.

Introduction

Gliomas represent the most prevalent and lethal primary tumors of the central nervous system (CNS). Encompassing a heterogeneous spectrum from low-grade lesions to biologically aggressive glioblastomas (GBM), these malignancies pose a formidable clinical challenge [1]. Despite the refinement of molecular classifications by the 2021 WHO CNS tumor guidelines—integrating IDH status and histone modifications—the prognosis remains dismal [2]. The current standard of care, relying on maximal resection and chemoradiotherapy, yields a median survival of merely 15–18 months for GBM, with recurrence being nearly inevitable [3]. This therapeutic plateau underscores that targeting intrinsic genomic drivers alone is insufficient, necessitating a paradigm shift toward extrinsic drivers within the tumor microenvironment (TME).

Historically, the intersection of neurobiology and neuro-oncology—a field now termed “cancer neuroscience”—was pioneered by foundational observations of glioma-associated epilepsy (GAE). Early studies established that gliomas act as active epileptogenic nodes, altering the microenvironment by releasing bulk glutamate (e.g., via the system xc- transporter) and disrupting local glial clearance to induce peritumoral excitotoxicity and seizures [4, 5]. While these early studies highlighted how tumors chemically manipulate the brain, subsequent transformative discoveries revealed that this relationship is profoundly bidirectional: the nervous system is not merely a passive victim, but an active driver of glioma progression [6–12]. It is now established that glioma cells hijack mechanisms of neural plasticity to form bona fide, functional synapses with host neurons [7–9, 13–16]. Through this direct “neuron-glioma crosstalk,” neuronal activity fuels tumor proliferation via AMPA/NMDA receptor signaling and depolarization currents, effectively integrating the malignancy into the neural circuitry [7, 8, 14]. However, while the electrophysiological consequences of this integration are well-documented, the specific molecular architects responsible for physically establishing and maintaining these malignant synapses remain incompletely understood.

The construction of these functional malignant synapses is not a solitary event but a highly orchestrated cascade involving multiple molecular players. While pre-synaptic initiators (such as activity-dependent cleavage of Neuroligin-3 and Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) secretion) lay the groundwork, and post-synaptic effectors (like AMPA/NMDA receptors) drive oncogenic signaling, the physical bridging of the synaptic cleft is equally crucial. In this context, matricellular proteins, particularly the thrombospondin (THBS) family [17–19], have emerged as canonical drivers. In the developing brain, astrocyte-secreted THBSs bind to the neuronal α2δ1 voltage-gated calcium channel subunit to promote excitatory synapse assembly [20, 21]. Recent evidence suggests that highly plastic tumors, such as pediatric high-grade gliomas (PHGG) and adult GBM, recapitulate this developmental program, utilizing molecules like THBS and other adhesion proteins to fuel their integration into neuronal networks [18, 19].

In this review, we synthesize structural and functional evidence to map the ‘glioma synaptome’—the collective network of synaptic inputs and structural tethers between host neurons and tumor cells. We spatially categorize the molecular drivers of this integration into pre-synaptic, synaptic cleft, and post-synaptic compartments. By dissecting these distinct domains, we elucidate how they collectively drive immunosuppression and tumor invasion, and how they contribute—alongside established mechanisms such as elevated bulk glutamate, glial dysfunction, and the disruption of perineuronal nets—to the multifactorial pathogenesis of glioma-associated epilepsy. Ultimately, dismantling this synaptogenic network offers a strategic, druggable avenue to disconnect gliomas from their neural fuel supply.

The architecture of the malignant synapse

The assembly and maintenance of functional malignant synapses between healthy neurons and glioma cells constitute a highly coordinated, spatially compartmentalized biological cascade [22]. Far from being a chaotic byproduct of tumor invasion, this architecture mimics developmental synaptogenesis. To systematically decode the “glioma synaptome,” we delineate this complex machinery into three distinct spatial and functional domains: pre-synaptic initiators, synaptic cleft organizers, and post-synaptic effectors (Fig. 1).

Pre-synaptic initiators: neuronal diversity and paracrine inputs

The initiation of the neuron-glioma crosstalk is fundamentally driven by the upstream electrophysiological excitability of host neurons. The pre-synaptic terminal acts as the primary secretory engine, actively releasing a diverse array of neurotransmitters and neurotrophins into the TME in a tightly activity-dependent manner [8, 22, 23].

Expanding the vesicular neurotransmitter repertoire

Historically, the pre-synaptic release of neurotransmitters in the context of brain tumors has focused primarily on glutamatergic vesicular release, which directly mediates post-synaptic depolarization. However, recent breakthrough evidence reveals that the pre-synaptic repertoire hijacked by tumors within the CNS is far more diverse. Crucially, recent landmark studies have identified the formation of bona fide GABAergic neuron-to-glioma synapses, particularly in pediatric diffuse midline gliomas (DMG) [13]. In the healthy mature brain, GABA typically exerts inhibitory effects. However, due to altered intracellular chloride gradients within DMG cells, GABA paradoxically acts as an excitatory neurotransmitter. The activation of post-synaptic GABA receptors on glioma cells drives depolarization, which subsequently fuels tumor proliferation [13].

Beyond GABA, other neurotransmitters are also actively exploited. For instance, cholinergic neuronal activity has been shown to robustly promote the growth of DMG, as active neurons release acetylcholine (ACh) into the microenvironment to directly stimulate tumor proliferation [14, 24, 25]. Furthermore, this activity-dependent hijacking extends beyond primary gliomas to brain metastases. Notably, breast cancer cells undergo a distinct transition to become GABAergic-responsive, heavily relying on paracrine GABA released by adjacent pre-synaptic neurons to facilitate their early survival and colonization in the brain parenchyma [26]. Collectively, this highlights a highly complex chemical repertoire—encompassing glutamatergic, cholinergic, and GABAergic signals—to sustain diverse malignant neural circuits [7, 13, 26].

Activity-dependent secretion of BDNF

Concurrently, neuronal firing triggers the active secretion of neurotrophic factors from the pre-synaptic terminals. BDNF has emerged as a key pre-synaptic mediator. Neuronal activity strictly regulates the vesicular release of BDNF into the synaptic cleft [27]. This activity-dependent neurotrophin serves as a crucial “fertilizer” that primes the TME, acting as a prerequisite for the subsequent structural anchoring and plasticity of the neuron-glioma circuit [27].

Synaptic cleft organizers: structural bridging and factor maturation

While pre-synaptic signals provide the chemical “fertilizer” for tumor growth, the establishment of a robust, long-lasting neuron-glioma circuit requires the physical stabilization of the synaptic cleft. This narrow extracellular space is not merely a passive gap, but an active zone intricately organized by secreted matricellular proteins, proteolytic factor maturation, and receptor anchoring.

The unexplored terrain of classical adhesion complexes

In the healthy CNS, the foundational architecture of the synaptic cleft heavily relies on classical synaptic cell adhesion molecules (SAMs). A primary bridging mechanism is the heterophilic interaction between pre-synaptic Neurexins (NRXN) and post-synaptic Neuroligins (NLGNs) [23, 28–30].

Notably, while the enzymatically cleaved domain of NLGN3 acts as a potent pre-synaptic mitogen, transcriptomic analyses reveal that highly invasive glioblastoma cells also robustly upregulate intact, membrane-bound Neuroligins and other adhesion molecules like SynCAM1 (CADM1) and N-cadherin [23, 30–32]. Theoretically, these molecules provide the crucial intercellular tethering that mechanically locks the tumor cell into the host’s neural circuitry. Yet, the direct assembly and physical anchoring of these trans-synaptic complexes in gliomas remain largely unexplored, representing a critical knowledge gap. Furthermore, extremely recent evidence highlights that glioma-secreted factors, such as C1QL1, actively orchestrate the pruning of healthy neural synapses to physically accommodate the expansion of tumor microtubes and the formation of malignant synapses [33].

While the establishment of these classical adhesion complexes is essential for synaptic anchoring and subsequent proliferation, it introduces an apparent biological paradox: how does this physical tethering align with the hallmark invasive and migratory nature of gliomas? This dichotomy is likely resolved through the highly dynamic, transient nature of malignant adhesions, reminiscent of the classical “go-or-grow” hypothesis [34, 35]. Glioma cells do not permanently immobilize themselves; rather, they dynamically alternate between a stationary, synaptically engaged state optimized for proliferation, and a detached, highly migratory state. Crucially, this transition is facilitated by the rapid proteolytic remodeling of the synaptic cleft. For instance, matrix metalloproteinases (such as ADAM10) actively cleave synaptic adhesion molecules and extracellular matrix tethers. This targeted proteolytic shedding effectively severs the physical anchor, allowing the glioma cell to detach, migrate along neuronal tracts (e.g., white matter bundles), and subsequently re-anchor to establish new synaptic connections in distant microenvironments. Thus, these adhesion complexes serve not as permanent anchors, but as transient docking stations that facilitate episodic bursts of growth during the invasive cascade.

The thrombospondin family as pivotal matricellular organizers

In the quest to identify the critical structural mediators of this aberrant connectivity, the thrombospondin family has emerged as the pivotal organizer of the malignant synaptic cleft. THBSs are secreted, calcium-binding matricellular glycoproteins categorized into trimeric Group A (THBS1, THBS2) and pentameric Group B (THBS3, THBS4, COMP) (Fig. 2) [17–19]. Unlike their historically recognized anti-angiogenic and angiogenic roles in peripheral cancers, their function in the CNS is distinct: they are canonical drivers of synaptogenesis. In the developing brain, astrocyte-secreted THBSs act as critical structural scaffolds [18, 36, 37]. They bind to the neuronal α2δ1 voltage-gated calcium channel subunit, initiating Rac1-dependent cytoskeletal reorganization to promote dendritic spine formation and excitatory synapse assembly [36, 37]. Recent evidence unequivocally demonstrates that glioma cells profoundly recapitulate this developmental program to physically fuel their integration into neuronal networks. Conserved structural domains within THBS, particularly the Epidermal Growth Factor (EGF)-like repeats, are repurposed to drive this pathogenic synaptogenesis [18, 36, 37]. This trans-synaptic bridging is hyperactive in highly plastic tumors, such as pediatric high-grade gliomas and adult glioblastomas. It enables them to firmly anchor into the host’s neural circuitry and secure a continuous supply of pre-synaptic growth signals.

The cleft as a proteolytic hub: generation of sNLGN3

Simultaneously, the synaptic cleft functions as a highly active proteolytic processing site. A hallmark mechanism is the activity-dependent shedding of synaptic proteins. Upon neuronal firing, the metalloproteinase ADAM10 is activated within the cleft, leading to the enzymatic cleavage of membrane-bound Neuroligin-3 (NLGN3) [23, 38]. This critical processing event releases the extracellular domain—soluble NLGN3 (sNLGN3)—into the microenvironment. This represents a specialized malignant adaptation that converts a classical adhesion molecule into a potent, mobile mitogen within the synaptic cleft.

BDNF-TrkB binding and synaptic anchoring

The physical stabilization of the malignant synapse is further reinforced by neurotrophin receptor interactions within the cleft. The previously secreted pre-synaptic BDNF subsequently binds to TrkB receptors on the surface of glioma cells [27, 39]. This BDNF-TrkB signaling axis dynamically regulates synaptic plasticity, facilitating the structural anchoring of the malignant synapse. It promotes the trafficking of crucial post-synaptic receptors to the membrane, ensuring that the tumor cell remains firmly locked into the host’s neural circuitry [27].

Post-synaptic effectors: the electrophysiological engine of proliferation

The post-synaptic glioma membrane functions as a highly sophisticated signal integration center. Rather than relying on a single dominant pathway, glioma cells employ a diverse array of post-synaptic effectors to translate chemical, mechanical, and trophic inputs from the neural circuit into sustained oncogenic drives. Strikingly, despite their distinct activation mechanisms, these diverse signals ultimately converge upon intracellular calcium mobilization and the PI3K/mTOR signaling axis.

The electrochemical axis: AMPAR-mediated depolarization

The most direct and rapid mode of neuron-glioma communication is mediated by the glutamatergic electrochemical axis. Synaptically released glutamate binds to α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid receptors (AMPARs) on the glioma post-synaptic membrane. Crucially, these tumor-associated AMPARs often lack the GluA2 subunit, rendering them highly calcium-permeable. Glutamate binding triggers rapid influxes of Na + and Ca2+, generating excitatory post-synaptic currents (EPSCs) and profound membrane depolarization [7]. This depolarization not only directly fuels tumor proliferation but also drives the formation and extension of tumor microtubes (TMs), enhancing the spatial integration of the malignant network [7].

Signal amplification via the tumor microtube syncytium

Crucially, the post-synaptic response to neuronal input is not confined to isolated, individual glioma cells. Instead, highly aggressive gliomas, particularly IDH-wildtype glioblastomas, weave complex macro-networks using ultra-long membrane extensions known as tumor microtubes [30]. The post-synaptic localized calcium transients, initiated by AMPA receptor activation, are rapidly propagated across the entire tumor mass through Connexin-43 (Cx43) gap junctions located along these TMs [7, 30]. This profound electrical coupling transforms the glioma into a synchronized functional syncytium. Within this extensive TM network, specific highly-connected glioma cells act as ‘pacemakers’, generating autonomous calcium rhythms that orchestrate tumor-wide resilience [40]. Consequently, a synaptic input received by a single cell at the invasive margin can broadly amplify proliferation signals throughout the tumor network and confer robust resistance to targeted therapies and radiation.

The mechanosensory axis: the sNLGN3-CSPG4-PIEZO1 cascade

Beyond classical electrochemistry, recent breakthroughs reveal an elegant mechanobiological dimension to the malignant synapse. The soluble NLGN3 (sNLGN3) generated in the synaptic cleft acts as a specific ligand for the Chondroitin Sulfate Proteoglycan 4 (CSPG4) receptor on the glioma surface. Upon sNLGN3 binding, tumor-associated ADAM10 initiates a secondary, regulated intramembrane proteolysis (RIP) of CSPG4. This cleavage event releases the CSPG4 intracellular domain (CSPG4-ICD) and simultaneously alters local membrane tension [41]. This mechanical shift is acutely sensed by PIEZO1, a mechanosensitive ion channel, which subsequently opens to allow a massive influx of extracellular Ca2+. This sNLGN3-CSPG4-PIEZO1 cascade highlights a novel paradigm where physical cleavage is transduced into a potent biochemical growth signal (as recently proposed in a preprint study, pending full peer-reviewed validation) [41]. Although the sNLGN3-CSPG4-PIEZO1 axis and its convergence on PI3K/mTOR signaling are demonstrated in adult IDH-wildtype GBM and pediatric DMG, these observations rely heavily on murine orthotopic xenograft models. Validating these preliminary, context-dependent findings within human tumors in situ remains a critical frontier.

The metabolic and neurotrophic axes: CHRM3 and TrkB

Parallel to ionotropic and mechanosensitive receptors, metabolic and neurotrophic receptors provide sustained proliferative inputs. In cholinergic niches, acetylcholine binds to the type 3 muscarinic receptor (CHRM3), a G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) on glioma cells. CHRM3 activation stimulates intracellular calcium transients, primarily driven by the release of Ca2 + from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [14, 25]. Concurrently, the binding of pre-synaptic BDNF to the post-synaptic TrkB receptor provides a potent neurotrophic stimulus. While primarily initiating robust downstream phosphorylation cascades, TrkB signaling also activates PLC-γ, further contributing to intracellular calcium mobilization, thereby perfectly aligning with the overarching calcium-centric convergence.

Convergence on the PI3K-mTOR central engine

The ultimate evolutionary advantage of this multi-modal receptor repertoire lies in its signaling convergence. Whether initiated by AMPAR-driven EPSCs, PIEZO1-mediated mechanosensation, CHRM3-induced ER calcium transients, or direct TrkB activation, the resulting surge in intracellular Ca2 + and receptor kinase activity universally ignites the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. As the central metabolic and proliferative engine, hyperactive PI3K-mTOR signaling drives rapid protein synthesis, tumor invasion, and extensive microenvironmental remodeling, seamlessly linking neural hyperactivity to inexorable glioma progression.

Therapeutic vulnerabilities: disconnecting the malignant synapse

The recognition of gliomas as synaptic entities fundamentally reshapes the neuro-oncological treatment paradigm. The profound reliance of tumor cells on neuronal inputs reveals a critical Achilles’ heel: survival and proliferation are contingent upon the maintenance of the neuron-glioma network. Consequently, pharmacological strategies aimed at uncoupling this malignant circuit—by targeting presynaptic excitability, cleft assembly, and postsynaptic syncytial signaling—represent a highly promising frontier in glioma therapy (Fig. 3).

Dampening neuronal hyperexcitability and receptor blockade

Because neuronal hyperactivity drives tumor progression, dampening presynaptic excitability and blocking postsynaptic receptors are key therapeutic strategies. A prominent target is the tumor-associated AMPA receptor. Perampanel, an FDA-approved AMPAR antagonist for epilepsy, demonstrates remarkable dual efficacy [7, 42–44]. It effectively crosses the blood-brain barrier (BBB) to block glutamatergic EPSCs, thereby controlling tumor-associated seizures. Simultaneously, it starves gliomas of their primary electrophysiological growth signal, exerting potent anti-proliferative effects.

Blocking synaptic assembly and ligand cleavage

Disrupting the physical scaffolds and mitogenic ligands within the synaptic cleft provides another crucial intervention window. To dismantle the THBS-mediated structural bridge, neurologically active drugs such as gabapentin and pregabalin can be repurposed. By competitively binding to the neuronal α2δ1 calcium channel subunit, gabapentin effectively prevents THBS from anchoring the presynaptic terminal, thereby severely impairing malignant synaptogenesis [6, 45, 46]. Concurrently, pharmacological inhibition of the metalloproteinase ADAM10 (e.g., using GI254023X) offers a precise mechanism to halt the cleavage of membrane-bound NLGN3. This blockade prevents the release of sNLGN3 into the microenvironment, cutting off the critical mechanosensory and proliferative cascade mediated by the CSPG4-PIEZO1 axis [41].

Dismantling the tumor microtube syncytium

Addressing the robust treatment resistance of high-grade gliomas requires targeting the macro-network that amplifies synaptic inputs. The Cx43 gap junctions, which orchestrate the electrical coupling within the tumor microtube (TM) syncytium, represent an ideal therapeutic vulnerability. Meclofenamate (MFA), a repurposed non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID), functions as a potent gap junction uncoupler [30]. By effectively sealing Cx43 channels, MFA fragments the functional glioma syncytium into isolated, vulnerable cells. This disconnection not only halts the tumor-wide propagation of calcium transients but also profoundly re-sensitizes aggressive glioma cells to standard-of-care radiotherapy and chemotherapy [30].

Targeting atypical neurotransmitter signaling

Beyond classical glutamatergic circuits, atypical neurotransmitter axes—specifically cholinergic and GABAergic signaling—present highly promising therapeutic vulnerabilities. In cholinergic-rich niches, gliomas heavily rely on acetylcholine to drive proliferation [14, 25]. Antagonizing the CHRM3 receptor uncouples the tumor from this input, suppressing ER-mediated calcium transients and depriving gliomas of a parallel route to PI3K/mTOR activation. Preclinical models strongly support this: administering the muscarinic antagonist scopolamine or the selective CHRM3 antagonist 4-DAMP restrains tumor growth and prolongs survival [14, 25].

Additionally, the paradoxical excitatory role of GABA-A receptors provides a unique intervention window for H3K27M-altered diffuse midline gliomas. These tumors hijack paracrine GABA for depolarizing survival signals, driven by an NKCC1-mediated inverted chloride gradient [13]. This presents profound dual clinical implications. First, it serves as a critical clinical warning: common palliative medications that allosterically enhance GABA-A signaling, such as benzodiazepines (e.g., lorazepam), have been unexpectedly shown to accelerate DMG proliferation and significantly shorten overall survival in preclinical models. Second, this axis can be therapeutically reversed. By utilizing NKCC1 inhibitors like the FDA-approved diuretic bumetanide, the normal chloride gradient can be restored. This pharmacological intervention negates the depolarizing effect of GABA, effectively stripping invasive tumor cells of their critical survival support [13]. Collectively, repurposing drugs to blockade these atypical neural inputs, while carefully avoiding contra-indicated neuromodulators, can profoundly disrupt the tumor’s metabolic engines and synergize with conventional therapies.

While these pharmacological strategies—including AMPAR antagonists, NKCC1 inhibition, and ADAM10 targeting—demonstrate immense preclinical promise, a realistic assessment of their clinical potential is necessary. First, the assertion that glioma cells rely strictly on neuronal inputs primarily applies to synaptically integrated subpopulations; in reality, many glioma cells within the tumor mass can proliferate independently of direct neuronal input. Second, because the anatomical and physiological conclusions supporting these vulnerabilities are currently derived predominantly from animal models and xenografts, these strategies must be recognized as preclinical proof-of-concept. The formidable translational hurdles transitioning these agents to the clinic, particularly regarding blood-brain barrier penetrance and dose-limiting neurotoxicity, are discussed in detail in the concluding section (Sect. 4).

Current limitations, future perspectives, and conclusion

The conceptualization of gliomas as synaptically integrated entities marks a watershed moment in neuro-oncology. Gliomas actively hijack the brain’s electrochemical circuitry to fuel inexorable progression and therapy resistance. While targeting these multi-modal signaling axes offers unprecedented therapeutic vulnerabilities, translating these breakthroughs into clinical reality faces two formidable challenges.

First, achieving therapeutic selectivity is paramount. Because the molecular machineries mediating malignant synaptogenesis (e.g., AMPARs, ADAM10, THBS) are conserved in healthy neural circuits, systemic blockade poses a severe risk of off-target neurotoxicity. Future pharmacological innovations must exploit tumor-specific synaptic adaptations to spare healthy cognitive networks. Second, the blood-brain barrier remains a major hurdle for effective drug delivery. Overcoming this requires advanced platforms, such as engineered nanocarriers or focused ultrasound (FUS), to ensure synapse-disconnecting agents effectively penetrate the tumor syncytium.

Moreover, the intricate architecture of these three synaptic domains leaves several critical “black box” questions unanswered, highlighting vital limitations and frontiers for future research. First, the specific mechanisms governing bidirectional signaling loops remain a critical frontier. For instance, there is currently no direct evidence indicating whether glioma cells can actively and specifically regulate the secretion of BDNF from pre-synaptic neurons. However, an indirect feed-forward loop is highly plausible: glioma-driven bulk glutamate release induces localized neuronal hyperexcitability, which subsequently triggers the classical activity-dependent release of BDNF from surrounding neurons. This BDNF then acts on tumoral TrkB receptors to enhance malignant synaptic plasticity, creating a vicious cycle. Determining whether tumors also employ direct, targeted paracrine signals to forcibly extract BDNF represents a vital area for future research. Second, evaluating the exact fraction of synaptically integrated cells is challenging. Current in vivo imaging suggests that not all tumor cells are synaptically engaged simultaneously. While synaptically integrated cells (often enriched at the invasive leading edge or within dense microtubular networks) heavily rely on these inputs, a substantial fraction of the tumor mass can still proliferate independently of direct neuronal connections. Third, the correlation between the specific neurotransmitter repertoire, tumor subtype, and anatomical brain region remains an unresolved enigma. While recent studies have identified glutamatergic synapses across various glioma entities and notably uncovered GABAergic synapses in pediatric DMG, it is premature to conclude that specific tumor subtypes preferentially hijack distinct neurotransmitter networks. A critical unanswered question is whether these synaptic phenotypes are intrinsically driven by the tumor’s molecular profile, or whether they merely reflect an opportunistic integration into the locally available neural circuits within a specific anatomical niche.

Looking ahead, the next frontier lies in decoding the multi-dimensional “tumor connectome.” Leveraging high-resolution multi-omics, particularly single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, will be crucial to map the precise spatiotemporal dynamics of these malignant circuits. Furthermore, exploring how aberrant synaptic signaling shapes the broader immune microenvironment—especially orchestrating the recruitment, polarization, and efferocytosis of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs)—will provide a more holistic understanding of glioma biology. Ultimately, disrupting the neuron-glioma axis represents a profound paradigm shift, offering renewed hope for conquering this intractable disease.
