Identification of adaptive selection footprints and adaptation strategies of hulless barley (Hordeum vulgare var. coeleste) on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau
Authors: Tongrui Chen, Jinqing Xu, Lei Wang, Handong Wang, Yawen Gan, En You, Haiyan Bian, Youlin Tang, Chao Deng, Lu Lu, Yihang Wang, Yan Yang, Luobu Zhaxi, Yuhu Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAfter millennia of cultivation on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau (QTP), hulless barley has developed remarkable adaptability. Decoding its genomic adaptive variations is crucial for elucidating the mechanisms underlying its plateau adaptation.ResultsIn this study, Genotyping 500 barley accessions generated 12,151 SNPs, revealing two major groups with geographic subdivisions. QTP barley exhibited lower genetic diversity and slower linkage disequilibrium decay, indicating stronger selection pressures or genetic drift. Gene flow revealed that Central Asia-West Asia wild barley represents the ancestral group, and gene exchange between the two major groups was limited. EigenGWAS and selective sweep analyses identified candidate genes associated with domestication and adaptation. Flowering-related genes were highlighted as potentially critical for adaptation to high altitudes. Importantly, nud and Rpg5 were recurrently detected across multiple adaptive regions.ConclusionA total of 500 barley accessions exhibit obvious population structure, which can be mainly divided into two distinct groups. Among them, hulless barley shows slow linkage disequilibrium decay and has undergone relatively strong selective pressure. Additionally, numerous candidate genes involved in adaptive processes have been identified. These findings elucidate the genetic basis of hulless barley’s adaptation to the QTP and underscore genomic signatures associated with environmental resilience and domestication.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12996-1.Keywords: Hulless barley, Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, Adaptability, Selection footprints

Introduction

The Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, known as the Earth’s highest altitude farming region [1] is globally recognized for its extreme environmental conditions, including low temperatures, low air pressure, oxygen deficiency, and intense ultraviolet radiation. Hulless barley (Hordeum vulgare var. coeleste), a staple food of the Tibetan people that has been cultivated for millennia, has demonstrated remarkable resilience to harsh conditions while maintaining relatively high yields and excellent quality.

The origin and domestication history of hulless barley has long been a subject of debate. Currently, two main viewpoints are widely recognized. One viewpoint suggests that Tibet is one of the centers of barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) origin and domestication, and that hulless barley was domesticated from native wild barley in Tibet [2–4]. The alternative viewpoint argues that hulless barley originated from eastern cultivated barley that spread to the QTP, and that the wild barley in Tibet is the result of dedomestication following natural hybridization and recombination of domesticated barley [5–9]. In recent years, a growing body of evidence has lent support to the second viewpoint. For example, Konishi [5] examined 22 samples of wild six-rowed barley in Tibet and found that it closely resembled the cultivated barley in Tibet in terms of morphology, yet differed from genuine wild barley. Moreover, the esterase complex gene loci on chromosome 3 H of wild six-rowed barley in Tibet were closely associated with certain QTP-adaptive quantitative trait loci (QTLs). Subsequently, Tanno and Takeda [6] reported that the genetic diversity of wild barley was 2.8–4.5 times that of wild six-rowed barley, and that the haplotypes of wild six-rowed barley were also present in six-rowed cultivated barley. Based on these findings, they concluded that wild six-rowed barley is unlikely to represent a true wild population and is more probably the result of hybridization. Pourkheirandish [7] analyzed the Btr1 and Btr2 resequencing data and found that wild six-rowed barley’s Btr1 and Btr2 contained cultivated barley haplotypes. They speculated that wild six-rowed barley originated from recombination following the hybridization of six-rowed landraces with genotypes btr1Btr2 and Btr1btr2. Zeng [8] conducted resequencing of hulless barley and utilized published exon capture sequencing data of barley. Their results indicated that hulless barley originated from eastern cultivated barley and was introduced to southern Tibet via northern Pakistan, India, and Nepal approximately 4,500 to 3,500 years ago. Furthermore, the low genetic diversity of hulless barley suggests that its population size contracted extremely rapidly after its introduction to Tibet. This is considered to be an outcome of its adaptation to QTP. At the same time, this low genetic diversity also casts doubt on the possibility of Tibet being the center of barley origin or domestication. Gao [9] investigated the haplotypes of Btr1 and Btr2 and found that the haplotypes carried by wild barley in Tibet were the same as those of cultivated barley. In contrast, the haplotypes in Western Asian wild barley populations were absent in cultivated barley. This finding indicates that the wild barley in Tibet originated from dedomestication after the hybridization and recombination of cultivated barley, rather than being the ancestral species of cultivated barley.

Since Darwin proposed the “Theory of Evolution”, investigating the mechanisms of species’ adaptive evolution has emerged as a major research focus. Adaptive evolution refers to the process by which species evolve specific phenotypic traits through natural selection to enhance their environmental adaptability, across various levels such as morphology, histology, physiological biochemistry, and behavior. The rapid advancement of molecular biology and sequencing technologies has led to a steady stream of studies uncovering the genetic basis of complex adaptive traits. The QTP’s extreme environment has given rise to a unique agro-ecosystem. These intense selective pressures have significantly driven the phenotypic and genetic adaptations of crops.

Currently, researchers have provided novel insights into dissecting the adaptive evolution of crops through omics approaches. Existing studies have demonstrated that the genetic basis for crops to adapt to the extreme environment of QTP encompasses the following aspects. First, retrotransposons in the genome have undergone significant expansion. An increase in the proportion of retrotransposons has been observed in the genomes of both wild barley and hulless barley [10–11]. Second, transcription factors associated with biotic and abiotic stresses have significantly proliferated. Zeng [11] discovered that genes involved in plant-pathogen interactions were under positive selection in the hulless barley genome. Subsequent transcriptome analysis revealed that the expression of genes closely related to the drought resistance of hulless barley, including NCED, PYR/PYL/RCAR, SnRK2, ABF, MYB/MYC, AP2/ERF, LEA, and DHN, were upregulated [12]. Third, genes in stress-response related signal transduction pathways are under positive selection. For instance, TaERF4, identified by Guo [13] in Tibetan semi-wild wheat, was positively selected during adaptation to the low-temperature environment. Additionally, Zeng [14] demonstrated that the accumulation of flavonoids in hulless barley was substantially higher than that in barley from plain areas, indicating that flavonoids play a crucial role in combating intense light and ultraviolet radiation. These studies imply that genes related to hormone signal transduction, plant-pathogen interactions, and secondary metabolite synthesis in hulless barley, as well as their regulatory mechanisms, differ from those in barley from plain areas. Consequently, hulless barley has evolved a unique genetic adaptation mechanism to cope with the harsh plateau environment [11].

This study aims to examine how hulless barley adapts to the QTP’s extreme environment, drawing on established knowledge of its origin, domestication, and genetic adaptation mechanisms. Based on genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS), we analyzed the population genetic structure, and applied EigenGWAS and selective sweep analyses to identify adaptive variations in the hulless barley genome. Overall, this study aims to reveal the genomic basis of local adaptation in hulless barley, with the ultimate goal of providing genetic resources and theoretical support for barley breeding targeted at high-altitude environments.

Methods

Sample collection, DNA extraction and GBS genotyping

A total of 500 barley accessions were categorized into two major germplasm groups: wild barley (comprising 106 wild two-rowed barley (Hordeum vulgare L. ssp. pontaneum (C. Koch) Ascherson et Graebner) and 10 wild six-rowed barley (Hordeum vulgare L. ssp. agriocrithon (Åberg) Á. Löve)) and cultivated barley (comprising 309 landraces and 75 modern cultivars). The public depositary authorit where these 500 accessions are stored, along with their deposition number, are also presented in Table S1, as detailed therein. These accessions originated from various regions, including West Asia (39), Central Asia (37), South Asia (40), the QTP (326), North America and Europe (13), and other barley-growing areas in China excluding the QTP (45). Specifically, Fig. 1A displays the geographical origins of 452 barley samples, while 48 samples with ambiguous origin information remain unlabeled. Figure 1B visually demonstrates the diversity of barley on the QTP.

The CTAB protocol [15] was used to extract DNA from young leaves. The GBS procedure was conducted as per the method described by Elshire [16]. Genomic DNA was briefly digested using the restriction enzyme ApeK I (G|CWCG). Barcoded libraries were then constructed to track individual accessions. Subsequently, DNA sequences adjacent to the ApeK I site were acquired using the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform. SOAPnuke v.2.1.7 (http://soap.genomics.org.cn/) [17] was utilized to filter the raw sequence data. Clean sequences were mapped to the Hordeum vulgare Hv IBSC PGSB v2 reference genome [18] using BWA v.0.7.15-r1140 [19], and raw SNPs were generated via GATK v.4.0.4.0 [20]. These SNPs were further filtered using VCFtools v.0.1.3 [21], resulting in the identification of an average of 755,159 SNPs per accession.

For subsequent analyses of population structure, linkage disequilibrium (LD), and gene flow, the SNP data was filtered under the following conditions: missing values ≤ 0.2, heterozygosity rate (Het. Rate) ≤ 0.2, and minor allele frequency (MAF) ≥ 0.05. PLINK v.1.9 [22] was employed to prune the selected SNPs to eliminate those in LD, with the specific parameter settings as follows: r2 = 0.5, a window size of 150 kb, and a step size of 5 kb. The SNPs used for EigenGWAS and selection sweep analysis were filtered according to the following conditions after extracting samples by groups: missing values ≤ 0.2, Het. Rate ≤ 0.2, MAF ≥ 0.05. Then, with the barley genome as a reference, SnpEff v.4.3t [23] was used to annotate the functional effects of the high quality SNPs obtained through screening.

Population structure analysis

The phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining (NJ) method in MEGA v.11 (https://megasoftware.net/) based on Nei’s genetic distance matrix, and the visual output was generated using iTOL v.6 (https://itol.embl.de/) [24]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using Eigensoft v.7.2.1 [25], with the graph plotted using the first three principal components. For population structure analysis, Admixture v.1.3.0 [26] was used to calculate population stratification, with K values set from 2 to 10 and five repetitions for each K value. To evaluate the LD decay patterns in different populations, 10% of the high-quality SNPs were randomly selected using PLINK v.1.9 [22] with the parameter “--thin 0.1”. Subsequently, PopLDdecay v.3.40 [27] was employed to calculate the squared correlation coefficient (r2) between SNP pairs with the parameter “--MaxDist 500”. Fixation index (Fst) and nucleotide diversity (θπ) were calculated using VCFtools v.0.1.3 [21] with a window size of 1,000,000 bp and a step size of 100,000 bp, and the graphs were plotted using ggplot2 v.4.0.2.

Gene flow analysis

Gene flow among different populations, using Aegilops searsii as the outgroup, was calculated with TreeMix v.1.1.3 [28]. The specific procedures were as follows: First, the VCF file was converted to tped format, followed by the calculation of allele frequencies for each population. Subsequently, a custom Python script was used to transform the allele frequency files into input files compatible with TreeMix v.1.1.3 [28]. The parameter settings were configured as: the migration edges (m) were set from 1 to 10, with each m value calculated five times. The number of SNPs per block (k) was set to 1,000, where k represents the window size used to account for linkage disequilibrium. Results from five repetitions for each m value were used as replicates to fit a linear model, thereby determining the optimal number of migration events.

EigenGWAS

EigenGWAS was performed by referring to the method described by Li [29]. Analyses were conducted using wild barley from Central Asia-West Asia (W-CW) and all landraces (All-Landrace) included in this study. Briefly, a genetic correlation matrix was generated using high-quality SNPs through the GEAR v.0.919 software (https://github.com/gear-tech/gear), followed by eigen-decomposition to obtain eigenvalues and eigenvectors. Eigenvalues represent the proportion of genetic variance explained by each principal component (PC), reflecting the magnitude of population genetic differentiation captured by each axis. Eigenvectors, by contrast, represent the loadings of individual SNPs on each PC, which reflect the contribution of each genetic variant to population structure and adaptive divergence. Based on the decomposition results, the first ten eigenvalues and their corresponding eigenvectors were calculated. The effect of SNPs approximately equals Fst, which can be estimated by regressing each SNP against each eigenvector. Genetic drift affects the estimation of the genetic effect at each locus. To filter out the influence of genetic drift, p-values were adjusted using the genomic control factor [30]. The corrected p-values (PGC) were used to detect the selected loci. A permutation test was performed 1,000 times using the first eigenvector as the phenotype to evaluate the null distribution. The 95th percentile was then taken as the criterion for significance. Finally, after log10 transformation, this value was used as the screening threshold.

Detection of selective sweeps

Vcftools v.0.1.3 [21] was used to calculate Fst and θπ of W-CW, landraces from West Asia (L-WA), landraces from Central Asia (L-CA), landraces from South Asia (L-SA), landraces from other barley-growing areas in China excluding the QTP (L-OC), and landraces from the QTP (L-T). The calculations used a window size of 1,000,000 bp and a step size of 100,000 bp. Regions in the top 5% were considered adaptive segments.

Referring to Chen [31], XP-CLR v.1.0 [32] modeled allele frequency differences across multiple loci between the two tested populations in each group, using the parameters: -w1 0.005 200 2000 1 -p0 0.95. Finally, we applied a spline window method implemented in GenWin v.0.1 [33] (smoothness = 100) to smooth the results. Genomic regions with W statistics (reflecting the smoothed cross-population composite likelihood ratio score) in the top 5% were selected as candidate adaptive intervals.

Identification of candidate genes

Candidate intervals were defined as the regions extending 200 kb upstream and downstream from the significant loci identified by EigenGWAS, and genes within these intervals were considered candidate genes. Similarly, adaptive segments identified by selection sweep analysis were also treated as candidate intervals, with their enclosed genes regarded as candidate genes. BLASTp was used to search for homologous genes of the candidate genes. The Pfam database (https://pfam.xfam.org/) was utilized to determine the functions of the protein domains of the candidate genes. Enrichment analysis of the candidate genes was conducted using the “GO Enrichment” tool in TBtools v.2.376 [34], with the significance threshold set at p < 0.05.

Results

Analysis of barley accessions via GBS

GBS was carried out on a diverse panel of 500 barley accessions. The results indicated that, on average, each sample generated 18,777,745 reads, which were successfully aligned to the barley reference genome [18] (Table S2). The complete set of initial sequence data has been deposited and stored in the Genome Sequence Archive (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa/) [35] within the BIG Data Center of the Beijing Institute of Genomics (BIG) [36], Chinese Academy of Sciences, under accession numbers CRA007788 and CRA022672. After applying filtering criteria of MAF ≥ 0.05, Het. Rate ≤ 0.2, missing values ≤ 0.2, and LD pruning with an r² threshold of 0.5, a total of 12,151 SNP markers were selected for subsequent analyses of population structure, LD pattern, and gene flow. For EigenGWAS and selective sweep analysis, the samples were first grouped according to specific research objectives. Following the same filtering criteria, high-quality SNPs were obtained for each group pair. Specifically, the numbers of SNPs used to identify selection footprints in the six group pairs—W-CW vs. All-Landraces, W-CW vs. L-T, L-WA vs. L-T, L-CA vs. L-T, L-SA vs. L-T, and L-OC vs. L-T—were 67,205, 56,499, 52,239, 50,885, 51,298, and 48,610, respectively.

Hierarchical organization of barley accessions

To comprehensively evaluate the population structure of the 500 barley accessions, a NJ tree, PCA, and population structure analysis were performed using the preselected 12,151 SNPs. The NJ tree revealed a distinct bifurcation into two major clades (Fig. 2A). The first major group consisted of barley from non-QTP cultivation areas (NQTP_CULT), and the second major group included barley from QTP cultivation areas (QTP_CULT). Among these, NQTP_CULT mainly consisted of hulled barley accessions from West Asia, Central Asia, South Asia, North America, Europe, and other domestic planting regions. This group could be further subdivided into W-CW, Central and West Asian landraces (L-CW), South Asian hulled barley landraces (L-SA-H), cultivars developed in North America and Europe (C-NC), and L-OC. Although South Asian hulless barley landraces (L-SA-N) were genetically closely related to the QTP_CULT, they were assigned to NQTP_CULT based on their geographic origin. QTP_CULT mainly consisted of hulless barley and wild barley accessions from the QTP and adjacent regions, which were further divided into L-T, QTP wild barley (W-T), and QTP improved cultivars (C-T) (Fig. 2A). PCA also revealed that the 500 accessions could be divided into two major groups. The first principal component (PC 1), which explained 52.80% of the total genetic variation, clearly distinguished W-CW, L-CW, C-NC, L-SA-H, and L-OC from QTP_CULT. The second principal component (PC 2), accounting for 21.99% of the genetic variation, separated L-SA-N and L-SA-H from L-OC. The third principal component (PC 3), explaining 16.82% of the genetic variation, separated W-T from L-T and C-T (Fig. 2B). NQTP_CULT showed a more scattered distribution pattern than QTP_CULT, indicating higher genetic diversity within NQTP_CULT. For population structure analysis, cross validation (CV) errors were calculated for K values from 2 to 10. The CV values decreased continuously with increasing K without an obvious inflection point (Fig. S1). Such a continuous decline is common in crop populations with complex genetic backgrounds and wide geographic origins, suggesting a pattern of continuous genetic differentiation rather than strictly discrete subpopulations in the present barley collection. Considering the overall results of the phylogenetic tree, PCA, and population structure, we used the clustering pattern at K = 7 as the primary framework for subsequent analyses and discussion. At K = 2, the 500 accessions could be broadly categorized into two major groups similar to the NJ tree and PCA results (Fig. 2C). The first major group consisted of barley from NQTP_CULT (①), and the second major group included barley from QTP_CULT (②). With increasing K values, the population structure was resolved into finer subgroups. At K = 3, L-OC within NQTP_CULT was clearly distinguished. At K = 4, L-SA-N in NQTP_CULT and W-T in QTP_CULT were separated from the other subgroups. At K = 5, C-T in QTP_CULT formed an independent group. At K = 6, L-SA-H in NQTP_CULT was distinctly distinguished. At K = 7, L-WA, L-CA and C-NC within NQTP_CULT each formed independent subgroups. From K = 8 to 9, L-T in QTP_CULT could be further subdivided into five subgroups. By comprehensively integrating the population structure dynamics from K = 2 to K = 9, together with geographical distribution and accession type, we finally defined nine stable subgroups: W-CW (W-CA and W-WA, 31), L-CW (L-CA and L-WA, 45), L-OC (45), L-SA-H (20), L-SA-N (20), C-NC (13), L-T (179), C-T (62) and W-T (85). In summary, by integrating three complementary analytical methods with information on accession type and geographical distribution, we classified the 500 barley accessions into two major groups and nine subpopulations.

Having elucidated the population genetic structure, we further investigated the LD decay patterns across different subgroups. The LD decay distance, defined as the point where r2 diminishes to half of its maximum value, was 176 kb for the W-CW subgroup. Within the NQTP_CULT subgroups, L-CW, L-SA-N, L-OC, and C-NC exhibited LD decay distances of 208 kb, 208 kb, 205 kb, and 208 kb, respectively, with no significant variation among them. Conversely, the QTP_CULT subgroups displayed longer LD decay distances: 219 kb for W-T, 221 kb for L-T, and 229 kb for C-T. These findings indicated that LD decays more slowly in QTP_CULT accessions than in NQTP_CULT accessions (Fig. 2D). This pattern suggests that the LD coefficient decays more slowly in hulless barley from the QTP_CULT, which might stem from strong natural or artificial selection or notable genetic drift.

To accurately quantify the genetic differentiation among the nine subgroups, pairwise Fst values were calculated (Fig. 2E and Table S3). The results indicated that the Fst values for each subgroup pair ranged from 0.06 to 0.40, reflecting considerable variation. Notably, W-CW and L-CW exhibited a relatively close genetic relationship, with a differentiation coefficient of 0.134. This aligns with the population stratification results, suggesting that the cultivated hulled barley in this region has a close genetic relationship with local wild barley, implying a possible domestication origin from local wild barley. Additionally, cultivated hulled barley from South Asia, North America, Europe, and other regions showed a close genetic relationship with the wild and hulled barley landraces from Central and West Asia, possibly due to introduction and domestication in West Asia. The clustering tree and population structure analysis results revealed a genetic difference between L-SA-H and L-SA-N, with an Fst of 0.240, indicating moderate differentiation. The Fst values between L-SA-N and W-T, L-T, and C-T were 0.171, 0.207, and 0.207, respectively. In terms of grouping, L-SA-N was closer to the hulless barley from QTP_CULT (Fig. 2E). Among the various subgroups (Fig. 2F and Table S4), W-CW exhibited the highest nucleotide polymorphism (1.292 × 10⁻⁶), while C-T showed the lowest (7.808 × 10⁻⁷). These results indicate that the population structure of the 500 barley accessions has a clear hierarchical organization and a relatively high level of genetic diversity.

Gene flow among barley subgroups

To explore whether historical gene flow events had occurred among the nine subgroups, the optimal number of migration edges was determined to be three by using the linear regression method, indicating a total of three gene flow events (Fig. S2). Figure 3A showed the phylogenetic tree of the nine subgroups without gene flow; when Aegilops searsii was used as the outgroup (root), W-CW was the first diverged group. Figure 3B presented the three gene flow events and the corresponding phylogenetic tree, which revealed that W-CW was the earliest diverged group, followed by L-CW and C-NC. After L-CW, L-SA-H and L-OC diverged sequentially, then W-T and L-SA-N, and finally L-T and C-T. Among the gene flow signals, the most significant gene flow was observed from L-SA-H to L-SA-N. During the historical cultivation of barley in South Asia, frequent gene exchange might have occurred between hulled and hulless barley landraces, due to geographical proximity or overlapping distributions, leading to a relatively close genetic relationship between these two subgroups. Second, there was substantial gene flow from L-OC to both L-T and C-T. However, gene flow from L-SA-H to QTP_CULT was relatively weak, as indicated by the lighter color intensity of the corresponding arrow in Fig. 3B. Furthermore, no gene flow was detected between QTP_CULT and the wild barley or landraces from Central and West Asia (Fig. 3B). These findings indicate that, compared to barley materials from Central and West Asia, QTP_CULT barley materials form a relatively isolated group. This isolation may be attributed to the barley’s adaptation to the QTP’s harsh environment and long-term artificial cultivation practices.

Identification of adaptive-related genomic regions and candidate genes

Given that West Asia is universally acknowledged as the origin center of barley, comparisons of W-CW with L-CA, L-WA, L-SA, L-OC, and L-T may reveal numerous genomic selection imprints stemming from natural and artificial selection during domestication and introduction. To pinpoint genomic regions linked to domestication and adaptation in diverse ecologies, we formed the W-CW vs. All-Landrace group and applied EigenGWAS. We selected the top 10 eigenvectors (Ev1 to Ev10) and used each as a phenotype for single-marker association analysis based on 67,205 SNPs. The average genetic correlation of the 340 barley materials was − 0.0035, indicating an effective population size of 288.22 and 28.53 effective genome segments. The first eigenvalue was 53.257, while the tenth was only 4.556 (Table S5). The genomic inflation factor (λGC), which adjusts for population stratification in association analysis [29], varied from 2.895 to 80.743 (Table S5 and Fig. S3). Based on each eigenvalue’s coordinate signs, the 340 accessions were divided into two subgroups. Fst, quantifying subgroup selection differentiation via EigenGWAS’s chi-square test (1 degree of freedom), showed marked variation from Ev1 to Ev10, peaking at 0.264 on Ev1 and troughing at 0.022 on Ev10 (Table S5). Figure 4A shows that the peaks of -log10 (PGC) and Fst are almost mirror images, validating the EigenGWAS-defined grouping. Using the top 10 eigenvectors, we identified 4,275 loci (Table S6). Notably, Ev5 and Ev6 harbored the most loci (1,403 and 1,425, respectively), comprising 65.6% of the total. The numbers on Ev7, Ev8, and Ev10 were 873, 170, and 270 (20.2%, 3.9%, and 6.3%), while Ev2, Ev3, and Ev9 had relatively few loci: 39, 93, and 38 respectively (Table S5). By designating 200 kb regions flanking each significant locus as candidate intervals, we identified 4,320 candidate genes, including 2,185 located on significant loci (Table S6). A total of 863 genes were enriched in cellular processes, mainly in macromolecule and cellular/protein metabolic processes, with some related to photosystem II and water-responsive proteins (Fig. S4). This highlights the close link between barley domestication and adaptation to diverse geographical/climatic conditions and key biological processes. We identified seven genes with known functions: six related to the flowering period, involving developmental and circadian clock regulation pathways, and one associated with grain color. Notably, multiple loci identified key photoperiod and circadian clock genes HvCCA1 [37] and HvLUX1 [38] (Fig. 4A and Table S7).

Genetic loci associated with adaptation to the QTP environment may represent selection targets during barley propagation and diffusion. To identify selection signatures linked to the adaptability of the hulless barley population, we employed selective sweep analysis, comparing the W-CW, L-WA, L-CA, L-SA, L-OC, and L-T groups. By using the top 5% of Fst, θπ and W statistic values as thresholds, we identified overlapping regions of selective sweeps across the five group pairs. These overlapping regions accounted for 111.6 Mb (2.33%), 207.1 Mb (4.32%), 78.27 Mb (1.63%), 152.7 Mb (3.19%), and 90.46 Mb (1.89%) of the Morex v2 reference genome [17] (Fig. 4B and F). Within the W-CW vs. L-T group pair, the most extensive adaptive region was detected on chromosome 4 H, with the primary differentiated area located within the 4 H: 100 Mb-495 Mb interval. Through integrated XPCLR and Fst analysis, a total of 653 genes were identified (Table S8). Notable among these were HvSHP1 [39], which is involved in floral meristem development; HvGI [40] and HvVrn3 [41], both of which play essential roles in photoperiod and circadian clock pathways; and the hulless grain gene nud [41] (Fig. 4B). Gene ontology enrichment analysis revealed that these 653 genes were annotated to 16 GO terms (Fig. S5). A significant number of genes were highly enriched in metabolic processes, particularly in pathways related to cellular macromolecule biosynthesis. Additionally, some genes were associated with nucleic acid and DNA catalytic activities (Fig. S5).

Within the L-WA vs. L-T group pair, the most extensive adaptive region identified on chromosome 4 H spanned 149.6 Mb (Table S9). Our analysis pinpointed a total of 1,034 genes within this region, including HvPRR59 [38], a pivotal gene that regulates photoperiod response with precision, and HvMADS68 [39], which is intricately involved in the development of floral meristems (Fig. 4C and Fig. S6). Gene ontology enrichment analysis indicated that a significant number of these genes were enriched in metabolic processes. Notably, some genes were specifically enriched in pathways associated with lipid synthesis and metabolism. Others were linked to nucleic acid binding and catalytic activities. Additionally, there were genes associated with aerobic respiration and components of the respiratory electron transport chain (Fig. S7).

Within the L-CA vs. L-T group pair, adaptive regions were relatively evenly distributed across the seven chromosomes, with the exception of chromosome 1 H. Our analysis identified 500 genes within these regions (Table S10), including HvWPSRLK [40], which is implicated in grain development, and nud [42] (Fig. 4D). Among these, 109 genes were enriched in primary metabolic processes, and 102 genes were enriched in cellular metabolic processes. Notably, some genes were enriched in DNA-binding transcription factor activity, DNA binding, and protein kinase activity (Fig. S8). The translation products of these genes include protein kinases that catalyze the essential processes of protein phosphorylation and dephosphorylation, zinc finger proteins, and U-box proteins.

Within the L-SA vs. L-T group pair, the most extensive adaptive region was identified on chromosome 5 H, spanning 65.2 Mb (Table S11). Our analysis detected 903 genes within this region, including HvCBF8A [43], a critical gene for cold stress response; SD1 [44], a dormancy-associated gene; HvADA2 [39], which precisely regulates heading date; and nud [42] (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, multiple functional genes were identified within the 559–600 Mb interval on chromosome 5 H (Fig. S9). For instance, the CBF gene family, along with HvVrn1 [45] and HvPhyC [46], which are significantly associated with growth habits and heading date within the 597.5–600 Mb interval. Additionally, a significant difference in θπ was observed between L-SA and L-T within the 575–585 Mb range (Table S12 and Fig. S9). This study revealed that this region contains multiple members of the SAUR hormone-responsive gene family, cytochrome P450 family genes, and NBS-LRR genes, strongly suggesting that this segment may play a pivotal role in hulless barley’s defense against pathogen invasion. GO Enrichment analysis indicated that some genes were enriched in pathways related to ion transmembrane transport and β-glucan synthesis and metabolism (Fig. S10).

Within the L-OC vs. L-T group pair, the most extensive adaptive region was identified on chromosome 5 H. Our analysis detected a total of 560 genes within this region, including HvWPSRLK [39] and HvADA2 [39], both of which are implicated in grain development and heading date regulation, respectively (Fig. 4F and Table S13). Gene ontology enrichment analysis revealed that these 560 genes were annotated to 22 GO terms (Fig. S11). Specifically, 147 genes were annotated to the catalytic activity category, and 196 genes were annotated to the biological process category. Notably, 9 genes were enriched in the hormone regulation pathway (Fig. S11), and detailed annotation confirmed that all of them are members of the SAUR hormone-responsive gene family, which has been reported to play a crucial role in plant adaptive growth [47].

In the four group pairs (W-CW vs. L-T, L-WA vs. L-T, L-CA vs. L-T, and L-SA vs. L-T), one locus (chr6H: 441591393) was identified within the candidate adaptive region (Fig. S12A), and it belongs to the upstream gene variant mutation type. Of particular importance, a total of seven genes commonly identified across all four group pairs were detected (Table S14 and Fig. S12B), specifically including HORVU5Hr1G045700, HORVU5Hr1G045710, HORVU5Hr1G045750, HORVU5Hr1G045780, HORVU5Hr1G045790, HORVU6Hr1G064720, and HORVU6Hr1G064740. Among these, HORVU5Hr1G045790 warrants special attention as the protein it encodes is a stem rust resistance protein, and this gene is also widely recognized by its alias “Rpg5”, which has been proven to confer significant rust resistance to barley [48]. In addition, a total of 59 flowering-time regulatory genes were identified through Fst, θπ, and XPCLR results. These include six genes involved in light perception and signal transduction, 13 circadian clock-associated genes, ten vernalization genes, nine genes related to gibberellin signaling and metabolism, 13 genes involved in floral meristem development, and eight grain development-related genes. These genes are involved in key biological processes including light signal perception and transduction, vernalization, transcription and hormone synthesis regulation, and grain development (Fig. S13). Notably, the primary genomic differentiation regions between L-WA, L-SA, and L-T were located on chromosomes 4 H and 5 H, respectively (Fig. S14).

Discussion

The population genetic structure of 500 barley accessions

Due to its complex climate and geographical conditions, the QTP has emerged as the most distinctive barley cultivation region globally. Its diverse array of wild and cultivated barley varieties provides valuable germplasm resources for exploring the evolutionary history and breeding initiatives of barley worldwide.

The clear genetic divergence between QTP_CULT and NQTP_CULT, together with further subdivision into nine genetically distinct subgroups, reflects obvious geographic structuring and long-term adaptive differentiation (Fig. 2). The close genetic relationship between QTP wild barley and hulless barley aligns with the findings of Gao [9]. The pronounced genetic differentiation between QTP_CULT and wild hulled barley from Central and West Asia, coupled with limited direct gene flow between the two major groups, indicates that QTP_CULT has evolved as a relatively independent genetic cluster, shaped by geographic isolation and obvious environmental filtering. The slower LD decay observed in QTP_CULT compared with NQTP_CULT is consistent with a history of stronger selection, including both natural adaptation to extreme highland conditions and strong artificial selection during local farming. Reduced recombination and extended LD may also reflect genetic drift associated with population bottlenecks during colonization and adaptation to the QTP.

The genetic diversity analysis revealed that NQTP_CULT exhibited significantly higher genetic diversity than hulless barley or QTP wild barley (Fig. 2F and Table S4), a finding that aligns with the results of Zeng [8]. This relatively low genetic diversity of hulless barley suggests a rapid contraction in population size, commonly referred to as a genetic bottleneck, following its introduction to the QTP. This bottleneck is a direct consequence of the adaptation process of hulless barley to the QTP environment [8]. Although QTP wild barley showed slightly higher diversity than cultivated hulless barley, its diversity remained lower than all six subgroups within NQTP_CULT (Fig. 2F and Table S4). Together with extended LD across the QTP_CULT genome, these observations support the hypothesis that Tibet is unlikely to represent a primary center of origin or domestication for hulless barley [8]. The phylogenetic tree constructed based on gene flow analysis (Fig. 3B) indicates that the W-CW group represents the earliest ancestral lineage. Before spreading to the southern region of the QTP, hulless barley might have passed through countries such as Afghanistan, Pakistan, India, and Nepal in South Asia. This finding supports one of the proposed dissemination routes of hulless barley [8–9].

The 85 wild barley accessions selected from QTP_CULT displayed a wide range of morphological variations, including two-rowed brittle spikes, two-rowed non-brittle spikes, six-rowed brittle spikes, six-rowed non-brittle spikes, hulled forms, and hulless forms. This morphological diversity contradicts the notion that their genetic diversity is significantly lower than that of other groups. Llorens [49] suggested that enhanced ultraviolet radiation in high-altitude regions increases cross-pollination events, thereby raising heterozygosity in self-pollinating crops. The higher morphological diversity is attributed to the presence of numerous strains and genes that maintain elevated heterozygosity levels [9]. However, whether the wild barley accessions from QTP_CULT are truly wild remains contentious, and additional biological evidence and research are required to confirm their origins.

Flowering-time genes are critical for the adaptation of hulless barley

Regions with high altitude and latitude are characterized by frigid climates and extended daylight durations [31]. To complete its life cycle, hulless barley must synchronize its flowering time with favorable weather conditions, thereby avoiding damage to sensitive floral tissues from extreme temperatures or drought. Cultivating hulless barley in such extreme environments exerts selective pressure on seasonal response genes, leading to the development of a “spring growth habit” that is advantageous for spring sowing under cold conditions. The 59 flowering-time related genes identified in this study highlight the polygenic basis of phenological adaptation to the QTP (Fig. S13). Previous studies have similarly emphasized the role of flowering genes in high-altitude and high-latitude adaptation. For example, flowering-time alleles were strongly correlated with altitude in maize landraces [50], analogous to patterns observed in hulless barley. In teosinte and high-latitude maize, genes such as GI, BAS1, and PRR7 contribute to high-altitude and high-latitude adaptation [31]. Extensive sequence variation in flowering-time regulatory genes has also been linked to environmental adaptation in barley [51]. Together with our findings, these observations suggest that flowering-time genes represent key targets of selection during hulless barley adaptation to high altitude and high latitude environments.

Morphological characteristics, agronomic and quality traits are under selection during the adaptation process of hulless barley

Over thousands of years of adaptation to the plateau environment, hulless barley has undergone selective pressure on various traits, including yield, quality, morphological characteristics, strong dormancy, and stress resistance. Recent metabolomics analyses have revealed that flavonoid accumulation in hulless barley landraces is significantly higher than in barley landraces cultivated in China’s plain regions [14]. This finding indicates the critical role of flavonoids in coping with intense light and ultraviolet radiation. The identification of HvMYB4H [52] (Fig. 4D and F), a key regulator of blue grain color, further supports adaptive selection on pigmentation pathways. Dark spike and grain colors, including black, blue, and purple, are highly prevalent in QTP barley and likely represent adaptive responses to intense UV radiation at high altitude.

Starch and sucrose metabolism pathways are key determinants of barley yield and quality. Genes involved in cellulose and β-glucan biosynthesis were significantly enriched (Fig. S5 and S10), indicating that grain quality traits have been actively selected during domestication and adaptation. The plant hormone signal transduction pathway also plays a crucial role in growth, development, and stress resistance. Genes enriched in DNA-binding transcription factor activity, DNA-binding, and protein kinase activity were identified (Fig. S8). The products of these genes include protein kinases that catalyze protein phosphorylation and dephosphorylation, zinc finger proteins, and U-box proteins, which are speculated to be involved in the adaptation of hulless barley to extreme plateau environments by regulating signal transduction and gene expression.

In the L-SA vs. L-T group comparison, multiple members of the SAUR hormone-responsive gene family, cytochrome P450 family genes, and NBS-LRR genes were identified within the 575–585 Mb region on chromosome 5 H (Table S12 and Fig. S9). Combined with the geographical and environmental differences between the two groups, these genes may be key candidate genes for the adaptation of hulless barley to different plateau microenvironments and are involved in responses to biotic and abiotic stresses. Additionally, the nud locus controlling hulless grain and loci such as int-c [53] and Vrs4 [54] regulating spike architecture were detected in multiple comparisons, indicating that these genes are not only classical domestication genes but also key targets of combined natural and artificial selection during highland adaptation. Overall, these results suggested that during hulless barley’s adaptation to the plateau’s extreme environment, multi-target selection has acted on morphological, agronomic, and quality traits to enhance resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses.

Key genomic regions in hulless barley during adaptation to the QTP’s extreme environment

Dondup [55] highlighted that the photosystem II response gene family on chromosome 2 H and the protein kinase family on chromosome 3 H are crucial for hulless barley’s adaptation to the QTP environment. In this study, six photosystem II response genes were identified, including HORVU2Hr1G057700, HORVU2Hr1G060880, HORVU2Hr1G090100, HORVU6Hr1G051650, HORVU2Hr1G090070, and HORVU7Hr1G113210. The identification of HORVU2Hr1G057700 and HORVU2Hr1G060880 is consistent with Dondup [55] findings. These genes may help hulless barley adapt to the intense light environment on the plateau by regulating photosynthetic efficiency.

Genomic differentiation analysis revealed that major divergence regions between L-WA, L-SA, and L-T are concentrated on chromosomes 4 H and 5 H (Fig. S14). These chromosomal patterns likely reflect divergent environmental pressures and variable intensities of genetic drift during independent migration routes. The long arm of chromosome 5 H has been implicated in cold tolerance and vernalization in Triticeae [56]. CBF family genes identified in this study may mediate low-temperature responses through feedback regulation, thereby enhancing freezing tolerance in QTP hulless barley (Fig. S9).

Conclusion

Hulless barley has developed robust adaptations to the QTP’s harsh conditions through prolonged cultivation. Genomic analysis of 500 barley accessions uncovered a distinct hierarchical population structure and unique genetic traits in hulless barley, including lower genetic diversity and slower LD decay compared to non-plateau barley. These findings indicated that hulless barley has undergone stronger selective pressures and genetic drift. Population structure and gene flow analyses suggest that hulless barley forms a relatively independent group compared to barley from Central and West Asia. During its adaptation, hulless barley experienced multi-trait selection targeting morphology, agronomy, and quality to combat environmental stresses. Notably, genes related to the flowering period, which are involved in key processes such as light signal perception, light signal transduction, vernalization, regulation of gene transcription, hormone synthesis, and grain development, played a critical role in adapting to high altitude and latitude. These insights enhance our understanding of the genetics underlying hulless barley’s adaptability and provide a foundation for its sustainable improvement in the QTP and beyond. However, this study relied on GBS sequencing data. Due to the inherent limitation of low sequencing coverage, some genetic loci with significant effects may not have been effectively identified. In addition, while multiple candidate adaptive genes were successfully identified in each group, it remains unclear which of these candidate genes play key roles, as well as which traits each gene specifically regulates and what biological functions each gene undertakes. In the future, whole-genome sequencing of hulless barley and the construction of a hulless barley pan-genome will greatly promote the excavation and functional analysis of key adaptive genes in hulless barley. This research direction can not only provide strong support for the breeding improvement and germplasm resource innovation of hulless barley, but also offer important references for identifying adaptation-related loci of wheat and other cereal crops on the QTP, thereby laying a solid theoretical foundation.
