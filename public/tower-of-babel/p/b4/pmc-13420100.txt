Sustainable Polyolefin Science: A Two-Voice Perspective toward a Shared Academic–Industrial Vision
Authors: Alexandra R. Albunia, Veronica Ambrogi, Markus Gahleitner, Giuseppe Milano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polyolefins have shaped modern society, but their future requires a fundamental shift in how they are designed, produced, used, and reused/recycled. This perspective, written as a two-voice contribution from academia and industry, examines how decades of scientific understanding enabled the rapid expansion of polyolefin applications. The proposed view is a system-level framework for polyolefin science, integrating catalysts, polymerization, formulation, processing, and end-of-life strategies. These elements are treated as strongly interconnected across the material life cycle, rather than as isolated domains. Today, sustainability challenges demand rethinking the traditional course of innovation by explicitly incorporating end-of-life considerations such as recyclability, reuse, and material recovery into early stages of application design. We argue that future progress still demands academic–industrial cooperation and hinges on coupling molecular, mesoscopic, and process scales, integrating digital and machine-learning tools with physical modeling and redesigning additives, composites, and recycling strategies with a circular mindset. Equally essential is the societal dimension: improved waste management, efficient collection systems, and improved educational paradigms for the next generation of scientists. By bridging fundamental insight into industrial practice, polyolefin science can evolve toward predictive, data-driven, and truly sustainable material design.

IntroductionWhy Polyolefins Matter

Polyolefins, mostly polyethylene (PE) in its basic types of high-density PE (HDPE), linear low-density PE (LLDPE) and low-density PE (LDPE), and polypropylene (PP) including a variety of homo- and copolymers, make up nearly 60% of the global plastics production, around 430 Mt produced in 2024. This is above the projections of Galli and Vecellio from 2004, who called polyolefins “the most promising large-volume materials for the 21st century.” There are three elementary reasons for this massive and rapid volume growth: (i) property diversification, (ii) price/performance relation, and (iii) industrialization rate at the same pace as know-how development.

The history of polyolefin production started in the 1950s, based on the Phillips catalyst and high-density polyethylene (HDPE). This was followed by the development of Ziegler–Natta catalysts, a groundbreaking discovery enabling low-pressure polymerization of olefins and the production of stereospecific polymers like isotactic polypropylene (iPP). For HD/LLDPE, the range of crystallinity, density, and with it flexibility and low-temperature performance has been widened by single-site catalysts like constrained geometry types and now goes from 860 to 970 kg·m–3, from “fringed micelles” to lamellae with more than 300 nm thickness. This enables such diverse applications as hot-melt adhesives, high-strength fibers, packaging films, beer crates, and pressure pipes for water and gas distribution to be based upon one class of chemically similar polymers. For PP, diversification has been achieved by multistage copolymerization and postreactor modification, including designing multiphase matrix/elastomer compositions and advanced fiber-reinforced composites. Contributions from polymer science and fracture mechanics have enabled covering applications from flexible infusion pouches to automotive constructions requiring a stiffness of more than 4 GPa with PP compositions. All that is achieved with a density below 1000 kg·m–3 (except mineral- or fiber-reinforced grades) and a cost of slightly more than 1000 €·t–1 (as of today).

However, while long-term applications have grown in importance and volume, packaging and other single-use items still make up a large share of PE and PP applications, causing a high share in consumer waste. A recent polymer material flow analysis for Europe indicates that of all polyolefins marketed from 1950 to 2016 only 7.5% have been recycled, while 13.7% are “in-use stock” (including automotive and infrastructure) and 48% landfilled, the remaining 16.5% being incinerated, partly for energy generation or as fuel substituent. Even with a rather low global warming potential (GWP) expressed as kg CO2 per kg of polymer, around 1.6 for PP and around 2.0 for PE, the fact that the majority of that lies in the stored calorific value of the polymer calls for intensified recycling independent of legal requirements. It also highlights the importance of alternative monomer sources, from bio-based to chemically recycled raw materials. In this context, environmental impacts and end-of-life considerations are increasingly being internalized into economic decision-making through regulatory frameworks, infrastructure costs, and broader societal pressures. This evolution is consistent with the classical, well-established theory of externalities and its modern extensions in climate economics (e.g., Nordhaus, RICE/DICE frameworks), where environmental constraints become integral components of optimization.

Moreover, the inherent risk of landfilling (especially if improperly maintained) and littering, dispersion of plastic waste, and resulting micro- and nanoplastics in the environment is a further motivation for more recycling. After an ambitious start of the circular economy concepts, the initial plans have been practically slowing down over time, with multiple factors playing a role. The challenges on the (visionary) timeline and targets can be only partly attributed to geopolitical-economic factors. Indeed, the lack of efficient technologies and sustainable, high-quality products plays a major role. We are seeking breakthrough innovations that can be serendipitous but require strong and tight collaboration among different competencies, with a circular view on materials and processes. More sustainable production of polyolefins, usage, and end-of-life solutions present challenges benefiting from a strong collaboration between academia and industry.

This perspective is not intended as a comprehensive literature review, but rather as a conceptual framework that organizes and connects existing knowledge across the polyolefin life cycle in a forward-looking manner. Herein, we offer a two-voice contribution, the authorship being equally divided between researchers from academia and from industrial R&D, proposing sustainable strategies for future polyolefins. In this context, sustainability is understood as “meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs,” in line with widely adopted international definitions (e.g., UN Sustainable Development Goals). Sustainability constraints are not solely driven by policy or trends but emerge from fundamental ecosystem functionality and resource limitations requiring scientifically grounded approaches. In this perspective, this concept is interpreted in scientific terms as a system-level problem, in which environmental, economic, and societal constraints must be balanced within physically realistic conditions.

Bridging Two Worlds: Academia and Industry

Academic and industrial research exist within different worlds, each with its own priorities, time scales, and measures of success. Academia is often portrayed as the natural home of creative, curiosity-driven exploration, while industry is thought to focus on concrete results and short development cycles. However, this distinction comes from stereotypes rather than from reality. Both systems, in their own ways, are subject to pressures favoring quick results: in academia, through the race for high-impact publications and visibility; in industry, through market expectations and the need for predictable returns. These internal dynamics, although understandable, can limit the capacity of both environments to take risks or pursue long-term innovative goals.

Our experience in collaborative projects has shown that the most fruitful partnerships arise from acknowledging differences among academic and industrial environments and learning how to move beyond them. When left to themselves, both systems become too comfortable and miss the opportunity for developing solutions with actual benefits for people and planet. Collaboration offers a way to escape these comfort zones. For the academic side, working with industry introduces the reality of constraints: materials that are imperfect, processes that must be scaled, and boundaries imposed by cost or regulation. We can consider these constraints as opportunities: they test theories and reveal where models meet the limits of the real world and need more concrete representation. For the industry, interaction with academia provides space for questioning assumptions and revisiting fundamentals that are too easily taken for granted in production.

The dialogue between the two approaches often produces unexpected directions that neither side could have anticipated alone. Multiple competences are often needed to solve “practical industrial problems” and understanding each other is key. Individual competences need to be integrated and communicated with a network of competence, and all share common or complementary goals. Ultimately, the bridge between academia and industry is valuable precisely because it “destabilizes” both. It challenges academia to think in terms of real-world impact and encourages industry to rediscover the strategic value of fundamental understanding.

The introduction and penetration of polyolefins in all aspects of our daily lives is one of the biggest testimonies of synergy that can be created by bridging the academic and the industrial worlds, as depicted in the inner cycle of Figure . Driven by the needs of a fast-developing society, polyolefins have revolutionized our living standards and contributed to the progress of technology, accompanying us into the Information Age. Economic drivers and policy frameworks inevitably influence research directions, but integrating these constraints within scientifically grounded sustainability strategies, such as those based on life-cycle analysis and internalization of environmental costs, can align economic incentives with long-term societal goals. The challenges that arose, after having been neglected for a long time, are now calling for new joint efforts to find innovative and sustainable solutions, putting back into focus the people and the planet’s needs (outer circle of Figure ). Polymerization and Catalyst Design

Catalyst design represents the foundation of the polyolefin life cycle, defining the molecular architecture of the resulting polymer and influencing its performance at every subsequent stage. Good examples of this strategy are catalysts for polypropylene–ethylene copolymerizations. Indeed, copolymers of iPP with ethylene or higher α-olefins span a wide range of properties and related applications. The resulting properties of each material in this class are mostly determined by comonomer content and molecular weight. An important concept is that the final properties of such copolymers are strongly related to the crystal structure and polymorphism of the final materials. For example, the transparency of the manufacts is determined by significant mesomorphic phases, while the sealing behavior of these materials from the onset of melting defines the sealing initiation. For pressure pipes requiring ductility and long-term resistance, a reduced lamellar thickness and enhanced tie-molecule density determine the desired properties. A very recent and relevant example of this strategic design is propylene–ethylene copolymers with isotactic propylene sequences obtained across the entire range of comonomer composition by De Rosa and co-workers. Using supported metallocene catalysts, copolymers in a wide range of compositions with different properties have been obtained. This leads to materials with properties spanning from polypropylene to polyethylene and from rigid plastomers to elastomers.

More generally, considerable efforts have been devoted to optimizing the performance of catalysts and achieving a molecular-level understanding of the underlying mechanisms. Over the past two decades, both experimental and density functional theory (DFT) studies have profoundly advanced the field, providing atomistic insight into polymerization mechanisms and clarifying the roles of supports, co-catalysts, and donor molecules. These investigations have elucidated fundamental aspects, such as stereoselectivity control and chain termination, establishing a solid foundation for linking molecular-level chemistry to macroscopic catalyst behavior. , Recent advances in quantitative structure–property relationships (QSPR) and machine-learning approaches are beginning to complement traditional catalyst development. For example, data-driven modelling has been used to rationalize the role of internal donors in Ziegler–Natta catalysts and to guide the design of metallocene systems with controlled polymerization behavior, highlighting the potential of these methods to explore complex structure–property relationships in polyolefin polymerization. Looking forward, such approaches are expected to play an increasing role in navigating the complex design space of catalytic systems.

At the opposite end of the scale, most industrial olefin polymerization processes employ heterogeneous catalysts in multistage reactor systems. From an engineering viewpoint, process performance depends on the balance between catalytic activity, heat and mass transfer, and morphological control required to achieve the desired polymer properties. However, these two levels of description, molecular chemistry and macroscopic behavior, have long evolved in parallel, leaving an intermediate region largely unexplored. Understanding how catalyst behavior at the molecular scale translates into reactor-scale performance remains a challenge, particularly in cases where molecular and morphological evolution are strongly coupled. A representative manifestation of this coupling is the evolution of catalyst morphology in MgCl2- or silica-supported systems, where the distribution of active sites and the early stages of polymer growth jointly determine particle fragmentation and the developing polymer morphology. Catalyst morphology control is crucial, as it governs the accessibility of active sites and thus affects polymer features. Moreover, morphological defects, irregular shape, low bulk density, or excessive roughness can lead to agglomeration, sheeting, and reactor instabilities. Recent studies have addressed these phenomena through an intermediate-scale approach that connects atomistic insights with process-level behavior. An illustrative example is the combined experimental and molecular-simulation investigation of stress generation and fragmentation in Ziegler–Natta catalysts, developed through collaboration between academic and industrial researchers. , Catalyst particle morphology evolving under different prepolymerization conditions has been rationalized by linking experimental observations with molecular-level modelling. Atomistic models of nascent polyethylene phases on magnesium dichloride surfaces enabled a quantitative assessment of internal pressure build-up during polymer growth, in agreement with McKenna’s classical hypothesis. This integrated approach provides molecular understanding of how local physical states and reaction conditions control fragmentation, offering predictive guidelines for tuning prepolymerization parameters and ensuring stable operability of the industrial process. Despite remarkable progress in combining experimental observations with molecular simulations, the connection between atomistic mechanisms and macroscopic behavior remains incomplete. Another interesting viewpoint about the characterization of individual catalyst particles for olefin polymerization, as well as for the reverse process for polyolefin decomposition using 2D and 3D imaging techniques, is reported in a recent review.

A more systematic effort is required to develop methodologies that couple molecular, mesoscopic, and process scales. Advancing this integrated view would allow polymer science to move toward more predictive models. The studies discussed here exemplify this direction and highlight the importance of maintaining a continuous dialogue between different scales of observation and interpretation.

Formulations of Plastics and Functional Additives

A key aspect in the design of plastic formulations relies on the use of functional additives able to customize properties and address specific applications of manufacts. Although PE- and PP-based plastics together account for a large share of the plastics market (around 60%), they are estimated to use only about 10% of the total additives produced. In contrast, polyvinyl chloride (PVC), which represents approximately 16% of polymer production, accounts for more than 30% of the total polymer additive consumption.

With the growing accumulation of plastics in marine and soil ecosystems, additives, together with residual monomers and non-intentionally added substances (NIASs), such as by-products and degradation compounds, have become a major focus because of their potential impacts on human health. The concerns on environmental and health impacts of plastic waste have driven efforts in the development of bio-based alternatives, mainly derived from agricultural crops, forestry, marine sources, and microorganisms or, alternatively, from secondary biomasses originating from waste streams.

For example, in the case of polyolefins, a plethora of bio-based flavonoids, such as quercetin derived from radish and red onions, rutin from some Citrus species, dihydromyricetin from Pinus and Cedrus plants, and α-tocopherol from sunflower oil, have been used, in amounts up to 0.3 wt %, as antioxidants for PE and PP systems, to replace traditional synthetic stabilizers. Recent research also focuses on extracting antioxidants from agro-industrial wastes. These compounds show good performance even in relatively high percentages, representing promising and sustainable alternatives to conventional compounds. However, for the wider adoption of sustainable additives and for moving toward a circular plastics industry, challenges in terms of costs, availability, and regulatory approval must be overcome. Because these additives are still in the early stages of commercialization, the achievement of homogeneous compositions at a large scale is the key to gaining industry acceptance. It is also crucial to avoid replacing known toxic substances with alternatives whose safety is often not properly evaluated. Therefore, it would be ideal to disclose the additives used throughout the entire plastics manufacturing process, from polymer producers to compounders to converters, to cooperate also with research and academic entities, and to create databases and best practices, enabling better supply chain coordination and smarter additive selection without compromising market competitiveness. Limited transparency in additive composition can also hinder the predictive understanding of material behavior, recyclability, and environmental impact. In this context, the development of standardized descriptors capturing the functional role and key physicochemical properties of additives (e.g., polarity, solubility characteristics, thermal stability, approximate concentration ranges) represents a promising direction. Such an approach may enable improved traceability and compatibility assessment in recycling streams, while preserving intellectual property constraints. Using such descriptors, a viable solution could also involve the use of computational chemistry based on Deep Learning (DL) technology to conceive new compounds, correlate chemical structures with their performance as additives for specific polymers, and obtain data on their leaching and toxicity. Using this predictive computational approach will accelerate the search for “sustainable” alternatives. Further insight is offered by life cycle assessment (LCA) based tools, by defining additive flow patterns, highlighting trade-offs among hazards, life cycle impacts, and recyclability to inform sustainability-driven decision-making.

Composite Design and Filler Dispersion

The concept of an intermediate modelling scale, previously discussed for catalysts, is equally relevant for polymer–filler composites. In these materials, the organization of polymer chains at interfaces governs dispersion, morphology, and macroscopic performance. Among the wide range of inorganic fillers, carbon black and silica nanoparticles are two important classes for industrial polymer technology. Despite decades of use, understanding how such particles disperse within polymer matrices and how interfacial regions evolve during processing remains a scientific and technological challenge. The molecular interactions governing polymer adsorption, chain confinement, and filler–filler connectivity occur at the nanometer scale but ultimately determine the mechanical, electrical, and rheological performance of the bulk material. In the industrial context, several efforts have been made to characterize the polymer-bound layer on the filler surface for rubbery materials by using simulations also in connection with complementary experimental studies. A direct experimental investigation of the bound layer (having a thickness of nanometers) is difficult, making molecular simulations very appealing. Atomistic simulations can provide an accurate description of interphases, but their application is limited to small polymer chain lengths, small nanoparticles, and short time scales. This limitation is strong if we consider that fillers employed for industrially relevant materials span several length scales, going from nanometers to micrometers. Recent developments in hybrid Particle–Field Molecular Dynamics (hPF-MD) have opened the way to explore these systems on the relevant length and time scales. , This approach was first applied to polyethylene–carbon black composites, capturing the hierarchical structure of filler aggregates and the conformational behavior of polyethylene chains near realistic carbon surfaces. Simulations revealed how curvature and surface disorder influence chain adsorption. The hPF framework thus provides a molecular foundation for the “bound layer” concept, widely invoked to interpret reinforcement and viscoelastic response in filled polymers. A subsequent combined experimental/simulation study extended this methodology to bidisperse polyethylene matrices, showing how the presence of a low-molecular-weight fraction promotes filler dispersion. Experiments performed for the same study with bimodal materials synthesized via the Borealis Borstar technology demonstrated that improved dispersion correlates with the fraction of short chains. Simulations rationalized this effect by showing that short chains preferentially occupy surface cavities of carbon black primary particles, displacing longer ones and enhancing interfacial entropy. This provides a molecular rationale for industrial practices exploiting multimodal or wax-modified polyolefins to improve processability and dispersion. Investigations of silica nanoparticles in polystyrene matrices can further illustrate the versatility of the multiscale approach. Analyses of two- and three-body potentials of mean force have shown that polymer-mediated many-body effects are crucial in determining the shape of aggregates. ,

These results exemplify how targeted experiments and simulations, developed in close academic–industrial collaboration, can converge toward a shared predictive understanding. The capacity to design and optimize composite materials through the integration of modelling, experiments, and industrial insight also lays the foundation for addressing the next challenge, ensuring their sustainability beyond use.

End-of-Life, Recycling, and Environmental Fate

A deeper understanding of the fate of polyolefins beyond their service life is one of the most pressing challenges in polymer science. Industrial polyolefins, such as PE and PP, undergo complex sequences of photo-oxidation, chain scission, and crystallinity changes leading to the formation of micro- and nanoparticles. Experimental studies simulating long-term exposure to sunlight and oxygen have demonstrated that fragmentation can occur on surprisingly short time scales, sometimes within months, generating submicrometric fragments with altered surface chemistry and reduced molecular weight. These fragments, often referred to as secondary micro- or nanoplastics, represent transient states rather than stable end products. Their physicochemical evolution is governed by the interplay among mechanical stress, oxidation, and environmental factors. The work by Huber and co-workers on isotactic polypropylene (iPP) films provides a detailed model of how environmental microplastics originate from common packaging materials. Accelerated ultraviolet aging showed severe chemical degradation, oxidation, and fragmentation into submillimeter particles to occur in less than 48 h. By coupling these results with an independent Arrhenius analysis, the study estimated realistic times for submillimeter particle formation ranging from approximately 9 months to just over 3 years, depending on local climate. These findings offer a mechanistic explanation for the apparent mass loss observed at the lower end of the environmental microplastic size distribution and for the mismatch between estimated plastic inputs and quantities of microplastics detected in field studies. On the molecular scale, recent simulation studies have begun to reveal the structures and behaviors of nanoplastics in aqueous environments. Coarse-grained models of oxidized polyethylene fragments have been developed to describe the morphology and surface chemistry of these particles after environmental exposure. Simulations indicate that oxidation generates more hydrophilic surfaces and a less thermodynamically stable fragment association in water if compared with unoxidized surfaces. This molecular description supports the view that nanoplastics are not inert but are dynamic, evolving entities. The complementarity between targeted experiments and molecular modeling would be particularly valuable here: accelerated weathering tests provide empirical degradation rates, while simulations can elucidate the microscopic structural transitions and intermolecular forces underlying fragmentation, aggregation, and dispersibility. Degradation pathways lead to a progressive loss of molecular integrity, ultimately transforming structured polymeric materials into highly dispersed, lower-value states. In this sense, the environmental fate of polyolefins is closely linked to the ability (or inability) to retain the molecular value throughout their life cycle. In cases where material recycling is technically or economically unfeasible, controlled combustion with energy recovery may represent a pragmatic option for managing polyolefin waste. However, while energy recovery may be viable under specific conditions, it converts polymer carbon into CO2, a high-entropy state, effectively eliminating the molecular value embedded in the material. Beyond environmental considerations, recycling can also contribute to reducing dependence on primary raw materials and increasing resource security, which is particularly relevant for regions such as Europe, where access to fossil-based feedstocks relies heavily on external supply chains.

From the point of view of environmental remediation, an additional complexity arises from the very nature of nanoplastics as incidental nanomaterials formed through degradation rather than being engineered with controlled features. For this broad heterogeneity, the field of nanoplastic research remains in its infancy with significant methodological gaps between the physical reality of environmental particles and the simplified systems currently accessible to observation or simulation. Emerging digital approaches have begun to bridge this divide. Image-based recognition of micro- and nanoplastics through machine learning and automated microscopy analysis provides a promising route to handle the variability of these materials and to obtain statistically meaningful datasets from complex environmental matrices. Such developments illustrate how data-driven methodologies can complement multiscale physical modeling, setting the stage for new predictive tools in sustainable polymer science. These tools are essential for addressing the complexity of real polyolefin waste streams, where composition and interfacial interactions govern material performance. In this context, a key challenge is the poor compatibility of mixed polyolefins, even between chemically similar polymers, such as polyethylene (PE) and polypropylene (PP). Recent advances in reactive compatibilization and upcycling chemistry demonstrate that such limitations can be overcome, enabling the transformation of mixed waste to higher-performance materials.

It is important to emphasize that polyolefins are not intrinsically more hazardous than other classes of materials. Their widespread presence in environmental compartments primarily reflects their large production volumes and capillary use rather than a fundamentally higher inherent risk. As for many other materials, the environmental impact of polyolefins is strongly governed by life cycle management. Biodegradability is often proposed as a universal solution to plastic waste, but it should not be considered a general remedy for improper waste management. Biodegradation processes are highly environment specific and may not be effective under typical conditions encountered in natural ecosystems or landfills. Therefore, sustainable strategies must be framed within a broader system perspective, where LCA provides a quantitative basis for comparing different end-of-life options.

Multiscale and Digital Strategies for Sustainable Design

The increasing complexity of polymer systems, together with the global demand for sustainable innovation, calls for integrating modeling, experimentation, and data analytics into unified, predictive frameworks. A more systematic integration of multiscale modeling, experimentation, and digital tools would enable predictive design strategies across all stages through continuous feedback within a shared digital core of modeling, AI, machine learning, and data analysis. A schematic view of this strategy is depicted in Figure as a conceptual representation of a circular polymer life cycle.

Conceptual representation of a circular, data-driven polymer life cycle. The digital core of the proposed strategy is the use of AI, machine learning (ML), and LCA models in connection with experimental and simulation data.

A promising route is the joint use of molecular simulations with machine-learning tools to accelerate polymer design. Two recent examples can show the potential of this approach. Carbone et al. used coarse-grained simulations to extract a set of physically motivated descriptors for a library of plasticizers. A decision-tree classifier trained on these descriptors accurately predicted miscibility and reduced the design space by almost an order of magnitude. This demonstrates how simulation-derived descriptors can guide efficient screening and formulation. Müller-Plathe et al. combined DPD simulations with ML models to predict compatibilization efficiency in graft copolymers. This study shows how ML can distill key structural factors from simulations and accelerate the exploration of complex design spaces. Together, these works illustrate how physics-informed ML transforms molecular simulations into predictive tools for sustainable polymer design. More generally, the emergence of multiscale and digital strategies represents a transformative opportunity for polymer science. From an academic and industrial perspective alike, polymer science is evolving into a data-driven discipline, where digital tools complement physical intuition and empirical expertise. Multiscale approaches, from quantum chemistry to continuum descriptions, provide the backbone of this evolution. Polymers are inherently multiscale entities: atomistic chemistry, chain conformations, and mesoscale morphology are deeply interconnected, making it impossible for any single level of theory to capture their full behavior. Bridging these scales remains one of the field’s greatest challenges, particularly when realistic time and length scales must be combined with chemical specificity. As already mentioned, hybrid particle-continuum techniques such as hPF-MD have emerged as a particularly effective approach to address this challenge. ,, By combining particle-based and field-based descriptions, it enables simulations of multicomponent polymer systems with chemical detail and computational efficiency. These simulations can capture the self-organization of blends, filled matrices, or partially degraded polymers and naturally generate large datasets that can feed digital and data-centric analyses. An example of this integration is provided by carbon nanotube (CNT)–polymer composites, where hPF-MD trajectories have been coupled with resistor-network models to predict electrical conductivity. Such studies demonstrated how mesoscale morphology descriptors can be quantitatively linked to macroscopic observables, a foundational step toward virtual testing of materials. Building on this, recent developments have introduced physics-informed artificial intelligence to bridge simulated microstructures and experimental properties. In particular, graph attention networks (GATs) were trained on hPF-MD trajectories of CNT/polymer composites. The resulting model reproduced conductivity trends with remarkable accuracy and, through interpretable attention maps, identified the structural motifs responsible for optimal conductivity. This synergy between simulation and AI embodies a paradigm for digital materials design. Future promising directions could involve digital twins of recycling processes informed by hPF-MD and/or other types of molecular models, graph neural networks for property recovery in reprocessed materials. This possible evolution well couples with the rise of Polymer Informatics: a data-centric paradigm extending the principles of materials informatics to the polymer world. Data-intensive methodologies, powered by AI and high-throughput simulations, can uncover hidden Processing–Structure–Property–Performance (PSPP) relationships and enable inverse design of sustainable polymers. One of the key challenges in applying data-driven approaches to polyolefins lies in the adequate representation of the polymer structure. As recently discussed, current text-based formalisms often fail to capture essential features of polyolefins, such as long-chain branching, molecular weight distributions, and compositional heterogeneity, thereby limiting the accuracy of structure–property relationships. Such specialized polymer databases and emerging academic–industrial repositories can provide the infrastructure needed to populate AI models with experimental and simulated data. Creating such a data ecosystem will require deep collaboration between Academia and Industry. By merging physics-based simulations, artificial intelligence, and life cycle thinking, polymer science can evolve into a predictive discipline in which materials are conceived, simulated, manufactured, and recycled within a unified framework.

Next Generation of Scientists

If industry is responsible for making innovation accessible to society, academia has the duty to train the next generation of scientists to tackle current and future challenges. Fundamental knowledge is essential to developing sustainable materials and processes that are highly performing. Plastic competencies, and more, in general, competencies for the chemical industry, could be at risk because of the negative perception of the public. Thus, the number of scientists in these areas has been declining. However, for a wealthy and safe society, polymers and chemical sciences will need to continue to exist.

It is important to understand how new and old solutions fit into the environment. To favor sustainability, students need to learn from nature and build a life-cycle-analysis mindset. At a very high level, developing system thinking skills would help enable scientists to find solutions with a reduced risk of drawbacks, get more familiar with several disciplines, and grow better communication skills across competencies.

Digital literacy must be integrated into the education system starting from the youngest age. Indeed, due to the race for publications in terms of number and citation records in academia, we are assisting to an “explosion” in the number of journals and papers. Thus, students need to be trained not only to search but also to filter the large amount of information. In principle, the use of Artificial Intelligence in Systematic Literature Reviews (SLR) has huge potential for this purpose. From a practical point of view, as recently reviewed, available tools for SLR are in their infancy and still suffer from poor user friendliness and lack of reliability proofs. According to the previous points, critical thinking has to be considered always as crucial for the right decision-making.

Conclusions and Outlook

Polyolefins have shaped modern society very profoundly. Their success was enabled by the convergence of catalyst innovation, molecular insight, multiscale structure–property understanding, and industrial scale-up. In only a few decades, academic–industrial efforts have made polyolefins indispensable, providing lightweight, low energy demand, and remarkably low-carbon-footprint materials compared to many traditional solutions. Today, however, the challenge has reversed direction. The historical trajectory of moving from monomers to final applications must now be matched by an equally rigorous pathway in the opposite direction, where end-of-life considerations inform earlier design choices.

A central message is that bridging academia and industry remains crucial. Collaboration does not aim to homogenize approaches but rather to make their differences productive. Academia gains relevance when confronted with industrial constraints, and industry gains resilience by revisiting fundamental mechanisms.

Sustainability requires rebuilding the life cycle from the reverse direction, explicitly accounting for recyclability, reuse, and material recovery already at the stages of polymer design, formulation, and processing. Across the polyolefin life cycle, the need for multiscale, physics-informed methodologies emerges as a unifying theme for linking molecular events to mesoscopic structures and macroscopic performance.

A more sustainable future for polyolefins also requires confronting the present environmental burdens. Micro- and nanoplastics remain an urgent problem that demands improved detection, monitoring, and remediation technologies. Yet, these fragments should be regarded not as an inevitable end state but as a transient symptom of past design and waste-management limitations. By integrating multiscale modeling, controlled additive strategies, and high-quality recycling streams, we envision reducing their formation at the source, shifting the emphasis from environmental removal to efficient recycling. Beyond environmental considerations, circular strategies can also contribute to reducing the dependence on primary raw materials and increasing resource security. This is particularly relevant for regions such as Europe, where access to fossil-based feedstocks relies heavily on external supply chains.

These transformations cannot rely solely on scientific and technological progress but require the active participation of society. Improved waste management systems and public awareness all constitute essential pillars of circularity. Strengthening the collaboration among citizens, institutions, producers, and waste-management operators is therefore indispensable to closing the loop. In this broader perspective, material design, recycling technologies, regulation, and consumer behavior must evolve jointly, each reinforcing the others. This long-term transformation depends on the preparation of the next generation of scientists. System thinking, life cycle awareness, digital literacy, and cross-disciplinary communication must become central elements of polymer science education. Students will need not only technical competence but also the ability to critically interpret vast amounts of information and assess AI-generated data.

In conclusion, a sustainable future for polyolefin science will emerge from the convergence of fundamental understanding, multiscale modeling, data-driven strategies, societal engagement, and robust academic–industrial cooperation. By aligning these elements within a full life cycle framework, we can transform polyolefins from a source of environmental concern into a cornerstone of sustainable technological development. The path forward is ambitious, but it offers the opportunity to redefine polymer science for the benefit of both people and planet.
