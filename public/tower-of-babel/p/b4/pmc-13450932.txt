Improving diagnostic clarity in fibro-osseous lesions using modified Gallego’s, alizarin red, and von kossa stains: A pilot comparative study
Authors: Sharon Sam, Vijaya Nirmala Subramani, Anand Ramanathan, Thamizhchelvan Harikrishnan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Distinguishing bone, cementum, and immature osteoid in maxillofacial fibro-osseous lesions (FOLs) can be challenging using routine haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining alone.Objective:To quantitatively evaluate the comparative diagnostic utility of H&E, Alizarin Red, Von Kossa, and Modified Gallego’s stains (MGS) in identifying mineralized components across FOL subtypes.Materials and Methods:In this pilot study, archival specimens of fibrous dysplasia (n = 2), cemento-osseous dysplasia (n = 2), cemento-ossifying fibroma (n = 2), and juvenile ossifying fibromas (n = 2) were stained. Independent pathologists scored structural feature visibility utilizing a semi-quantitative 0–3 scale. Visibility scores, inter-observer, and intra-observer reliability were analysed using Friedman, Wilcoxon signed-rank, and Gwet’s AC1 tests.Results:Qualitatively, MGS provided consistent bone (green) versus cementum (red) differentiation, Alizarin Red isolated immature osteoid, and Von Kossa mapped mature, phosphate-rich calcifications. Quantitatively, inter-observer reliability demonstrated strong to perfect consensus, while intra-observer stability was moderate-to-perfect for MGS, slight-to-substantial for Alizarin Red, and poor-to-moderate for Von Kossa. Descriptively, MGS achieved the highest mean clarity score (2.93 ± 0.25), followed closely by Alizarin Red (2.80 ± 0.40) and Von Kossa (1.80 ± 1.17). Global Friedman and post-hoc pairwise comparisons confirmed that these differences were statistically significant (P = 0.0003), with both MGS (P = 0.0069) and Alizarin Red (P = 0.0057) yielding significantly superior diagnostic clarity over Von Kossa (P < 0.01).Conclusion:Integrating a targeted special stain selection protocol significantly overcomes the descriptive limitations of routine H&E, reducing diagnostic ambiguity in maxillofacial FOL configurations.

INTRODUCTION

Fibro-osseous lesions (FOLs) of the jaws represent a heterogeneous spectrum of pathologic conditions in that normal bone is variably replaced by fibrous connective tissue containing mineralized deposits.[1] These lesions include fibrous dysplasia, cemento-osseous dysplasia, cemento-ossifying fibroma, juvenile trabecular ossifying fibroma, and juvenile psammomatoid ossifying fibroma.[2] Although sharing overlapping histopathological features, these entities differ in clinical presentation, radiographic patterns, and biological behaviour.[3]

Histopathological examination remains essential for accurate diagnosis; however, routine haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining may not reliably distinguish between immature osteoid, cementum, and calcified deposits especially in early or mixed lesions leading to potential diagnostic ambiguity.[4]

Special histochemical stains can enhance visualization and differentiation of mineralized components. Alizarin Red highlights calcium-rich deposits such as early osteoid whereas Von Kossa detects phosphate or carbonate minerals and Modified Gallego’s stain (MGS) differentially colours bone (green) and cementum (red), thereby allowing more precise identification.[5] For instance, in a comparative evaluation of fibrous dysplasia and ossifying fibroma using MGS and Van Gieson stains, cementum stained red, bone stained greenish-yellow, with fibrous dysplasia mineralization appearing greenish-yellow, while ossifying fibromas exhibited both greenish-yellow and reddish calcifications highlighting differences in tissue origin and potential aggressiveness.

Despite the potential diagnostic value of these methods, there remains a notable gap in systematic, comparative studies evaluating the relative utility of routine H&E versus Alizarin Red, Von Kossa, and MGS across different FOL subtypes. Most existing research either focuses selectively on one stain or examines limited lesion types, hindering comprehensive understanding and clinical application. Therefore, the aim of this study is to compare the diagnostic utility of H&E, Alizarin Red, Von Kossa, and MGS in the histopathological evaluation of FOLs of the jaws, focusing on their ability to identify and differentiate key mineralized components across various lesion subtypes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and case selection

This retrospective study was conducted on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) archival specimens of FOLs retrieved from the Department of Oral Pathology and Microbiology, Sri Ramachandra Dental College and Hospital, Sri Ramachandra Institute of Higher Education and Research (SRIHER), Chennai, India. The study cohort comprised eight histopathological confirmed cases, including fibrous dysplasia (n = 2), cemento-osseous dysplasia (n = 2), cemento-ossifying fibroma (n = 2), juvenile trabecular ossifying fibroma (n = 1), and juvenile psammomatoid ossifying fibroma (n = 1). Clinical details and descriptive features were retrieved from archival records and tabulated for analysis.

Tissue preparation

Paraffin blocks were sectioned at 4 μm thickness. The sections were deparaffinized by heating at 57°C for 10 min, followed by immersion in xylene to remove residual paraffin. Standard H&E staining was performed for routine histopathological evaluation. Subsequently, three histochemical staining techniques namely Alizarin Red, Von Kossa, and MGS were carried out for differential identification of mineralized components [Figure 1].

Preparation and staining procedures

For Alizarin Red staining, the slides were first deparaffinized and stained with a 2% Alizarin Red solution for 2 to 5 min. Excess dye was shaken off, and the sections were blotted. The slides were then dehydrated in 100% acetone for 30 s, followed by dips in an acetone-xylene solution. Finally, the sections were cleared in xylene and mounted using a synthetic mounting medium.

For Von Kossa staining, paraffin sections were deparaffinized and hydrated to water. The sections were then incubated with a 1% aqueous silver nitrate solution in a clear glass Coplin jar placed under ultraviolet light for 20 min. After rinsing in several changes of distilled water, unreacted silver was removed using 5% sodium thiosulfate for 3 min. The slides were rinsed again in distilled water, counterstained with 0.1% nuclear fast red solution for 2 min and subjected to a final rinse in distilled water. Finally, the sections were dehydrated through graded alcohol, cleared in xylene, and coverslipped using a permanent mounting medium.

For Modified Gallego’s staining, the sections were initially deparaffinized and stained in haematoxylin for 15 to 18 min. After rinsing in distilled water, the slides were placed in a mordant solution prepared by mixing 200 mL of distilled water, 1.5 mL of concentrated nitric acid, 1 mL of 40% formaldehyde, and 1.5 mL of iron chloride for two minutes. The sections were then stained with a solution containing 5 mL of carbol fuchsin in 50 mL of 0.4% acetic acid, rinsed with distilled water, and washed with the mordant for 1 to 2 min. Subsequently, the slides were stained with 0.01% aniline blue in saturated picric acid containing 2 mL of light green for 5 min. The sections were then dehydrated, cleared with xylene, and mounted in DPX. A summary of the histochemical staining protocols used in the study is provided in Table 1.

Histological analysis

Mounted sections were examined for histopathological features by three independent oral pathologists who were blinded to the diagnosis using both light microscopy at 4x, 10x, and 40x magnifications. Each histological feature was scored on a semi-quantitative intensity (0 − 3) scale that is a modified version of the scale described by Kunche et al. (2017).[6] In our modified scale, 0 indicated not visible, 1 indicated poor visibility, 2 indicated moderate visibility, and 3 indicated excellent visibility.

To ensure that identical microscopic fields were evaluated across the different stains, consecutive serial sections of 4 μm thickness were utilized. Observers navigated to the exact same regions in each serially stained slide by identifying specific morphological and architectural landmarks. Consensus scores for each feature were determined when at least two of the three observers agreed. The visibility of each feature per stain and lesion type was descriptively tabulated.

Statistical analysis

Inter-observer and intra-observer reliability among the three independent observers was evaluated using raw percentage agreement, Cohen’s κ, and Gwet’s AC1 coefficients. To evaluate intra-observer reliability, all archival specimens were re-evaluated and re-scored by each independent observer after a wash-out period of 2 weeks following the initial assessment. Descriptive summary metrics, including mean, standard deviation (±SD), and median were calculated for the semi-quantitative visibility scores across all stains. To evaluate whether differences in diagnostic visibility scores among Alizarin Red, Von Kossa, and MGS were statistically significant, a non-parametric global Friedman test was implemented, followed by post-hoc pairwise comparisons using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Statistical significance was defined a priori at P < 0.05.

RESULTS

All five FOL subtypes, fibrous dysplasia, cemento-ossifying fibroma, juvenile trabecular ossifying fibroma, juvenile psammomatoid ossifying fibroma, and cemento-osseous dysplasia, were successfully evaluated using routine H&E staining and three histochemical techniques (Alizarin Red, Von Kossa, and MGS).

Routine H&E staining provided a good baseline visualization of overall tissue architecture across all lesions; however, it demonstrated limited capability in distinguishing between immature osteoid, mature bone, and cementum-like deposits. The application of special stains successfully overcame these limitations. In fibrous dysplasia, Alizarin Red effectively identified immature osteoid, while Von Kossa mapped phosphate-rich calcified deposits, and MGS clearly outlined green bone trabeculae [Figure 2]. For cemento-ossifying fibroma and cemento-osseous dysplasia, Modified Gallego’s stain proved superior by providing a sharp contrast between bone (green) and cementum (red) [Figures 3 and 6]. In the juvenile ossifying fibromas, MGS distinctly delineated calcification patterns in the trabecular subtype [Figure 4] and highlighted clear red-tinged psammomatoid bodies in the psammomatoid subtype, where H&E showed unclear mineral content [Figure 5]. Quantitative evaluation confirmed distinct visibility profiles. MGS achieved the highest mean clarity index (2.93 ± 0.25, median = 3.0), followed by Alizarin Red (2.80 ± 0.40, median = 3.0), while Von Kossa demonstrated lower visibility (1.80 ± 1.17, median = 2.0) [Table 6]. The global Friedman test demonstrated a statistically significant difference in diagnostic clarity across the stains (P = 0.0003). Post-hoc testing revealed that both Modified Gallego’s and Alizarin Red yielded significantly higher clarity values than Von Kossa (P < 0.01), while no significant structural visibility difference was observed between Modified Gallego’s and Alizarin Red (P = 0.3173) [Table 7].

DISCUSSION

FOLs of the maxillofacial region comprise a diverse group of conditions in which normal bone is replaced by fibrocellular tissue containing varying degrees of mineralized material.[7] Although lesions such as fibrous dysplasia, ossifying fibroma, and cemento-osseous dysplasia share overlapping microscopic features, their clinical behaviour and prognosis differ significantly.[8] Because the mineralized components such as the woven or lamellar bone, cementum-like material, or dystrophic calcifications often appear similar on routine sections, these lesions pose a diagnostic challenge.[7] A clearer understanding of the fibrous and osseous elements is therefore essential for accurate diagnosis and treatment planning.

H&E remain the primary stain used in histopathology and is invaluable for assessing overall tissue architecture.[910] However, H&E often fails to differentiate bone, cementum, and immature osteoid, particularly in mixed or early-stage lesions.[11] This limitation creates diagnostic ambiguity in conditions where mineralized patterns overlap or transition, such as in FD and OF. FD is characterised by metaplastic woven bone, while OF is a true neoplasm containing cementicles and both woven and lamellar bone.[12] Distinguishing these tissues on H&E alone is difficult, leading to potential misinterpretation and underscoring the importance of special stains as adjunctive tools to highlight specific tissue characteristics.[11]

The present study compared the diagnostic performance of H&E, Alizarin Red, Von Kossa, and MGS across different FOLs to determine their value in identifying specific mineralized components and reducing diagnostic uncertainty. Inferential analysis established that significant variations in diagnostic clarity exist among these histochemical modalities. Both Modified Gallego’s and Alizarin Red provided distinct, top-tier visualization advantages over the Von Kossa technique.

Among the stains evaluated, MGS provided the most consistent and reliable differentiation between bone and cementum. Bone exhibited green or greenish-yellow staining, while cementum appeared distinctly red, enabling clear distinction even in complex lesions. This superior differentiation is attributed to the stain’s ability to highlight extracellular matrix differences that are often morphologically indistinct on H&E.[5] These findings support earlier work demonstrating its utility in distinguishing hard tissue components in odontogenic lesions and FOLs.[1314]

Alizarin Red demonstrated strong calcium-binding properties, making it effective in highlighting immature osteoid and early mineralization. This was particularly evident in FD and cemento-osseous dysplasia, where immature bone formation is a key feature.[15] Currently, there is a lack of published literature quantitatively comparing these specific histochemical parameters in FOLs, making this investigation the first to mathematically validate their reliability. Quantitative analysis revealed no significant difference in diagnostic visibility between Alizarin Red and MGS, indicating that both provide excellent clarity for mapping early or poorly mineralized margins. Additionally, Alizarin Red demonstrated perfect inter-observer agreement and acceptable intra-observer reproducibility.

Von Kossa stain effectively highlighted phosphate-rich calcified deposits and proved useful in identifying mature mineralized areas. It was especially informative in cemento-osseous dysplasia and juvenile psammomatoid ossifying fibroma, where dense calcifications are characteristic. These findings reinforce the stain’s established role in visualizing mature mineralized tissue in bone pathology.[16] However, statistical evaluation highlighted performance limitations for this method. Von Kossa recorded a significantly lower and highly variable visibility index compared to the other two special stains. While it demonstrated uniform inter-observer evaluation trends, its internal intra-observer reproducibility was remarkably low. Consequently, while Von Kossa remains an excellent mapping tool for mature phosphate deposits, it lacks the stable clarity required to function as an independent primary diagnostic tool.

While H&E is indispensable for initial tissue assessment, its inability to clearly distinguish bone, cementum, and osteoid contributes to diagnostic uncertainty particularly in small biopsies or lesions with transitional histology.[17] The combined use of Modified Gallego’s, Alizarin Red, and Von Kossa provides additional histochemical clarity and reduces the likelihood of misinterpretation. Few previous studies have compared multiple stains across various FOLs; as such, the present findings add meaningful evidence to guide targeted stain selection.[514] The substantial-to-perfect inter-observer consensus achieved across the evaluated stains mathematically validates these histochemical techniques as highly reproducible components for clinical diagnostic pathology frameworks, successfully mitigating human diagnostic subjectivity.

Strengths of this study include the systematic comparison of four commonly used stains, application across multiple FOLs, and the use of blinded observers to minimise bias. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The limited sample size of eight cases is justified by the relative rarity of these FOLs and the strict inclusion criteria, which required well-preserved archival specimens suitable for all four staining procedures. The relatively small sample size limits statistical generalisability, and variations in fixation and archival tissue quality may have affected staining intensity. Furthermore, it is acknowledged that the specific semi-quantitative scoring system utilized to evaluate staining visibility has not undergone formal prior validation, as it is a modified version of the scale originally proposed by Kunche et al. (2017).[6] The analyses were descriptive and semi-quantitative; incorporating digital morphometric evaluation in future studies would improve objectivity and facilitate quantitative comparisons.

While the current study focused on the utility of special stains under standard light microscopy, polarized microscopy plays a highly valuable, complementary role in the evaluation of FOLs. By assessing collagen fibre orientation and birefringence, polarized light allows pathologists to clearly distinguish between the parallel lamellations of mature bone and the random fibrillar pattern of immature woven bone.[12] Furthermore, it is instrumental in identifying the characteristic ‘quilt’ pattern or radial fringes unique to cementicles.[12] Future studies combining these special histochemical stains with polarized microscopic analysis could further enhance the precise structural mapping of mineralized components in FOLs.

Future directions should include larger multicentre studies with robust statistical design to validate these findings. Integration of digital image analysis may offer more precise quantification of mineralized components, and continued exploration of additional histochemical and immunohistochemical markers may further enhance diagnostic clarity in complex or atypical FOLs.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study highlights the importance of a targeted stain-selection approach in overcoming the diagnostic limitations of routine H&E in FOLs. Incorporating special stains, particularly Modified Gallego’s for clear differentiation between bone and cementum, Alizarin Red for identifying immature osteoid, and Von Kossa for highlighting phosphate-rich mature mineralized deposits, significantly enhances diagnostic accuracy and reduces ambiguity in lesions with overlapping mineralization patterns. This integrated staining strategy improves reproducibility, supports more confident clinical decision-making, and provides clearer interpretation of mineralized tissue components. Future multicentre studies with larger sample sizes, complemented by quantitative digital image analysis, are warranted to validate these results and further standardise histochemical protocols for evaluation of FOLs.

Clinical relevance statement

Special stains, particularly Modified Gallego’s, can significantly improve the histopathological differentiation of bone and cementum in fibro-osseous lesions, reducing diagnostic ambiguity and enhancing accuracy even when clinical and radiological data are detailed but inconclusive.
