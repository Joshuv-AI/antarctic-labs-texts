In Vitro Venous Catheter Material Comparison: Microbial Adhesion in a Thrombotic Blood Flow Model
Authors: Kristen LeRoy, Daniel Donahue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective:There is an ongoing need for intravenous catheters designed to reduce the risk of thrombosis and catheter-related bloodstream infections. The aim of this study was to compare thrombus formation and microbial adhesion on catheters made from hydrophilic biomaterial (HBM), thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU), and fluorinated TPU.Methods:Using the industry-recognized in vitro blood loop and static model, thrombus and microbial adhesion were quantified in the presence of 7 clinically relevant microbes.Results:Thrombotic and microbial adhesion to HBM catheters was significantly reduced in the presence of clinically relevant microbes. Thrombotic adhesion to HBM catheters showed a significant average reduction greater than 96.3% (2-tailed, paired; P ≤ . 03) for all microbes tested compared to TPU catheters. Compared to microbial adhesion to TPU catheters, HBM catheters averaged more than a 4-log reduction (>99.99% reduction) of microbial difference for all 7 microbes tested (2-tailed, paired; P ≤ .003 for all microbes). Reductions in thrombotic and microbial adhesions were independent of clotting factors.Conclusions:Although large clinical trials are needed, these in vitro results build on the growing evidence to support the use of HBM catheters to prevent common complications associated with vascular access devices.

BACKGROUND

Intraluminal thrombotic catheter occlusion and catheter-related bloodstream infections are among the most common complications of vascular access device insertions.1 Central line-associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI) is associated with significant increases in the unadjusted intensive care unit length of stay, hospital length of stay, and total hospital costs.2 More than 300 million intravenous (IV) catheters are inserted in American patients each year.3,4 Therefore, a major goal of catheter manufacturers is to design a device with features aimed at reducing the risk of intraluminal thrombotic catheter occlusion and catheter-related bloodstream infection.

Peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs) are widely used for long-term venous access in hospitalized patients.5 Despite their widespread use, the potential for occlusion, infection, and mechanical failure still exists.5 Estimates of thrombotic catheter occlusion rates are 7.4% to 35%,6-8 and life-threatening CLABSIs are reported in 0.5% to 13% of PICC insertions.9 There is currently no evidence to suggest the use of 1 type of catheter over another10,11; however, most strategies to prevent thrombotic catheter occlusion and CLABSI have targeted provider and device factors such as catheter material.2 With the growing number of catheters on the market, there has been ongoing research comparing the efficacy and safety of these devices in both in vitro and in vivo settings with the aim of making selection easier.

Most IV catheters are constructed of polyurethanes and polytetrafluoroethylene. As soon as the skin and vein are broken and blood comes into contact with these foreign materials, it sets off a biochemical chain reaction attempting to close the wound and encapsulate the foreign material. The encapsulation is the body’s recognition of the foreign material and ultimately leads to thrombus formation within and on the catheter surface.12-15 Thrombus formation attracts various blood cells and platelets, as well as pathogens that were introduced upon insertion of the device. In fact, catheter-related thrombus formation is associated with a high risk of bloodstream infections.6,12,13 Thermoplastic urethane (TPU) catheters are hydrophobic, making them more likely to be thrombogenic.16 To counteract the thrombotic nature of a TPU catheter, manufacturers often add a surface modification, coating, or additive. Results from a recent randomized, controlled clinical trial demonstrated that device failure due to infectious or non-infectious complications was similar for standard polyurethane PICCs, hydrophobic PICCs, and those coated with the antimicrobial chlorhexidine gluconate.11 These findings underscore the need for new materials to reduce the risk of catheter-related complications.

Catheters constructed of hydrophilic biomaterial (HBM) have distinct advantages over TPU catheters because they are a composite of hydrophilic polymers and have a higher wettability compared to TPU.7,17 Previous studies of catheters constructed of HBM have shown that this novel hydrophilic material has an inherently low interfacial tension that enables them to resist thrombosis. In a recent retrospective, comparative study of PICCs of poly(vinyl alcohol)-based hydrogel composite, Bunch et al16 report that these structural properties enable HBM catheters to outperform traditional TPU catheters with respect to thromboresistance.

The aim of the current study was to compare thrombus formation and microbial adhesion on catheters made from HBM, TPU, and fluorinated TPU using an in vitro blood flow model. This benchtop model allows researchers to assess microbial counts and thrombus formation potential of different catheter materials, which may better inform the selection of catheters that minimize infection and thrombus-related risks.

METHODS

The in vitro blood flow loop assays were conducted by an independent laboratory, Thrombodyne MDEC, LLC. The studies were conducted from January 2024 to October 2024.

Four types of sterile catheters were used in the study: HydroPICC (Access Vascular Inc) and HydroMID (Access Vascular Inc), which are both composed of HBM; PowerPICC (Becton Dickinson [BD]), which is composed of thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU), and BioFlo PICC with Endexo and PASV Valve Technology (AngioDynamics), which is composed of fluorinated (fluoro)-TPU. Of note, the composition and manufacturing processes for the HydroPICC and HydroMID catheters are the same; the only difference between them is the length. All catheters met the mechanical strength standards described in ISO 10555, which is a standard that outlines the general requirements for intravascular catheters (devices indicated for single-use medical applications such as administering fluids, medications, or blood draws). Performing thorough testing in accordance with the standard ensures that the devices are not only reliable but also meet performance criteria, which are critical for patient care and safety.

Evaluation of Microbial Adhesion and Thrombus Formation: HBM Catheters vs TPU and Fluoro-TPU Catheters

Study Objective

The purpose of the in vitro assays was to assess each catheter’s ability to deter thrombus accumulation and the adhesion of microbes to catheter surfaces. This model is recognized by the US Food and Drug Administration as the gold standard for simulating blood flow through a catheter under clinically relevant catheter conditions.11,18,19 No control was used, as the objective of the study was a performance comparison between catheter materials. Each catheter type was separately tested in the presence of 3 clinically relevant Gram-negative (Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa [P. aeruginosa]) and 3 Gram-positive (Staphylococcus aureus [S. aureus], Staphylococcus epidermidis, Enterococcus faecalis) bacterial species as well as a fungal species (Candida albicans). Twelve experiment replications were performed in the presence of S. aureus. The American Type Culture Collection catalog numbers for each specimen are listed in Tables 1 and 2. Given that statistical significance was achieved in the S. aureus experiments that included 12 replications, 6 replications were performed in the presence of the other microbes. All catheters were prepared according to manufacturer’s instructions outlined in the package inserts. HBM catheters require a 5-minute hydration period before use, which allows them to absorb water and make the luminal and extraluminal surface more lubricious. In Vitro Blood Loop Assay Protocol

For each experiment replication, fresh bovine blood from a single animal was collected into blood collection bags prefilled with heparin. Autologous platelets were radiolabeled with Technetium-99m (99mTc; [purchased from University of Utah Intermountain Radiopharmacy]), then added to the blood along with the microbial samples. Coagulation happened too quickly when the inoculum was 1 × 106 CFU/mL, so the initial inoculum added to the whole blood samples was adjusted to 1 × 105 CFU/mL. Following inoculation, the catheters were exposed to the flowing blood and microbes for 2 hours, then removed.

Thrombus Quantification

Thrombus formation from the adhered radiolabeled platelets within the lumen of the catheter was measured using a Geiger counter. The radioactive counts (counts per min) were used to quantify thrombus accumulation in the samples. The radioactive counts were relative to each loop; therefore, thrombus accumulation was analyzed on a per-flow loop basis, where the TPU or fluoro-TPU was used as controls of each experiment. The results of each experiment were then averaged and statistically quantified. Laboratory best practices and ethylene oxide sterilization of the finished samples were used to ensure they were sterile. Each sample was cultured to confirm sterility.

Microbial Adhesion Quantification

The biological material from the luminal and external surfaces of the catheter was removed via rinsing and sonication, diluted for colony counting, then plated on the appropriate agar for the microbe. The cultured agar plates were incubated for 24 hours, and microbial adhesion was measured by counting the colony-forming units. The microbial counts were presented as colony-forming units per milliliter (CFU/mL), which is a measure of the viable microbial cells that were adhered/grown during the flow and incubation (static) phases of testing. No additional materials were used as a control. Statistical Analysis

The experiments were repeated for each of the 4 types of catheters to assure the validity of the results and all data were expressed as the means ± standard error. A 2-sided t test statistical analysis was performed on the microbial adhesion data normalized to the logarithmic values using Minitab Statistical Analysis.

RESULTS

Thrombus Accumulation

The HBM significantly reduced thrombotic adhesion in the presence of all 7 microbes used in the in vitro loop assays (Figure 2). When analyzing thrombus accumulation data as a percentage of TPU, the HBM catheters showed a significant average reduction greater than 96.3% (Two-tailed, paired; P ≤ .03) for all microbes tested compared to the TPU catheters (Table 1). When analyzing thrombus accumulation data as a percentage of fluoro-TPU, the HBM catheters showed an average reduction greater than 94.9% for all microbes tested compared to the fluoro-TPU group (Table 1). When analyzing thrombus accumulation data as percentage of TPU, thrombus adhesion to fluoro-TPU catheters varied in the presence of each microbe, ranging from 22.1% to 95.8% (Table 1). Microbial Adhesion

Microbial adhesion to the HBM catheters was reduced when evaluating against competitive catheter devices. The log-change from initial microbial concentration (1 x 105 CFU/mL) to the final microbial surface concentration after incubation is shown in Figure 3. Compared to microbial adhesion to traditional TPU catheters, HBM catheters averaged more than a 4-log reduction (>99.99% reduction) of microbial difference for all 7 microbes tested (2-tailed, paired; P ≤ .003 for all microbes; Table 2). When compared to fluoro-TPU catheters, there was an average 1.7- to 5-log reduction in microbial adhesion to HBM catheters (Table 2). When compared to TPU catheters, fluoro-TPU catheters were less than 1-log average reduction in microbial adhesion for all microbes (Table 2). Some of the fluoro-TPU samples did show a less significant reduction against E. coli and P. aeruginosa; however, the remainder of the fluoro-TPU samples showed growth or no change on the surface, relative to the initial inoculum. In stark contrast, zero or no detectable levels of microbial growth were observed for the majority of HBM catheters (no microbial growth on HBM in 29 of 48 samples, or 60% of the samples studied). Fluoro-TPU had 3 of 48 samples with no observed microbial growth on their surfaces; these 3 samples were solely in the E. coli group. DISCUSSION

In this study, 3 types of catheter materials, HBM, TPU, and fluoro-TPU, were tested for their ability to deter thrombus formation and microbial adhesion. Compared to the catheters constructed of TPU, the HBM catheters were resistant to microbial adhesion as well as thrombus formation. Specifically, microbial growth was detected on nearly all the TPUs and all the fluoro-TPU catheters. The HBM’s more favorable thrombogenicity profile and reduced susceptibility to microbial colonization can be attributed, at least in part, to its unique surface and reduced insertion forces, which have been previously reported.16,20

The results of the current study confirm findings from a similar in vitro study by Mannarino et al.21 Investigators used the blood loop model to measure thrombus accumulation and platelet adhesion on PICCs constructed of HBM. Up to an average of 97% reduction in platelet adhesion was reported for HBM catheters used in the study. These data suggest that, compared to TPU controls, HBM demonstrated no change in thromboresistance performance when exposed to microbial contamination in the in vitro blood loop. Data from in vitro models investigating differences in microbial adhesion among catheters is limited. Pathak et al22 used a percutaneous insertion model to demonstrate that a catheter constructed of a unique copolymer consisting of polyurethane and polycarbonate outperformed commonly used TPU catheters with regards to microbial attachment and biofilm formation. Like HBM catheters, polyurethane catheters are neither coated nor impregnated with an antimicrobial substance. Instead, the novel material makes up the entire catheter body. The current study demonstrates that microbial adhesion on HBM catheters was the lowest among the 4 catheter types tested. There was also a weak trend between microbial growth and thrombus accumulation (Figure 4). The trend toward a parabolic relationship between thrombus and microbial adhesion suggests thrombotic material can accelerate microbial colonization of the surface. When handling freshly collected bovine blood and bacteria cultures in the same flow loop, variability is expected to be high; therefore, this trend is noteworthy. Timsit et al4 reported a similar trend in the clinical setting, where the risk of catheter-related sepsis was 2.62-fold higher when thrombosis occurred (P = .011). The multi-phase blood loop model used in this study incorporates flow-induced thrombosis (with bacteria/fungi present) that forms on the catheter with a static (incubation) phase, which allows the microbes present in the thrombus and on the catheter to replicate. This is followed by a processing phase to remove the microbes present and quantify them. In vitro models, such as this, can provide a valuable assessment of device thrombosis and microbial adhesion/growth in a simulated catheter environment.23 The conditions (eg, blood flow, anticoagulation) in in vitro models are relatively more controlled than in in vivo models.18,19 Extraneous parameters, such as vessel size, animal activity, variable hemostasis and homeostasis, infection, etc, that can confound in vivo assessments, can be eliminated in the in vitro model. This allows the evaluation to be focused on a specific variable (eg, surface chemistry), with other parameters remaining relatively constant. Further, since the devices are subject to the same conditions, such an in vitro model provides a direct comparison of the relative thrombogenicity and microbial adhesion of the devices.

While the substantial reductions in thrombus formation and bacterial adhesion demonstrated by HBM-based catheters suggest a meaningful advancement in vascular access technology, the speed of adoption of a novel device into routine clinical practice can be influenced by a range of economic, operational, and administrative factors. Hospitals and health systems often operate within structured contracting and formulary review cycles that can slow the introduction of new technologies, regardless of clinical merit. Additionally, clinician familiarity with conventional polyurethane-based catheters contributes to a measured pace of change, despite compelling data supporting the benefits of newer materials. However, as health care institutions increasingly prioritize patient safety, outcomes-based procurement, and infection prevention initiatives, there is growing opportunity for advanced technologies, such as HBM, to gain adoption through pilot evaluations, cost-effectiveness analyses, and alignment with value-based care goals. This change is further supported considering the anticipated changes to hospital-onset bacteremia reporting requirements and Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services penalties.

Adding an HBM catheter to the existing clinician toolbox to support a particular patient population at increased risk for catheter-related complications, such as individuals undergoing oncology treatment, immunocompromised patients, or those with a history of catheter failure, may further support early clinical integration and help demonstrate the value of advanced biomaterials in vascular access care.

LIMITATIONS

While results of this study may not be directly extrapolated to all in vivo situations, they serve as 1 indicator of device thrombosis and thromboresistance as well as microbial adhesion/growth. The hemostatic system is inherently less activated in vivo since platelets and clotting proteins contact the endothelium, which mitigates and controls the reactivity of these hemostatic components. In contrast, platelets and clotting proteins are more activated when they come in contact with polymer surfaces in vitro (even in the presence of heparin). Therefore, the test parameters chosen for this in vitro study, while guided by clinical conditions, also take into consideration the aforementioned limitations of the in vitro setting and are selected based on over 30 years of empirical device thrombosis assessment experience by Thrombodyne investigators.

CONCLUSIONS

A reduction in thrombus accumulation with HBM catheters has previously been demonstrated and has been confirmed by the findings of the in vivo model described in this study. Substantial reductions in microbial adhesion were observed. Although large clinical trials are needed to determine whether these findings can be replicated in the clinical setting, these in vitro results build on the growing evidence to support the use of HBM catheters to prevent common complications associated with vascular access devices.
