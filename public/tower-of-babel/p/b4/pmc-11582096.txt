Investigating changes in the premotor cortex-derived frontal-striatal-thalamic subcircuit in attenuated psychosis syndrome
Authors: Naoyuki Katagiri, Hiromi Tagata, Takashi Uchino, Yu Arai, Junichi Saito, Kouhei Kamiya, Masaaki Hori, Masafumi Mizuno, Takahiro Nemoto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Frontal-striatal-thalamic circuit impairment is presumed to underlie schizophrenia. Individuals with attenuated psychosis syndrome (APS) show longitudinal volume reduction of the putamen in the striatum, which has a neural connection with the premotor cortex through the frontal-striatal-thalamic subcircuit. However, comprehensive investigations into the biological changes in the frontal-striatal-thalamic subcircuit originating from the premotor cortex in APS are lacking. We investigated differences in fractional anisotropy (FA) values between the striatum and premotor cortex (ST-PREM) and between the thalamus and premotor cortex (T-PREM) in individuals with APS and healthy controls, using a novel method TractSeg. Our study comprised 36 individuals with APS and 38 healthy controls. There was a significant difference between the control and APS groups in the right T-PREM (odds ratio = 1.76, p = 0.02). Other factors, such as age, sex, other values of FA, and antipsychotic medication, were not associated with differences between groups. However, while FA value reduction of ST-PREM and T-PREM in schizophrenia has been previously reported, in the present study on APS, the alteration of the FA value was limited to T-PREM in APS. This finding suggests that ST-PREM impairment is not predominant in APS but emerges in schizophrenia. Impairment of the neural network originating from the premotor cortex can lead to catatonia and aberrant mirror neuron networks that are presumed to provoke various psychotic symptoms of schizophrenia. Our findings highlight the potential role of changes in a segment of the frontal-thalamic pathway derived from the premotor cortex as a biological basis of APS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11682-024-00906-6.

Introduction

Impairment of the dopaminergic nervous system has been considered to be involved in schizophrenia. Dopamine receptors are abundant in the striatum, which is located between the frontal lobe and thalamus. The frontal lobe plays a critical role in internal representation, while the thalamus receives information from environmental events through almost all the sensory nerves (Alexander et al., 1986; Potvin et al., 2019). Therefore, dopamine receptors in the striatum are considered to mediate the “salience” between environmental events and internal representations (Kapur, 2003; Pankow et al., 2012). Dysfunctions in the frontal-striatal-thalamic circuit can lead to misprocessing of information between environmental events and internal representations, resulting in the inappropriate assignment of salience. Aberrant salience is considered a crucial biological factor underlying self-disorders, a core symptom of schizophrenia (Leube et al., 2008; Shepherd, 2013; Mishara et al., 2016).

Numerous studies have substantiated the “functional” abnormality between the frontal lobe, striatum, and thalamus using fMRI or PET in schizophrenia (Cocchi et al., 2014; Xu et al., 2021). Further, recent research revealed “structural” changes in the frontal lobe-striatum-thalamus circuit. The fractional anisotropy (FA) value derived with diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) is a parameter of white matter fiber bundles integrity, which connect and relay information among brain regions (Kelly et al., 2018). Studies have reported fragmented evidence of reduced FA values between the frontal lobe, striatum, and thalamic subregions in schizophrenia (de Leeuw et al., 2015; Keshavan et al., 2020; Csukly et al., 2021). Notably, the advent of a novel method, TractSeg, has enabled accurate analysis of the FA value of the whole brain, including a part of the frontal-striatal-thalamic subcircuit (Wasserthal et al., 2018). Wasserthal et al. (2020) revealed significant reductions of FA values in the left and right thalamus-premotor cortex, left and right striatum-frontal-orbital cortices, and both striatum-premotor cortices in catatonic individuals with schizophrenia compared to those in healthy controls. Additionally, they noted a significant difference in the FA values of the right striatum-fronto-orbital and left striatum-premotor cortices between non-catatonic individuals and healthy controls. Overall, these findings corroborate the broad range of disruption of white matter fiber bundles in frontal-striatal-thalamic circuit in schizophrenia.

However, it remains unclear whether the pathological changes in the frontal-striatal-thalamic circuit appear before onset of psychosis, including schizophrenia. Few studies have examined whether changes in the frontal-striatal-thalamic circuit arise even in attenuated psychosis syndrome (APS), which exhibits subthreshold psychotic symptoms. Furthermore, the frontal-striatal-thalamic circuit comprises different parallel subcircuits originating from different frontal cortex regions. The biological changes caused by a particular subcircuit and its mechanism of appearance are unclear.

In our previous investigation of the frontal-striatal-thalamic circuit, we focused on structural changes in the subregions of the striatum, i.e., the putamen, caudate, and nucleus accumbens, in individuals with an at-risk mental state (ARMS); we observed a significant reduction in the right putamen volume over a 1-year follow-up period (Katagiri et al., 2019). Notably, most of the participants with ARMS were individuals with APS, suggesting potential biological changes in the putamen in APS. The putamen connects to the premotor cortex via the frontal-striatal-thalamic subcircuit. This subcircuit originates from the premotor cortex and connects with putamen in the striatum and thalamus. The premotor cortex is responsible for planning movements and is a major part of the mirror neuron network (Martin, 2012; Gallese et al., 1996). Biological changes originating from the premotor cortex neural networks are related to catatonia, and aberrant mirror neuron networks that underlie several psychotic symptoms of schizophrenia (Pridmore et al., 2008; Mehta et al., 2014; Peters et al., 2016; Heyes & Catmur, 2022).

To date, no study has investigated the impairment of neural networks originating from the premotor cortex in individuals with APS. Furthermore, comprehensive analyses of structural changes in the frontal-striatal-thalamic subcircuit, presumed to be a biological base of several psychotic symptoms in individuals with APS, are lacking (Waszczuk et al., 2021). Therefore, herein, we hypothesized that biological changes in the frontal-striatal-thalamic subcircuit originate from the premotor cortex, in individuals with APS. To this end, we analyzed FA values between the striatum-premotor cortex (ST-PREM) and thalamus-premotor cortex (T-PREM) using the novel method, TractSeg (Wasserthal et al., 2018). Additionally, we investigated the influence of age, sex, handedness, and antipsychotic medication on these FA values.

Methods

Participants

Participants in this study were individuals with APS, aged 18–40 years, recruited from the Toho University Omori Medical Center. APS diagnosis was established using the Structured Interview for Prodromal Syndrome (SIPS) (in Japanese) (Miller et al., 2003; Kobayashi et al., 2007) during their first consultation. The SIPS includes the Scale of Prodromal Symptoms (SOPS), a 19-item scale designed to define and diagnose the psychosis prodrome and to characterize the prodromal symptoms and their severity. The SOPS contains four subscales for positive, negative, disorganization, and general symptoms. The dosage of antipsychotics prescribed for individuals with APS was expressed as milligram equivalents of chlorpromazine (Leucht et al., 2015). Healthy controls were recruited from among students at a neighboring university, their relatives and acquaintances, and independent sources in the community. Healthy controls were identified using the Structured Clinical Interview for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (SCID) Non-Patient Edition (in Japanese) (First et al., 1997; Takahashi et al., 2010). To determine the handedness, we used the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory (Oldfield, 1971) in both control and APS groups. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants after the study was fully explained. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Toho University (Approval No: A19078 and A22045).

Image acquisition

Individuals with APS and controls underwent MRI. The MRI data were obtained using a 1.5-T scanner (Signa HDxt; GE Medical Systems, Waukesha, WI, USA) with a single-shot, spin-echo echo-planar imaging sequence. Diffusion MRI data were acquired using the following parameters: “b” was set at 1,000 s/mm2 along 30 noncollinear directions; a single b value of 0 s/mm2; echo time (TE) = 77 ms; repetition time (TR) = 13,000 ms; a 3 mm3 isotropic voxel; field of view (FOV) = 240 × 240 mm; and 60 slices.

Image processing

Raw images were initially denoised and corrected for Gibbs ringing, per the methods outlined by Veraart et al. (2016) and Kellner et al. (2016). Next, correction for eddy current and motion was performed using the eddy tool in the FMRIB Software Library (FSL Version 6.0.4) (Andersson & Sotiropoulos, 2016). Finally, the images were minimally smoothed with a Gaussian kernel with sigma value of 1 mm to suppress the effects of residual noise and Gibbs artifacts (Maximov et al., 2019). The DTI FA map was computed using standard weighted least square fit implemented in MRTrix3, as described by Veraart et al. (2013). Segmentation of the white matter tracts was performed using TractSeg, which allows semi-automatic reconstruction of the fiber bundles in subjects’ native space (Wasserthal et al., 2018). For the purpose of this study, we focused on the T-PREM and ST-PREM (Fig. 1).

Statistical analyses

All datasets were analyzed using SPSS version 28 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). First, we analyzed differences between the total group of healthy controls and the total group of individuals with APS. A t-test was used to examine differences in age and FA values in the bilateral ST-PREM and T-PREM, and the chi-squared test was used to investigate the differences in sex between total control and total APS groups. Further, considering the influence of age and sex (Schwehm et al., 2016), a logistic regression model was used to analyze the adjusted odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for the risk of the total APS group associated with FA values of bilateral ST-PREM and T-PREM, with adjustments for potential confounding factors such as age and sex. Additionally, we investigated the correlation of FA values in the bilateral ST-PREM and T-PREM with SOPS scores in total APS group.

Second, to rule out the influence of handedness and antipsychotic medication, we further analyzed the differences between the right-handed healthy control group (control-R) and right-handed and non-medicated APS group (APS-R-N). Similar to the first step, a t-test was used to investigate differences in age and FA values in bilateral ST-PREM and T-PREM, and the chi-squared test was used to examine the differences in sex between the control-R and APS-R-N groups. Subsequently, a logistic regression model was used to analyze the adjusted OR with 95% CI for the risk of APS-R-N associated with FA values of bilateral ST-PREM and T-PREM, with adjustments for potential confounding factors such as age and sex.

As FA values range from 0 to 1, the FA value for each region of the bilateral ST-PREM and T-PREM was multiplied by 102 to calculate the OR. Multicollinearity was checked using the tolerance test and variance inflation factor (VIF). A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Further, to elucidate the pathological changes in neural fiber bundles in ST-PREM and T-PREM, differences in mean diffusivity (MD), axial diffusivity (AD), and radial diffusivity (RD) values derived from the DTI of bilateral ST-PREM and T-PREM were compared between the control-R and APS-R-N groups using t-test. Moreover, to assess the influence of antipsychotic medication on ST-PREM and T-PREM, the correlation between FA values in bilateral ST-PREM and T-PREM and antipsychotic dose at the time of the DTI scan was analyzed in the right-handed medicated APS (APS-R-M) group. The antipsychotic dosage was log10 transformed to reduce skewness (CPZ eq-mean-log) (Inada and Takagi, 2015).

Discussion

Dysfunction of the frontal-striatal-thalamic circuit is considered a pathogenesis of APS as well as schizophrenia (Mishara et al., 2016; Pantelis et al., 1997; Dandash et al., 2017; Howes et al., 2009a, b; Steullet et al., 2020). We previously reported a reduction in the longitudinal volume of the right putamen in the striatum in individuals with ARMS, most of whom were individuals with APS (Katagiri et al., 2019). As the putamen connects to the premotor cortex through frontal-striatal-thalamic subcircuit originating from the premotor cortex, we hypothesized that biological changes in the frontal-striatal-thalamic subcircuit derived from the premotor cortex in individuals with APS. In the current study, we employed a novel method, TractSeg, to analyse the DTI values between ST-PREM and T-PREM and examined the effects of age, sex, handedness, and the use of antipsychotic medication on these DTI values.

FA value changes in the frontal-striatal-thalamic circuit originate from the premotor cortex in APS

In the first step, logistic regression analysis with adjustments for potential confounding factors, such as age and sex, revealed a significantly lower FA value in the right T-PREM in the total APS group than that in the total control group. Antipsychotics are dopamine antagonists, and dopamine receptors are abundant in the striatum. Thus, antipsychotics may influence the ST-PREM and T-PREM via the frontal-striatal-thalamic subcircuit, which includes the striatum. Therefore, in the second step, we ruled out the influence of handedness and antipsychotic medication. Although the numbers of participants in Control-R and APS-R-N groups were relatively low, there were no significant differences in terms of sex and age between the groups. The logistic regression analysis revealed a significantly lower FA values in the right T-PREM in the APS-R-N group than in the Control-R group, consistent with results of the first step. Additionally, FA values in the bilateral ST-PREM and T-PREM were not significantly correlated with antipsychotic dose in the APS-R-M group. These findings suggest that there are biological changes in white matter fiber bundle, which connects and relays information among brain regions, between the right premotor cortex and thalamus in individuals with APS, regardless of sex, age, handedness, and medication. Further, focusing on the right T-PREM, MD (p = 0.01) and RD (p < 0.01) values were increased, but AD value (p = 0.79) was unchanged. The increased MD and RD values and the unchanged AD are thought to indicate demyelination of neural fiber bundles (Feldman et al., 2010). Importantly, the results support a possibility of demyelination of the right T-PREM in APS-R-N individuals. However, multiple comparisons using whole t-test with Bonferroni correction showed that the differences in RD and MD values in the right T-PREM were not significant (Supplemental Table 4). More cases need to be collected to clarify whether demyelination of neural fiber bundles occur in the right T-PREM in APS.

Lesions of the premotor cortex cause apraxia (Martin, 2012), and dysfunction of the premotor cortex is supposed to underlie catatonia which is observed in several psychiatric diseases, including schizophrenia (Northoff, 2002; Walther et al., 2019). Additionally, the premotor cortex is a major part of the mirror neuron network (Gallese et al., 1996; Burns, 2006), and an aberrant mirror neuron network is related to affective echo-phenomena symptoms, ego-boundary disturbances, negative symptoms, social cognition impairments, and catatonia (Pridmore et al., 2008; Mehta et al., 2014). Recent studies suggest that pathological changes in the premotor cortex, the lowest layer of the lateral frontal cortex, underlie impairment of a broad range of the lateral frontal cortex, including the prefrontal cortex, which is in the upper layer (Abe & Hanakawa, 2009; Koechlin & Summerfield, 2007; Yeo et al., 2011; Fine & Hayden, 2022). While many studies have corroborated that the impairment in the premotor cortex relates to schizophrenia, our result, that the impairment of neural network originating from the premotor cortex underlies APS provides a new perspective.

Reduction of the FA value of the T-PREM in APS

Few studies have investigated whether impairments in frontal-thalamus and frontal-striatum emerge simultaneously and within the same subcircuit in APS (Cho et al., 2016). In the present study, we comprehensively and simultaneously investigated the structural changes in the frontal-striatal-thalamic subcircuit that originate from the premotor cortex in individuals with APS. Wasserthal et al. (2020) reported FA value reduction in both ST-PREM and T-PREM using TractSeg in individuals with schizophrenia. However, in the present study, the alteration in the FA value was limited to T-PREM in APS, suggesting that ST-PREM impairment is not predominant in APS but emerges in schizophrenia (Walther et al., 2019). It has been long hypothesized that impairment of the thalamus-frontal cortex could secondly provoke an adverse hyperdopaminergic state in the striatum, resulting in the onset of schizophrenia (Davis et al., 1991; Steullet, 2020; Dorph-Petersen & Lewis, 2017; Meyer-Lindenberg et al., 2002; Shin et al., 2022). Therefore, it is possible that biological changes in the T-PREM rather than in the ST-PREM may be related to the emergence of subthreshold psychotic symptoms in APS.

Pathophysiology of aberrant frontal-striatal-thalamic circuit

The longitudinal volume reduction of the right putamen observed in the study by Katagiri et al. (2019), along with the reduction of the FA value of the right T-PREM, suggests a biological change in the right side of a subcircuit of the frontal-striatal-thalamic circuit. This subcircuit, originating from the premotor cortex, is referred to as the “skeletomotor loop.” Previous animal studies have suggested that these loops not only control the motor movement of skeletal muscles but also integrates voluntary motor information with reward information via dopaminergic and other signals (Isomura et al., 2013). However, most previous studies on frontal-striatal-thalamic subcircuits were conducted on animals (Hunnicutt et al., 2016; Haber et al., 2009). Human studies have revealed a more complex and numerous sets of frontal-striatal-thalamic subcircuits, which contain features of multiple conventional subcircuits rather than corresponding precisely to just one (Cocchi et al., 2014; Draganski et al., 2008).

More recent evidence suggests that impairments relate to frontal-striatal-thalamic circuits underlie several psychiatric disorders, such as depression, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, substance misuse, as well as schizophrenia (Peters et al., 2016; Hong, 2023). Meanwhile, an increasing number of epidemiological studies have shown that APS, previously referred to as the “schizophrenia prodrome,” does not necessarily progress to schizophrenia exclusively, but to other several psychiatric disorders, such as depressive disorder, personality disorder. This has led to the proposal of the concept of transdiagnostic psychiatry (McGorry et al., 2018). Altogether, these findings raise the possibility that disruption of the frontal-striatal-thalamic circuit may be one of the biological bases of APS which then transitions to various diseases. Further follow-up studies, considering transdiagnostic psychiatry, are needed to clarify differences in the changes in the frontal-striatal-thalamic circuits during the transition from APS to various psychiatric disorders.

Limitations

First, the sample size was small, limiting the generalizability of our findings. Second, the present study focused on difference in ST-PREM and T-PREM between the APS and control groups, and did not compare DTI values of the whole brain. Therefore, the results of this study represent only one of the whole-brain white matter changes that occur in the APS group. Third, 1.5 T MRI and 30 directions limit precise analysis of nerve fiber bundles in the brain. Findings that would have been observed with higher resolution imaging may have been missed. Finally, antipsychotic medication may have some effects on the PREM, thalamus, and especially the striatum. As the antipsychotics dosage at the time of DTI scan is different from the cumulative dose of antipsychotics, this may limit our understanding of the influence of antipsychotics. The effects of duration, dose, and type of antipsychotic medication on DTI levels should be analyzed in larger studies involving a higher number of APS subjects taking antipsychotic medications.

Conclusion

Frontal-striatal-thalamic circuit impairment is presumed to be a biological base of several psychotic symptoms, including self-disorders, a core symptom of schizophrenia. In this study, we demonstrated a significant reduction in FA values in the right T-PREM, which can be considered to be part of the frontal-striatal-thalamic circuit derived from the premotor cortex in individuals with APS, compared with healthy controls. Our results indicate that the reduction of neural connection between the premotor cortex and thalamus in individuals with APS could contribute to the emergence of subthreshold psychotic symptoms.
