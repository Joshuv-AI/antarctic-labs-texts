Assessing quality and reliability of online videos on tachycardia: a YouTube video-based study
Authors: Zexi Li, Chunyi Yan, Xiaojun Lyu, Fanghui Li, Rui Zeng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundConsidering the adverse clinical consequences of pathologic tachycardia and the potential anxiety caused by physiological tachycardia in some heathy individuals, it is imperative to disseminate health information related to tachycardia for promotion in early diagnosis and appropriate management. YouTube has been increasingly used to access health care information. The aim of this study is to assess the quality and reliability of English YouTube videos focusing on tachycardia and further delve into strategies to enhance the quality of online health resources.MethodsWe conducted a search using the specific key words “tachycardia” in YouTube online library on December 2, 2023. The first 150 videos, ranked by “relevance”, were initially recorded. After exclusions, a total of 113 videos were included. All videos were extracted for characteristics and categorized based on different topics, sources or contents. Two independent raters assessed the videos using Journal of American Medical Association (JAMA) benchmark criteria, Modified DISCERN (mDISCERN) tool, Global Quality Scale (GQS) and Tachycardia-Specific Scale (TSS), followed by statistical analyses. All continuous data in the study were presented as median (interquartile range).ResultsThe videos had a median JAMA score of 2.00 (1.00), mDISCERN of 3.00 (1.00), GQS of 2.00 (1.00), and TSS of 6.00 (4.50). There were significant differences in JAMA (P < 0.001), mDISCERN (P = 0.004), GQS (P = 0.001) and TSS (P < 0.001) scores among different sources. mDISCERN (P = 0.002), GQS (P < 0.001) and TSS (P = 0.030) scores significantly differed among various contents. No significant differences were observed in any of the scores among video topics. Spearman correlation analysis revealed that VPI exhibited significant correlations with quality and reliability. Multiple linear regression analysis suggested that longer video duration, sources of academics and healthcare professionals were independent predictors of higher reliability and quality, while content of ECG-specific information was an independent predictor of lower quality.ConclusionsThe reliability and educational quality of current tachycardia-related videos on YouTube are low. Longer video duration, sources of academics and healthcare professionals were closely associated with higher video reliability and quality. Improving the quality of internet medical information and optimizing online patient education necessitates collaborative efforts.

Background

Tachycardia, a widespread and clinically significant cardiac rhythm disorder, is marked by an excessively rapid heart rate, a critical determinant influencing both myocardial metabolism and cardiac output [1, 2]. Tachycardia can be broadly classified into two main categories: supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) and ventricular tachycardias (VT). While most individuals experience physiological tachycardia during activities such as exercise or in response to anxiety [3, 4], a significant portion of the population still grapples with pathological forms, which necessitate medical attention.

Considering the potential for a prolonged course of pathologic tachycardia to lead to irreversible detrimental outcomes, early diagnosis and appropriate management become crucial for the patient’s health. Additionally, individuals experiencing physiological tachycardia may harbor concerns about potential illness, leading to anxiety. The existing information asymmetry between healthcare professionals and the general public disrupts individuals’ awareness of their conditions, which may also aggravate doctor-patient conflicts [5]. Therefore, it is imperative to deliver precise health education about tachycardia to the public, addressing both information and the importance of seeking medical advice for accurate diagnosis and management.

The proliferation of the internet and the exponential surge in digital information have led to a growing number of individuals opting to consult and search for disease-related information online [6, 7]. YouTube™, ranking as the second most popular search engine on the Internet, trailing only behind Google [8], boasts a remarkable monthly visit count exceeding 22.8 billion [9] and yields videos about specific medical information and tutorials that contain potential applicability for patient education and even management [10]. Thus, YouTube shows great potential to serve as a valuable medium for disseminating health-related information to the general public [11]. However, these videos from YouTube are not subject to any measures of quality control or peer-review processes, leading to uneven quality in the information presented [12], particularly within the medical field. Consequently there remains a significant risk of acquiring misleading or inadequate information pertaining to health-related issues [11, 13], potentially fostering erroneous perceptions in patients and contributing to inappropriate self-assessment of their conditions. Hitherto, to the best of our knowledge, no research has appraised the educational quality and reliability of tachycardia-related information available on YouTube. This study seeks to fill this gap by evaluating the quality of English YouTube videos focusing on tachycardia. Additionally, it intends to analyze relevant influencing factors, delving further into strategies to enhance the quality of online health resources.

Methods

Ethical considerations and information consent

As videos on YouTube were all publicly accessible and the study did not involve any direct participation of humans or animals, obtaining ethics committee approval and information consent was not required.

Search strategy and video characteristics

Before conducting the search, we used The Google Trends, a website offering information of trends in search behaviors on Google, to identify the most frequently used term about tachycardia information. The terms including tachycardia, supraventricular tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia, high heart rate, fast heart beat were used in our search and turned up “tachycardia” and “high heart rate” to be the most commonly used terms (Fig. 1). Accordingly, we searched using specific key words “tachycardia” and “high heart rate” in YouTube online library (https://www.youtube.com) on December 2, 2023. Moreover, owing to a lack of medical knowledge, the general public is unlikely to search terms like “supraventricular tachycardia” or “ventricular tachycardia” directly when experiencing a rapid heartbeat. To avoid potential influence on the results, we created an exclusive account and eliminated browser cache and search history, also turning off data recording. The search results were displayed by the sorting of “relevance”(YouTube’s default settings) and the first 75 videos of each string were preliminarily recorded. Based on the result of Google Trends, this search strategy could simulate users’ search habits to the greatest extent and has been applied in previous studies [8]. We initially screened video titles and descriptions and those related to tachycardia were included for further screening. Exclusion criteria comprised the following terms: (1) duplicates, (2) irrelevance to tachycardia, (3) non-English language, (4) audio-only or vision-only video, (5) unacceptable audio/visual quality. Ultimately, 113 videos were retained for further analysis (Fig. 2).

The retained videos were viewed in their entirety, and we extracted the basic data including: (1) title and URL, (2) uploader, (3) upload date, (4) time since uploaded, (5) video duration, and characteristic data including (1) view count, (2) view ratio (view per day), (3) like and dislike count, (4) like ratio (likes/(likes + dislikes)), (5) comment count, (6) uploader verification, (7) country/region of origin, (8) host channel average daily view count, (9) host channel subscriber count, (10) total view count of host channel, (11) channel video power index (VPI). The VPI calculation formula is as follows: view ratio × like ratio/100. VPI serves as an index applied to quantify video popularity and has been reported in previous studies [14–16]. Some indices were collected through reliable third-party browser plugins [17, 18].

Categorization of video content and source

According to the topic that videos introduced, the videos were classified into three groups: (1) Group TC (general introduction to tachycardia), (2) Group SVT (content focused on supraventricular tachycardia), (3) Group VT (content focused on ventricular tachycardia). Based on the content, the videos were also classified into the following categories: (1) disease-specific information (2) Patient experience, (3) surgical management, (4) non-surgical management, (5) advertisement, (6) ECG (electrocardiogram) -specific information. Each video corresponded to only one topic. If a video contained multiple content themes, the content covering the largest proportion or which offered the most beneficial information to viewers was determined.

On the basis of previous studies [19, 20] and our actual searching results, all videos were divided into the following categories according to the their source: (1) academic (uploaders affiliated with a research group or university/college), (2) healthcare professionals (physician, nurse etc.), (3) nonphysician (health specialists other than licensed physicians), (4) medical source (e.g. health-related website), (5) TV program.

Assessment of video reliability and quality

Three general scoring scales were incorporated to assess the retrieved videos: 1): Journal of American Medical Association (JAMA) benchmark criteria (Table 1), proposed by Silberg et al. [21], was applied to assess the video source reliability by four core standards, including authorship, attribution, disclosure, and currency. JAMA criteria is a binary (yes or no) rating table, on a scale from 0 (poor reliability) to 4 (highest level of reliability). 2): Modified DISCERN (mDISCERN) criteria (Table 2), originally formulated by Charnock et al. [22] and refined by singh et al. [23], was used to verify the video reliability from an alternative perspective. The mDISCERN criteria is a binary yes/no rating table, including five questions, primarily evaluate whether the video’s purpose and informational content are clear. 3): Global Quality Scale (GSQ) criteria [24] was utilized to assess the richness and validity of video information (Table 3). GQS constitutes a scoring scale spanning from 1 to 5, where elevated grades signify superior quality.

While the scoring criteria above only offer a general evaluation of video quality, there are no specialized tools available for assessing the educational content pertaining to tachycardia. Thus drawing upon clinical expertise and previously published studies, the Tachycardia-Specific Scale (TSS) (Table 4), a 15-item assessment designed to appraise aspects including (1) patient presentation, (2) introduction to tachycardia, (3) examinations and diagnosis of tachycardia, (4) treatment measures (5) outcome with or without treatment, was created. A higher score, with a maximum of 15 points, signifies that the video delivers exemplary educational content on tachycardia. Conversely, a lower score suggests limited informational value. While TSS has not been conclusively validated, the feasibility of employing scales with a similar structure, as demonstrated in previous studies [25, 26] using other specific-disease scales, has been confirmed.

All the included videos were assessed by two independent cardiologists using the scoring systems above, and this process was repeated after a two-week interval. The initial results assessed by the two doctors were documented independently. In cases of any discrepancies, a third reviewer intervened to reconcile differences and arrive at a unanimous result.

Statistical analysis

The study utilized descriptive statistics to quantify video characteristics, reliability and educational quality. We applied multiple imputation techniques for missing data. Due to the non-normal distribution of data, all continuous variables were presented as median values with interquartile ranges. All qualitative data were represented as proportions. Differences between groups, categorized according to video topics, sources, or contents, were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, with Bonferroni correction applied for post-hoc analysis. We explored correlation among VPI, JAMA, mDISCERN, GQS, and TSS scores using Spearman’s correlation analysis. To identify independent predictors influencing these indices, multiple linear regression analysis was conducted. This analysis incorporated variables which demonstrated a univariate relationship (P < 0.20) with the target index or those deemed relevant from prior research. The selection of variables was carefully done to ensure the number of events was sufficient to maintain a parsimonious regression model [27].

We assessed the Intraobserver and interobserver consistency for the four scoring scale (JAMA, mDISCERN, GQS, TSS) through the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). According to established guidelines, ICC values were interpreted as follows: excellent (> 0.90), good (0.75–0.90), moderate (0.50–0.75), and poor (< 0.50) [28].

All statistical analyses were executed with IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 software. All tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was determined at a P value of less than 0.05.

Results

Video baseline characteristics

Out of the 150 initially screened videos, 113 were eligible and included in the study. The earliest video was uploaded on March 24, 2010. Over time, the yearly distribution of included videos showed a general increase, with more than half (62.83%, 71/113) released between 2019 and 2023 (Fig. 3). Excluding videos with unknown original country, the top three countries contributing videos most were USA (65), India (12) and UK (10) (Fig. 4). Upon analyzing all videos, the median value of time since uploaded, video duration and view count of each video were 1487.00 (1820.00) days, 225.00 (430.00) seconds and 22,008.50 (91,361.00), respectively. Every video received the median likes and dislikes count of 295.00 (1188.50) and 7.00 (24.00) with the median comments of 32.00 (105.00). The median view ratio, like ratio and VPI were using established formulas, resulting in values of 16.52 (61.95), 98.25 (4.17) and 16.04 (60.24), respectively. 29 videos were published by officially verified uploaders. Every video host channel has the median Total view count of 12,866,105 (51,867,284), the median subscriber count of 72,200.00 (452,950.00), the median average daily view count of 13,900.00 (45,900.00) (Tables 5 and 6).

Video sources and contents

All the videos were sorted into five sources, with the majority originating from medical source (34/113, 30%), healthcare professionals (28/113, 25%), academic (27/113, 24%), and only a minor proportion originating from nonphysician (19/113, 17%) and TV program (5/113, 4%) (Fig. 5). There were significant differences in like count (P = 0.049) among different video uploading sources (Table 5). Post hoc tests revealed that videos from healthcare professional(s) received significantly (P = 0.029) more likes than academic source. Among five categories based on video content, disease-specific information was the most frequently covered (57/113, 50%), followed by ECG-specific information (27/113, 24%), non-surgical management (16/113, 14%), patient experience (6/113, 5%), surgical management (4/113, 4%) and advertisement (3/113, 3%) (Fig. 6). Among the three groups based on specific topics, Group SVT (49/113, 43%) accounted for the largest proportion, followed by Group VT (36/113, 32%) and Group TC (28/113, 25%) (Fig. 7).

Video reliability and educational quality

The median values for JAMA, mDISCERN, GQS and TSS scores of all videos were 2.00 (1.00), 3.00 (1.00), 2.00 (1.00) and 6.00 (4.50), respectively (Table 6). The Intraobserver reliability within each rater was excellent for all four scales. Two raters achieved excellent agreement in JAMA scores (ICC: 0.915, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.878–0.940), mDISCERN scores (ICC: 0.918, 95% CI: 0.884–0.943), GQS scores (ICC: 0.925, 95% CI: 0.893–0.948), TSS scores (ICC: 0.935, 95% CI: 0.907–0.954).

There were significant differences in JAMA (P < 0.001), mDISCERN (P = 0.004), GQS (P = 0.001) and TSS (P < 0.001) scores among different video uploading sources. Videos from academic source showed significant (P < 0.05) higher scores in mDISCERN than nonphysician and medical source. videos from academics and healthcare professionals both exhibited significantly (P < 0.05) higher scores in JAMA than TV programs as well as in GQS and TSS than nonphysician source. videos from healthcare professionals showed significant (P < 0.05) higher scores in JAMA and TSS than medical source. Meanwhile, mDISCERN (P = 0.002), GQS (P < 0.001) and TSS (P = 0.030) scores significantly varied among videos of various contents. Videos focusing on ECG-specific information had lower GQS and TSS scores than those covering disease-specific information, as well as lower GQS scores than those featuring non-surgical management, with statistical significance (P < 0.05). The content of patient experience led to significantly (P < 0.05) lower scores in mDISCERN compared to any other contents, except for advertisement (P > 0.05). There were no significant differences in VPI among different video topics, uploading sources, and contents. Additionally, no significant differences were observed in any of the scores among video topics. More detailed analytical results are displayed in Table 6.

Factors affecting video popularity, reliability and educational quality

Spearman correlation analysis revealed positive and significant correlations among every pair of JAMA, mDISCERN, GQS, and TSS scores (P < 0.001 for each pair). Additionally, these scores exhibited significant correlations with VPI (P < 0.05) (Table 7).

Multiple linear regression analysis showed that a higher VPI was correlated with a higher comment count (P < 0.001), longer video duration (P = 0.020), a higher host channel subscriber count (P = 0.040) and a verified uploader (P = 0.001). In comparison to the disease-specific information, content about surgical management emerged as an independent predictor for lower VPI (P = 0.017); A higher JAMA score was associated with a lower comment count (P = 0.008), greater view ratio (P = 0.011), The videos from academics (P = 0.015) and healthcare professionals (P = 0.005) were correlated to higher JAMA scores than medical source; A higher mDISCERN score was in correlation with a lower comment count (P = 0.001), longer video duration (P = 0.032), greater like ratio (P = 0.027). Compared to medical source and disease-specific information, the academic source (P < 0.001) were associated with higher mDISCERN scores, while content about patient experience (P < 0.001) and advertisement (P = 0.013) were associated to lower mDISCERN scores; A higher GQS score was closely related to longer video duration (P = 0.002). The source of nonphysician (P = 0.032) was an independent predictor of lower GQS scores compared to medical source, and the content about Non-surgical management (P = 0.034) and ECG-specific information (P < 0.001) were related to higher and lower GQS scores respectively than the CS-specific information. Group SVT (P = 0.015) was an independent predictor of high GQS scores compared to Group TC; A higher TSS score was in correlation with longer video duration (P < 0.001). In comparison to the medical source, both academic (P = 0.018) and healthcare professionals (P = 0.034) sources were associated with higher TSS scores, while the nonphysician source (P = 0.014) was associated with lower TSS scores. Additionally, content of ECG-specific information (P = 0.003) was an independent predictor of lower TSS scores compared to disease-specific information. Discussion

Tachycardia, a prevalent issue in clinical practice, may arise from physiological and/or pathological origins. Considering the potential hazards associated with pathological tachycardia, it becomes imperative to promote early detection and rhythm management [1]. Additionally, owing to the information asymmetry between healthcare providers and general public, healthy individuals experiencing physiological tachycardia may become anxious due to concerns about having a serious condition [29]. A study indicates that approximately 70–80% of netizens apply the internet to obtain health information, shaping their initial perceptions [30]. YouTube is recognized globally as the largest video-share platform [8] and contains a significant volume of medical-related content, which serves as an excellent means for medical knowledge dissemination. However, due to the deficiencies of patients in assessing the accuracy of information, this could potentially lead to various issues in the patient‒doctor relationship [31, 32], thereby reducing patient compliance.

Through quantitative scoring methods including JAMA, GQS, mDISCRN, TSS scores and other characteristic indexes, we evaluated videos from three perspectives, including reliability, educational quality and popularity. The current results reveal that the reliability and educational quality of tachycardia-related videos on YouTube are unsatisfactory. This suggests a relatively high risk of encountering incomplete, inaccurate, or potentially misleading information for netizens seeking information about tachycardia on YouTube. Our finding is in consistent with some previous research in the field of public health. Ahmet Oztermeli et al. revealed the poor quality of videos covering developmental dysplasia of the hip (DHH) on YouTube, reporting mean JAMA, GQS and DDH scores of 1.37, 2.46, 4.63, respectively [33]. Similarly, Vincent Wu et al. concluded the quality of information for Functional Endoscopic Sinus Surgery (FESS) remains poor on YouTube, with the mean mDISCERN, JAMA and FESS scores of 1.91, 1.91, 3.54, respectively [34]. In contrast, certain studies have suggested a more positive attitude towards medical videos on YouTube. Tolga Memioglu et al. analyzed 50 videos about myocarditis during the COVID‑19 pandemic and found moderate reliability and quality, with mean mDISCERN and GQS scores of 3.88, 3.63, respectively [14]. Shubham Holge et al. concluded that the YouTube videos regarding myocardial infarction exhibit high-quality content, as evidenced by a higher average GQS score of 3.6 [16]. The discrepancies in these conclusions may be attributed to various factors, such as differences in source proportions, studied fields, and video characteristics. Shubham Holge et al. employed specific inclusion criteria, requiring the video length to be between 2 and 20 min when analyzing YouTube videos regarding myocardial infarction [16]. This criterion could effectively exclude excessively long or short videos which are more likely to be of low quality.

While the overall reliability and educational quality of YouTube videos were low, videos from academics and healthcare professionals demonstrated significantly higher levels than those from other sources. The source of academics and healthcare professionals could also be considered as an independent predictor of high reliability and quality. This finding is consistent with several previous studies. Selçuk Yildiz et al. found that videos on Vestibular Rehabilitation sourced from universities or occupational organizations had statistically significantly higher mDISCERN criteria scores, modified JAMA scores, and GQS values (4.25, 4 and 5 respectively, P < 0.001) [35]. Additionally, Tugba Ozsoy-Unubol et al. found that videos on fibromyalgia uploaded by physicians had the significantly higher DISCERN scores (56, P < 0.001) compared to other sources [36]. Similar results were also reported in some other studies [10, 20, 37, 38]. Nonetheless, due to lack of regulation and the absence of an access and censorship system, it is easy for unqualified individuals to publish videos on medical topics, potentially disseminating unprofessional and unsubstantiated information [25, 39]. Uploaders of medical source and nonphysician may be uncertified and lack sufficient medical knowledge, providing opportunities to present misleading information. The source of nonphysician could also be considered as an independent predictor of low quality. Furthermore, the prevalence of misinformation on YouTube underscores the need for more stringent oversight and regulation within the medical content domain.

Furthermore, video duration showed a significant positive regression relationship between mDISCERN, GQS, and TSS respectively in our study, which demonstrated the crucial role of video duration in influencing the reliability and quality of content. Longer video duration offers creators the opportunity to delve into intricate facets of a topic, present detailed analyses, and provide viewers a more profound educational experience. This observation aligns with findings from other research studies [36, 40, 41]. In our study, the median duration of all included videos was 225.00 (430.00) seconds, which is shorter than the median duration of 261 s reported in Caitlin Hsuen Ng et al.’s study on systemic lupus erythematosus [42] and the mean duration of 21.9 min reported in Cagri Unal-Ulutatar et al.‘s study on systemic sclerosis [43], where they found the majority of videos being reliable and of high quality. Moreover, Caitlin Hsuen Ng et al. observed that the videos in the useful groups were significantly longer than those in misleading video groups [43]. In the contemporary video industry, the recent emergence of fast-food culture (FFC) leads creators to craft concise, fast-paced video offerings or split videos into series, limiting the duration to less than 10 min to cater viewers’ preference [44]. The FFC trend, while catering to viewers’ preferences for shorter content, may inadvertently compromise the delivery of comprehensive information. In our study, 81.42% (92/113) of videos were within a 10-minute timeframe. However, despite the increasing number of producers catering to the trend of FFC, we still found that a higher VPI was associated with longer video duration, which contradicted the original intention of FFC to attract more viewers. We infer that when choosing health education sources, users may also be inclined to watch videos with more comprehensive information to a certain extent.

The video topics were more evenly distributed, encompassing the TC, SVT, and VT groups. There was no significant difference observed in terms of reliability, quality, and popularity among these diverse topics. However, concerning specific content, the included videos exhibited a considerable homogeneity, with approximately half (50%) of the videos discussed disease-specific information. The lower reliability observed in videos about patient experience may be attributed to the low objectivity of such content and the absence of reliable, balanced and unbiased sources of medical information. To address this issue, encouraging academic affiliations, healthcare professionals and reputable medical organizations to contribute content on patient experiences could improve reliability with their expertise and credibility. The lower quality of ECG-specific information may be attributed to excessive specialization and a lack of sufficient coverage of other relevant aspects. Similarly, Taylan Akgun et al. concluded that YouTube was an inadequate source of information for ECG after analyzing 119 YouTube videos [45]. Additionally, videos about ECG, possibly crafted for healthcare professionals, tended to be overly professional and technical, making it difficult for the general public to understand. Despite this, the accessibility of these videos remains high, posing a significant challenge as patients frequently encounter and view these videos without the ability to pre-assess their specialized content. Therefore, institutions and professionals are needed to produce more engaging and higher quality videos suitable for patient education.

In the present study, the median VPI was calculated as 16.04. No significant differences were observed in VPI among different video topics, sources, and contents. However, videos produced by healthcare professional(s) gained significantly more likes than those from academic source. This discrepancy may arise because the VPI incorporated a broader range of metrics, thereby providing a more comprehensive reflection of video popularity than mere like count alone. Spearman correlation analysis revealed a significant positive relationship between VPI and reliability or quality. In the study conducted by Yurdaisik et al. including YouTube videos related to breast cancer, the mean VPI was reported as 94.10 [46]. Similarly, Kuru et al. analyzed YouTube videos about rotator cuff tears and the mean VPI was calculated as 90.6 [47]. Our results suggest that, although videos about tachycardia gained relatively restrained popularity among the public, those with higher dissemination value receive commensurate levels of attention. Contrary to our findings, several studies have reported that YouTube videos uploaded by laypersons are of poor quality, despite having higher rates of likes and VPI values [48]. This discrepancy may stem from various factors attributed to the differences in studied fields and video content.

A higher VPI was associated with a higher comment count, longer video duration and higher host channel subscriber count. The uploader verification may lead to a higher VPI, while the content of surgical management may lead to a lower VPI. A higher comment count may indicate better engagement with the video. Similarly, channels with a higher subscriber count or uploader verification generally enjoy greater popularity, thus increasing the likelihood that their videos will attract a broader audience. The general public may have limited knowledge about catheter ablation as a treatment strategy for tachycardia or other arrhythmias, and may even be unaware of the existence of this form of treatment, which could result in a lack of motivation to directly watch related videos. Hence, it is crucial to spread information about surgical management in other videos related to tachycardia.

This study had several unavoidable limitations, Firstly, YouTube search results are dynamic, meaning that our findings represent only reflect the results at a specific point in time. Secondly, searches were pragmatically confined to the English language, potentially inducing selective bias and reducing external validity. Besides, YouTube is not the only dataset available and videos from other platforms were not incorporated. Hence, the external validity of the data may be limited and may not reflect the scenario over the whole internet. Despite the adoption of a double-review process, the quality scales, GQS and TSS, were subjective and lacked strict validation, which caused inevitable confounders in our evaluation. Furthermore, the TSS encompassed comprehensive contents of tachycardia, whereas the majority of videos included in our study (81.42%) were within a 10-minute timeframe. Consequently, these videos tended to struggle to present all TSS content, potentially resulting in bias. Additionally, users seeking information on tachycardia may find more comprehensive details from a multitude of complementary videos on YouTube. Therefore, relying solely on the results from individual videos may not accurately quantify the holistic information available to users on YouTube. Further exploration into more comprehensive evaluation methods is warranted.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that overall reliability and educational quality of tachycardia-related videos on YouTube are low, which suggests a relatively high risk of encountering incomplete, inaccurate, or potentially misleading information. Video popularity was correlated with video reliability or quality. No significant difference were observed in terms of reliability, quality among diverse topics. The videos with longer duration or from source of academic or healthcare professionals could lead to higher reliability and quality. Improving the quality of internet medical information and optimizing online patient education necessitates collaborative efforts. We advocated academics and healthcare professionals to create more engaging and concise videos that comprehensively cover tachycardia knowledge. Meanwhile, collaboration between video studios and established clinicians needs to be fostered. Furthermore, video platforms should enhance regulatory, upgrading both screening systems and review mechanisms.
