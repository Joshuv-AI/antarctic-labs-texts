Predictive value of baseline risk factors and early prostate-specific antigen (PSA) kinetics for achieving deep PSA decline in metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer: implications for treatment intensification
Authors: Peimin Zhou, Zichuan Han, Yichuan Wang, Rui Yang, Wei Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly identification of treatment response is essential for risk-adapted management in metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC). This study aimed to explore the predictive value of baseline clinicopathological factors and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) kinetics for deep PSA decline at 6 months in patients with mHSPC.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted involving 364 patients with de novo mHSPC. The primary endpoint was defined as PSA at 6 months ≤0.2 ng/mL. Logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors, and a nomogram was constructed. Model performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and calibration analysis.ResultsIndependent predictors of poor PSA response included baseline alkaline phosphatase (ALP) >100 IU/L [odds ratio (OR) =0.363, P=0.02], International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) grade group 5 (OR =0.215, P=0.01), perineural invasion (OR =0.345, P=0.006), high disease volume (OR =0.270, P=0.02), and PSA >0.7 ng/mL at 2 months (OR =0.027, P<0.001). A predictive nomogram demonstrated discrimination (AUC = 0.907) and calibration. In a subgroup analysis of patients with PSA >0.7 ng/mL at 2 months, docetaxel addition was associated with a higher likelihood of achieving PSA response (OR =7.810, P=0.01).ConclusionsPSA at 2 months may serve as a useful biomarker for predicting deep PSA response in patients with mHSPC. The proposed nomogram may facilitate early risk stratification and warrants further validation in prospective multicenter studies.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Early prostate-specific antigen (PSA) kinetics, particularly a PSA level >0.7 ng/mL at 2 months, is the strongest predictor of failure to achieve deep PSA decline (≤0.2 ng/mL) at 6 months in patients with de novo metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC).

• A predictive nomogram integrating baseline clinicopathological features and dynamic PSA changes demonstrated favorable apparent discrimination (area under the curve =0.907).

• Among patients with PSA >0.7 ng/mL at 2 months, docetaxel addition was associated with a higher likelihood of deep PSA response, but this retrospective finding should be considered hypothesis-generating.

What is known and what is new?

• Early PSA decline is recognized as a prognostic biomarker in mHSPC, but its role in guiding treatment intensification remains unclear.

• This study identifies a practical 2-month PSA threshold and integrates it with baseline pathological and clinical factors to construct a clinically applicable prediction model.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Dynamic PSA monitoring at 2 months may provide an early signal for biochemical response assessment.

• Patients with inadequate early PSA decline may represent a higher-risk subgroup for future prospective studies of treatment intensification.

Introduction

Prostate cancer is the second most common cancer in men worldwide, with 1,466,680 new cases reported in 2022. Among these, 10–20% present with de novo metastases, leading to a poor 5-year relative survival rate of only 32% (1-3). Management of metastatic hormone-sensitive prostate cancer (mHSPC) has significantly advanced, with the combination of androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) and second-generation androgen receptor pathway inhibitors (ARPIs) now established as the standard of care (4). Landmark trials such as ARCHES and TITAN have shown that ADT plus ARPIs significantly improves overall survival (OS) compared to ADT alone (5-7). Furthermore, the ARASENS and PEACE-1 trials demonstrated that early triplet therapy (ADT + ARPIs + docetaxel) significantly prolongs OS in high-volume disease, emphasizing the importance of risk-adapted treatment strategies (8,9). Despite these advances, a critical unmet need persists: the absence of validated tools to predict which patients will benefit from chemotherapy escalation versus those who may avoid unnecessary toxicity. Existing models, such as the Chemohormonal Therapy Versus Androgen Ablation Randomized Trial for Extensive Disease in Prostate Cancer (CHAARTED) criteria and Systemic Therapy in Advancing or Metastatic Prostate Cancer: Evaluation of Drug Efficacy (STAMPEDE) risk stratification, largely focus on baseline clinical factors (e.g., tumor volume, visceral metastases) without integrating dynamic biomarkers like prostate-specific antigen (PSA) kinetics (10,11). Early PSA decline and achieving PSA ≤0.2 ng/mL at 3 months have emerged as important prognostic indicators, yet their role in guiding chemotherapy decisions remains unexplored (12). Therefore, we conducted this retrospective study to explore the predictive value of baseline clinicopathological characteristics and early PSA kinetics to forecast 6-month PSA levels—a surrogate marker for long-term response (13). Our objectives are twofold: (I) to predict 6-month PSA responses in patients with newly diagnosed metastatic prostate cancer; and (II) to identify patients at risk of suboptimal biochemical response who may be candidates for further prospective evaluation of treatment intensification strategies. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1132/rc).

Methods

Study population

This retrospective, single-center study included patients with de novo metastatic prostate cancer treated at Peking University First Hospital between January 1, 2019, and August 31, 2024. Inclusion criteria were a confirmed diagnosis, complete baseline and follow-up data, and a minimum of 6 months of treatment without undergoing radical prostatectomy. A total of 364 eligible patients were analyzed. Patients with incomplete baseline or follow-up data were excluded from the analysis; therefore, a complete-case analysis approach was applied. No imputation for missing data was performed. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (No. 2024yan583). The informed consent was waived by the committee due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Clinical parameters

Baseline demographic and clinical data—including age, laboratory findings, pathological results, and imaging data—were extracted from electronic medical records. Histological classification, Gleason score, International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) grade group (GG), perineural invasion (PNI), and extracapsular extension were assessed using transrectal ultrasound-guided prostate biopsy specimens according to the 2019 ISUP guidelines. All biopsy slides were reviewed by two experienced genitourinary pathologists. PNI was defined as the presence of tumor cells surrounding, tracking along, or invading nerve structures within the biopsy specimens. Extracapsular extension was recorded when tumor infiltration beyond the prostatic capsule was identified on pathological assessment. Discrepancies between the two pathologists were resolved through joint review and consensus discussion. Disease burden was evaluated using 18F-prostate-specific membrane antigen positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-PSMA-PET/CT) and classified according to CHAARTED criteria. High-volume disease was defined as the presence of visceral metastases and/or ≥4 bone metastases with at least one bone lesion beyond the vertebral column and pelvis. Patients who did not meet these criteria were classified as having low-volume disease. Treatment typically began within 1 month of diagnosis and continued for at least 2 months, with monthly PSA monitoring during the first 6 months. Treatment escalation (e.g., addition of docetaxel or ARPI upgrade) was based on PSA kinetics. The primary endpoint was PSA response at 6 months after treatment initiation, defined as a PSA level ≤0.2 ng/mL. The outcome (PSA level at 6 months) was obtained from laboratory records, representing an objective measurement; therefore, blinding of outcome assessment was not applicable.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 and R 4.2.3. Patients were stratified based on 6-month PSA response. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and expressed as medians [interquartile ranges (IQRs)]; comparisons employed the Mann-Whitney U test. Log2 transformation of PSA values was used to analyze early PSA kinetics, and the 1-month −log2(PSA) slope was calculated.

Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared using Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests. A linear mixed-effects model was applied to compare PSA trajectories over time. Univariate logistic regression identified predictors of 6-month PSA response, including both baseline and dynamic variables.

Subgroup analyses focused on patients with poor early responses (PSA >0.7 ng/mL at 2 months). Cutoff points for baseline PSA, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and hemoglobin were derived from existing literature, while the optimal PSA cutoff at 2 months was determined using Youden’s index from receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis (14,15).

Significant variables from univariate analyses were included in multivariate logistic regression models, ensuring minimal multicollinearity. Disease volume was retained, whereas metastatic site specifics were excluded to avoid redundancy. Significant predictors were then incorporated into a nomogram. The model’s discrimination and calibration were evaluated using the area under the ROC curve (AUC) and bootstrapped calibration plots, respectively. Two-sided P values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. The model was internally evaluated using ROC and calibration analysis. All analyses were performed in this single development dataset; no separate validation cohort was available, and therefore no comparison between development and validation samples could be undertaken.

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics and response

A total of 432 patients were initially screened, of whom 68 were excluded because of incomplete baseline data, unavailable follow-up data, unknown treatment regimens, or prostate surgery within the first 6 months; 364 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure S1). As shown in Table 1, the median age of the patients was 69.0 years (IQR, 64.0–76.0 years), with a median baseline PSA of 102.00 ng/mL (IQR, 38.60–404.98 ng/mL) and ALP of 92.0 IU/L (IQR, 67.0–233.0 IU/L). Pathological evaluation revealed pure adenocarcinoma in 45.6% of cases; notable variant features included cribriform patterns (22.3%) and intraductal carcinoma (11.5%). The majority of patients (61.3%) had ISUP GG 5 tumors, and 40.7% exhibited PNI.

Regarding metastasis, 78.6% had skeletal involvement (M1b), and 62.9% were classified as high-volume disease per CHAARTED criteria. The most common initial therapy was ADT combined with second-generation ARPIs (apalutamide, enzalutamide, darolutamide, or abiraterone). Treatment adaptations based on PSA responses included escalation with docetaxel, radiotherapy, or switching antiandrogen agents.

At 6 months, patients achieving PSA ≤0.2 ng/mL had significantly lower baseline PSA (96.88 vs. 151.90 ng/mL, P<0.05) and ALP levels (81.00 vs. 116.00 IU/L, P<0.05), lower ISUP grades, lower rates of PNI, and lower disease volumes compared to non-responders. Furthermore, those with favorable PSA responses were more likely to have received second-generation ARPIs (76.7% vs. 58.2%) and less likely to have been treated with first-generation antiandrogens like bicalutamide.

Treatment intensification was less common among non-responders, with 68.8% maintaining their original regimen compared to 59.2% in the responsive group. These differences were statistically significant (Table 1).

Monthly variations in PSA levels

We evaluated baseline PSA levels and monthly PSA changes during the first 6 months of treatment (Table S1 and Figure S2). The greatest absolute and relative PSA reductions were observed within the initial 2 months. As expected, patients achieving a PSA response at 6 months consistently exhibited lower absolute PSA values each month compared to non-responders. Furthermore, their relative decline from baseline and monthly percentage reductions were significantly greater than those in non-responders (P<0.001). These findings suggest that the PSA level at 2 months may serve as an early predictor of the 6-month treatment response.

Predictive factors for 6-month PSA response

ROC curve analysis was first performed to determine the optimal cutoff value of PSA at 2 months for identifying failure to achieve PSA ≤0.2 ng/mL at 6 months. PSA at 2 months showed good discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.873 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.814–0.928]. The cutoff of 0.7 ng/mL provided the maximum Youden index of 0.746, with a sensitivity of 88.2% and a specificity of 86.4%.

To identify predictors of 6-month PSA response, we performed univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses (Table 2). In the multivariate model, several factors emerged as significant. Elevated baseline ALP levels (>100 IU/L) were associated with a reduced likelihood of PSA response [odds ratio (OR), 0.363; 95% CI: 0.151–0.871; P=0.02]. Patients with ISUP GG 5 tumors had significantly lower response rates compared to those with GG ≤3 (OR, 0.215; 95% CI: 0.064–0.724; P=0.01). Similarly, PNI (OR, 0.345; 95% CI: 0.161–0.739; P=0.006) and high disease volume (OR, 0.270; 95% CI: 0.087–0.835; P=0.02) were independently associated with poorer outcomes. Notably, a PSA level >0.7 ng/mL at 2 months was strongly predictive of non-response at 6 months (OR, 0.027; 95% CI: 0.011–0.066; P<0.001).

Based on these variables, a nomogram was developed (Figure 1A), achieving an AUC of 0.907 (95% CI: 0.872–0.941) (Figure 1B). Calibration plots showed good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities (Figure 1C). The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test indicated no evidence of poor calibration for the final prediction model (P=0.62). In the development cohort, the apparent calibration slope was 0.98 and the apparent calibration-in-the-large was −0.03, further supporting acceptable apparent calibration. To evaluate the incremental predictive value of early PSA kinetics, we constructed an additional baseline-only model excluding PSA at 2 months. The baseline-only model excluding PSA at 2 months showed a substantially lower AUC of 0.735 compared with the full model incorporating PSA at 2 months (Figure S3).

Given the strong predictive value of the 2-month PSA level, a subgroup analysis was conducted in patients with PSA >0.7 ng/mL at 2 months. The results were consistent with those of the overall cohort (Table 3), identifying ALP elevation, PNI, and high disease volume as adverse factors. Notably, treatment intensification, particularly the addition of docetaxel, was associated with a higher likelihood of achieving PSA ≤0.2 ng/mL at 6 months compared with no treatment change (OR, 7.810; 95% CI: 1.612–37.843; P=0.01). However, because treatment adjustment was not randomized, this association may be affected by treatment-selection bias and residual confounding.

Further multivariate analysis comparing stable therapy versus docetaxel addition (Table S2) revealed that docetaxel use was associated with higher odds of achieving PSA ≤0.2 ng/mL at 6 months (OR, 3.910; 95% CI: 1.372–11.145; P=0.01). Discussion

Early prediction of treatment response is essential for optimizing therapy in mHSPC. This study presents a comprehensive model integrating baseline clinicopathological features and dynamic PSA kinetics to predict 6-month PSA outcomes.

Our finding that elevated baseline ALP is associated with a reduced likelihood of PSA response (OR, 0.363) aligns with its established role as a marker of skeletal metastases and tumor burden (16,17). Prior studies in metastatic castration-resistant prostate cancer (mCRPC) have similarly linked ALP reductions to improved survival with therapies like radium-223 (16). Our data extend these observations to mHSPC, emphasizing the potential need for bone-targeted or intensified systemic therapies in patients with high ALP.

ISUP GG 5 was another strong negative predictor of PSA response (OR, 0.215), consistent with the known biological aggressiveness of high-grade tumors (18) These high-grade tumors are biologically aggressive, exhibiting genomic instability, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and androgen receptor (AR) hyperactivity, likely explaining their relative resistance to ARPIs (19,20). Alternative strategies, such as poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors or chemotherapy, may be necessary for this subgroup.

PNI independently predicted poorer outcomes (OR, 0.345), reinforcing its known role in facilitating extraprostatic extension and therapeutic resistance (21,22). Our data suggest that PNI-positive patients may benefit from earlier systemic intensification, including docetaxel-based therapy.

High disease volume also predicted a lower likelihood of PSA response (OR, 0.270). Despite evidence from ARASENS and other trials demonstrating that high-volume patients derive significant survival benefits from triplet therapy, clinical inertia may limit treatment escalation (8). Our findings highlight the need for proactive treatment intensification, even in patients with perceived poor prognosis.

The 2-month PSA threshold of 0.7 ng/mL emerged as the most powerful predictor (OR, 0.027), surpassing traditional 3-month markers (23). Rapid early PSA decline signals effective AR pathway suppression, while early plateaus suggest the emergence of resistant clones (24). Nevertheless, recent evidence from apalutamide-based studies further supports the prognostic relevance of PSA dynamics in mHSPC/mCSPC. In the TITAN post hoc analysis, deep, rapid, and durable PSA decline with apalutamide plus ADT was associated with longer OS, radiographic progression-free survival, time to PSA progression, and time to castration resistance (25). Similar findings were reported in the Asian subgroup of TITAN, providing relevant context for Asian patients (26). Recent real-world studies, including a 432-patient multicenter cohort and the European ArtemisR study, further showed that deep PSA responses are achievable in routine clinical practice with apalutamide-based therapy (27,28). In addition, a multicenter real-world study of apalutamide combined with stereotactic body radiotherapy suggested that PSA kinetics may remain informative in contemporary multimodal treatment settings (29). These studies support the broader clinical relevance of PSA dynamics, while our 2-month PSA threshold of 0.7 ng/mL should still be interpreted as an exploratory early biochemical marker requiring external validation.

Our subgroup analysis suggested that patients with poor early PSA decline (PSA >0.7 ng/mL at 2 months) who received docetaxel had a higher likelihood of achieving deep PSA response at 6 months. However, this observation should be interpreted with caution, as treatment allocation was not randomized and is subject to significant selection bias and residual confounding. Therefore, no causal inference can be made regarding the benefit of treatment intensification based on this retrospective analysis. Instead, these findings suggest that early PSA kinetics may help identify a biologically unfavorable subgroup of patients with suboptimal response to standard therapy, in whom more intensive treatment strategies may be considered for further prospective evaluation.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduces potential biases and unmeasured confounders, despite multivariable adjustment. Second, although PSA response has been associated with survival outcomes, survival data were not available in our cohort. Therefore, all conclusions should be interpreted in the context of PSA response prediction rather than direct clinical benefit (30). Third, the nomogram was developed and evaluated within the same cohort. Although the model showed favorable discrimination and calibration, its performance may be overestimated because no independent external validation cohort was available. Therefore, the model should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than ready for direct clinical implementation. Future multicenter prospective studies are required to externally validate the model and assess its generalizability across different populations and treatment settings. Fourth, several continuous variables, including baseline PSA, ALP, and hemoglobin, were dichotomized in the present study. Although these cutoffs were selected based on prior evidence, or clinically interpretable laboratory reference ranges, dichotomization may lead to loss of information, reduced statistical power, and potential instability in threshold selection (15). Future studies should evaluate these predictors as continuous variables and apply flexible modeling approaches, such as restricted cubic splines, to further assess the robustness and generalizability of the selected thresholds. Moreover, decision curve analysis was not performed, and the clinical net benefit of this nomogram remains to be determined in future prospective studies with survival endpoints.

Conclusions

This study establishes a predictive model combining baseline clinical features and early PSA kinetics to identify mHSPC patients at risk of suboptimal PSA response at 6 months. A PSA level >0.7 ng/mL at 2 months emerged as the strongest independent predictor of poor response. The nomogram demonstrated favorable discrimination and calibration in this single-center cohort; however, external validation is required before it can be applied to clinical decision-making. Furthermore, since the primary endpoint is a surrogate biomarker rather than a clinical survival outcome, these findings should be interpreted as predictive of biochemical response rather than clinical benefit.

Docetaxel addition was associated with improved PSA response in a subgroup of patients with poor early PSA decline; however, this retrospective, non-randomized finding is exploratory and requires prospective validation before informing treatment decisions. Future prospective studies should focus on external validation and explore the integration of molecular and genomic markers to further refine risk stratification and optimize individualized treatment strategies in mHSPC.
