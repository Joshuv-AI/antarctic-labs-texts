Impact of race-neutral GLI reference equations in Northeast Asian patients with IPF
Authors: Hyeonsu Lee, Chang-Hoon Lee, Kwonhyung Hyung, Sun Mi Choi, Sang-Min Lee, Jimyung Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI) has recently introduced race-neutral reference equations for pulmonary function tests, replacing traditional ethnic-specific equations. However, the clinical impact of this transition in Northeast Asian patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) remains unclear.MethodsWe evaluated the implications of switching from the ethnic-specific equations to the race-neutral GLI equations in 903 Korean patients newly diagnosed with IPF between 2005 and 2020. Per cent predicted values of forced vital capacity (FVC) and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) were calculated using both equations and used to derive the baseline gender-age-physiology (GAP) index scores. We then compared the prognostic performance of GAP staging derived from each equation in predicting all-cause mortality. Additionally, we assessed the clinical impacts of 1-year absolute declines in FVC and DLCO % predicted, as determined by each equation.ResultsThe race-neutral GLI equations yielded higher FVC % predicted and lower DLCO % predicted values compared with the ethnic-specific equations, resulting in GAP stage reclassification in 203 of 903 patients (22.5%), predominantly toward lower stages. Despite these differences, the agreement in GAP staging between the two equations was substantial (Cohen’s kappa 0.628) and their predictive performance for mortality did not significantly differ (C-index 0.686 vs 0.684; p=0.598). One-year absolute declines in FVC and DLCO % predicted were similarly associated with increased mortality, regardless of reference equation used.ConclusionsIn Northeast Asian patients with IPF, race-neutral GLI equations resulted in reclassification of GAP stage for some patients but did not affect the overall prognostic performance of the GAP index in predicting mortality.

Introduction

Pulmonary function test (PFT) results are interpreted by comparing measured values with the predicted values derived from reference populations.1 These predicted values are calculated based on age, sex, height and race of healthy individuals.24 However, the inclusion of race as a variable has become controversial because racial differences are increasingly attributed to socioeconomic and environmental disparities rather than inherent genetic differences.57 To address this issue, efforts have been made to establish a single, race-neutral set of reference equations for interpreting PFT. In 2022, a race-neutral spirometry reference that removed race adjustment was developed and incorporated into the current Global Lung Function Initiative (GLI Global) reference equations.8 9

Transitioning from ethnic-specific to race-neutral reference equations has significant clinical and social implications. A recent large-scale multiethnic study found that applying race-neutral GLI equations could lead to reclassification of lung function status in a substantial portion of the population, potentially altering the classification of ventilatory impairment and eligibility for disability compensation. These changes can significantly influence medical decision-making and healthcare policies.10 However, alterations in classification criteria may raise concerns regarding potential overestimation or underestimation of disease severity. Therefore, it is essential to evaluate the impact of transitioning reference equations on the accuracy of prognosis prediction, which is one of the fundamental roles of PFT.1113

Given the crucial role of PFT in predicting the prognosis of chronic lung diseases, it is essential to examine the impact of adopting race-neutral GLI equations across a range of disease conditions and diverse patient populations. For instance, in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), the use of race-neutral GLI equations has been shown to differentially affect the grading of COPD severity between Black and White patients.10 Asian populations, which constitute a substantial portion of the global population, remain relatively under-represented in the derivation of the current race-neutral GLI equations.9 Therefore, assessing their applicability and implications in Asian cohorts is particularly important. Indeed, such assessments have been conducted in Asian patients with COPD.14 15 However, the impact of race-neutral GLI equations remains largely unexplored in Asian patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), one of the most lethal chronic lung diseases.

IPF is a chronic fibrotic interstitial lung disease (ILD) characterised by a progressive decline in lung function and poor prognosis.16 17 PFT is commonly used for assessing IPF severity, with forced vital capacity (FVC) and diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) serving as well-established indicators of disease progression. The per cent predicted values of these parameters are used to calculate the gender-age-physiology (GAP) index, a widely used prognostic model for IPF.18 The choice of reference equations can influence the grading of disease severity and GAP staging in patients with IPF; thus, this study aimed to evaluate the implications of adopting the race-neutral GLI equations for predicting the prognosis of Northeast Asian patients with IPF.

Methods

Study design and population

This study aimed to compare ethnic-specific reference equations with race-neutral GLI reference equations in predicting the prognosis of Northeast Asian patients with IPF. Given that the GAP index is a well-established prognostic model for IPF,18 we assessed whether the baseline GAP index and stage were influenced by the use of different reference equations for interpreting PFT results. We then analysed which GAP stage, based on the two equations, better predicted mortality outcomes. Furthermore, we evaluated the 1-year changes in lung function (absolute changes in per cent predicted values) using both reference equations and examined whether the assessment of disease progression differed between them. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. E-2504-086-1630). Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research because of our retrospective study design.

This observational study included Korean patients newly diagnosed with IPF based on a multidisciplinary assessment at Seoul National University Hospital, a tertiary referral centre in South Korea, between January 2005 and December 2020. IPF was diagnosed according to contemporary international guidelines through multidisciplinary discussion based on the presence of a usual interstitial pneumonia pattern on chest CT and/or surgical lung biopsy findings after exclusion of identifiable secondary causes of ILD.19 Patients without baseline spirometry or DLCO results and those lacking height or weight measurements necessary for calculating the predicted values of lung function were excluded. Patients with a history of prior lung resection were also excluded because of its potential impact on lung function. For the 1-year longitudinal analysis of lung function, only patients with available 1-year follow-up PFT data were included.

Data collection and study outcomes

Electronic medical records and PFT database were retrospectively reviewed to collect demographic and relevant clinical data including PFT measurements. Baseline and 1-year follow-up PFT data were used for analysis. Baseline PFT data were defined as measurements obtained within ±3 months of the initial diagnosis, and 1-year follow-up PFT data were defined as measurements obtained between 9 and 15 months after diagnosis.

The FVC % predicted and DLCO % predicted were calculated using both the ethnic-specific and race-neutral GLI equations.9 20 The ethnic-specific equations (Korean reference equations applied in this study) for FVC and DLCO are listed in online supplemental Table 1 in the Additional File.21 22 Mortality data were primarily obtained from the Ministry of Public Administration and Security of South Korea and supplemented with institutional medical records when available. Survival status was tracked until 31 December 2021. Patients who underwent lung transplantation were classified as deceased.

Using the FVC and DLCO % predicted, the GAP index and stage were determined for each patient based on both reference equations. We evaluated the agreement in the GAP stages of the two equations and analysed which stage was more accurate in predicting mortality. Additionally, using the 1-year follow-up PFT data, longitudinal changes in the FVC and DLCO % predicted were assessed. One-year absolute declines in FVC and DLCO % predicted were calculated using both equations and subsequently integrated into a mortality prediction model. Two prediction models—one based on ethnic-specific equations and the other on race-neutral GLI equations—were developed and compared. Finally, we investigated whether the proportion of patients classified as having significant disease progression over 1 year differed between the models. Significant disease progression was defined using the cut-off values of 5% for absolute decline in FVC and 10% for absolute decline in DLCO, consistent with the recently introduced criteria for progressive pulmonary fibrosis.23

Statistical analysis

Clinical characteristics were analysed across baseline GAP stages derived from both ethnic-specific and race-neutral GLI equations. The agreement in GAP staging was assessed using Cohen’s kappa test. Continuous variables were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test, while categorical variables were analysed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. Data were presented as medians (IQRs) for continuous variables and numbers (%) for categorical variables.

All-cause mortality was compared across the GAP stages of the two reference equations using Kaplan–Meier survival curves, and survival differences were evaluated using log-rank tests. Cox proportional hazard regression was conducted to assess the risk of mortality according to the GAP stage (GAP stage 2 vs 1 and stage 3 vs 1), and HRs with 95% CIs were calculated. Harrell’s C-indices were computed and compared with evaluate the predictive performances of the GAP stages from the two reference equations.

The impact of the two reference equations on the assessment of disease progression during the first year was analysed. First, the proportion of patients classified as having significant disease progression at 1-year follow-up was compared between the two equations. Then, the effects of the degree of 1-year absolute decline in FVC and DLCO % predicted on future mortality were analysed using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for age, sex, height, weight and baseline lung function. Similar to the analysis of the baseline GAP stage, Harrell’s C-indices were calculated and compared to evaluate the differences in predictive performance between models based on the race-neutral GLI equations and those based on the ethnic-specific equations. Sensitivity analyses were additionally performed by further adjusting for comorbidities and 6 min walk distance when available. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Statistical significance was set at p<0.05.

Results

Patient characteristics and survival outcomes

Between January 2005 and December 2020, 1426 patients were newly diagnosed with IPF. Of these, 523 patients were excluded because of missing baseline PFT data (n=490), prior lung resection (n=28) or absence of anthropometric measurement data (n=5). Consequently, 903 patients were included in baseline analysis. For the 1-year longitudinal analysis, 319 of 903 patients were included, because the 584 patients had missing 1-year follow-up PFT data (figure 1). Baseline GAP stage and risk prediction

At baseline, the median FVC was 2.6 (2.0–3.2) L, and the median DLCO was 10.3 (7.7–13.1) mL/min/mm Hg (table 1). Using the race-neutral GLI equations, FVC % predicted was 15.9% (12.0%–19.4%) higher, while DLCO % predicted was 4.2% (3.1%–5.1%) lower compared with calculated values using the ethnic-specific equations (p<0.001). Discrepancies in FVC % predicted were more pronounced in older individuals and male patients, while differences in DLCO % predicted remained consistent across all age groups (online supplemental figure 1).

The agreement in GAP staging between the two equations was substantial (Cohen’s kappa 0.628, p<0.001); however, some discrepancies were observed (table 2). The race-neutral GLI equations classified a greater proportion of patients as GAP stage 1 (49.7% vs. 42.7%) and a lower proportion as GAP stage 3 (10.1% vs. 15.4%) compared with the ethnic-specific equations. Among 378 patients classified as GAP stage 2 using the ethnic-specific equations, 99 (26.2%) were classified as GAP stage 1 using the GLI equations. Similarly, among 139 patients classified as GAP stage 3 using the ethnic-specific equations, 58 (41.7%) were classified as GAP stage 2 using the GLI equations.

Disease progression at 1 year and risk prediction

Online supplemental table 2 summarises the baseline characteristics and PFT results of the 319 patients with available 1 year follow-up PFT data and the 584 patients without available follow-up data. Among the 319 patients included in the 1 year longitudinal analysis, the median FVC and DLCO were 2.7 (2.2–3.3) L and 10.8 (8.4–13.3) mL/min/mm Hg, respectively. Compared with the included patients, those excluded from the longitudinal analysis had lower baseline FVC and DLCO values. Among these excluded patients, 111 patients were excluded because of death within 1 year after diagnosis.

Over the 1-year period after diagnosis, the median change in FVC % predicted was −1.0% (−5.1%–2.1%) based on the ethnic-specific equations and −1.5% (−6.1%–2.6%) based on the GLI equations. Similarly, the median change in DLCO % predicted was −2.7% (−7.7%–2.6%) based on the ethnic-specific equations and −2.4% (−7.2%–2.4%) based on the GLI equations.

The proportion of patients classified as having significant disease progression (1-year FVC decline ≥5% and/or DLCO decline ≥10%) was 34.8% using the ethnic-specific equations and 38.9% using the race-neutral GLI equations, with substantial agreement (Cohen’s kappa=0.832, p<0.001). Among 208 patients not classified as having significant disease progression using the ethnic-specific equations, 19 (9.1%) were reclassified as having significant disease progression when using the GLI equations.

Patients who experienced significant disease progression during the first year had a higher risk of future mortality regardless of the reference equations used (online supplemental figure 2). Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that patients categorised as having significant disease progression based on either reference equations had comparable survival curves (online supplemental figure 3).

Regardless of the equations used, the 1-year absolute declines in FVC and DLCO % predicted were associated with future mortality after adjusting for covariates including baseline lung function (table 3). When comparing Model 1 using the ethnic-specific equations and Model 2 using the race-neutral GLI equations, the HRs for mortality per 1% decline in FVC and DLCO % predicted were similar. Harrell’s C-index also showed a comparable predictive performance between the two models (Model 1, 0.773 vs Model 2, 0.774, p=0.462). Similar findings were observed in sensitivity analyses further adjusting for comorbidities and 6 min walk distance (online supplemental table 3).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that using the race-neutral GLI reference equations, rather than the current regional ethnic-specific reference equations, did not result in a significant difference in predicting all-cause mortality risk in Northeast Asian patients with IPF, based on either baseline GAP stage or 1-year lung function decline. However, some discrepancies in GAP staging were observed between the two reference equations. Particularly, patients were more likely to be down-staged when the race-neutral GLI equations were used, primarily because of the higher FVC % predicted values derived from the GLI equations. Nonetheless, the assessment of 1-year absolute changes in lung function yielded comparable results between the two equations, with no significant difference in the classification of patients with significant disease progression.

PFTs are essential tools for diagnosing, grading and monitoring of lung diseases. Previous studies have shown that lung function parameters play a crucial role in predicting all-cause mortality in both the general population and patients with chronic lung diseases, such as COPD and IPF.11 12 24 Clinicians often interpret PFT results using per cent predicted values rather than absolute values, making the choice of reference equations a critical factor. However, the traditional inclusion of race in PFT reference equations has become controversial, leading to the development of race-neutral GLI equations as a new global standard.57 Changes from ethnic-specific to race-neutral reference equations may have important clinical implications; thus, it is essential to evaluate the impact of race-neutral reference equations in diverse populations and disease contexts. This is particularly critical for Asian populations, which account for a substantial proportion of the global population, yet remain under-represented in the development of the current race-neutral GLI equations compared with White and Black populations.9

FVC and DLCO are well-established prognostic indicators in patients with IPF, and the GAP stage, which incorporates the per cent predicted values of these parameters, is widely used for risk stratification.18 Since these values would differ depending on the reference equations used, the choice of equations may affect GAP staging and its predictive performance for mortality. To date, no studies have examined how the transition to race-neutral equations affects mortality prediction in Asian patients with IPF, despite the global relevance of this population given its large demographic proportion. In our study, adopting the race-neutral GLI equations in Northeast Asian patients with IPF, as compared with the ethnic-specific equations, resulted in higher FVC % predicted and lower DLCO % predicted, which resulted in a trend toward lower GAP stages. This finding is consistent with that of a recent study that examined the impact of the race-neutral GLI equations in Korean patients with COPD,14 15 wherein a substantial proportion of patients were reclassified as having milder disease when using the race-neutral GLI equations.

Despite the observed differences in GAP staging, the overall predictive performance of the GAP model for all-cause mortality remained unaffected, regardless of the reference equations used. These results provide supportive evidence for the clinical adoption of the race-neutral GLI equations in Asian patients with IPF, in accordance with the current international recommendations.8 Although some patients were down-staged when using the race-neutral GLI equations compared with the ethnic-specific equations, there was no significant difference in the overall prognostic performance between the GAP models derived from the two equations. This suggests that the Northeast Asian population-specific equations may overestimate disease severity in some cases, potentially leading to higher GAP stages without corresponding differences in the actual outcomes.

Given that reference equations can influence the calculation of longitudinal absolute changes in per cent predicted values of lung function parameters, we assessed the 1-year absolute changes in FVC and DLCO % predicted using both equations. The longitudinal changes in lung function are clinically important for evaluating disease progression and serve as key endpoints in IPF clinical trials. In our study, the use of the race-neutral GLI equations did not significantly affect the assessment of 1-year disease progression. Patients classified as having significant disease progression based on the two equations demonstrated nearly identical Kaplan–Meier survival curves, which also supports the feasibility of applying the race-neutral GLI equations in this population.

An intriguing finding was the opposing effects of the race-neutral GLI equations on the per cent predicted values of FVC and DLCO, compared with the ethnic-specific equations. The GLI equations led to a generally higher FVC % predicted, particularly in older individuals, and a lower DLCO % predicted. This pattern contrasts the findings of a recent study in predominantly White patients with ILD, where the GLI equations resulted in lower FVC % predicted and higher DLCO % predicted, compared with the traditionally used European Coal and Steel Community (ECSC) reference equations.25 This discrepancy may reflect possible ethnic-specific effects of the current race-neutral GLI equations. Although the GLI equations were developed as a race-neutral average reference, they may not optimally represent Northeast Asian populations.9 Expanding the dataset to include more individuals from under-represented diverse ethnic groups is essential for refining globally applicable, truly inclusive race-neutral reference equations.

Despite these important insights, this study has some limitations. First, this study was conducted exclusively in the Korean population, which limits the generalisability of our findings to other ethnic groups such as Southeast Asian populations. Given the potential regional and ethnic variations in lung function determinants, further studies are required to validate the applicability of the race-neutral GLI equations in patients with IPF across diverse populations. Second, the single-centre study design may have introduced a selection bias. A multicentre national cohort study is warranted to confirm and strengthen the validity of our findings. Third, a substantial number of patients were excluded because of missing baseline PFT data, which may have introduced additional selection bias. Fourth, because the longitudinal analysis included only patients with available 1-year follow-up PFT data, selection and survivorship bias may have influenced the results. Patients excluded from the longitudinal analysis had lower baseline lung function, potentially reflecting greater disease severity. Fifth, mortality analyses were based on all-cause mortality because cause-specific mortality data were not available, limiting assessment of IPF-related mortality.

In summary, our study showed that the race-neutral GLI equations resulted in higher FVC % predicted and lower DLCO % predicted values compared with the ethnic-specific equations in Northeast Asian patients with IPF. Although this led to lower GAP stage classification for some patients, the GLI equations-based GAP stage maintained a consistent predictive performance for all-cause mortality. Furthermore, the use of the GLI equations did not significantly alter the assessment of 1-year disease progression based on absolute changes in FVC and DLCO % predicted values. These findings support the safe implementation of race-neutral GLI equations in clinical practice for Northeast Asian patients with IPF.
