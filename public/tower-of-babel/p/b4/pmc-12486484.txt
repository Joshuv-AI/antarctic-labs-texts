Prevalence and factors associated with the irrational use of antibiotics by pregnant women in the city of Marrakech, Morocco: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Naima Aoutil, Mohamed Cherkaoui, Mohamed Bouskraoui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntibiotics are essential drugs for preventing maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality. However, their excessive and inappropriate use is leading to the emergence of antibiotic resistance, a major public health problem worldwide. Pregnant women’s attitudes and knowledge about the use of antibiotics have a significant impact on their practice and, consequently, on maternal and infantile child health. This study therefore aims to assess the knowledge, attitudes and self-reported practices of pregnant women regarding the appropriate use of antibiotics. It also seeks to identify the factors associated with the irrational use of antibiotics by pregnant women in the city of Marrakech.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional study conducted between January and April 2022. The study population included all women receiving postnatal care in birthing centers or during vaccination sessions in health centers in the city of Marrakech. Using a self-administered questionnaire, we collected data on women’s knowledge of antibiotic use, attitudes and self-reported practices during pregnancy. Regression analysis was performed to identify the factors associated with the irrational use of antibiotics by pregnant women in the city of Marrakech.ResultsOut of 292 women, 58.2% (n = 170) of pregnant women used antibiotics during their pregnancy. The use of prescription antibiotics represents 76.1% (n = 137) and 23.9% (n = 43) self-medicated. The most commonly used antibiotics were amoxicillin (n = 91, 53.5%) and amoxicillin clavulanic acid (n = 79, 46.5%). The symptoms most frequently reported by pregnant women were respiratory (n = 97, 53.9%), urinary (n = 38, 21.1%) and otorhinolaryngological (n = 24, 13.3%). The multiple logistic regression model showed that women’s family structure and level of education were significantly associated with knowledge. The number of consultations was significantly associated with attitudes. Family structure, level of education, number of children, number of consultations and place of birth were significantly associated with practice.ConclusionThis study highlighted knowledge gaps and misconceptions about antibiotics among pregnant women, as well as inappropriate attitudes and practices that encourage suboptimal use of these drugs. These results indicate the need to develop targeted educational programs to improve understanding and appropriate practice of the use of antibiotics during pregnancy. At the same time, they reinforce the imperative need for more rigorous regulations on the distribution of antibiotics, to ensure their responsible use and preserve their long-term efficacy.Keywords: Drug misuse, Pregnancy, Anti-Bacterial agents, Health knowledge, Attitudes, Practice, Risk factors, Self medication

Introduction

Antibiotic resistance is responsible for a significant increase in morbidity and mortality, longer hospital stays, higher healthcare costs and a tendency to resort to more expensive and often more toxic treatments [1].

The transmission of multi-resistant bacteria occurs in all groups of the human population, including pregnant women. According to a meta-analysis, the prevalence of maternal colonization with extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacteriaceae in Africa exceeds that reported in high- and middle-income countries, increasing the risk of neonatal colonization and/or subsequent infection with ESBL-producing strains [2]. In Morocco, maternal colonization by group B streptococcus (GBS) is also a cause for concern, with a prevalence rate of 24% among pregnant women [3]. Exposure to antibiotics during pregnancy can have both short- and long-term adverse effects on the children. One study revealed an increase in the prevalence of resistant organisms in the maternal intestinal flora, as well as an increase in the rate of Gram-negative bacteremia in low-birth-weight newborns, the latter being associated with exposure to antibiotics [4].

In addition, prenatal exposure to antimicrobials is associated with a reduction in birth weight of approximately 138 g [5]. In addition, antimicrobial exposure during pregnancy has recently been linked to childhood obesity [6]. Furthermore, the use of perinatal antibiotics is associated with lower average Apgar scores [7]. Moreover, the use of antibiotics by pregnant women at any time during pregnancy increases the risk of asthma exacerbations [8].

Antibiotic resistance poses a significant public health threat in both developed and developing countries. Globally, the primary cause of this emergence of resistance is the misuse and overuse of antibiotics [9].

Several studies have demonstrated that a significant number of pregnant women use non-prescription medications, including some antibiotics, to manage both minor and major symptoms during pregnancy [10]. According to various sources, up to 65% of pregnant women have used antibiotics at some point during their pregnancy, with beta-lactam antibiotics comprising over 67% of all antibiotics prescribed [7].

Young women and those from disadvantaged backgrounds are the most likely to use antibiotics during pregnancy [11]. Other studies have shown an association between antibiotic exposure and pregnancy factors such as parity, stage of pregnancy and mode of delivery [7]. Factors linked to the use of antibiotics by pregnant women without a prescription include level of education and household income [10]. The characteristics of pregnant women associated with a high perception of risk were primiparity, older age, higher education and the choice not to use medication during pregnancy [12]. Indeed, pregnant women need to be educated about the potential dangers of self-medicating with antibiotics, and regulations on prescribing and dispensing antibiotics need to be applied more strictly [10].

The aim of the present study was to assess the level of knowledge, attitudes and practices related to antibiotic use and to explore the predictive factors of irrational antibiotic use among pregnant women in the city of Marrakech, Morocco.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional study was carried out between January and April 2022 in health centers and maternity wards in the city of Marrakech. The aim was to analyze the factors influencing the use of antibiotics by pregnant women. Three health centers were selected from the list of health centers in the city of Marrakech. A random stratification method was used to select the health centers. We divided the districts into three levels according to their socio-economic criteria. For the birthing centers, we opted for a census given the limited number (two birthing centers).

People who were aware of the term antibiotic were included, and healthcare personnel were excluded from this study.

Data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire, With the exception of illiterate women, for whom interviews were conducted. In these cases, the questions were explained orally by the interviewer. The questionnaire was designed on the basis of a literature review of comparable studies [13]. Before being used for the main survey, the questionnaire was tested on a random sample of 30 participants, selected at the survey site but not included in the final study sample. The responses of the participants in the pilot study were examined to assess the clarity, relevance and comprehension of the questions. A test of internal consistency was carried out using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, which revealed a value of 0.75 for knowledge, 0.74 for practices and 0.50 for attitudes.

The questionnaire used comprised five parts: the first concerned the socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents; the second dealt with the use of antibiotics; the third explored women’s knowledge of antibiotics; the fourth looked at pregnant women’s attitudes to the use of antibiotics; and the fifth described pregnant women’s practice regarding the use of antibiotics. Postpartum women admitted to birthing centers and those attending vaccination services within six months of giving birth received an explanation of the research objectives, a guarantee of anonymity, and verbal informed consent was obtained before administering the questionnaire.

Answers to questions on Knowledges, Attitudes and Practice were given a score of 1, for a correct answer, while an incorrect or uncertain answer was given a score of 0. Total scores for each domain - Knowledge, Attitudes and Practices - were calculated on the basis of participants’ answers to a series of specific questions: 8 questions to assess knowledge, 9 questions to measure attitudes and 9 questions on practices. In order to establish a threshold for distinguishing between a good and a poor level in each area, the median score was used as the classification criterion [14]. Thus, participants with a score above the median were considered to have ‘good knowledge’, ‘appropriate attitudes’ and ‘good practice’.

Data analysis

The analysis of the data in this study included both descriptive and analytical components. The information collected from the questionnaires was entered, analyzed and recorded using SPSS software. Descriptive statistical analyses were carried out to detail the socio-demographic characteristics of the participants, their use of antibiotics, and their knowledge, attitudes and practice towards antibiotics.

The association between categorical variables was examined using the chi-square test. Variables with a p ≤ 0.25 were included in the logistic regression model [15]. In addition, logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with participants’ levels of knowledge, attitudes and practices. The significance level (α) was set at 0.05 for all statistical analyses. Finally, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to assess the strength of the relationships between the responses to the questions on knowledge, attitudes and practices.

Results

A total of 292 people responded to the questionnaires, compared with the 300 questionnaires distributed.

The socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants

Of the 292 women who took part in this study, half (n = 164, 56.2%) were under the age of 29. The average age of the participants was 32.5 years, with a standard deviation of 6.8 years, and more than half (n = 180, 61.6%) had fewer than three children. A small percentage (n = 37, 12.7%) were illiterate, while a quarter (n = 73, 25%) had received primary education. Over a third (n = 110, 37.7%) had a secondary education and 24.7% (n = 72) had attended university (see Table 1).

Among pregnant women, 58.2% (n = 170) used antibiotics during their pregnancy, mainly on prescription (n = 137, 76.1%) and 23.9% (n = 43) as self-medication. The antibiotics most frequently used were amoxicillin (n = 91, 53.5%) and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (n = 79, 46.5%). The symptoms most frequently reported by pregnant women were respiratory (n = 97, 53.9%), urinary (n = 38, 21.1%) and otorhinolaryngological (n = 24, 13.3%) (Table 1).

Knowledge of pregnant women about antibiotics

The results of this study show that 30.8% of pregnant women have a good knowledge of antibiotics. More than half of pregnant women (n = 189, 64.7%) mentioned that antibiotics are effective against bacterial infections and that their use can cause side effects (n = 126, 43.4%) and allergic reactions (n = 115, 39.4%), while only (n = 52, 17.8%) know that antibiotics are ineffective against viral infections. And over two-thirds (n = 223, 76.4%) of pregnant women believe that antibiotics are powerful drugs that can fight all germs. Almost (132, 45.2%) were aware that unnecessary use of antibiotics can render them ineffective. Only (n = 58, 19.9%) knew that antibiotics are not effective against most colds and coughs. Almost half (n = 143, 51.6%) knew that antibiotics are not medicines used to relieve pain and fever (Table 2).

Attitudes of pregnant women towards antibiotics

This study shows that 50.7% of pregnant women have appropriate attitudes (n = 81, 27.7%) think that they should stop taking an antibiotic when they feel better. Some 27.2% (n = 63) of women think that when they have a cold, they should take antibiotics to help them get better faster. 20.1% (n = 56) think that when a family member is sick, they should give him or her antibiotics. Nearly a third (n = 63, 27.2%) of women think that when they have a cold, they should take antibiotics to help them get better faster (Table 2).

Practice of pregnant women regarding the use of antibiotics

According to the study, 71.9% of women reported good practice. More than half (n = 119, 66.5%) completed the last course of antibiotics as prescribed. 29.1% (n = 85) gave someone else an antibiotic that was not prescribed for them. 39% (n = 114) used an antibiotic initially prescribed for an infection that returned later. 21.9% (n = 64) used an antibiotic initially prescribed for another illness. More than half of pregnant women (n = 164, 56.2%) usually keep antibiotics at home in case of emergency. 27.7% use the remaining antibiotics to treat respiratory infections. More than half (n = 174, 59.6%) take antibiotics according to the instructions. most pregnant women (n = 199, 88.4%) check the expiry date of antibiotics before taking them (Table 2).

Factors associated with knowledge, attitudes and practices

The multiple logistic regression model showed that family structure (nuclear) and level of education (OR = 0.096; CI= [0.03; 0.25]) were significantly associated with knowledge. The nuclear structure (OR = 2.48; CI = [1.19; 5.18]) had better knowledge than the extended structure. Women with lower levels of education had less knowledge than women with higher levels (Table 3).

Discussion

The study highlighted the excessive and inappropriate use of antibiotics by pregnant women, with almost 58.2% (n = 170) having used these drugs during their pregnancy, a figure significantly higher than that observed in other research [16, 17]. Other studies have showed that antibiotics account for around 80% of drug prescriptions during pregnancy, with between 20% and 40% of pregnant women having received them in various countries in recent years [3]. This trend in the use of antibiotics during pregnancy has increased worldwide over the last decade [18], raising concerns about increased prenatal exposure to antibiotics and antimicrobial resistance [19].

Self-medication with antibiotics is common practice worldwide [20]. The results of this study showed that around 23.9% (n = 43) of pregnant women self-medicated with antibiotics, which represent a higher rate than that reported in other similar studies (15,5%) [21].

Pregnant women who self-medicate are motivated by the desire to save time and money, to find rapid relief from illness and by knowledge of the signs and symptoms [22]. Antenatal care has been shown to be a protective factor against self-medication. Strengthening existing maternal health services helps to reduce these practices [21]. Similarly, health establishments must provide health education on the risks associated with self-administered medicines to all pregnant women attending antenatal care services [21].

The results of this study also showed that 76.1% (n = 137) of pregnant women used prescription antibiotics. Higher results were reported in a similar study (86%) [22]. Another study showed that a third of pregnant women received at least one prescription for antibiotics during pregnancy. Respiratory infections (n = 97, 53.9%), urinary tract infections (n = 38, 21.1%) and otorhinolaryngological infections (n = 24, 13.3%) were the main reasons for taking antibiotics reported by pregnant women. Similar results have been reported in other studies [23]. Prescribing antibiotics during pregnancy is unavoidable because of the proven risk of complications during this period, perinatal infections and infections in newborn babies [24]. Insufficient access to high-quality laboratory diagnostic tests can have major implications for the care of pregnant women, potentially leading to inaccurate diagnoses and inappropriate treatment regimens [25]. Infection surveillance, including effective treatment, helps to ensure good control of perinatal infections [26]. A systematic review and meta-analysis have showed that the administration of probiotics during pregnancy, particularly in the third trimester, was associated with reduced recto-vaginal colonization of group B Streptococcus (GBS) at 35–37 weeks and a safe perinatal profile [27]. The implementation of a simple educational program led to a significant improvement in the prescribing of antimicrobials, there were significant improvements in the selection of appropriate antimicrobials, appropriate duration and compliance with guidelines after the intervention. The program included face-to-face training sessions on the principles of antimicrobial stewardship, antimicrobial resistance, current prescribing guidelines and microbiological testing [28].

The results of this study also show that the antibiotics most frequently used by pregnant women during pregnancy are amoxicillin (n = 91, 53.5%) and amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (n = 79, 46.5%). In other studies, penicillin was also the class of antibiotics most frequently (39.7%) prescribed to pregnant women, followed by macrolides (13%). Among the combined penicillin, amoxicillin-clavulanic acid was commonly used (21.8%) [29]. Studies show that the majority of clinicians reported prescribing broad-spectrum antibiotics more often (56.9%) than narrow-spectrum antibiotics (31.4%) [25]. While initial broad-spectrum antibiotic treatment should be restricted or adjusted according to the results of culture and susceptibility testing in order to minimize the risk of death due to ineffective initial treatment, as well as the risk of antibiotic resistance resulting from prolonged treatment and the use of broad-spectrum antibiotics [30]. Indeed, it is recommended that healthcare professionals keep abreast of new safety data published on an ongoing basis and incorporate this information into prescribing practices in order to ensure the safe use of antibiotics in this vulnerable population. It is also essential that more studies are carried out into the side effects of drugs on pregnant women [29].

This inappropriate use of antibiotics is influenced by several factors. At an individual level, therapeutic preferences, knowledge and attitudes towards antibiotics are important factors in antibiotic use; Individual factors influencing inappropriate use include incorrect ideas, such as the conviction that antibiotics are effective against viruses, or the misconception that the antibiotic treatment prescribed can be stopped as soon as symptoms disappear [19]. This study also showed similar results. More than two-thirds (n = 223, 76.4%) of pregnant women believe that antibiotics are powerful drugs that can fight all germs. And 82.2% (n = 240) of women do not know that antibiotics are not effective against viruses. What’s more, 80.8% do not know that antibiotics are not effective against most colds and coughs.

The study also highlighted inappropriate attitudes, with 27.7% (n = 81) thinking that they should stop taking antibiotics when they feel better. Some 27.2% (n = 63) of women thought that when they had a cold, they should take antibiotics to help them get better more quickly. The number of consultations was significantly associated with attitudes. Women who had had between 2 and 4 consultations had better attitudes than women who had had fewer than 2 consultations.

The study also revealed some inappropriate practice. Almost 33.5% (n = 60) did not complete the last course of antibiotics as prescribed, 39% (n = 114) used an antibiotic initially prescribed for an infection that subsequently returned, 21.9% (n = 64) used a TBA initially prescribed for another illness. This result is similar to another study [19]. More than half of pregnant women (n = 164, 56.2%) usually keep antibiotics at home in case of emergency. And 26.7% (n = 78) use the remaining antibiotics to treat respiratory infections. This result is superior to another study [19]. More than half (n = 174, 56.6%) take antibiotics according to the instructions. 88.4% (n = 199) check the expiry date of antibiotics before taking them. Socio-economic status has been shown to be a determinant of antibiotic use during pregnancy, where young women and women living in deprived areas were more likely to be prescribed antibiotics during pregnancy [11]. Another study also showed that visits to an obstetrician/gynecologist were also a protective factor in the use of antibiotics [3]. This study also showed that socio-economic status and consultations were factors associated with the use of antibiotics during pregnancy. Nuclear structure, women with higher levels of education, women with fewer than three children, women of urban origin and those who had more than two consultations were protective factors.

Studies show that patients with higher knowledge scores were six times more likely to report desirable practices [31]. This study also showed a significant and positive relationship between knowledge, attitude and practice, with improved knowledge promoting appropriate attitudes and practice in line with recommendations. Similar results were reported in a Palestinian study on the relationship between the level of knowledge and attitudes and practices [13]. According to this study, pregnant women’s knowledge is influenced by their level of education and family structure. Women from nuclear family structures had better knowledge than those from extended family structures, and women with higher levels of education had better knowledge.

Educational interventions are effective approaches for reducing the overuse and inappropriate use of antibiotics. The results of a literature review showed that educational interventions were associated with improvements in the overall rate of prescribing, dispensing and consumption of antibiotics, as well as a significant reduction in antibiotic costs. These results support the need for public health actions to qualify primary care providers, mainly in low- and middle-income countries [32].

This study has limitations that should be taken into account. The cross-sectional design limits establishing causal links between responses and predictors. In addition, the data for this study were collected from questionnaires, which were based on the self-assessment of the participants. Practices and knowledge were reported by the participants themselves, which may introduce recall or subjectivity biases.

Conclusion

The results of this study show a positive and significant relationship between the knowledge, attitudes and practices of pregnant women. Indeed, the importance of education and awareness campaigns on the proper use of antibiotics is essential to reduce the inappropriate use of these drugs. Large-scale initiatives are essential to raise awareness among pregnant women and women of childbearing age, with the aim of promoting the safe and judicious use of drugs during pregnancy.

These campaigns should target women from extended family structures, less educated women, women with more than two children and women living in rural areas.

Specific modules could be integrated into secondary school and university curricula to reach a wide audience of women of childbearing age. In addition, regular prenatal consultations should be encouraged and supported, as they represent a strategic opportunity to promote optimal health practices during pregnancy.
