Anaphylactic Shock Induced by Povidone‐Iodine Vaginal Irrigation During Pregnancy Complicated by Fetal Distress: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Yanmei Li, Shilin Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAnaphylaxis during pregnancy is common, but severe anaphylaxis during pregnancy is relatively rare and may have serious implications for both the mother and the fetus, potentially leading to maternal death or neonatal death. Anaphylaxis caused by iodophor skin disinfection is commonly observed, but there has been no reported case of severe anaphylaxis occurring during pregnancy when iodophor is used for vaginal douche. Our case report suggests that pregnant women should be cautious about the potential risk of severe anaphylaxis when using iodophor vaginally and highlights the importance of managing severe allergic reactions in pregnancy.Keywords: anaphylactic shock, povidone‐iodine, pregnancy, vaginal irrigation

Introduction

Anaphylaxis during pregnancy is a rare but life‐threatening event that poses significant risks to both maternal and fetal health. The physiological changes of pregnancy, including altered immune responses and cardiovascular adaptations, may influence the presentation and severity of anaphylactic reactions, complicating diagnosis and management.

While various allergens—such as medications, insect stings, and food allergens—have been well documented in non‐pregnant populations [1, 2], iatrogenic causes including antiseptic solutions have gradually gained attention in recent decades [3, 4]. PVP‐I is a widely used antiseptic agent for perineal, vaginal, and cesarean skin disinfection, as well as vaginal irrigation in the management of vaginitis [5]. Although generally considered safe, rare cases of hypersensitivity reactions, including contact dermatitis and anaphylaxis, have been reported in non‐pregnant populations [4, 6, 7]. However, to our knowledge, no previous cases of anaphylactic shock following PVP‐I vaginal irrigation during pregnancy have been described in the literature.

We present a case of severe anaphylaxis induced by PVP‐I vaginal irrigation in a third‐trimester pregnant woman, complicated by acute fetal distress. This report details the clinical presentation, emergency interventions, and neonatal outcomes, highlighting the challenges in managing such high‐risk scenarios. Additionally, we provide a literature review on pregnancy‐associated anaphylaxis to discuss current diagnostic and therapeutic approaches, aiming to enhance awareness and optimize clinical strategies for this critical condition.

Case Presentation

Presenting Complaints and Disease History

A 27‐year‐old gravida 2 para 0 woman at 40+6 weeks of gestation was admitted for labor induction due to late‐term pregnancy. Her medical history included chronic urticaria with recurrent flares and multiple drug allergies (cephalosporins, itraconazole, allicin soft capsules, clotrimazole). Notably, she experienced syncope with a generalized rash after oral itraconazole. Physical examination revealed no abnormalities except a BMI of 27.99 kg/m2. Fetal heart rate (FHR) was 142 bpm, and late‐pregnancy ultrasounds and biochemical tests were unremarkable.

Prior to induction, vaginal swab confirmed vulvovaginal candidiasis, prompting vaginal lavage with 50 mL of 0.5% PVP‐I (Anerdian III). Fifty minutes post‐lavage, she developed facial urticaria, followed by generalized pruritus, tongue edema, and irregular uterine contractions. Vital signs initially remained stable (BP 101/75 mmHg, SpO2 100%), but then progressive respiratory distress, hypotension (99/59 mmHg), tachycardia (107 bpm), and FHR deceleration (90–110 bpm) occurred.

Treatment

Management included intravenous dexamethasone (10 mg) and oral loratadine (10 mg) upon diagnosing an allergic reaction. When diagnosing anaphylactic shock, epinephrine (0.5 mg bolus followed by 0.15 μg/kg/min infusion), rapid fluid replacement, calcium gluconate (1 g), and high‐flow oxygen (6 L/min) were administered simultaneously with left uterine displacement. Despite resuscitation, fetal distress prompted emergency cesarean delivery under general anesthesia. When disinfecting the skin before surgery, considering the patient's allergy to iodine, sterile physiological saline was used for skin disinfection. At the same time, there were consultant pediatricians present to prepare for neonatal rescue work during the cesarean section. A male neonate (3360 g) was delivered with Apgar scores of 6, 8, and 9 at 1, 5, and 10 min, respectively, and transferred to neonatal intensive care. Postoperatively, the mother was transferred to the intensive care unit, and her hemodynamics stabilized with epinephrine and noradrenaline infusions. Continuous monitoring of vital signs, oxygen therapy, and recording of urine output occurred simultaneously while administering intravenous azithromycin to prevent infection. After surgery and the first day after surgery, chest and abdominal X‐rays were performed, and all were normal. Blood gas analysis, myocardial enzymes, and renal function were normal (Table 1).

Final Diagnosis

Anaphylactic shock, mild neonatal asphyxia, and moderate anemia.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

On the first day after surgery, the mother's rash subsided, vital signs remained stable, and biochemical tests were normal. She was transferred back to the general ward and discharged on postoperative day 3. Due to limited hospital conditions, tryptase testing was not completed. Additionally, the doctors advised the mother to complete allergen testing after discharge. Furthermore, the clinical team conducted case discussions to guide physicians in screening for disinfectant allergies during preoperative assessments, thereby mitigating severe allergic outcomes. Future allergic reactions should prompt postoperative allergen testing, tryptase testing, and IgE testing to confirm the diagnosis. Neonatal follow‐up at 30 days revealed no neurodevelopmental abnormalities.

Discussion

Pathophysiology and Risk Factors

Anaphylaxis during pregnancy results from a critical interaction between immunological hypersensitivity and gestational adaptations. The IgE‐mediated mechanism begins with allergen‐specific IgE cross‐linking on mast cells and basophils, leading to the release of histamine, tryptase, leukotriene, and prostaglandin [1]. These mediators collectively cause systemic vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, and bronchoconstriction, culminating in hypotension, respiratory compromise, and tissue hypoxia [8, 9]. However, pregnancy introduces unique modifiers: elevated progesterone levels may more potently activate mast cells, thereby inducing IgE‐mediated allergic reactions [2, 10, 11]. This immunological shift may explain why some women with preexisting atopy experience exacerbated hypersensitivity during gestation, as observed in our patient with chronic urticaria and multiple drug allergies.

Notably, the hemodynamic consequences are exacerbated in late pregnancy due to aortocaval compression, which reduces venous return by 25%–30% [12]. Furthermore, anaphylactic vasodilation synergistically worsens placental perfusion, creating a “double‐hit” effect on placental. Maternal hypotension (systolic blood pressure < 80 mmHg) reduces uterine artery blood flow, precipitating fetal acidosis within minutes [13, 14]. Our case illustrates this cascade: the patient's blood pressure dropped to 99/59 mmHg at 50 min post‐exposure, coinciding with fetal bradycardia (90 bpm) and necessitating emergent delivery.

Triggers and Diagnostic Challenges

According to reports, antibiotics, latex, and anesthetics account for 40%–60% of obstetric allergy cases as allergens [1, 15, 16]. With increasing reports of local disinfectants such as PVP‐I causing allergies in non‐pregnant patients, obstetric and postpartum women should also be vigilant that these agents can also cause serious allergic reactions [3, 17, 18]. PVP‐I contains three potential allergens: iodine, povidone (polyvinylpyrrolidone), and nonionic surfactants. Of these, povidone is the most frequent sensitizer, causing both IgE‐mediated reactions and delayed‐type hypersensitivity [18, 19]. Due to frequent underreporting of antimicrobial allergy histories, the need for detailed allergy histories in prenatal care is underscored.

Diagnosis remains clinical, as serum tryptase levels—the gold standard biomarker—peak 60–90 min post‐onset and normalize within 24 h [2]. In our patient, the temporal association between PVP‐I lavage and symptom progression (urticaria → angioedema → hypotension) strongly supported anaphylaxis. However, the absence of tryptase testing (due to institutional limitations) highlights a critical gap in low‐resource settings. Notably, emerging biomarkers, such as platelet‐activating factor (PAF) and chymase, demonstrate diagnostic potential but require validation in pregnant cohorts [2].

Epinephrine Controversy and Evidence‐Based Practice

The use of epinephrine in pregnancy has long been debated. Early primate studies warned that epinephrine is associated with fetal bradycardia (defined as FHR < 110 bpm) and acidosis (umbilical artery blood gas pH < 7.2). However, this effect has not been found when catecholamines are directly injected into the fetal circulation, suggesting that vasoconstriction in the uterine circulation may be the main cause of fetal hypoxemia [20]. However, human pharmacokinetic data reveal lower placental transfer at standard doses (0.3–0.5 mg IM) due to epinephrine's rapid metabolism by placental catechol‐O‐methyltransferase [21]. Contemporary guidelines unanimously endorse epinephrine as first‐line therapy, emphasizing that maternal survival is the prerequisite for fetal viability [8, 22, 23].

Our case demonstrates epinephrine's dual benefits: the initial 0.5 mg IV bolus stabilized maternal blood pressure (100–105/60–65 mmHg), while the subsequent infusion (0.15 μg/kg/min) prevented rebound hypotension. Crucially, no neonatal arrhythmias or neurological sequelae were observed, aligning with recent cohort studies [24, 25]. These findings rebut historical concerns and reinforce the 2020 World Allergy Organization (WAO) recommendations for weight‐based dosing (0.01 mg/kg IM, max 0.5 mg) without pregnancy‐specific reductions [8, 26].

Adjunctive Therapies: Glucocorticoids and Beyond

Despite widespread use, glucocorticoids lack robust evidence for acute anaphylaxis management. A 2017 systematic review of 4268 cases found no reduction in biphasic reactions or mortality with corticosteroid administration [27]. Paradoxically, high‐dose methylprednisolone may worsen hypotension through nitric oxide‐mediated vasodilation [28]. In our patient, although dexamethasone (10 mg IV) was received initially, maternal hemodynamics only stabilized following epinephrine administration. The phenomenon indicates that dexamethasone contributed minimally to acute stabilization, emphasizing the importance of timely use of epinephrine in the treatment of anaphylactic shock. This aligns with the WAO's position that glucocorticoids should only serve as adjuncts to prevent late‐phase symptoms in high‐risk patients (e.g., asthmatics) [8, 29].

Delivery Timing and Fetal Monitoring

The decision to proceed with emergency cesarean section hinges on two factors: (1) maternal response to resuscitation and (2) fetal status. Guidelines recommend delivery within 5 min of maternal cardiac arrest (4‐min rule) to optimize neonatal outcomes [23, 30]. In non‐arrest scenarios, persistent fetal bradycardia (< 110 bpm for > 10 min) or recurrent late decelerations warrant intervention, as seen in our case. Notably, the neonate's Apgar scores in our case (6 at 1 min) reflected transient hypoxia but showed rapid recovery with resuscitation, emphasizing the resilience of term fetuses to short‐term insults [14, 22]. In addition, the choice of anesthesia (general vs. neuraxial) requires careful consideration when deciding on cesarean section. In our case, general anesthesia was used for emergency cesarean section due to the unstable hemodynamics of the mother. However, there have also been case reports of allergic shock caused by emergency cesarean section under general anesthesia [31]. Therefore, some scholars believe that compared to general anesthesia, where different classes of multiple drugs are used, subarachnoid anesthesia may be a suitable alternative in a stable obstetric patient [32].

Prevention and Future Directions

Preventive strategies for PVP‐I hypersensitivity remain underdeveloped. Patch testing with chlorhexidine digluconate (1% aqueous), PVP‐I, or iodine has been proposed but needs more clinical data to evaluate its clinical relevance [33, 34, 35]. For high‐risk patients, chlorhexidine or hypochlorous acid solutions may serve as alternatives, though their safety profiles in pregnancy require further study [36, 37]. Additionally, multidisciplinary drills simulating obstetric anaphylaxis could improve team readiness—a critical factor in our patient's successful outcome.

Conclusion

When anaphylaxis occurs, especially when experiencing difficulty breathing and hemodynamic instability, immediate epinephrine administration ((1:1000): 0.3–0.5 mL) [26] left lateral positioning, and fetal surveillance are required. Emergency cesarean delivery should be considered for refractory maternal shock or fetal compromise. Routine PVP‐I allergy testing and standardized obstetric anaphylaxis protocols are urgently needed. In the future, further research is warranted to establish biomarkers and consensus guidelines [2].
