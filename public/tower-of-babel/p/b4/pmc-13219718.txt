Alpha-1 antitrypsin as a new biomarker of inflammation, infection and disease severity in community-acquired pneumonia: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Siqing Huang, Shuna Wei, Fang Luo, Yuanxiong Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlpha-1 antitrypsin (α1-AT), an acute-phase protein, demonstrates anti-inflammatory, antiviral, and immunomodulatory properties. The early detection of disease severity and pathogen in community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) is crucial for patient management. The role of α1-AT in this context has not been fully elucidated. Our study compared plasma α1-AT levels between 74 CAP patients and 86 healthy controls. We measured plasma α1-AT concentrations via ELISA, assessed disease severity using CURB-65, CRB-65, and SMART-COP scores, and analyzed correlations between α1-AT levels with inflammatory markers, disease severity, and pathogen subtypes. Our findings revealed that plasma α1-AT was higher in CAP patients than in controls (p < 0.0001) and correlated positively with IL-6 and TNF-α. In addition, α1-AT concentrations increased with CAP severity and were positively correlated with CURB-65, CRB-65, and SMART-COP scores. Exploratory analyses revealed elevated α1-AT in Gram-positive bacterial and mycoplasma infections, with high predictive value for Gram-positive cases (AUC 0.9479; 95% CI 0.8997–0.9961). These findings suggest α1-AT as a potential adjunctive biomarker for assessing inflammation and disease severity in CAP, while its role in pathogen discrimination requires validation in larger cohorts.Keywords: Alpha-1 antitrypsin, Community-acquired pneumonia, Inflammation, PathogenSubject terms: Biomarkers, Diseases, Immunology, Medical research, Microbiology

Introduction

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) remains one of the leading global causes of mortality and morbidity, posing a significant public health challenge worldwide1,2. Data from the National Center for Health Statistics of the United States show that there are more than 1.7 million emergency department visits in the U.S. each year, among which CAP is the leading discharge diagnosis3. Among hospitalized CAP patients, approximately 2–24% require intensive care unit (ICU) admission, with a mortality rate as high as 50% in ICU-admitted cases4. In China, the overall incidence of CAP is 7.13 cases per 1000 person-years5. With accelerated population aging and prolonged life expectancy in China, the incidence and mortality rates of CAP are projected to rise significantly, leading to a persistent increase in the disease burden of CAP6.

The clinical presentation of CAP is highly heterogeneous, ranging from mild, ambulatory illness to severe, life-threatening sepsis requiring intensive care. This spectrum underscores a fundamental challenge in its management: the need for early, accurate diagnosis coupled with a rapid and reliable assessment of disease severity. Causative organisms of CAP include a diverse range of bacterial, viral, and atypical pathogens and remain difficult to identify quickly and accurately using current diagnostic techniques7–9. The timely initiation of appropriate antibiotic therapy is crucial for optimizing clinical outcomes in patients with pneumonia. Furthermore, preventing the unnecessary use of antimicrobial agents is essential to curb the development of antimicrobial resistance, reduce the risk of adverse drug reactions, and avoid the imposition of additional healthcare costs. Due to the inability of current microbiological tests to identify the causative pathogen in over 50% of pneumonia cases10–12, a biomarker-based adjunctive diagnostic approach may facilitate more targeted administration of antimicrobial agents.

Alpha-1 antitrypsin (α1-AT), a serine protease inhibitor with a plasma half-life of 3–5 days13, is primarily synthesized by hepatocytes and is also produced and expressed in other cell types, including neutrophils, macrophages, alveolar epithelial cells, intestinal mucosa, and corneal tissues14–16. Its primary physiological role involves neutralizing neutrophil elastase (NE) activity to maintain protease-antiprotease homeostasis17. As an acute-phase protein13, circulating α1-AT concentrations range between 88.0 and 136.9 mg/dl under physiological conditions18, increasing by 75–100% during inflammatory states, infections, surgical stress, or tissue damage19. Notably, α1-AT levels can exceed 160 mg/dl in systemic inflammation19, correlating with its anti-inflammatory properties14. Similar to C-reactive protein (CRP), α1-AT is markedly elevated during acute systemic lupus erythematosus20 and actively suppresses endotoxin-triggered TNF-α release from monocytes, neutrophils, and pulmonary epithelium21,22. Furthermore, α1-AT is related to IL-6 signal transduction23 and demonstrates its capacity to attenuate IL-1β synthesis and secretion in LPS-stimulated peripheral blood mononuclear cells24. Beyond anti-inflammatory properties, α1-AT exhibits broad-spectrum antiviral activity against H1N1 influenza, HIV-1, and human macrophage virus in both in vitro and in vivo models13,25,26. Clinically, plasma α1-AT correlates with mortality in critically ill COVID-19 patients27,28 and is significantly increased during dengue and malarial infections29,30. Collectively, these observations underscore the expanding recognition of α1-AT as a pivotal component of the acute inflammatory response.

Previous studies on α1-AT focused mostly on its potential as a biomarker for non-infectious diseases and its relationship with infectious diseases, single inflammatory mediators, and pathogens. This study aims to investigate the potential of plasma α1-AT as a biomarker in CAP. The primary objective is to compare plasma α1-AT levels between CAP patients and healthy controls and to evaluate its diagnostic performance. Secondary objectives include examining the correlations between α1-AT and disease severity scores (CRB-65, CURB-65, SMART-COP). In addition, exploratory analyses are conducted to assess whether α1-AT levels differ across pathogen subtypes.

Materials and methods

Research population

The diagnostic criteria conformed to the 2016 Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Adults with Community-Acquired Pneumonia31: 1. community-acquired; 2. pneumonia-related clinical manifestations, including cough, sputum production, or worsening of existing respiratory symptoms, with or without purulent sputum, accompanied by chest pain, dyspnea, and hemoptysis; fever; evidence of pulmonary solid lesions and/or audible moist rales; peripheral blood white blood cell (WBC) count exceeding 10 × 109/L or below 4 × 109/L, with or without a leftward shift in nuclear morphology; 3. interstitial alterations with or without pleural effusion, ground glass shadows, lobar or segmental solid shadows, or recent patchy infiltrates on a chest image. The clinical diagnosis was definite when all three criteria were met. CAP should be differentially diagnosed from pulmonary conditions, including tuberculosis, tumors, non-infectious interstitial disease, vasculitis, edema, atelectasis, embolism, and eosinophilic infiltration.

Patients who visited the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine and the Health Screening Center of The Third Affiliated Hospital, Southern Medical University between January 2023 and July 2023 were included in this prospective cohort study. A total of 78 CAP patients and 110 healthy subjects were recruited. After excluding 4 patients with incomplete information and 24 healthy subjects who were not eligible, 74 CAP patients and 86 healthy subjects participated in the study. Pneumonia severity was scored using the SMART-COP32, CURB-6533, and CRB-6534 scales (Table 1). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Third Affiliated Hospital, Southern Medical University (Approval No. 2022-lunshen-046). Written informed consent was obtained from the legal guardians of all participants. This research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria for CAP patients: (1) patients diagnosed with CAP according to current guidelines; (2) aged > 18 years; (3) provision of written informed consent. CAP patients were recruited from the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine.

Inclusion criteria for healthy controls: (1) aged > 18 years; (2) no clinical or radiological evidence of CAP or any acute infection at the time of enrollment; (3) provision of written informed consent. Healthy controls were recruited from the Health Screening Center during the same period.

Exclusion criteria for the two groups: (1) severe immunodeficiency, comorbid severe cardiovascular disease, or hepatic insufficiency; (2) pregnancy or breastfeeding; (3) chronic lung diseases (e.g., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, lung cancer, asthma) that could affect baseline inflammatory status; (4) other conditions known to influence α1-AT levels, including chronic inflammatory diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease), chronic infections (e.g., hepatitis, tuberculosis), or liver diseases (e.g., cirrhosis, hepatitis).

Experimental methods

Clinical characteristics (including gender, age, height, weight, etc.), laboratory parameters (including WBC count, lymphocyte count, neutrophil count, hemoglobin, serum albumin, CRP, etc.), and levels of inflammatory cytokines (including IL-6, IL-8, IL-1β, and TNF-α) of the study participants were collected from the hospital’s electronic medical record system.

Blood samples were obtained following an overnight fast and kept at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, the blood samples were centrifuged at 1,200 rpm for 10 min. Plasma was collected and preserved in a refrigerator at − 80 °C. The level of α1-AT was determined by ELISA assay using the Cusabio Human α1-AT ELISA Kit (Cat. no. CSB-E11719h, Cusabio, USA).

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 19.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA) were used for statistical analysis. Categorical variables were presented as counts (n). We used Kolmogorov–Smirnov to check the test distribution. Continuous variables that showed a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and variables that did not follow a normal distribution were reported as median (interquartile range). The chi-square test and independent samples t-test were used to compare categorical variables and continuous variables between groups, respectively. Two groups of non-parametric data were compared by the Mann–Whitney U test. Spearman or Pearson correlation coefficients were used to assess the correlations of plasma α1-AT levels with routine blood indicators, inflammatory cytokines, and disease severity scores in CAP patients. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the diagnostic performance of plasma α1-AT. Areas under the ROC curve (AUCs) were calculated with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of research participants

This study cohort comprised 74 CAP patients (41 males, 33 females) and 86 healthy controls (52 males, 34 females). The baseline characteristics of the study population were summarized in Table 2. No significant differences were found between the two groups in terms of age, gender, and body mass index (BMI) (p > 0.05). The levels of α1-AT, WBC count, neutrophil count, and CRP were higher in the CAP group than in the control group (p < 0.05). In addition, the levels of lymphocytes, hemoglobin, and serum albumin were lower in the CAP group compared to the control group (p < 0.05). Median SMART-COP, CRB-65, and CURB-65 scores for CAP patients were 1.0, 1.0, and 1.0, respectively (Table 3).

Plasma α1-AT levels in CAP patients: association with inflammatory markers and diagnostic performance

Levels of plasma α1-AT, WBC, neutrophil, and CRP were higher in the CAP group as compared to those in the control group (p < 0.0001). Conversely, lymphocyte count in the CAP group was relatively low (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1). Correlation analysis revealed that plasma α1-AT levels were negatively correlated with albumin (r = − 0.2412, p = 0.0492). However, no significant correlations were found between plasma α1-AT levels and white blood cells, neutrophils, lymphocytes, or hemoglobin (Table 4). Furthermore, in CAP patients, plasma α1-AT levels showed positive correlations with the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-6 (r = 0.3458, p = 0.0288) and TNF-α (r = 0.6217, p = 0.0003). No significant correlations were observed with CRP, IL-8, or IL-1β (Table 5).

ROC curve analysis was employed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of plasma α1-AT levels for distinguishing CAP patients from healthy controls. Results showed that α1-AT demonstrated moderate diagnostic accuracy, with an AUC of 0.7643 (95% CI 0.6926–0.8361). For comparison, CRP showed an AUC of 0.8793 (95% CI 0.8016–0.9570), and WBC showed an AUC of 0.7213 (95% CI 0.6355–0.8070) (Fig. 1F). The optimal threshold for plasma α1-AT was 482.5 nIU/ml, yielding a specificity of 80.1% and a sensitivity of 59.0%.

To assess whether the elevated α1‑AT levels in CAP patients were independent of potential confounders, we performed multivariable regression analysis. Variables that showed significant differences between the CAP and control groups in univariate analysis (WBC count, lymphocyte count, neutrophil count, hemoglobin, serum albumin, and CRP) were included as covariates. In this model, none of the included covariates showed a statistically significant independent association with elevated α1‑AT. Complete results are presented in Table 6.

Plasma α1-AT can be used as a marker to assess the severity of CAP

CRB-65, CURB-65, and SMART-COP scales were used to score the severity of CAP, and the relationship between plasma α1-AT level and CAP severity was analyzed. Plasma α1-AT levels were elevated in CAP patients with a CRB-65 score ≥ 3, as compared to patients with a lower CRB-65 score (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2A). Plasma α1-AT levels were the lowest in patients with a CURB-65 score of 0–1 and the highest in patients with a CURB-65 score of 3–5 (Fig. 2B). Moreover, it was found that plasma α1-AT levels in patients with a SMART-COP score of 3–4 were considerably higher than those with a SMART-COP score of 0–2 (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2C). Plasma α1-AT level was positively associated with the CRB-65 (r = 0.7196, p < 0.0001), CURB-65 (r = 0.8653, p < 0.0001), and SMART-COP (r = 0.5915, p < 0.0001) scores in CAP patients (Table 7). α1-AT levels increase with escalating disease severity.

Exploratory analysis: association between plasma α1-AT and pathogen subtypes

Sputum culture, alveolar lavage culture, or next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed in CAP patients before administration of antibiotics. After excluding normal colonizing bacteria, CAP patients were classified according to the dominant bacteria. A total of 51 patients in the CAP group were infected with clear pathogens, and 35 patients were infected with a single dominant bacterium (Table 8). Of the 35 patients, 2 patients showed viral infections, 11 patients had Gram-positive (G+) bacterial infections, 12 patients had Gram-negative (G−) bacterial infections, 7 patients showed mycoplasma infections, and 3 patients showed fungal infections.

The ability of plasma α1-AT level in predicting different infections was evaluated using the ROC curve analysis. As shown in Fig. 3B, AUC values were 0.8571 for mycoplasma infection (95% CI 0.7466–0.9677), 0.6443 for G− bacterial infection (95% CI 0.5003–0.7883), and 0.9479 for G+ bacterial infection (95% CI 0.8997–0.9961). The optimal thresholds of plasma α1-AT for G− bacterial infection, G+ bacterial infection, and mycoplasma infection were 337.5 nIU/ml (sensitivity 83.3%, specificity 52.4%), 482.5 nIU/ml (sensitivity 100%, specificity 83.0%), and 439.0 nIU/ml (sensitivity 85.7%, specificity 75.6%), respectively.

Discussion

This prospective study systematically investigated the clinical value of plasma α1-AT in CAP. Three principal findings were revealed: (1) Plasma α1-AT levels were elevated in CAP patients compared to healthy controls; (2) α1-AT exhibited positive correlations with pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNF-α) and validated severity assessment tools (CRB-65, CURB-65, SMART-COP); (3) In exploratory analyses, α1-AT indicated promising predictive accuracy for G+ bacterial infections, although its performance for other pathogen subtypes was limited. Taken together, these findings suggest that α1-AT may be a potential multidimensional biomarker in CAP, warranting further investigation in larger, well-defined cohorts.

α1-AT is a 52 kDa acute-phase glycoprotein demonstrating anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, anti-apoptotic, and serine protease inhibitory properties35. As a unique endogenous anti-inflammatory agent, α1-AT serves as a critical regulator modulating cytokine synthesis and release, potentially suppressing inflammatory cascades22,36. Exogenous α1-AT supplementation inhibits TNF-α production in murine RAW264.7 cells37, and significantly reduces IL-8, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β levels in human whole blood38. Consistent with this, the data from the present study showed that the median plasma α1-AT level in patients with CAP was 1.6-fold higher than that in the healthy control group, and it increased with the elevation of cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α.

CAP is associated with high morbidity and mortality, making early and systematic assessment of this disease of crucial importance. Validated clinical prediction rules, including CRB-65, CURB-65, and SMART-COP scores, are widely implemented for CAP risk stratification32–34,37. We found that as the CURB-65, CRB-65, and SMART-COP scores increased, the α1-AT level exhibited a progressive upward trend, with the strongest correlation observed with the CURB-65 score (r = 0.8653, p < 0.0001). Although the diagnostic accuracy of α1-AT (AUC 0.7643) was lower than that of the CURB-65 score (AUC 0.9010), it provides a laboratory-based quantitative alternative to clinical scoring systems—an advantage that is particularly prominent in scenarios where the objective assessment of indicators such as “confusion” or “respiratory rate” may be subject to subjectivity. This finding may have clinical significance, as early stratification of disease severity could inform decisions regarding hospital or ICU admission, potentially helping to reduce both undertreatment and overtreatment.

A notable finding of this exploratory analysis is that α1-AT exhibited promising performance in predicting G⁺ bacterial infections (AUC 0.9479, 95% CI 0.8997–0.9961), with an optimal cut-off value of 482.5 nIU/ml (sensitivity 100%, specificity 83.0%). In contrast, its predictive value for G⁻ bacterial infections was limited (AUC 0.6443). This pathogen-specific divergence likely reflects the distinct inflammatory programs triggered by G⁺ versus G⁻ bacteria. G⁺ pathogens such as Streptococcus pneumoniae and Staphylococcus aureus release cell-wall constituents—including peptidoglycan and lipoteichoic acid—that potently recruit and activate neutrophils, thereby up-regulating α1-AT synthesis to counteract protease-mediated lung injury36,39,40. Conversely, G⁻ bacteria (e.g., Klebsiella pneumoniae) primarily induce endotoxin-driven inflammatory responses, yet no functional antiprotease activity is detectable41. This difference may help optimize empirical antibiotic treatment regimens, but it needs to be validated in larger-sample cohort studies.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, some clinical data were collected retrospectively, which may introduce potential selection bias. Second, the lack of baseline inflammatory cytokine data from the healthy control group precludes a full assessment of the systemic inflammatory state in these individuals. Third, this study lacked a priori sample size calculation, and the sample size, particularly for pathogen-specific analyses, was relatively small. Consequently, we were unable to further subclassify specific pathogen subtypes or perform robust statistical analyses on viral infections, and the findings related to G+ bacterial discrimination should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. Fourth, this was a single-center study without external validation. Multicenter studies with larger, diverse cohorts are needed to confirm the generalizability of our findings. Fifth, we did not collect hard clinical outcomes such as ICU admission, need for mechanical ventilation, or mortality. Although α1-AT correlated with severity scores, severity scores are surrogate markers rather than true patient outcomes. The prognostic value of α1-AT for patient-centered outcomes remains unknown and warrants investigation in prospective studies designed specifically for this purpose.

Conclusions

This study indicates that plasma α1-AT levels are elevated in patients with CAP and correlate with disease severity, suggesting that α1-AT may serve as an adjunct biomarker for assessing the inflammatory burden. Furthermore, exploratory analyses revealed preliminary associations between α1-AT levels and specific pathogen types, particularly G+ bacterial and mycoplasma infections, warranting further investigation.
