Failure of salvage therapies in radiation-induced necrosis of a free fibula flap: a case report
Authors: Ziad Albash, Wajih Kashkash, Anton Sergeevich Sharapo, Zaderenko Igor Alexandrovich, Hassan Alsayed Hachem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Osteoradionecrosis (ORN) of a free fibula flap following mandibular reconstruction is a rare but severe complication in head and neck cancer survivors. Despite advances in microvascular reconstruction and radiotherapy protocols, late-onset ORN remains a therapeutic challenge with limited evidence guiding management.Case presentation:A 53-year-old male smoker presented 2 years post-resection of pT4aN1M0 (Stage IVA) oral floor squamous cell carcinoma, having undergone mandibular reconstruction with a free fibula flap and adjuvant radiotherapy (66 Gy). Routine CT surveillance at 1 year revealed asymptomatic radiation-induced changes in the flap. By 2 years, he developed pain, trismus, swelling, titanium bar extrusion, and large sequestra. Biopsy confirmed ORN. Initial conservative therapy – including antibiotics, NSAIDs, and pentoxifylline with α-tocopherol – failed. Subsequent sequestrectomy provided only transient relief, with recurrent sequestra and persistent symptoms. Ultimately, the fibula flap was completely removed, and the patient declined a second free flap; reconstruction was completed with a pectoralis major myocutaneous flap, resulting in an uneventful recovery.Clinical discussion:This case underscores the aggressive nature of ORN in vascularized bone flaps and the limitations of conventional salvage therapies.Conclusions:When conservative and surgical salvage fail, prompt flap excision and alternative reconstruction may be necessary. This case provides longitudinal, image-supported documentation of sequential treatment failure and patient-driven deviation from standard free flap salvage.

Introduction

Osteoradionecrosis (ORN) is indeed a serious late complication of radiotherapy for head and neck cancer, characterized by exposed, necrotic bone that fails to heal over at least 3 months in the absence of recurrent tumor[1,2]. The pathophysiology involves radiation-induced tissue changes, including hypoxia, hypovascularity, and fibrosis[2,3]. Free fibula flaps have become the gold standard for mandibular reconstruction due to their reliable vascular pedicle, sufficient bone length for extensive defects, and bicortical structure that facilitates osseointegration with dental implants[6–8]. The independent blood supply of vascularized bone grafts like the fibula flap is theorized to make them more resistant to radiation-induced damage compared to native mandibular bone[9,10]. However, emerging evidence confirms that even well-vascularized free flaps are not immune to the cumulative effects of high-dose radiotherapy[10,11]. Adjuvant radiotherapy is consistently associated with a marked reduction in long-term dental implant survival within fibula flaps[6,11], with one study showing 5-year implant survival rates of 73.8% in irradiated fibulae compared to 91.8% in non-irradiated cases[11]. This risk is particularly pronounced when radiation doses exceed 60 Gy[10]. Furthermore, patient-specific risk factors, such as continued tobacco use, significantly increase the risk of postoperative wound complications and reoperation, which can jeopardize the reconstruction[12].

Although several treatment algorithms exist for ORN of the native mandible – including conservative medical management (e.g., pentoxifylline and tocopherol, or PENTO), sequestrectomy, and hyperbaric oxygen therapy – there is no consensus on managing ORN specifically within free fibula flaps[13,14]. The paucity of reported cases and the unpredictable response to standard therapies pose significant clinical dilemmas, particularly when reconstructive failure threatens both function and quality of life[8,13,15].

This article presents a rare case of late-onset ORN affecting a free fibula flap used for mandibular reconstruction after chemoradiotherapy for oral squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). Despite multimodal salvage attempts – including medical therapy, sequestrectomy, and delayed surgical intervention – the condition progressed relentlessly, ultimately necessitating complete flap removal. We aim to highlight the limitations of current therapeutic approaches, emphasize the need for vigilant post-radiotherapy surveillance, and advocate for early consideration of alternative reconstructive strategies when vascularized bone flaps show signs of radiation injury. Unlike native mandibular ORN, the clinical behavior and treatment response of ORN within free fibula flaps remain poorly characterized in the literature. This case specifically documents the asymptomatic radiographic onset, sequential failure of multimodal salvage, and successful non–free-flap rescue in a single patient. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria[16].

Case presentation

Patient history and initial diagnosis

A 53-year-old male with a significant history of active tobacco use presented to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery (OMFS) with a 2-year history of combined modality treatment for SCC of the floor of the mouth (pathologic stage pT4aN1M0, Stage IVA). Initial management included en bloc resection of the primary tumor with segmental mandibulectomy, followed by immediate reconstruction using a free vascularized fibula flap. Postoperatively, the patient received adjuvant external beam radiotherapy to a total dose of 66 Gy without concurrent chemotherapy, in accordance with institutional protocols.

Postoperative surveillance and early radiographic findings

Following reconstruction, the patient entered a structured surveillance program involving clinical evaluations and contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) scans of the head and neck every 3–6 months. At the 1-year postoperative mark, routine imaging revealed early signs of radiation-induced changes within the fibula flap, including reduced medullary density and subtle cortical irregularities. Despite these radiographic abnormalities, the patient remained asymptomatic and exhibited no clinical evidence of infection, exposure, or functional impairment (Fig. 1). Clinical deterioration and diagnosis of osteoradionecrosis

Two years after the initial reconstruction, the patient developed progressive symptoms, including persistent pain, facial swelling, trismus, and extrusion of the titanium reconstruction bar through the intraoral mucosa. Imaging demonstrated extensive osteolytic changes and large, well-demarcated sequestra within the fibula segment. An incisional biopsy of the exposed bone confirmed the diagnosis of ORN, with histopathology revealing necrotic bone lacking viable osteocytes and no evidence of tumor recurrence (Fig. 2). Treatment course and outcomes

Initial management consisted of conservative therapy, including broad-spectrum antibiotics, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and a combination regimen of pentoxifylline (400 mg twice daily) and α-tocopherol (vitamin E, 800 IU daily). Despite 4–6 weeks of medical treatment, the patient showed no clinical improvement. A surgical sequestrectomy was then performed to remove nonviable bone fragments; however, within 3 months, new sequestra formed, and symptoms of pain and swelling recurred. Given the failure of both medical and limited surgical interventions, the multidisciplinary team recommended complete excision of the compromised fibula flap (Fig. 3). The patient declined a second microvascular free bone flap due to concerns about surgical burden and potential repeat complications. Instead, definitive reconstruction was achieved using a pectoralis major myocutaneous flap. The patient recovered well postoperatively, with complete wound healing and no complications at the 2-week follow-up, and was discharged in stable condition (Fig. 3). Discussion

The development of ORN in a free fibula flap challenges the long-held assumption that vascularized bone grafts are inherently resistant to radiation injury[17]. Although the fibula flap is celebrated for its robust blood supply and structural reliability in mandibular reconstruction, this case demonstrates that it is not immune to the cumulative microvascular and cellular damage caused by high-dose radiotherapy (66 Gy)[18]. Radiation induces endothelial dysfunction, tissue hypoxia, and fibrosis even in well-perfused tissues[17], particularly when compounded by ongoing tobacco use – a known independent risk factor for impaired wound healing and microcirculatory compromise[19]. This suggests that the “protected” status of free flaps may be overstated in the context of aggressive adjuvant therapy[8].

The clinical progression in this patient – from asymptomatic radiographic changes at 1 year to florid sequestration and hardware extrusion at 2 years – highlights the insidious nature of ORN in reconstructed bone, which can progress over many months or even years[2]. Unlike native mandibular ORN, which often follows dental extraction or trauma, radiation-induced necrosis in a free flap may evolve silently until catastrophic failure occurs[20]. The absence of early symptoms, such as pain or exposed bone, can delay therapeutic intervention, allowing extensive devitalization of the graft to develop[2]. This supports the notion that routine surveillance imaging in high-risk patients should not only assess for tumor recurrence but also actively screen for early signs of bone viability loss, such as osteolytic lesions or cortical erosions visible on CT scans, even in the absence of clinical complaints[21].

Current management protocols for ORN – such as the pentoxifylline-tocopherol-clodronate (PENTOCLO) regimen or surgical sequestrectomy – are largely extrapolated from studies on native mandibular bone, and their efficacy in vascularized bone flaps remains unproven[22]. In the case of free flap ORN, both medical therapy and limited surgical debridement can fail to halt disease progression, as this therapeutic resistance underscores a critical knowledge gap[23]. The pathophysiology of ORN in free flaps may differ fundamentally from that in native bone, potentially involving flap-specific factors such as pedicle vulnerability, altered biomechanics, or compromised peri-implant soft tissue envelopes[20,23]. Consequently, standard algorithms derived from experience with the native mandible are likely insufficient for this distinct subset of patients[23].

The ultimate decision to excise the entire fibula flap represents a significant reconstructive turning point, often pursued to eliminate the source of chronic inflammation and recurrent sequestration[17]. Although radical, this approach was necessary to control the disease process and relieve patient symptoms[17]. The patient’s refusal of a second free bone flap – understandable given prior complications and surgical burden – led to successful salvage with a pectoralis major myocutaneous (PMM) flap[24]. Although this salvage option sacrifices bony continuity and future dental rehabilitation, it provides reliable soft tissue coverage and functional stability[24]. This outcome reinforces the importance of tailoring reconstructive goals to individual patient priorities, especially in salvage settings where quality of life may outweigh structural ideals[25].

This case also raises important questions about prophylactic strategies, as the prevention of ORN in free-flap populations requires further investigation[23]. Emerging approaches, such as prophylactic hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT), remain controversial and lack robust evidence in free-flap populations, with studies showing limited benefits[26,27]. Similarly, the use of radioprotective agents like amifostine, while showing promise in preclinical models for protecting vascularity, lacks strong clinical evidence for preventing ORN in free-flap patients[28–30]. More promising may be early intervention at the first sign of radiographic change – before sequestration occurs – including aggressive smoking cessation, which is a key modifiable risk factor for flap ORN[23]. Optimization of nutritional status is also a critical component of ORN prevention and management. Future research should explore whether early-stage ORN in free flaps responds better to multimodal regimens than late-stage disease, as advanced ORN is associated with more complex healing challenges.

Interestingly, the growing application of large language models (LLMs), such as ChatGPT, in healthcare scenarios offers new opportunities for synthesizing unstructured clinical data from rare case reports. A recent machine learning-driven informatics study by Guo et al. mapped the global research landscape of ChatGPT across six major healthcare clusters, including clinical decision support, medical education, and data processing with safety considerations[31]. Among these, clinical decision support and health management emerged as a core cluster, highlighting the potential of LLMs to assist clinicians in organizing diagnostic thoughts, integrating information, and supporting systematic clinical reasoning – particularly in complex, low-incidence conditions such as ORN of free flaps[31]. While challenges remain regarding reliability, interpretability, and data privacy, future research may explore whether LLM-based pattern recognition can help identify early predictors of treatment failure in flap ORN by aggregating findings from multiple case reports. This case, therefore, not only documents a rare clinical course but also underscores the potential value of AI-assisted tools in transforming individual case experiences into collective, actionable knowledge.

Although individual elements of ORN in free flaps have been reported, this case offers three specific contributions not previously assembled in a single report. First, it provides longitudinal imaging correlation from an asymptomatic stage (1 year post-radiotherapy) to complete flap failure, reinforcing that radiographic changes alone warrant proactive intervention. Second, it documents the sequential failure of three salvage approaches – medical therapy (PENTO), surgical sequestrectomy, and delayed observation – in the same patient, demonstrating that treatment resistance in flap ORN can be progressive rather than isolated. Third, it describes a patient-driven decision to decline a second free flap, with successful salvage using a pectoralis major myocutaneous flap, offering practical guidance for shared decision-making in irradiated patients who wish to avoid further microsurgery.

Strengths and limitations of this report include its detailed longitudinal clinical documentation and clear illustration of therapeutic failures and eventual resolution. The consistent imaging follow-up and histologic confirmation of ORN enhance diagnostic validity. However, as a single-case report, it cannot establish causality or generalizability. The absence of pre- and post-radiotherapy dosimetry data for the fibula flap limits our understanding of the precise radiation burden. Additionally, patient-reported outcome measures (e.g., pain scores, functional assessments) were not formally collected, which would have strengthened the evaluation of treatment impact.

Lessons learned from this case are multifaceted. First, vascularized bone flaps, though superior to non-vascularized grafts, are not invulnerable to radiation injury – especially in smokers receiving high-dose radiotherapy. Second, asymptomatic radiographic changes in irradiated flaps warrant close monitoring and early multidisciplinary discussion, rather than passive observation. Third, when conservative and limited surgical measures fail, timely excision of nonviable bone and adoption of alternative reconstructive strategies (e.g., pedicled soft tissue flaps) can prevent prolonged morbidity. Finally, shared decision-making is essential: respecting patient autonomy in declining further complex microsurgery led to a pragmatic and successful outcome. This case advocates for a paradigm shift – from salvage to prevention, and from structural perfection to functional resilience.

Conclusion

This case underscores that free fibula flaps, despite their vascularized nature, are not immune to ORN following high-dose radiotherapy, particularly in active smokers. The failure of both conservative medical management and limited surgical interventions highlights the aggressive and refractory course ORN can take in reconstructed bone. Early recognition through vigilant radiographic surveillance – even in asymptomatic patients – is critical to prompt intervention. When salvage strategies prove ineffective, timely excision of the necrotic flap and transition to alternative reconstructive options, such as pedicled soft tissue flaps, may be necessary to halt disease progression and restore function. This case advocates for a proactive, individualized approach that prioritizes patient-centered outcomes over reconstructive idealism in complex post-radiotherapy scenarios. By documenting sequential salvage failures and a successful alternative pathway, this report provides a practical, image-supported reference for clinicians managing refractory ORN in free fibula flaps.
