Prognostic Role of Acute-on-Chronic Liver Failure in Acute Variceal Bleeding: A Multicenter Retrospective Study in Australia
Authors: Rajiv Kurup, Huu Hoang Hiep Dao, Pei-Yu Lin, Scott Read, Wai-See Ma, Jacob George, Golo Ahlenstiel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimsAcute variceal bleeding (AVB) is a life-threatening complication of cirrhosis that can precipitate acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF), leading to poor outcomes. This multicenter retrospective study evaluated the impact of ACLF on the long-term outcomes of patients admitted with AVB and compared the performance of prognostic scores in predicting 1-year mortality.MethodsA total of 168 patients with cirrhosis admitted with AVB between January 2013 and December 2022 were included. ACLF was diagnosed using the Chronic Liver Failure-Consortium Organ Failure (CLIF-C OF) score at admission and reassessed at 48 hours. Prognostic scores including the Child-Pugh, AIMS65, and CLIF-C model scores were evaluated. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to identify predictors of 6-week rebleeding, and Kaplan-Meier analysis was conducted to assess survival outcomes.ResultsACLF was present at admission in 21 patients (12.5%) and developed within 48 hours in two (1.2%). Although ACLF at admission was associated with a higher unadjusted 1-year mortality rate, it was not independently predictive after adjusting for hepatic reserve (Child-Pugh score) and age (hazard ratio, 0.97; 95% confidence interval, 0.32 to 2.94; p=0.951). The Child-Pugh score was the strongest predictor of the 1-year mortality rate (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve=0.718). The absence of hepatic encephalopathy independently predicted 6-week rebleeding (p=0.015). Endoscopy timing (<12 hours vs ≥12 hours) did not influence the 6-week mortality rate, rebleeding, or the 1-year survival rate in either the ACLF or non-ACLF group.ConclusionsAlthough ACLF identifies patients with AVB at high risk, it primarily reflects the severity of the underlying liver dysfunction rather than serving as an independent predictor. Management should prioritize stabilization and optimization prior to endoscopy and individualized follow-up to evaluate transplant candidacy.

INTRODUCTION

Acute variceal bleeding (AVB) represents a severe form of cirrhosis decompensation, with rebleeding rates exceeding 20% and a notable impact on prognosis.1,2 AVB can result in acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF), a distinct clinical entity first coined by the European Association for the Study of Liver-Chronic Liver Failure (EASL-CLIF) Consortium during the CANONIC (Chronic Liver Failure Consortium Acute-on-Chronic Liver Failure in Cirrhosis study) study.3,4 ACLF occurs in about 30% of patients with cirrhosis admitted with acute decompensation (AD) and is characterized by intense systemic inflammation with failure of other organ systems and is linked to elevated early mortality rates. ACLF complicates about 12% to 20% of AVBs and carries a 6-week mortality of approximately 48%.4-6 Therefore, identifying high-risk patients early may help guide timely interventions, including transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS) placement or liver transplantation.7,8 Recent studies have highlighted the substantial influence of ACLF on early mortality in patients with AVB, showing markedly higher mortality compared to those without ACLF.9,10 Studies led by Trebicka and Terres have examined 12-month mortality but have not investigated outcomes with longer-term follow-up.11,12 Traditional liver-specific scores such as the Child-Pugh and Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) scores, have proven effective in predicting mortality in patients with AVB.13,14 However, there is emerging evidence that other scoring systems, such as AIMS65, which have been mainly validated in non-variceal bleeding settings is also useful in predicting mortality in AVB.15 Additionally, other CLIF-Consortium (CLIF-C) scores such as the CLIF-C Organ Failure (CLIF-C OF) score used to diagnose ACLF in patients presenting with AD and the CLIF-C AD (CLIF-C AD) score developed to predict mortality in patients with AD, have not been studied in the longer-term prognosis of patients with AVB.16,17

In this study, we aimed to investigate longer-term outcomes of patients admitted with AVB, particularly the impact of the presence of ACLF. We also sought to (1) evaluate the prognostic accuracy of scoring systems for 1-year mortality, (2) assess risk factors for 6-week rebleeding, and (3) evaluate the impact of endoscopy timing on clinical outcomes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients with cirrhosis admitted with AVB at Westmead and Blacktown-Mount Druitt Hospitals between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2022, were included in this multicenter retrospective study. The diagnosis of cirrhosis was established through clinicopathological evaluation or histological confirmation via liver biopsy. Endoscopic confirmation of AVB was performed based on Baveno II.18 Exclusion criteria included the following: no endoscopy to confirm AVB, endoscopy-confirmed non-variceal bleeding, hepatocellular carcinoma, and incomplete medical records. Follow-up periods were assessed from the date of initial admission to the date of death or loss of follow-up or until the censor date December 31, 2023. Ethical approval was obtained from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Western Sydney Local Health District (Ref: 2021/ETH00149).

1. Data collection

Data on demographics, liver disease etiology, laboratory results, endoscopic findings, hospital stay, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and patient outcomes were extracted from electronic medical records. Ascites was identified via ultrasound and graded per International Ascites Club criteria. The severity of hepatic encephalopathy (HE) was graded using the West-Haven criteria.

2. Calculation of prognostic scores

Liver-related scores, namely Child-Pugh score, MELD, MELD-sodium (MELD-Na), aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet ratio index, and fibrosis-4 index, were calculated according to their original formulae.14,19-22 The FIPS score, initially introduced to assess post-TIPS survival, has been shown to predict 1-year mortality in patients with AD.23,24 We also included the neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio, given the role of systemic inflammation in the development of ACLF and the AIMS65 score which was created to determine the prognosis of patients with upper gastrointestinal bleeding.25,26

ACLF at admission was defined based on EASL-CLIF criteria and classified using the CLIF-C OF score. The CLIF-C OF score defines failure of each organ system and stratifies ACLF into AD (non-ACLF) and grades 1, 2 and 3. As per Moreau et al.,4 organ failures were defined as follows: liver failure as total bilirubin ≥205.2 µmol/L (≥12 mg/dL); coagulation failure as international normalized ratio (INR) ≥2.5; kidney failure as serum creatinine >176.8 µmol/L (>2 mg/dL) or need for renal replacement therapy; circulatory failure as requirement for vasopressors to maintain blood pressure; respiratory failure as arterial oxygen partial pressure to fraction of inspired oxygen ≤200, peripheral oxygen saturation to fraction of inspired oxygen ≤214, or mechanical ventilation unrelated to airway protection in the absence of grade III/IV HE; and brain failure as grade III or IV HE, based on the West-Haven criteria.4 ACLF status was reassessed at 48 hours and patients who met this criterion only at this point were defined as “new ACLF at 48 hours.” Classification was based on retrospective review of clinical and laboratory data. Adjudication was not blinded, which is acknowledged as a limitation.

AD was defined as: (1) no organ failure; (2) isolated non-renal organ failure with creatinine <132.6 µmol/L (<1.5 mg/dL) and absence of HE; (3) isolated neurological failure with creatinine <132.6 µmol/L (<1.5 mg/dL). Grade 1 ACLF was defined as: (1) isolated kidney failure; (2) single failure of the liver, coagulation, circulatory, or respiratory systems, accompanied by a serum creatinine level between 132.6 and 168 µmol/L (1.5–1.9 mg/dL) and/or mild to moderate HE; or (3) isolated cerebral failure with a serum creatinine level in the same range. Grade 2 and grade 3 ACLF were defined by the presence of two and three or more organ failures, respectively.

The CLIF-C AD and CLIF-C ACLF are additional scores proposed to predict mortality 28-day, 90-day, 180-day, and 365-day for AD and ACLF patients, respectively.16,17 These scores, along with the CLIF-C OF, were recalculated at 48 hours into admission to capture disease progression during the critical early phase. The CLIF-C AD was calculated using the formula: CLIF-C AD=10×(0.03×age [years]+0.66×ln (creatinine [mg/dL])+1.71×ln (INR)+0.88×ln (white blood cell count [×109 cells/L]) – 0.05×(sodium [mmol/L])+8). The CLIF-C ACLF score was calculated by combining the CLIF-C OF score, age, and white blood cell count with the following formula: CLIF-C ACLF score=10×(0.33×CLIF-C OF score+0.04×age [years]+0.63×ln (white blood cell count [×109 cells/L])–2).

3. Statistical analyses

Baseline demographic, laboratory, and endoscopic data were systematically recorded using a standardized proforma. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.1.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of numerical variables was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Numerical variables with normal or skew distribution were presented as mean±standard deviation or median with interquartile range, respectively. Normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed using the Student t-test, whereas non-normally distributed data were assessed with the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical data were described as numbers (percentages) and compared via chi-square analysis. Multivariable analyses were performed with Cox proportional hazards regression for 1-year mortality. Clinically relevant covariates (age, sex, ACLF status, and Child-Pugh score) were considered for inclusion. To minimize collinearity, MELD and Child-Pugh were not entered simultaneously. Logistic regression for 6-week rebleeding was performed and variables with a univariate association at p<0.05 were considered for inclusion. Final models were constructed using backward elimination, retaining variables with p<0.05. Results were reported as hazard ratios (HRs) or odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Subgroup analyses stratified patients by ACLF status (present at admission vs new at 48 hours vs none) and by timing of endoscopy (<12 hours vs ≥12 hours). A 2×2 stratified analysis (ACLF vs no ACLF×early vs delayed endoscopy) was performed for short-term outcomes (6-week mortality and rebleeding). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to assess the predictive performance of prognostic scores for 1-year mortality. The optimal cutoff value was determined using the maximum Youden’s index. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were calculated and reported with 95% CIs. Cumulative survival curves were plotted with the Kaplan-Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. The level of statistical significance was set to p<0.05.

RESULTS

This study enrolled 168 cirrhotic patients presenting with AVB who met predefined inclusion criteria. Baseline demographic and clinical data are summarized in Table 1. The majority were male (72.6%), with a mean age of 59.6±13.6 years. Alcohol-related liver disease was the most common cause of cirrhosis (38.1%), followed by hepatitis C infection (22.0%) and metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (20.8%). Twenty-one patients (12.5%) had ACLF at admission (57.1%, 38.1% and 4.8% for grade 1, grade 2 and grade 3, respectively). Two (1.2%) developed new ACLF at 48 hours, and 145 (86.3%) had no ACLF at any point. Due to the small number of patients with new ACLF at 48 hours, they were described separately but excluded from formal subgroup survival analyses. Patients with and without ACLF had similar baseline characteristics regarding sex and cirrhosis etiology, though males were more common in the ACLF group. Endoscopy findings demonstrated no significant difference between source of variceal bleeding, proportion of grade III esophageal varices or presence of high-risk stigma between the two groups. Patients with ACLF exhibited higher white cell counts, C-reactive protein, bilirubin and creatinine at admission and at 48 hours, albumin, aspartate transaminase, and INR (Table 2).

As anticipated, ascites and HE were more common at admission among patients with ACLF. All prognosis scores were higher in the ACLF group, except the neutrophil-lymphocyte ratio (Table 3). Additionally, ACLF patients had longer lengths of stay, more ICU admissions and shorter time to endoscopy than the non-ACLF group. Overall, 17 patients (10.1%) died within 6 months, and 29 patients (17.3%) died within 1 year. Patients with ACLF had significantly higher mortality rates at both 6 months and 1 year compared to those without ACLF (6-month: 23.8% vs 7.5%, p=0.006; 1-year: 33.3% vs 15.0%, p=0.027) (Table 3).

1. Cumulative survival rates of cirrhosis patients hospitalized with AVB

Median follow-up period for all study subjects was 55.1 months. During the 1-year follow-up, seven patients with ACLF and 22 without ACLF died, resulting in significantly lower cumulative 1-year survival in the ACLF group compared to those without ACLF (65.0% vs 82.9%, p=0.035) (Fig. 1A). Over the entire study period, patients in ACLF again showed a lower survival rate compared to those not in ACLF (36.8% vs 52.2%, p=0.039) (Fig. 1B).

2. Multivariable analysis of 1-year mortality

In unadjusted analyses, the presence of ACLF at admission was associated with elevated 1-year mortality. However, in the Cox regression model including age, gender, and Child-Pugh score, ACLF at admission was no longer an independent predictor (HR, 0.97; 95% CI, 0.32 to 2.94; p=0.951). In contrast, age (HR, 1.04 per year; 95% CI 1.01 to 1.08; p=0.005) and Child-Pugh score (HR, 1.34 per point; 95% CI, 1.10 to 1.62; p=0.003) remained significant predictors, while gender was not (HR, 1.24; 95% CI, 0.52 to 2.94; p=0.630). Additional details can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

3. Timing of endoscopy and short-term outcomes

When stratified by ACLF status and timing of endoscopy, there were no significant differences in short-term outcomes. Six-week mortality was similar in early versus delayed endoscopy groups, both in ACLF patients and non-ACLF patients (7.7% vs 12.5% and 4.2% vs 3.5%, p=0.869). Rebleeding within 6 weeks also did not differ significantly (23.1% vs 12.5% and 15.4% vs 23.4%, p=0.638). These findings are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

4. Cumulative survival rates according to timing of endoscopy

In the non-ACLF group, there was no significant difference in 1-year survival between patients who underwent endoscopy within 12 hours and those who underwent endoscopy after 12 hours (78.7% vs 85.4%, p=0.299) (Fig. 2A). Similarly, in the ACLF group, 1-year survival rates did not significantly differ between early and delayed endoscopy (61.5% vs 71.4%, p=0.790) (Fig. 2B).

5. Risk factors associated with 6-week rebleeding for cirrhosis patients with AVB

Univariable logistic regression was performed to identify factors associated with 6-week rebleeding among all AVB patients. HE (p=0.025), bilirubin at 48 hours (p=0.045), AIMS65 (p=0.045), and the Child-Pugh score (p=0.020) were significantly associated (Table 4). Following multivariable logistic regression analysis, HE (p=0.015) was the sole independent predictor of 6-week rebleeding (Table 5).

6. Six-week rebleeding rates according to timing of endoscopy

There was no significant difference in 6-week rebleeding between early and delayed endoscopy (16.9% vs 22.5%, p=0.436). Mean rebleeding-free survival was 36.9 days in both groups (Supplementary Fig. 1).

7. Performance of prognostic scores for 1-year mortality

The ROC curves of the various prognostic scores are compared in Fig. 3. The prognostic scores were evaluated for their ability in predicting 1-year mortality and calculations were based on cutoff values generated from the ROC curves. The performances of all prognostic scores are described in Table 6. The area under the ROC curve (AUROC) was highest for the Child-Pugh score at 0.718 (p<0.001) with an optimal cutoff of 6.5 and a p-value in the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test of 0.073. Among all the scoring systems, the Child-Pugh score demonstrated the highest sensitivity and negative predictive value. The presence of ACLF at 48 hours had the highest specificity and positive likelihood ratio, albeit without reaching statistical significance. Among the CLIF-C scores, the CLIF-C AD at 48 hours had the best sensitivity (66.7%) and specificity (63%).

DISCUSSION

In this multicenter retrospective study, we investigated the implications of ACLF on longer-term outcomes in cirrhosis patients presenting with AVB and found that ACLF may serve as a high-risk clinical marker. The 1-year and overall mortality rates in ACLF patients were significantly higher compared to those without ACLF, and the Child-Pugh score was found to most reliably predict 1-year mortality in all AVBs compared to a variety of other prognostic scores.

ACLF is marked by systemic inflammation and a rapid decline in organ function, resulting in multisystem failure and high early mortality.4 In this study, the white blood cell count, C-reactive protein, and aspartate transaminase levels were significantly higher in ACLF patients which is consistent with the current theory that an elevated inflammatory response is a key driver in the pathogenesis of ACLF. Unsurprisingly, the presence of ACLF is linked to poorer outcomes in patients with AVB. Previous studies have shown that ACLF significantly worsens early mortality outcomes in patients with AVB, with higher mortality rates observed at 28 days, 6 weeks, and 90 days compared to those without ACLF.9,10 To date, 1-year mortality has only been examined in two other studies. Our study is unique in this context as it included a minimum follow-up of 12 months and a median follow-up of 55.1 months. The 1-year mortality rate in our ACLF cohort was 36.4% and was considerably lower than previous studies (55% and 77.5%), likely due to the exclusion of hepatocellular carcinoma patients in our study.11,12

Our adjusted Cox regression clarified that ACLF at admission, though identifying patients at high risk, lost statistical significance for long-term mortality after adjustment for age and hepatic reserve (Child-Pugh score). This finding suggests that ACLF represents a surrogate of advanced hepatic decompensation rather than a direct causal factor. Clinically, this underscores the importance of viewing ACLF as a dynamic marker of disease severity that should guide early risk stratification and management intensity.

As the CLIF-C ACLF score has demonstrated improved accuracy in predicting mortality when measured at 48 hours,16 we included various other scoring systems including CLIF-C AD and CLIF-OF assessed at the same time point to evaluate study outcomes. After comparison with these prognostic scores, the Child-Pugh score emerged as the best performing score in predicting 1-year mortality in patients with AVB and was the only score with an AUROC of >0.7. This is likely because it incorporates biochemical and clinical parameters that are lacking in other scores, such as ascites and HE which are established manifestations of portal hypertension. The Child-Pugh score likely offers a more complete evaluation of liver function, enhancing its utility in predicting long-term mortality among patients with cirrhosis. Indeed, mortality is more likely to be influenced by long-term effects of portal hypertension, particularly ascites and hepatorenal syndrome.

To identify risk factors for 6-week rebleeding, numerous variables including biochemical parameters and prognostic scores were analyzed. Interestingly, multivariable logistic regression demonstrated a negative association between the presence of HE at admission and 6-week rebleeding. Over half (55%) of patients with HE were in ACLF and admitted to the ICU, suggesting that greater clinical attention and monitoring may have influenced rebleeding risk. However, the small number of ACLF cases (n=21) may have limited statistical power to detect a true association. Additionally, potential confounding related to intensive care management and treatment variability should be acknowledged, as our dataset did not capture detailed intervention data (e.g., transfusion thresholds, vasoactive agent use and dosing, or repeat endoscopy frequency). Therefore, this finding should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and interpreted cautiously, rather than as evidence of a protective effect of ICU management.

A further important observation from this study was the lack of clinical benefit of endoscopy performed within 12 hours of admission, even after accounting for patients with ACLF at admission who had a median admission to endoscopy time of 3.6 hours. This contrasts with current guidelines which recommend performing endoscopy for variceal bleeding within 12 hours.27,28 A large prospective multicenter study involving 3,319 patients by Zhang et al.29 found that the timing of endoscopy (either <12 hours or 12–24 hours) made no difference in the incidence of treatment failure within 5 days in patients with AVB, however there was a higher in-hospital mortality rate in the <12-hour group. Collectively, these findings highlight the importance of adequate resuscitation, medical management and sufficient time for pre-endoscopic optimization of the patient's clinical state, as well as allowing enough time to improve the quality of the endoscopic examination through adequate fasting and the use of prokinetics to clear the esophagus and stomach of food and blood. These findings challenge current guidelines and suggest that pre-endoscopic optimization may take precedence over early endoscopy, particularly in high-risk ACLF patients.

ACLF defines a group of high-risk patients with increased long-term mortality who may require organ support in an ICU or rescue interventions such as pre-emptive TIPS. They should also have careful follow-up to assess eligibility for liver transplantation, underscoring the need for individualized and multidisciplinary management strategies. As far as we are aware, we are the first to investigate long-term mortality in patients presenting with AVB in the presence of ACLF at admission and dynamic reassessment at 48 hours, with a minimum follow-up period of 1-year and a median follow-up of 55.1 months. This provides critical insights into the long-term outcomes of this high-risk population. A stringent definition of AVB based on the Baveno II definitions was used to ensure accuracy and consistency in patient identification. Additionally, the evaluation of patient deterioration/improvement during the early critical period was made possible by the dynamic recalculation of important prognostic scores, such as CLIF-C ACLF, CLIF-C AD, and CLIF-C OF score 48 hours post-admission. By incorporating a variety of current prognostic scores, this study provides a thorough evaluation of these scores, highlighting their relative performance in this patient population. This study has certain limitations. First, its retrospective design inherently carries a risk of selection bias. Additionally, the subjective diagnosis of HE may have been affected by observer bias. Another limitation was the small ACLF subgroup, which limited certain analyses. Despite these limitations, the multicenter design, detailed statistical modelling, and extended follow-up period strengthen the robustness and clinical relevance of our findings.

In conclusion, ACLF should be regarded as a high-risk clinical marker reflecting impaired hepatic reserve and systemic inflammation, rather than an independent prognostic driver. Among the prognostic models evaluated, the Child-Pugh score demonstrated superior performance in predicting 1-year mortality in all patients with AVB, confirming the long-term implications of portal hypertension. Nonetheless, ACLF remains clinically relevant as it identifies patients with AD at high risk of early deterioration and mortality. Management should be tailored to the presence or absence of ACLF, with early consideration of interventions such as pre-emptive TIPS and timely liver transplant assessment.
