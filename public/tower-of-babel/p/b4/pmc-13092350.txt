Testosterone, cortisol, and fNIRS‐based cortical activation associated with competitive task persistence and difficulty
Authors: Kathleen V Casto, Dale J Cohen, Cameron Hicks, Andrew Bowser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMotivation for social or resource‐related rewards is regulated by areas of the brain that control executive functioning and regulate attention, including the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and temporoparietal junction (TPJ). Testosterone and cortisol are two steroid hormones that influence behaviors related to motivation in social competition and are thought to do so via their independent and interactive effects on these same brain networks. Yet there remains relatively limited evidence for functional hormone–brain correspondence during status contests in humans. In ~120–130 participants, we measured frontal‐temporal cortical patterns of neural activity via functional near‐infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS), salivary testosterone and cortisol, and task performance in a competitive key‐pressing contest for rewards in which the cognitive difficulty of the task was varied. Participants completed the task under one of three conditions for incentivizing performance: a cash prize, positive social judgement, or negative social judgement. The competitive task was associated with increased neural activity bilaterally across the PFC and decreased TPJ activity, especially as task difficulty increased. Individuals who performed better showed greater frontal cortical activation overall and were more likely to have increasing testosterone across the task, but only if cortisol levels simultaneously declined. While hormone change across the task had limited direct ties to brain activity, basal testosterone predicted right vlPFC activation, while the interaction of basal testosterone and cortisol predicted activity in the right TPJ depending on task difficulty. Incentive condition had no clear effects on patterns of brain activity or hormone–brain relationships. These findings support an emerging model of testosterone and cortisol's influence on implicit brain processes underlying attention and goal salience when pursuing social goals. More broadly, this research raises new directions for understanding the neuroendocrine mechanisms behind social and reward‐seeking behavior.Keywords: cortisol, dual‐hormone, fNIRS, testosterone, competition

INTRODUCTION

Motivation for social or resource‐related rewards is regulated by complex brain networks that connect sensory‐perceptual processing, integrate prior history, assign reward value, and regulate downstream attentional resources and behavioral effort. These networks are thought to be influenced by steroid hormones, including testosterone and cortisol, which modulate the adaptive brain and body response to social stimuli. 1 Despite decades of work on the hormone–brain interplay in non‐human animal models, less is known about how these hormones influence brain processes underlying social motivation in humans. For example, a foundational assumption in behavioral neuroendocrinology is that basal hormone levels or rapid hormonal responses to social encounters facilitate adaptive behavioral responses in real time by altering neural activity in the brain. Yet there is limited evidence linking endogenous hormonal exposure to patterns of neural activity during socially relevant motivation tasks. The current study aims to address these gaps by testing how basal and reactive levels of testosterone and cortisol relate to competitive motivation under different incentive schemas and the related patterns of functional neural activity.

A brief primer on testosterone and cortisol physiology in humans

Testosterone is a steroid hormone synthesized by the gonads, adrenal glands, and via peripheral conversion in target tissues in both women and men. 2 , 3 , 4 Basal—or baseline—levels are significantly higher in adult males due to the greater contribution from specialized testicular cells that produce relatively higher quantities. Cortisol is also a steroid hormone but is synthesized and released primarily from the cortex of the adrenal gland, in similar quantities in women and men. 5 , 6 Testosterone and cortisol are regulated by the brain and, in turn, regulate brain functioning. Bioavailable levels of these hormones diffuse readily into brain tissue where testosterone binds at androgen receptors and cortisol at glucocorticoid and mineralocorticoid receptors throughout the brain, 7 , 8 , 9 including in areas involved in social motivation/reward, attentional salience, emotion‐processing, and cognition. 3 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 These connections—between hormone signaling and functional areas of the brain—have largely been established in animal models; evidence in humans remains relatively limited.

Most of the circulating portions of both hormones are strongly bound to blood proteins. 14 , 15 Unbound portions of these steroids are considered “free” or available for passive or active transport to target tissues including the brain. 16 , 17 , 18 Saliva sourced steroid hormone concentrations reflect the unbound portions of the total circulating load, as salivary glands receive these molecules once they are free to diffuse (or be transported) into peripheral tissues. 19 For this reason, and the relative ease of collecting saliva over blood, salivary levels of testosterone and cortisol are commonly used in neuroscientific and biobehavioral research.

Basal and reactive testosterone and cortisol in relation to social behavior

Salivary or blood levels of testosterone and cortisol are thought to reflect individual differences in relatively longer‐term exposure to the social or environmental conditions that elicit their production (in addition to natural variation due to factors such as age, genetics, and health status). Influential biosocial theory on testosterone postulates that basal levels are higher in times of heightened social challenge (e.g., breeding season in some animals) and among those who are more socially dominant. 20 , 21 Relatively higher levels are thought to coordinate a brain–body state that heightens the perception of environmental stimuli relevant to social status and resource acquisition as well as facilitate the energetic and motor output to mount a successful behavioral response. Similarly, cortisol production is thought to facilitate the perception of potential social or safety challenges and the behavioral response to evade such threats. However, sustained activation of cortisol production may reflect a potentially unhealthy stress accumulation or resource depletion. 22 , 23 , 24

Hormone levels also change rapidly within the context of a temporary social challenge, increasing to coordinate brain–body systems necessary to mount a behavioral response in real time. Cortisol is well known for its relatively rapid response to laboratory tasks marked by social threat. 25 , 26 , 27 The role of cortisol in these contexts is considered to be adaptive in that it plays a role in activating the physiological, brain, and behavioral responses necessary to meet the demands of, or recovery, from a stress exposure. 5 , 28 However, less is known about transient changes in testosterone. Derived from evolved systems for mate competition, a transient rise in testosterone has been demonstrated in human contests for social dominance and is thought to facilitate competitive and aggressive behavior in this context. 29 , 30 In support of this hypothesis, both testosterone and cortisol also increase substantially in men and women over the course of naturalistic and formal competition (e.g., a sanctioned sporting event), independent of outcome. 31 In constructed laboratory competitions, transient testosterone increases have been observed, more so in men and in contests that end in victory, and these changes are associated with higher likelihood of displaying aggressive or status‐preserving behavior. 32 , 33 Further, increases in testosterone, in the context of a status contest or from exogenous administration, have been related to higher competitive motivation and persistence in effort‐based tasks. 34 , 35 , 36

The dual‐hormone effect and implicit social motivation

Research on social behavior has shown that, under certain circumstances, outcomes such as social status among peers and dominant behavior when assigned the role of leader are predicted by levels of testosterone only when also accounting for levels of cortisol. 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 This finding, termed the dual‐hormone effect, suggests that testosterone and cortisol levels have a dependent or interactive effect on status‐relevant behavior. 41 Although these effects presumably act through alterations of brain circuitry related to orienting and activating the appropriate downstream behavior, the dual‐hormone effect has not been tested in relation to brain function during a social‐status relevant task.

In addition to filling this gap, connecting research on the behavioral effects of testosterone and cortisol to brain function would contribute to recent theoretical proposals in this area of research: While the dual‐hormone effect is supported in some studies measuring behavior directly, it is not well supported when asking participants to explicitly state their desire for social status or self‐report their tendency to behave in a dominant, agentic, or aggressive manner. 42 , 43 Further, research suggests that the influence of testosterone and cortisol on behavior is implicit, operating more subtly and outside of conscious awareness. 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 These two key pieces of evidence led to the proposal that testosterone and cortisol, including the dual‐hormone interaction, influence behavior by altering subconscious perceptual and motivational systems that influence attention and effort rather than cognitive systems for controlling and integrating thoughts, mentalizing, or reflecting. 48 , 49

Neuroimaging research on testosterone and cortisol

Prior neuroimaging research has provided initial evidence on the brain areas where testosterone and cortisol may act to influence social behavior. Testosterone has been associated with altered activity in areas of the brain when viewing photos of faces that are potentially threatening (photos of angry vs. happy faces), including the amygdala and frontal regions involved in risk and reward processing such as the orbital frontal cortex (OFC) (basal salivary levels in men 50 ; exogenous administration in women, 51 , 52 , 53 ; for review, Reference 54). Radke et al. 55 showed that testosterone's effect on amygdala activity was related to increased behavioral approach towards threatening stimuli suggesting that testosterone enhances motivational salience in the context of a social challenge. Connectivity studies show that testosterone interacts with this motivational salience pathway through reducing functional correspondence between the amygdala and the OFC. 51 Further, testosterone administration, in the context of goal‐directed escape from a threat, is linked to increased activity in the left OFC and a decoupling from the subcortical fear/threat areas. 56 Thus, lateral or bilateral OFC activity during social threat may indicate the degree of self‐regulatory processing involved in downregulating impulses and facilitating motivation to seek social rewards that are potentially risky. Indeed, neural activity in the medial OFC has been shown to mediate the relationship between basal testosterone and aggressive responses to unfairness (rejecting an unfair offer in the ultimatum game) such that lower OFC activity explained the positive relationship between testosterone levels and aggression. 57

Other frontal regions have also been linked to testosterone and regulatory aspects of social motivation. Volman et al. 58 found that endogenous testosterone in men predicted whether subjects showed activity in ventral lateral PFC (vlPFC) during a task that required participants to inhibit automatic approach–avoidance tendencies to explicitly evaluate the emotional valence of the faces. Similarly, Stanton et al. 59 found that men's testosterone levels were positively correlated with ventral medial PFC (vmPFC) activity when viewing angry compared to neutral faces. When evaluating traits, rather than emotions, adolescent boys' testosterone levels have been positively related to activity in the dorsal medial PFC (dmPFC) specifically when they were evaluating themselves, that is, indicating whether positive and negative traits were self‐descriptive. 60 Testosterone administration in men and women has been found to alter complex social behaviors, such as empathy and generosity via the modulation of frontal‐subcortical connectivity 51 as well as activity in, and subcortical connectivity to, a region of the temporal cortex known as the temporoparietal junction (TPJ). 61

In a largely separate literature, cortisol also appears to play a role in activating goal‐directed behavior in the context of a social or safety threat. Cortisol administration has shown a positive effect on activation in areas of the frontal cortex associated with motivation salience in the context of anticipating active escape of a threat, but a negative effect on activation in subcortical areas associated with fear processing in the context of an inescapable threat. 62 However, the role of cortisol in regulating areas of the brain underlying social‐specific stress may depend on whether the task requires the regulation of negative emotion. Endogenous cortisol responses to viewing negative emotional imagery have been linked to lower activity in the vlPFC. 63 Other studies have shown that cortisol reactivity during mild to moderate stress is correlated with vlPFC and OFC activation indicating that these areas play a role in emotional aspects of the stress response. 64 In review of the literature, Harrewijn et al. 65 found that although effects were inconsistent across studies, there are trends for higher endogenous cortisol being linked to increased temporal cortex but decreased vmPFC activity across various social stressors. However, when being asked to regulate negative emotion through distraction, necessary for staying focused on goal‐relevant stimuli, cortisol administration has been shown to initially increase activity in the vlPFC. 66 , 67 Similarly, individual differences in cortisol responses when performing in a socially evaluative stress task have been found to be positively correlated with task‐associated hemodynamic response in selective frontal areas. 68

Few studies have considered testosterone and cortisol together. In one study, Duell et al. 69 found that in a mixed‐sex sample of adolescents, those with relatively high testosterone and low cortisol (measured from hair, which represents basal levels over a ~3‐month period) showed greater conformity to peers who demonstrated prosocial behavior (e.g., giving to charity). Further, this hormonal profile was linked to higher activity in areas of the brain associated with social motivation and salience, including the TPJ and OFC (as well as the insula and caudate nucleus) during a prosocial decision‐making task. This suggests that high cortisol might counteract the motivational salience effects of testosterone. Indeed, cortisol administration has been shown to have wide‐ranging downregulatory effects on brain areas associated with reward anticipation. 70 , 71 However, to the best of our knowledge, the dual‐hormone hypothesis has not been directly tested in relation to brain activity when individuals are asked to perform in an effort‐based task for status relevant rewards, a more direct test of the underlying proposal that testosterone and cortisol alter social behavior through implicit motivational systems.

The current study

To fill the gaps in prior literature on the mechanisms by which steroid hormones regulate social motivation, this study will test how basal and reactive testosterone, cortisol, and their interactions relate to frontal‐temporal cortical patterns of neural activity measured via functional near‐infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) and task performance in an effort‐based contest for rewards. We build on prior work by developing a task in which individual differences in motivation are assessed by key‐pressing persistence along three conditions of cognitive difficulty (single key repetition, keys in alphabet order, and keys in backward alphabet order). The use of alphabet retrieval to manipulate task difficulty is in line with foundational neuroimaging research establishing areas of the TPJ, particularly the angular gyrus, in processing semantic information, retrieving order sequence, and orienting attention. 72 , 73 Prior research has also indicated that both task‐based motivation and related areas of brain activity differ based on whether the reward is social or monetary. 74 , 75 , 76 , 77 Thus, we also test the hormonal correlates of cortical activity and performance under three incentive conditions: (1) monetary reward, (2) a positive social judgement, and (3) a negative social judgement.

We generally predict that frontal areas associated with motivational salience and social rewards will show more neural‐related activity with greater task difficulty and increased task performance. Further, activation in areas of the TPJ may depend on task difficulty, 78 which creates concurrent demands of working memory in frontal areas and reduced self‐referential processing, suggesting the possibility of a frontal‐TPJ tradeoff or anti‐correlation. 79 , 80 , 81 Finally, we predict that higher testosterone and lower cortisol will be related to increased frontal activity, particularly the vlPFC and OFC. To test the comprehensive testosterone, cortisol, and testosterone × cortisol models with a complex mixed 3 × 3 condition design, we employ a conservative and rigorous data analytic approach with a relatively large mixed‐sex sample. This interdisciplinary study bridges disparate literatures in social behavioral neuroendocrinology and neuroscience and provides new information on the mechanisms by which hormones regulate social behavior via their influence in underlying brain circuitry.

METHODS

Participants

Participants were recruited from the psychology department subject pool at a large midwestern US university. There were no exclusion criteria, but potential participants were told that the study involved neuroimaging where they would be required to wear a fabric headcap over their hair. One hundred and sixty‐six participants completed the study. An additional 35 participants were excluded from analyses for the following reasons: (1) a hairstyle that prevented adequate fNIRS data quality, (2) an inability to produce a saliva sample that could be assayed for hormones, and (3) an experimenter error in protocol execution or data recording.

A final sample of 131 participants were included in subsequent data analysis (additional data loss for individual variables/outcomes is detailed below). The average age of participants was 19.5 (SD = 2.1), 64% reported a White racial/ethnic identity, 74% reported being assigned female at birth, 70% reported a current gender identity as a woman, and 89% reported that they are right‐handed. This study was approved by the university ethics committee.

Procedure overview and lab environment

Upon arrival to the laboratory, participants were asked to store all personal items away from the testing room including electronics, phones, and smartwatches and to silence all device notifications. Following consent, participants were instructed to rinse their mouths in preparation for the first saliva sample at the lab sink on the way to the adjacent testing room.

Participants were seated in the testing room where their head cap size was measured prior to beginning the study procedures (the overall components and timing are shown in Figure 1A). Next, participants completed a series of surveys on a tablet for demographic information and other questionnaires beyond the purpose of this study for approximately 15 min. This survey period allowed the experimenters to set up the head cap and for the participant to return to physiological baseline.

The study procedure, head cap set up, and lab environment.

Following the survey and rest period, participants provided the first saliva sample (details below). Next, the head cap was set up on the participant's head and the fNIRS signal quality was assessed and calibrated. The experimenter read a task instructions sheet in which the between‐groups experimental condition was applied. Participants then completed the Speed Typing Task (details below). Following the task, participants rinsed their mouths with water, completed a post‐task survey, and then gave the second saliva sample. The environmental conditions for the experiment are shown in Figure 1. Participants were seated in a comfortable chair, in a windowless room, with semi‐transparent fluorescent light filters covering the overhead lights.

The overall study design takes several approaches. First, we test the effects of two key independent variables under which participants completed the Speed Typing Task—(1) Trial Type (varying along 3 levels of cognitive difficulty) and (2) Incentive Condition (Cash, Positive Social Judgement, Negative Social Judgement)—on basal and reactive hormone levels, task performance (the number of correct button presses), and neural signatures of brain activity. Next, we test the correlational relationships between the predictor variables, hormones, trial type, and incentive condition (main effects and interactions), on the outcome variables of task performance and brain activity.

Behavioral task

For this research, a new cognitive‐behavioral task was developed to elicit individual differences in task‐based persistence, a measure of effort‐based motivation. The Speed Typing Task (Figure 2) requires participants to press specific keys on a keyboard as accurately and quickly as possible. It was designed to be similar to the lever press for assessing motivation in rodent models, with additional cognitive components to alter task difficulty. Participants were told that the task was a competition and that their performance would be recorded as the total number of correct key presses averaged across all competition trials.

Block design of the Speed Typing Task design.

There were three trial types delivered in a block design: (1) Typing the alphabet in the forward direction starting from the letter presented at the beginning of the trial (Forward); (2) Typing the alphabet in the backward direction starting from the letter presented at the beginning of the trial (Backward); and (3) Typing one key repeatedly (Repetition). The trial types were designed to vary by task difficulty: Backward (highest difficulty), Forward (moderate difficulty), and Repetition (lowest difficulty). Each trial type was experienced three times, with each trial lasting 30 s. There were 15 s rest intervals after each trial before the instructions for the next trial type were presented. Trial type order was randomized for each participant. The task was programmed and administered in PsychoPy desktop application. 82

As accurately described to the participant, behavioral performance was scored and recorded as the total number of correct keys pressed across the Forward, Backward, and Repetition trials. To control for typing skill, participants were instructed to type with only one finger (dominant hand pointer finger) and their compliance was monitored by the experimenter. Nonetheless, to check for effects of typing experience, we asked the following question on a pre‐task survey, “Have you had an experience or occupation with high‐levels of typing or transcribing (e.g., gaming, scribing, typewriting)?” Thirty‐eight percent of participants asked this question said “Yes.” To assess performance effects, we compared the performance scores (total correct key presses) between those who reported experience with typing and those who reported no such experience. Although the total score was higher in the Yes group (M = 197.8, SD = 56.2) compared to the No group (M = 184.2, SD = 42.8), this difference was not significant. Exploration of performance scores based on reported typing experience in each trial type revealed that the better overall performance for those with typing experience was due to their relatively better performance on the repetition trials (“Yes”: M = 139.8, SD = 30.9; “No”: M = 123.5, SD = 34.7; t‐contrast: t = 2.3, p = .01), but not the forward (“Yes”: M = 51.6, SD = 19.3; “No”: M = 50.2, SD = 16.1; t‐contrast: not sig) or backward (“Yes”: M = 12.0, SD = 9.3; “No”: M = 10.5, SD = 8.8; t‐contrast: not sig) alphabet trials.

Further, we conducted a post‐task check to verify understanding of the task instructions (“I understood the task instructions,” on a 5‐point Likert scale; strongly disagree = 1, strongly agree = 5) and participants strongly agreed, on average (M = 4.6, SD = 0.5). Finally, as a validation check on self‐perceived effort compared to performance, participants' responses to the item “I tried hard to perform well” were significantly and positively related to the total performance score (r = 0.24, p = .04), an effect that was accounted for by the repetition and forward trial types, but not the backward. Indeed, the stepwise decrease in the correlation value between self‐reported effort and performance matches what would be expected as task difficulty increases (repetition: r = 0.30; forward: r = 0.21; backward: r = 0.03).

Between‐groups experimental condition

A goal of this study is to assess how aspects of social‐motivational incentives impact task effort, patterns of neural activity during performance, and the connection between hormones and brain activity. To test this, participants completed the Speed Typing Task under one of three incentive conditions for task performance: (1) a cash prize, (2) social judgment—positive, and (3) social judgment—negative. The instructions given for each condition are shown in Table 1. Conditions were randomized among the participants. For the cash condition, the prize money was distributed at the end of the study during the debriefing such that participants did not know the outcome (win or loss relative to others and by how much) until after the last saliva sample.

Assessing neural activity with fNIRS

Head cap and Optode layout

The fNIRS Optodes' Location Decider (fOLD) toolbox 83 was used to determine initial source and detector optode placement on a 10–10 EEG NIRScap (NIRx Medical Technologies LLC). Using the pre‐programmed Brodmann's areas with confirmation across the AAL and Julich atlases, frontal cortex and temporoparietal junction were selected with a specificity of 30%. Estimated locations were then transferred to an adult ICBM‐152 MNI head model in NIRSite 2021.4 (NIRx Medical Technologies LLC). The cap montage consisted of 16 sources and 15 detectors. Shown in Figure 3, the resulting montage consisted of 40 channels overlying unique spatial areas of the frontal and temporal–parietal cortex. The source‐detector distance (M = 35.38 mm; SD = 3.93 mm), 10‐10 EEG position, and associated anatomical regions (with specificity) are listed in Table S1, Supporting Information. A total of 8 short channel detectors were added to source optodes in equally spaced intervals across the montage layout to measure and account for physiological noise.

Source (red dots) and detector (blue dots) optode placement of the prefrontal cortex and temporal–parietal junction. Blue circles indicate the placement of short channel detectors. This figure was made with the NIRx NIRSite software.

Data acquisition

Cortical hemodynamic activity was recorded using the dual‐tip, continuous‐wave fNIRS system (NIRSport2 16‐16 Core System Unit, NIRx Medical Technologies LLC, Germany). Detected light wavelength signals (760 and 850 nm) were recorded with a frequency of 5.086 Hz and computed with NIRx data acquisition software, Aurora fNIRS.

Hormone collection and assay

Participants were instructed not to exercise, smoke, or consume caffeinated beverages or food within 1 h prior to arriving at the laboratory for the study. All sessions were scheduled between 12 and 5 PM to control for circadian effects on basal testosterone and cortisol levels. Prior to each saliva sample, participants were instructed to rinse their mouths with filtered water. They were then given a plastic vial and a paper towel, and detailed instructions on the process for producing passive drool and funneling their saliva into the tube. Approximately 1–3 mL of saliva was collected for each sample via passive drool into 5 mL plastic vials. Participants were given ~7 min to produce the saliva sample.

Saliva samples were immediately placed into a 0°C refrigerator and then transferred to a −80°C freezer after the study session ended. Samples were shipped on dry ice to Salimetrics SalivaLab (Carlsbad, CA) and assayed for testosterone and cortisol by high sensitivity enzyme immunoassay (EIA) kits with samples tested in duplicate. Samples were thawed to room temperature, vortexed, and then centrifuged for 15 min at approximately 3500 rpm (1500g) immediately before performing the assay. The cortisol assay has a lower limit of sensitivity of 0.007 μg/dL, a standard curve range from 0.012 to 3.0 μg/dL, and an average intra‐assay coefficient of variation (CV) of 4.60% and an average inter‐assay CV of 6.00%. The testosterone assay has a lower limit of sensitivity of 0.458–1 pg/mL, a standard curve range from 6.1 to 600 pg/mL, and an average intra‐assay coefficient of variation of 4.60% and an average inter‐assay CV of 9.85%. The manufacturer kit manual for the testosterone assay includes results from an antibody sensitivity analysis indicating that the percentage of cross‐reactivity of cortisol spiked at 1000 ng/mL in the testosterone RIA was <0.004, indicating little to no cross‐reactivity. For the analyses performed for this study, the intra‐assay CV% were 3.48% for testosterone and 2.74% for cortisol.

Widely used immunoassays for testosterone in saliva (i.e., radio‐immunoassays and enzyme‐immunoassays) have been criticized for limited detection sensitivity, especially in small ranges that are typical in females, leading to female‐specific patterns of inflated levels. 84 , 85 , 86 Due to this, and the notable sex difference between males and females, testosterone levels are standardized (z‐scored) within sex. Further, reference to individual differences in these standardized basal levels should be interpreted as only relative to others within the sample studied here (measured via the same assay procedure), not as indicative of absolute levels relative to the population.

Data preprocessing and analysis

Participant filtering

We collected fNIRS data from 131 participants. Before analysis, we removed seven participants because of experiment issues (e.g., participant's data file did not have the appropriate triggers to mark stimulus onsets). During pre‐processing, we removed one participant because they had more than 50% bad channels. Finally, we removed three participants from second level analysis because they did not report their sex assigned at birth as either male nor female (a necessity for the hormone analysis, which standardized hormone levels within each sex). Neuroimaging analyses were thus conducted with an N = 120. Of the remaining 120 participants with valid neuroimaging data, we were unable to measure hormone levels in 23 participants, with an additional 3 participants missing one measurement of either testosterone or cortisol limiting the ability to calculate the change score. Thus, analyses involving both neuroimaging data and hormones were conducted with an N = 94–97.

Behavioral and hormone data

For all behavioral analyses, task trial type was coded as a continuous variable as a function of task difficulty, with repetition = 1, forward = 2, and backward = 3. Incentive condition was coded as a factor. Performance was the number of correct key presses for each level of task difficulty. To eliminate the influence of task non‐performance, we removed all performance values that equaled 0. This filter removed one participant's repetition value. To remove potential outliers, we trimmed all values greater than 3 SDs from the mean performance within each trial type. This removed 1% of performance values. Analyses of the within‐subject task difficulty and between‐subjects incentive condition on the behavioral and hormone data, as well as ANOVAs and mixed model regression, were conducted in R (R Core Team, 2021).

fNIRS data preprocessing

All preprocessing and analyses of fNIRS data were conducted using the NIRS Brain AnalyzIR toolbox (Huppert Brain Imaging Lab 87 ). Preprocessing steps first included the removal of individual channels in which the scalp coupling index (SCI) was below 0.75, trimming the data to 10s before the stimulus event (pre‐baseline) and 20 s after the stimulus event (post‐baseline). Then, the raw light wavelength amplitude signal data was converted to optical density. To remove motion artifacts, we applied the wavelet filter with spline removal (sthresh = 16 88 ).

Chromophores

The optical density signals were then converted to concentrations of oxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR) using the modified Beer–Lambert Law. The change in HbO and HbR signal from the onset of a task stimulus is a theoretical and empirically supported measure of neural activity derived from the physical properties of neuro‐vascular coupling. The expected canonical hemodynamic response function is represented by an increasing HbO signal with a simultaneously decreasing HbR signal (by a smaller factor) within 4–8 s after each stimulus onset, summated temporally for the duration of stimulus presentation. Essentially, this signal is an artifact in the blood corresponding to the metabolic needs of an active population of nearby neurons.

General linear model (GLM) level 1

In the first level analysis, the Conical HRF was modeled with a third order polynomial using the autoregressive, iteratively reweighted least‐squares (AR‐IRLS) model with short channel regressors in the GLM module for both HbO and HbR. We removed outliers using the function “nirs.modules.RemoveOutlierSubjects” with the default 0.05 cutoff.

GLM level 2

We conducted all second level analyses in R. To remove potential outliers, the highest and lowest 2.5% of beta values were trimmed by task (repetition, backwards, and forwards) and type (HbO and HbR). This method of trimming does not assume that the betas are normally distributed. Channel betas were converted into ROIs using the tapered contrast vector method for region of interest (ROI)‐based fNIRS analysis in Brain AnalyzIR 89 —the weights of each channel for corresponding ROIs are listed in Supporting Information. This approach uses head‐model data to more precisely define the relative weighting of the fNIRS channels contributing to the ROI and, effectively, reduces the number of statistical tests. All analyses were conducted by these ROIs. Second‐level group analyses were conducted using a general linear model with categorical predictors. To facilitate the direct estimation of condition‐specific means and simplify the construction of contrast matrices, the model was constructed without a global intercept (i.e., a cell‐means parameterization).

To control the false discovery rate (FDR) for each analysis, we converted all p‐values into q‐values across all ROIs (within type) using the qvalue package. 90 The significance level was set at q = 0.05. All betas, t‐values, and p‐values for all comparisons across all ROIs are included in the full results output file located on the osf.io page for this study (https://osf.io/djwkv/).

Chromophore reporting

The main analyses in this study focus on HbO data. The HbO signal is the primary hemodynamic response of interest due to precedent for better signal‐to‐noise ratio. Further, a focus on HbO simplifies the analysis pipeline and interpretation, which is already complex and requires a large number of analyses. However, to increase transparency and in accordance with best practices in fNIRS data reporting, 91 we include HbR data in the publicly available data set and the main results for the HbR data are included in Supporting Information (Figures S5–S8).

Data availability and code transparency

Data and analysis code are publicly available on the open science framework (https://osf.io/djwkv/).

RESULTS

Hormone descriptives

Given the within‐individual consistency of hormone levels (see correlations in Table 2) and to reduce the potential bias of a single measure, we calculated basal levels of testosterone and cortisol by averaging the levels from time 1 (TestA and CortA) with their respective time 2 level (TestB and CortB) to be used in subsequent analyses. To directly compare the difference in the patterns of the HbO response across the three trial types and incentive conditions, we conducted pairwise comparisons for each ROI (using the emmeans R package 92 ). For trial type contrasts (Figure 7, bottom left panel), the only significant differences in which greater HbO levels were seen were in the Forward compared to Repetition trials in the Frontal Medial gyrus (left) and Frontal Inferior gyrus (left) ROIs. Significantly lower HbO levels were found when contrasting Backward over Forward trials in the Frontal Superior Medial gyrus (left and right) and the Frontal Superior gyrus (left). For the incentive condition contrasts (Figure 7, bottom right panel), there were no significant effects of HbO signal based on whether the participant was incentivized with a cash prize or pos/neg social judgement; although the negative social judgement condition had more increased HbO levels in superior frontal areas and the cash had more increased HbO levels in middle and inferior frontal areas relative to the other conditions, none of the comparisons reached statistical significance (q‐value <0.05).

Combined, these results show that completing the speed typing task, across trial type and incentive condition, was generally linked to increased HbO signal in frontal and decreased HbO signal in tempo‐parietal regions. Incentive condition did not produce significantly different patterns of hemodynamic activity. However, HbO increases in the left middle and inferior frontal areas were specific to speed typing the alphabet in the forward direction. The decreased HbO signal in the tempo‐parietal regions appears most pronounced in the alphabet trials compared to the single‐key repetition trials. Finally, hemodynamic activity in the left and right superior medial frontal areas decreased when attempting to quickly type the alphabet in the backward compared to forward direction.

DISCUSSION

The broad purpose of this study was to explore how basal and reactive testosterone, cortisol, and their interaction relate to task‐based performance and frontal‐temporal brain activity under different conditions of task difficulty and social reward incentive schemas. To this end, we introduced a new task designed to elicit individual differences in effort‐based competitive key‐pressing performance under different conditions of task difficulty, the Speed Typing Task. Further, we experimentally varied the social context in which performance was incentivized: either a cash prize for more accurate key presses over other participants, a positive social judgment (where good performance was framed as a positive personality trait), or a negative social judgment (where good performance was framed as a negative personality trait). Analysis of the behavioral data revealed that task performance was manipulated as intended; typing the alphabet backwards resulted in the lowest number of correct key presses, typing the alphabet in the forward direction resulted in significantly more correct key presses, and single‐key repetition produced, as expected, the highest number of correct key presses. These results provide validation to the task conditions as a manipulation of cognitive task difficulty. Under these conditions, motivation to perform must overcome different levels of perceived and actual challenge. Results also revealed that the between‐groups manipulation of task incentive also influenced performance, although effects were less robust. Specifically, the group that completed the task under the cash incentive performed the best, correctly pressing the most keys significantly more than the negative social judgment group (the effect was not significant over the positive social judgment group).

Task‐associated patterns of brain activity

Regardless of the trial difficulty or incentive condition, completing the task generally resulted in increased hemodynamic signal indicative of brain activity in frontal areas and decreased activity in the TPJ. Further, the pattern of activation in the TPJ was strongly linked to task difficulty such that more cognitively difficult trials showed the greatest deactivation (HbO signals significantly below baseline levels). These findings provide new evidence in support of the hypothesis that these regions, in the role as a key node of the default mode network (DMN), are deactivated to reduce internal and self‐referential processing that would interfere with the cognitive demands of task performance, 93 , 94 , 95 , 96 a process that could depend on the attentional and working‐memory components of the task. 97 , 98 Thus, data from the present study contribute to existing models of network switching: areas of the DMN appear to trade off with areas of the central executive network (dorsal‐lateral prefrontal areas of the superior middle gyrus) and ventral attention and salience networks (ventrolateral prefrontal areas of the frontal medial and inferior gyri). 99 , 100 Results presented here also add new evidence to suggest that the magnitude of TPJ/DMN deactivation or off‐switching is consistent with shifting task demands.

Our results also revealed some specific nuance between the trial types which may be linked to aspects of language processes inherent to the serial recall of learned patterns of the alphabet. Specifically, typing the alphabet in the forward direction resulted in significantly greater activity in the left middle and inferior frontal gyri—areas associated with working memory, attention, and language processing and production. 72 , 101 , 102 , 103 Typing the alphabet backwards, despite being significantly more difficult, only resulted in relatively decreased activity in the bilateral frontal superior gyrus. This finding is contrary to expectations for brain activity comparisons when varying cognitive difficulty, discussed above. This finding may be explained by the significantly greater complexity and demand of typing the alphabet backwards under time and performance pressure. 97 , 104 Indeed, performance scores in the backward condition suggest that participants struggled to perform and may have engaged in less cognitive or emotional regulatory processing. Individual differences in effort or ability, as measured by performance, are thus likely relevant to task‐specific patterns of brain activity.

Task performance

Brain activity and performance

It is critical for neuroimaging studies to connect the structural patterns of brain activity to individual differences in the functional output of a cognitive or motivational task to establish whether there is evidence that the magnitude of neural activity in an area of interest facilitates performance. 105 In this study, we found that better performance, standardized within each trial type to control for difficulty, was generally related to increased HbO betas in most areas of the brain assessed, with the strongest effects in the left and right frontal inferior gyrus as well as the left precentral and angular gyri. The significant and strong positive relationship between the number of keys accurately typed within each task and the left precentral gyrus, particularly in the repetition trials, is not surprising as this area includes motor planning and production regions corresponding to the right hand (used for typing) (e.g., Reference 106). However, the positive relationship in other frontal mid and inferior areas suggest that task‐specific motivation or cognitive control is promoted by increased neural activity, reflecting what is likely only a small part of the more complex network synchrony and reconfiguration that promotes cognitive performance. 107

The role of task difficulty

There was a general trend for the positive relationship between performance, standardized within task, and brain activity to be dependent on task difficulty, although this trend was significant only in the left angular gyrus. Specifically, better performance predicted increased brain activity in the repetition trials, when task difficulty was lowest and where the goal to win or perform well was least likely to be impacted by cognitive skill. This finding suggests that brain activity associated with goal‐directed behavior is specific to tasks where effort is not thwarted by excessive challenge or where skill and challenge are matched, consistent with major tenets of the flow state hypothesis. 108 Although the contributions of specific brain regions underlying the flow state are not well understood, the role of the left angular gyrus may be involved, particularly given evidence of prior associations with intrinsic goal achievement. 109

The role of incentive condition

Although participants performed slightly better in the cash incentive condition compared to the social judgement conditions, we generally found no significant effects of brain activation in the task by incentive condition. Although it was predicted that these socially relevant contextual conditions would alter motivation, and thus underlying brain processes, this result is consistent with prior literature showing limited effects of social versus monetary task incentives on patterns of brain activity (e.g., Reference 110). The negative versus positive valence of the incentive manipulation did not appear effective in altering participants' performance. However, cortisol levels decreased significantly across the task period in the positive social judgement group, compared to the cash and negative judgement groups. As the cortisol response is thought to reflect the stress of social self‐threat, 26 this result suggests that incentivizing task performance with a positive social judgement may have created a social context marked by reduced social self‐threat. Rather than increasing performance, social incentives may be more relevant to social–emotional behavioral outcomes and related brain processes. Future research is needed to test the relationship between brain activity and motivated performance under more salient social interventions.

Hormonal correlates of performance

Generally, basal levels of testosterone and cortisol were not related to task performance. However, we did find an effect of the interaction between the change in testosterone and change in cortisol across the task and task performance. Specifically, an increase in testosterone over the task period was associated with better overall task performance but only if cortisol levels also decreased (relative to others). Further analysis of the interaction by the trial type revealed that it was significant only in the forward alphabet trials, where moderate task difficulty produced a context of maximal challenge–skill balance. These results suggest that a pattern of decreasing cortisol during the task may reflect a lower social self‐threat context. In this context, testosterone increases, reflecting a state of higher task‐based competitive motivation, translate to better performance. However, for those whose cortisol increased across the task, perhaps due to heightened perceived social self‐threat or sense of uncontrollability, testosterone increases were associated with worse performance—a stress–motivation mismatch. This finding is consistent with foundational work on the dual‐hormone hypothesis in which Mehta et al. 111 found that testosterone and cortisol reactivity interacted to predict performance in financial negotiations. As in the present study, Mehta et al. 111 found that an increase in testosterone combined with a decrease in cortisol predicted better earnings. These authors proposed that rising cortisol levels interfere with testosterone's link to behaviors aimed at maximizing rewards because rising cortisol reflects a state in which there is a conflict between the social and financial goals of the situation. The results of the present study extend this dynamic state‐based account of the dual hormone effect to behavior in a contest for social and monetary rewards. Further, our results provide new information on the additional role of the task demands: the dynamic dual‐hormone effect was maximal when task performance was manageable (moderate difficulty), precisely when motivational state should have the greatest impact on performance. This suggests that the endocrine profile of rising testosterone and falling cortisol corresponds to a state of “confident challenge” where the drive to persist and perform well is coupled with a sense of control and reduced threat.

Hormone–brain correspondence

Hormone level changes across the task

Exploration of the hormone–brain correspondence for hormonal change across the task revealed only one significant effect after adjusting for the risk of false positives: testosterone change across the task period was related to activity in the left angular gyrus, depending on the trial type/task difficulty. Relative increases in testosterone predicted increased activation in the backwards alphabet (hardest) condition but decreased activity in the repetition (easiest) condition. Thus, testosterone change may play a role in shifting recruitment of this area based on the task demands. As discussed above, the angular gyrus, part of the TPJ and larger DMN, is implicated in aspects of multisensory integration and association with prior knowledge as well as assigning goal‐relevant meaning to this information for the purpose of planning action. 73 More recently, scholars have proposed that a main function of the TPJ, including the angular gyrus, is to connect internal and external states by “contextual updating” of behavioral goals with incoming information about the social and perceptual environment. 112 Transient testosterone increases in response to a challenge in the environment, including social encounters, are thought to promote adaptive behavior relevant to the specific context of the social challenge. 21 , 29 , 31 Although results here are early and warrant more confirmatory analysis, they raise the possibility that testosterone changes, either directly or indirectly through sub‐cortical connections, prime this area of attention reorienting and social contextualizing necessary for adaptive behavioral modulation to the demands of the task or context.

Basal hormones

Generally, we found that basal levels of hormones were more associated with brain activity than patterns of hormone change. Basal levels of testosterone (controlling for participant sex) were generally positively related to individual differences in task‐associated activity in frontal areas, while cortisol levels were generally negatively related to activity in these areas. Basal testosterone was significantly and positively associated with brain activity in the right middle frontal gyrus, which (for our optode placement) corresponds to the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (vlPFC). This region appears particularly important as an intersection point between dorsal and ventral attention networks mediating the control of attention as well as, specifically, motor inhibition aspects of cognitive control. 113 , 114 Further, the right vlPFC has been implicated specifically in the downregulation of negative emotions during social stress. 115 , 116 , 117 Our finding is somewhat inconsistent with prior research showing that endogenous testosterone levels are inversely related to activity in vlPFC when attempting to control automatic tendencies of approach–avoidance when viewing social–emotional stimuli. 58 Thus, our preliminary findings contribute new evidence that higher levels of testosterone may play a role in controlling attentional and emotional aspects of task performance during social competition.

Other effects of basal hormones on brain activity were specific to areas of the right TPJ, as discussed above, a critical node of the default mode network (DMN). In this area (the parietal inferior, angular, and temporal middle gyri), there was a robust three‐way interaction between testosterone, cortisol, and task difficulty (or trial type). When the cognitive challenge of the contest was the greatest (in the backward alphabet condition), a profile of high testosterone and high cortisol was associated with decreased TPJ activity. When there was virtually no cognitive challenge (during the repetition trials), this same high testosterone/high cortisol profile was associated with increased TPJ activity. A profile of high testosterone and low cortisol generally showed the opposite: increased or unchanged TPJ activity in the backwards alphabet trials and TPJ deactivation in the repetition trials. Further, all effects for basal hormones in this study were right lateralized, consistent with prior evidence that the influence of basal steroid hormones on brain activity may be specific to the right hemisphere (e.g., References 118, 119).

Combined, these results provide initial evidence that basal cortisol levels modulate how testosterone relates to the allocation of cognitive resources and the neural control of task‐related attention and focus. A high testosterone and high cortisol profile is linked here to the maintenance of a vigilant, externally focused brain state when incurring the most difficult challenge and an internally focused or self‐referential brain state while least challenged. This more broadly suggests that these hormones play a combined role in contextually tuning the mental shift from internal to external information and the tradeoffs between task goals and processing their social relevance (e.g., References 60, 120). These implications are consistent with at least one other study of the dual‐hormone effect in relation to brain activity in which a high testosterone and low cortisol profile predicted TPJ and OFC activity when adolescents were engaged in social conformity to prosocial peers. 69 Indeed, these findings were interpreted as potentially indicative of the role of basal testosterone and cortisol in promoting reward salience, that is, attentional sensitivity to social rewards, in the context of social decision‐making. Combined, our results support this emerging model of dual‐hormone regulation of implicit brain processes underlying attentional salience involved in reward‐seeking in contexts relevant to social status.

Integrating across the hormone–brain–behavior results

The exploratory nature of the present study resulted in a complex set of findings that warrant some conceptual integration for the purpose of informing future work and drawing broader implications. A major overarching goal of this line of research is to develop a working model for hormones like testosterone and cortisol on how they influence behavior via alterations in brain circuitry involved in social status and social threat perception and action. Thus, despite the tentativeness of the results of the present study, we draw several implications for future research.

First, although basal levels of testosterone were not related to goal‐directed behavior in this study, they were generally related to greater frontal and temporal activity (largely in the right hemisphere) associated with visual–spatial attention and emotion processing. Consistent with studies on testosterone's effect on reward valuation specific to certain social and emotional stimuli, our results suggest that testosterone more prominently figures into perceptual aspects of status cues in one's environment and the directing of attentional control on status goals rather than the direct or linear facilitation of the quantity of behavioral effort.

Second, the dual hormone correlates of status motivation (effort‐based differences in performance independent of task difficulties) were specific to their transient fluctuations over the course of the social challenge (hormone change pre‐post task). However, these task‐related changes were largely not associated with brain activity during the specific conditions of the task—possibly due to the inconsistent measurement timescales of brain activity (over the seconds of the trials) and more global hormone shifts observable in saliva over the ~20 mins of the task and sample production. However, one particularly robust effect emerged: Activity in the left angular gyrus, an area associated with higher‐order cognition, sensory integration, and goal orientation (applying self‐referential meaning to needed actions), was positively related to performance in the lowest cognitive challenge condition. It was in this exact area and under this more direct motivation–behavior condition where we found a robust effect for testosterone change. Specifically, increased testosterone across the task was related to decreased left angular gyrus activation. Because increased activity here was linked to better performance, this suggests that testosterone increases could downregulate cognitive processes needed to decide goal relevance and plan motor action under some circumstances. Indeed, when looking at individual differences in basal levels (mean exposure across the task), people with relatively high cortisol levels showed a positive relationship between basal testosterone and the angular gyrus, as well as other regions of the TPJ, when the task was easy—also a time when angular gyrus activity was linked to better performance. When the task was hard, these same higher cortisol individuals showed a negative relationship between testosterone and TPJ activity, potentially thwarting performance.

Thus, we make the following predictions for future research:

Prediction 1. Testosterone exposure plays a role in upregulating areas of the brain involved in cognitive processing of control, attention, action planning, and goal orientation when engaged in a competitive task that requires these functions to perform.

Prediction 2. Cortisol, in combination with task demands, moderate testosterone's effects on brain activity, but only in temporal‐parietal regions. Because these areas regulate aspects of the DMN, higher cortisol levels may serve as a key stimulant of self‐referential goal calibration in the face of intervening stress or social self‐threat perception.

Limitations

There are several key limitations to our study design and the interpretations of results. First, excepting a small effect on task performance, our incentive condition manipulation had little to no impact on participants' behavior, hormonal responses, and patterns of brain activity. However, our study design lacked a “no incentive” condition which would have aided in assessing the overall impact of any incentive, that is, the motivational effects of having task performance confer no benefit on the participant's personal or monetary standing. Incentivize structure, monetary and social, may have little impact on motivation in a generic laboratory task, but we were unable to determine that with our design. However, the lack of effects between the incentive group types could be due to the fact that the condition instructions and design were not persuasive or effective in activating motivational priorities differently. Further, as is the case for most lab paradigms, the Speed Typing Task may not be a direct measure of implicit motivation for social status or performance outcomes relevant to real‐world social status.

The experimental design, while necessary to address the primary goals of the study, was complex. It included manipulations of incentives and task difficulty, as well as behavioral, hormonal, and brain activity measurements. This complex design generated a large number of statistical comparisons. To control for the false discovery rate, we implemented an FDR correction (converting p‐values to q‐values). A consequence of this correction is reduced statistical power, which may have inhibited the detection of effects in our between‐subjects manipulation of social context. As this was exploratory research, the results—particularly the null findings for social context—should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. Further, although the sample size (N ~120) is relatively large for single neuroimaging studies, some analyses, particularly those involving three‐way interactions, were likely underpowered to detect small effects. Here we provide initial effect size estimates and directional predictions for future, more confirmatory studies.

An additional limitation of the present study is the use of commercial enzyme immunoassay kits for the measurement of testosterone in saliva. Prior research has revealed issues with immunoassay methods for salivary testosterone at lower levels, including relatively low correspondence to blood levels and to other assay methods as well as overestimation of levels in women (e.g., References 84, 85, 86). Indeed, the raw testosterone levels reported here represent a sex difference, that although consistent with the prior literature using similar assay methods, is not consistent with the expected sex difference in blood levels and saliva levels measured using some other assay methods (for discussion and reference, see page 32 of Reference 31). This could be due to cross‐reactivity with other hormones in saliva (insufficient sensitivity of the assay) or some unknown factor that affects androgen stability in saliva differently for men and women. Although this issue presents important validity concerns for our measure of testosterone, standardization within sex and a focus on within sample relative differences and within person change may mitigate some of these concerns. Assay protocol and reliability, reported in the methods section, meet industry standard best practices and standards for within lab and within saliva‐sample metrics for reliability. Nonetheless, findings regarding basal testosterone levels should be interpreted with some caution and require replication. Comparison of our results to prior and future work may be limited to instances in which the same assay methods are used.

There are a relatively limited number of prior studies that have tested endogenous hormone levels (and patterns of reactivity) in relation to functional brain activity. Despite the importance of studying these links, this kind of design has several limitations. First, due to the correlational rather than experimental design, the results cannot speak to the causal nature of the relationships. Indeed, hormone–brain relationships are often bidirectional, influenced by multiple coordinated systems in the body, and diffuse in their impact on tissues throughout the body. Evidence of hormone–brain correspondence in this study should be interpreted as initial support for a relationship that is likely indirect, whereby hormones influence the probability of patterns of brain activity through multiple pathways. Further, linking endogenous hormone changes to brain activity is complicated by the different timescales of change. Whereas trial‐based brain activity is measured in seconds and averaged across 30‐s blocks of time, hormonal responses to social encounters (measured in saliva) typically occur over the course of minutes, in some cases peaking 20–30 min after a stimulus has been introduced. Thus, we do not suggest that patterns of change, as measured here, directly stimulate changes in brain activity. Rather, the hormone change across the task should be viewed as a marker of general hormonal exposure across the task, relative to each individual's own baseline, and a signal of activation of the system for releasing the hormone into (or reserving the hormone from) the biological active pool. However, only through studying endogenous hormone levels and changes in real time can we realistically understand the ecological complexity of hormone–brain–behavior relationships.

CONCLUSION

In this study, we explored the neuroendocrine basis of competitive motivation by examining the correlates of testosterone and cortisol with brain activity in frontal and TPJ cortical regions. Completing the speed‐typing task generally resulted in increased activity in frontal cortical areas and decreased activity in the TPJ, across trial type and incentive conditions. Additionally, there was a robust, inverse relationship between task difficulty and TPJ engagement, supporting the view that cognitive challenges trigger a reallocation of resources to task‐positive (frontal) networks. Participants who performed better in the task generally showed comparatively greater activation across the cortical areas assessed, and this effect was most pronounced in repetition trials where difficulty was lowest and effort‐based motivation was most relevant to performance.

Task performance was positively related to testosterone change across the task, but only if cortisol levels showed a simultaneous decrease—providing new evidence for a reactive dual‐hormone effect on performance in this effort‐based competition task. These patterns of hormone change, however, were not directly related to cortical activity, except for one robust effect in which testosterone change in the positive direction was associated with decreased activity in the angular gyrus in the easiest trials. Overall, changing levels of testosterone and cortisol across a contest for rewards may influence the strength and persistence of efforts to succeed. Although this increased effort is also reflected in the executive and attentional areas of the cortex, the overall patterns of change were largely unrelated to levels of activity in the brain, suggesting more indirect pathways for real‐time hormone–brain correspondence.

Perhaps the most important finding regarding hormone–brain relationships involved individual differences in basal levels of testosterone and cortisol. Basal testosterone levels (standardized within sex) uniquely predicted increased vlPFC activity in the right hemisphere. In the right TPJ, the relationship between basal testosterone and brain activity was dependent on both an individual's cortisol levels and task difficulty. This finding provides a novel neural mechanism for the dual‐hormone hypothesis, indicating that cortisol acts as a key modulator in how testosterone relates to the brain circuitry controlling cognitive resources and attention during social contests for status‐related rewards. Combined with other preliminary work, this study contributes to an emerging understanding of how testosterone and cortisol impact social behavior via, perhaps, subtle alterations in motivational salience through attentional shifting to task or goal relevant content.

Although the study was exploratory and the relationships are nuanced, findings could lead to new avenues for future work in this understudied area of hormone–brain correspondence during competitive and status‐related motivational challenges. This work has broad implications for developing models for endocrine regulation of motivation and attention related brain processes underlying social and reward‐seeking behavior.
