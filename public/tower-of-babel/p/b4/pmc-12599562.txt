Complete mitochondrial genome of the Korean Clawed Salamander Onychodactylus koreanus Min, Poyarkov & Vieites, 2012 (Caudata: Hynobiidae)
Authors: Jun Seong Jeong, Ji-Hwa Jung, Yun Jung Kim, Shin Ae Lee, Kyung Jun Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report the complete mitochondrial genome of the Korean Clawed Salamander (Onychodactylus koreanus), endemic to the Korean Peninsula. The 16,445 bp mitogenome, assembled from Illumina HiSeq data, contains 37 genes (13 PCGs, 22 tRNAs and 2 rRNAs) plus the control region and has an A + T content of 68.6%. Phylogenetic analysis confirms it is a distinct lineage within Onychodactylus, closely related to O. fischeri and O. zhaoermii. These findings enhance our understanding of Hynobiidae diversity and evolution.

Introduction

Amphibian populations are declining globally due to habitat loss and climate change, raising concerns about their conservation (Lee et al. 2021; Lacroix et al. 2024). As ecological intermediaries and sensitive bioindicators, they play a vital role in ecosystem health (deMaynadier and Hunter 1995; Anderson and Johnson 2018). The Korean Clawed Salamander (Onychodactylus koreanus), a member of the family Hynobiidae, inhabits fast-flowing streams in forested, mountainous regions of the Korean Peninsula (Werner and Glennemeir 1999; Park 2005; Shin et al. 2020). It was designated a distinct, endemic species in 2012 following a comprehensive taxonomic revision (Poyarkov et al. 2012). Despite studies on its morphology and genetics (Suk et al. 2018; Borzée et al. 2022), genomic data for this species remain limited. This is partly due to its exceptionally large nuclear genome (102.5–103.5 pg for the diploid genome; Poyarkov et al. 2012), which makes sequencing challenging. In contrast, the mitochondrial genome offers a more accessible alternative.

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is a valuable molecular marker widely used in phylogenetic and evolutionary studies due to its high mutation rate and maternal inheritance (Pereira 2000; Weisrock et al. 2013). Recent research on Hynobiidae highlights the importance of mitogenomic data for understanding genetic structure and diversity within the family (Chen et al. 2015). In this study, we present the complete mitochondrial genome of O. koreanus and analyze its phylogenetic placement within Hynobiidae. This genomic resource will contribute to refining the taxonomy, evolutionary history, and conservation genetics of Asian salamanders.

Materials and methods

A specimen of Onychodactylus koreanus was collected by Ji-Hwa Jung and Yun Jung Kim on June 14, 2022, from Baegunsan Mountain, Shin, 2020 Jeollanam-do, South Korea (35°06′23″N, 127°36′02″E), under official permission (Permit No.: 2022-00002). The specimen was identified based on morphological characteristics such as body shape, coloration, and costal grooves (Poyarkov et al. 2012) (Figure 1). The specimen was deposited at the Honam National Institute of Biological Resources (URL: https://ibis.hnibr.re.kr/, contact person: Ji-Hwa Jung, e-mail: herpeto8712@hnibr.re.kr) under the voucher number HNIBRAM162.

Image of the onychodactylus koreanus collected from Baegunsan Mountain, South Korea. The photograph was taken by Ji-Hwa jung using a canon 5D mark IV digital camera (canon inc., Tokyo, Japan) and processed with helicon focus software.

Genomic DNA was extracted from tail tissue using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. A sequencing library was prepared using the TruSeq Nano DNA Kit (Illumina, USA) and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform, yielding 53,116,972 reads. De novo assembly was performed with NOVOPlasty (Dierckxsens et al. 2017), resulting in a 16,445 bp mitogenome with an sequencing depth of 309,720× (organType: mito; gRange: 12000–22000; K-mer: 33; Insert size: 300). Coverage uniformity was confirmed visually (Figure S1). The mitogenome was annotated using the MITOS Web Server (Genetic code: Vertebrate; Reference data: RefSeq 89 Metazoa) (Bernt et al. 2013) and aligned with sequences from other Hynobiidae species for accuracy. A mitogenome map was generated using Proksee (Grant et al. 2023).

Phylogenetic analysis included the complete mitochondrial genomes of 17 Hynobiidae species retrieved from GenBank (Table S1), along with two Cryptobranchidae species used as outgroups. Protein-coding genes (PCGs) were aligned with RevTrans 2.0 (Alignment method: MAFFT; Translation table: Vertebrate mitochondrial) (Wernersson and Pedersen 2003), rRNAs with MAFFT 7.245 (Katoh and Standley 2013). Divergent regions—defined as poorly aligned or gap-rich regions—were removed using Gblocks 0.91b with default parameters, except that sequence type was set to ‘Codons’ for PCGs and ‘DNA’ for rRNAs (Castresana 2000). tRNA genes were excluded from the phylogenetic analysis because their short length and conserved secondary structures make them less informative and prone to alignment ambiguity. IQ-TREE 2.2.2 (Minh et al. 2020) was used for tree construction under the GTR + I + G model with 1,000 ultrafast bootstraps and SH-aLRT tests. A partitioned dataset of 41 subsets (12S, 16S, and codon positions of 13 PCGs) was used, and ModelFinder selected the best model per partition.

Results

The complete mitochondrial genome of O. koreanus was 16,445 bp in length and contained 37 genes, including 13 PCGs, 22 tRNA genes, 2 rRNA genes (srRNA and lrRNA), and a non-coding control region (D-loop) (Figure 2). The overall A + T content is 68.6%, which was characteristic of amphibian mitochondrial genomes (Table S1). Most of the 13 PCGs used ATG as the start codon, except for COX1, which began with GTG. The termination codon was primarily TAA, with a few genes ending in TAG or a single T (Table S2). The total length of the PCGs was 3,789 bp, while the D-loop spanned 787 bp. The srRNA and lrRNA measured 931 bp and 1,582 bp, with AT contents of 63.4% and 67.2%, respectively. The 22 tRNA genes ranged from 67 to 75 bp in length, with anticodons corresponding to the expected amino acid assignments, further confirming the conserved gene structure observed in other amphibian mitochondrial genomes.

Map of the complete mitochondrial genome of onychodactylus koreanus, drawn using proksee (Grant et al. 2023). The GC content is shown as a black line plotted inside the circular genome map, with peaks representing regions of higher GC content. tRNAs are abbreviated with a one-letter code for the corresponding amino acid, and the anticodon is shown in parentheses.

The relationships among major genera within the family Hynobiidae were strongly supported by high bootstrap values, with most nodes showing 100% support, confirming their monophyletic nature (Figure 3). This finding highlights the well-preserved evolutionary history of the family. Onychodactylus koreanus was firmly positioned within the genus Onychodactylus, exhibiting a clear phylogenetic relationship with closely related species, Onychodactylus fischeri and Onychodactylus zhaoermii.

Discussion and conclusion

We successfully sequenced and analyzed the complete mitochondrial genome of O. koreanus for the first time, providing a valuable genetic resource for elucidating its mitogenomic structure and understanding the phylogenetic position and evolutionary history of Hynobiidae. Our results confirm that O. koreanus is placed within the genus Onychodactylus and shares a close evolutionary relationship with O. fischeri and O. zhaoermii, consistent with previous molecular studies. The genus Onychodactylus includes 12 recognized species, 10 of which were described since 2012 (Frost 2025). The mitochondrial genome of O. koreanus presented here provides a key resource that, along with future data, will help resolve relationships within the genus and identify potential cryptic species.

Phylogenetic analysis further supports the monophyly of Hynobiidae and enhances the resolution of interspecies relationships, highlighting the importance of complete mitogenomic data in salamander systematics and conservation genetics. Overall, our findings contribute to the expanding molecular dataset of Hynobiidae, supporting ongoing efforts to refine taxonomy, biogeography, and conservation strategies for amphibian species.
