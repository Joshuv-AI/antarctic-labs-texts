TMEM132A is associated with metabolic reprogramming, macrophage-oriented immune remodeling, and breast cancer progression
Authors: Yi Chen, Ruonan Lin, Jinghao Pan, Lucy Yue Lau, Yibo Hua, Qiqi Chen, Richard J Aldridge, Elizabeth A Whitmore, Wenjia Guo, Zehao Hong, Boyang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer progression reflects malignant cell programs coupled with immune remodeling. Biomarkers that connect tumor intrinsic states with microenvironmental interactions remain needed. We evaluated TMEM132A as a candidate marker in breast cancer. Bulk cohorts from The Cancer Genome Atlas, Genotype Tissue Expression, and Gene Expression Omnibus were analyzed to quantify TMEM132A expression, diagnostic discrimination, and associations with clinicopathologic features and survival. Single cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics localized TMEM132A across cell populations and tissue regions. Cell communication inference with correlation based validation and differential expression based enrichment, including GSEA and GSVA, were performed to characterize TMEM132A related biology. TMEM132A was consistently upregulated in tumors and separated tumor from normal tissue across datasets. High TMEM132A expression was associated with poorer outcomes and more advanced clinical categories. Single cell and spatial analyses localized TMEM132A mainly to malignant epithelial populations and tumor enriched regions, with reproducible links to macrophage related features. Cell communication analyses suggested that TMEM132A-positive malignant cells engage macrophage-associated signaling via the ANXA1–FPR3 and IL34–CSF1R axes; this inference was supported by correlation-based validation and is consistent with macrophage-oriented immune remodeling during breast cancer progression. TMEM132A-high tumors were enriched for cell cycle and DNA replication programs and showed higher activity in glycolysis, pentose phosphate pathway, oxidative phosphorylation, and pyrimidine metabolism. Introduction

Breast cancer (BRCA) is one of the most frequently diagnosed malignancies among women worldwide and remains a leading cause of cancer related mortality in the female population [1]. Despite substantial advances in surgical management, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, chemotherapy, and targeted treatments over recent decades, which have significantly prolonged survival in a subset of patients, disease recurrence, distant metastasis, and therapeutic resistance continue to represent major obstacles to long term clinical outcomes [2, 3]. Breast cancer exhibits pronounced heterogeneity at both the molecular and cellular levels, and patients with similar clinicopathological characteristics often experience markedly different prognoses [4, 5]. Recent studies in triple-negative breast cancer have demonstrated that transcriptome-based molecular signatures can effectively stratify prognosis, reflect immune landscape features, and provide therapeutic guidance. Advanced molecular imaging approaches, such as FAPI-based PET/CT, further highlight the impact of refined staging on treatment decision-making and clinical management in breast cancer [6, 7]. Therefore, systematic elucidation of the key molecular mechanisms driving breast cancer progression and immune evasion is of critical importance for optimizing risk stratification and developing more precise therapeutic strategies.

Transmembrane proteins play essential roles in mediating signal transduction from the extracellular microenvironment to intracellular pathways and are widely involved in fundamental biological processes such as cell adhesion, migration, proliferation, and intercellular communication [8, 9]. An increasing body of evidence has demonstrated that aberrant expression of transmembrane proteins is closely associated with tumor initiation and progression across multiple cancer types [10, 11]. For example, recent studies have reported that TMEM71 functions as an immunoregulatory transmembrane protein in breast cancer, promoting immune cell infiltration and enhancing responsiveness to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy, thereby serving as a potential predictive biomarker for immunotherapy [12]. In contrast, certain members of the TMEM family exhibit pronounced oncogenic properties. TMEM150A is overexpressed in glioma and facilitates epithelial mesenchymal transition and the formation of an immunosuppressive microenvironment, which is closely associated with poor patient prognosis. Similarly, TMEM45A has been shown to enhance glycolysis and cellular proliferation in hormone receptor positive breast cancer through activation of the AKT mTOR signaling pathway, thereby contributing to resistance to cyclin dependent kinase four and six inhibitors [13, 14]. Collectively, these findings highlight the pivotal roles of transmembrane proteins in tumorigenesis, metabolic reprogramming, and immune regulation, underscoring their potential as novel therapeutic targets. Transmembrane protein one hundred thirty two family member A (TMEM132A) has been proposed to participate in cytoskeletal regulation and cell to cell interactions; however, its biological function in cancer has not been fully elucidated [15]. Emerging evidence suggests that TMEM132A may also possess oncogenic potential in multiple malignancies. In gastric cancer, elevated TMEM132A expression is significantly associated with poor patient prognosis and promotes tumor cell proliferation, migration, and invasion through activation of the Wnt signaling pathway, effects that can be reversed by Wnt pathway inhibitors [16]. Meanwhile, in prostate cancer, TMEM132A has been identified as a downstream target of the transcription factor E2F1, which directly binds to its promoter region to enhance transcription, thereby driving cancer cell proliferation and correlating with unfavorable clinical outcomes [17]. These studies suggest that TMEM132A may contribute to malignant progression through dual mechanisms involving signaling pathway activation and transcriptional regulation. Taken together, available evidence suggests that TMEM132A may function as a pro-oncogenic transmembrane protein in multiple tumor types and may contribute to malignant phenotypes through Wnt signaling activation or transcriptional regulatory networks. Although its role in breast cancer has not yet been systematically characterized, existing evidence supports the hypothesis that TMEM132A may participate in metabolic remodeling and microenvironmental regulation [18]. Consequently, its expression patterns, clinical significance, and mechanistic roles in breast cancer warrant further investigation.

The tumor microenvironment plays a critical role in breast cancer initiation, progression, and therapeutic response, with tumor associated macrophages recognized as key contributors to immunosuppression, tumor invasion, and metastasis [19–21]. Gene signatures reflecting myeloid cell differentiation states and natural killer cell–related programs predict clinical outcome, immune microenvironment features, and treatment response in solid tumors, consistent with evidence that tumor-derived signals drive macrophage immunosuppressive programs and noninflamed tumor microenvironments [22, 23]. In addition, adjuvant radiotherapy itself can substantially influence systemic immune status in breast cancer, and treatment-related lymphocytopenia has been reported as a frequent event associated with unfavorable clinical outcomes [24, 25]. In recent years, rapid advances in high throughput technologies such as single cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics have enabled the dissection of tumor cell subpopulations and their interactions with immune cells at single cell resolution and within spatial contexts [26, 27]. These approaches provide powerful tools for identifying malignant cell subsets with specific immunoregulatory functions and for uncovering potential mechanisms of intercellular communication. However, whether TMEM132A defines a distinct malignant subpopulation with unique immunomodulatory properties in breast cancer remains unclear.

Based on this background, the present study integrates multi cohort transcriptomic data with single cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses to systematically investigate the expression landscape, clinical relevance, and immunological implications of TMEM132A in breast cancer. We aimed to evaluate the association between TMEM132A expression and patient prognosis, immune microenvironment remodeling, and to further explore potential cell-to-cell communication mechanisms mediated by TMEM132A-positive malignant cells. This study provides novel insights into the role of TMEM132A in breast cancer and offers a theoretical foundation for its development as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target.

Materials and methods

Expression preprocessing and standardization

Bulk transcriptomic analyses were performed cohort-wise (without direct pooling of raw expression values across platforms); to mitigate residual cross-sample scale effects within each cohort, gene expression values were transformed into dimensionless Z-scores using Z=(x − µ)/σ, where x denotes the expression value of a given gene in a sample, and µ and σ denote the mean and standard deviation calculated within the corresponding cohort. Extreme outliers were removed by excluding samples with Z-scores < − 3 or > 3 for the target gene. After outlier removal, tumor types (for pan-cancer summaries) or subgroup analyses were retained only when the corresponding normal group contained at least three samples.

Data resources, cohort selection, and integrative analysis framework

Public datasets were selected a priori based on endpoint availability and annotation completeness and were used in an endpoint-specific manner rather than being pooled with numerical weighting across platforms. TCGA-BRCA (with GTEx normal breast as reference) served as the primary discovery/reference bulk cohort for expression patterns and clinicopathologic associations, whereas independent GEO cohorts were used for external validation only when the relevant endpoints (e.g., tumor/normal labels, survival definitions, or clinicopathologic variables) were available and compatible. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomics were used to localize TMEM132A across cellular populations and tissue regions and to interpret microenvironmental context, while pharmacogenomic resources were used for exploratory compound prioritization. A dataset-by-endpoint summary describing cohort usage and available annotations is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Differential expression analyses

For paired tumor–adjacent normal comparisons within TCGA-BRCA, TMEM132A expression differences were assessed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. For unpaired tumor versus normal comparisons (TCGA tumor vs. GTEx normal; and external validation cohorts), the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied. Two-sided P values were reported, and multiple testing correction was performed when analyses involved gene panels (e.g., immunomodulators/chemokines) using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) approach.

Diagnostic performance evaluation

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were conducted to evaluate the ability of TMEM132A expression to discriminate tumor from normal samples. ROC curves were generated using the pROC R package, and the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) was computed. Smooth ROC curves were plotted to improve visualization. AUC values closer to 1.0 indicate higher discriminative performance; AUC > 0.7, > 0.8, and > 0.9 were interpreted as moderate, good, and excellent diagnostic accuracy, respectively. For model-based discrimination settings, calibration curves were used to describe agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was applied to evaluate calibration.

Clinicopathological association analyses

Associations between TMEM132A expression and clinicopathological categories (e.g., stage, grade, metastatic status, and molecular subtype) were evaluated using non-parametric tests. For two-group comparisons, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used. For comparisons across three or more independent groups (e.g., multi-level subtype strata), the Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test was applied, followed by post hoc pairwise comparisons when appropriate. Categorical distributions across TMEM132A expression strata were assessed using the chi-square test.

Survival analyses and cutoff determination

Overall survival (OS), disease-specific survival (DSS), progression-free interval (PFI), and disease-free interval (DFI) were analyzed when available. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated using the survival and survminer R packages. The optimal cutoff for dichotomizing TMEM132A expression (high vs. low) was determined using survminer (maximally selected rank statistics), with an additional constraint requiring the proportion of samples in each group to be ≥ 0.30 to avoid extreme imbalance. Alternative cutoffs (including a median split) were examined; for Kaplan–Meier visualization we used a maxstat-derived cutoff with a minimum group-size constraint (≥ 30% per group), and we additionally supported survival associations using restricted cubic spline modeling. Unless otherwise specified, ‘TMEM132A-high’ and ‘TMEM132A-low’ refer to bulk-cohort expression strata defined by the cutoff strategy used in the corresponding analysis (e.g., maxstat-derived cutoff for survival analyses, top/bottom 30% for differential expression analyses, or quartiles for immune-feature distribution analyses). Survival differences were assessed using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression was applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs.

Restricted cubic spline modeling for non-linear effects

To explore potential non-linear relationships between continuous TMEM132A expression and survival risk, restricted cubic spline (RCS) Cox models were fitted. The number of spline knots (3–5) was selected by comparing Akaike information criterion (AIC) values across candidate models, and the model with the lowest AIC was retained. Smooth HR curves and 95% CIs were visualized using the smoothHR package. P values for overall association and non-linearity were reported.

Differential expression and pathway enrichment in bulk cohorts

TCGA-BRCA samples were stratified into TMEM132A-high and TMEM132A-low groups by selecting the top 30% and bottom 30% of samples according to TMEM132A expression, an extreme-contrast strategy commonly used to enhance signal-to-noise for downstream enrichment analyses while retaining adequate sample size in each group. To address potential cutoff-induced bias, we additionally repeated key pathway-level comparisons using a median split as a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1), and the main enrichment patterns remained directionally consistent. Differential expression analysis was performed using limma. Genes were ranked by log2 fold change (log2FC) (or equivalently by standardized delta values when Z-score was used) for downstream enrichment analyses. Over-representation analyses for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways were conducted using clusterProfiler, with FDR-adjusted P ≤ 0.05 considered significant. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using clusterProfiler::GSEA with curated gene sets, including Hallmark and KEGG-related collections. Normalized enrichment score (NES) and FDR were reported. Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) was applied to quantify pathway activity differences between TMEM132A-high and TMEM132A-low tumors.

Functional state scoring based on CancerSEA signatures

CancerSEA functional state gene sets representing 14 tumor-related cellular states were collected. Pathway/state activity was quantified using a z-score–based scoring strategy implemented in the GSVA framework (method = “zscore”), and scores were further standardized using the scale function. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between TMEM132A expression and functional state scores, and significance was assessed using cor.test.

Immune landscape profiling and immunoregulatory feature analyses

The immune subtype framework (C1–C6) and pan-cancer immune landscape features were referenced from the TCGA immune landscape study. Immune subtype labels (C1–C6) and associated immune/genome features were referenced from the pan-cancer immune landscape framework, and TMEM132A expression differences across immune subtypes were assessed using Kruskal–Wallis tests. To summarize immune and genome-state feature distributions across TMEM132A expression strata, patients were categorized into quartiles (Q1–Q4), where Q1 denotes the highest 25% and Q4 denotes the lowest 25% of TMEM132A expression. Mean feature values within each quartile were calculated (excluding missing values) and visualized using pheatmap. This immune subtype and feature framework follows the established TCGA immune landscape study. For immune regulatory molecule profiling, immunomodulators, chemokines, and related immune gene sets were obtained from TISIDB. Differential expression between TMEM132A-high and TMEM132A-low tumors was assessed using Wilcoxon tests, and results were visualized with heatmaps [28].

Spatial transcriptomics analysis

To characterize spatial distribution patterns of TMEM132A in BRCA tissues, spatial transcriptomic data were analyzed through an integrated spatial atlas resource (Sparkle) and/or original 10x Visium datasets when available [29]. Expression matrices were normalized using standard preprocessing (e.g., Seurat NormalizeData), and spatial expression was visualized using Seurat SpatialFeaturePlot. To assess spatial heterogeneity relative to malignant content, spots were stratified based on deconvolution-derived malignant cell proportions: spots with malignant proportion > 0 were defined as malignant regions (Mal), while spots with malignant proportion = 0 were defined as non-malignant regions (nMal). Differential expression of TMEM132A between Mal and nMal regions was evaluated using Wilcoxon tests [30]. Group means were visualized using bar plots. Spot-level correlation analyses were performed to evaluate associations between TMEM132A expression and inferred cell-type abundances.

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis and visualization

Public BRCA scRNA-seq datasets were analyzed using Seurat workflows with author-provided or curated cell-type annotations [31]. TMEM132A expression was mapped across cell populations using feature projection on UMAP embeddings. To mitigate dropout effects and enhance visualization of sparse signals, kernel density–based estimation was performed using Nebulosa when appropriate [32]. Average expression of TMEM132A across major cell types was calculated and visualized using ggplot2. Cells were stratified according to the analytical objective: for cell-level presence/absence analyses, cells with detectable TMEM132A expression were defined as TMEM132A-positive and those without detectable expression as TMEM132A-negative; for abundance-based comparisons within a dataset, cells were further categorized into TMEM132A-high and TMEM132A-low groups using dataset-specific thresholds. Cell-type composition shifts between groups were summarized as proportions. For pathway activity comparisons at the single-cell level, AUCell was used to score immune, metabolic, signaling, proliferation, cell-death, and mitochondria-related gene sets. Differential activity between TMEM132A strata was assessed using limma on per-cell or per-cluster scores after filtering comparisons where either group contained zero cells.

Cell–cell communication inference

Intercellular communication was inferred using cell–cell interaction frameworks (e.g., CellChat) on scRNA-seq datasets. Global interaction networks were constructed, and outgoing/incoming signaling strengths were quantified for malignant subsets stratified by TMEM132A status. Ligand–receptor pairs contributing to malignant-to-macrophage interactions were prioritized based on interaction strength and consistency across datasets. Correlation-based validation was performed by assessing co-variation between TMEM132A and candidate ligand/receptor genes in bulk TCGA-BRCA and within malignant compartments in single-cell data [33].

Drug sensitivity association and connectivity map analysis

To explore TMEM132A-associated therapeutic hypotheses, correlations between TMEM132A expression and drug response metrics were evaluated in pharmacogenomic resources including PRISM and CTRP (drug response summarized by AUC) and GDSC (AUC and/or IC50) [34–36]. Spearman correlation coefficients were computed, and compounds were ranked by correlation magnitude. Connectivity Map (CMap)-based drug repositioning was performed by constructing a TMEM132A-associated gene signature comprising the top 150 upregulated and top 150 downregulated genes derived from differential expression between TMEM132A-high and TMEM132A-low tumors [37]. Signature matching against compound perturbational profiles was conducted using an optimal signature-matching strategy (e.g., XSum), generating similarity/connectivity scores across compounds; lower (more negative) scores were interpreted as candidate compounds potentially reversing the TMEM132A-associated transcriptional signature; this signature-level inference does not imply direct target engagement.

Molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation

To generate a structural hypothesis regarding potential binding between candidate compounds and TMEM132A, molecular docking was conducted to generate an initial ligand–protein complex. The stability of the complex was then evaluated using molecular dynamics (MD) simulation (e.g., 100 ns). Standard structural stability and dynamics metrics were computed, including root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), hydrogen bond occupancy, and free energy landscape analyses. The dominant low-energy conformational basin was used to summarize the most stable binding state over the simulation trajectory.

Statistical analysis and software

All analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.3) unless otherwise specified. Key R packages included pROC (ROC/AUC), survival and survminer (survival analyses and cutoff selection), limma (differential expression), clusterProfiler (GO/KEGG enrichment and GSEA), GSVA (pathway scoring), smoothHR (RCS visualization), pheatmap/ggplot2 (visualization), AUCell (single-cell gene set activity scoring), and Seurat (single-cell and spatial workflows). Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant unless otherwise stated. Multiple testing correction was applied where appropriate using the Benjamini–Hochberg method.

Results

TMEM132A is consistently upregulated in breast cancer and associated with tumor progression

In this study, we implemented an integrative, multi-cohort workflow that combined bulk transcriptomic profiling, single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, immune and metabolic state inference, pharmacogenomic drug-response modeling, and structure-based in silico analyses to comprehensively characterize the expression pattern and clinical relevance of TMEM132A in breast cancer. Such integrative single-cell and bulk transcriptomic strategies have been increasingly recognized as effective for capturing immune landscape features and informing prognostic assessment [38]. TCGA-BRCA (with GTEx normal breast as reference) served as the primary discovery/reference bulk cohort, while independent GEO cohorts were used for endpoint-matched external validation when the relevant annotations were available (Supplementary Table S1). Importantly, we did not pool heterogeneous platforms into a single combined model; instead, analyses were performed cohort-wise/modality-wise using platform-appropriate preprocessing, and robustness was assessed by directional replication across independent datasets. We further integrated immune landscape profiling, cancer immunity cycle scoring, pathway enrichment and activity analyses, ligand–receptor crosstalk inference with correlation-based support, and cross-platform drug sensitivity association analyses, followed by molecular docking and molecular dynamics simulation, to construct a coherent framework linking TMEM132A-high malignant states with macrophage-oriented microenvironmental features and candidate therapeutic vulnerabilities (Fig. 1).

To comprehensively characterize the expression pattern and clinical relevance of TMEM132A in breast cancer (BRCA), we first examined its transcriptional levels across multiple independent cohorts. In the TCGA_BRCA dataset, TMEM132A expression was markedly elevated in tumor tissues compared with normal breast tissues (Fig. 2A). This differential expression pattern was further validated in the integrated TCGA and GTEx cohort, where TMEM132A remained consistently upregulated in malignant samples (Fig. 2B). Concordant results were observed across several independent GEO datasets, including GSE20711, GSE22820, and GSE162228, which were included for tumor–normal expression validation based on available sample labels, supporting robust tumor-associated upregulation of TMEM132A across platforms and populations (Fig. 2C–E; Supplementary Table S1).

We next assessed the potential diagnostic value of TMEM132A in distinguishing tumor from normal tissues. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses revealed that TMEM132A exhibited good discriminative performance in the TCGA_BRCA cohort, which was further enhanced when normal samples from GTEx were incorporated (Fig. 2F-G). Paired sample analysis further confirmed that TMEM132A expression was consistently higher in tumor tissues than in matched adjacent normal tissues, supporting its tumor-specific upregulation (Fig. 2H).

At the clinicopathological level, TMEM132A expression showed a significant association with clinical stage, with differential expression observed across stage I to stage IV tumors (Fig. 2I). Moreover, TMEM132A expression progressively increased with higher histological grades in the TCGA_BRCA cohort and was reproducibly validated in multiple independent GEO datasets (Fig. 2K-T), indicating a close relationship between TMEM132A expression and tumor differentiation status. Analysis of metastatic status further demonstrated that TMEM132A expression was elevated in patients with distant metastasis compared with those without metastatic disease (Fig. 2U-V).

In addition, molecular subtype analysis based on the TCGA cohort revealed a non-random distribution of TMEM132A expression across distinct breast cancer subgroups, suggesting subtype-specific expression preferences (Fig. 2W). At the tissue level, immunohistochemical staining data from the Human Protein Atlas showed stronger TMEM132A protein expression in breast cancer tissues relative to normal breast tissues (Fig. 2X). Consistently, immunofluorescence analysis confirmed the expression and subcellular localization of TMEM132A in breast cancer cell lines (Fig. 2Y). Collectively, these multi-level analyses demonstrate that TMEM132A is consistently upregulated in breast cancer and closely associated with clinicopathological features indicative of tumor progression.

Upregulation of TMEM132A is associated with adverse clinical prognosis in breast cancer

To evaluate the prognostic relevance of TMEM132A in breast cancer, we systematically examined the relationship between TMEM132A expression and patient outcomes across multiple datasets and survival endpoints. A summary landscape integrating different survival types and analytical settings indicated that elevated TMEM132A expression was predominantly linked to adverse prognosis in breast cancer, with no evidence suggesting a protective association (Fig. 3A). Consistently, in the TCGA_BRCA cohort, higher TMEM132A expression corresponded to an increased hazard ratio for disease-specific survival, supporting its unfavorable prognostic implication (Fig. 3B).

For survival visualization, expression strata were defined using an outcome-separating cutoff with a minimum group-size constraint (≥ 30% per group), which improves separation in settings where risk may vary nonlinearly across the expression distribution. Kaplan-Meier analyses further demonstrated that patients in the TMEM132A-high group had significantly poorer overall survival than those in the TMEM132A-low group in the TCGA_BRCA cohort (Fig. 3C). Similar patterns were observed for disease-specific survival and disease-free interval, indicating that the adverse prognostic association of TMEM132A was consistent across distinct clinical endpoints (Fig. 3D-E). These results suggest that TMEM132A expression stratifies breast cancer patients into groups with different risks of unfavorable outcomes.

To validate the robustness of these findings, we performed external prognostic verification in multiple independent GEO cohorts with available and compatible follow-up endpoints (Supplementary Table S1). In GSE7390, the TMEM132A-high group was significantly associated with shorter overall survival and relapse-free survival compared with the TMEM132A-low group (Fig. 3F-G). Concordant results were observed in additional datasets, including GSE11121 and GSE17705, where elevated TMEM132A expression correlated with inferior disease-free or relapse-free survival (Fig. 3H-I). Importantly, the unfavorable prognostic pattern was reproducibly confirmed across a broad panel of independent cohorts, such as GSE20685, GSE22219, GSE42568, GSE45255, GSE58812, and GSE61304, despite differences in sample size, follow-up duration, and clinical composition (Fig. 3J-R). Across these datasets, the high-expression group consistently exhibited worse survival outcomes compared with the low-expression group.

In addition to dichotomized comparisons, restricted cubic spline analysis was applied to characterize the continuous relationship between TMEM132A expression and mortality risk. The results showed a steadily increasing risk of adverse outcomes with rising TMEM132A expression levels, without an apparent threshold effect or risk reversal, supporting a progressive association between TMEM132A expression and unfavorable prognosis (Fig. 3S). Consistently, quartile-based stratification analyses revealed a higher proportion of relapse events in groups with elevated TMEM132A expression, further reinforcing the positive relationship between increasing TMEM132A expression and adverse clinical outcomes (Fig. 3T-U). Collectively, these multi-cohort and multi-endpoint analyses indicate that higher TMEM132A expression is associated with progressively worse prognosis in breast cancer.

Spatial evidence supports TMEM132A enrichment in tumor regions and its stable association with macrophage infiltration

To validate the tumor-associated expression pattern of TMEM132A at spatial resolution, we analyzed multiple breast cancer spatial transcriptomic samples from the GSE210616 cohort (Fig. 4A-G). Across all included specimens, the malignant (Mal) regions consistently exhibited higher mean TMEM132A expression than the non-malignant (nMal) regions (Fig. 4A). Visual inspection of the histological images together with spatial spot mapping further showed that TMEM132A-high signals were largely confined to tumor-enriched areas, whereas relatively non-tumor regions displayed low or minimal expression (Fig. 4B-G). These spatial observations provide independent support for a tumor-biased distribution of TMEM132A expression within breast cancer tissues.

Building on the cell-type spatial annotation, we next assessed how TMEM132A expression relates to the local cellular composition of the tumor microenvironment. Correlation analyses demonstrated that, in every individual sample, TMEM132A expression was positively associated with the abundance of tumor cells (Fig. 4H), indicating that spatial TMEM132A elevation aligns with tumor cell-rich niches. Notably, TMEM132A also showed a consistent positive association with macrophage abundance across all analyzed samples (Fig. 4H). This directionally concordant pattern suggests that TMEM132A-high tumor regions tend to co-occur with macrophage-enriched microenvironments, reflecting a stable spatial linkage between TMEM132A expression and immune cell infiltration profiles. Collectively, multi-sample spatial transcriptomic evidence confirms the enrichment of TMEM132A within malignant regions and reveals a reproducible association with tumor cells and macrophage infiltration in breast cancer.

TMEM132A expression shows broad associations with immune infiltration and immune regulatory features

To further evaluate the relationship between TMEM132A and the tumor immune microenvironment, we performed integrative analyses linking TMEM132A expression to immune infiltration patterns and immune-related molecular features. Immune infiltration estimates suggested that TMEM132A expression correlated with the abundance of multiple immune cell populations, with heterogeneous directions and magnitudes across distinct cell subsets (Fig. 5A). Consistently, correlation matrices derived from broader cohort-level analyses indicated reproducible associations between TMEM132A and immune cell composition as well as immune-related phenotypic features (Fig. 5B). At the functional level, TMEM132A-associated signals were linked to immune response-related processes, including terms related to immune activation, inflammatory responses, and antigen recognition (Fig. 5C). When patients were stratified by TMEM132A expression quartiles, immune response indicators and genome state-related features displayed differential distributions across quartile groups (Fig. 5D). We further profiled immune regulatory molecules across key modules, including co-inhibitory and co-stimulatory factors, cell adhesion molecules, and antigen presentation-related components, and simultaneously summarized their transcriptional expression, methylation patterns, and copy number alteration features under TMEM132A expression stratification considered in this analysis (Fig. 5E). In addition, TMEM132A showed broad associations with multiple categories of immune-related molecules, including immune checkpoint-related factors, immunosuppressive and immunostimulatory mediators, and chemokines (Fig. 5F), collectively indicating a stable linkage between TMEM132A expression levels and immune status as well as immune regulatory molecular features.

Single-cell mapping localizes TMEM132A predominantly to malignant cells and associates high expression with coordinated microenvironmental shifts

Across two independent breast cancer single cell cohorts, BRCA_EMTAB8107 and BRCA_GSE148673, UMAP based annotation consistently resolved a malignant compartment together with diverse immune and stromal populations (Fig. 6A-D). In EMTAB8107, major subsets included Mono Macro, M1, CD8T, CD8Tex, CD8Tcm, B, Plasma, Mast, Fibroblasts, Myofibroblasts, Endothelial, and Tprolif, whereas GSE148673 comprised Mono Macro, CD8T, CD4Tconv, B, Fibroblasts, Endothelial, Epithelial, and Tprolif (Fig. 6A, C). Feature projection of TMEM132A showed that high TMEM132A signals were primarily concentrated within malignant clusters in both datasets, while immune clusters displayed comparatively sparse signals (Fig. 6B, D). Cell type averaged expression further supported that TMEM132A reached its highest mean level in malignant cells. Detectable expression was also present in selected non malignant lineages, particularly stromal and epithelial compartments, including Fibroblasts and Endothelial cells in EMTAB8107 and Epithelial, Fibroblasts, and Endothelial cells in GSE148673. Mono Macro cells also showed measurable expression, indicating that TMEM132A is not strictly restricted to tumor cells, although its expression is most prominent in the malignant compartment (Fig. 6E, F).

(D) Feature plot of TMEM132A expression in BRCA_GSE148673. (E, F) Mean TMEM132A expression across annotated cell types in BRCA_EMTAB8107 and BRCA_GSE148673. When cells were stratified into TMEM132A-high and TMEM132A-low groups, both cohorts displayed directionally consistent and substantial compositional shifts. In EMTAB8107, the TMEM132A-high group showed an increase in malignant cells from 19.5% to 53.2%, accompanied by higher proportions of Fibroblasts from 11.6% to 32.3% and Endothelial cells from 4.9% to 8.5%. In parallel, Mono Macro cells decreased from 8.0% to 1.2%, CD8T cells from 25.1% to 0.6%, and CD8Tex cells from 16.7% to 0.1% (Fig. 6G). Similar trends were reproduced in GSE148673, where malignant cells increased from 35.6% to 80.6% and Epithelial cells from 10.4% to 13.3%, whereas Mono Macro cells decreased from 25.9% to 1.9%, CD8T cells from 8.0% to 0.1%, CD4Tconv cells from 10.9% to 0.2%, and B cells from 2.7% to 0.1% (Fig. 6H). Together, TMEM132A-high status was accompanied by malignant enrichment, reduced myeloid and T cell representation, and relatively increased stromal or epithelial components.

Pathway bubble plots indicated strong cell type dependent functional differences, with the most prominent signals concentrated in malignant cells (Fig. 6I, J). In malignant compartments from both cohorts, proliferation and cell cycle programs were enriched, including E2F targets, G2M checkpoint, mitotic spindle, and broader cell cycle modules, together with oncogenic signaling terms such as MYC targets V1 and MYC targets V2, and additional involvement of p53 pathway and Wnt beta catenin signaling. Metabolic and mitochondrial programs were broadly accentuated, spanning glycolysis, fatty acid oxidation, fatty acid metabolism, cholesterol homeostasis, NAD biosynthesis and metabolism, ROS and glutathione metabolism, and oxidative phosphorylation. These changes extended to mitochondrial structure and respiratory chain modules, including cardiolipin synthesis, cristae formation, electron carriers, cytochromes, Complex I through Complex V, Fe S cluster biogenesis, iron homeostasis, mitochondrial biogenesis, mitochondrial translation, mitochondrial protein import and targeting, and mtDNA related processes. In parallel, multiple regulated cell death associated terms appeared across cell types, including autophagy, apoptosis, ferroptosis, cuproptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis, suggesting that TMEM132A associated states coincide with diverse stress response and death related programs in a lineage dependent manner (Fig. 6I, J).

To clarify how TMEM132A-associated malignant states are related to intercellular signaling patterns, we performed cell communication analyses in BRCA_EMTAB8107 and BRCA_GSE148673. Global interaction networks revealed dense connectivity in both cohorts, with malignant cells serving as major hubs (Fig. 7A). Quantitative comparisons further showed that TMEM132A-high malignant cells displayed stronger outgoing and incoming interaction strength than TMEM132A-low malignant cells, indicating a broadly enhanced communication profile (Fig. 7B). Directional network visualization consistently highlighted malignant to myeloid signaling, with macrophages representing a dominant recipient population in both datasets (Fig. 7C).

We then prioritized ligand receptor pairs that contributed most prominently to malignant to macrophage communication. Two axes, ANXA1 to FPR3 and IL34 to CSF1R, showed particularly strong interaction intensity, with signals originating from TMEM132A-high malignant cells and targeting macrophages across both cohorts (Fig. 7D). Within the malignant compartment, TMEM132A correlated positively with the corresponding ligands, including ANXA1 in EMTAB8107 malignant cells and IL34 in GSE148673 malignant cells, supporting coordinated expression patterns in tumor cells (Fig. 7E). Single cell feature plots and TCGA BRCA correlations further supported alignment between TMEM132A and these pathway components, including ANXA1, IL34, FPR3, and CSF1R, and their distribution across malignant and myeloid related compartments (Fig. 7F and M).

Prior studies have linked FPR3- and CSF1R-related signaling to tumor-associated macrophage functional programs and immunosuppressive microenvironmental features. Based on this rationale, we examined whether these nodes co varied with an immunosuppressive cytokine context in TCGA BRCA. TMEM132A-high tumors exhibited higher TGFB levels, and TGFB1 correlated positively with TMEM132A and with ANXA1, as shown in the three subpanels of (Fig. 7N). Correlation analyses further demonstrated concordant positive relationships among TMEM132A, FPR3, CSF1R, and TGFB1, together with coordinated associations between ligands and receptors, including ANXA1 with FPR3, IL34 with FPR3, IL34 with CSF1R, and CSF1R with TGFB1 (Fig. 7O).

Finally, spatial transcriptomics from GSE210616 supported tissue level co localization. Combined signatures of TMEM132A with ANXA1 and FPR3, and TMEM132A with IL34 and CSF1R, showed structured regional expression across multiple sections (Fig. 7P and R). In spatial correlation matrices, these signatures displayed consistent positive associations with tumor cells and macrophages, aligning with the malignant to macrophage communication features identified in single cell analyses (Fig. 7Q and S).

Transcriptomic and pathway characteristics associated with TMEM132A expression

To characterize transcriptomic alterations associated with TMEM132A expression in breast cancer, we performed differential expression and downstream enrichment analyses using an contrast design (top 30% vs. bottom 30% of TMEM132A expression) to increase interpretability of pathway signals. A large number of genes were identified as significantly upregulated or downregulated between the two groups (|log2 fold change| > 1, adjusted P < 0.05), reflecting widespread transcriptional differences linked to TMEM132A expression (Fig. 8A).

(A) Differential expression analysis between TMEM132A-high and TMEM132A-low tumors shown as a volcano plot. (B-D) Functional enrichment analyses of TMEM132A-associated DEGs, including pathway-gene relationships, KEGG pathway over-representation, and Gene Ontology categories. (E, F) Cross-cohort pathway enrichment analyses using limma-based DEGs and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) comparing TMEM132A-high and TMEM132A-low tumors across multiple GEO datasets and TCGA-BRCA. To organize these differentially expressed genes (DEGs) at the functional level, a pathway-gene interaction network was constructed. Multiple DEGs converged on shared pathways, highlighting coordinated transcriptional patterns across defined biological programs (Fig. 8B). Consistently, KEGG pathway over-representation analysis demonstrated that TMEM132A-associated DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways related to cell cycle regulation, chemical carcinogenesis-receptor activation, neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, glutamatergic synapse, protein digestion and absorption, and aldosterone synthesis and secretion (Fig. 8C).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis further summarized the biological characteristics of these DEGs. Enriched biological processes and cellular components were mainly associated with epithelial structure and differentiation, including intermediate filament organization, keratinization, apical membrane organization, and collagen-containing extracellular matrix. Enriched molecular functions included fatty acid binding and ion channel activity, indicating concurrent alterations in metabolic and membrane-associated features (Fig. 8D).

To assess the reproducibility of pathway enrichment, limma-derived DEGs comparing the top 30% and bottom 30% TMEM132A expression groups were analyzed across multiple independent GEO cohorts and the TCGA-BRCA cohort. A substantial proportion of KEGG pathways exhibited consistent enrichment patterns across datasets, supporting the stability of TMEM132A-associated functional signatures (Fig. 8E). As a sensitivity analysis, we repeated the KEGG pathway evaluation and pathway-level comparisons using a median split of TMEM132A expression and the major enrichment patterns were highly similar to those obtained using the top/bottom 30% strategy, indicating that our conclusions are not driven by a single cutoff choice (Fig. 8E–G and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further identified pathway-level differences between TMEM132A-high and TMEM132A-low tumors. Gene sets positively enriched in the TMEM132A-high group were primarily related to DNA replication, cell cycle progression, and checkpoint regulation, whereas negatively enriched gene sets were associated with alternative signaling and metabolic programs (Fig. 8F).

At the pathway activity level, GSVA analysis in the TCGA-BRCA cohort showed that TMEM132A-high tumors displayed increased activity of pyrimidine metabolism, glycolysis and gluconeogenesis, pentose phosphate pathway, oxidative phosphorylation, tricarboxylic acid cycle, and glutathione-related metabolism. In contrast, TMEM132A-low tumors showed relative enrichment of fatty acid metabolism and amino acid degradation pathways (Fig. 8G). Correlation analysis further demonstrated that TMEM132A expression was positively associated with apoptosis, cell cycle, DNA damage, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, invasion, metastasis, and proliferation signatures, while associations with angiogenesis, hypoxia, inflammation, quiescence, and stemness were weak or not significant (Fig. 8H).

Drug sensitivity prediction and molecular dynamics analysis targeting TMEM132A

To prioritize candidate compounds potentially associated with TMEM132A expression, we systematically evaluated correlations between TMEM132A and drug response across multiple pharmacogenomic resources. In the PRISM dataset, TMEM132A expression showed significant correlations with the response to multiple small-molecule compounds, encompassing both positive and negative associations (Fig. 9A). These drug-gene associations were subsequently ranked according to their correlation strength with drug response metrics.

Drug sensitivity prediction associated with TMEM132A expression and molecular dynamics analysis of the clofibrate–TMEM132A complex. (A–E) Correlation analyses between TMEM132A expression and drug response across PRISM, CTRP, GDSC1 and GDSC2 datasets, based on AUC and IC50 metrics, with compound ranking according to correlation strength. (F, G) Connectivity Map analysis showing compound connectivity scores across cancer types and ranking of candidate compounds in breast cancer, based on transcriptional signature similarity. (H) Molecular docking model (in silico predicted pose) of clofibrate bound to TMEM132A. (I–L) Molecular dynamics simulation of the clofibrate–TMEM132A complex, including root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), radius of gyration (Rg), and hydrogen bond dynamics. (M, N) Free energy landscape analysis of the clofibrate–TMEM132A complex and identification of the lowest-energy conformational basin in the simulated trajectory

Analysis of PRISM-derived area under the curve (AUC) values further identified multiple compounds whose sensitivities were significantly correlated with TMEM132A expression, including both positively and negatively associated agents (Fig. 9B). Comparable correlation patterns were observed in the CTRP dataset, where TMEM132A expression demonstrated reproducible associations with drug response profiles (Fig. 9C). In addition, drug sensitivity analysis based on GDSC data confirmed significant correlations between TMEM132A expression and the response to a subset of anticancer compounds when evaluated using both AUC and IC50 metrics (Fig. 9D-E). Together, these analyses identified convergent TMEM132A–drug response associations across independent pharmacogenomic platforms; these correlation-based findings are exploratory and do not establish causality or therapeutic efficacy.

To further integrate these results at the transcriptomic perturbation level, a Connectivity Map (CMap) analysis was performed. Connectivity scores varied across cancer types, with BRCA showing a comparatively strong signature-level connectivity signal (Fig. 9F). In the CMap signature-matching analysis, clofibrate exhibited a negative connectivity score in BRCA, suggesting a potential ability to counteract the TMEM132A-associated transcriptional signature. Consistent with this observation, clofibrate ranked among the more negatively connected compounds in BRCA (Fig. 9G). Overall, pharmacogenomic correlations and CMap results are hypothesis-generating and do not demonstrate target engagement or clinical benefit.

To generate an in silico structural hypothesis for a possible clofibrate–TMEM132A interaction, we performed molecular docking followed by molecular dynamics (MD) simulation. Docking analysis predicted a plausible binding pose of clofibrate within a putative pocket of TMEM132A, involving contacts with several residues (Fig. 9H). Based on the docking conformation, a 100 ns MD simulation was performed to evaluate complex stability. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) of the clofibrate-TMEM132A complex reached equilibrium at approximately 20 ns and remained stable thereafter, with an average RMSD of 5.47 Å (Fig. 9I). Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis showed residue-level flexibility ranging from 0.74 to 85.00 Å, with an average RMSF of 2.61 Å (Fig. 9J). The radius of gyration (Rg) ranged from 30.82 to 36.08 Å (mean 32.48 Å), suggesting no marked global expansion of the modeled complex over the trajectory (Fig. 9K). Hydrogen bond analysis showed that 0–4 hydrogen bonds were present throughout the simulation, with an average of 1.36 hydrogen bonds (Fig. 9L). Free energy landscape analysis revealed a predominant low-energy basin in this trajectory, suggesting a dominant conformational cluster for the modeled clofibrate–TMEM132A complex (Fig. 9M–N). Importantly, docking and MD simulations provide computational hypotheses only and do not demonstrate biological binding, functional inhibition, or in vivo target engagement.

Discussion

In the interpretation of key findings, our comprehensive multi-omics analyses indicate that TMEM132A is a tumor-associated marker linked to adverse clinical outcomes and immune–metabolic features in breast cancer. TMEM132A expression was significantly upregulated in tumor tissues compared with normal counterparts, associated with advanced pathological stage, and predictive of unfavorable clinical outcomes. By integrating bulk transcriptomic, spatial transcriptomic, and single-cell datasets, we further found that TMEM132A-high tumors were characterized by enhanced cell cycle activity, profound metabolic reprogramming, and macrophage enrichment accompanied by immunosuppressive signaling features, supporting TMEM132A as a candidate node associated with tumor proliferation, metabolism, and immune evasion.

An increasing body of evidence supports the tumor-promoting role of TMEM132A across multiple cancer types. In lung adenocarcinoma, higher TMEM132A expression has been reported to be associated with immune microenvironment remodeling, increased susceptibility to venous thromboembolism, and unfavorable clinical outcomes [39]. Oncogenic functions of TMEM132A have been reported in gastric cancer, where TMEM132A promotes tumor cell proliferation and migration through activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway [16]. Mechanistically, TMEM132A has been demonstrated to interact with Wntless, thereby stabilizing Wnt ligand transport and activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling [40]. More recent studies have demonstrated that TMEM132A enhances β-catenin stability and promotes LRP6 phosphorylation, leading to activation of canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling. Given the established role of this pathway in tumor growth and invasiveness, these findings suggest a potential role for TMEM132A in malignant progression [41, 42]. Taken together, prior experimental studies provide mechanistic context for TMEM132A-related oncogenic programs, and our breast cancer multi-omics results are consistent with TMEM132A being associated with proliferative and metabolic activation.

Beyond its tumor-intrinsic effects, our results highlight a close association between TMEM132A expression and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in breast cancer, particularly macrophage enrichment. More broadly, comprehensive decoding of the tumor microenvironment has become central to biomarker discovery and the development of immunotherapeutic strategies [43]. This is consistent with recent integrative single-cell and spatial transcriptomic analyses of breast ductal carcinoma progression that resolve stage-specific immune ecological remodeling and macrophage-linked microenvironmental shifts [44, 45]. Tumor-associated macrophages are well recognized as key mediators of immune evasion, angiogenesis, and metastasis, and their abundance often correlates with poor therapeutic response. We observed that TMEM132A-high tumor regions co-express IL-34 and CSF1R, two critical regulators of macrophage recruitment and differentiation. Previous studies have shown that IL-34/CSF1R signaling sustains macrophage survival and can shape macrophage functional programs that support tumor progression, while blockade of this axis can reduce macrophage infiltration and tumor burden [46]. In addition, macrophage-modulating pathways such as the ANXA1/FPR3 axis have been implicated in breast tumor progression [46–48]. Collectively, these observations support an association between TMEM132A-high states and macrophage-related immunosuppressive signaling features; whether TMEM132A directly modulates cytokine-mediated tumor–macrophage communication requires dedicated functional validation.

Our functional enrichment analyses further revealed that TMEM132A-high tumors are enriched for pathways related to cell cycle progression, MYC targets, glycolysis, and oxidative phosphorylation, indicating a state of metabolic activation. In parallel, targeting mitochondrial functions and behaviors is increasingly recognized as a promising strategy for overcoming cancer chemoresistance [49]. This finding is consistent with emerging evidence linking Wnt/β-catenin signaling to metabolic reprogramming and immune exclusion, and provides a rationale for future studies to clarify the directionality and causal relationships involved. In breast cancer, activation of Wnt/β-catenin has been shown to drive metastasis and suppress antitumor immune responses. Given reports that TMEM132A can stabilize components of the Wnt pathway, future studies should test whether TMEM132A influences Wnt/β-catenin activity and downstream metabolic programs in breast cancer, and whether such effects relate to immune evasion. Moreover, metabolic reprogramming in both tumor and immune compartments is increasingly recognized as being associated with therapy resistance and immunosuppression, which is consistent with the integrated immune–metabolic phenotype observed in TMEM132A-high tumors [50].

When considering speculation and future directions, the strong association between TMEM132A and immune metabolic dysregulation also suggests potential therapeutic implications. Targeting macrophage-related pathways, such as CSF1R inhibition or macrophage reprogramming strategies, has shown promise in reducing tumor progression and enhancing responses to immunotherapy [51–53]. In this context, TMEM132A may represent a candidate node for future functional interrogation rather than a validated therapeutic target. Our pharmacogenomic association analyses and CMap signature matching prioritized clofibrate as a candidate compound in BRCA, and docking/MD provided an in silico structural hypothesis for a possible interaction [54]. Importantly, these computational results do not demonstrate biological target engagement, functional inhibition, or in vivo efficacy. Confirmation will require biochemical binding and target-engagement assays (e.g., SPR/ITC/CETSA) and cellular/in vivo perturbation experiments to determine whether TMEM132A is druggable and whether clofibrate (or related compounds) modulates TMEM132A-associated phenotypes. Key limitations and the most immediate validation priorities are summarized below.

When acknowledging the limitations of this study, several key points should be noted. First, dichotomizing a continuous biomarker (including outcome-optimized thresholds) can inflate effect estimates and may reduce portability across cohorts, particularly when endpoint definitions and patient composition differ. To mitigate this, we constrained cutoff selection to avoid extreme imbalance (minimum group-size requirement), validated findings across independent cohorts, and complemented group-based Kaplan–Meier analyses with continuous restricted cubic spline modeling; nevertheless, clinically transferable cutoffs will require prospective validation. While our multi-omics analyses provide robust correlative and bioinformatic evidence, direct mechanistic validation is still lacking. Future studies should investigate whether TMEM132A directly regulates IL-34/CSF1R or ANXA1/FPR3 signaling and delineate its impact on macrophage recruitment, functional state/phenotypic programs, cytokine secretion, and tumor metabolic programs. In vivo loss-of-function or pharmacological inhibition studies will be essential to determine whether targeting TMEM132A can restore antitumor immunity, reverse metabolic reprogramming, or sensitize breast tumors to immunotherapy. Together, these efforts will be critical for determining whether TMEM132A can be functionally targeted and for validating the therapeutic relevance of the hypotheses generated by our in silico analyses.

Conclusion

By integrating bulk, single-cell, spatial, and multi-cohort analyses, we identified TMEM132A as a tumor-enriched biomarker in breast cancer that is associated with adverse clinical outcomes and advanced disease stage. TMEM132A was predominantly expressed in malignant cells and was closely linked to macrophage-related immune features. Cell–cell communication analyses suggested that TMEM132A-positive malignant cells may engage macrophage-associated signaling through the ANXA1–FPR3 and IL34–CSF1R axes, potentially contributing to macrophage-oriented immune remodeling during malignant progression, a finding supported by correlation-based validation. TMEM132A-high tumors also exhibited enrichment of cell cycle and DNA replication programs accompanied by remodeling of metabolic pathways. Collectively, these results support TMEM132A as a risk stratification marker and a candidate molecule associated with malignant cell–macrophage crosstalk in breast cancer.
