Association of quantitative renal surface nodularity with the renal dysfunction progression in patients with arterial hypertension
Authors: Qiong Wu, Jun Zhou, Haotian Yang, Liang Pan, Wei Xing, Jie Chen, Jiule Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertensive nephropathy, a major cause of end-stage renal disease, lacks reliable noninvasive biomarkers. Renal surface nodularity (RSN) on CT may reflect nephrosclerosis, but existing methods fail to quantify nodularity heterogeneity or predict renal decline. This study developed a novel CT-based RSN metric and assessed its prognostic value in hypertensive patients.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included hypertensive patients who underwent contrast-enhanced CT. Patients with bilateral renal surface irregularities were assigned to the RSN group, with age- (± 2 year) and sex-matched controls (non-RSN group) randomly selected at a 1:1 ratio. RSN was quantified using three surface roughness metrics. A semi-quantitative RSN score was also calculated based on the distribution and depth (> 50% cortical thickness) of localized surface defects. The primary endpoint was composite renal dysfunction progression (≥ 25% eGFR decline or initiation of renal replacement therapy). The secondary endpoint was defined as an annual decline in eGFR > 5 ml/min/1.73 m² per year.ResultsA total of 242 patients were included (median age, 64 years; 70.25% male). Strong linear correlations were observed between quantitative RSN metrics and semi-quantitative score (all ρ > 0.9, P < 0.001). Over a median follow-up of 38.00 months (Interquartile range: 22.00-56.25), patients in the RSN group had a significantly higher risk of renal dysfunction progression (33 vs. 5 events; HR = 5.22, P < 0.001). After adjusting sex, age, hypertension grade, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, eGFR, and renal volume, renal dysfunction progression was independently associated with arithmetic mean deviation (HR = 1.798, 95% CI: 1.296–2.495, P < 0.001), maximum height (HR = 1.606, 95% CI: 1.177–2.191, P = 0.003), and ten-point unevenness height (HR = 2.239, 95%CI: 1.567–3.197, P < 0.001), respectively. The similar statistical results trend was present between RSN and the secondary endpoint.ConclusionsCT-based quantitative RSN was developed as a novel imaging biomarker for renal dysfunction progression in patients with arterial hypertension.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-025-01995-5.

Introduction

The kidney serves as a major target organ in arterial hypertension, with hypertensive nephropathy ranking as the second most prevalent etiology of end-stage renal disease [1, 2]. The pathogenesis of hypertensive nephropathy is characterized by myointimal hyperplasia in interlobular and afferent arterioles, accompanied by hyaline arteriolosclerosis, resulting in glomerular and tubular ischemia that evolves into widespread glomerulosclerosis, tubular atrophy, and interstitial fibrosis [3]. Given this irreversible progression, the development of accurate and clinically feasible methods for renal dysfunction progression remains critical for effective prevention and management of hypertensive nephropathy [4].

The clinical assessment of hypertensive nephropathy currently depends on nonspecific biomarkers, including serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and urinary albumin excretion, alongside patient symptomatology. However, these conventional indicators suffer from significant limitations. They lack pathological specificity and could be confound by extrarenal factors. Serum creatinine levels are influenced by muscle mass, dietary protein intake, and nephrotoxic drugs, while urinary albumin excretion fluctuates with blood pressure and glycemic control [5–7]. While kidney biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing hypertensive nephropathy, its clinical utility is limited by invasiveness and procedural risks, including perirenal hematoma, infection, and hematuria. In contrast, non-invasive imaging modalities, contrast-enhanced abdominal CT, serve critical roles in cardiovascular assessment and the exclusion of secondary hypertension etiologies (e.g., renal parenchymal disease, adrenal disorders, or renal artery stenosis) [8, 9]. Beyond their established diagnostic applications, these imaging techniques may offer valuable adjunctive data for predicting renal dysfunction progression.

Image-based renal surface nodularity (RSN), being analogous structural change in renal imaging reported by Mayo Clinic researchers, was characterized by nephron hypertrophy and nephrosclerosis with increased surface roughness in transplanted kidney biopsies [10, 11]. RSN may play a potential role in identification and monitoring of nephrosclerosis [10]. Subsequent studies developed a semi-quantitative RSN grading system, demonstrating its association with both hypertension and hypertensive nephropathy [12–14]. Our prior work further established that quantitative RSN independently correlated with elevated cystatin C levels (>1.02 mg/L) with an odds ratio of 14.37 (95% confidence interval: 2.75, 75.15) [13]. However, two critical knowledge gaps persist: (1) existing quantitative RSN methodologies fail to incorporate heterogeneity in nodularity size, and (2) the prognostic significance of quantitative RSN features for renal dysfunction progression in hypertensive patients remains unclear.

Therefore, this study seeks to: (1) develop a novel multi-dimensional quantitative RSN based on contrast-enhanced CT imaging, and (2) investigate the longitudinal relationship between quantitative RSN characteristics and the renal dysfunction progression in patients with arterial hypertension.

Materials and methods

Demographic information

This multi-center study was approved by the local institutional review board (No: 2021-science-153). The study included the patients from four hospitals (the Third Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, the First Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, the Second Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University, and Changzhou No. 2 People’s Hospital) from January 2012 to December 2021 (Fig. 1). The inclusion comprised: (1) admission to the department of cardiovascular medicine due to arterial hypertension; (2) performance of abdominal contrast-enhanced CT scan within the one-week interval. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) history of urinary tract infection or obstruction; (2) congenital kidney anomalies or hereditary nephropathies; (3) renal cysts or masses exceeding 1 cm in maximal diameter; (4) prior renal surgery; (5) significant renal size asymmetry; (6) history of renal infarction; (7) active or prior malignancy; (8) incomplete renal function data or inadequate CT image quality for quantitative analysis.

Patient selection flow chart

The RSN group comprised of patients exhibiting RSN of bilateral kidneys on contrast-enhanced CT imaging. RSN positivity was defined as the presence of ≥ 3 localized surface defects on each of three consecutive hilar slices during the arterial phase, as assessed by an experienced radiologist (10 years of specialization, T. Wang). Patients not meeting the criteria were assigned to the non-RSN controls. Non-RSN controls were randomly matched to RSN patients at 1:1 by age (± 2 year) and sex.

Collected covariates included: (1) Demographics: age, sex; (2) Comorbidities: hypertension grade (WHO/ criteria), diabetes mellitus (yes or no), hyperlipidemia (yes or no), hyperuricemia (yes or no), heart failure (yes or no), nephrolithiasis (yes or no); (3) Lifestyle factors: smoking status (yes or no), alcohol consumption (yes or no); (4) Renal function markers (within 7 days pre-CT): urine protein, and eGFR.

The patients were monitored for ≥ 0.5 years. The primary composite endpoint is ≥ 25% decline in eGFR or initiation of renal replacement therapy (dialysis/transplantation) [15]. The secondary endpoint was defined as an annual decline in eGFR >5 ml/min/1.73 m² per year. The annual decline in eGFR was defined as the ratio of change in eGFR over time in years [16].

Imaging acquisition

Multicenter imaging was performed using several scanner models: General Electric: Optima 64 and Revolution CT, Siemens Healthineers: Somatom Definition Flash CT, and Philips Healthcare: Brilliance 256 ICT. The main scanning parameters were as follows: slice thickness/spacing 5 mm, field of view 35–40 cm, matrix size 512 × 512, tube voltage 120 kV, automatic tube current modulation. A power injector was used, with an injection dose of 1.0–1.5 ml/kg. Renal cortical phase CT scans were conducted 30–40 s post-injection, respectively.

Renal volume measurement

Renal volume (both kidneys per patient) was measured using arterial-phase CT axial images processed in 3D-Slicer software (version 5.4.0) through the following standardized protocol: after setting appropriate abdominal window settings (width: 200 HU; level: 60 HU), the renal volume of both kidneys (excluding pelvis, vessels and perirenal fat) was manually delineated using the Segment Editor module, with subsequent volume calculation (cm³) automatically performed for each patient.

Image analysis

Both quantitative and semi-quantitative RSN assessments were performed on one axial CT image of the left kidney at renal hilum level at the arterial phase (window width: 200 HU, window level: 60 HU). All CT images were primarily evaluated by an abdominal radiologist with 10 years of experience (Reader 1), who was blinded to renal function data. To assess the inter-observer agreement in quantitative and semi-quantitative RSN, a second radiologist (Reader 2) with 5 years of abdominal imaging experience independently performed an additional image analysis of data from the Third Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (n = 148).

Semi-quantitative RSN assessment

The semi-quantitative RSN assessment comprised two components: coverage score and severity score. The renal surface was systematically divided into 10 equal segments for standardized assessment. The coverage score (range 0–10) reflected the number of segments exhibiting irregular renal surface contour. The severity score (range 0–10) quantified segments demonstrating local defects exceeding 50% of cortical thickness. The composite RSN score, representing the sum of both components, yielded a maximum possible score of 20 (Fig. 2).

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range, IQR), while categorical data were described by the frequency and percentage of each category. Inter-observer agreement was assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient with >0.75 indicating good consistency [20]. The comparison between the two groups was performed using t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, or chi-square test. Correlations between quantitative metrics and semi-quantitative RSN score were evaluated using Spearman. Kaplan-Meier curves were constructed to estimate renal dysfunction prognosis. Variables with P < 0.20 in univariable Cox regression were entered into the multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models, along with traditionally considered risk factors (age, sex, and hypertension grade) that were included irrespective of their univariable significance. The RSN metrics (ten-point unevenness height, maximum height, and arithmetic mean deviation) were standardized, and HRs for these metrics are expressed per 1-standard deviation (SD) increase. For other covariates, HR is expressed per unit of the original measurement scale. The univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to assess the associations between the variables and the secondary endpoint. P < 0.05 was statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 27.0.1.0) or R software (Version 4.3.3).

Correlation between quantitative and semi-quantitative RSN

The Ra ranged from 1.01 to 6.83. The Ry ranged from 3.09 to 24.41. The Rz ranged from 1.07 to 6.86. A strong linear correlation was observed between the quantitative RSN metrics and the semi-quantitative RSN, respectively (all ρ > 0.9, Fig. 5).

Longitudinal outcomes and regression analyses

Over a median follow-up of 38.00 months (IQR: 22.00–56.25), the main endpoint event was present in 33 patients in RSN group and 5 patients in non-RSN group (P = 0.001, Fig. 6). In univariable Cox regression, the elevated RSN parameters (arithmetic mean deviation, maximum height, and ten-point unevenness height) were associated with increased risk of renal dysfunction progression (P < 0.05, Table 3).

Discussion

This study developed a novel automated method for quantitative RSN using arterial-phase CT imaging. The developed quantitative RSN metric exhibited strong linear correlations with semi-quantitative RSN score. The three quantitative RSN metrics emerged as robust independent risk factors for renal dysfunction progression in patients with arterial hypertension.

The quantitative RSN assessment method developed in this study demonstrates significant advantages over traditional semi-quantitative approaches. First, semi-quantitative assessment relies on visual interpretation, making the results susceptible to observer subjectivity [24]. In contrast, the automated image processing and analysis used in this method reduces subjective bias and enhances inter-operator reproducibility [25]. Second, the quantitative RSN method is user-friendly, reduces reliance on operator expertise, and significantly shortens interpretation time, thereby enhancing efficiency [26]. Finally, this method enables multi-dimensional quantification of RSN, providing more precise information on renal morphological changes and renal dysfunction progression. In the long-term dynamic monitoring of renal function, changes in quantitative RSN may facilitate timely adjustment of therapeutic interventions.

The heterogeneous nature of hypertensive nephropathy necessitates comprehensive morphological evaluation [27, 28]. Under ideal conditions, if renal surface nodules in a patient with hypertensive nephropathy were perfectly uniform in size and distribution, these three quantitative RSN metrics would yield identical results. However, in clinical reality, the pronounced spatial heterogeneity of renal surface nodules imposes inherent limitations on conventional unidimensional assessments: while the arithmetic mean deviation of RSN reflects overall variation, it fails to identify focal severe lesions; conversely, the maximum nodule height captures extreme outliers but overlooks diffuse injury patterns. In contrast, the ten-point unevenness height measurement provides a comprehensive “point-to-area” integration by simultaneously incorporating both the five largest nodules (reflecting extreme values) and five lowest valleys (reflecting background heterogeneity). This dual-perspective approach enables more accurate characterization of the true pathological changes in hypertensive nephropathy. Therefore, ten-point unevenness height shows promise as a distinct imaging biomarker for hypertensive renal dysfunction progression.

To our knowledge, elevated blood pressure levels typically correlate with more severe renal dysfunction progression. However, our study revealed no significant association between hypertension grade and renal dysfunction prognosis, which may be explained by several factors. First, acute blood pressure elevation (measured at a single timepoint in this study) may not reflect chronic hemodynamic burden or circadian variability being known determinants of renal dysfunction progression [29, 30]. Second, the duration of hypertension exposure (unavailable in our retrospective cohort) represents a critical determinant of renal damage accumulation that may outweigh the importance of absolute timepoint values [31]. Third, emerging genetic evidence highlights the role of inherited susceptibility in modulating hypertension-related nephropathy independent of blood pressure severity [32]. These considerations suggest that quantitative RSN metrics, as imaging biomarkers capturing cumulative renal structural changes, may provide more robust prognostic stratification than conventional hypertension grading in clinical practice.

The coexistence of hypertension and diabetes confers a higher risk of renal dysfunction progression, reflecting the increased susceptibility to renal injury [33]. This synergistic effect can be attributed to several interconnected mechanisms, including metabolic disturbances (e.g., insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, oxidative and endoplasmic reticulum stress), activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system leading to glomerular damage and proteinuria, as well as shared histopathological features, such as arteriolar hyalinosis, glomerular hypertrophy, and hyperfiltration [34, 35]. These mechanisms highlight the importance of strict control of both blood pressure and blood glucose to mitigate renal disease progression [36].

The observed independent association of hyperlipidemia with a lower risk of renal dysfunction progression in our study requires careful consideration. Notably, the generalizability of this finding may be limited, given that the study consisted solely of hypertensive patients referred for a contrast-enhanced CT, who likely have distinct clinical characteristics. Future studies with detailed information on treatments, particularly lipid-lowering therapy, are crucial for elucidating this association. Although a low baseline eGFR is a well-established risk factor for renal dysfunction progression, it was not independently associated with the outcome in our multivariable Cox regression analysis. This suggests that the predictive power of eGFR was overshadowed by the RSN metrics, indicating their stronger impact on risk stratification in our study.

This study has several limitations. First, the inclusion criteria were restricted to hypertensive patients, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Nevertheless, the pathophysiological pathways of renal injury are shared between hypertensive and diabetic nephropathy. Therefore, the RSN metrics, which capture the renal structural alterations, may also have potential applicability for risk stratification in non-hypertensive populations, such as patients with diabetes. Second, retrospective design relying on electronic medical records constrained our ability to comprehensively assess renal function changes and account for all potential confounders. Third, although we observed significant associations between RSN metrics and renal dysfunction progression, the observational nature of this study precludes definitive causal conclusions. Last, in this study, a ≥ 25% decline in eGFR was used as the primary endpoint. This definition may increase the sensitivity for detecting renal dysfunction progression but could also capture transient or less clinically meaningful changes in kidney function. Given the relatively small number of outcome events in our cohort, this threshold was selected to ensure adequate statistical power. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to validate the findings using more established surrogate endpoints for kidney disease progression.

Conclusion

This study developed the CT-based quantitative renal RSN, showing significant association of the three quantitative RSN metrics with renal functional decline in hypertensive patients, indicating quantitative RSN metrics can serve as novel imaging biomarkers of renal dysfunction progression.
