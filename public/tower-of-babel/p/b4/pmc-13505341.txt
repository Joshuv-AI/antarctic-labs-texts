An Idiographic, Mixed Methods Approach to Understanding Adolescent-Dog Relationships in the Context of Social Anxiety
Authors: Megan K. Mueller, Jasmine Mote, Eric Anderson, Brianna Paquette, Siyu Zhang, Angelique Zhong, Jordanne Brown, Linda Charmaraman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocial anxiety is prevalent among adolescents, however there is limited research examining how adolescents with social anxiety can be supported in coping adaptively with stressors. In particular, close relationships with dogs may provide regulation and support in this context.ObjectiveThe objective of this study is to use an idiographic, mixed methods approach to explore adolescent–dog relationships in real-life contexts.MethodsWe present four illustrative cases of adolescents with high levels of social anxiety who represent a range of levels of pet-dog relationship quality, integrating quantitative and qualitative data sources: (1) continuous electrodermal activity using ambulatory psychophysiological sensors to measure arousal, (2) ecological momentary assessment of real-time of emotion and behavior, (3) quantitative survey data from both adolescents and parents/guardians, and (4) in-depth qualitative interviews (conducted separately with both parents and youth).ResultsFindings indicate individual variability in how and when adolescents seek proximity to their dogs and in the coherence between physiological arousal, loneliness, and positive/negative affect. This variability was often linked to the strength of the adolescent’s relationship with their dog. There were differential patterns in the quality of adolescent-dog relationships and how this relationship may be linked to anxiety reduction, with some youth reporting a pathway between their dog relationship and outcomes and others showing no clear associations.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the value of combining digital phenotyping with qualitative narratives to explore lived experiences of adolescent anxiety. Findings highlight the potential for more personalized, ecologically valid interventions that leverage existing family and pet relationships.

Adolescence is a developmental period of social transitions that can play a key role in shaping social and emotional functioning linked to long-term mental health and well-being (Graber & Brooks-Gunn, 1996; Resurrección et al., 2014). During this stage, the rapid changes in social relationships as well as physical, cognitive, and psychosocial domains can increase susceptibility to mental health challenges. Social anxiety is especially salient in this context, as adolescents place greater emphasis on peer relationships and gradually shift their primary sources of emotional and social support away from parents and toward peers (Chiu et al., 2021; Leigh & Clark, 2018). Recent research has demonstrated that relationships with companion animals1, and dogs in particular, may attenuate the effects of social stressors and anxiety for youth (Kerns et al., 2018; Kertes et al., 2017) and may facilitate specific behavioral and cognitive strategies associated with adaptive coping (Mason et al., 2026). However, the existing evidence regarding pets and mental health in adolescence is limited. Specifically, there are notable gaps regarding specificity in how relationships with dogs can support adaptive behaviors and emotions in real-world contexts, and how these relationships may vary across different individuals and circumstances. Idiographic, person-centered research approaches that leverage multiple data sources are key to addressing these gaps in knowledge, and can expand our understanding of how dogs may support adolescents experiencing social anxiety. These person-centered approaches can often better maintain ecological validity while providing tailored, family-specific interventions to support adolescents, their dogs, and their families.

Dog Interactions and Psychophysiological Reactivity

Heightened physiological arousal is a key feature of social anxiety (Banerjee & Sharp, 2008) and the reduction of arousal can assist with coping with social anxiety via emotion regulation and reduction in appraisal of stress. Recent evidence has shown that during a social stressor, a pet dog can buffer perceived social stress (Kertes et al., 2017) and support positive affect (Janssens et al., 2021; Kerns et al., 2018). Further, emerging evidence indicates that interacting with an animal can attenuate physiological responses related to stress by impacting the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis cascade in ways that support adaptive responses through support and appraisal modulation (Pendry & Vandagriff, 2020), as well as physical touch (Beetz et al., 2012; Binfet et al., 2022; Polheber & Matchock, 2014).

The interaction between the social and physiological aspects of human-dog relationships is a significant component of understanding how dogs can reduce physiological arousal (a key component of adaptive coping) for youth with social anxiety. Prior research on dog interactions and physiological responses has focused on therapy dog interactions in interventions or structured experimental designs (Barker et al., 2010; Pendry & Vandagriff, 2019). These types of interventions typically involve a therapy dog handler and a trained, unfamiliar dog, which differs substantially from interactions in daily life with familiar pet dogs. While more structured animal-assisted therapy approaches have been effective in reducing anxiety in some settings (Binfet et al., 2022; Pendry et al., 2018), prior work showed that short periods of contact with an unfamiliar therapy dog did not buffer acute social stressors in a laboratory setting (Mueller et al., 2021). Given the nature of social anxiety (compared to more generalized acute anxiety), therapy animal interactions could potentially unintentionally increase anxiety from social interactions with the animal handlers who are often not familiar to the participants. In the specific context of adolescent social anxiety, interactions with adolescents’ own dog in their daily life may be more impactful on physiological arousal than interacting with an unfamiliar therapy dog. However, little research focuses on the role of dogs in the contexts of daily life over a longer time period (i.e., not during an isolated acute stressor) and how these interactions may be related to adolescent functioning across many different contexts (e.g., family interactions, peer interactions, coping with stressful events). Therefore, we need to understand the complex interplay between the social and psychophysiological processes involved in interacting with a pet dog within a non-intervention setting, including when and how adolescents seek out their dogs for support.

Idiographic Approaches to Measuring Dog Interactions in the Context of Social Anxiety

Since impairments from social anxiety are often context-dependent (i.e., only related to or in response to social interactions), as well as individually specific, an idiographic approach is needed to clarify how and when dogs may confer physiological and affect-related benefits. Specifically, an approach that combines ecological momentary assessment (EMA) with wearable sensors measuring physiological arousal, in addition to quantitative surveys and qualitative interviews, can help provide a nuanced, comprehensive understanding of adolescents’ social anxiety (and the associated experiences of loneliness deriving from social anxiety) in the context of their dog interactions.

Ecological momentary assessment, also known as the experience sampling method, encompasses brief self-report questionnaires administered to participants as they live their daily lives. (Csikszentmihalyi & Larson, 2014; Myin-Germeys et al., 2009). Often through personal mobile devices, participants are prompted multiple times a day for days or weeks to report on various experiences. EMA can be combined with other passive data streams from a smart device as a means of digital phenotyping, or “moment-by-moment quantification of the individual-level human phenotype in situ using data from personal digital devices” (Onnela, 2021, p. 45). The combination of active (e.g., EMA) and passive (e.g., geolocation) data offers multiple strengths over traditional assessment methods, including the remote, unobtrusive assessment of a variety of phenomena that are context-specific (e.g., interacting with one’s pet dog in one’s home) (Onnela, 2021). Momentary reports of constructs such as social anxiety and loneliness show both within- and between-subjects variability as well as discriminant validity from trait-based measures (Goodman et al., 2021; Halvorson & Kuczynski, 2024).

Modern wearable sensors can add information about physiological arousal by measuring biological signals such as electrodermal activity. These signals can be continuously and unobtrusively measured while participants are engaging in the activities of their daily lives. Wearable sensors worn on the wrist are generally not as accurate as wired laboratory equipment (e.g., Mindware or Biopac), or mobile equipment designed specifically for research (e.g., (Kleckner et al., 2021; Qu, 2024), however wrist based sensors are acceptable and feasible for adolescents to wear for long periods of time (King et al., 2023). Empatica wrist sensors such as the E4 (prior generation) and Embrace (current generation), offer enough accuracy to track changes in laboratory stressors (Mueller et al., 2021) and are more accurate than other consumer devices (Ronca et al., 2023).

Recent arguments have been made to take a phenomenological approach when utilizing EMA, especially within mental health populations (Wilson et al., 2025), but these approaches have not been used extensively in human-animal interaction research. While EMA offers opportunities to take an idiographic approach to understanding participants’ lived experiences, researchers often fall back on only using traditional quantitative approaches (e.g., translating a quantitative trait-based measure to a state-based one). The inclusion of qualitative data alongside digital phenotyping allows for a deeper exploration of any dynamic, context-dependent relationships we may observe through both active and passive data streams. For example, if a participant shows reductions in electrodermal activity (EDA) activity and self-reported social anxiety when they are with their dog, a qualitative interview with that participant’s parent may shed additional light on the contributing factors as to why there is high coherence for that individual in arousal and self-reported anxiety that we would otherwise fail to assess.

The value of combining person-specific methods that involve digital phenotyping and ambulatory approaches to measuring psychophysiology with more traditional methods of self-report survey data and qualitative interviews is becoming more widely acknowledged (Busshart et al., 2026; Onnela & Rauch, 2016), yet these approaches have not yet been used in the context of person-dog relationships. In the context of adolescent-dog interactions, these person-specific methods are particularly important due to the high level of potential variability in how youth and dogs interact and in what contexts. For example, identifying when and how adolescents with social anxiety seek out contact with their dogs, if the dog’s physical presence is necessary for conferring support or if the relationship provides overall resilience, as well as possible coherence among active, passive, and qualitative data streams, will better inform our phenomenological understanding of how dogs could support adolescents with social anxiety. This type of integrated, comprehensive data analysis may also inform recommendations for more effective, personally tailored interventions that address the specific needs of individual families and their dogs within their contexts.

Current Study

The goal of this study is to illustrate the value of an ideographic approach by presenting four cases of adolescents within our sample in which we integrate multiple sources of data to capture a holistic, individualized assessment of how dog interactions may support adolescents with social anxiety. For each participant, we report data from continuous measures of EDA, combined with EMA of emotions and behaviors (loneliness, positive and negative affect, and social anxiety) collected during a week-long period, quantitative survey data from both the adolescent and their parent/guardian, as well as themes from qualitative interviews.

Design

This study uses a mixed-methods, longitudinal study design that includes self-report survey data, qualitative in-depth interviews, repeated measures ecological momentary assessment combined with continuous psychophysiological data collection.

Participants

For this set of case studies, participants were selected from a broader sample of 514 adolescent/parent dyads participating in the Teen & Dog Study (Mueller et al., 2025), a longitudinal mixed-methods study assessing the role of dog companionship in adaptive coping for adolescents with social anxiety. Participants in the broader study include adolescents aged 13 to 17 years and their parents or guardians. Adolescents were eligible to participate if they had at least one dog living with them in the household where they spent at least 50% of their time during the study period, and screened as having high social anxiety based on the Social Anxiety Scale for Adolescents (SAS-A), where high anxiety is defined as a score of 50 or higher; (La Greca & Lopez, 1998). To be eligible, participants needed to be able to speak and read English, Spanish, or Portuguese, have a parent/guardian willing to participate, and live in the United States.

For this case study analysis, four participants (referred to by pseudonyms) were selected based on the following criteria: (1) participation in all elements of the study, including the quantitative survey, EMA/EDA, and interview, (2) good adherence to the EMA prompts (defined as responding to over 60% of the prompts), and (3) a range of dog relationship quality across the four participants in order to descriptively explore patterns in data for participants at different levels of relationship quality. Two participants were chosen for having high positive dog relationship scores [Ellie and Lena], and two were chosen for having lower dog relationship scores or higher levels of friction with their dog [Molly and Lennox].

Procedure

Survey

Youth and their parents were asked to complete a quantitative survey that was administered online through the Research Electronic Capture (REDCap) platform (Harris et al., 2009). Parents and youth were sent unique links to separate online surveys, which took approximately 15–25 min to complete. Participants were compensated with a $20 gift card for each survey. Data from the first year of surveys are presented in this paper; youth/parent dyads will continue to complete annual surveys for a total of four years.

Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) and Electrodermal Activity (EDA)

Participants completed a seven-day (one week) data collection period. Prior to the start of this period, participants were shipped a package with an Empatica EmbracePlus wristband, an iPad (if needed), an instruction booklet, and a prepaid return envelope and label to return the study materials after the completion of data collection. During the data collection period, participants wore the Empatica EmbracePlus wristband (Empatica Inc., n.d.), which continuously measures EDA. Participants were instructed to wear the wristband on their non-dominant hand, 1 to 2 centimeters above their wrist. Participants were also instructed to wear the wristband at all times, except while swimming or charging the wristband. For comfort and to allow for charging, participants could elect to remove the wristband while sleeping. In addition to the physiological arousal data, participants completed EMA prompts using the LifeData mobile app, which is a secure platform designed for use in clinical trials (LifeData, n.d.). The EMA survey asked participants to self-report their activities and emotions. All EMA responses were timestamped, allowing for integration with EDA data. A fixed sampling approach was used to maximize adherence for participants (Vachon et al., 2019) given the high variability in adolescents’ daily schedules (e.g., school days vs. weekends; days with extracurriculars vs. not). Participants received seven prompts per day at two-hour intervals (e.g., 8am, 10am, 12pm, 2pm, 4pm, 6pm, 8pm local time). Participants were compensated with a $100 gift card for the seven-day data collection period, plus an additional $25 gift card if they respond to 60% or more of the EMA prompts.

Qualitative Interviews

Parent-youth dyads were interviewed separately by trained research assistants and audio-recorded for transcription. Interviews were approximately 45 min in duration and conducted via video call in English. Two interview guides (one for youth and one for their parent/guardians) were used to assess the family-level processes related to adolescent-dog relationships. Participants were compensated with a $40 gift card for each interview.

Measures

Survey Measures

Social Anxiety Scale for Adolescents

Participants were screened for eligibility using the SAS-A; (La Greca & Lopez, 1998). The SAS-A is an 18-item measure meant to assess adolescents’ subjective experience with social anxiety. Items were rated on a Likert scale from 1 (not at all) to 5 (all the time). Individual scores were summed to create an aggregate score which could range from 18 to 90; Cronbach’s α = 0.87. Aggregate scores of greater than 50 indicate clinically high levels of social anxiety, and those equal to or lower than 36 indicate low social anxiety (La Greca & Lopez, 1998). Participants were required to score above 50 in order to be eligible to participate in the study.

Pet-Dog Relationship Inventory

Family-dog relationships were evaluated using the Child and Parent-report versions of the Pet Dog Relationship Inventory (PDRI-C, PDRI-P) (Kerns et al., 2023). The 30-item measure assesses six factors in pet dog relationships: Affection, Nurturance of Pet, Emotional Support from Pet, Companionship, Friction from Pet, and Pets as Substitutes for People. The PDRI-C asks youth to report on their relationship with their dog, and the PDRI-P prompts parents to report how they view their adolescent’s relationship with the dog. Items are measured on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (almost never true) to 5 (almost always true). Example items include “I feel devoted to my family dog,” for the child version, and “My child comforts the family dog when it’s upset,” for the parent version. Both PDRI measures are scored by aggregating the responses across the items; higher scores indicate higher endorsement of that quality. Cronbach’s alphas for the subscales ranged from .82 to .90 for the PDRI-C and .82 to .89 for the PDRI-P.

Responses to Stress Questionnaire (RSQ) - Peer Stress

To evaluate adolescents’ coping strategies for managing peer stress, we used the Child and Parent Self-Report Responses to Stress Questionnaires - Peer Stress (RSQ) (Connor-Smith et al., 2000). The 57-item measure reports both volitional coping techniques and involuntary responses to domain stressors and specific situations. Participants rate how often they use each coping method or experience each type of involuntary stress response on a scale of 1 (Not at all) to 4 (A lot). The measure yields six factors: primary control coping (problem solving, emotional regulation, emotional expression), secondary control coping (positive thinking, cognitive restructuring, acceptance, distraction), disengagement coping (avoidance, denial, wishful thinking), involuntary engagement (rumination, intrusive thoughts, physiological arousal, emotional arousal, involuntary action), and involuntary disengagement (emotional numbing, cognitive interference, inaction, escape). Proportion scores are created for each factor to indicate the ratio of each strategy relative to other coping strategies. Cronbach’s alphas for the subscales ranged from 0.80 to 0.93 for youth-report and 0.74 to 0.93 for parent-report.

UCLA Loneliness Scale

To measure adolescents’ perceived loneliness, a modified 5-item version of the UCLA Loneliness Scale was used (Panayiotou et al., 2023). Items are measured on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (Not at all) to 4 (A lot). Sample items include “[h]ow lonely do you feel?” and “[d]o you feel that people are around you but not with you?” The items are summed to create an overall loneliness score (with positively valenced items reverse-scored), with higher scores indicating higher reported loneliness, Cronbach’s α = 0.74.

Physiological Arousal

Physiological arousal was measured using the Empatica EmbracePlus wristband which continuously records EDA, an index of sympathetic nervous system activation (Empatica Inc., n.d.). The wristband uses embedded sensors to measure skin conductance in microsiemens (μS). Higher values indicate greater physiological arousal. The wristband connected via Bluetooth to the participant’s mobile device using the Empatica Care Lab mobile app, which automatically uploaded the data to the study’s Empatica cloud storage.

Ecological Momentary Assessment Measures

Dog Presence

Participants were asked A) “Are you with your dog right now?” and B) “Who were you with in the past 2 hours?” If participants selected “My dog” on question B, they were prompted to respond to the dog interaction questions.

Dog Interactions

Four items were used to assess participants’ interactions with their dog. Participants were first asked to report the amount of time spent with their dog in the past two hours by selecting one of four response options: less than 30 min, 30 min to one hour, 1 h to 1 h 30 min, or 1 h 30 min to 2 h. Next, participants were asked what they are/were doing with their dog by selecting all that applied (e.g., dog was with me on the couch or bed, walking/running, petting, playing fetch or another game, riding in the car, obedience/training, dog was in the same room as me, feeding, or other [open response]). Participants also rated how enjoyable their interaction was on a Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 4 (very enjoyable).

Social Interactions

Participants were asked who they were with in the past two hours. They were instructed to select all that apply from the following list: my parents, my siblings, other family members, friends, school or teammates, teachers or coaches, strangers, other animals, I was not with anyone, or other (open response).

Social Anxiety

Three items were averaged to assess perceived momentary social anxiety. Participants were asked how much they agree with the items, rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 7 (very much), with higher scores indicating greater levels of social anxiety. The items included: “Right now, I am worried that I will say or do the wrong things,” “Right now, I am worried what other people think of me,” and “Right now, I am afraid that others do not approve of me.” Kashdan and Steger (2006) adapted these items from past research (Rodebaugh et al., 2004), and Goodman et al. (2021) demonstrated acceptable reliability in prior EMA study protocols.

Loneliness

Perceived momentary loneliness among adolescents was assessed using a modified 4-item version of the UCLA Loneliness Scale (Panayiotou et al., 2023). Items were rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 4 (a lot). Example items include: “Right now, do you feel that you are in tune with others?” (reverse-scored) and “Right now, do you feel that no one really knows you well?” Items were averaged and higher scores indicated greater levels of loneliness.

Affect

Participants reported their momentary positive and negative affect using six items, consistent with the affective circumplex model of emotion (Russell et al., 1980). Positive affect was assessed with the items: “Right now, how calm do you feel?”, “Right now, how excited do you feel?”, and “Right now, how happy do you feel?” Negative affect was assessed with the items: “Right now, how sad do you feel?”, “Right now, how anxious do you feel?” and “Right now, how angry do you feel?” Item were rated and averaged from a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 4 (a lot), with higher scores indicating greater levels of positive or negative affect, respectively.

Location and Physical Activity

Activity and location questions were included to provide context when interpreting changes in physiological data and responses to other EMA prompts, respectively. Participants reported whether they had engaged in any physical activity or exercise (e.g., running, playing sports) since the last EMA prompt. Responses were recorded as “yes” or ‘no.” Additionally, the location question offers valuable environmental context, which can help with understanding where the participant is while responding to the prompts, which may influence both their physiological responses and emotional experiences recorded.

Data Analysis

Quantitative survey data and EMA prompt data were analyzed using SPSS version 30.0. Quantitative data are presented descriptively, with participant scores compared descriptively to the overall sample means. For the EMA data, participant responses when they were with their dog versus without their dog are reported descriptively, including with means (M) and standard deviations (SD) for momentary social anxiety, positive and negative affect, and loneliness across the entire data collection period. Given the case study design, inferential statistics were not conducted, and the results focus on descriptive comparisons to illustrate potential differences associated with dog presence.

EDA data were analyzed in R (version 4.4.2) across 7-day monitoring periods using a comprehensive preprocessing pipeline that included participant-specific time zone standardization based on device metadata, precise timestamp alignment between EMA prompts and EDA measurements. Missing or invalid data were detected using Empatica’s built in proprietary algorithms. To capture physiological responses associated with dog presence assessments, average EDA was calculated as the mean skin conductance level within a 10-minute window (± 5 min) surrounding each EMA prompt. Separate EDA averages were computed for time points where participants were with their dog or not with their dog. All data processing, statistical analysis, and visualization were conducted using the dplyr, lubridate, ggplot2, and plotly packages in R.

Qualitative interviews were reviewed in their entirety for each of the four cases, with two authors identifying relevant themes/quotes that were pertinent to each participant’s survey and EMA prompt data. As this was a case study, interview data were not analyzed systematically using a specific theoretical approach, rather the relevant data were purposively extracted to illustrate or provide more context surrounding the quantitative findings.

Overall, the three different data streams were integrated through a holistic, descriptive examination of each case, including visual inspection of EDA plots with other quantitative data layered on to EDA. The case study design allowed interpretation of the different types of data in relation to one another, allowing us to generate insights for each adolescent and their dog within their particular context. We did not conduct any inferential statistics or testing given the descriptive, case-based nature of the analyses.

Results

Survey measures and EMA responses for each case as well as the overall sample averages are reported in Tables 1 and 2. For each of the following cases, the dogs were reported as being family pets, not tied to only one family member.

Ellie – High Dog Relationship Quality

Ellie was a 14-year-old female participant who identified as White, non-Hispanic/Latino/a/x/Spanish. Ellie lived in a suburban location in the Northeast with her two parents, three siblings, and a 1-year-old Shepherd/Husky dog as their only pet. Ellie did not report any mental health diagnoses or current mental health treatment. She scored 71 on the social anxiety scale, higher than the overall sample mean of 62.25.

Dog Relationship Quality (Self-Reports and Interviews)

Ellie was selected for inclusion based on her high PDRI scores on the positive subscales of dog relationship quality (affection, nurturance emotional support, and companionship). Both she and her parent reported high levels of youth-dog bonding at the top range of the scale. This bond was underscored by her mother, who reflected Ellie’s emotional attachment to the dog:

It’s super, super strong…. I think [dog] might be the first person, creature she says hello to, and good morning to and the last one that she says good night to, and she definitely cannot go to sleep without saying good night to her, for sure.

Despite these strong ratings, Ellie also reported more friction with her dog (Ellie’s score = 18, sample mean = 10.24; see Table 1), although her parent did not report high friction (score = 10). In her interview, Ellie suggested that some of the friction could be centered around her dog coming into her room while she is not there and disrupting her space:

Ellie: I feel like if she’d stop coming into my room when I leave the house, I’d be a lot happier with her. But yeah, other than that, I think we’re good.

Interviewer: Yeah. So, like she just like comes into your room and does she like mess things up?

Ellie: Whenever I make my bed, she’ll come in here and move all the blankets around to make herself comfortable, which yeah.

Ellie indicated that she is very involved in caring for her dog, helping daily with tasks such as feeding, walking, playing, spending time with, participating in training/agility classes, dressing up her dog, and shopping for her dog.

Stress and Coping Strategies (Self-Reports and Interviews)

Ellie reported that her dog helps her through situations where she is worried about being around people. Both Ellie and her parent reported that she had relatively high levels of peer stress (youth score = 27, parent = 24, both above the sample mean of 21.86). Ellie’s self-reported overall coping scores were about average compared to the overall sample, with her highest reported domains being secondary control, which includes positive strategies (acceptance, positive thinking, cognitive restructuring), and involuntary engagement, which includes less adaptive strategies (rumination, intrusive thoughts, physiological arousal, emotional arousal). Ellie shared that she talks with her dog when she is anxious, stating “Yeah, I’ll sometimes just like rant to her because she’ll actually listen….I like talking to her, especially because she doesn’t interrupt me when I’m talking. Yeah, I usually do feel better after talking to her.”

Overall, Ellie seemed to have utilized some adaptive coping strategies for managing peer stress yet also experienced less adaptive arousal behaviors. In contrast, Ellie’s parent rated involuntary engagement as Ellie’s highest coping strategy, suggesting a mismatch between parent and adolescent perceptions of coping or increased visibility of these arousal behaviors for the parent.

Dog-Adolescent Interactions, Anxiety, Loneliness, and Affect (EMA)

Ellie’s EMA data aligned with the strong relationship quality indicated in the survey and interview measures. Her dog was present for 58% of the prompts (higher than the sample average of 38%), and she reported being with her dog within the last 2 h for 67% of the prompts, highlighting the frequent time they spent together. Enjoyability of these interactions was above the scale midpoint and was generally positive, with no interactions rated as not enjoyable. Ellie’s momentary reports of social anxiety were lower than compared to the overall sample and were slightly lower when she was with her dog. She reported slightly higher momentary loneliness compared to the overall sample (though her global loneliness score was lower than the overall sample average). Ellie reported slightly lower loneliness when her dog was present compared to when her dog was absent. Similar patterns emerged for affect. Positive affect was slightly higher when her dog was present compared to when absent, while negative affect was slightly lower with her dog present than when absent.

Physiological Arousal

Two examples of Ellie’s EDA data on a weekday and a weekend are presented in Fig. 1, covering a 14-hour window from 7:00 to 21:00. On the weekend day (Fig. 1A), Ellie’s EDA showed substantial variability, with a peak at right before 10:00 followed by an immediate drop that occurred when she was with her dog. To provide some context for this peak, she reported that she had been playing fetch or another game with her dog in the time prior to the 10:00 prompt, which could mean that her increased arousal was linked to physical activity. During the weekend day (Fig. 1B), her EDA data demonstrated several distinct peaks, with another peak immediately preceding a drop where she reported that she had been playing a game/fetch with her dog. During the peak that occurred midafternoon (14:00), Ellie reported being with friends and strangers; this social interaction may have been associated with physiological arousal. Across the 7-day sampling window, Ellie exhibited higher mean EDA during moments when she was with her dog (0.45 μS) compared to without the dog (0.24 μS). Though speculative, this might be consistent with Ellie’s report that she likes to spend time with her dog to cope with being around others (i.e., she is spending time with her dog during moments of heightened physiological arousal).

Summary

Overall, the combination of Ellie’s data illustrates a strong relationship characterized by high engagement and a potentially positive impact of dog interactions on loneliness and affect. While their interactions may include some friction around the dog disrupting Ellie’s space, this could be a function of her dog’s relatively young age and does not seem to be overall detrimental to their relationship. Patterns in EDA were less clear, with no apparent positive impact of dog contact on EDA, though this may be confounded by Ellie’s report that she enjoys spending time with her dog during anxiety-provoking contexts (i.e., with other people).

Lena – High Dog Relationship Quality

Lena was a 16-year-old female participant who identified as both Black/African American and White, non-Hispanic/Latinx/Spanish. Lena lived in an urban location with her two parents, one sibling, and a 2-year-old mini Berner-doodle as their only pet. Lena did not report any mental health diagnoses or current mental health treatment. She scored 72 on the social anxiety scale, higher than the overall sample mean of 62.24.

Dog Relationship Quality (Self-Reports and Interviews)

Lena reported relatively high levels of relationship quality with her dog across all the subscales on the PDRI and low levels of friction. In her interview, she described caring for her dog daily and engaging in various activities such as playing, spending time with him, taking him to the vet or groomer, feeding him, going shopping or to restaurants with him, and even having him sleep in her bed with her. She noted that her dog also engages with her friends and facilitates their interactions: “I have my one friend who really loves him. She wants to take him for walks with me and stuff, and just cuddle with him.”

However, in her interview, Lena emphasized that she is not as attached to her dog as her mother: “she’s [the mother] just very attached. It’s like her favorite thing is the dog.” In another exchange, Lena indicated how her relationship with the dog compared to her mother’s:

Interviewer: Do you feel similar when you’re out of the house? Or is it more intense with her [your mother]?

Lena: I wouldn’t say I’m that attached to him. I’ll go out like a lot and I’d be with my friends like all day. I’ll come back later, and I’m not like really longing for him. But then, when I am with him, I’m like obviously happy and stuff.

Interestingly, Lena’s mother rated Lena’s relationship with the dog as very strong, at the highest point on the scale for each dimension (and lowest for friction). Her mother also noted that their dog helps to de-escalate conflicts between the two of them:

I think it’s helped in terms of if me and her [Lena] have an argument, and I feel really upset and she feels really upset. She [Lena] goes up to her bedroom, and we sort of decompress for a little bit. And I think [her dog] is good in those areas where he is like that calming presence, and he’ll go up with her, and it’s like he knows that she’s upset. And so, he’ll be just next to her. He likes to give kisses, so I think that helps in terms of like having him around her when me and her at odds.

Stress and Coping Strategies (Self-Reports and Interviews)

Both Lena and her mother reported that Lena had higher than average peer stress compared to the overall sample. At the same time, she reported how helpful her dog is in helping her cope:

If I have like people over, let’s say, like, my mom has people over. I feel better when [my dog is] downstairs with me because sometimes I get anxious about if they’re asking me questions. Then I have [dog] there, and I can like pet him, and he can kind of distract me, so he makes it less awkward.

Similar to Ellie, Lena’s most utilized coping strategies were secondary control coping (acceptance, positive thinking, and cognitive restructuring) and involuntary engagement (rumination, intrusive thoughts, physiological and emotional arousal, involuntary action). In comparison, Lena’s mother reported her most used strategy was involuntary engagement, which, similar to Ellie, may be a reflection of dysregulation being more apparent to the adolescent’s parent.

Dog-Adolescent Interactions, Anxiety, Loneliness, and Affect (EMA)

Lena’s EMA data supported her statement that she is happy when she is actively with her dog and previous statements related to her dog helping her cope with socially anxious situations. She reported being currently with her dog for 42% of the prompts and had been with him within the last 2 h for 53% of the prompts. She rated all of these interactions as highly enjoyable – indeed, she never rated the interaction below the highest enjoyability rating throughout her EMA reports, which was notable within the sample.

Despite reporting higher than average peer stress in self-report measures, Lena’s scores on momentary social anxiety were lower than the overall sample. Additionally, she reported lower anxiety when with her dog compared to when her dog was not present. Lena’s global loneliness score was somewhat higher than the overall sample, yet her momentary loneliness was similar to the sample mean. Interestingly, she reported slightly higher loneliness when she was with her dog compared to when not with her dog. For affect, Lena reported slightly higher positive affect and lower negative affect when with her dog compared to when not with her dog.

Physiological Arousal

Examples of Lena’s EDA data during 14-hour windows from 7:00 to 21:00 on both a weekday and a weekend day are shown in Fig. 2. EDA levels on the weekday demonstrated several distinct and sharp peaks, most notably in the late morning (~ 09:30–10:30) and again in the mid-afternoon (~ 15:30–16:30), when she reported being in a public place with teammates and coaches. These peaks occurred during moments when she was not with her dog, denoted by red dots and coincided with elevated self-reported momentary anxiety. In contrast, EDA levels were lower and more stable during periods in the early evening (~ 17:30–20:00) when she reported being at home with her dog (denoted with green dots). On the weekend day, Lena showed a slightly different pattern, with high EDA levels in the morning (when she reported being with her dog), some gaps in data during the middle of the day with a drop in EDA that slowly increased towards the evening hours when she reported being at a someone else’s house with friends. Overall, across the 7 days, Lena’s average EDA was lower when she was with her dog (1.80 μS) compared to when not with her dog (2.35 μS).

Summary

Lena’s combination of data indicates that she has a good relationship with her dog, yet her parent has an even more elevated perception of the strength of their relationship, which could be related to the mother’s own strong attachment to the dog. Her interview responses suggest that Lena’s dog may help facilitate positive coping in social situations and peer interactions, though her overall coping still includes involuntary strategies that may be less adaptive. Interestingly, Lena reported in interviews that her relationship with her dog was not as strong as her mother’s relationship with their dog. However, her EMA reports suggest a highly enjoyable relationship in daily life with her dog and less social anxiety when with her dog, potentially reflecting a coping strategy that Lena’s mother might be more aware of than Lena herself. Alternatively, perhaps Lena views the relationship less “strategically” yet enjoys interacting with her dog in the moment.

Molly – Low Dog Relationship Quality

Molly was a 14-year-old female participant who identified as Black/African American, and non-Hispanic/Latino/a/x/Spanish. She lived in a rural location in the Southeast with her two parents, no siblings, and a 3-year-old Presa Canario dog, who was the only pet living in the household. Molly did not report any mental health diagnoses or current mental health treatment. She scored 61 on the social anxiety scale, which was similar to the overall sample mean (62.24), though still above the cutoff for high social anxiety (50).

Dog Relationship Quality (Self-Reports and Interviews)

Molly was selected for inclusion based on her low dog relationship quality, as indicated by the PDRI. Among the four cases, this dyad had the lowest scores on the positive relationship subscales (affection, nurturance, emotional support, and companionship), which were well below the mean scores for the full study sample. Further, both parent-reported friction (score = 13) and teen-reported friction (score = 16) were well above the sample means. Taken together, this suggests Molly had a relatively weak relationship with her dog. However, she did report helping to care for her dog a few days per week and engaging in activities such as feeding and spending time with her.

During the qualitative interview, her parent described the dog as being more connected to Molly than Molly was to the dog, often seeking out interaction and showing signs of missing her.

I think the dog is a little more caring of her [Molly]. You know, even if she’s [Molly’s] not. If she’s [Molly’s] not here, she’ll [the dog] you know, go in her room looking for her, she’ll lay on her rug like she misses her, you know misses her if she’s not there. So I think [dog] might care a …little more for her.

Stress and Coping Strategies (Self-Reports and Interviews)

When asked directly in the interview whether having a pet affected how Molly manages stress, she stated “no.” Similarly, when asked about the dog’s role in Molly’s anxiety, her parent recognized Molly’s social anxiety, however also reported that activities such as walking the dog did not encourage greater social interaction with others:

Interviewer: Do you think having a pet is able to help with some of those problems? For instance, if she’s walking the dog, does she interact a little bit more?

Parent: No.

Interviewer: Okay, so she’s not as likely to talk to others. She just walks the dog like “Nope, I’m just walking the dog. Don’t look at me. Don’t talk to me.”

Parent: Yes.

Molly’s perceived peer stress score on the RSQ (score = 20) was comparable to the other case study participants. However, her parent’s perception of her peer stress was notably lower (score = 10), indicating a discrepancy between the youth and parent perception of peer stressors. Molly reported engaging in coping strategies at similar levels to the overall sample, with her most frequently used strategy being involuntary engagement (rumination, intrusive thoughts, physiological arousal, emotional arousal, and involuntary action).

Dog-Adolescent Interactions, Anxiety, Loneliness, and Affect (EMA)

Molly’s EMA data appeared to align with her survey data, as she reported a relatively low percentage of time spent with her dog. She reported being currently with her dog during 21% of the prompts and had been with him in the past 2 h for 23% of the prompts. Further, she reported lower enjoyment during interactions with her dog compared to the overall sample. Although none of the interactions were rated as not enjoyable, 60% were rated as only slightly enjoyable, 40% as moderately enjoyable, and 0% as very enjoyable.

Despite Molly’s relatively low global social anxiety score compared to the rest of the sample (61), she reported the highest momentary social anxiety of the four cases, which was well above the sample mean. This suggests that Molly may have differentially reported global social anxiety as compared to momentary anxiety or was experiencing higher than normal social anxiety during the week-long sampling burst. Molly reported relatively similar momentary social anxiety when with her dog compared to without her dog, consistent with her and her parent’s report that her dog is often not used as a coping strategy for Molly’s anxiety. Regarding loneliness, Molly reported higher overall loneliness via the survey compared to the sample mean. In the EMA, she also reported slightly higher momentary loneliness compared to the overall sample, which was also the highest among the four case study participants. There was minimal variation in loneliness regardless of whether she was with her dog or not with her dog, unlike some other participants who showed more differentiation by dog presence. Similarly, Molly had slightly lower positive affect and slightly higher negative affect compared to the overall sample, though these differences were not substantial. Molly reported slightly lower positive affect when with her dog compared to when not with her dog. There were no notable differences in negative affect with compared to with or without her dog.

Physiological Arousal

Molly’s EDA data from both a weekend day and a weekday during a 14-hour time window from 7:00 to 21:00 are presented in Fig. 3. Across both days, Molly’s EDA was notably low and flat across the entire day, with the exception of a single sharp spike at around 9:00 on the weekday and 11:00 on the weekend day, which quickly dropped back to baseline. These spikes both occurred in between EMA time points when she reported not being with her dog, and it is notable that for both example days, Molly did not report spending much time with her dog. Molly’s EMA-reported social anxiety was relatively high throughout the day despite low variation in her EDA; this suggests that Molly’s physiological arousal may not cohere with her self-reported anxiety. For both days, the remainder of the day showed very low EDA levels (mostly below 0.5 μS) with little variation. Molly’s average EDA across the 7-day period was similar (and very low) when she was with her dog (0.07 μS) compared to without her dog (0.08 μS), which was consistent with her EMA data. These findings support the broader interpretation that dog interactions may have had limited influence on Molly’s emotional or physiological state during this day.

Summary

Through combining the survey, EMA, and interview data, there is little evidence that the presence of Molly’s dog impacted her anxiety, loneliness, or affect, and her self-reported dog relationship quality is not high. This overall lack of a clear impact of dog interactions is supported by her parent’s interview and her EMA self-reports indicating relatively low enjoyment of dog-related interactions. Further, the combination of low perceived support, high friction, and elevated loneliness in the dyad’s survey responses suggest that Molly is not currently interacting with her dog in a way that supports social or emotional regulation.

Lennox – Low Relationship Quality

Lennox was a 17-year-old female participant who identified as white and non-Hispanic/Latinx/Spanish. She lived in a suburban, mid-Atlantic region with 4 parents/guardians, 1 sibling, and 2 dogs. The dog she focused on for the study was a 3-year-old Labrador/Pointer mix. Lennox was currently utilizing both therapy and medication for ADHD. Lennox scored the highest on social anxiety (75) of any of the four cases.

Dog Relationship Quality (Self-Reports and Interviews)

Lennox was selected as an example of a participant who reported relatively low relationship quality on the PDRI, although not as low as the previous case (Molly). In particular, Lennox scored especially low on the “pets as substitutes” subscale, suggesting that she did not consider her dog as a substitute for human social support to the same extent as the other participants. Lennox reported caring for her dog less frequently than the other participants (once a week), though she did report playing and spending time with her dog.

When asked to describe whether she feels close with her dog, Lennox stated: “I do, although sometimes she does annoy me.” Lennox also noted that her dog occasionally ingests unintended objects, which can cause stress for the family: “I mean, she has a tendency to eat things she shouldn’t, or try to eat things she shouldn’t. When she was younger, there was a carabiner clip on her cage, and she got it off and chewed it. And then at another point, she ate linoleum flooring.” She recalls one time in particular: “I mean, the only thing I can really think of is like last time [she] got sick, my parents were worried and that made them a little, I guess, snappy, short-tempered a little bit with some things.” However, Lennox also noted positive aspects of her relationship, where her dog is not judgmental in ways that people may be.

Interviewer: Do you feel like [your dog] sometimes understands you in a way that people that other people aren’t able to?

Lennox: …. she ignores things that I would rather people ignore….Like [she] does not care what grade I get in school….[She] does not care that I would rather wear skirts than pants.

Stress and Coping Strategies (Self-Reports and Interviews)

Lennox noted in her interview that it was unusual for her dog to be present in social situations:

Interviewer: Yeah, so in those instances, do you ever feel like [your dog] can kind of help with those problems, like help you feel less anxious?

Lennox: I mean, those situations never really occur in places where she is.

Lennox and her mother differed in their perceptions of her use of coping strategies, where Lennox reported most often using secondary control strategies of positive thinking, cognitive restructuring, and acceptance, and her mother reported more involuntary engagement strategies such as rumination, intrusive thoughts, physiological and emotional arousal and involuntary action. Despite this, Lennox’s mother reflected on how their dog supports Lennox:

Parent: I mean, so the dog doesn’t go out with us anywhere outside the house, per se, so she wouldn’t necessarily be able to sit down and interact with the dog if she was out in public and she was anxious. But if she comes home and she’s either had, you know, a rough day, or something happened at school, or whatever, and she comes home, and I know she’ll immediately go and go cuddle with [the dog].

Like, “oh, you had a rough day” like, kind of like it’s an indicator like she, and she’s got to cuddle with her first thing in the morning. Once she’s ready to go to school, you know, every morning, you know, “Come here, [dog].” You know, she calls her over ‘cause she knows she’ll sit with her and cuddle her, and it does seem to help with the anxiety. And she’ll even tell me as much. She says it helps her calm down.

Dog-Adolescent Interactions, Anxiety, Loneliness, and Affect (EMA)

Lennox’s EMA data indicated that her dog was only currently with her for 7% (n = 2) of the prompts, though she had interacted with her dog within the past 2 h for 44% of the prompts. This is consistent with her other reports that she does not spend a lot of time with her dog, particularly in social situations. However, she rated her enjoyment of those interactions relatively high. None of the interactions were rated as not enjoyable, 8% as slightly enjoyable, 25% as moderately enjoyable, and 67% as very enjoyable.

Interestingly, although Lennox had the highest overall trait social anxiety score, she reported the lowest momentary social anxiety of any of the four cases, and well below the sample mean. Her anxiety scores were similarly slightly lower when she was with her dog compared to when not with her dog. Regarding loneliness, Lennox reported relatively low overall loneliness and momentary loneliness compared to the overall sample. She reported slightly higher loneliness when with her dog compared to without her dog, though this finding should be interpreted with caution given that her dog was only present with her during a small proportion of the prompts. She reported slightly higher positive affect than the overall sample, and similar negative affect. Additionally, Lennox reported lower positive affect and slightly higher negative affect with compared to without her dog, however again with a very small sample of responses with her dog.

Physiological Arousal

Figure 4 shows two examples of Lennox’s EDA data during both a weekday and a weekend day for a 14-hour period from 7:00 to 21:00. For the weekday, EDA values showed moderate variability across the day, with mild to moderate fluctuations between 07:30 and 16:00, and a pronounced series of peaks beginning around 15:30, reaching the highest levels shortly before 18:00. Lennox reported being at an extracurricular activity/sport during this time period, which could explain her elevated arousal. Notably, Lennox only completed two EMA prompts on this day, and there were no prompts when she reported being with her dog. This lack of in-the-moment dog interaction is consistent with Lennox’s report of few dog interactions. The weekend day (Fig. 4B) showed similar patterns, with Lennox reporting not being with her dog during any EMA time point. There are two EDA spikes, one that is concurrent with an EMA response at 10:00 where Lennox reported being with her family and siblings, however not with her dog. The second spike around 15:00 was not during an EMA prompt, although Lennox did report at the 16:00 prompt that she had been at friend’s house during that time period.

Lennox’s mean EDA was lower during dog-present moments across the 7-day period (< 0.001 μS) compared to dog-absent moments (0.30 μS), suggesting a possible calming effect of the dog when present. However, due to the very low frequency of dog-present EMA prompts, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Self-reported anxiety scores were only slightly lower with her dog present, consistent with Lennox’s statement that the dog is more of a comfort at home rather than in public-facing social contexts.

Summary

Overall, Lennox’s data does not support a significant role of her dog in how she manages anxiety. Although she and her mother report that her dog has a role in helping to comfort her after a long day, there is also some reported tension around her dog eating unwanted objects that may lead to more family stress. Also, Lennox does not appear to spend as much time with her dog as some of the other participants. Although there is no evidence to support that this relationship is detrimental, Lennox’s interaction with her dog does not appear to be a major contributor to her coping strategies.

Discussion

This case study paper used an idiographic, mixed-mixed approach to examine the person-specific nature of adolescent-dog relationships in the context of social anxiety. In the four presented cases, we integrated quantitative survey data, EDA, EMA of behaviors and emotions during a week-long period, and qualitative interviews with both adolescents and their parents. This approach highlighted variability in how adolescents interact with their pet dogs, the way these interactions may (or may not) support their mental health, and differences between parents and adolescent perceptions of dog relationships. These cases illustrate the variation in experiences of dog relationships for teenagers with high social anxiety, and underscore the value of an ideographic, multi-modal, real-world approach to capturing these relationships. Person-centered approaches to studying human-animal relationships may be a way of more comprehensively assessing the potentially beneficial role of pet dogs for adolescents with high social anxiety. Additionally, they point to possible moderators of these benefits, such as dog relationship quality and the context of social interactions (e.g., at home versus in public).

Cross-Case Variability in Adolescent-Dog Relationships

Across the four cases, there was variability in the quality and profile of the relationship between the adolescents and their pet dogs, underscoring the value of person-centered measurement approaches. This was partially due to the design of the study, as two of the cases (Ellie and Lena) were selected because of their high dog relationship quality scores, while the other two cases (Molly and Lennox) were selected because of their low dog relationship quality scores. However, there were also differences within the “low” and “high” dog relationship quality groups. For example, although Ellie reported high on affection, nurturance, emotional support, and companionship with her dog, she also reported high friction with her dog and noted in her interview that she is bothered when her dog comes into her room when she is not there. Ellie also reported most of her interactions with her dog as only slightly enjoyable. In comparison, all of Lena’s scores indicated a strong, positive relationship with her dog and she rated all her momentary interactions with her dog at the maximum on the enjoyment rating. Both Ellie and Lena also reported frequently engaging in various activities with their dogs, including helping to care for them, spending time with them, and taking them out to restaurants or shops.

Similarly, while Molly and Lennox both had low dog relationship quality scores, the profiles of their relationships differed. Although both had overall poor relationship quality scores across all subscales, Molly’s scores indicated that friction with her dog was particularly high, while for Lennox, the pets as substitutes scale was particularly low. This may indicate that the primary reason for their less positive relationships with their dogs differs, with Molly’s potentially being due to conflict with her dog and Lennox perhaps because she did not view her dog as a substitute for interactions with other humans. Molly and Lennox also reported less engagement in dog-related activities compared to Ellie and Lena. Molly reported helping take care of her dog a few times per week and spending some time with her, while Lennox reported caring for her dog less frequently and spent the least amount of time with her dog, as indicated by the few times she was with her dog for the EMA prompts.

Person-Centered Approaches to Exploring Pet Dog Interaction and Mental Health Support

A primary aim of this case study paper was to illustrate the value of taking a person-centered approach to integrating multiple types of data in order to descriptively explore differences in mental health and related outcomes (i.e., momentary social anxiety, loneliness, positive affect, and negative affect) based on dog presence for adolescents with social anxiety. The preliminary evidence indicated mixed results across the four cases, which further strengthens the rationale for using analytic approaches that account for variation between people and contexts. For example, Ellie, Lena, and Lennox all self-reported lower momentary social anxiety during prompts with their dogs present compared to when they were not with their dog. For Lena and Lennox, social anxiety appeared to align with their EDA data: they both had generally lower physiological arousal when their dog was present. Further, the qualitative reports from both the parents and adolescents offered multiple explanations to support this finding, such as the dog providing distraction and comfort, reducing awkwardness during social situations, and being someone they can talk to without fear of judgment. These findings align with past research indicating that interacting with one’s dog can reduce distress during a stressor (Matijczak et al., 2024) and that dogs can facilitate social interactions (Wells, 2004).

Consistent with prior research suggesting that pet dogs provide emotional benefits (Janssens et al., 2021; Kerns et al., 2018), Ellie and Lena (who reported high quality relationships with their dogs overall) reported higher positive affect and lower negative affect during prompts with their dog present. However, contrary to the prior research yet consistent with other measures, Lennox and Molly (who reported lower quality relationships with their pet dogs overall) reported the opposite pattern with lower positive affect and higher negative affect during prompts with their dog, suggesting that dog relationship quality could be a moderating factor in affect-related benefits of dog interaction.

The patterns of loneliness also differed across the cases, further suggesting ideographic differences. While Ellie reported slightly lower momentary loneliness with her dog present, there was no notable difference for Molly, and Lena and Lennox reported higher loneliness with their dogs present. The relationship between pet interactions and loneliness has been complicated, with mixed results across studies (Kretzler et al., 2022). It could be that the existing validated loneliness measures are capturing human-focused loneliness without reflecting accurately whether participants feel the presence of their animal impacts their overall feelings of being lonely. There could also be age-related factors at play, as adolescents are developing autonomy and peer relationships are more salient (Allen & Loeb, 2015), which can contribute to their overall perceptions of social relationships and loneliness. Alternatively, it could be the case that the findings are capturing differences between trait and state loneliness, and how that may vary from person to person or by different social norms. For Lena and Molly, they both reported slightly higher global loneliness compared to the sample mean, and then Lena reported slightly more momentary loneliness when with her dog compared to without, while Molly did not substantially differ. This suggests that perhaps their loneliness is simply more stable, and thus there were not a lot of momentary differences between being with dog vs. without dog. It may be that loneliness can be independent from physiological arousal/anxiety moment-to-moment. For example, increases in momentary social anxiety may reflect that youth are spending more time with others, which cumulatively may help improve loneliness over time. Therefore, if youth are interacting with their dogs to help cope with momentary anxiety, there may be individual variability in whether these interactions are linked to long term loneliness reduction. Again, person-centered assessment is of value, enabling a more nuanced assessment for individual adolescents regarding whether pet dogs are facilitating coping strategies and ability to engage with others, or are being used as a replacement for human social interactions.

It is possible that some of the variability across cases is related to the strength and specific nature of the adolescent-dog relationships. Overall, there appeared to be more positive outcomes during dog presence for the adolescents with strong dog relationships (i.e., Ellie and Lena), and there was limited evidence for adolescents who had weaker relationships with their dogs (i.e., Molly and Lennox). Given this, it is important to assess why relationship quality varies, and if there are any actions or supports that could help improve this relationship. It could be that by encouraging more activities such as playing, games, or even structured interactions such as training or agility could strengthen the bond between the adolescent and their dog (Barcelos et al., 2020; Riddoch et al., 2022). It is also worth exploring if there are any particular behavioral characteristics of the adolescent’s dog that may not be compatible with the adolescent’s interaction style, and to work with the family to generate specific strategies for optimizing this relationship or helping adolescents understand their dogs’ behavior. Alternatively, it may be the case that pet dogs are only beneficial for mental health support for certain adolescents who enjoy interacting with dogs. There may be individual variability in what types of animals (or in some cases, no animals at all) that youth feel connected to; dog-human interactions are not universally desired or beneficial for everyone.

Unlike purely experimental paradigms that attempt to assess changes in anxiety and physiological arousal in the laboratory, combining passive and active ambulatory data streams allow us access to moment-to-moment changes in these constructs within the context of how adolescents are living their daily lives. These cases illustrate the rich inter- and intra-variability in physiological arousal (assessed through ambulatory EDA) and self-reported social anxiety among individuals and how interactions with pet dogs may be associated with these factors. For someone such as Lena, being with one’s pet dog appears to reduce both EDA and social anxiety, and these constructs appear to cohere with one another. This suggests that continuing to promote positive interactions with her pet dog may be positive for Lena. On the other hand, for someone such as Molly, interactions with her pet dog appeared to have little association with either her social anxiety or physiological arousal, and these did not appear to relate to one another. Thus, it may not be in Molly’s best interests to either engage more frequently with her pet dog. Without this idiographic approach, these nuances would be lost and opportunities for more targeted interventions to address the unique needs of each adolescent would be missed.

Comparison of Parent and Adolescent Report

Across cases, there was a consistent mismatch between parent and youth perceptions of coping strategies. Parents often reported their adolescents used higher levels of involuntary engagement coping strategies (e.g., rumination, physiological and emotional arousal), while adolescents more often reported strategies such as secondary control (e.g., acceptance, positive thinking). This pattern is consistent with prior work highlighting that discrepancies in parent and youth report of child behaviors are common more broadly in different research, applied, and experimental setting (De Los Reyes & Kazdin, 2005). In the context of our study, the discrepancy may reflect that parents have a greater awareness of observable dysregulation, while adolescents are more aware of their internal coping efforts that may be less visible to others. These discordances between parents and adolescents further underscore the need for multi-modal, multi-informant research approaches.

Limitations

There are several limitations that should be considered. First, the inclusion of only female participants is a limitation; although the larger study includes participants of diverse genders, ongoing data collection and our requirement that cases have data across all methods limited this case study to female participants only. While the case study design allowed for the comprehensive integration of multiple streams of data (Crowe et al., 2011), which would not have been possible with a larger sample size, the selected cases may not be representative of other adolescents with social anxiety. With regard to the specific data used in this case study, EDA can be influenced by many factors outside of anxiety (such as physical activity or sleep quality), which may explain some of the mismatch between self-reported anxiety and EDA. In real world longitudinal studies like this one, the cause and interpretation of EDA sensor data is not always clear. For instance, Molly had an unusual EDA pattern: relatively low, flat EDA with one large peak around mid- morning in the two days presented. The cause of those peaks is not known, but because they happen multiple times, and the Empatica algorithm did not flag them as instances when the sensor was incorrectly worn, the pattern does not appear to be due to a hardware malfunction. There were also several cases (Molly and Lennox) who reported relatively little interactions with their dogs, which is informative however also limits our understanding of how their dogs contributed to anxiety. Similarly, it may be the case that dogs cannot be present during some common contexts of social stress (such as within a school setting).

The case study methodology is not designed for inferential testing and therefore is limited in generalizability and the ability control for confounding factors. This study was designed to illustrate the value of person-centered, multi-modal approaches in the context of adolescent-dog relationships and therefore was not intended to be able to make causal inferences regarding the relationship between pet dog relationships and mental health. Although the EMA data provide some insight into the presence of a pet dog and momentary self-reported mental health, we are not able to determine whether changes in mental health outcomes are caused by dog interactions, or whether adolescents seek out their dogs in response to pre-existing emotional states.

Conclusion and Implications

This series of case studies underscores the strengths of using a comprehensive, idiographic, mixed methods approach to understand the role of dog interactions in youth with social anxiety. Combining quantitative global survey data with EMA allowed for useful assessments of trait and state levels of outcomes (e.g., loneliness, social anxiety). We were also able to capture the idiographic context to youth-dog relationships that may not easily be captured in more experimental paradigms. These person-specific patterns were buttressed by in-depth qualitative interviews with multiple family members to better understand family dynamics and both adolescent and parent perceptions of youth coping with social anxiety. Finally, using multiple data types allowed us to explore the coherence (or lack thereof) in physiological arousal with other aspects of affect and emotion in situ as adolescents live their daily lives. Overall, this series of case studies suggests that there is significant variability in youth experiences with their pet dogs and how their relationship may be associated with adaptive coping with social anxiety. Using multiple methods is critical in understanding the full scope of individual adolescent-dog relationships and how perceptions of youth-dog interactions may vary between adolescents and their parents. Future research that focuses on understanding the context of an individual family and their dog will better inform recommendations for individually tailored interventions and support across other mental health contexts.
