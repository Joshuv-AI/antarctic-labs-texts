Serial measurement of serum pancreatic lipase immunoreactivity and trypsin-like immunoreactivity concentrations in cats with chronic enteropathy
Authors: Dimitra A Karra, Katerina T Moraiti, Jonathan A Lidbury, Joerg M Steiner, Shelley J Newman, Paula R Giaretta, João Pedro Cavasin, Panagiotis G Xenoulis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesSerial measurement of serum feline pancreatic lipase immunoreactivity (fPLI) and trypsin-like immunoreactivity (fTLI) concentrations in cats with chronic enteropathy (CE) has not been previously reported. The aim of the current observational study was to serially evaluate serum fPLI and fTLI concentrations in cats with different forms of CE.MethodsA total of 60 cats with CE were included, diagnosed either with immunosuppressive-responsive enteropathy (IRE; n = 22), food-responsive enteropathy (FRE; n = 7) or low-grade intestinal T-cell lymphoma (LGITL; n = 32, one diagnosed previously as FRE progressed to lymphoma). Serum fPLI and fTLI concentrations were measured in 252 and 245 serum samples, respectively, from 60 cats with CE at initial presentation and during re-examination.ResultsSerum fPLI concentrations indicative of pancreatitis were found in 3/60 cats (one with IRE and two with LGITL) at initial presentation and in an additional 8/60 cats (four with IRE and four with LGITL) during treatment (24/252 serum samples). Serum fTLI concentrations indicative of exocrine pancreatic insufficiency were found in 2/60 cats (both with IRE) at initial presentation and during re-examination (n = 3/245). None of the cats with FRE had fPLI concentrations above or fTLI concentrations below the respective reference intervals (RIs).Conclusions and relevanceSerum fPLI concentrations may fluctuate during treatment in a subset of cats with IRE or LGITL and, in some cats, are indicative of pancreatitis. Serum fTLI concentrations may vary within and outside the RI in a relatively small proportion of cats with IRE. The long-term clinical importance of these findings requires further study.

Introduction

Chronic enteropathy (CE) in cats has been categorised into chronic inflammatory enteropathy (CIE) and low-grade intestinal T-cell lymphoma (LGITL).1 –6 Typically, CIE is further subclassified based on the response to treatment into immunosuppressant-responsive enteropathy (IRE) and food-responsive enteropathy (FRE). 1

Exocrine pancreatic disease in cats is increasingly recognised, with pancreatitis being the most common disorder and exocrine pancreatic insufficiency (EPI) diagnosed more frequently than previously reported.7 –10 Chronic intestinal inflammation is linked to pancreatitis in cats, but the mechanisms are unknown.8,11 In a recent study, CIE coexisted with pancreatitis in 2/27 cats diagnosed with ‘triatitis’ via histopathology, although the prevalence of abnormal serum feline pancreatic lipase immunoreactivity (fPLI) and feline trypsin-like immunoreactivity (fTLI) was not reported. 12 Another study found gastrointestinal (GI) disease in 15% of cats with chronic pancreatitis and 13% with acute pancreatitis, including IRE, chronic gastric ulcers and necrotising enteritis. 13 Intestinal disease, enteritis and dysbiosis have been proposed as precursors of pancreatitis. 14

CE, pancreatitis and EPI can have similar clinical signs and clinicopathological findings.9,11,15 In cats, multiorgan abdominal inflammation, such as concurrent inflammation of the small intestine and pancreas, is clinically significant and may complicate treatment and worsen outcomes compared with single-organ disease.

Despite the routine measurement of serum fPLI and fTLI in cats with GI signs, no studies have reported serial fPLI and fTLI concentrations in a large group of cats with CE. A single study reported serial serum fPLI concentrations in four healthy cats and two cats with FRE from a research colony, thus not providing any meaningful insight into longitudinal results of fPLI in cats with CE. 16 The aim of this study was to describe serum fPLI and fTLI concentrations in cats with CE at initial presentation and during treatment follow-up.

Materials and methods

This was a prospective observational study. The study protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the University of Thessaly, Greece (AUP numbers: 115/6.10.2020, 144/24.08.2022).

Cats were enrolled in the study if they: (1) had clinical signs compatible with CE, diarrhoea or loose stools of more than 3 weeks’ duration; vomiting once or more per week for 2 months or longer, weight loss, hyporexia and/or anorexia; (2) underwent upper and lower endoscopy; (3) had no other diseases that could explain the clinical signs; and (4) had a diagnosis of CIE or LGITL based on histopathological evaluation combined with immunohistochemistry in ambiguous cases.

All cats underwent the same initial diagnostics: history, physical examination, complete blood count, serum biochemistry with electrolytes, cobalamin and folate, urinalysis, faecal parasite testing (zinc sulfate flotation), faecal PCR for Tritrichomonas blagburni, and upper and lower endoscopy with multiple GI biopsies. The Feline Chronic Enteropathy Activity Index (FCEAI) was calculated for all cats with CE. 17

Endoscopic examination was performed using a flexible videogastroscope (EG-2490K; PENTAX Medical EMEA) with an outer diameter of 8 mm and a 2.4 mm biopsy channel. Approximately five, ten, five and five biopsies were collected using 2.2 mm forceps from the stomach, duodenum, ileum and colon, respectively, and were examined by three board-certified pathologists. Findings were reported using the World Small Animal Veterinary Association histopathological scoring system. 18

Food was withheld in all cats for at least 8 h before serum collection. Serum was stored at −20°C until analysis, for up to 7 days, and shipped overnight on ice and analysed at the Gastrointestinal Laboratory at Texas A&M University.

Serum fPLI (measured by Spec fPL) and fTLI concentrations were measured in serum samples at initial presentation as well as at each re-examination as previously described.19,20

Data were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. All data were non-normally distributed and were therefore described as median and analysed using Mann–Whitney tests. Significance was set at P <0.05. Correlation comparisons were evaluated using Spearman’s correlation. All data were analysed using a commercially available statistical software package (GraphPad Prism 8).

Serum fPLI

Serum fPLI concentration was indicative of pancreatitis (⩾8.8 µg/l) in 24/252 (10%) samples and within the equivocal range (4.4–8.8 μg/l) in 32/252 (12%) samples. At initial presentation, 3/60 (5%) cats (one with CIE-IRE and two with LGITL) had serum fPLI indicative of pancreatitis, and 8/60 (13%) cats (four with CIE-IRE and four with LGITL) were within the equivocal range. During treatment, 8/60 (13%) cats (four with CIE-IRE and four with LGITL) had at least one serum fPLI measurement indicative of pancreatitis; four had initially normal values and four were initially in the equivocal range. Overall, 11/60 (18%) cats (five with CIE-IRE, six with LGITL) had at least one fPLI result indicative of pancreatitis when measured repeatedly over time; four (36%) showed persistent elevation on follow-up (⩾3 measurements over ⩾ 4 months). Overall, 19/60 (31%) cats (nine with CIE-IRE and 10 with LGITL) had at least one measurement in the equivocal range.

No difference in serum fPLI concentration was found between cats with LGITL and cats with CIE or cats with CIE-IRE, after the exclusion of cats with CIE-FRE (Table 4). Cats with CIE-FRE had significantly lower serum fPLI concentrations compared with cats with CIE-IRE (Table 4). None of the CIE-FRE cats had abnormal fPLI concentrations. Most cats with CIE-IRE who had increased fPLI concentrations had isolated increases (eg, in only one of the re-examinations). In contrast, in cats with LGITL, some cats had consistently increased serum fPLI concentrations. Serum fPLI concentration for each cat at each time point are displayed in Figures 1–3.

Serum fTLI

Serum fTLI concentrations were measured in 245 samples. Serum fTLI concentration was indicative of EPI (⩽8 µg/l) in 3/245 (1%) samples and within the equivocal range (8–12 μg/l) in 6/245 (2%) samples. At initial presentation, 1/60 (1%) cats with CIE-IRE had serum fTLI concentrations indicative of EPI and none in the equivocal range. During treatment, 2/60 (3%) cats, both with CIE-IRE, had serum fTLI concentration indicative of EPI: one had an initially normal fTLI concentration and the other had a fTLI concentration indicative of EPI. The one cat with the initial fTLI concentration indicative of EPI had follow-up measurements during treatment that were in the equivocal range but then returned into the EPI range, and treatment for EPI was started. Overall, 2/60 (3%) cats had at least one serum fTLI measurement indicative of EPI and 3/60 (5%) cats (two with CIE-IRE and one with LGITL) had at least one serum fTLI measurement within the equivocal range. One cat at one re-examination had concurrently a serum fPLI concentration consistent with pancreatitis and serum fTLI concentration consistent with EPI. One cat at one re-examination had concurrently a serum fPLI concentration indicative of pancreatitis and serum fTLI concentration within the equivocal zone. Serum fTLI concentrations were significantly higher in cats with LGITL compared with cats with CIE and cats with CIE-IRE, when cats with CIE-FRE were excluded (Table 4). No difference was found in serum fTLI concentrations between cats with CIE-FRE and those with CIE-IRE (Table 4). None of the cats with CIE-FRE had abnormal serum fTLI concentrations.

Serum fPLI and FCEAI

A weak positive correlation was found between serum fPLI concentration and FCEAI in cats with CE (r = 0.15, 95% confidence interval [CI] 0.013–0.28; P = 0.02) and in cats with CIE (r = 0.25, 95% CI 0.06–0.44; P = 0.009). In cats with LGITL, no correlation was found between serum fPLI concentration and FCEAI (r = 0.07, 95% CI −0.13 to 0.25; P = 0.49). In only one cat (with LGITL), increases in serum fPLI concentration indicative of pancreatitis during treatment were associated with increases in FCEAI and in a flareup of diarrhoea, weight loss and decreased appetite.

Serum fTLI and FCEAI

No correlation was found between serum fTLI concentration and FCEAI in cats with CE (r = –0.04, 95% CI −0.19 to 0.09; P = 0.49), in cats with CIE (r = –0.1, 95% CI −0.3 to 0.1; P = 0.3) or in cats with LGITL (r = –0.01, 95% CI −0.17 to 0.2; P = 0.87). However, both cats with CIE and EPI were poorly controlled before the addition of pancreatic enzymes to the treatment and improved (resolution of clinical signs) after the addition of pancreatic enzymes (Creon 25000, dose to effect). In one cat (with LGITL) with concurrent serum fPLI and fTLI concentrations within the equivocal zone at one re-examination, flareup of diarrhoea was noted.

No correlation was found between serum fPLI concentration and albumin in cats with CE (n = 184, r = –0.05, 95% CI −0.2 to 0.09; P = 0.46), in cats with CIE (n = 79, r = –0.17, 95% CI −0.39 to 0.05; P = 0.11) or in cats with LGITL (n = 105, r = 0.06, 95% CI −0.14 to 0.25; P = 0.56).

Serum fPLI and cobalamin

At initial presentation, a weak negative correlation between serum fPLI and cobalamin concentrations was found in cats with CE (n = 56, r = –0.36, 95% CI −0.57 to −0.10; P = 0.006), a moderate negative correlation in cats with CIE (n = 26, r = –0.53, 95% CI −0.76 to −0.16; P = 0.006) and no significant correlation in cats with LGITL (n = 30, r = –0.16, 95% CI −0.50 to 0.22; P = 0.38). After initiation of treatment, most of the cats received cobalamin supplementation; therefore, the correlation between fPLI and cobalamin was not evaluated.

Discussion

This is the first study serially evaluating serum fPLI and fTLI concentrations in a large group of cats with CE. Increased serum fPLI concentrations indicative of pancreatitis were found in 18% of cats when measured repeatedly over time. In one retrospective study evaluating the outcome of 65 cats with CIE, fPLI was measured in 27 cats and was abnormal in 19 (70%) cats. 21 The higher prevalence of increased serum fPLI concentrations in that study might be explained by differences in the study design and inclusion criteria. For example, in the cited study, serum fPLI concentration was measured when the clinician suspected pancreatitis based on history, physical examination and imaging findings, 21 whereas in our study, fPLI was measured in all cats. In humans, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is linked to a range of exocrine pancreatic disorders, including benign asymptomatic increases in pancreatic enzymes, acute pancreatitis, autoimmune pancreatitis, chronic pancreatitis and EPI. 22

Approximately half of the cats in our study with a serum fPLI concentration indicative of pancreatitis were diagnosed with LGITL. To the authors’ knowledge, there are no published reports on the prevalence of pancreatitis in cats with LGITL. Interestingly, cats with LGITL appeared to have more consistently increased serum fPLI concentrations compared with cats with CIE-IRE.

In our study, all cats with CIE-FRE had serum fPLI concentrations within normal limits. However, the number of cats in this subgroup was small (n = 7), and therefore no firm conclusions can be drawn. Importantly, these findings do not exclude the possibility that cats with elevated fPLI concentrations may still respond to dietary management. Further studies are needed to confirm this finding.

Overall, cats with CIE-FRE had normal fPLI concentrations, cats with CIE-IRE had mainly isolated increases in serum fPLI concentrations, while cats with LGITL more commonly had persistent increases in serum fPLI concentrations, suggesting that the more severe the intestinal disease, the more likely it is that there is concurrent evidence of pancreatitis.

A very weak positive correlation was found between serum fPLI concentration and FCEAI before and during treatment. Although statistically significant, the strength of the association was minimal and likely of limited clinical relevance. This is to be expected, however, as the majority of cats in this study had normal or questionable fPLI concentrations and only a small number of cats had fPLI indicative of pancreatitis. Interestingly, in at least one cat, increases in serum fPLI concentrations were associated with clinical flareups. It is plausible that prednisolone treatment in cats with CE and increased serum fPLI concentrations also treated concurrent chronic pancreatic inflammation. There are reports suggesting that prednisolone could be beneficial in cats with chronic pancreatitis, although it is uncertain whether this is due to a reduction in pancreatic inflammation or treatment of concurrent CIE.1,14 In a recent study of cats with chronic pancreatitis, treatment with immunosuppressive doses of prednisolone improved clinical scores compared with both the control and ciclosporin groups; however, in contrast with ciclosporin, it did not cause any significant decreases in serum fPLI concentrations. 21 Another explanation might be that chronic pancreatitis was asymptomatic in those cases. However, the duration of the study was likely too short to evaluate the long-term outcome of cats with increased serum fPLI concentrations regarding survival and development of complications, such as EPI or diabetes mellitus. In one study of dogs with IRE, increased serum canine pancreatic lipase immunoreactivity concentrations were associated with a negative outcome and was the only variable shown to significantly affect survival. 22

Weak to moderate positive correlations were found between serum fPLI concentrations and certain histopathological scores in the duodenum and ileum and in total histopathology scores. These findings suggest that pancreatic injury is associated with more severe intestinal lesions. Further studies are needed to clarify whether more severe intestinal lesions predispose to the development of pancreatitis or vice versa.

In our study, serum fPLI was negatively correlated with serum cobalamin concentrations at initial presentation, consistent with previous studies reporting subnormal serum cobalamin concentrations in cats with CIE, EPI, intestinal lymphoma and pancreatic inflammation. 17 In one study of cats, increased serum fPLI concentration was associated with hypocobalaminaemia. 23 The association between increased fPLI and hypocobalaminaemia is unclear; however, more severe intestinal disease may be more likely to cause both pancreatic inflammation and hypocobalaminaemia. In our study, increased serum fPLI concentrations were associated with more severe intestinal disease (based on histopathological scores), and therefore hypocobalaminaemia was most likely associated with disease severity.

Serum fTLI concentrations suggestive of EPI were found in a relatively small percentage of cats (3%) and only in cats with CIE-IRE in our study. To date, there are no previous reports investigating the prevalence of EPI in cats with CE. Notably, serum fTLI concentration fluctuated within and outside of the diagnostic range for EPI in a single cat. This likely reflects suspected EPI from acinar cell loss secondary to chronic pancreatitis. This is supported by our finding of an increased serum fPLI concentration in one of the two cats with serum fTLI concentrations indicative of EPI at one re-examination. Since a degree of acinar cell regeneration is expected in cats with chronic pancreatitis, it seems reasonable that some cats have fTLI and fPLI values that fluctuate due to the balance of acinar cell damage and acinar cell regeneration. Clinically, our finding would suggest that re-evaluation of serum fTLI concentration may be warranted in cats with clinical signs suggestive of EPI that have a serum fTLI concentration at the lower end of the reference interval. Importantly, both cats with EPI received pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy and showed clinical improvement.

Concurrent EPI has been reported in human patients with IBD, especially in those who go out of remission. 24 However, several of these reports relied on diagnostic tests that can be falsely affected by chronic intestinal disease, so this comparison should be made with caution. However, a decrease of serum fTLI concentration in cats with chronic intestinal disease is not expected, and thus our findings would truly suggest concurrent CE and EPI.

Data on serum fPLI and fTLI concentrations in cats over time are limited. Another recent study serially evaluated serum fPLI concentration (measured as Spec fPL) in four clinically healthy cats and two cats with FRE. 16 Concentrations ranged from normal to markedly increased in both groups. Notably, two of the cats that were considered clinically healthy exhibited both abnormal serum fPLI concentrations and ultrasonographic evidence of an enlarged and hypoechoic pancreas, both indicative of pancreatitis. One cat had mildly enlarged abdominal lymph nodes and the other had slight thickening of the lamina muscularis in the duodenum, jejunum and colon, suggesting that subclinical chronic intestinal disease or pancreatitis could not be excluded. 16 In the two cats who developed large bowel diarrhoea after a diet change as part of a food trial, increased serum fPLI concentrations were found at least once during the follow-up period. However, chronic subclinical pancreatitis or CE could not be definitively ruled out either. 16 The findings of this last study are in sharp contrast with our findings. Possible explanations for this discrepancy could include the small sample size of the study by Moscoso Uribe et al 16 (only six cats in total), group housing conditions, the fact that only colony cats were included and differences in clinical evaluation of the patients.

One limitation of our study could be that histopathological examination of the pancreas was not performed in any of the cats enrolled and, therefore, definitive exclusion or confirmation of pancreatitis is not possible. Although histopathological examination of the pancreas is considered the gold standard for diagnosing pancreatitis, it is invasive and expensive, susceptible to sampling error and is rarely used in clinical practice. 8 As these were all clinical cases, pancreatic biopsy was not feasible.

Conclusions

Cats with CE may have increased serum fPLI concentrations. Cats with CIE-FRE had normal fPLI concentrations, cats with CIE-IRE had mainly isolated increases in serum fPLI concentrations, while cats with LGITL more commonly had persistent increases in serum fPLI concentrations, suggesting that the more severe the intestinal disease, the more likely it is that there is concurrent evidence of pancreatitis. Increased serum fPLI concentrations may be of clinical importance in isolated cases of cats with CE of LGTIL. The clinical importance of pancreatitis in cats with CE remains unclear because of the small number of affected cats and lack of long-term follow-up. Further long-term studies are needed, and are underway, to determine the outcome of cats with CE with increased serum fPLI concentrations.

EPI is less commonly seen in cats with CE; however, it does exist in some cats with CE. The impact of EPI is more clearly apparent than pancreatitis, as cats with EPI require treatment with pancreatic enzymes. Failure to diagnose concurrent EPI may lead to overall treatment failure.
