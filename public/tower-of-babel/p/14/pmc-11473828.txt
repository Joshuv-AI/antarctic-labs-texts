The validity and reliability of a Japanese version of the web-based hamstring outcome score
Authors: Shogo Tsutsumi, Noriaki Maeda, Anders Hauge Engebretsen, Yusuke Kamatsuki, Yasuharu Nagano, Sayo Kuroda, Ayano Ishida, Yuki Tamura, Tsubasa Tashiro, Kazuki Kaneda, Satoshi Arima, Mitsuhiro Yoshimi, Makoto Komiya, Yukio Urabe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to translate a web-based hamstring outcome score (HaOS) questionnaire to the Japanese context. First, the validity and reliability of the translated questionnaires were examined among Japanese athletes. Second, the association between the previous history of hamstring strain and HaOS was examined. The translation was performed according to internationally recognized methodologies. A total of 233 athletes were asked to complete the HaOS questionnaire, and four questions were added to examine the content validity of the questionnaire. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated to evaluate the test–retest reliability. Internal consistency was determined by calculating Cronbach’s alpha values. The translated questionnaires had high acceptance and compliance rates, with a response rate of 84.4%. The ICC for each scale ranged from 0.80 to 0.93, and Cronbach's α from 0.72 to 0.96, confirming good reliability and high internal consistency. HaOS of the previous injury group (93.4 [78.9, 100]) was significantly lower than that of the healthy group (86.2 [68.1, 100]) (p < 0.05). The translated HaOS questionnaires were valid, reliable, and acceptable for monitoring hamstring conditions in Japanese athletes.

Introduction

Acute hamstring strain injury (HSI) is the most common muscle injury during sports involving high-speed running1–3. The injury burden (the cross-product of severity and incidence) of HSI is high4, and the recurrence rate ranges from 4 to 68%5. Therefore, various studies have been conducted worldwide to develop preventive strategies for HSI. For example, decreased eccentric muscle strength and short muscle fascicle length have been reported to be risk factors for the development of HSI6,7. Despite these findings, the incidence of HSIs has not decreased8. One reason for this is the low compliance with the HSI prevention protocol with eccentric hamstring strength training9. This may be due to concerns that delayed onset muscle soreness following eccentric strength training negatively affects athletic performance.

Muscle injuries, such as HSI, are generally acute injuries, whereas some reported that they are chronic injuries that result from repeated microscopic damage10. Actually, a prospective study investigating the relationship between running load and HSI using a global positioning system revealed that the risk of HSI increases with increasing running load approximately 1–2 weeks before the occurrence of HSI11. Therefore, regular monitoring of hamstring condition may be important for preventing injury. We hypothesized that an indicator used for this purpose could be the hamstring outcome score (HaOS), which was developed by Engebretsen12. HaOS is assessed using a self-administered questionnaire based on the subjective assessment of the hamstring and is calculated according to the history of HSI and the current hamstring condition of the athlete. Previous studies using HaOS have reported that pre-season HaOS is associated with the occurrence of HSIs during the season13. Hence, from a conditioning perspective, HaOS can be a contributing factor to the prevention of HSI.

HaOS has been translated for use into other languages, including Dutch14, and is expected to gain more attention in the future. However, it has not yet been translated for use in Japan. Therefore, this study aimed to translate HaOS into Japanese, culturally adapt it to the Japanese context, and investigate the validity and reliability of the HaOS questionnaire. In addition, we examined whether the Japanese version of HaOS varied according to a previous history of HSI.

Methods

The questionnaire

The original HaOS questionnaire comprises two parts. Part 1 consists of a history of HSI. Part 2 consists of five aspects: (1) symptoms, (2) soreness, (3) pain, (4) function in sports, and (5) quality of life [2815]. The questions are scored on a scale from 0 to 4, ranging from no complaints to maximum complaints13. HaOS comprises an overall score and a score for each subdomain. The scores are calculated as a percentage of the maximum score; players with no complaints receive a score of 100%. Scores are calculated as 1 − (score/maximum score) × 100 (%)13.

In this study, the questionnaire was distributed electronically to all athletes and was designed to monitor their condition weekly, regardless of whether they had any current hamstring-related symptoms. The questionnaire was made available on the Google Forms platform (Alphabet, Mountain View, CA, USA), and the web link was distributed to athletes through representatives. Two university and twelve high school team coaches or managers were asked to distribute the online survey to their athletes with permission from their institution. Before starting the questionnaire, we distributed ID numbers to each team, and asked the team representatives to assign ID numbers to the players. All players were asked to fill out their own ID numbers in the questionnaire. In this way, all data were completely anonymized, and we could confirm whether players had responded to the web-survey through ID numbers. The first page of the questionnaire clearly stated that participants were not to respond to it more than once by themselves and that they could respond anonymously. All data were completely anonymized prior to access. On the same page, the purpose and methodology of the online survey were explained, and willingness to participate was obtained by clicking a button confirming participation in the online survey. Only respondents willing to participate in the questionnaire were allowed to proceed to the next page. Minor participation was accompanied by a parental opt-out document stating that a parent or legal guardian could refuse the minor’s participation. Minors were not allowed to participate in the survey unless they themselves wanted to participate and clicked on two buttons indicating that their parent or legal guardian had seen the opt-out document and allowed them to participate.

Athlete and recruitment

This study’s anonymous online survey was conducted from June 20, 2022, to October 31, 2022. Coaches or trainers from two high school and one university rugby teams, ten high school and one university track and field teams, and one high school soccer team were asked if they were willing to participate in the study. After interest in this study was expressed, a meeting was held with each team’s coach or trainer, and if requested, the athletes, to explain the aim of this study and obtain consent. The participation criteria included being at least 16 years old and having the ability to speak and understand Japanese. In this study, the definition of HSI was (1) injuries requiring medical treatment, such as icing due to sudden pain on the back of the thigh during sports activities, and (2) injuries that make it impossible to participate in subsequent games or practices15. According to the guidelines, a sample size of 5–10 participants per scale item is considered sufficient to establish the validity and reliability of a scale20. Given that the HaOS consists of 19 items, we required a minimum of 95 to 190 athletes. Thus, the athletes participated in the survey regardless of whether they had a current or previous injury, and translated questionnaires were ultimately distributed to 276 athletes. Based on having or not having a history of HSI, they were divided into previous injury group and healthy group. The previous injury group was subdivided into two groups to assess the relationship between the number of injuries and HaOS: one-time injury group and recurrent group (defined as those who responded that they had experienced HSI more than twice on the same side).

Content validity and reliability

Content validity was documented by adding the following four dichotomous questions (Yes/No) after the questionnaire21,22: (1) Do you think this questionnaire contains appropriate questions about the sport you are participating in? (2) Was it difficult to complete the questionnaire? (3) Would you like to make any changes or additions to the questionnaire? (4) Do you think the web-based methods worked well? After the four questions, participants entered their comments in the free space. If no responses were received after two days, reminder announcements were sent. To evaluate test–retest reliability, participants were asked to complete the questionnaire again within 24–72 h of the first answer19.

Statistical analysis

The basic information of the participants was represented by means, standard deviations (SD), and coefficients of variation. Test–retest reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC2,1) and calculated for each subdomain score and absolute agreement. Based on previous studies, 0.70 or higher value of ICC indicates high test–retest reliability23,24. Measurement error (athlete score systematic and random errors that are not attributable to true changes in the construct being measured) was calculated using standard error of the mean (SEM = SD × √1−ICC) and was converted to smallest detectable chance (SDC) with 95% confidence interval (SDC95 = 1.96 × √2 × SEM)25. Internal consistency reflects the degree of interrelatedness among the items in the questionnaire and verifies the homogeneity of the questionnaire23,25. In the analysis of internal consistency, Cronbach’s values were calculated for each subscale of HaOS, except symptoms: a subscale consisting of one question. A value between 0.70 and 0.95 is deemed to indicate good internal consistency24,25.

For comparison of HaOS and its subscales between the previous injury group and healthy group, the Shapiro–Wilk test was used to check for normality, followed by an unpaired t-test or Mann–Whitney U test. Further, one-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare three groups (healthy, one-time injury, and recurrent group). As a post-hoc test, the Bonferroni method or the Steel–Dwass method was used. All data were analyzed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan). The significance level was set at 5%.

Results

Translation

No major problems occurred with the sequential translation of the questionnaire. Minor issues included the misuse of prepositions, synonyms, and present and past tenses, and awkward sentence structures.

Cognitive debriefing

Seven male university track and field athletes provided the following comments.

Part 1. The location of the hamstring was not well understood; thus, we added a figure to visually make the hamstring location clear (posterior aspect of the thigh).

Part 2. Questions 6 and 7. It was not clear whether it asked about during daily life or during exercise. Therefore, we added the explanation ‘including the daily living activity’.

Content validity

Two hundred thirty-three athletes were followed for the HaOS questionnaire two times. The response rate was 84.4%. Of all the respondents, 172 athletes answered four additional questions; all but three athletes agreed that the questions were relevant to their sports. Nearly all the participants indicated that the questionnaire was not difficult to complete, that no items should be changed or added to the questionnaire, and that the web-based method worked well. Suggestions for linguistic improvements were minor.

Reliability

Test–retest reliability analysis was possible with 144 participants who completed the questionnaire twice within 24–72 h. The ICC was 0.84 (95% CI 0.81–0.88) for symptoms, 0.83 (95% CI 0.81–0.84) for soreness, 0.83 (95% CI 0.82–0.85) for pain, 0.80 (95% CI 0.77–0.82) for function in sports, and 0.88 (95% CI 0.86–0.90) for quality of life. Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to translate the HaOS questionnaire, developed as an aid in the prevention and treatment of HSI, into Japanese. In this study, the HaOS questionnaire was translated from English to Japanese, and the translation was completed by an expert committee. As a result, the respondents were able to answer the questions without hesitation. Regarding the HaOS reliability, all subscales had an ICC of 0.80 or higher, indicating high reliability23,24. For internal consistency, the Cronbach’s alpha for HaOS subscales ranged from 0.72 to 0.96, demonstrating that the items in the questionnaire are homogeneous and not redundant24,25. In addition, the use of web-based functions makes it easy for the athletes and their coaches to monitor their condition and is less burdensome.

The second objective was to examine the differences in HaOS depending on the presence or absence of a history of HSI. We found a significant difference between those with and without a history of HSI. A previous study of amateur soccer athletes in the Netherlands reported that athletes with HSI in the previous season had lower total HaOS and subscale scores than non-injured athletes13. In the present study, the athletes were divided into groups based on whether they had a previous injury, regardless of when they had experienced HSI, and the results were similar to those of a previous study13. However, there are cases with a history of HSI that do not have recurrent. Therefore, it is meaningful to examine the association between the number of previous injuries and HaOS. Thus, we further subdivided the previous injury group by recurrence and found significant group differences in the three subscales, symptoms, soreness, and quality of life. One possible explanation for the high recurrence rate of HSI is a premature return to sport due to underestimation of severity26,27. A previous study using HaOS used a cutoff score of 80% or lower to classify athletes at a high risk of HSI12. According to a prospective study of amateur soccer athletes, the probability of HSI within a season was 18% for athletes with one previous injury and increased to 28% for athletes with two previous injuries13. These findings suggest that premature return to sport after a HSI may contribute to a poor prognosis, resulting in a higher recurrence rate of HSI. To prevent this, regular monitoring of the hamstring condition using HaOS may be helpful.

Further, HaOS may be useful as a follow-up after HSI. Although an imaging evaluation, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), is known to be the gold standard for assessing the severity of HSI28,29, it is an expensive examination and difficult to assess in the field. Moreover, there is limited evidence to support their role in predicting return to competition30. Furthermore, a systematic review of the recurrence risk of HSI showed the possibility that clinical examination was more appropriate than imaging31. One reason for this may be that improvement on imaging after muscle strain occurs later than improvement on clinical examination. In fact, it has been reported that even if abnormal MRI findings (residual high intensity signal in the affected area) persisted at return to play, players were able to return with a low risk of recurrence32,33. While some clinical evaluations have not been adequately examined for validity and reliability34, we found in the present study that the Japanese version of HaOS shows high validity and reliability. In the future, it is expected that the application of HaOS from the onset of HSI to the return to sports would improve the criteria for return to sports and the extraction of cases with poor prognosis. Internet-based surveys have the advantage of being less burdensome for athletes and easier to conduct on an ongoing basis35. Hence, regular monitoring by the Japanese version of HaOS is necessary to confirm its effectiveness in preventing not only the recurrence of HSI but also the first onset of it.

Several limitations were encountered during this study. First, it was not possible to investigate criterion-related validity. This was due to the lack of an existing method for objectively assessing hamstring condition. This problem has been reported previously21. Second, there was a reduction in the number of athletes used in the test–retest reliability analysis. This was due to limiting the time to 72 h or less; however, this did not present as a problem in terms of the sample size. Third, the lack of consideration of competition characteristics was not considered. In the future, we plan to conduct a larger prospective study that considers competition characteristics.

In conclusion, the validity and reliability of the Japanese translation of HaOS were both demonstrated. Further, the total score and subscales of HaOS differed depending on the presence or absence of a preexisting injury, and those with a preexisting injury had lower scores than those without a preexisting injury. The Japanese translation of HaOS would be useful for monitoring individual hamstring conditions in Japanese athletes (Supplementary file 1).
