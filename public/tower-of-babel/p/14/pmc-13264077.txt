A multi-condition acoustic dataset of ball bearings for fault diagnosis
Authors: Cuifeng Xu, Xiaoyu Wang, Jing Huang, Yuanning Lu, Haitao Su, Jiaqi Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work provides a multi-condition, long-duration acoustic dataset for ball bearings, covering five states: normal, cage fracture, inner race pitting, outer race pitting, and compound inner-outer race pitting. These data were acquired separately for each of the combinations of three rotational speeds (800, 1000, and 1200 r/min) and three load levels (0%, 15%, and 30% of the rated torque, where 100% corresponds to 6 Nm). Acoustic signals were synchronously recorded using two B&K 4966 and one CRY333 microphones, with a continuous 50-minute recording for each of the 45 operational conditions at a sampling rate of 32,768 Hz. The raw data are provided in the proprietary .bkc format, with a total duration of 2250 minutes. Files are organized in a hierarchical directory structure (speed-load-fault type) and accompanied by metadata tables. The dataset supports tasks such as feature extraction, pattern recognition, and can serve as a benchmark for developing and validating fault diagnosis algorithms for rotating machinery, particularly for evaluating model performance across varying operating conditions.Keywords: Condition monitoring, Predictive maintenance, Pattern recognition, Feature extraction, Machine learning, Non-contact sensing

Background

Non-contact acoustic sensing is becoming an increasingly important approach for the condition monitoring and fault diagnosis of rotating machinery, especially considering the practical challenges and high costs associated with installing traditional vibration sensors. However, the development of robust, cross-condition intelligent diagnostic models is frequently hindered by the limitations of existing public acoustic datasets. Currently available datasets are typically restricted to single operating conditions or possess small sample sizes, lacking systematically varied operational data. To address this gap, this multi-condition acoustic dataset of ball bearings was compiled. We utilized a rotor-bearing fault simulation test bench to systematically record acoustic signals across explicit combinations of various load levels, rotational speeds, and typical bearing fault types, providing a foundational data source for multi-condition pattern recognition.

Data Description

This dataset consists of acoustic data acquired under varying load and speed conditions. The load was controlled by a self-cooling brake, and the rotational speed was maintained at one of three preset levels by the main drive motor. The acoustic signals were captured using three microphones: a CRY333 and two B&K 4966 models, positioned as detailed in Section 3.1 (Test Rig Description). Their respective sensitivities were 23.98 mV/Pa (CRY333 at position 1), 49.76 mV/Pa (B&K at position 2), and 46.95 mV/Pa (B&K at position 3). All microphones were positioned near the bearing housing. Acoustic signals were collected using a B&K Type 3052-A-030 acquisition card at a sampling frequency of 32,768 Hz. The raw data were recorded by BK Connect software and are provided in the proprietary .bkc format (the software's default output). The data can also be exported from this software into .csv files containing the following columns: ``Time'', ``Signal 1'', ``Signal 2'', and ``Signal 3''. Here, ``Signal 1'' (unit: Volts, V) was acquired by the Zhaohua Electronics CRY333 microphone, while ``Signal 2'' and ``Signal 3'' (unit: Pascals, Pa) were acquired by the B&K 4966 microphones.

The naming and description of the acoustic files follow the operating and fault conditions of the rotating machine, as outlined below. Files sharing the same prefix correspond to the same combination of operational condition (speed and load) and fault type. Each file contains 10 minutes of data, with files 1-5 together constituting the full 50-minute recording for that condition. For example, the five files nm800r-0-32Khz.1.bkc, nm800r-0-32Khz.2.bkc, nm800r-0-32Khz.3.bkc, nm800r-0-32Khz.4.bkc, and nm800r-0-32Khz.5.bkc all describe the acoustic data of a normal bearing under 800 r/min and 0 load, each containing ten minutes of data, totaling 50 minutes. For conciseness, the notation nm800r-0-32Khz.1-5.bkc is used to refer to this set.

Specifically, the spectrograms (subplot (b) in Figs. 1-5) represent the distribution of acoustic energy across both time and frequency domains. By comparing these spectrograms, researchers can intuitively observe how structural defects introduce periodic, high-energy acoustic impulses (transients) across specific broad frequency bands, which are distinctly absent in the acoustic profile of a normal bearing. Aligning these observed periodic acoustic impulses with the theoretical fault frequencies of the bearing provides a reliable basis for identifying the specific fault type.

To further illustrate the data quality and the influence of the unisolated test environment, a background noise analysis is provided. As shown in Fig. 6, the ambient noise in the laboratory is a stationary, continuous random noise lacking sudden high-energy interference in the time domain, and its acoustic energy is inherently weak in the frequency domain. Because the test setup utilized near-field acoustic measurement (microphones positioned closely adjacent to the bearing housing), the acoustic emissions from the bearing heavily dominate the captured signal due to the inverse-square law of acoustics. Consequently, a high Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) is maintained. As demonstrated in the raw time-domain waveforms of the faulty bearings (e.g., Fig. 2(b) for inner race pitting), the crucial repetitive high-frequency transient acoustic impulses generated by the bearing defects remain highly distinct and are not obscured by the ambient baseline.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Test rig description

The HZXT-008 rotor-bearing comprehensive fault simulation test bench was employed. The experimental system comprised a 930mm × 180mm × 200mm solid T-slot cast iron platform, a 400W motor with its mounting bracket, a 10mm diameter straight steel shaft, a 6200 deep groove ball bearing, an adjustable eccentric bearing housing (precision: 0.01mm), a coupling, an epicyclic gearbox (speed ratio 3:1), a loader, and the test bench control system, as shown in Fig. 7. To effectively suppress macroscopic structural resonance and isolate floor vibrations, the heavy cast iron platform (weighing approximately 45 kg) was properly mounted on dedicated vibration isolation pads.

Layout of the rotor-bearing comprehensive fault simulation test bench and its components.

The drive system, a 400W variable-frequency motor, supplied the three rotational speeds used in this experiment. It was powered by 220V AC, with a rated speed of 3000 r/min and a maximum speed of 6000 r/min. The loading device, which provided the three load levels, had a rated torque of 6 Nm, a rated voltage of 24V DC, and employed a self-cooling method.

Based on near-field acoustic sensing principles, three microphones (two B&K 4966 and one CRY333) were positioned closely adjacent to the bearing housing to significantly improve the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for bearing-specific sounds and capture its acoustic characteristics under different rotational speeds, loads, and fault conditions. An overview of the complete system and the placement of its components is provided in Fig. 8.

Overall view of the experimental system.

Description of faulty bearings

This section details the structure of the rolling bearings used and the types of faults introduced. A rolling bearing typically consists of an inner ring (inner raceway), an outer ring (outer raceway), rolling elements (e.g., balls), and a cage. The inner and outer raceways contain grooves. The rolling elements are positioned between these raceways and are evenly spaced by the cage, which serves to support axial or radial loads and reduce friction [3].

Five conditions of deep-grove ball bearings were utilized: one normal bearing and four with artificially introduced typical faults (Fig. 8). The specific faults are as follows: a) Compound inner-outer race pitting: Single-point pitting defects were present on both the inner and outer raceway surfaces; b) Inner race pitting: A single-point pitting defect was present only on the inner raceway surface; c) Cage fracture: The cage was completely fractured, resulting in a loss of its rolling element separation function; d) Outer race pitting: A single-point pitting defect was present only on the outer raceway surface; e) Normal bearing: Served as the healthy baseline with no defects.

All faults were manufactured under controlled conditions using methods such as electrical erosion, wire cutting, or fatigue testing to simulate damage forms representative of those occurring in actual operation. It should be explicitly noted that the pits visible on the side surfaces of the bearing rings (as shown in Fig. 9) are merely external visual markers provided by the manufacturer. The actual physical defects are located directly inside the raceways, perfectly aligned with these external markers. This design allows researchers to quickly identify the internal fault location without disassembling the bearing. During operation, the rolling elements continuously impact these internal defects, guaranteeing the accurate generation of fault-specific acoustic emissions.

Limitations

The acoustic data in this dataset were collected using a rotor-bearing comprehensive fault simulation test bench in a laboratory setting. Although environmental background noise was not artificially suppressed during the recording process, the acoustic scene may still lack the complexity, severe interference, and overlapping machinery noise typically found in actual industrial environments.

Additionally, the operational conditions were constrained by the specifications of the test rig. The maximum rotational speed was limited to 1200 r/min, and the applied load reached a maximum of 30% of the rated torque (6 Nm). Consequently, the dataset does not encompass the acoustic characteristics of bearings operating under extreme high-speed or heavy-load conditions.

Finally, the bearing faults (e.g., pitting and cage fracture) were artificially manufactured under controlled conditions using methods such as electrical erosion or wire cutting. While representative, these artificially induced defects might present slight differences in acoustic emission profiles compared to damages resulting from natural wear and degradation over a prolonged lifecycle.
