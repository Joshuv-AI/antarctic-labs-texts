Methods of a cluster-randomized, type II hybrid implementation effectiveness trial to prospectively assess extended-duration thromboprophylaxis for at-risk medical patients being discharged to prevent hospital-associated venous thromboembolism
Authors: Scott C Woller, Scott M Stevens, Joseph R Bledsoe, James Hellewell, Adam Kraft, Allison M Butler, Masarret Fazili, James F Lloyd, Paige S Christensen, Ithan D Peltan, Geoffrey D Barnes, Benjamin D Horne
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVenous thromboembolism (VTE) is the third leading cause of preventable hospital-associated (HA) death. Most HA-VTE, including fatal pulmonary emboli, occur among medically ill patients. The rate of symptomatic VTE more than doubles over the first 21 days after hospital discharge. Trials have demonstrated that the burden of HA-VTE may be reduced with postdischarge thromboprophylaxis; however, few patients receive this therapy. We formerly validated the ability of eVTE (eVTE is the abbreviation for a risk assessment tool constituted by 2 calculations: one predicts 90-day VTE and the other predicts 30-day major bleeding derived from only elements of the complete blood count and basic metabolic panel and age) to identify medical patients being discharged with both an elevated risk of VTE and a low risk of bleeding.ObjectivesImplement a cluster-randomized, stepped wedge, type II hybrid implementation/effectiveness trial generating an alert among select at-risk patients upon discharge for implementation of thrombosis chemoprophylaxis in a 23-hospital not-for-profit healthcare system.MethodsWe use the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance framework to guide implementation and outcomes reporting.ResultsThe primary outcome for aim 1 (implementation) is the prescription of rivaroxaban 10 mg daily for 30 days as postdischarge thromboprophylaxis among at-risk patients. The primary efficacy and safety outcomes (effectiveness) are the 90-day composite of symptomatic VTE, myocardial infartcion, nonhemorrhagic stroke, all-cause mortality, and 30-day major bleeding.ConclusionThe eVTE trial will provide high-quality, real-world evidence on the effectiveness and safety of a pragmatic intervention to implement targeted postdischarge thromboprophylaxis using decision support embedded in the electronic health record.Keywords: extended-duration thromboprophylaxis, medical patients, prophylaxis, risk score, thrombosis, venous thromboembolism

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) is the third leading cause of preventable hospital-associated (HA) death. Approximately 8 million patients are hospitalized in the United States for a nonsurgical indication each year [1]. An estimated 75% of all HA-VTE and 70% to 80% of fatal pulmonary emboli occur among hospitalized medically ill patients [2,3]. The rate of symptomatic VTE more than doubles over the first 21 days after hospital discharge [4], and as high as 75% of all HA-VTE events are believed to occur after hospital discharge [5,6]. Postdischarge anticoagulant prophylaxis, also referred to as extended-duration thromboprophylaxis (EDT), may reduce VTE but can be burdensome and carries a risk of bleeding. While it is estimated that nearly 1 in 4 discharged medical patients may benefit from EDT [7], prospective randomized control trials have demonstrated the challenges associated with identifying the best candidates for EDT. In fact, the prospective randomized controlled trials assessing EDT did not achieve the predetermined outcome, and existing guidelines suggest against the routine use of EDT [8]. As such, clinical uptake has been slow [[9], [10], [11], [12]].

Identifying patients that would benefit from EDT has been stymied by the observation that patients who are at an increased risk of postdischarge VTE often also have a concomitant significant risk of bleeding. Previous studies have suggested a narrow margin of potential benefit for the application of EDT; therefore, it would be ideal to identify the cohort of patients who have a concomitant high risk of VTE and a low risk of major bleeding.

We derived [13] and externally validated [14] a novel risk prediction model model we term ‘eVTE’ which is the abbreviation for the aggregate of 2 risk prediction models. One risk prediction model predicts 90-day VTE and the second predicts 30-day major bleeding among medical patients being discharged. The eVTE model utilizes only routinely available coded data elements, including age and components of the complete blood count and basic metabolic profile [13]. The eVTE calculation separately estimates each individual patient’s risk of thrombosis and risk of major bleeding, creating a conventional 2 × 2 grid of thrombosis and major bleeding risk, thereby identifying a cohort of patients who have an estimated high risk of VTE and a low risk of major bleeding. These represent patients predicted to have a net clinical benefit for EDT.

Based on trials demonstrating the effectiveness of EDT for the prevention of postdischarge VTE [9,12], the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved a direct oral anticoagulant for this indication [15]. Post hoc analyses have suggested that there is still a greater probability of benefit should a low-risk-for-bleeding population be identified [16]. However, formerly proposed risk prediction scores require knowledge of patient history and incorporate laboratory testing that is not a part of routine medical hospitalization [9,12]. The eVTE score overcomes those barriers as it utilizes routinely available, objective, standardized coded data elements that do not require human assessment or input and are sensitive to both declines and improvements in health.

Increasingly, widespread electronic health record (EHR) adoption to advance evidence-based interventions through automated clinical decision support is occurring [17]. Medical informatics has guided the development of Substitutable Medical Applications and Reusable Technologies on Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources platforms [18,19]. Application programming interface tools including the creation of application galleries for disseminating clinical decision support are emerging. Integrating clinical decision support into routine workflow is a potentially powerful tool to support efficient and value-based delivery of high-quality healthcare generally and for HA-VTE prevention specifically [20]. We hypothesize that embedded decision support based on eVTE will overcome current barriers to postdischarge HA-VTE risk reduction. However, because this hypothesis requires prospective validation [21,22], we will employ a study design that can test implementation (aim 1) while reporting the effectiveness of the intervention to improve important patient outcomes (aim 2) [23].

The eVTE trial is a pragmatic, prospective, cluster-randomized, stepped wedge, type II hybrid implementation/effectiveness trial. Cluster randomization permits simultaneous exposure to the alert at groups of hospitals where hospitalists practice to assure balanced deployment and avoid potential bias such as Hawthorne effect. Type II effectiveness-implementation trials place equal importance on clinical effectiveness and implementation outcomes [23,24]. This is an appropriate design given this trial’s aims to both assess the introduction of an alert to a healthcare system (implementation) and to report the effect of the alert on the clinical outcome of postdischarge thrombosis. While we estimate that the study will not be sufficiently powered to quantitatively assess the composite clinical outcome, we nevertheless place equal importance on the implementation outcome and the patient’s important clinical outcomes. Furthermore, while eVTE was derived to predict postdischarge VTE and major bleeding because of evidence suggesting that rivaroxaban may decrease arterial cardiovascular outcome events [16] and wishing to report all aspects of patient-important “net clinical benefit” after conferring with statisticians being mindful of power, we elected to include arterial thrombotic outcome events as part of the composite efficacy outcome. We use the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework to structure our approach to implementation, outcomes measurement, and sustainability [25,26] and to describe our assessment of the implementation and effectiveness of aims 1 and 2 (Table 1).

Setting

The eVTE trial will be conducted in the legacy hospitals of Intermountain Health. Legacy Intermountain is a not-for-profit healthcare system comprising 23 hospitals that provides care to approximately one-half of Utah residents and is connected with a single EHR (Cerner) with all clinical data stored in a centralized electronic data warehouse that resides behind the Intermountain Health firewall.

Study Aims and Hypotheses

Aim 1

We hypothesize that alerting will lead to a significant increase in EDT prescription in the intervention phase compared with the baseline phase. Framed in the context of RE-AIM, the reach (R) of aim 1 is all medical patients discharging that meet inclusion criteria (Table 3). The number of prescriptions of EDT rivaroxaban sent by the hospitalist or surrogate with responsibilities for discharging a patient (hereafter referred to as the “discharging clinician”) during the intervention will be compared with the number of prescriptions sent during the baseline phase among analogous patients. The intervention of alerting will be introduced in a step-wedge fashion to hospitals selected at random. The operational effectiveness (E) outcome will be reported in terms of the number of prescriptions sent among at-risk patients. Adoption (A) will be reported at system and individual levels. At the system level, the activation rate of the alert in the institution will be reported. At the individual level, adoption will be reported in the context of the hospitalist’s interaction with the alert in the EHR, and we will report the rate at which the discharging clinician elects to accept or reject the alert and the reason for doing so. Implementation (I) will be reported as the step-wedge introduction of the alert at hospitals (system) and response to the alert by the discharging clinician (individual). We will measure implementation success at the 6- and 12-month marks. Maintenance (M) is often considered only measurable at 2 years [25]; however, we will report the adherence to eVTE practice at the 12-month mark (end of study).

Aim 2

We hypothesize that there will be a signal of net clinical benefit during the intervention phase compared with the baseline phase. The reach (R) of aim 2 is all discharging patients that meet inclusion criteria for an alert and those for which the alert is accepted. The clinical effectiveness (E) outcome of the alert will be reported in terms of observed primary efficacy outcome (90-day VTE, nonhemorrhagic stroke, myocardial infarction [MI], and death) and the primary safety outcome (30-day major bleeding), and the net clinical benefit (the primary efficacy outcome + the primary safety outcome) and components of the outcome compared among patients in the baseline phase and patients in the intervention phase for whom an alert is generated and for whom an alert is accepted. Adoption (A) and implementation (I) will be reported as in aim 1. Maintenance (M) will be reported at the end of the trial (12 months), acknowledging the literature suggests that maintenance is only generally measurable at 2 years [25].

Study Procedures

Implementation of the eVTE study necessitates innovation in 2 broad areas. The first is a technical area of embedding the calculation logic and selective surfacing of the eVTE alert in clinical workflow. The second is outcome ascertainment.

Foundational work for each aim will include generating an alert to the appropriate discharging clinician when the discharge process is initiated. The alert will be actionable and include selections for EDT or a contraindication thereof. The alert will include a “hover info box” with links to detailed information regarding eVTE for the discharging clinician.

eVTE risk score calculation

The eVTE risk score has 2 independent components. The first is for thrombosis risk, and the second is for major bleeding. “eVTE” refers to the aggregate risk assessment for thrombosis and bleeding components that inform the actionable recommendation for thromboprophylaxis. eVTE performance in a research dataset was previously reported [13,27,28]. For this trial, the eVTE risk calculation tool had to be built into the EHR. eVTE risk calculation using the coded data elements from the most recent basic metabolic panel and complete blood count and patient age will occur at the time that patient discharge is initiated (Figure 1).

eVTE calculation for estimating the risk of venous thromboembolism = y. A risk score of ≥ 7 identifies those patients at high risk for venous thromboembolism. eVTE calculation for estimating the risk of major bleeding = z. A risk score of ≥ 8 identifies those patients estimated to not be at a low risk for major bleeding.

Electronic data warehouse repository

eVTE risk calculations and all outcomes will be stored in an electronic data warehouse data table behind the Intermountain firewall. Each data element of effectiveness, adoption, and implementation along with 30- and 90-day clinical outcomes are stored as we have previously described [29] and published [27,[30], [31], [32], [33]].

Identification of eligible patient cohort

Data and outcomes of all medical patients being discharged will be recorded. For the purposes of the alert, hospitalized medical patients will be eligible for an eVTE alert if the criteria in Table 3 are met.

Alerting

The discharging clinician communicates with nursing to begin the discharge process. The nurse initiates a discharge order, and once that order is in place, the next time that the discharging clinician places any order, the eVTE risk score will be calculated. If a patient meets the criteria, then the alert will “pop up” during the discharge ordering process (Figure 2). The alert will include the risk message and cue the discharging clinician to (1) accept the alert (triggering the electronic prescription of rivaroxaban), (2) reject the alert for perceived bleeding risk, (3) reject the alert because of disagreement with the risk assessment, or (4) reject the alert for any other reason. Alternatively, the discharging clinician may elect to “snooze the alert” until the subsequent time that an order is placed or permanently electing out of the process (if they are not involved in patient discharge).

Recording alert effectiveness

Recording alert effectiveness for aim 1

The hospitalist upon receiving an alert will be able to (1) snooze the alert once, (2) accept the alert leading to the medication prescription, or (3) reject the alert for one of the reasons noted above. The effectiveness of the alert will be reported as the number of prescriptions sent (numerator) over all alerted patients (denominator) during the intervention phase. The alerted patients are those at a high risk of thrombosis and a low risk of bleeding and represent the target population for the alert. We will similarly report when the discharging clinician elects to snooze, accept, or reject the alert with each of these outcomes in the target population (numerator) over all alerts fired (denominator). This will be compared with the number of prescriptions sent (numerator) for all at-risk patients that would meet the criteria to receive an alert as a function of inclusion criteria, including renal function, platelet count, pregnancy status, etc. (denominator) during the baseline phase. Because no alerts exist during the baseline phase, a sensitivity analysis will be performed to compare the rate of prescription (numerator) among all discharging patients (denominator) between the baseline and intervention periods.

EDT is defined as the rate of prescription for rivaroxaban 10 mg daily guided by CDS for 30 days, including any duration of 28 to 45 days (the duration approved by the FDA) in the study cohort. Rivaroxaban fill rates will be recorded when available through the insurer that is affiliated with the health system.

Recording alert effectiveness for aim 2

The outcomes of VTE, MI, stroke, and major bleeding will be ascertained using validated EHR interrogation as previously described [13,27,[30], [31], [32], [33]]. VTE will be defined as a positive imaging result for thrombosis in the lower extremities or pulmonary embolism (ascertained by natural language processing interrogation of clinical radiology reports in the EHR) [29]. Arterial vascular outcomes of MI and nonhemorrhagic stroke will be identified if EHR interrogation identifies one of the following:

(1)A coronary artery disease-related atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease event, including 1) having a primary International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, inpatient diagnosis code of coronary artery disease or acute MI or 2) having undergone a documented percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass graft surgery at Intermountain Health.

(2)A cerebrovascular-related event, including i) having a primary International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, inpatient diagnosis of ischemic stroke, or ii) having undergone a documented carotid artery endarterectomy or stenting at Intermountain Health [[31], [32], [33]].

Major bleeding will be defined as per International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis guidelines [34] and will be derived electronically as we have previously reported [13,35]. Death events will be identified via a preexisting linkage of the electronic data warehouse to Utah State Death Records and the Social Security Death Master File. We will determine the outcomes of return to the emergency department and rehospitalization by the corresponding encounter code across the Intermountain Health system.

Defining alert effectiveness for aim 2

Alert effectiveness for aim 2 will be calculated by comparing the rate of the clinical composite outcome among those patients for which an alert was accepted leading to the prescription of rivaroxaban with the rate of the clinical composite outcome among patients that would otherwise have qualified for an alert during the baseline phase.

eVTE pilot testing

The eVTE alert functionality will first be validated in “silent mode” so that the thrombosis and bleeding risk are available to investigators without engaging clinical workflow. To ensure that the alert is sent to the proper discharging clinician, a period of selective alerting to only members of the eVTE pilot team will occur such that 1) the alert surfaces only in the correct person workflow, 2) the alert’s “1-click solution” functions to send a proper medication prescription, 3) a 254-character explanation accompanies each prescription (to orient the patient to the indication for this postdischarge prescription), 4) the “1-click solution” triggers documentation of the discharging physician’s interaction with the alert, and 5) the alert’s “hover info box” functions.

Primary outcome (implementation)

All outcomes are reported in Table 2. The primary outcome will be the prescription of the qualifying dosing of rivaroxaban among at-risk patients discharging from all 23 Intermountain Health hospitals. The step-wedge design will permit the prescription of EDT during the intervention phase to be compared with the prescription of EDT during the baseline phase when no alert is available to the discharging clinician. The proportion of EDT prescriptions will be compared between the baseline and intervention phases. We will report the proportions of alerts that are accepted and rejected and the reasons for rejection.

Primary efficacy and safety outcomes (effectiveness)

The primary efficacy outcome will be a composite of clinically overt symptomatic VTE, myocardial infartcion (MI), nonhemorrhagic stroke, and all-cause mortality within 90 days of hospital discharge assessed among those patients for whom an alert is generated during the intervention phase compared with the analogous population of at-risk patients during the baseline phase when no alert is available to the discharging clinician. The primary safety outcome is major bleeding within 30 days of discharge assessed among those patients for whom alerting leads to the prescription of EDT during the intervention phase compared with the analogous population of at-risk patients during the baseline phase when no alert is available to the discharging clinician. We will perform as-treated analysis to report the primary efficacy and safety outcomes in the cohort of patients for which the alert is accepted and rejected, and furthermore among all subgroups of patients assessed to be at variable risk of VTE and bleeding based on eVTE. Additional secondary outcomes are found in Table 2. To assess for health equity, patient characteristics including demographics, diagnosis codes, and comorbidities will be captured.

Randomization and treatment allocation

Alert activation will occur at monthly intervals for clusters of 2 to 4 hospitals beginning 1 month after study initiation (Table 4). The sequence of hospital activations was assigned randomly using a computer-generated sequence. To assure balanced implementation and to avoid potential bias (eg, Hawthorne effect) when deploying eVTE alerts, clusters were created by first assigning hospitals to 1 of 3 groups based on annual discharge volume and then assigning 1 hospital from the highest volume, 1 hospital from the intermediate volume, and 2 hospitals from the lowest volume group to each cluster for activation (Table 5). Pairs of hospitals where discharging clinicians work at both hospitals were considered a single hospital for group and cluster assignment to avoid spillover effects.

Statistical analysis, power calculations, and rationale

Analyses will be conducted on an intention-to-treat basis. Descriptive statistics will be used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics of patients eligible for the study. Patients’ treatment assignment for analyses will be based on the eVTE activation status in place at the discharging hospital on the day of discharge. Should a patient have greater than 1 hospital discharge within 90 days, the first discharge will be analyzed.

Primary outcome statistical analysis and power

The analysis for the primary outcome will use a mixed-effects logistic regression model comparing the prescription of EDT during the months preceding the implementation of the alert with the prescription of EDT during the months that the alert is sent, with a random effect for each hospital. Potential confounders including however not limited to age, biological sex, race, and ethnicity are identified a priori and included in the model as a secondary analysis.

Based on review of preliminary data from the study health system, 5 EDT prescriptions are currently made for about 9000 hospitalized medical patient discharges. During the baseline phase, we estimate that approximately 2100 patients will be considered eligible for EDT. Assuming that approximately 14% of discharges will be eligible for EDT and the alert will be accepted 75% of the time (estimated from pilot data), during the intervention phase, we estimate that there will be 5900 alerts generated that lead to thromboprophylaxis. Our trial will have 80% power to detect an increase of prescriptions to 32 per 9000 discharges, with 90% and 95% power to detect increase to 38 or 44 prescriptions per 9000 discharges, respectively.

Primary efficacy and safety outcome statistical analysis

The primary efficacy outcome (effectiveness) of this trial will be the composite outcome of 90-day postdischarge clinically overt VTE, nonhemorrhagic stroke, MI, and death. The time to each event will be assessed. A mixed-effects Cox regression model with random effect for hospital will be performed. Assuming a rate of 1.9% of patients experiencing a VTE, MI, or nonhemorrhagic stroke in the year after hospitalization and inclusion of 2100 patients before eVTE implementation and 5900 patients after eVTE implementation in the analysis, we will have 85% power to detect an improvement in the incidence of the key clinical composite outcome from 1.9% to 1%. We will have 42% power to detect a change from 1.9% to 1.34%, representing a relative change similar to that observed by Raskob et al. [16]. We do not include death in this power estimate, given the uncertainty of the effect attributed to rivaroxaban; however, we note the importance of all-cause mortality in the clinical outcome. For the primary safety outcome, major bleeding within 30 days, a mixed-effects Cox regression model with random effect for hospital will be performed. We will report the proportions of major bleeding among the cohort of patients for which the eVTE alert would fire during the baseline phase in relationship to the major bleeding observed during the intervention phase and also for all patients when the alert is accepted for the prescription of EDT.

Other secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes are exploratory analyses that will report on subsets of the data in relationship to the discharging clinician’s response to the alert and use as-treated analyses. For secondary exploratory analyses to be feasible, competing risks (such as VTE, MI, stroke, and death) may be assessed while adjusting for potential covariates (such as age and duration of hospitalization, among others).

Safety

The eVTE trial was reviewed by the Intermountain Health institutional review board (IRB) and granted a waiver of consent per 45 CFR 46.116(f)(3); IRB reference: 037950; NCT06232551. Sending an alert to the discharging clinician to consider the prescription of an FDA-approved medication was deemed as involving no more than minimal risk to subjects, and obtaining signed informed consent was deemed not practicably feasible. The study team will provide oversight and interim analysis and report to the IRB at 6 months following trial initiation. A potential harm of this intervention is an increased risk of bleeding among those discharged patients for whom the alert is generated (when the discharging clinician prescribes the anticoagulant, and the patient fills the prescription and takes the medication). Because this trial is assessing the interaction of the hospitalists with an alert to consider an intervention that resides within the standard of care, no formal stopping criteria exist. Members of the study team with expertise in clinical trial implementation (S.C.W., I.D.P., and S.M.S.), data safety monitoring (S.M.S., J.R.B., I.D.P., and B.D.H.), clinical hospital medicine (A.K. and J.H.), statistics (B.D.H., A.M.B., and I.D.P.), and external to the institution (J.R.B.) will review the interim analysis.

Discussion

Most patients potentially eligible for EDT upon discharge from US hospitals, including those in our health system, do not receive a prescription to prevent VTE. For our system alone, we estimated that over 10,000 patients annually are at elevated risk of VTE but low risk of bleeding and could benefit from this therapy. The reach of eVTE is broad, assessing candidacy among all hospitalized medical patients but selectively sending an alert only when risk thresholds suggest benefit for EDT. Presently, the close margin of safety and no practicable process to identify candidate patients continues to stymie the judicious implementation of EDT. This trial will serve as a template for future implementation processes to embed clinical decision support in routine discharge workflow and may provide a path to overcome the barriers to use of EDT in medically discharging patients.

Our design has several strengths. The step-wedge implementation will permit assessment of the effect at a system and individual level upon turning on the alert. Following implementation, the intervention tools will reside in the EHR with the ability to maintain uniform adoption as part of standard workflow without reliance on human resources. Once the eVTE logic is embedded in the EHR, the burden of adoption is anticipated to be negligible. Hospitalists’ feedback will inform and modifications to the alert adherent with the RE-AIM framework guidance that advises using experience to adapt the design [26]. Equally important is our ability to capture potential negative effects as is described as best practice for implementation/effectiveness studies [26,36,37], and while prospective randomized studies reporting the effect of EDT on all-cause mortality have been underpowered to provide certainty regarding this endpoint, we include this patient-important outcome as part of the composite primary outcome. Maintenance is historically among the most difficult domains reported in implementation science; however, the automated nature of a timely selective eVTE alert is characteristic of an intervention that once deployed will be effective at the individual and system levels.

The effectiveness of the eVTE alert on the composite clinical outcome and the individual components of thrombosis and bleeding will be reported. These data could inform power calculations to design future research including deployment of eVTE within other health systems. Selecting appropriate patients for EDT has been clinically difficult in the past because of the burden associated with existing risk assessment models that include consideration of clinical history that may be difficult to ascertain routinely and the dependency on laboratory tests such as D-dimer that are not a part of routine medical hospitalization [9,12]. eVTE overcomes this barrier using only coded data that is reliably available in routine inpatient care.

Informed by our interest in adopting pragmatic implementation with actionable risk assessment, we reviewed the patient eligibility criteria of published prospective randomized controlled studies (eg, including only those patients aged >40 years with heart failure, acute respiratory illness, infection, inflammatory, or rheumatologic disease) [9,12]. For the eVTE trial, we elect implementation among all hospitalized medical patients as a pragmatic design considering that all adult hospitalized medical patients are initially eligible (with exclusion criteria as noted in Table 3). This decision was informed by the fact that first, the eVTE alert was derived and validated among all hospitalized medical patients, and second, any future implementation would be more practicable if identification of candidate subgroups of hospitalized patients was unnecessary.

We elected a prospective, randomized, stepped wedge, type II hybrid implementation/effectiveness trial design as the ideal design for many reasons. The benefits of this approach include but are not limited to 1) pragmatic deployment, 2) mitigation of possible confounding effects, 3) maintenance of a contemporary control group, 4) full implementation of an intervention suspected to add benefit to our healthcare system, and 5) the favorable performance of this study design, when compared (for example) with a parallel cluster study design, because of our large cluster sizes [38]. The step-wedge design allows us to distinguish between the effects of the alerts and any external influence that may also affect the outcomes. This design also allows for implementation at the randomized sites as feasible, creating flexibility for any unexpected delays in alert notifications and ensuring that the delays will not affect outcomes as the step-wedge approach controls for the effect. Deploying eVTE as described adheres to best practice, assures an objective measurement of the eVTE intervention, and advances eVTE on the hierarchy of evidence for clinical decision rules [22].

Limitations include that the eVTE prediction tool has limited discrimination. The initial derivation cohort for postdischarge thrombosis risk prediction resulted in an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.64 and the eVTE bleed prediction AUC was 0.69 [13]. External validation of eVTE in an independent dataset of 113,000 patients yielded an AUC of 0.59 for thrombosis, and the eVTE bleed AUC was 0.78 [28]. The number needed to treat to prevent 1 VTE event is estimated to be 256, and the number needed to harm for major bleeding is 443. However, eVTE performs comparably with the performance of clinical risk prediction scores used in the prospective randomized controlled trials. A systematic review and meta-analysis of those studies estimated a number needed to treat to protect against the outcome of symptomatic VTE/VTE-related death was 250 in comparison with a number needed to harm of 333 for the outcome of major/fatal bleeding [39]. Additional limitations include that this trial is being conducted in 1 healthcare system, and while we are able to ascertain results only in the case patients return to our integrated healthcare system for subsequent care, prior unpublished data indicate that more than 90% of patients return for subsequent care and, among those who do not return, it is reasonable to assume that admission to non-Intermountain hospitals will be at random with respect to use or nonuse of the eVTE tool. We are unable to ascertain whether every patient fills the prescription for rivaroxaban prescribed at discharge; however, for those patients who use the insurance product owned by Intermountain Health, we will be able to report drug fill rates in proportion to prescriptions provided. We are unable to ascertain and report clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding; however, we will report unscheduled emergency department visits.

Conclusion

eVTE results will serve to inform the utility of a pragmatic clinical trial design to implement clinician behavioral change related to an embedded alert and describe the impact of postdischarge EDT to mitigate HA thrombosis.
