Tdrd15 is dispensable for male fertility and spermatogenesis in the golden hamster
Authors: Miao Zhu, Yijian Xiang, Haodong Wang, Shanmeizi Zhao, Wentao Zeng, Jianmin Li, Bing Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Tudor domain-containing proteins (TDRDs) constitute an evolutionarily conserved protein family and are critical for germline development and piRNA pathway regulation, with established roles in male fertility. While multiple TDRD family members have been functionally linked to spermatogenic impairment, the precise biological role of TDRD15 remains to be elucidated. We used CRISPR/Cas9-mediated gene editing to generate Tdrd15 knockout (KO) golden hamsters (Mesocricetus auratus), a model necessitated by the absence of a functional Tdrd15 ortholog in the mouse genome, to investigate its function in male reproduction. Phylogenetic analysis demonstrated that TDRD15 is strongly conserved among eutherian mammals, with testis-restricted expression patterns in hamsters. Despite the successful induction of frameshift mutations and significant transcriptional knockdown, Tdrd15 KO males maintained normal fertility parameters, including unaltered testicular architecture, spermatogenic progression (confirmed by periodic acidic–Schiff (PAS) staining and immunohistochemistry), and sperm quality metrics determined using a computer-assisted analysis. Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) revealed compensatory overexpression of paralogous Tdrd genes in KO testes, implying functional redundancy within this protein family. This study provides the first experimental evidence that TDRD15 is dispensable for male fertility in golden hamsters under physiological conditions, thereby challenging the prevailing assumptions of its obligatory function in spermiogenesis. Altogether, these findings support a more targeted allocation of research efforts within the field of male reproductive biology.

Introduction

Spermatogenesis is a highly coordinated biological process that is orchestrated through multifaceted regulatory networks. These networks govern precise stage-specific transcriptional regulation across distinct developmental phases [1]. Tudor domain-containing proteins (TDRDs), characterized by their conserved Tudor domains, are germline-enriched factors that are critical for gametogenesis and germ cell growth [2, 3]. One of the major functions of TDRDs involves binding the methylated arginine residues on P-element-induced wimpy testis (PIWI) proteins through their Tudor domains, a mechanism essential for piRNA biogenesis and germline integrity [4–7]. More than 10 members of the TDRD protein family have been identified across diverse organisms, which include Tdrd1, Tdrkh (Tdrd2), Tdrd3, Rnf17 (Tdrd4), Tdrd5, Tdrd6, Tdrd7, Stk31 (Tdrd8), Tdrd9, Tdrd10, Tdrd12, Tdrd13, and Tdrd15 [3, 8].

Experimental studies in mice have demonstrated that genetic ablation or mutagenesis of multiple Tdrd genes disrupts spermiogenesis, causing male infertility. In addition, consistent with study findings using murine models, clinical evidence reveals that both heterozygous and homozygous pathogenic variants in TDRD genes (e.g., TDRD1, TDRKH, TDRD5, TDRD6, TDRD7, TDRD9, and TDRD12) contribute to human spermatogenic failure [9–14], manifesting as non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA) or severe oligozoospermia. Biallelic variants in TDRD5 impair piRNA biogenesis, thereby reducing germ cell-derived piRNA levels and compromising spermatogenic progression [11].

Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of further investigation into the functional roles of TDRD family members in regulating male reproductive capacity. While multiple TDRDs are implicated in spermatogenesis and fertility maintenance, the specific contribution of TDRD15 remains poorly characterized. Functional studies of TDRD15 have been precluded by the absence of a Tdrd15 ortholog in the mouse genome. Therefore, we utilized the CRISPR/Cas9 system to generate Tdrd15-knockout models in the golden hamster [15], to investigate its reproductive role. Comprehensive analyses, including testicular/epididymal histology, immunohistochemical profiling, and computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA), revealed preserved spermatogenic processes and largely intact fertility parameters in Tdrd15-deficient males. The observed phenotypic resilience suggests the presence of potential functional compensation mechanisms within the TDRD protein network, consistent with the established genetic redundancy patterns in reproductive biology.

Materials and methods

Animals

This study was approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Nanjing Medical University (No. IACUC-2411048) and was conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All golden hamsters were purchased from Beijing Vital River (Charles River) and Liaoning Changshen Biotechnology and raised at the Experimental Animal Center of Nanjing Medical University. The golden hamsters were allowed free access to food and water throughout the experiment. All the cages were exactly the same size and material. Animals were housed in a climate-controlled facility (20–26 °C, 30–70% humidity, 14 h light–10 h darkness cycles). All golden hamsters were treated humanely and all efforts were made to minimize suffering.

Generation of Tdrd15−/− golden hamster

sgRNAs targeting Tdrd15 Exon 1 (5′-gggtgtcgatgagttttgtctggtgg-3′) were designed (http://crispor.gi.ucsc.edu/), PCR-amplified with T7 promoters, and transcribed in vitro (HiScribe T7 Kit, NEB). The preparation of donor/recipient hamsters and microinjection were carried out as detailed previously [16–18]. Two-cell embryos from superovulated donors were injected with a mixture of 50 ng/μL Cas9 mRNA and 20 ng μL sgRNA under a red-filter-equipped microscope in a red-light environment. Embryos were cultured briefly (mHECM9 + HSA, 10% CO2, 37.5 °C), and transferred (15–20 embryos/recipient) into true pregnant albino hamsters.

Genotype identification

Founders were mated with wild-type (WT) hamsters to establish the F1 generation. Genomic DNA from F1 progeny was screened using a commercial one-step genotyping kit (Vazyme, PD101-01), followed by PCR amplification with flanking primers (forward: 5′-cgagtgtcttcccaaggatgttc-3′; reverse: 5′-gggtgaccatttctctccaactg-3′) and verified by Sanger sequencing.

Fertility test

To evaluate the reproductive competence of gene knockout (KO) golden hamsters, fertility testing was systematically performed using two complementary approaches.

First, sexually mature KO golden hamsters (8–10 weeks of age) were housed individually with proven fertile wild-type (WT) partners of the opposite sex at a 1:1 ratio, allowing sufficient opportunities for natural mating. The number of litters and time to first litter were recorded as primary fertility endpoints.

Second, a controlled timed-mating protocol was employed based on the characteristic 4-day estrous cycle of golden hamsters. At 6–7 p.m. on the fourth day of the estrous cycle, KO males were paired with wild-type counterparts. The following morning, vaginal smears were collected and examined microscopically for the presence of sperm to confirm successful copulation. Upon detection of sperm in vaginal smears, the male was removed from the mating cage. The female was subsequently monitored for pregnancy status, with litter size recorded upon parturition. If no sperm was identified, the above procedure was repeated.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from testicular tissue using TRIzol™ reagent (Vazyme, R411-01) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA purity and concentration were assessed spectrophotometrically. Subsequently, 2 μg of total RNA was then reverse transcribed into complementary cDNA using a PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Yugong Biotech, EG15133S). Quantitative real-time PCR was carried out on a Roche real-time PCR system using the following 20 μL reaction mixture: 10 μL SYBR Green Mix, 0.4 μL each of forward and reverse primers (sequences in Table 1), 2 μL cDNA template, and 7.2 μL ddH2O. The comparative 2−ΔΔCT method was utilized to calculate the relative gene expression with the housekeeping gene as internal control. Tissue processing and histological analysis

For spermatozoa samples, preparation was instead conducted by spreading cells onto microscope slides and allowed to air-dry. These cells were then fixed for 20 min using 1% paraformaldehyde in PBS. The hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining was conducted by washing samples thrice with PBST. Testes and epididymides from Tdrd15+/+ and Tdrd15−/− golden hamsters were fixed in modified Davidson’s solution (48 h), dehydrated through an ethanol gradient (70%, 2 h; 80%, 2 h; 90%, 2 h; 100%, 1 h), paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. After rehydration, sections were stained with hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) or periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) using standard protocols.

Sperm morphology and motility assessment

For sperm deformity analysis, head and tail abnormalities were quantified separately. Computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA) was performed using the Ceros™ II system (Hamilton Thorne). Briefly, caudal epididymal sperm were suspended in human tubal fluid medium (HTF) and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min under atmospheric conditions prior to analysis.

Immunofluorescence

Testes tissues of Tdrd15-genotyped hamsters underwent 48-h fixation in modified Davidson’s reagent, standard ethanol series dehydration (70–100%), paraffin embedding, and microtome sectioning (5 μm). Following deparaffinization, tissue sections were rehydrated through a descending ethanol series and processed for antigen retrieval by microwave heating in citrate buffer (20 min). To perform immunostaining, the sections underwent permeabilization using 0.1% Tritonx-100, following the addition of 5% BSA to the blocking step. Samples were then incubated overnight with appropriate primary antibodies at 4 °C, followed by incubation for 1 h with secondary antibodies. Anti-ACRV1 (Proteintech,14040-1-AP) was used at 1:400, anti-LIN28A (cell signaling, 8641S) was used at 1:400, anti-SOX9 (Sigma-Aldrich, AB5535) at 1:500, and anti-γH2AX (Sigma-Aldrich, MABE61) was used at 1:300. Samples were mounted using DAPI-containing antifade medium (Beyotime, P0131) and imaged on an Olympus Fv3000 confocal microscope.

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay

Apoptosis was detected using the TUNEL assay kit (Vazyme, A113-01), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Paraffin sections were rehydrated and treated with proteinase K, then reacted with TUNEL labeling mix buffer at 37 °C. Images were obtained under an Olympus Fv3000 confocal microscope. Fifty tubules per male were analyzed.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assay

HEK293T cells were transfected with plasmids encoding Myc-tagged TDRD15 and Flag-tagged PIWIL1, PIWIL2, or PIWIL3, respectively, using standard transfection protocols. Forty-eight hours post-transfection, cells were harvested and lysed in IP lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. For each Co-IP reaction, 20 μL of pre-washed Anti-DYKDDDDK (Flag) Magnetic Beads (Selleck, Cat# B26101) were incubated with 200 μL of clarified cell lysate for 14 h at 4 °C temperature under gentle rotation. The beads were then washed three times with PBST buffer (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 8.1 mM Na2HPO4, 1.76 mM KH2PO4, 0.5% Tween-20, pH 7.4) to remove non-specifically bound proteins. Bound proteins were eluted by boiling the beads in 1 × SDS loading buffer for 5 min. Eluates were subjected to SDS-PAGE, followed by immunoblotting analysis using anti-Myc antibody (Proteintech, 16286-1-AP, rabbit, 1:1000) to detect Myc-TDRD15, and anti-Flag antibody (Thermo Fisher, PA1-984B, rabbit, 1:1000) to confirm the pulldown of Flag-tagged PIWI proteins.

Western blotting

Tissues were collected from 10-week-old WT and Tdrd15 KO testes and lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail on ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 13,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (E11201, Vazyme, China). A total of 30 μg of protein per sample was separated on 10% SDS–PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes for immunoblotting. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-TDRD6 (lab produced; 1:4000) and anti-GAPDH (60004-1-Ig, Proteintech; 1:50,000). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied, and signals were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence system.

Small-RNA library construction and sequencing

Small-RNA libraries were prepared according to established protocols [17]. In brief, purified RNA samples were first heated at 72 °C for 3 min to denature secondary structures, followed by ligation of the 3′ adapter. To eliminate excess adapter, a combination of lambda exonuclease and 5′ deadenylase was employed. Subsequent steps included 5′ adapter ligation and reverse transcription. The resulting cDNA was preamplified, and 5 μL of the product was used for the final amplification reaction. Amplified libraries were size-fractionated on a 6% polyacrylamide gel, and DNA fragments ranging from 130 to 160 bp were excised for purification. All qualified libraries were subjected to sequencing using the NovaSeq 6000.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism version 9.5 was utilized to conduct statistical analysis. The normality of the data was analyzed first, and then statistical tests were performed. All relevant statistical analyses were performed in biologic triplicate. Unpaired Student’s t tests were used to verify differences between Tdrd15+/+ and Tdrd15−/− golden hamsters. Data are the mean ± standard deviation (SD). P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate a significant difference.

To investigate its functional relevance in male reproduction, we systematically characterized TDRD15 expression patterns in Mesocricetus auratus. Interrogation of published transcriptomic data revealed predominant TDRD15 expression in round spermatids [18] (Fig. 1B). Leveraging the synchronized progression of the first spermatogenic wave in rodents, we analyzed TDRD15 expression dynamics across defined postnatal timepoints (days 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 42, and 56), which sequentially represent distinct spermatogenic stages: spermatogonia, early spermatocytes, round spermatids, elongating spermatids, and spermatozoa. Developmental profiling revealed initial Tdrd15 transcript detection at postnatal day 28 (P28; Fig. 1D), corresponding to the first wave of spermiogenesis in golden hamsters. This temporal expression pattern demonstrated remarkable consistency with both published RNA-seq data and established spermatogenic timelines.

The observed phylogenetic conservation and stage-restricted expression during germ cell maturation collectively suggest that TDRD15 participates in evolutionarily conserved mechanisms regulating spermatogenesis.

Generation of Tdrd15−/− golden hamsters

We next wanted to elucidate the role of TDRD15 in male reproduction, for which we used CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing and established Tdrd15-deficient golden hamster models (Fig. 2A). Targeted sequencing analyses identified induced mutations containing a 3-bp insertion concomitant with a 41-bp deletion in exon 1, which were verified by PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing. These genetic modifications caused frameshift alterations and premature translational termination (Fig. 2B, C). Comparative qPCR assays between wild-type and mutant specimens revealed a marked reduction in Tdrd15 transcript abundance within testicular tissues (Fig. 2D). Normal fertility of Tdrd15−/− golden hamsters

The Tdrd15−/− golden hamsters exhibited normal growth and behavioral phenotypes. Mating trials demonstrated intact fertility in both male and female knockout animals, with litter sizes comparable to those of wild-type controls (Fig. 3C). A comprehensive evaluation of reproductive parameters revealed no significant differences in testicular morphology or testis-to-body weight ratios between the genotypes (Fig. 3A, B). Computer-assisted sperm analysis (CASA) revealed normal sperm motility profiles and progressive movement patterns in Tdrd15−/− males (Fig. 3D, E). Histopathological examination using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed that the aberrant sperm in Tdrd15−/− golden hamsters did not show any significant difference compared with Tdrd15+/+ and Tdrd15−/− golden hamsters (Fig. 3F, G). A comprehensive evaluation of germ cell development using established biomarkers demonstrated comparable cellular compositions between the following groups: LIN28A + spermatogonial stem cells (SSCs) adjacent to basement membranes, SOX9 + Sertoli cells with a characteristic nuclear morphology, γH2AX + spermatocytes in meiotic prophase, and terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) + apoptotic cells. Functional redundancy of TDRD6

Consistent with the canonical role of Tudor domain-containing proteins, co-immunoprecipitation assays verified a specific physical interaction between TDRD15 and PIWI proteins (Fig. 6A), indicating that TDRD15 retains the conserved capacity to engage the core piRNA machinery. To determine whether this interaction influences piRNA biogenesis, we performed small RNA sequencing on total RNA from adult testes. Following spike-in normalization, the results showed no significant difference in total piRNA levels between Tdrd15−/− and wild-type testes (Fig. 6B, C). Moreover, the characteristic 5′ nucleotide bias of piRNAs—critical for their functional specificity—remained unaltered in the absence of TDRD15 (Fig. 6D). Given the lack of an observable phenotype upon Tdrd15 deletion, we hypothesized that functional compensation among homologous Tudor family proteins might account for this resilience. To explore this, we conducted a phylogenetic analysis of TDRD proteins, which revealed that TDRD15 and TDRD6—both highly expressed in testes—cluster together with high bootstrap support (Fig. 6E). Given their structural homology and testis-enriched expression, we assessed whether Tdrd15 knockout elicits compensatory changes in the expression of related genes. RT-qPCR analysis of 13 conserved Tdrd family members in testicular tissues from Tdrd15 ⁻/ ⁻ and wild-type hamsters identified a significant upregulation of Tdrd6 transcripts in knockout animals (Fig. 6F). This increase was further confirmed at the protein level via Western blotting (Fig. 6G). These results suggest that compensatory overexpression of TDRD6 may offset the loss of TDRD15, highlighting a potential mechanism of functional redundancy within the TDRD family in the testicular environment.

Discussion

The members of the TDRD protein family have been implicated in reproduction by regulating piRNA biogenesis and transposon silencing via nuage assembly, mechanisms critical for maintaining genomic stability and ensuring proper spermiogenesis and fertility [19–21]. Despite these established functions, the specific contribution of TDRD15 to male spermatogenesis and fertility has remained unexplored. Because mice do not encode Tdrd15, we selected golden hamsters (M. auratus) as the model organism to investigate its functional necessity. We used CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing to successfully generate TDRD15-knockout golden hamsters, with gene disruption meticulously verified by PCR genotyping and Sanger sequencing.

Tdrd15-deficient males exhibited preserved reproductive capacity under standard laboratory conditions, with no significant differences in the testis-to-body weight ratios, testicular/epididymal histology, litter size, or germ cell morphology compared to the wild-type counterparts. Notably, small RNA sequencing further demonstrated that TDRD15 knockout does not impair global piRNA abundance or alter the characteristic 5′ nucleotide bias, indicating its dispensability in piRNA biogenesis. Although our initial analyses revealed no overt spermatogenic defects in Sertoli or spermatogenic cells, subtle phenotypes, such as age-dependent fertility decline or meiotic irregularities, may remain undetected without advanced longitudinal or single-cell resolution studies. These findings collectively indicate that TDRD15 is dispensable for maintaining fertility homeostasis in golden hamsters under physiological conditions.

The absence of a pronounced phenotypic manifestation in TDRD15-null models may reflect evolutionary conserved functional redundancy within the reproductive pathways. Recent CRISPR/Cas9-based knockout studies in mice have revealed that a substantial proportion of testis-enriched genes, despite their tissue-specific expression patterns, are not individually essential for maintaining fertility [22–25]. Reproductive capacity exhibits functional robustness through genetic redundancy, wherein multiple genes with overlapping roles collectively maintain fertility homeostasis. Similar to Tdrd15, Tdrd8 exhibits predominant expression within male germ cells and has not been found essential for spermatogenesis in murine models [26, 27]. Alterations in testicular gene expression exhibit correlations with reproductive function; however, experimental evidence from murine models demonstrates that these genetic perturbations do not necessarily exert deterministic effects on ultimate fertility outcomes [22].

Our results align with the emerging evidence from studies on CRISPR/Cas9 knockout models demonstrating that numerous reproductive organ-enriched genes lack deterministic roles in deciding fertility outcomes. We established the non-essentiality of TDRD15 in hamster spermatogenesis to provide critical guidance for prioritizing genetic targets in male infertility research. Such empirical elimination of candidate genes prevents redundant investigative efforts across laboratories and enables focused exploration of genes with demonstrated causal roles in regulating fertility.

Our findings demonstrate that TDRD15 does not function as a causative factor in the pathogenesis contributing to male infertility. This conclusion holds significant implications for research prioritization, as it can prevent redundant investigations across laboratories, thereby optimizing resource allocation and research efficiency. Furthermore, these data provide valuable guidance to reproductive scientists to strategically direct their efforts toward investigating genes with definitive roles in fertility regulation, rather than pursuing non-essential genetic targets.
