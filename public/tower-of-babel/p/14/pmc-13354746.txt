Introduction

Patellar tendinopathy is a condition commonly seen in athletes involved in jumping or high‐impact sports and can significantly impair the ability to perform even basic activities of daily living [1]. Conservative treatments are the first line of intervention for patellar tendinopathies [2, 3], which include medical and physical therapies such as activity modification, exercise rehabilitation, and therapeutic modalities. However, chronic or non‐responsive cases may require surgical options, such as patellar tendon debridement, longitudinal or needle tenotomies, partial or complete patellectomy, and patellar tendon repairs. Known complications of these surgeries include prolonged inflammation, delayed tendon healing, and deficits in quadriceps strength [4, 5]. These challenges are magnified in patients with comorbidities such as diabetes, which can further impair healing [6].

Comorbid conditions, such as diabetes mellitus, have been linked with impaired tissue healing due to microvascular complications, altered inflammatory response, and reduced collagen synthesis, which can delay overall musculoskeletal recovery [6]. In addition to these physiological factors, psychological well‐being has emerged as a crucial determinant of rehabilitation outcomes. Depression, fear‐avoidance beliefs, and low self‐efficacy have been associated with poorer functional recovery following orthopedic surgeries [7]. Conversely, supportive social environments and active patient engagement have been shown to facilitate recovery by enhancing adherence to therapy and improving outcomes [8, 9, 10, 11]. Further, evidence from a systematic review emphasizes that patients with strong social support and proactive communication with their care teams are at least twice as likely to achieve optimal rehabilitation goals [7]. Overall, these findings are particularly relevant in complex surgical cases where long‐term motivation and consistent participation in therapeutic interventions are required for successful functional restoration.

Surgical intervention for chronic patellar tendinopathy, including debridement or tendon reconstruction, carries other potential complications such as residual anterior knee pain, infection, graft failure, or prolonged recovery timelines. Some patients have reported experiencing patellar baja, stiffness, hypertrophic scarring, or deficits in strength and function that persist beyond standard recovery periods [7, 12]. Similar complications have also been documented in surgeries addressing related tendon pathologies, such as Achilles or Quadriceps tendon repairs, where improper rehabilitation or delayed neuromuscular activation could lead to chronic dysfunction [13]. These considerations suggest the need for evidence‐based, progressive rehabilitation strategies that address both tissue healing and motor retraining to optimize surgical outcomes. Previous literature has identified specific postoperative complications following patellar tendon surgery that can affect both short‐term and long‐term recovery. A study reported that athletes who underwent open patellar tendon surgery experienced a high incidence of prolonged pain, with approximately 30% unable to return to their previous level of sport [14]. In a follow‐up study [15], the use of minimally invasive arthroscopic techniques showed promise but still demonstrated variability in functional outcomes and patient satisfaction. These studies suggest that while surgery can alleviate symptoms, recovery trajectories are not always linear, and complications may require extended rehabilitation and multimodal adjunctive strategies. Surgical intervention is generally reserved for patients who fail nonoperative management, particularly those with tendon tears involving > 50% of tendon thickness or marked tendon enlargement (> 8.8 mm). Both open and arthroscopic debridement are considered viable options, though current evidence does not demonstrate superior outcomes with either approach, and high‐quality comparative studies remain lacking [15].

One relatively novel adjunct in musculoskeletal rehabilitation is ultrasound biofeedback, which provides real‐time visual feedback that enhances motor control and neuromuscular re‐education [16]. This procedure allows both patients and clinicians to observe muscular engagement during movement performance and facilitates volitional muscle activation during rehabilitation. Ultrasound biofeedback is postulated to be particularly useful in post‐surgical rehabilitation where precise quadriceps reactivation is essential and subtle physiological improvements may be difficult for patients to detect without objective feedback [17, 18, 19]. Among the quadriceps muscles, the vastus intermedius demonstrates the greatest change in muscle quality and the strongest relationship to volitional performance, making it a key target for ultrasound‐based assessment and retraining [18]. Given its potential benefits, particularly in cases involving complex medical and psychological factors, ultrasound biofeedback might offer a valuable strategy for optimizing functional outcomes in post‐operative rehabilitation. This is especially relevant in patients recovering from tendon surgeries where traditional cues for muscle engagement may be insufficient or unreliable.

This case report aims to illustrate the role of ultrasound biofeedback as a complementary rehabilitation tool in the post‐operative management of a medically complex patient following surgical intervention for chronic patellar tendinopathy. The purpose of the study was to evaluate how integrating real‐time visual feedback can facilitate targeted neuromuscular reactivation and support functional recovery in the presence of systemic challenges such as diabetes and psychosocial stressors. We hypothesized that the incorporation of ultrasound biofeedback into a structured, individualized rehabilitation program could enhance patient engagement, improve quadriceps activation, and expedite the restoration of knee function.

Case History

The patient was a 24‐year‐old active male, pursuing a medical doctoral degree and involved in recreational volleyball and basketball, with a history of well‐managed Type 1 Diabetes. The patient presented to the outpatient PT clinic with right knee pain and instability following surgical intervention performed for patellar tendinopathy. He demonstrated high motivation to return to athletic activity and reported that he would comply well with any prescribed home exercises. The patient received good psychosocial support from family and peers, particularly during periods when he was unable to drive post‐surgery. He reported having a strong social support system, including assistance with transportation and with balancing recovery alongside his medical school responsibilities. His affect remained positive and engaged throughout care. He often asked about the recovery timeline and exercised caution, asking before engaging in activities beyond protocol.

Interestingly, as a medical student himself, the patient connected well with the clinical team. He expressed appreciation for the ultrasound biofeedback component of his care, noting it helped him visualize his progress and stay motivated. This patient's curiosity and mutual understanding with the student therapists further contributed to his high engagement and adherence.

Clinical Findings

At the initial post‐operative physical therapy (PT) evaluation, the patient reported pain levels of 8/10 at worst and 4/10 during examination with a notable extension lag of 8° and flexion to 80° with pain at end ranges. He used crutches for partial weight‐bearing. Quadriceps contraction strength was poor (Grade 3 on manual muscle testing) due to post‐surgical pain and swelling. Localized warmth and swelling were observed but without signs of infection. The patient's response to pain appeared somewhat underreported; he often responded casually to pain‐related inquiries and downplayed discomfort during sessions. This initially masked the full extent of his pain‐related limitations and delayed the calibration of intervention intensity. As rehabilitation progressed, it became clear that more specific questioning was necessary to accurately assess his pain levels. He demonstrated high levels of effort and participation in sessions and often attempted to exceed prescribed activity levels, which required the clinical team to reinforce his adherence to post‐operative guidelines. Despite this, his engagement and willingness to perform therapeutic tasks were viewed as indicators of strong rehabilitation potential.

Methods

Investigations

Surgical Treatment

The surgical procedure consisted of open patellar tendon debridement combined with concurrent management of the tibial stress reaction. During the debridement, particular attention was paid to the patellar tendon–Hoffa fat pad interface, since in chronic patellar tendinopathy this anatomical region is characterized by neovascularization and neoinnervation (so‐called neo‐vessels and neo‐nerves) that migrate from the superficial layers of Hoffa's body into the deep paratenon and are considered key pain generators. Accordingly, the release of these neo‐vessels and neo‐nerves located at the tendon–fat pad interface, together with the lysis of fibrotic adhesions at the tendon–fat gliding plane, was performed to restore the physiological gliding of the patellar tendon over the underlying Hoffa body. A retro‐patellar bursectomy was also completed, and adjunctive techniques were used to stabilize the lateral tibial plateau addressing the stress reaction. Pre‐operative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) indicated chronic patellar tendinopathy with a lateral tibial plateau stress reaction. The surgical procedure included patellar tendon debridement and repair of the tibial stress fracture. This case involved multiple concurrent pathologies requiring a multifaceted surgical approach, which included retro‐patellar bursectomy and the use of adjunctive techniques to stabilize the lateral tibial plateau. The dual nature of the procedure presented increased challenges during the early rehabilitation phase, particularly in achieving quadriceps reactivation and range of motion.

The presence of Type 1 Diabetes added further complexity by increasing the risk for delayed healing, necessitating close clinical monitoring. The interdisciplinary care team ensured that the rehabilitation plan was responsive to potential complications and carefully sequenced to match healing progress.

The post‐surgical rehabilitation prescribed was based on the Robert Duerr Post‐Operative Rehab Protocol.

Phase 1 (Weeks 0–2) focused on early protection, with the knee brace locked in extension during weightbearing and removed only for range of motion (ROM) exercises and rehabilitation. Exercises during this phase included straight leg raises with the brace on, quadriceps sets, ankle pumps, and patellar mobilizations.

Ultrasound Biofeedback

As part of the post‐operative rehabilitation protocol, Rehabilitative Ultrasound Imaging (RUSI) was integrated into motor control exercises (MCE) to serve as a visual biofeedback tool. RUSI was used by the physical therapist to assess and monitor changes in muscle morphology, such as thickness, cross‐sectional area, and volume, during targeted exercises aimed at reactivating deep stabilizing muscles and improving neuromuscular coordination. Motor control exercises were selected to promote precise activation patterns, and RUSI provided real‐time, noninvasive, low‐cost imaging that enabled both the clinician and patient to visually confirm muscle engagement. The transducer was placed perpendicular to the long axis of the thigh, approximately two‐thirds of the distance between the anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) and the superior border of the patella, as described by a study [21]. Imaging was obtained in sagittal standardized view(s) to ensure consistent visualization of the targeted musculature. This form of augmented proprioceptive and psychological feedback enhanced the patient's awareness and volitional control of muscle activity. According to another study, RUSI used during MCE has been shown to be more effective in increasing muscle thickness, activation, and exercise accuracy compared to verbal or tactile feedback alone [19]. In the present case, ultrasound biofeedback was specifically directed at the quadriceps muscle belly (rectus femoris and vastus intermedius) rather than at the patellar tendon region, since the primary rehabilitation objective was volitional reactivation and hypertrophy of the quadriceps. For a comprehensive sono‐anatomical description of the anterior knee region relevant to ultrasound‐guided rehabilitation of the patellar tendon [22]. The use of ultrasound biofeedback in this case was especially beneficial in promoting early and accurate quadriceps activation, a critical component of functional recovery following patellar tendon surgery.

Results

Following 10 weeks of structured rehabilitation incorporating ultrasound biofeedback, the patient demonstrated clinically meaningful improvements across all measured domains. Pain intensity decreased from 8/10 to 3/10 on the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), representing a 62.5% reduction and falling below the threshold typically associated with activity‐limiting pain. Active knee range of motion improved progressively, with resolution of the initial 8° extension lag and restoration of near‐full flexion, while quadriceps manual muscle testing improved from Grade 3 to functional strength consistent with return‐to‐sport readiness. Functional capacity, measured using the Lower Extremity Functional Scale (LEFS), improved from 21/80 at initial evaluation to 65/80 at final evaluation, an absolute gain of 44 points, which substantially exceeds the established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 9 points for this measure. Serial rehabilitative ultrasound imaging documented concurrent structural gains, with rectus femoris thickness increasing from 2.3 to 2.65 cm (+15.2%) and vastus intermedius thickness increasing from 1.95 to 2.2 cm (+12.8%), consistent with targeted muscle hypertrophy in the specific quadriceps components prioritized during biofeedback‐guided motor control exercises (Table 1; Figures 2 and 3). The patient progressed through all four rehabilitation phases without adverse events or complications related to his Type 1 diabetes and reported full return to recreational volleyball and basketball at the conclusion of the rehabilitation program.

Discussion

This case underscores how ultrasound biofeedback may enhance outcomes after complex orthopedic procedures. The patient faced delayed healing risks due to dual surgical interventions and diabetes. Utilization of ultrasound biofeedback offered visual, real‐time feedback that improved quadriceps engagement and functional progression.

In alignment with findings by two studies, this report supports ultrasound's superiority over tactile or verbal cues for targeted activation [19, 23]. Compared to EMG biofeedback, ultrasound offers structural and functional feedback, aiding both assessment and retraining. Another study further validates the visual input's role in neuroplasticity and movement pattern re‐learning [24, 25].

However, the efficacy of ultrasound biofeedback is debated. A study found no statistically significant difference between groups receiving ultrasound feedback versus verbal cues in lumbopelvic training [16]. Similarly, a study reported that RUSI was effective in demonstrating muscle activity but did not lead to significant functional gains [26]. These findings suggest that effectiveness may depend on different factors including the clinical context, target muscles, and patient characteristics. For example, patients with impaired proprioception or difficulty interpreting internal cues may benefit more from visual feedback than those with intact neuromuscular control [27]. The timing, frequency, and integration of ultrasound biofeedback within a broader rehabilitation program might influence its impact on outcomes. Therefore, while ultrasound biofeedback holds promise as a tool for enhancing motor re‐education, its use should be tailored to the individual patient's needs, goals, and rehabilitation stage to maximize its potential benefits.

While this case report could provide some valuable insight into the role of ultrasound biofeedback in post‐surgical muscle retraining, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The study design is limited by its single‐subject nature, reducing generalizability. Additionally, data collection was inconsistent due to missed visits and time constraints, which may have introduced variability in the outcomes. The absence of a control group also limits the ability to compare ultrasound biofeedback directly with other established rehabilitation interventions.

Despite these limitations, the clinical impact of this study is promising. Ultrasound biofeedback offers a non‐invasive, real‐time visualization method to enhance neuromuscular activation and promote improved muscle function. The ability to monitor progress objectively through imaging may encourage better patient adherence to rehabilitation protocols. However, the absence of long‐term follow‐up limits our understanding of whether the observed improvements were sustained over time. Future studies should include extended follow‐up periods to determine the durability of outcomes and to assess whether ultrasound biofeedback contributes to long‐term functional gains and reduced recurrence of symptoms. In addition, future directions could explore integrating ultrasound biofeedback into larger controlled trials and across different patient populations to establish broader clinical applicability. Further research could also investigate optimal training protocols, including dosage, frequency, and progression, to maximize the clinical effectiveness of ultrasound biofeedback. Future prospective studies should additionally incorporate standardized video documentation of ultrasound‐guided rehabilitation sessions as supplementary material, providing a dynamic educational resource that would optimize the clinical reproducibility of these interventions for readers and clinicians.

Conclusion

Ten weeks of ultrasound biofeedback resulted in positive outcomes, including reduced pain, increased muscle thickness, and improved functional ability in a complex post‐surgical patellar tendinopathy case. Future studies with larger samples and control groups are recommended to validate our findings and to determine the generalizability and potential of ultrasound biofeedback as a standard adjunct in musculoskeletal rehabilitation.
