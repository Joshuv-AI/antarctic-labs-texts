A synthetic community promotes macadamia growth via root‐microbe interactions and systemic plant responses
Authors: Xiaofang Yao, Qiumei Liu, Wenlin Wang, Qiujin Tan, Chunheng Zhou, Dejun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe employed an 18‐month pot experiment and an integrated multi‐omics approach to elucidate the potential mechanisms underlying synthetic community (SynCom)‐mediated macadamia growth promotion. SynCom inoculation enhanced soil nitrogen cycling and potential nutrient (e.g., iron) acquisition. It simultaneously increased soil extracellular enzyme activities and enriched beneficial bacteria such as Rhodanobacter, Ferrovibrio, and Rhodoblastus in the root compartment. In addition, SynCom inoculation promoted the accumulation of rhizosphere metabolites, including the microbial siderophore pyochelin, and drove the co‐enrichment of transcriptomic and metabolomic pathways, notably the arginine biosynthesis pathway. This coordinated response may have stimulated nitrogen and iron cycling in the soil, thereby promoting the healthy growth of macadamia.

To the Editor,

The plant microbiome plays an important role in plant nutrition, stress tolerance, and pathogen prevention, and is regarded as the second genome of plants [1, 2]. To harness these beneficial functions for sustainable agriculture, the application of synthetic communities (SynComs) has emerged as a promising strategy. SynComs can effectively model or augment the synergistic functions of the natural microbiome, thereby overcoming the ecological inconsistencies and functional limitations typically associated with single‐strain inoculants [3, 4]. However, the complex biological mechanisms underlying prolonged SynCom–host interactions remain incompletely understood.

Current research on SynComs is predominantly confined to short‐lived annual crops, such as rice [5] and cucumber [1]. Consequently, their long‐term stability, potential field applications, and underlying mechanisms in perennial woody crops remain largely unexplored. Emerging evidence from a multi‐omics perspective suggests that introduced beneficial microbes can trigger profound metabolic and transcriptional responses in the host [6]. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether these induced interactions can be sustained to reshape the root‐associated environment in perennial systems. We hypothesized that SynCom inoculation could induce a coordinated regulation of both the host plant metabolism and the resident rhizosphere microbiome over an extended period.

To address this knowledge gap, we conducted an 18‐month pot experiment using macadamia (Macadamia integrifolia) under SynCom inoculation (Bacillus cereus, Bacillus subtilis, Pseudomonas fluorescens, Enterobacter cloacae, Trichoderma harzianum, Trichoderma viride, Penicillium simplicissimum, and Penicillium oxalicum) and control treatments. Most of these strains exhibit antagonistic activity against the macadamia pathogens (Pestalotiopsis microspora and Cladosporium sp.; unpublished data) and have been reported as widespread beneficial microbes [7]. Utilizing integrated microbiome, metabolome, and transcriptome analyses, we investigated the long‐term effects of SynCom inoculation on plant‐associated microbial community assembly, soil nutrient profiles, and host metabolic shifts. Our study aims to provide comprehensive, multi‐omics evidence that the SynCom application represents a powerful and sustainable biological strategy to enhance nutrient cycling and promote the fitness of perennial crops.

EFFECTS OF SYNTHETIC COMMUNITY ON MACADAMIA GROWTH, NUTRIENT CYCLING, AND MICROBIAL COMPOSITION

In the 18‐month pot experiment, SynCom inoculation significantly enhanced macadamia growth parameters, with shoot height, stem diameter, leaf number, shoot dry weight, and root length increasing by 33.4%, 4.0%, 12.6%, 40.3%, and 22.1% (Figure 1A−C). In contrast, no significant differences in most of these parameters were observed between treatments during the first 15 months after inoculation. Additionally, SynCom inoculation reshaped soil nutrient profiles. Soil available nutrient contents were markedly enhanced, with nitrate nitrogen (NO3 −‐N) and dissolved organic nitrogen (DON) increasing by 197.3% and 61.7%, respectively, and microbial biomass nitrogen (MBN) rising by 23.2% (Figure 1D). Correspondingly, the activities of core nutrient‐cycling enzymes, including β‐glucosidase (BG), N‐acetyl‐β‐d‐glucosaminidase (NAG), leucine aminopeptidase (LAP), and acid phosphatase (ACP), increased dramatically, with increases in activity ranging from 63.3% to more than 1200.0% (Figure 1D). These profound enhancements in exoenzyme activities and nitrogen pools suggest that SynCom inoculation effectively stimulates the cycling of nitrogen [8].

To determine the influence of the SynCom on the plant microbiome assembly, we first explored the effects of spatial compartments on the soil microbiome. Samples were harvested from the bulk soil, rhizosphere, and root compartment following the method of a previous study [9]. SynCom inoculation enriched specific unique taxa without altering overall alpha diversity (Figure S1A−C). This indicates that SynCom inoculation may represent a targeted functional optimization rather than a disruptive ecological shift, a finding consistent with observations from another study [10]. SynCom inoculation reshaped the soil‐root microbiome (Figure S2). In the bulk soil, it suppressed fungi (e.g., Mortierella, Cladosporium) and promoted bacteria (Anaeromyxobacter, Thiobacillus, and Rhodobacter) involved in iron, sulfur, and nitrogen cycling (Figure S2A,B). In the rhizosphere, fungal suppression persisted, while bacterial enrichment was more selective (Rhodanobacter, Halorubrum) (Figure S2C,D). The root compartment showed the most pronounced restructuring, with an enrichment of iron‐oxidizing (Ferrovibrio), nitrifying/denitrifying (Rhodoblastus), and potential growth‐promoting taxa, and the suppression of anaerobic fermenters (Prevotellaceae, Escherichia, and Shigella) (Figure 1E). Rhodanobacter exhibited consistent enrichment throughout the soil‐root continuum. Species within this genus, such as Rhodanobacter thiooxydans FW510‐R12, are renowned for their denitrification capabilities and stress resilience [11], suggesting their potential contribution as keystone taxa in plant growth and nitrogen turnover.

EFFECTS OF SYNTHETIC COMMUNITY ON MACADAMIA METABOLOME

To elucidate the mechanisms of SynCom‐induced effects on the macadamia rhizosphere microbial community, we collected macadamia root exudates (which capture a complex of plant‐derived and microbially‐secreted metabolites) following an in situ static culture method [12], and analyzed them using ultra‐performance liquid chromatography‐quadrupole time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry (UPLC‐QTOF/MS). A total of 3420 compounds were detected across all samples, resulting in the identification of 647 differential metabolites, with 419 up‐regulated and 228 down‐regulated in the SynCom group (Figure S3). OPLS‐DA revealed distinct metabolite profiles among treatments (Figure 1F). SynCom inoculation significantly upregulated metabolites associated with microbial iron competition, plant secondary metabolism, and amino acid metabolism, including pyochelin, isopentenyl phosphate, l‐Homocysteine, and mearnsitrin. The upregulation of pyochelin (a potent microbial siderophore) provides a mechanistic explanation for the enriched iron‐cycling taxa (e.g., Ferrovibrio) and the concomitant suppression of pathogenic fungi via competitive iron exclusion [13, 14]. Conversely, metabolites associated with pathogen activity (cytochalasin B) and lipid peroxidation/oxidative stress (9,10‐DHOME, 12,13 −DHOME) were downregulated (Figure 1G). Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) analysis further confirmed the metabolic shift towards amino acids, cofactors/vitamins, and terpenoids/polyketides (Figure 1H).

Correlation analysis revealed that the core enriched genera Rhodanobacter, Ferrovibrio, and Rhodoblastus were positively correlated with the upregulated metabolites (N6‐(L‐1,3‐Dicarboxypropyl)‐l‐lysine, pyochelin, isopentenyl phosphate, l‐Homocysteine, and mearnsitrin), but negatively correlated with the downregulated metabolites (12,13‐DHOME, cytochalasin B, and 10‐deoxymethynolide) (Figure S4). These upregulated metabolites were primarily involved in lysine biosynthesis and degradation, amino acid biosynthesis, sulfur metabolism, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis, whereas 12,13‐DHOME was mainly associated with linoleic acid metabolism. Crucially, both the enriched bacterial taxa (e.g., Rhodanobacter, Rhodoblastus) and their associated upregulated metabolites (e.g., pyochelin, N6‐(L‐1,3‐Dicarboxypropyl)‐l‐lysine) exhibited strong positive correlations with soil nitrogen‐cycling parameters (MBN, DON, NO3 −‐N, NH4 +‐N, and related enzymes) and plant growth parameters (root length, shoot biomass). In contrast, downregulated metabolites (e.g., 12,13‐DHOME, cytochalasin B) were significantly negatively associated with soil nitrogen availability and plant growth (Figure S5A−D). These findings suggest that shifts in the microbial community are closely associated with changes in both soil nutrient turnover and nitrogen distribution within the plant [15, 16].

MULTI‐OMICS REVEALS SYNCOM‐MEDIATED REGULATION OF HOST METABOLISM AND TRANSCRIPTION

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying SynCom‐induced changes in microbial community structure and rhizosphere metabolite profiles, we next performed integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses. Two‐way orthogonal partial least squares (O2PLS) analysis showed a strong correlation between the root exudate metabolome and the transcriptome, suggesting that specific genes and metabolites are tightly linked (Figure 2A). The nine‐quadrant diagram further illustrates that the majority of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differential metabolites are co‐localized in the co‐upregulation (top‐right) and co‐downregulation (bottom‐left) quadrants (Figure 2B), demonstrating a strong positive correlation between DEGs and metabolic fluxes. KEGG analysis identified 43 pathways significantly co‐enriched by both DEGs and differential metabolites (Figure 2C), which were primarily associated with amino acid metabolism, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, and lipid metabolism, among which, the arginine biosynthesis pathway was notably co‐enriched (Figure 2D). The enrichment of arginine may accelerate the absorption and transport of NH4 +‐N, thereby improving plant stress tolerance [17]. Furthermore, the co‐enrichment of the siderophore pyochelin and the iron‐cycling bacterium Ferrovibrio indicates a potential role for SynCom inoculation in facilitating plant iron acquisition [14, 18].

Finally, co‐inertia analysis visually confirmed this synergy. The ordination triplot revealed that key SynCom‐enriched genera (Rhodanobacter, Rhodoblastus, and Ferrovibrio) were spatially co‐located with dense clusters of DEGs and upregulated metabolites along Axis 1, mathematically signifying a positive coupling (Figure 2E). The subsequent correlation network highlighted Rhodanobacter as a central hub, densely connected to pyochelin and l‐Homocysteine. Furthermore, metabolites such as N(alpha)‐acetyl‐L‐2,4‐diaminobutyrate served as bridging nodes connecting different bacterial genera (Figure 2F). These shared metabolic hubs suggest strong metabolic interactions among root‐associated microorganisms, potentially stabilizing the microbial community and facilitating long‐term plant growth promotion.

In summary, unlike most previous SynCom studies conducted in annual crops over relatively short time scales, our findings suggest that SynCom‐induced reprogramming of host metabolism and microbiome assembly may persist in a perennial woody crop system, highlighting the potential importance of long‐term host–microbe feedbacks in SynCom performance. The SynCom may function as an ecological catalyst that facilitates coordinated interactions between the host plant and the rhizosphere microbiome. Through specific transcriptional regulation, the host may reshape its root exudate profile, which in turn could contribute to a rhizosphere environment that promotes the accumulation of microbial functional metabolites, including the siderophore pyochelin. The resulting rhizosphere metabolic environment may selectively enrich and stabilize key functional taxa, such as Rhodanobacter, Ferrovibrio, and Rhodoblastus, facilitate microbial metabolic interactions, and potentially contribute to the cycling and transformation of essential nutrients, particularly nitrogen. Ultimately, the coordinated interplay among host transcriptional regulation, root exudate remodeling, and microbial community assembly may be associated with improved nutrient acquisition and plant growth performance. Given the inherent limitations of studying a perennial woody crop system, our conclusions are based on a systems biology‐derived ecological metabolic model inferred from multi‐omics associations. Advanced sequencing technologies, in particular, served as the foundational tool enabling these multi‐omics insights [19]. Future studies employing reverse‐genetics approaches and targeted functional experiments will be required to validate the causal relationships proposed here. Collectively, our findings provide a theoretical basis for understanding the potential mechanisms by which SynCom inoculation may influence nutrient cycling and support the growth and fitness of perennial crops.
