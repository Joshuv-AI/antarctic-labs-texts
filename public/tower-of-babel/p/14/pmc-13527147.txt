Endovascular thrombectomy with or without intravenous thrombolysis in large-vessel ischemic stroke: an updated meta-analysis
Authors: Thorsten Bischof, Matthias Jackwerth, Markus Zeitlinger, Bernd Jilma, Georg Gelbenegger, Anselm Jorda, Felix Bergmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGuidelines continue to recommend intravenous thrombolysis before endovascular thrombectomy for acute ischemic stroke due to large-vessel occlusion. Its added benefit remains debated. This meta-analysis investigated whether bridging therapy with systemic thrombolytics improves functional independence compared with thrombectomy treatment alone. A systematic search of randomized controlled trials comparing intravenous thrombolysis plus endovascular treatment versus endovascular thrombectomy alone was conducted. We searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and medRxiv to June 3, 2025. Search terms included “stroke”, “thrombectomy”, and “thrombolysis” and synonyms. The primary efficacy outcome was functional independence at 90 days, defined as a modified Rankin scale (mRS) score of 0 to 2. Random-effects models with inverse-variance weighting were used to calculate pooled risk ratios. Heterogeneity was quantified by Chi-square and I2 statistics. The study was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42022361110). Seven trials (BRIDGE-TNK, DEVT, DIRECT-MT, DIRECT-SAFE, MR CLEAN-NO IV, SKIP, and SWIFT DIRECT), comprising a total of 2,884 patients (1,436 received endovascular thrombectomy alone; 1,448 received intravenous thrombolysis plus thrombectomy), were included. The risk of bias of the included studies was moderate. Functional independence (mRS score of 0 to 2) occurred in 48.1% of patients (n = 690) treated with endovascular thrombectomy alone compared with 51.2% of patients (n = 742) in the intravenous thrombolysis plus endovascular thrombectomy group (risk ratio 0.94, 95% CI, 0.87–1.01). Secondary efficacy and safety outcomes did not differ significantly. Introduction

Large-vessel occlusions account for almost half of all acute ischemic strokes1. Intravenous thrombolysis, historically dominated by alteplase, has long been considered the cornerstone of reperfusion therapy2. The establishment of endovascular thrombectomy transformed stroke management in patients with large-vessel occlusion3. However, this did not replace systemic thrombolysis. Current guidelines from the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association and the European Stroke Organisation still recommend intravenous thrombolysis for eligible patients who present within 4.5 h of symptom onset, even when an endovascular thrombectomy is planned and intracranial hemorrhage has been excluded on brain imaging4,5. In recent years, tenecteplase has emerged as an alternative thrombolytic agent6. However, the adoption of tenecteplase and its guideline positioning and dosing regimens differ4–6. Whether this bridging strategy yields superior outcomes compared to endovascular thrombectomy alone remains debated7.

Systemic fibrinolysis lyses distal thrombi and promotes early reperfusion but carries the risk of thrombus fragmentation, which increases the likelihood of intracranial and extracranial hemorrhage8. Moreover, the administration of intravenous thrombolysis can prolong the interval from hospital arrival to arterial puncture – delays that are associated with lower rates of functional independence and worse neurological outcomes9,10. Conversely, omitting systemic fibrinolysis may achieve lower rates of successful reperfusion. Taken together, these considerations call into question the risk-benefit balance of adding intravenous thrombolysis to endovascular thrombectomy11.

Two recent meta-analyses at patient-level12 and trial-level13 included six randomized controlled trials (DEVT14, DIRECT-MT15, DIRECT-SAFE16, MR CLEAN-NO IV17, SKIP18, and SWIFT DIRECT19 comparing intravenous thrombolysis plus endovascular thrombectomy to endovascular thrombectomy alone in patients with acute ischemic stroke due to large-vessel occlusions. Both showed a non-significant trend favoring intravenous thrombolysis prior to endovascular thrombectomy for functional independence. Of the included trials two demonstrated the non-inferiority of endovascular thrombectomy alone, while four did not meet their predefined non-inferiority margins14–19. However, the recently published BRIDGE-TNK trial demonstrated a statistically significant benefit of the bridging strategy, warranting an updated meta-analysis to reassess the evidence in light of the new data20.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This systematic review and trial-level meta-analysis aimed to compare the level of functional independence achieved by patients with acute ischemic stroke patients and large-vessel occlusions after 90 days, when treated with endovascular thrombectomy alone versus intravenous thrombolysis plus endovascular thrombectomy.

Randomized controlled trials published in English between January 1, 2000, and June 3, 2025, were identified by searching PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, the Cochrane Library, and the medRxiv preprint server. Search terms included “stroke”, “thrombectomy”, and “thrombolysis”, along with corresponding Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and synonyms. The detailed search strategies for each database are listed in the Appendix Table 1.

Eligible trials met the following criteria: (i) randomized-controlled design, (ii) direct comparison of intravenous thrombolysis plus endovascular thrombectomy versus thrombectomy alone, and (iii) reporting of functional outcomes or provision of sufficient data to calculate them. Exclusion criteria included non-randomized studies, observational cohorts, reviews, trial protocols, and retrospective analyses. This study was registered in PROSPERO (registration ID: CRD42022361110).

Data analysis

Two reviewers (T.B. and F.B.) independently screened titles, abstracts, and, if necessary, full texts to determine eligibility. This meta-analysis based its results on intention-to-treat cohorts, as reported in each trial. There were no post-hoc exclusions of trial subgroups, nor any reclassification of outcomes or definitions. The same reviewers (T.B. and F.B.) performed data extraction from published articles and supplementary material, using standardized data collection sheets. Unpublished and patient-level data were not available. References from the included trials and prior systematic reviews were reviewed to capture additional studies. In case of disagreements or discrepancies between reviewers, discussions with a third reviewer (G.G.) resolved it.

The modified Rankin scale (mRS) is an ordinal measure of disability after stroke ranging from 0 to 6, with following categories: 0: no symptoms; 1: no clinically relevant disability; 2: slight disability; 3: moderate disability; 4: moderate-to-severe disability; 5: severe disability (complete dependence on daily care); and 6: death. The primary outcome was functional independence at 90 days, defined as a mRS score of 0 to 2. Secondary outcomes included excellent neurological outcome (mRS score of 0 to 1), health-related quality of life, measured by the EuroQoL Group 5-Dimension 5-Level Self-Report questionnaire (EQ-5D-5 L), which evaluates mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression, successful reperfusion on final angiography, defined as the modified or expanded thrombolysis in cerebral infarction (mTICI/eTICI) score of 2b to 3, corresponding to ≥ 50% reperfusion of the target vessel, and all-cause mortality at 90 days. Safety outcomes encompassed both symptomatic and asymptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage and procedural complications (i.e., puncture-site bleeding, vessel dissection, contrast extravasation, new-territory infarction, and embolization). Included trials used different definitions for symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage: Heidelberg bleeding classification14,15,17,19–21, the Safe Implementation of Thrombolysis in Stroke-Monitoring Study (SITS-MOST) criteria14,16,18,22, and the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke (NINDS) criteria14,18,23.

Predefined subgroup analyses for the primary efficacy endpoint (mRS 0 to 2 at 90 days) stratified patients by sex, age (< 60 versus ≥ 60, <65 versus ≥ 65, or < 70 versus ≥ 70 years), geographic region (Asian versus non-Asian), baseline National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score (< 16 versus ≥ 16 or < 18 versus ≥ 18), Alberta Stroke Program Early CT score (ASPECTS) (< 8 versus ≥ 8), presence of tandem occlusion, occluded artery (internal carotid artery (ICA) versus middle cerebral artery (MCA), and time from stroke onset to randomisation (< 120 versus ≥ 120, <125 versus ≥ 125, or < 169 versus ≥ 169 min). Specific cut-off values for individual trials are presented in Appendix Table 3. The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) criteria rated the certainty of evidence for the primary outcome24. The Cochrane Risk of Bias tool evaluated each study25.

Statistics

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate. For trials that reported outcome distributions as percentages, category counts were calculated by multiplying the percentage by the intention-to-treat denominator and round while preserving each trial’s total sample size. Alle calculations for effect estimates were defined by taking the endovascular thrombectomy alone group as the numerator and the intravenous thrombolysis plus thrombectomy group as the denominator. A random-effects model with inverse-variance weighting calculated pooled risk ratios (RRs) reporting 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and two-sided p-values. Absolute event counts for binary outcomes (e.g., mRS 0 to 2, all-cause mortality) were combined via inverse-variance, random effects pooling. An unadjusted ordinal logistic regression within each trial provided a common OR for the distribution of mRS scores for 0 to 6. These ORs, along with EQ-5D-5 L comparisons and subgroup analyses at 90 days, were pooled using inverse-variance weighting under a random-effects model, reporting 95% CIs.

Heterogeneity among included trials was quantified by Chi-square statistics and the Higgins I2 statistics. I2 values were interpreted as minimal (0% to 29%), moderate (30% to 49%), high (50% to 74%), or very high (75% to 100%)26. Subgroup interactions were tested by stratified heterogeneity. Sensitivity analyses included sequential exclusion of each trial, re-assessing the impact of employing a fixed-effects model instead of a random-effects model, and repetition of the ordinal mRS scale score analysis using covariate-adjusted values to confirm robustness. Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan Version 5.4.1, The Cochrane Collaboration) and R-studio (Version 2024.04.2 + 764, Vienna, Austria).

Results

The screening of randomized controlled trials (Appendix Fig. 1) identified seven eligible studies (BRIDGE-TNK20, DEVT14, DIRECT-MT15, DIRECT-SAFE16, MR CLEAN-NO IV17, SKIP18, and SWIFT DIRECT19 including 2,884 patients. 1,436 patients were randomized to endovascular treatment alone and 1,448 patients were randomly assigned to intravenous thrombolysis plus endovascular thrombectomy. Appendix Table 2 provides a summary of each trial’s primary, secondary, and safety outcomes. All enrolled patients had acute ischemic stroke caused by an intracranial proximal occlusion and presented directly to centers capable of performing endovascular thrombectomy. Large-vessel occlusions predominantly comprised anterior circulation occlusions (ICA, MCA [M1/M2]). Posterior circulation occlusions (basilar/vertebrobasilar and posterior cerebral artery (PCA) [P1]) were permitted in DIRECT-SAFE and BRIDGE-TNK, accounting for 68 out of 2,884 patients (2.4%). Patients were eligible for treatment within 4.5 h of symptom onset. Four trials (BRIDGE-TNK, DEVT, DIRECT-MT, SKIP) enrolled patients in Asia, two trials (MR CLEAN-NO IV, SWIFT) in non-Asian regions (Australia, Canada, Europe, New Zealand), and the DIRECT-SAFE trial recruited patients at both Asian (China, Vietnam) and non-Asian (Australia, New Zealand) sites. Five trials (DEVT, DIRECT-MT, DIRECT-SAFE, SKIP, and SWIFT DIRECT) used a non-inferiority study design, one (MR CLEAN-NO IV) was powered for both non-inferiority and superiority, and (BRIDGE-TNK) was a superiority trial.

Alteplase was the primary thrombolytic agent in five studies with a dose of 0.9 mg/kg (maximum 90 mg), 10% as an initial bolus and the remaining dose infused over 60 min. In the SKIP trail, a dose of 0.6 mg/kg was used27. In the DIRECT-SAFE trial, 17% of patients received tenecteplase rather than alteplase due to site specific procedures. BRIDGE-TNK administered tenecteplase with a dose of 0.25 mg/kg bolus (maximum 25 mg) over 5 to 10 s, followed by a saline flush to all patients.

Primary outcome

Functional independence (mRS 0 to 2 at 90 days) was achieved in 690 of 1436 patients (48.1%) treated with endovascular thrombectomy alone, compared to 742 of 1448 patients (51.2%) receiving intravenous thrombolysis plus endovascular thrombectomy, corresponding to a RR of 0.94 (95% CI, 0.87–1.01; p = 0.09; I2 = 9%). The pooled distribution of mRS scores from 0 to 6 at 90 days by treatment group is shown in Fig. 2. The median mRS score at 90 days was 3 (IQR 1–4) in the thrombectomy alone group and 2 (IQR 1–4) in the thrombolysis plus thrombectomy group, with a pooled common unadjusted OR of 0.92 (95% CI, 0.81–1.05; p = 0.22; I2 = 0%) (Appendix Fig. 2).

Secondary efficacy outcomes

An excellent neurological outcome (mRS 0 to 1 at 90 days) was observed in 424 of 1436 patients (29.5%) treated with thrombectomy alone compared to 454 of 1448 patients (31.3%) receiving thrombolysis plus thrombectomy (RR 0.94, 95% CI, 0.84–1.05; p = 0.25; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 3A). All-cause mortality after 90 days was 240 out of 1436 (16.7%) in the thrombectomy-only group and 237 out of 1448 (16.4%) in the thrombolysis plus thrombectomy arm (RR 1.02, 95% CI, 0.88–1.28; p = 0.63; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 3B). Health-related quality of life, measured by EQ-5D-5 L at 90 days, was similar between the groups (RR 0.98, 95% CI, 0.93–1.05; p = 0.63; I2 = 51%) (Fig. 3C). The rate of successful reperfusion (mTICI/eTICI 2b to 3) reached 1190 of 1382 (86.1%) in the thrombectomy alone group compared to 1244 of 1404 (88.6%) in the intravenous thrombolysis plus endovascular thrombectomy group (RR 0.98, 95% CI, 0.95–1.01; p = 0.14; I2 = 31%) (Fig. 3D).

Forest plots of pooled risk ratios for secondary efficacy outcomes comparing endovascular thrombectomy alone versus intravenous thrombolysis plus thrombectomy: (A) Excellent neurological outcome (B) All-cause mortality (C) Health-related quality of life (D) Successful reperfusion, defined as a Treatment in Cerebral Ischemia (TICI) score of 2b to 3.

Safety outcomes

Intracranial hemorrhage occurred in 448 out of 1407 patients (31.8%) who received thrombectomy-only treatment, compared with 499 out of 1411 patients (35.4%) who received systemic fibrinolysis plus thrombectomy (RR 0.89, 95% CI, 0.79–1.01; p = 0.06; I2 = 18%) (Fig. 4A). The rate of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage was 58 of 1163 (5.0%) versus 76 of 1167 (6.5%) (RR 0.77, 95% CI, 0.58–1.04; p = 0.08; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 4B). Puncture site bleeding was significantly lower in patients without intravenous thrombolysis (17 of 1257 patients [1.4%]) compared with the use of fibrinolytics (38 of 1297 patients [2.9%]); (RR 0.48, 95% CI, 0.27–0.85; p = 0.01; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 4C). Treatment-related complications, including vessel dissection (RR 1.07, 95% CI, 0.48–2.37; p = 0.88; I2 = 22%), contrast extravasation (RR 1.05, 95% CI, 0.83–1.33; p = 0.68; I2 = 0%), embolization (RR 1.01, 95% CI, 0.82–1.24; p = 0.94; I2 = 0%), and new infarction (RR 1.28, 95% CI, 0.91–1.80; p = 0.16; I2 = 0%) did not differ significantly between arms (Appendix Fig. 4).

Forest plots of pooled risk ratios for secondary safety outcomes comparing endovascular thrombectomy alone versus intravenous thrombolysis plus thrombectomy: (A) Any intracranial hemorrhage (B) Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (C) Puncture-site bleeding.

Subgroup outcomes

None of the prespecified subgroups provided statistically evidence of interaction with treatment effect for the primary efficacy outcome of functional independence (mRS 0 to 2 at 90 days). Specifically, interaction tests were non-significant between female and male patients (test for subgroup differences: Chi2=0.86; p = 0.35; I2 = 0%), elderly and non-elderly patients (test for subgroup differences: Chi2=0.42; p = 0.52; I2 = 0%), Asians and non-Asians (test for subgroup differences: Chi2=0.03; p = 0.86; I2 = 0%), low and high baseline NIHSS (test for subgroup differences: Chi2=0.11; p = 0.74; I2 = 0%), low and high ASPECTS (test for subgroup differences: Chi2=0.30; p = 0.58; I2 = 0%), tandem lesions and non-tandem lesion status (test for subgroup differences: Chi2=1.83; p = 0.18; I2 = 45.5%), ICA and MCA occluded arteries (test for subgroup differences: Chi2=0.15; p = 0.70; I2 = 0%), and time to randomization (test for subgroup differences: Chi2=0.01; p = 0.94; I2 = 0%) (Appendix Fig. 5).

The incidence of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage rates was observed in 52 of 879 patients (5.9%) in the thrombectomy alone group versus 67 of 887 patients (7.6%) in the bridging strategy group in the Asian population (RR 0.77, 95% CI, 0.54–1.10; p = 0.15; I2 = 0%) and 24 of 553 patients (4.3%) versus 32 of 551 patients (5.8%) in the non-Asian population (RR 0.74, 95% CI, 0.39–1.42; p = 0.37; I2 = 23%) (test for subgroup differences: Chi2=0.01; p = 0.91; I2 = 0%). However, this regional difference did not reach statistical significance (Appendix Fig. 6).

Sensitivity analyses

The findings of our study remained consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses. The switch between fixed-effects and random-effects models did not change estimates. Estimates for symptomatic ICH were consistent across definition frameworks. There was no evidence of differences for Heidelberg-defined symptomatic ICH (RR 0.78, 95% CI, 0.56–1.09; p = 0.15, I2 = 0%) or NINDS-defined symptomatic ICH (RR 0.76, 95% CI, 0.42–1.36; p = 0.35, I2 = 0%) (test for subgroup differences: Chi2=0.01; p = 0.94; I2 = 0%) (Fig. 4B). The exclusion of the DEVT trial from the primary efficacy outcome (mRS 0 to 2 at 90 days) produced a statistically significant pooled RR of 0.92 [95% CI 0.85–0.99] (Appendix Fig. 9). Similarly, exclusion of the DEVT trial in the ordinal shift analysis (mRS distribution 0 to 6) yielded an unadjusted common OR of 0.83 [95% CI 0.71–0.98]) for the ordinal mRS score 0 to 6 (Appendix Fig. 9). Finally, repeating the ordinal mRS shift analysis using covariate-adjusted results from each trial generated a common OR of 0.87 [95% CI 0.73–1.03] (Appendix Fig. 7).

Bias and certainty assessment

All seven trials used an open-label design, which was a necessity given the emergency nature of acute ischemic stroke management. However, it introduced potential bias (Appendix Fig. 8). To mitigate detection bias, all trials used blinded assessment of clinical endpoints.

According to GRADE criteria, the certainty of evidence was rated as moderate. Effect estimates were generally consistent across trials, but the 95% CIs for the primary outcome were wide and included the predefined non-inferiority margins, limiting confidence in asserting true equivalence between intravenous thrombolysis plus thrombectomy and endovascular thrombectomy alone for respective trials (Appendix Table 4).

Discussion

This updated trial-level meta-analysis of seven randomized controlled trials, involving 2,884 patients, found no statistically significant difference in functional independence (mRS 0 to 2 at 90 days) between patients treated with endovascular thrombectomy alone and those treated with endovascular thrombectomy with preceding intravenous thrombolysis (RR 0.94, 95% CI, 0.87–1.01; p = 0.09; I2 = 9%). Although effect estimates indicated a numerical trend toward bridging strategy, these patterns should be interpreted cautiously. These findings do not provide evidence for routinely omitting systemic fibrinolytics, and are consistent with the current guideline recommendations for administering intravenous thrombolysis to eligible patients presenting at endovascular thrombectomy-capable centers within 4.5 h4,5,28. Our meta-analysis builds upon previous findings12,13. Majoie et al. pooled patient-level data from six randomized controlled trials and reported an unadjusted OR of 0.92 [95% CI 0.77–1.10] for functional independence for endovascular thrombectomy alone versus bridging thrombolysis with endovascular treatment12. In a trial-level meta-analysis of the same comparison, Horvath et al. reported a pooled RR of 0.96 [95% CI 0.88–1.04]13. The recently published BRIDGE-TNK trial demonstrated a statistically significant benefit of tenecteplase plus endovascular thrombectomy over endovascular thrombectomy alone for functional independence20. Tenecteplase can be administered as a rapid bolus over 5 to 10 s, in contrast to the 1-hour infusion required for alteplase6. This quicker administration has the potential to streamline clinical workflows and reduce issues such as delays in arterial puncture or the transport of patients, which may be postponed until the alteplase infusion is completed29,30. The inclusion of BRIDGE-TNK in this updated meta-analysis expands the evidence base for acute ischemic stroke due to large-vessel occlusion. Although the trial demonstrated superiority, its inclusion did not alter the overall findings of previous meta-analyses12,13.

Excluding the DEVT trial resulted in a shift towards a statistically significant benefit of bridging therapy for both the pooled risk ratio for functional independence and the unadjusted common odds ratio for the full range of mRS scores (0–6). DEVT was one of the smaller trials (n = 234) and reported a median drug-to-groin time of ~ 40 min. This was substantially longer than in other studies31. Additionally, DEVT exclusively enrolled Chinese patients, a population with higher rates of intracranial atherosclerosis and potentially different workflow practices, both of which may have limited the observed benefits of thrombolysis31,32.

Analyses of secondary outcomes similarly demonstrated near-equivalence between the two treatment strategies. Rates of excellent neurological recovery, all-cause 90-day mortality, and patient-reported quality of life (EQ-5D-5 L) provided no evidence of superior efficacy with or without intravenous thrombolysis. Although systemic fibrinolysis prior to thrombectomy showed a non-significant trend toward higher rates of successful reperfusion (mTICI/eTICI 2b–3), rates of any ICH were only numerically increased, and symptomatic ICH remained comparable between groups. This contrasts with earlier meta-analyses, which reported a significant increase in asymptomatic hemorrhages with bridging therapy, while symptomatic hemorrhage rates remained stable12,13. However, it is important to note that both symptomatic and asymptomatic post-thrombectomy hemorrhages have been independently associated with worse functional outcomes33,34. In the regional subgroup analyses, symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage rates did not differ between Asian and non-Asian centers, even though stroke incidence is higher in Asia, especially China32. The only significant difference observed in secondary safety outcomes was a lower rate of puncture-site bleeding in patients who did not receive intravenous thrombolysis. This likely reflects the local effects of systemic fibrinolysis on vascular access sites. However, other procedural complication rates, including vessel dissection, contrast extravasation, embolization, and infarction in new vascular territories, were similar between groups. These findings suggest that omitting intravenous thrombolysis does not reduce the risk of device-related procedural complications.

Importantly, these findings apply exclusively to a highly selected population (i.e., acute ischemic stroke, large-vessel occlusions, endovascular thrombectomy-capable stroke center, presenting within 4.5-hours of symptom onset). Consequently, results cannot be generalized to patients transferred to non-thrombectomy centers, patients with medium- or distal-vessel occlusions who are not eligible for endovascular thrombectomy, or patients who experienced prolonged delays in thrombectomy treatment. Moreover, the heterogeneity of fibrinolytics (agent, dose) in the current evidence should not be ignored. While tenecteplase is gaining traction as a promising fibrin-specific alternative to alteplase, clinical experience beyond BRIDGE-TNK is limited30. Future research should target dedicated head-to-head trials comparing alteplase versus tenecteplase in populations eligible for endovascular thrombectomy to determine whether thrombolytic agent choice influences neurological outcomes35. While current evidence raises the hypothesis that bridging therapy with tenecteplase, but not necessarily alteplase, may be associated with improved neurological outcomes, this requires further confirmation20. In parallel, cost-effectiveness analyses are warranted to determine value across different healthcare systems and workflow models.

Limitations

This meta-analysis has limitations. Our analyses were based on aggregated trial-level data, which is considered less informative than patient-level data. However, individual patient data were unavailable, precluding harmonized subgroup analyses and covariate-adjusted analyses. Nevertheless, we assume that patient-level data would not have revealed any significant differences in the findings of the primary endpoint due to the homogeneity and randomized design of included studies36. Although we pooled all the available randomized evidence (7 trials; n = 2884), several results remained imprecise, which was reflected by wide CIs and limit the precision of our estimates. Moreover, subgroup cut-off values varied between studies. Thrombolytic regimens were not uniform across trials and differences in dosing and administration (infusion versus bolus) may dilute agent-specific effects and limit applicability to any single thrombolysis protocol. Additionally, data were predominantly derived from anterior circulation large-vessel occlusion. Occlusions in the basilar artery were included in two trials and only in small numbers. Therefore, external validity to posterior circulation is limited. Moreover, hemorrhage outcomes were defined using different criteria (Heidelberg, SITS-MOST and NINDS), which reduced the comparability and precision of the pooled safety estimates. Nonetheless, sensitivity analyses were comparable (Fig. 4B). The overall risk of bias was primarily driven by the open-label treatment allocation, whereas randomization procedures, outcome assessment, and follow-up were largely at low risk. However, certainty was limited mainly by imprecision and inconsistency.

Conclusion

There was no statistically significant benefit in achieving favorable neurological outcomes between endovascular thrombectomy alone and intravenous thrombolysis followed by thrombectomy. These findings do not provide a robust basis for routinely administering intravenous thrombolysis, despite a non-significant trend. Exploratory sensitivity analyses yielded statistically significant estimates toward bridging therapy, which may cautiously indicate a potential benefit. Notably, results apply to acute large-vessel occlusion stroke treated within 4.5 h of symptom onset23.
