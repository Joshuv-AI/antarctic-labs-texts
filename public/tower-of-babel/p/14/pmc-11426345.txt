Stress cardiomyopathy
Authors: Mehras Motamed, Jairo Tavares Nunes, Jacob Allen Udell
Published: 2024

Stress cardiomyopathy is a transient, reversible form of nonischemic cardiomyopathy

The incidence rises with age, predominantly affecting females after menopause. Physical and emotional stressors such as acute respiratory failure, grief, and anxiety often trigger the condition. However, approximately one-third of patients do not have an identifiable stressor.1

Symptoms mimic those of acute coronary syndrome and include chest pain, dyspnea, and syncope

It is part of the spectrum of myocardial infarction without coronary artery obstruction. Spontaneous coronary artery dissection shoul be considered in the differential diagnosis. Approximately 5%–12% of patients develop cardiogenic shock.2 Cardiogenic troponin is elevated in all patients, although often moderately.2 Electrocardiography often shows ST elevation, but patients may present with T-wave inversion, QT prolongation, and other nonspecific abnormalities.3 Echocardiography typically shows reduced ejection fraction and systolic left ventricular apical ballooning with midventricular, basal, or focal wall hyperkinesis.2

Coronary angiography is often required to exclude acute coronary syndrome

Diagnosis requires left ventricular regional wall motion abnormalities that extend beyond a single coronary artery distribution in the absence of obstructive coronary artery disease. Approximately 7%–13% of patients may have left ventricular outflow tract obstruction caused by basal left ventricular hypercontractility.4

Treatment is supportive and guided by hemodynamics and the presence of left ventricular outflow tract obstruction

Treatment of hemodynamically stable patients follows standard guidelines for patients with reduced ejection fraction. In hemodynamically unstable patients with left ventricular outflow tract obstruction, fluid resuscitation and low-dose β-blockers should be cautiously considered. Vasopressors, specifically phenylephrine, should be used for hypotension. 4 Anticoagulation should be considered in patients with large areas of akinesis and continued until left ventricular ejection fraction recovers.2

Most patients fully recover systolic function within 4 weeks4

However, in-hospital mortality is about 2%–7%. The recurrence risk is approximately 2%–4% per year.5
