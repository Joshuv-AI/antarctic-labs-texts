Pre-treatment with baicalein alleviates the diquat-induced microglial pyroptosis through gut microbiota–derived indole-3-propionic acid and DDX3X/G3BP1 pathway
Authors: Ting Li, Mengxiao Feng, Ping Wang, Miao Jiang, Jaiyu Cui, Mengxia Jin, Congying Song, Liying Lin, Yuan-Qiang Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBaicalein, a naturally occurring flavonoid with well-documented anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects, has shown therapeutic promise in multiple models of neurological disorders. However, whether baicalein can counteract diquat-induced neurotoxicity remains uncertain. This study reveals that baicalein alleviates diquat-induced neuroinflammation and microglial pyroptosis in mice in a gut microbiota (GM)-dependent manner. In the in vivo experiments, multi-omics analyses demonstrated that baicalein elevates the GM-derived metabolite indole-3-propionic acid (IPA), which was sufficient to suppress pyroptosis. In parallel, IPA was identified as a critical mediator of baicalein's neuroprotective effects, as exogenous IPA administration recapitulated baicalein's protection, and baicalein treatment significantly elevated IPA levels. Mechanistically, through inhibiting the DEAD-box helicase 3 X-linked / GTPase-activating protein (SH3 domain) binding protein 1 pathway by in vitro experiments. Introduction

Diquat is a non-selective bipyridine herbicide known for its effectiveness in controlling agricultural weeds (Zhou and Lu 2022). However, its increasing application has been accompanied by a rising number of acute poisoning cases. This trend has raised serious public health concerns, particularly because central nervous system (CNS) complications account for a large proportion of diquat-related fatalities. Despite growing awareness of its toxic effects, specific diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for diquat poisoning are still lacking (Li et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a, b, c, d).

Microglia are essential for monitoring and responding to neural microenvironment changes. Exogenous compounds can induce microglial polarization and apoptosis through multiple mechanisms. For example, manganese promotes polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype by activating leucine-rich repeat kinase 2 (Pajarillo et al. 2023). Diquat exposure induced oxidative stress in hippocampal microglia, activating the NOD-like receptor thermal protein domain associated protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome facilitated inflammatory cascade (Wang et al. 2024a, b, c). The NLRP3 inflammasome acts as a key pattern recognition receptor that senses cellular danger signals, assembles upon activation, and promotes caspase-1 activation, ultimately resulting in pyroptosis (Zhao et al. 2025).

Intestinal homeostasis is increasingly acknowledged as vital for brain immune function (Ohara and Hsiao 2025).The gut microbiota (GM) generates metabolic byproducts like short-chain fatty acids that serve as signaling molecules affecting brain activity(Schneider et al. 2024). Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) from healthy young mice has been shown to alleviate metabolic disturbances and reduce neuroinflammation, thereby improving cognitive function in recipient mice (Chevalier et al. 2025). Modifying the gut-brain axis (GBA) can impact pyroptosis by altering the neural microenvironment, which is partly due to changes in inflammatory mediator secretion by microglia.

Baicalein, a flavonoid isolated from the roots of Scutellaria baicalensis, exhibits noteworthy anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties in neuroinflammation therapy (Zhang et al. 2025). Baicalein reduces glycolytic activity in lipopolysaccharide-stimulated BV2 microglial cells, thereby alleviating neuroinflammation (Wang et al. 2023a, b). However, the potential of baicalein to counteract diquat-induced neurotoxicity, and whether its efficacy involves the modulation of the GBA to suppress microglial pyroptosis, remains an open question.

Accordingly, we aimed to explore the neuroprotective effects of baicalein against diquat, with a specific focus on the involvement of GM-derived metabolites and their downstream signaling pathways in microglia.

Materials and methods

Animal treatments and grouping

The hospital's laboratory animal facility provided C57BL/6 J mice (six weeks). A diquat poisoning model was established by intragastric administration of diquat dibromide (300 mg/kg; Aladdin, Shanghai, China) (Wang et al. 2024a, b, c). Dexamethasone (5 mg/kg; Aladdin) was administered daily by gavage for up to 14 days (Cui et al. 2023). baicalein (100 mg/kg; BBI Life Sciences, Shanghai, China) was administered daily via gavage for 14 days (Han et al. 2024). To diminish the GM, mice received a broad-spectrum antibiotic cocktail (ABX; 1.86 mg/mL ampicillin sodium, 0.96 mg/mL vancomycin hydrochloride, 1.86 mg/mL neomycin sulfate, and 1.20 mg/mL metronidazole in sterile saline; GLPbio, Shanghai, China) via oral gavage at 0.1 mL/day for 14 days (Hu et al. 2024). FMT was conducted daily for 14 days. Fresh fecal pellets were collected within 30 min of defecation from donor mice that had received baicalein (100 mg/kg/day) via oral gavage for 14 days. The collected feces were immediately pooled and homogenized in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 100 mg feces per 1 mL PBS). After brief centrifugation at 800 × g for 3 min to eliminate large particles, the supernatant containing the microbial suspension was collected and used within 15 min of preparation. Each recipient mouse received 0.1 mL of this fresh fecal suspension via oral gavage daily. For in vivo evaluation of IPA efficacy, mice received indole-3-propionic acid (IPA; Aladdin) (20 mg/kg body weight). IPA was dissolved in sterile saline and orally gavaged once daily for 14 consecutive days immediately after diquat exposure (Yang et al. 2025). Figs. S1 provides the detailed information on animal grouping and treatment regimens, n = 6 per group.

Treatment of BV2 cells

The BV2 microglial cell line was used in this study. The cell line was authentically identified with the Resource Identification (RRID) number: RRID:CVCL_0182 (abm Good, Richmond, Canada). BV2 murine microglial cells were cultivated in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Grand Island, USA) enriched with 1% (100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin; Gibco) and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco). We maintained cells at 37 °C in a humidified environment containing 5% CO₂. For experiments, cells were plated in 6-well plates and incubation was conducted for 24 h before treatment.

For the gain‑of‑function study, BV2 cells were transfected with a plasmid overexpressing DEAD-box helicase 3 X-linked (DDX3X) or a negative control plasmid (GenePharma, Shanghai, China) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). At 24 h post‑transfection, cells were exposed to 10 µmol/L diquat. For groups receiving IPA, (1 μg/mL; Aladdin), IPA was added 6 h after diquat, and cells were incubated for an additional 18 h (total diquat exposure 24 h). Four experimental groups were established: (1) diquat, (2) diquat + IPA, (3) pcDNA‑Ddx3x + diquat, and (4) pcDNA‑ Ddx3x + diquat + IPA.

For the loss‑of‑function study, cells were transfected with 50 nM small interfering RNA (siRNA, Sangon, Shanghai, China) targeting Ddx3x (siDdx3x, sense: 5′-AAGCAAGCAAAGGGCGUUAUU-3′, antisense: 5′-AUAACGCCCUUUGCUUGCUUU-3′), G3bp1 (siG3bp1, sense: 5′-AAGGAUGGAGUACGUGCAAUU-3′, antisense: 5′-UUGCACGUACUCCAUCCUUUU-3′), or negative control siRNA (sense: 5′-UUCUCCGAACGUGUCACGUTT-3′, antisense: 5′-ACGUGACACGUUCGGAGAATT-3′) using Lipofectamine 3000. For double knockdown, both siDdx3x and siG3bp1 were added at 50 nM each. At 24 h post‑transfection, cells were treated with 10 µmol/L diquat. IPA (1 μg/mL) was added 6 h later, and cells were incubated for an additional 18 h. Six groups were established: (1) diquat only, (2) diquat + IPA, (3) diquat + siDdx3x, (4) diquat + IPA + siDdx3x, (5) diquat + IPA + siG3bp1, and (6) diquat + siDdx3x + siG3bp1.

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining

Fresh brain tissue was preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde (Solarbio) and subsequently sectioned for HE staining. Microscopy imaging was captured under a microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). A semi-quantitative scoring system was used to assess both inflammatory cell infiltration and neuronal damage (Klopfleisch 2013). The criteria are as follows: level 0 – no inflammatory cells, normal neurons; level 0.5 – rare scattered inflammatory cells, occasional neuronal pyknosis; level 1.0 – mild infiltration (scattered mononuclear cells, no perivascular clustering), mild neuronal damage (occasional dark shrunken neurons); level 1.5 – mild-to-moderate infiltration (small perivascular cuffs), mild-to-moderate neuronal damage (patchy neuronal loss, increased pyknosis); level 2.0 – moderate infiltration (multifocal perivascular cuffs, parenchymal clusters), moderate neuronal damage (extensive pyknosis, visible gaps); level 2.5 – moderate-to-severe infiltration (dense perivascular cuffs extending into parenchyma), moderate-to-severe neuronal damage (most neurons dark, shrunken, or lost); level 3.0 – severe infiltration (dense sheets of inflammatory cells, tissue disruption), severe neuronal damage (almost complete neuronal loss).

Quantification of IPA levels

Using a specific kit (Coibo Bio, Shanghai, China), IPA levels were quantified, thereby measuring absorbance at 450 nm.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA isolation from brain tissue or BV2 cells was conducted via an RNA extraction kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Complementary DNA synthesis was conducted via a reverse transcription kit (Vazyme). The qRT-PCR was carried out with SYBR Green (Vazyme) on a CFX384 (Bio-Rad, Hercules, USA). The synthesis of primers was conducted by Sangon (Table S1).

Western blot (WB)

Proteins were extracted from small intestinal tissue, brain tissue, and BV2 cells. Samples were separated on 4%–10% SDS–polyacrylamide gels (GenScript, New Jersey, USA) and transferred onto membranes (Millipore) and incubation was conducted with primary antibodies against NLRP3 (1:500, Abmart, Shanghai, China), Gasdermin D (GSDMD, 1:500, Abmart), caspase-1 (Casp1, 1:500, Abmart), DDX3X (1:1,000, Affinity), G3BP1 (1:2,000, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), and β-actin (1:50,000, ABclonal, Wuhan, China). After washing, membranes were incubated with species-matched secondary antibodies (Proteintech). Chemiluminescence imaging (Biorad) was used to visualize protein bands.

16S rRNA amplicon sequencing

Genomic DNA extraction from fecal samples was performed through the FastDNA SPIN Kit for Feces (MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, USA). The amplification of the V4–V5 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was conducted by real-time PCR via primers 515 F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGG-3′) and 907R (5′-CCGTCAATTCMTTTRAGTTT-3′). An Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (San Diego, USA) was utilized for Amplicon sequencing. QIIME2 was utilized to process the sequencing data. We trimmed adaptor and primer sequences with the cutadapt plugin, and ASVs were denoised and filtered using DADA2. Taxonomic classification of ASVs was conducted via a Naive Bayes classifier pre-trained on the RDP database (v11.5), with a confidence threshold of 0.8.

mRNA sequencing

The preparation of libraries was conducted employing the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit, and an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform was utilized for sequencing to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw reads were assessed for quality using FastQC, followed by alignment to the reference genome with HISAT2. We quantified gene expression with HTSeq-count, and differential expression analysis was carried out with DESeq2.

Global metabolic profiling

Homogenization of about 20 mg of each fecal sample was conducted in 400 μL of pre-cooled methanol–water (Thermo Fisher Scientific) via steel beads and a tissue homogenizer (45 Hz, 2 min; Wonbio, Shanghai, China), followed by ultrasonic treatment at 40 kHz for 10 min (Fuyang, Shenzhen, China). The homogenate was incubated overnight at − 40 °C. A 150 μL aliquot of the supernatant was then analyzed using LC–MS/MS (Waters, Milford, USA). Mass spectrometry was performed with a spray voltage range of − 3000 to + 3800 V.

Raw data were processed with Progenesis QI v2.3 (Nonlinear Dynamics, Newcastle, UK). Precursor and product ion mass tolerances were set to 5 ppm and 10 ppm, respectively. Metabolite identification was based on matching exact mass, MS/MS fragmentation patterns, and isotopic distribution against the Human Metabolome Database, LipidMaps v2.3, METLIN, and in-house spectral libraries. Features exhibiting ≥ 50% missing values in any experimental group were excluded. Additionally, differential metabolites were defined as those with fold change (FC) ≥ 1.5 or ≤ 0.67, P < 0.05 (Student’s t-test, adjusted for multiple comparisons using Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate), and variable importance in projection score ≥ 1.0 from orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis models, which were validated by 200 permutation tests.

Molecular docking

Binding affinities and interaction modes between the drug candidate and its target were analyzed using AutoDock Vina (v1.2.2). The molecular structure of IPA was acquired from PubChem (CID: 397; https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). The 3D structure of DDX3X (PDB ID: 5E7I; resolution: 2.5 Å) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (http://www.rcsb.org/pdb/home/home.do). We converted the protein and ligand files to PDBQT format, removed water molecules, and added polar hydrogens. Docking was conducted via AutoDock Vina (http://autodock.scripps.edu/) with a grid box of 30 × 30 × 30 Å and grid spacing of 0.05 nm centered on the protein domain to allow ligand flexibility.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism (v9.3.1; Bethesda, MD, USA) or R (v4.1.1; R Core Team, 2021) were utilized for statistical analyses. Group comparisons were conducted utilizing a two-way ANOVA with subsequent Dunnett's test. Volcano plots (ggplot2 in R) were used to identify DEGs (|log₂FC|≥ 0.5, false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05) and differential metabolites. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment was performed with clusterProfiler (FDR < 0.05), and top 20 pathways were visualized as bubble plots. Gut microbiome data underwent principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) using the vegdist package (v2.6–4) with Bray–Curtis distance. The pheatmap package (v1.0.12) was utilized to visualize pyroptosis-related differentially expressed genes (DEGs). To analyze correlations between pyroptosis-related DEGs and operational taxonomic units. Correlations were computed using the corr.test function from the psych package (v2.4.3), applying Benjamini–Hochberg correction to manage the false discovery rate. The Mantel test by ggcor package was employed to investigate the mediators in the GBA by integrating data from 16S sequencing, RNA Sequencing, and fecal metabolomics. The Bray–Curtis distance matrix was applied for microbiota and metabolomic data, while pearson correlation distance was used for gene expression data; statistical significance was evaluated with 999 permutations (P < 0.05). ImageJ (v1.51 k; NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA) was utilized for quantifying WB outcomes. Outcomes are reported as mean ± standard. *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001 were regarded as significance.

Results

High-dose baicalein alleviates neuroinflammation and pyroptosis provoked by diquat

Unlike CON, the DQ group displayed marked inflammatory cell infiltration into the hippocampus (Fig. 1A). Baicalein administration led to a dose-dependent reduction in neuroinflammatory changes. Monocyte infiltration gradually decreased across the BAI 25, BAI 50, and BAI 100 groups, with BAI 100 showing the greatest reduction relative to DQ. Moreover, the BAI 100 group exhibited better therapeutic performance than DXMS in histopathological analysis of hippocampal tissue. Consistent with histology, inflammatory cytokines tumor necrosis factor α (Tnfα), interleukin-1β (Il1β), and Il6 mRNA levels followed a similar trend (Figs. 1B–D). These cytokines were elevated in DQ versus CON, and their expression generally declined with increasing baicalein dose, reaching the lowest levels in BAI 100. Although not statistically significant, Il1 mRNA was lower in both BAI 100 and DXMS versus DQ. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis revealed that genes downregulated upon treatment were significantly enriched in pyroptosis-related inflammatory signaling pathways, including TNF signaling, nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling, and NOD-like receptor signaling (Fig. S2E). Concurrently, upregulated genes were involved in p53 signaling and necroptosis, suggesting a global suppression of pro-inflammatory and pro-pyroptotic cellular death programs (Fig. S2F). Previous work from our team showed that diquat exposure induces neuroinflammation partly through the activation of pyroptosis (Wang et al. 2024a, b, c). To examine whether baicalein could mitigate this process, we analyzed markers related to pyroptosis and inflammation. At the transcriptional level, the BAI 100 group showed increased mRNA expression of Trem2, Gbp3, and Apip, along with reduced mRNA expression of Nlrp3, Gsdmd, Casp4, and Aim2 (Fig. 1E). The transcriptomic findings were confirmed by qPCR and WB analyses, which focused on key pyroptosis-related genes (Figs. 2F–G, Figs. S2A–D). These results showed a consistent pattern, indicating that baicalein treatment, particularly at 100 mg/kg, significantly down-regulated pyroptosis-related genes.

Alterations in pyroptosis-related gene expression are related to GM disruption

Figure 2A illustrates that the CON group had a significantly higher number of unique ASVs relative to the DQ group. β-diversity analysis indicated a clear difference in microbial community composition (Fig. 2B). The BAI 100 group displayed a distinct microbial distribution pattern unlike the DQ group. α-diversity indices, including Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson, showed that microbial diversity was higher in the BAI 100 group in contrast to the DQ group (Figs. 2C–E). Further correlation analysis identified specific associations between GM genera and pyroptosis-related molecules (Fig. 2F). Genera such as Alistipes, Anaerobacterium, Barnesiella, and Intestinimonas were negatively correlated with inflammasome components and pro-inflammatory cytokines, including Pycard, Nlrp1b, Nlrp3, and Il18. In contrast, Clostridium_IV showed a positive reaction to these markers. Moreover, Casp8 was inversely related to Bacteroides but positively correlated with Butyricicoccus and Streptococcus. These correlative findings provide candidate taxa for future mechanistic investigation.

Baicalein alleviates diquat-induced pyroptosis in a GM-dependent manner

Depletion of the GM by ABX markedly attenuated the protective impacts of baicalein. In the ABX + BAI group, inflammatory cell infiltration was aggravated (Fig. 3A), and the mRNA levels of Tnfα and Il6 were increased (Figs. 3B, C). Pyroptosis-associated proteins were also overexpressed, unlike the BAI group (Figs. 3D–H). In contrast, FMT reproduced the therapeutic benefits, significantly reducing inflammatory infiltration and pyroptosis marker levels relative to the DQ group. Altogether, intact GM is required for baicalein to alleviate diquat-induced pyroptosis.

IPA is identified as a key gut-brain mediator elevated by baicalein

A comprehensive fecal metabolomic analysis was performed to identify potential metabolites through which baicalein exerts its effects. Significant alterations were identified in a panel of metabolites, including N-decanoyl histidine, tauro-β-muricholic acid, glycochenodeoxycholate-3-sulfate, and IPA (Fig. 4A). These metabolites were notably enriched in several key pathways, such as tryptophan metabolism, autophagy, GABAergic synapse, and the citrate cycle (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, mantel test analysis demonstrated that N-decanoyl histidine, tauro-β-muricholic acid, glycochenodeoxycholate-3-sulfate, and IPA exhibited significant correlations with both GM composition and DEGs in brain tissue (Fig. 4C). These outcomes illustrate that these metabolites may act as vital molecular links mediating gut–brain crosstalk. Given previous evidence that IPA can reduce neuroinflammation (Wang et al. 2023a, b; Wang et al. 2025a, b, c, d), we focused on this metabolite. Subsequent quantification showed that baicalein significantly increased IPA levels across multiple compartments, from the gut lumen (feces, intestinal fluid) to the circulation (serum) and the brain (hippocampus), supporting its role as a potential GBA modulator (Figs. 4D–G).

To directly evaluate the therapeutic potential of IPA, we administered 20 mg/kg exogenous IPA to diquat-exposed mice. As shown in Fig. S3, exogenous IPA treatment reproduced the protective effects observed with baicalein. IPA significantly reduced inflammatory cell infiltration in the brain (Fig. S3A), down-regulated the mRNA expression of key pro-inflammatory cytokines including Tnfα and Il6, as well as pyroptosis-related genes Nlrp3 and Gsdmd (Fig. S3B–E), and suppressed pyroptosis-associated protein levels (Fig. S3F–I). These findings demonstrate that IPA alone is sufficient to alleviate diquat-induced neuroinflammation and pyroptosis, recapitulating the core therapeutic effects of baicalein. This, together with the observation that baicalein significantly elevates IPA levels in vivo, supports the role of IPA as a key mediator of baicalein's neuroprotection.

IPA attenuates diquat-induced BV2 microglial pyroptosis

The protective role of the GM–derived metabolite IPA was further confirmed in BV2 microglial cells. IPA directly reduced the cytotoxic effects of diquat, as evidenced by improved cell viability (Fig. 5A) and decreased expression of inflammatory cytokines (Figs. 5B–D) and pyroptosis-related molecules at mRNA and protein levels (Figs. 5E–I, Figs. S4A–D). Mechanistically, IPA restored cellular homeostasis by downregulating DDX3X expression and enhancing G3BP1 expression (Figs. 5J–L, Figs. S4E–F), suggesting its specific involvement in this stress-responsive pathway. These in vitro outcomes illustrate that IPA is an important functional mediator that directly suppresses microglial pyroptosis.

IPA directly targets the DDX3X/G3BP1 axis to suppress microglial pyroptosis

To elucidate the molecular mechanism by which IPA regulates the DDX3X/G3BP1 axis, we first assessed their potential direct interaction. Ddx3x overexpression in the presence of IPA abolished its anti-inflammatory and anti-pyroptotic effects. As shown in Fig. S5A, Ddx3x overexpression significantly weakened the protective effect of IPA against diquat-provoked cytotoxicity, making the cells more vulnerable to damage. At the molecular level, Ddx3x overexpression in the presence of IPA abolished its anti-inflammatory and anti-pyroptotic effects. This was reflected by significantly overexpressed mRNA levels of inflammatory cytokines and pyroptosis-related genes, including Tnfα, Il1β, Il6, Il18, Gsdmd, and Nlrp3 (Figs. S5B–G). In agreement with the transcriptional results, immunofluorescence and WB analyses showcased that Ddx3x overexpression markedly elevated NLRP3 levels (Fig. 6A, Figs. S5H–P) and activated the downstream pyroptotic cascade.

Conversely, knockdown experiments using siRNA targeting Ddx3x and/or G3bp1 revealed the hierarchical organization of this pathway. Diquat exposure induced the highest levels of pyroptosis and proinflammatory cytokines expression (Fig. 6B, Figs. S6). IPA treatment alone significantly reduced these markers. Notably, Ddx3x knockdown alone mimicked IPA's protective effect, reducing pyroptosis and proinflammatory cytokines to levels comparable to IPA treatment, indicating that Ddx3x suppression is sufficient for protection. When Ddx3x was knocked down in the presence of IPA, no further reduction occurred, suggesting IPA and Ddx3x knockdown act through the same pathway. In contrast, G3bp1 knockdown attenuated IPA's protection, with pyroptosis and proinflammatory cytokines levels approaching diquat control, identifying G3BP1 as a downstream effector. Most importantly, simultaneous knockdown of both genes abolished the protection conferred by Ddx3x knockdown alone, yielding pyroptosis and proinflammatory cytokines levels similar to the diquat-only group. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that IPA directly targets DDX3X and that the pro-pyroptotic and pro-inflammatory effects of DDX3X are mediated entirely through G3BP1 inhibition. Consistent with this regulatory mode, molecular docking analysis further validated a direct binding potential between IPA and DDX3X, with a binding energy of −7.47 kcal/mol indicative of a stable binding conformation(Fig. 6C).

Baicalein attenuates diquat-induced pyroptosis via the microbiota-derived IPA and the DDX3X/G3BP1 axis in vivo

Analysis of brain tissue further confirmed the critical role of the DDX3X/G3BP1 pathway in vivo (Figs. 7A–C, Figs. S7A–B). Diquat exposure induced a coordinated alteration in protein expression, characterized by increased DDX3X and reduced G3BP1 levels. These pathological changes were reversed by treatment with baicalein or dexamethasone. Notably, the regulatory effect of baicalein on this pathway was abolished when the GM was depleted by antibiotics (Figs. 7D–F, Figs. S7C–D), highlighting the implication of the GBA. Given the modulatory role of microbial metabolite IPA to this pathway in vitro (Figs. S7E–I), we conclude that baicalein exerts its neuroprotective effect by promoting the growth of IPA-producing gut microbes. The resulting increase in systemic IPA then suppresses pyroptosis by modulating the cerebral DDX3X/G3BP1 signaling axis (Fig. 7G).

Discussion

Diquat exposure has been shown to induce neuroinflammation, leading to neuronal injury and neurological dysfunction (Qiu et al. 2025; Wang et al. 2025a, b, c, d). Increasing evidence highlights the intestinal microbiota as a key regulator of neuroinflammatory processes, with gut dysbiosis contributing to their pathogenesis (Huang et al. 2025; Wen et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2025a, b). Herein, high-dose baicalein markedly attenuated diquat-induced neuroinflammation, likely through modulation of the NLRP3 signaling pathway. This finding aligns with previous reports describing baicalein's broad neuroprotective actions. Earlier studies demonstrated that baicalein suppresses neuroinflammation and ameliorates intestinal barrier disruption by improving tight junction protein expression (Du et al. 2024; Guo et al. 2023). Additionally, it mitigates oxidative stress and apoptosis in intestinal epithelium and restores GM balance by regulating intracellular signaling, maintaining intestinal barrier integrity (Liu et al. 2023). baicalein inhibits NLRP3 activation, thereby limiting pyroptosis (Chen et al. 2022). Together, these established mechanisms provide a strong rationale for our findings on the anti-neuroinflammatory effects of baicalein in diquat-induced neurotoxicity.

While our findings are in line with earlier studies, the novelty of this work lies in the systematic elucidation of baicalein's mechanism of action in a diquat-induced model. Herein, we observed associations between GM composition and hippocampal pyroptosis-related gene expression. Notably, this correlation showed a negative association with beneficial genera such as Lactobacillus and Intestinimonas, whose administration can alleviate neuroinflammation (Yun et al. 2024), and a positive association with potentially harmful genera such as Clostridium_IV, which contributes to the pathogenesis of other CNS disorders (Lin et al. 2024). This suggests that specific GM taxa serve as key modulators of neuroinflammatory and pyroptotic signaling, thereby exerting a substantial influence on hippocampal integrity. While these correlative findings do not establish causality, they provide candidate taxa for future mechanistic investigation and serve as hypothesis-generating observations that guided our subsequent functional studies.

An intriguing observation from our study is that antibiotic-mediated microbiota depletion attenuated but did not completely abolish baicalein's neuroprotective effects, suggesting that baicalein exerts both microbiota-dependent and -independent activities. While the predominant protective mechanism involves the gut-derived metabolite IPA, the residual protection observed in antibiotic-treated animals points to direct CNS actions. Consistent with this, baicalein crosses the blood–brain barrier, reaching a brain-to-plasma ratio of almost 0.12 (Fong et al. 2014). Once in the brain, baicalein directly suppresses microglial activation, reduces oxidative stress, and protects blood–brain barrier integrity (Cai et al. 2025; Guo et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2025a, b, c, d). Researchers have also utilized drug delivery systems to improve the efficiency of baicalein's entry into the blood–brain barrier. Here, we mainly discuss the mechanisms dependent on the GM (Xu et al. 2025a, b).

Our findings extend this understanding by identifying IPA as a key mediator upregulated by baicalein treatment. The sufficiency of IPA was established through exogenous administration experiments, which recapitulated baicalein's protective effects. Furthermore, the dependence of baicalein's efficacy on an intact GM, coupled with the significant elevation of IPA by baicalein treatment, strongly suggests that IPA is an essential effector molecule in the GBA mediating baicalein's neuroprotection. IPA, a GM–derived metabolite, is produced mainly by species from the Clostridiaceae and Peptostreptococcaceae via dietary tryptophan metabolism (Serger et al. 2022). Brain IPA originates entirely from systemic circulation, as microbial conversion of tryptophan to indole derivatives is essential for its production (Owe-Larsson et al. 2025). The anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective properties of IPA show that it supports neuronal survival through multiple signaling pathways (Jiang et al. 2024; Li et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024a, b, c).

IPA integrates with the DDX3X/G3BP1 pathway, a signaling axis increasingly recognized for its critical role in neuroinflammatory injury (Paget et al. 2023). Our in vitro findings further elucidate the regulatory mechanism within this axis, demonstrating that DDX3X/G3BP1 likely modulates pyroptosis-related genes both at the transcriptional and protein levels in diquat poisoning. We, therefore, propose that IPA translates GM changes into a neuroprotective signal that suppresses the pro-pyroptotic DDX3X/G3BP1 pathway in microglia, thereby refining and substantiating the mechanistic framework of baicalein's gut–brain–mediated neuroprotection.

Beyond the immediate context of acute diquat poisoning, the mechanistic insights from this study may have broader implications for other CNS injuries, such as stroke and traumatic brain injury. The core pathological events identified here—microglial pyroptosis, neuroinflammation, and oxidative stress—are common hallmarks shared across these conditions (Du et al. 2025; Packer et al. 2025). Diquat exposure induces neuroinflammation through mechanisms that parallel those observed in cerebral ischemia, including NLRP3 inflammasome activation and subsequent pyroptosis. Additionally, diquat has been shown to induce α-synuclein accumulation and apoptosis through dysregulation of chaperone-mediated autophagy and macroautophagy (Kim and Koh 2021), processes increasingly implicated in neuronal fate determination following acute brain injury. The GBA, a central focus of our study, also modulates outcomes in stroke and traumatic brain injury, with microbiota-derived metabolites like IPA exerting neuroprotective effects (Chen et al. 2026). Therefore, the therapeutic strategy validated here—targeting GM to enhance IPA production and suppress microglial pyroptosis—may extend beyond poisoning scenarios to other acute neurological disorders, strengthening the clinical translational value of our findings.

This investigation has several limitations. First, the outcomes are derived from mouse models and require validation in human cohorts to confirm clinical translatability. Second, no formal sample size calculation was performed. The small sample size may limit statistical power, particularly for multi-omics analyses, and increase the risk of false-positive findings. Future studies with larger cohorts and appropriate power calculations are warranted. Third, a baicalein-only control group was not included in the experimental design. While this does not diminish the main conclusions, inclusion of such a group would help distinguish baicalein’s baseline effects from its protective effects against diquat-induced neuroinflammation. Fourth, while exogenous IPA recapitulates baicalein’s protective effects, the strict necessity of IPA for baicalein’s neuroprotection remains unproven. The antibiotic depletion experiment confirms the requirement of an intact microbiota but does not specifically implicate IPA, as antibiotics deplete all microbial taxa and metabolites. Definitive proof would require selective IPA depletion while preserving the gut microbial community, an important direction for future research.

Conclusion

In summary, this work reveals that baicalein protects against diquat-induced neuroinflammation through a GM-dependent mechanism, with IPA identified as a key functional mediator. IPA alone is sufficient to recapitulate baicalein's effects, and an intact microbiota is required for its efficacy, positioning the IPA–GBA as a vital pathway in suppressing microglial pyroptosis. These findings offer new insights for microbiota-targeted therapies in diquat-induced neuroinflammatory disorders.
