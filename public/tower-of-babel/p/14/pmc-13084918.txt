Sexual harassment, stress and suicide attempt among international college students in the USA
Authors: Kruti S Chaliawala, Rebecca A Vidourek, Keith A King
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSexual harassment is a significant public health concern on US college campuses, yet limited research has examined its prevalence and correlates among international students. This study investigates the prevalence of sexual harassment among international college students and examines associations with stress and suicide attempts.MethodsA cross-sectional secondary data analysis was conducted using the 2022 American College Health Association–National College Health Assessment . The analytic sample included 13 242 international students. Sexual harassment in the past 12 months served as the primary outcome. Predictor variables included stress level, suicide attempt in the past year, number of sexual partners, academic level and sex assigned at birth. Descriptive statistics, univariate logistic regression and a final multivariate logistic regression model were used to examine associations.ResultsAmong 13 242 international college students, 6.1% (n=799) reported sexual harassment in the past 12 months. In the adjusted logistic regression model, moderate to high stress was associated with higher odds of sexual harassment (adjusted odds ratio (AOR) =1.800, 95% CI 1.470 to 2.205), and past-year suicide attempt was associated with higher odds of reporting sexual harassment (AOR=4.112, 95% CI 3.035 to 5.570).ConclusionThe study underscores the need for targeted interventions and policies that account for gender-specific and population-specific vulnerabilities. International students face unique stressors, underscoring the need for comprehensive support systems at multiple levels. Limitations include self-reported data and a cross-sectional design. Future research should employ longitudinal designs and diverse samples to deepen understanding and strengthen support mechanisms, thereby fostering safer educational environments.

Introduction

Sexual harassment on college campuses is a widespread concern with potential consequences for students’ health and educational access. Sexual harassment is commonly defined as unwelcome conduct of a sexual nature, including unwelcome sexual advances, requests for sexual favours and other verbal or physical conduct.1 In educational settings, conduct may constitute sexual harassment under Title IX when it is so severe, pervasive and objectively offensive that it effectively denies or limits equal access to an education programme or activity, and Title IX also encompasses certain forms of sexual violence and related misconduct.2 3 Together, these definitions emphasise that sexual harassment can include a range of behaviours, but the defining feature is that the conduct is unwelcome and interferes with a student’s ability to participate fully in campus life.1 3

Despite significant research documenting the prevalence of sexual harassment, gaps remain in understanding how various factors intersect to influence students’ experiences, particularly among marginalised groups. Data from the Rape, Abuse & Incest National Network4 reveal that approximately 13% of all college students face rape or sexual assault, with undergraduate females experiencing these issues at a rate of 26.4%. However, the experiences of international students, a group facing unique stressors such as cultural adaptation, language barriers and social isolation, are not as thoroughly examined.5 6 These stressors may increase vulnerability to harassment and exacerbate its effects on mental health.

Understanding the intersectionality of race, gender and other sociodemographic factors is crucial for addressing the nuances of sexual harassment. Studies indicate that female students are at a heightened risk of harassment and that social support networks play a vital role in mitigating its adverse effects.6 Moreover, cultural and racial factors significantly contribute to students’ vulnerability.5 7 For international students, these intersecting identities can amplify the impact of sexual harassment, making it essential to consider these factors in developing effective support systems.8 The interplay of stress and suicide attempts is crucial in understanding the impact of sexual harassment. High levels of stress and suicide attempts have been linked to increased vulnerability to harassment and poorer mental health outcomes.9 10 For international students, the additional pressures of adjusting to a new environment and educational system can intensify these challenges, making it essential to consider these factors in developing targeted interventions.11 12

In addition to psychological factors such as stress and suicide attempt, interpersonal and behavioural characteristics may also shape vulnerability to sexual harassment. Prior literature suggests that relational context and sexual behaviour patterns may influence exposure to harassment and victimisation experiences.13 However, these associations should not be interpreted as causal or as implying individual responsibility for victimisation. Rather, factors such as the number of sexual partners may serve as markers of broader social exposure, peer network characteristics or participation in social environments with more frequent interactions. For international students navigating new social and cultural contexts, such relational variables may reflect differences in social integration rather than inherent vulnerability. Accordingly, the present study examines the number of sexual partners as an associated variable, not as a causal determinant of harassment.

To address these issues comprehensively, it is crucial to understand how specific variables such as stress levels, suicide attempts and the number of sexual partners intersect with experiences of sexual harassment. This study aims to fill this gap by examining how these factors influence the likelihood of harassment among international students. The current study intentionally focuses on international students to centre a population whose health and campus climate experiences are often not reported separately in campus surveys and related research. Hence, the research questions: (1) What is the prevalence of sexual harassment among international college students in the USA? (2) How do levels of stress, suicide attempts and sociodemographic factors (such as relationship status, number of sexual partners, year of school and biological sex) affect the experience of sexual harassment among international college students?

Methodology

Study design

The present study employed a cross-sectional secondary data analysis approach using the 2022 dataset derived from the American College Health Association’s National College Health Assessment (ACHA-NCHA). Ethical clearance was obtained from the corresponding university’s Institutional Review Board (IRB), explicitly seeking the Determination of Non-Human Subject research status due to the data’s anonymity and public availability (2023-0755).

Participants

The original dataset comprised a total of n=1 02 905 college students from diverse academic institutions across the USA. However, for this study, the analysis focused solely on the subset of international college students in the sample, with a total of n=13 242 participants.

Procedure

The ACHA-NCHA survey was administered electronically to students at participating colleges and universities across the USA during both the Spring and Fall semesters of 2022. Institutions self-selected to participate in the survey, and within these institutions, a random sample of students was invited to complete the survey via email. This sample selection aimed to ensure broad representation across various demographics, including year of study, gender, race/ethnicity and international student status. For the Fall 2022 data, a survey was conducted between August and December 2022, with 33 774 students from 51 institutions participating. Of these institutions, 61% were public, and 86% were 4-year institutions. The mean response rate for Fall 2022 was 11%, and the median was 9%. For the Spring 2022 data, the survey was conducted between January and June 2022, with 69 131 students from 129 institutions participating. The mean response rate for Spring 2022 was 13%. Although the survey provides extensive insights into students’ health behaviours and attitudes, response rates were relatively low, averaging 11% in Fall 2022 and 13% in Spring 2022. These rates suggest that certain groups, such as students with limited access to technology or those less engaged with campus life, may be underrepresented. Conversely, students more engaged with health-related issues might be overrepresented. The survey results reflect the voices of students who chose to participate but may not fully capture the experiences of all subgroups within the student population. For instance, underrepresented minority groups or international students may have been less likely to respond, potentially limiting the generalisability of the findings to these populations. Although the survey aims to be inclusive, non-response bias remains a possibility, and the data may not fully reflect all student experiences at participating institutions.

Instruments

For the current study, several questions from the ACHA-NCHA were analysed to explore the participants’ experiences. Sexual harassment was assessed through the question: “Within the last 12 months, have you had problems or challenges with any of the following? Sexual Harassment (unwelcomed sexual advances, requests for sexual favors, and other verbal or physical conduct of a sexual nature)” (No (1), Yes (2)). Participants were also asked to rate their overall stress level experienced in the last 30 days, with response options ranging from No stress (1) to High (4). A suicide attempt was investigated through the question: “Within the last 12 months, have you attempted suicide?” (No (1), Yes (2)). Relationship status was queried with the question: “What is your relationship status?” with options including Not in a relationship (1), In a relationship but not married/partnered (2) and Married/partnered (3). Furthermore, participants were asked if they held a visa (for example, F-1 or M-1) to study or work in the USA, with response options of No (1) or Yes (2). Socio-demographic factors examined in the study included sex assigned at birth, year in school and ethnicity, each with corresponding response categories.

Data analysis

After receiving approval from the IRB, data analysis was conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences V.26.0. For the primary data analysis, participants were categorised based on their responses to the question regarding visa status, specifically whether they answered ‘Yes’ or ‘No’ to holding a student visa. This separation was done to understand the health behaviours, outcomes and attitudes of international students (those on student visas) as a priority population. The background information was analysed using descriptive statistics such as frequency. Research question one was analysed using frequency and descriptive statistics. The second research question was analysed using a univariate logistic regression. The significant variables from the univariate regression analysis were then analysed using a final logistic regression model. The continuous variables in the dataset were initially examined to assess their associations with the outcome variables of interest, including sexual harassment, stress levels, suicide attempt and socio-demographic factors. Certain continuous variables were dichotomised to facilitate analysis and interpretation within the logistic regression framework. This dichotomisation process involved categorising continuous variables into two distinct groups based on predetermined thresholds or meaningful cut-off points. Stress levels were dichotomised into ‘Low stress’ and 'High stress’ categories. Table 1 presents the original four response categories for descriptive purposes, whereas tables2 3 present the dichotomised variable used in the regression models. Number of Sexual Partners was dichotomised into ‘0-1 partner’ and ‘Multiple partners (≥2)’ categories. Similarly, Relationship status and Academic Year were dichotomised based on relevant criteria. This dichotomisation enabled the use of univariate logistic regression to examine the individual associations between each dichotomised variable and the outcome. Subsequently, variables demonstrating significant associations in the univariate analyses were included in the final multivariate logistic regression model to assess their independent contributions while controlling for potential confounders.

Results

Participant demographics

The secondary data analysis incorporated a diverse sample of n=13 242 international college students in the USA. Table 1 presents detailed participant demographics. Regarding biological sex, most international students identified as female (n=7633, 57.8%), followed by male participants (n=5568, 42.1%). A tiny percentage (0.1%, n=9) identified as intersex. Sex assigned at birth was modelled as a binary predictor. Because the intersex category was very small (n=9), these responses were excluded from regression due to sparse cell size. In terms of transgender identity, 2.3% (n=306) of participants identified as transgender. The breakdown of gender identity demonstrated that 56.0% (n=7390) identified as women, 41.6% (n=5489) as men, and the remaining participants identified across various gender identities, including trans women (0.1%, n=13), trans men (0.2%, n=23), genderqueer (0.3%, n=40) and non-binary (1.1%, n=139). Regarding stress levels experienced in the last 30 days, 3.5% (n=459) of participants reported no stress, 25.4% (n=3357) reported low stress, 46.7% (n=6182) reported moderate stress and 24.1% (n=3191) reported high stress. Concerning suicide attempts, 305 (2.3%) of participants reported attempting suicide in the last 12 months.

Prevalence of sexual harassment

The study examined the prevalence of sexual harassment among international college students in the USA over the past 12 months. The findings revealed that 6.1% (n=799) of students reported experiencing sexual harassment, which includes unwelcome sexual advances, requests for sexual favours and other verbal or physical conduct of a sexual nature. Among those who reported harassment, 646 (81.6%) were female, and 145 (18.4%) were male. Additionally, 580 (73.5%) were undergraduate students, while 209 (26.5%) were graduate students. Regarding relationship status, 564 (70.6%) had multiple partners, and 452 (56.7%) were not in a relationship. Furthermore, 84.4% (n=674) of the participants reported moderate to high stress levels, and 64 (8.1%) had attempted suicide in the past year.

Sexual harassment, stress, suicide attempt

Several socio-demographic variables were explored to understand their relationship with experiences of sexual harassment among international college students in the USA (table 2). Univariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to examine these associations. The results revealed several significant findings. Primarily, regarding the number of partners, students with multiple partners were more likely (OR=1.285, 95% CI 1.098 to 1.502) to report experiencing sexual harassment. However, no significant association was found between relationship status and sexual harassment (p=0.832). Furthermore, undergraduate students were twice as likely (OR=2.329, 95% CI 1.980 to 2.739) to report sexual harassment compared with graduate students. Additionally, female students were three times more likely to report sexual harassment compared with male students. Moreover, students reporting moderate to high-stress levels were significantly two times more likely to experience sexual harassment (OR=2.285, 95% CI 1.880 to 2.776). Lastly, participants who had attempted suicide in the last 12 months were four times more likely to report sexual harassment (OR=4.445, 95% CI 3.339 to 5.916).

The significant variables from the univariate logistic regression model were analysed in the final logistic regression model (table 3) to explore the predictors of experiencing sexual harassment among international college students within the USA. Of the initial n=13 242 participants, 451 were excluded due to missing data, leaving a final sample of n=12 791 for analysis. The logistic regression model uncovered that all predictor variables significantly contributed to predicting sexual harassment experiences. The Hosmer and Lemeshow test indicated a good fit for the model (χ² = 2.215, df=7, p=0.947). Moreover, the classification table demonstrated an overall correct classification rate of 94.0%. The model summary indicated that the predictors explained 8.7% of the variance in sexual harassment experiences, with a Nagelkerke R² of 0.087. As per the adjusted odds ratios (AOR), individuals identifying as female (AOR=3.105, 95% CI 2.568 to 3.754), undergraduate students (AOR=1.928, 95% CI 1.631 to 2.278) and those reporting moderate-high-stress levels (AOR=1.800, 95% CI 1.470 to 2.205) were more likely to report experiences of sexual harassment. Similarly, participants who reported multiple partners (AOR=1.332, 95% CI 1.132 to 1.568) and those who had attempted suicide in the last 12 months (AOR=4.112, 95% CI 3.035 to 5.570) had higher odds of experiencing sexual harassment.

Discussion

The findings of the present study illuminate a concerning aspect of higher education: the prevalence and determinants of sexual harassment among international college students in the USA. Consistent with established research,6 14 15 the present analysis reveals that 6.1% (n=799) of international students reported experiencing sexual harassment in the past 12 months.

However, prevalence estimates of sexual harassment among US college students vary across national and institutional surveys, in part because definitions, item wording and recall periods differ, which limits direct comparisons across studies and samples.16 Accordingly, the 6.1% prevalence observed in this international student sample should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect, at least in part, differences in disclosure rather than differences in exposure. Underreporting among international students may occur due to concerns about immigration-related vulnerability, unfamiliarity with institutional reporting processes and limited trust in campus resources.17 18 Cultural stigma and social norms surrounding sexual harassment and sexual violence may further discourage disclosure, potentially leaving trauma unaddressed and contributing to longer-term adverse outcomes.19 20

Furthermore, the findings of this study align with the broader literature on risk and protective factors for sexual harassment at higher education institutions. Individual-level factors, such as prior experiences of violence or substance use, contribute to a higher risk of perpetration.13 Similarly, social influences, including peer pressure and the normalisation of aggressive sexual behaviour within certain groups, play a critical role in fostering environments where harassment is more likely to occur. On the other hand, protective factors like strong institutional policies, educational programmes that challenge harmful gender norms and the presence of supportive social networks can act as significant deterrents.13

Although the data indicate a pressing need for support, the current analysis does not provide information on whether international students are accessing mental health and sexual harassment resources. This is a critical gap, as previous research suggests that international students may underuse campus resources due to stigma, lack of awareness or cultural differences in seeking help. Future studies should explore the availability and use of support services by international students, including counselling, health services and sexual harassment prevention programmes, to better understand and address potential barriers to accessing care.21

The current investigation explored socio-demographic factors related to sexual harassment among international college students. Consistent with previous research,7 11 the present study found a significant gender disparity, with female students experiencing sexual harassment at a much higher rate compared with their male peers, accounting for 81.6% (n=646) of the cases. This gender-based disparity underscores the need for gender-specific strategies to address sexual harassment in higher education settings. However, it is crucial to contextualise these findings within the broader student population. Comparative data on domestic students could offer a more comprehensive understanding of whether international students are disproportionately affected by these issues or if these trends reflect general patterns observed in all student populations.

The analysis also revealed critical connections between stress levels and sexual harassment. Specifically, participants reporting moderate to high stress were significantly more likely to experience sexual harassment, with an OR of 2.285 (95% CI 1.880 to 2.776). Additionally, students who had attempted suicide in the past 12 months were four times more likely to report sexual harassment (OR=4.445, 95% CI 3.339 to 5.916). These findings are aligned with existing literature linking sexual harassment to adverse mental health outcomes.122224 However, the cross-sectional nature of the survey limits the ability to determine the direction of these relationships. Future longitudinal studies should explore whether increased stress leads to a higher risk of victimisation or if the experience of harassment exacerbates stress and suicide attempts.

Regarding multiple sexual partners, we observed higher odds of reporting sexual harassment among students reporting two or more partners (OR=1.285, 95% CI 1.098 to 1.502). One plausible explanation is that a higher number of partners may function as a proxy for greater social exposure to settings where unwanted sexual conduct is more likely, including parties, bars and other contexts in which alcohol use is common and supervision is limited.25 26 Alternatively, this association may reflect residual confounding rather than a direct mechanism, because partner count can correlate with other unmeasured factors (eg, frequency of socialising, alcohol use, relationship dynamics or prior victimisation) that were not assessed in the analytic model; therefore, findings should be interpreted as correlational and warrant further testing in studies that directly measure these hypothesised pathways.25 27 Furthermore, the association observed between the number of sexual partners and sexual harassment should be interpreted cautiously. The cross-sectional design does not permit causal inference, and the findings do not suggest that sexual behaviour causes harassment. Harassment is the responsibility of perpetrators, not victims. The observed association may reflect increased exposure to social settings, peer networks or relational contexts in which interactions are more frequent.

The current study also identified that undergraduate students were notably more likely to report sexual harassment, with 73.5% (n=580) of cases occurring among this group. This finding highlights that individuals in the earlier stages of their academic careers may be more vulnerable due to less familiarity with campus resources and increased social interactions. In summary, although the study provides valuable insights into the experiences of international students, it also highlights the necessity of comparative analyses with domestic students and longitudinal studies to better understand the complexities of sexual harassment, stress and victimisation. Addressing these issues requires targeted interventions that consider the specific vulnerabilities and experiences of different student populations, including enhanced support services and prevention programmes tailored to the diverse needs of both international and domestic students.

Implications

The implications of this study on sexual harassment, stress and suicide attempt among international students are multifaceted and extend across individual, institutional, policy and international office levels. This study highlights several critical areas where targeted interventions and support systems are essential.

The findings underscore the need for international students to have access to mental health resources that address their unique challenges. Elevated stress and suicide attempts associated with experiences of sexual harassment emphasise the necessity for culturally sensitive counselling services. Such services should be tailored to the specific stressors faced by international students, including cultural adaptation, language barriers and social isolation.11 Effective support networks must be established to encourage students to seek help without stigma and to provide them with resources to manage academic and social pressures.28

International offices are pivotal in supporting students throughout their academic journey within the USA. They should implement comprehensive orientation programmes that cover recognising and reporting sexual harassment, accessing mental health services and managing stress and cultural adjustment.7 Ongoing support, including regular check-ins and tailored services, can mitigate risks associated with sexual harassment and its impact on mental health. By fostering an inclusive environment, international offices can enhance students’ overall well-being and academic success.12

Universities must adopt comprehensive strategies to address sexual harassment and its effects. This includes mandatory training for students, faculty and staff on recognising and responding to harassment.5 Institutions should create a campus culture that prioritises the safety and well-being of all students, with particular attention to vulnerable groups such as international students. Enhancing mental health services to be more accessible and responsive to the diverse needs of the student body is crucial.9

On a broader scale, universities and colleges should advocate for stronger federal and state regulations protecting international students from sexual harassment and ensuring adequate mental health support. Policies must guarantee that international students receive the same protections as domestic students, with additional considerations for their unique vulnerabilities. Institutions should also push for policies that address the intersection of harassment, stress and suicide attempt, acknowledging the need for comprehensive mental health support.8 10

Limitations

The current study provides valuable insights into the prevalence of sexual harassment, stress and suicide attempts among international college students; however, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the study’s cross-sectional design inherently limits the ability to draw causal inferences. Though associations between sexual harassment and mental health outcomes were identified, this design does not allow for establishing the directionality or causality of these relationships. Longitudinal research is essential to explore causative factors and understand the long-term impacts of sexual harassment on stress and suicide attempts among international students.

Second, the reliance on self-reported data introduces potential biases, including recall and social desirability bias. Participants may underreport or overreport their experiences due to the sensitive nature of the topics or fear of stigma, which could affect the reliability of the findings. Future research should incorporate diverse data sources, such as clinical assessments or institutional records, to validate self-reported data and improve accuracy. Another limitation is the sample composition. Although the study included a substantial number of international students, it may not fully represent all international students in the USA. Variations in factors such as country of origin, cultural background and length of stay could influence experiences of sexual harassment and mental health outcomes. Therefore, future research should aim for more diverse and representative samples to capture a broader range of experiences.

Moreover, the study did not account for potential moderating effects of contextual factors such as campus climate, support systems and cultural differences in perceptions of harassment and mental health. These variables could significantly influence the relationships between sexual harassment, stress and suicide attempts. Including these contextual factors in future research could provide a more nuanced understanding of the issues. The measurement tools used in the study also present limitations. While standardised questionnaires were likely used to assess sexual harassment, stress and suicide attempt, these tools may not fully capture the complex nature of these experiences, especially across different cultural contexts. Future research should consider developing or adapting validated measures that are culturally sensitive and appropriate for diverse international student populations. Additionally, the study’s focus on international students within the USA limits the generalisability of the findings to other contexts. International students in other countries may face unique challenges due to varying educational systems, cultural norms and immigration policies. Comparative studies across different countries could provide valuable insights into the global experiences of international students.

Lastly, the study did not explore the impact of institutional policies and practices on the prevalence and experiences of sexual harassment and mental health outcomes. Understanding how factors such as the availability of support services, reporting mechanisms and campus climate influence these issues could inform more effective interventions and policies. Future research should include a comprehensive examination of institutional factors to better understand their role in mitigating or exacerbating the problems identified.

An additional limitation concerns the measurement of suicidality. Suicide attempts represent a more severe behavioural outcome within the suicidality continuum and may not capture individuals experiencing ideation without behavioural enactment. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted specifically in relation to suicide attempts rather than broader suicidal thoughts. Future research should include both ideation and attempt measures to more comprehensively examine the spectrum of suicidality among international students. In summary, although this study offers important insights into the differential rates of victimisation among international students, addressing these limitations in future research will enhance the robustness of the findings and contribute to more effective strategies for supporting the well-being and success of this population.

Future research

Given the limitations and findings of the current study, several paths for future research are recommended to deepen the understanding of sexual harassment, stress and suicide attempt among international college students. Future research should employ longitudinal designs to establish better causal relationships between sexual harassment, stress and suicide attempt. Longitudinal studies can track changes over time, providing insights into the long-term impacts of sexual harassment on mental health and identifying critical periods for intervention. Subsequent studies should aim for more diverse and representative samples of international students. This includes ensuring a wide range of countries of origin, cultural backgrounds and lengths of stay in the host country. Such diversity would enhance the generalisability of the findings and provide a more comprehensive understanding of the varying experiences among international students. Future research should examine environmental and contextual factors, such as transportation patterns and campus infrastructure, that may influence exposure to sexual harassment. Public transit environments have been identified as potential high-risk spaces for harassment among university students,29 and international students may disproportionately rely on these systems. Future research must incorporate multi-method approaches to overcome the limitations of self-reported data. This could include clinical assessments, institutional records and qualitative methods such as interviews or focus groups. The triangulation of data from different sources would enhance the reliability and depth of the findings and open the potential for groundbreaking discoveries in international education. Exploring contextual factors and potential moderators is crucial for a nuanced understanding of the issues. Future studies should investigate the role of campus climate, availability of support systems, cultural differences and personal resilience in influencing the relationship between sexual harassment, stress and suicide attempt. Understanding these variables could help tailor interventions to specific contexts and populations. The development and validation of culturally sensitive measurement tools are essential. Future research should focus on adapting existing measures or creating new instruments that accurately capture the experiences of international students across different cultural contexts. Ensuring cultural appropriateness would improve the accuracy and relevance of the data collected. Conducting comparative studies across different countries and educational systems would provide valuable insights into the global experiences of international students. Such studies could highlight unique challenges and joint issues, informing international best practices for supporting international students’ well-being.

Future research should include a comprehensive examination of institutional factors, such as the presence and effectiveness of support services, reporting mechanisms and overall campus climate. Understanding how these factors influence the prevalence and experiences of sexual harassment and mental health outcomes can guide the development of more effective institutional policies and practices. Investigating the effectiveness of various interventions and prevention programmes is crucial for mitigating the negative impacts of sexual harassment on international students. Future research should evaluate existing programmes and develop new ones, focusing on culturally appropriate and gender-sensitive approaches. Longitudinal evaluations of these programmes would provide evidence of their long-term effectiveness. Exploring the role of psychological and social support mechanisms, including peer support, counselling services and community networks, is vital. Future studies should assess the availability, accessibility and utilisation of these supports among international students and their impact on reducing stress and suicide attempts. Research should also focus on the implications of various institutional, national and international policy frameworks. Understanding the effectiveness of different policy approaches can inform the development of more robust and supportive policies for international students. In conclusion, future research should adopt a multifaceted and culturally sensitive approach to address the complex issues of sexual harassment, stress and suicide attempts among international students. By doing so, it will be possible to develop more effective strategies and interventions to support this vulnerable population’s well-being and academic success.

Conclusion

The present study contributes to the limited but growing body of research on sexual harassment and violence experienced by international college students in the USA. This study underscores this population’s distinct vulnerabilities compared with their domestic peers. The findings reveal that international students, particularly women, report higher rates of sexual harassment and may experience significant barriers such as language difficulties, social isolation and limited access to support services, which exacerbate their challenges in navigating such experiences.

Though previous literature has broadly established that being female or younger increases the risk of sexual harassment, this study highlights explicitly how international students may be more vulnerable due to unique cultural, social and institutional factors. By prioritising the victimisation rates of international students, this research illuminates the critical need for tailored interventions and support systems that address the specific needs of international students. Future research should continue to build on this foundation by examining how cultural and contextual factors further shape the experiences of international students and how institutions can implement strategies to mitigate the risk of harassment in this population.
