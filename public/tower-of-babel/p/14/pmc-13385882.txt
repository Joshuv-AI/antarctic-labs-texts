Physics-guided monotone stacking for reliable prediction of pile load–settlement curves using SPT– profiles and geometry

ABSTRACT
AbstractAxial load–settlement (P–S) curves govern pile serviceability checks and support performance-based foundation decisions, yet full static load tests to high load levels remain expensive and are often unavailable. This paper presents PILE-Stack, a physics-guided, monotone stacked ensemble that predicts the complete P–S response of single piles from routine site investigation inputs, including SPT– depth profiles and pile geometry. The method combines mechanics-aware feature design with diverse level-0 experts and a monotone-constrained meta-learner, then applies a pile-wise isotonic projection that guarantees non-decreasing settlement with increasing load. The framework also quantifies uncertainty using quantile models that are calibrated with split-conformal prediction to obtain distribution-free prediction intervals. Evaluation follows a leakage-safe protocol using GroupKFold by PileID and a disjoint 20% PileID-wise holdout. On the external test set, PILE-Stack achieves , RMSE mm, and MAE mm. Service-range accuracy remains stable (SMAPE, WAPE), while curve-level agreement is strong (mean normalized AUC error ; mean DTW mm). The monotonicity audit reports zero predicted violations. Conformalized intervals deliver near-nominal pile-wise coverage, stay tight at working loads, and widen logically as nonlinearity increases. Ablation results show that including a lightweight mechanistic base and expert diversity reduces RMSE by 12% and MAE by 17% relative to a no-physics variant. Introduction

Load–displacement (load–settlement) curves from static pile load tests are the de facto basis for verifying axial capacity and serviceability of deep foundations. Yet, conducting tests to failure is expensive, time-consuming, and often impractical for production piles; many field programs terminate at working or proof loads and rely on curve interpretation or extrapolation for ultimate/load–service predictions 1,2. At the same time, site investigation campaigns routinely collect stratigraphic indicators such as standard penetration test (SPT) blow counts and effective vertical stress profiles (), which encode toe/shaft resistance trends but are underutilized in curve-level prediction. Translating these ubiquitous profiles into full load–displacement response could reduce testing burden while improving reliability of service-load settlement checks and capacity extrapolation.

Data-driven methods are natural candidates for this translation. However, off-the-shelf regressors struggle with two properties that are intrinsic to pile behavior: (i) monotonicity in load, i.e., settlement should not decrease as axial load increases, and (ii) mechanics coherence, i.e., features and model structure should reflect physically meaningful invariants (e.g., slenderness, near-toe stiffness surrogates). Absent these guardrails, models may interpolate well on average but produce implausible curve shapes, particularly in service regions where small errors translate into large serviceability risk. Recent work across structural mechanics argues for explicitly embedding physics and monotonicity into learning systems to prevent such pathologies 3–7. Comparable calls have emerged within structural optimization and metamaterials, where physics-guided ML and surrogate frameworks are increasingly favored for sample-efficient, constraint-aware prediction.

Three methodological gaps motivate this study. First, classical curve-fitting approaches (e.g., Chin/Kondner hyperbolic interpretations) are valuable for extrapolating tests not carried to failure 1,2, but they typically treat each test in isolation and do not leverage site-scale covariates (SPT/ profiles, geometry), limiting generalization across piles and sites. Second, many ML applications in geotechnical engineering emphasize point capacity (scalar) prediction from SPT/CPT or pressuremeter indices, rather than curve-level response; moreover, they rarely enforce physically required monotonicity in load or perform leakage-safe grouping when the same pile contributes multiple load steps 8,9. Third, despite the centrality of uncertainty to design verification, predictive intervals in prior work are often ad-hoc; few studies combine quantile regression with distribution-free calibration to guarantee nominal coverage under finite samples 10,11. Finally, common evaluations report only pointwise RMSE/MAE; they seldom assess curve similarity and physics plausibility–e.g., area-under-curve (AUC) distortion and dynamic-time-warping (DTW) distance–which better reflect engineering risk when designing from full response histories 7.

Monotone learning, isotonic regression, stacking, and conformal calibration are individually known methods; their role here is to solve a specific engineering problem in which multiple load steps from the same pile form a dependent curve, settlement must remain non-decreasing with load, and uncertainty must be expressed at both service and high-load regions. Compared with recent ML studies that focus on scalar pile capacity, maximum displacement, or seismic buckling response 12–14, the proposed framework contributes: (i) full axial P–S curve prediction from SPT– profiles and geometry; (ii) mechanics-aware profile descriptors that encode toe, shaft, stress-normalized, and load-normalized effects; (iii) leakage-safe out-of-fold stacking by PileID; (iv) monotone-in-load prediction through constrained learners and pile-wise isotonic projection; and (v) conformalized uncertainty intervals for serviceability-oriented interpretation.

Background and related work

Load–settlement behavior and hyperbolic/Chin models

Axial load tests on piles reveal a characteristic nonlinearity of the load–settlement (P–S) response driven by toe and shaft mobilization, soil nonlinearity, and interface frictional slip. With increasing load, the curve typically transitions from a near-linear regime at small strains to a strain-hardening phase and, in many cases, to an apparent asymptote as ultimate capacity is approached15. Early analytical interpretations captured this shape with hyperbolic relations. Kondner2 showed that the stress–strain behavior of clays can be approximated as hyperbolic, which motivated simple hyperbolic forms for P–S such as

where are curve parameters reflecting initial stiffness and curvature. Building on that idea, Chin1 proposed a practical extrapolation for pile load tests not carried to failure by exploiting the linearity of S/P versus S; the intersection yields an estimate of the ultimate load. Subsequent variants—including Decourt-type interpretations and load-transfer formulations—refined extrapolation and the separation of shaft and base contributions, but still rely on low-parameter curve families and smooth monotone shapes15.

These classical models remain attractive because they (i) encode mechanical plausibility (monotone S(P), diminishing stiffness), (ii) require few parameters, and (iii) can be fit robustly to sparse test data. However, they struggle to represent site-to-site variability tied to stratigraphy and stress history, and they provide limited pathways to inject profile information (e.g., SPT N across depth or effective stress profiles) except through ad hoc indices. This motivates hybrid approaches that preserve the shape priors of hyperbolic/Chin methods while leveraging data-driven learning to exploit richer subsurface descriptors.

ML for geotechnical prediction (SPT/pressuremeter/etc.)

Over three decades, machine learning (ML) has been used to predict pile capacity and settlements from in-situ tests such as SPT, CPT, and pressuremeter (PMT), as well as geometric and construction variables. Early studies demonstrated that artificial neural networks could outperform empirical correlations for driven pile capacity using SPT or CPT summaries16,17. Subsequent work expanded to kernel methods (SVR), tree ensembles (GBDT/Random Forest), and evolutionary models, often reporting improved accuracy over single-formula approaches and better adaptability to mixed foundations and soils18–20. Recent reviews in geotechnical ML highlight two persistent challenges: site-specific bias (models capturing local geology but generalizing poorly) and data curation (heterogeneous testing standards, noisy SPT records, and limited full-load curves)17.

SPT-based modeling is particularly appealing due to historical availability, but raw N-values integrate complex effects (energy ratio, hammer type, overburden). Meaningful features therefore normalize by effective stress or depth and summarize near-toe and along-shaft behavior via weighted averages, gradients, or convexity indices. PMT-derived moduli () and limit pressures () offer more direct stiffness proxies, yet are less widely available. In practice, studies that combine profile-aware features (e.g., near-toe SPT and descriptors) with flexible regressors tend to achieve stronger predictive performance and better physical interpretability than those relying solely on global scalars16,17,20. Still, most black-box models are not guaranteed to be physically plausible (e.g., strictly non-decreasing settlement with increasing load), which can lead to unrealistic load steps in predicted curves. This limitation has motivated shape-constrained or physics-guided ML strategies.

Recent geotechnical AI studies further show the value of optimized and explainable models for complex soil–structure response. Raja et al. 21 proposed a hybrid ensemble network for multiclass prediction of liquefaction-induced lateral spreading, showing the usefulness of ensemble learning for geohazard classification. El-Sekelly et al. 22 developed explainable AI models for predicting the unconfined compressive strength of bio-cemented sands and used feature-importance, SHAP, and partial-dependence analyses to connect predictions with geotechnical variables. Raja et al. 23 also demonstrated the value of Bayesian optimization and explainable machine learning for seismic site amplification prediction. These studies support the broader shift toward interpretable and optimized AI in geotechnical engineering. However, most recent models still focus on scalar outputs such as class labels, strength, displacement, or amplification factors. The present study addresses a complementary curve-level task by predicting the full axial pile load–settlement response while enforcing monotonicity and calibrated uncertainty.

Recent studies on pile–soil systems further show the increasing role of machine learning and explainable artificial intelligence in geotechnical response prediction. Zhang et al. 12 developed machine-learning models for maximum displacement prediction in soil–pile–superstructure systems subjected to laterally spreading ground, showing that data-driven models can support rapid seismic displacement assessment. Wang et al. 13 integrated AutoML and SHAP for explainable pile buckling capacity prediction in liquefiable strata, highlighting the importance of both predictive accuracy and feature-level interpretation. Zhang et al. 14 investigated the influence of inertial loads on pile buckling behavior in liquefiable deposits and showed that pile response is strongly affected by load interaction, slenderness, and soil state. These studies motivate the broader movement toward data-driven pile response assessment; however, they mainly address maximum displacement or buckling-related response under seismic or liquefaction conditions. The present study addresses a different but complementary problem: full monotone axial load–settlement curve prediction from routine SPT– profiles and pile geometry.

Monotone ML and physics-informed learning

Imposing known shape properties of the P–S curve ( ) can dramatically reduce implausible predictions and shrink hypothesis space. Classical order-restricted regression offers a principled way to enforce monotonicity via isotonic regression, which fits a non-decreasing function consistent with observations6. Modern ML toolkits also provide monotonicity constraints within gradient-boosted trees (e.g., LightGBM, CatBoost), enabling partial order constraints on selected features without sacrificing nonparametric flexibility5,24. For smooth low-bias fits, generalized additive models (GAMs) can incorporate monotone spline terms for P, yielding interpretable components while retaining nonlinearity in other predictors25. Post-hoc isotonic calibration applied per pile to further guarantees non-decreasing predictions even when upstream learners are imperfect.

Beyond shape constraints, physics-informed learning integrates mechanistic structure into training, either as soft penalties, architecture choices, or curated features. Karpatne et al.26 formalized theory-guided data science, arguing that hybridization can improve generalization in small-data regimes. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) embed governing equations into loss functions and have shown promise across mechanics; while PINNs are less common for routine pile design, the same principle—regularizing toward known physics—applies to settlement curves, where a light-weight mechanistic base (e.g., a global hyperbolic prior) can steer ensembles toward realistic shapes27. In practice, combining (i) monotone components in P, (ii) profile-aware geomechanical features, and (iii) a low-parameter mechanics base offers a pragmatic route to physics-consistent predictions with controlled complexity.

Uncertainty quantification in regression

For engineering decisions, point estimates are insufficient; calibrated uncertainty bounds are required for serviceability checks and risk communication. Quantile regression estimates conditional quantiles directly and provides asymmetric prediction intervals aligned with heteroscedastic data28,29. In tree ensembles, quantile loss can be optimized to obtain predictions. However, nominal quantiles may be miscalibrated out of sample. Conformal prediction offers distribution-free calibration that adjusts intervals on a held-out calibration set to achieve target coverage under minimal assumptions11,30. Conformalized quantile regression combines both ideas to produce efficient, finite-sample valid intervals around flexible base learners10.

For curve-level agreement, distance measures such as Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) can compare predicted and measured P–S trajectories while allowing minor misalignment31. Integral metrics (e.g., AUC difference) summarize energy-like discrepancies between curves and complement pointwise errors (RMSE/MAE) and plausibility checks (monotonicity violations). Together, these tools provide a comprehensive lens on accuracy, physical consistency, and uncertainty—criteria that are indispensable for reliability-oriented pile assessment in practice.

Dataset and preprocessing

Variable definitions and units

This study analyzes a curated database, from literatures32–34, of axial pile load tests in which each observation represents a (pile, load step) pair and all steps belonging to the same physical test are tied by a unique PileID. The predictor set was chosen to expose the principal drivers of settlement at service and near-ultimate states: (i) geometry (D, L) governing mobilized shaft and base areas; (ii) depthwise penetration resistance (SPT) as a proxy for in situ stiffness/strength; (iii) depthwise effective vertical stress capturing overburden and stress level effects; and (iv) the applied load P and a pile-type flag.

To balance fidelity with statistical robustness, the soil profile is represented by ten SPT segments and ten matching segments from ground surface to toe, plus an explicit toe SPT value. Pile grouping and curve reconstruction

Static load testing produces a discrete set of load–settlement pairs that, when ordered by the applied load, trace the characteristic nonlinear response of a foundation pile. Because settlement is a mechanically irreversible measure under monotonic loading, the population curve for a given pile is expected to be nondecreasing in S with P, aside from small experimental artefacts (e.g., seating corrections, dial-gauge jitter, re-zeroing). For supervised learning, faithfully reconstructing these per-pile curves from point clouds is a prerequisite: grouping must be robust, ordering must preserve the physical load sequence, and residual non-monotonicity should be diagnosed rather than silently absorbed. This subsection documents how the raw records were grouped into piles, ordered, and checked prior to modeling.

Each record was assigned to a unique PileID constructed as a stable hash of static descriptors (geometry, profile segments, and type), thereby linking all points collected for the same physical test (see Fig. 9(a)). Within each PileID, samples were sorted by the applied load P to obtain a discrete curve from first load step to the last. No smoothing or imputation was performed at this stage; the goal was a transparent reconstruction suitable for subsequent diagnostics and for the post-hoc monotonicity enforcement applied later in the pipeline in Post-hoc monotonicity enforcement.

Outlier detection and handling

Reliable prediction of the load–settlement response requires a training set that is representative yet not unduly influenced by spurious observations. In pile load testing, atypical readings may arise from bedding-in at low loads, dial-gauge slip, mis-synchronised load–settlement pairs, or transcription errors. Rather than aggressively pruning data, we follow a robust, distribution-aware strategy that (i) flags leverage points and (ii) employs modelling choices (monotone constraints, quantile losses, conformal calibration) that remain stable in their presence. This approach is consistent with modern robust statistics, in which resistant scale estimators (e.g., MAD) and high-breakdown covariance estimates (e.g., MCD) are used to detect outliers without assuming Gaussianity.

Train/validation/test splits (GroupKFold by PileID)

Accurate assessment of generalization requires that correlated measurements from the same physical experiment do not appear across training and validation/test folds. In pile load testing, a single static load test contributes several (P, S) points along one curve; these points share site conditions, instrumentation, and boundary effects. Randomly shuffling rows would therefore leak information from a pile’s early-load points into folds that are used to predict its late-load points, yielding optimistically biased error estimates. To prevent such leakage and to emulate the intended deployment scenario (predicting new piles at unseen sites), we adopt grouped resampling where the group key is the hashed PileID. Concretely, we reserve a disjoint holdout by unique PileID for final testing and perform 5-fold GroupKFold on the remaining for model selection, hyperparameter tuning, and uncertainty calibration.

Methodology

Classical hyperbolic/Chin interpretations capture the smooth, non-decreasing nature of pile load–settlement curves but do not leverage site-scale covariates (SPT and profiles) and struggle to generalize across piles; unconstrained black-box ML, in turn, can violate monotonicity and leak information when multiple load steps from the same test appear across folds. To address these gaps, PILE-Stack is a physics-informed, monotone stacked ensemble that (i) builds mechanics-aware descriptors from SPT– segments and geometry (e.g., L/D, toe-weighted and stress-normalized indices, depthwise gradients) and tags service loads; (ii) trains six complementary level-0 experts–GAM with an increasing spline in P, monotone GBDTs (LightGBM/CatBoost), kernel smoothers (SVR/KRR), and a tiny partially monotone MLP–using leakage-safe GroupKFold by PileID to generate out-of-fold (OOF) predictions; (iii) fits a monotone LightGBM meta-learner on the OOF table augmented with minimal physics features; (iv) applies a per-pile isotonic projection to guarantee non-decreasing ; and (v) produces distribution-free uncertainty via quantile meta-models calibrated by split conformal prediction. Evaluation uses a disjoint 20% PileID-wise hold-out, with pointwise (RMSE/MAE/, service SMAPE/WAPE), curve-level (AUC-error, DTW), monotonicity audits, and interval calibration/width diagnostics. An overview is shown in Fig. 8, and the grouping/splitting protocol in Fig. 9.

Mechanics-aware feature engineering and service tagging

Pile settlement under axial loading is governed by a coupled set of mechanisms: (i) the geometric stiffness of the pile (diameter D and embedded length L), (ii) the stress–strain response of the surrounding soil, which hardens with increasing effective confinement, and (iii) the distribution of resistance between toe bearing and shaft friction, which depends on the depthwise variability of penetration resistance and stress state. Any learning pipeline that treats the subsurface as a single homogeneous parameter risks conflating these effects and producing non-physical responses outside the calibration range. We therefore construct a compact set of mechanics-aware descriptors that summarize geometry, depthwise Standard Penetration Test (SPT) and effective vertical stress segments, and their physics-motivated interactions. These descriptors are used by all level-0 experts and the meta-learner (Sec. Methodology); they are deterministic functions of the raw inputs and introduce no oracle information.

Stacking and training protocol

Stacked generalization is a principled way to combine heterogeneous predictors by learning a second-stage model that maps the base predictions to the target variable4. For geotechnical load–settlement curves, stacking is attractive because different hypothesis classes capture different aspects of the physics: smooth trends (GAM), thresholded interactions (GBDTs), kernelized similarity (KRR/SVR), and a monotone neural response to load. However, naive stacking can overfit if the meta-learner “sees” the same samples the base models were trained on. To prevent leakage and to honor the curve structure of the data, we employ group-aware out-of-fold (OOF) generation with groups equal to PileID (i.e., all points from a given pile are kept together in the same fold). This ensures that within-fold validation mimics the deployment scenario in which unseen piles are predicted from previously calibrated models.

Post-hoc monotonicity enforcement

Measured load–settlement curves are theoretically non-decreasing with respect to applied load P for a stable foundation system; local reductions in S arise from reading noise, bedding disturbances, or digitization errors. Even when the level-0 experts and the meta-learner are constrained to be monotone in P in expectation, finite-sample irregularities can still yield slight local decreases along a predicted curve for a given pile. To guarantee physically admissible curves without re-training any model, we apply a post-hoc, per-pile shape-constrained projection based on isotonic regression. Isotonic regression computes the least-squares fit subject to a monotone order constraint, i.e., the -projection of the unconstrained prediction onto the cone of non-decreasing sequences6. It is parameter-free, admits an O(n) Pool-Adjacent-Violators Algorithm (PAVA) solution, and is widely used as a minimal-distortion calibrator in statistics and machine learning40,41.

The fitted mapping is a left-continuous step function; for unseen P values we linearly interpolate between steps to avoid plotting artifacts, and we clip predictions outside (same setting as in our implementation).

For loads that are not identical to the isotonic knots, the monotone map is evaluated by linear interpolation within the observed load range of that pile and by boundary clipping outside that range. The measured settlements are not used when applying this projection to validation or test predictions.

For training piles, we fit (19) to out-of-fold meta-predictions versus load, and apply the learned monotone map to the within-fold validation loads; for the external test holdout, we fit per-pile maps to the fold-trained meta-predictions and evaluate them at test loads. In both cases, the observed settlements S are not used by the isotonic corrector at prediction time, hence no leakage occurs. Practically, we use a standard isotonic solver with increasing=true and out_of_bounds=clip. The adjustment removes all strict decreases ( in Section 4.1) while changing the unconstrained predictions by the smallest squared amount needed to satisfy monotonicity.

The isotonic projection enjoys several desirable properties: (i) it is the closest monotone curve to the original predictions in the sense (non-expansive projection), (ii) it preserves the load ranking (isotone map), and (iii) it is shape-constrained but nonparametric–no additional tuning is required6. Because the transform acts only along the pilewise P–axis, it leaves the feature representation unchanged and therefore has negligible impact on split-conformal uncertainty coverage built on the meta-feature space (Section 3.5); empirically, coverage remains near the nominal level. When extremely irregular predictions occur (e.g., very sparse loads), a convex blend with a small can soften step plateaus if desired; we did not require this in our data.

Embedding monotonicity inside every base learner can be restrictive for some methods (e.g., kernel machines) and may increase bias; conversely, ignoring monotonicity risks implausible curves. The post-hoc projection provides a light-weight, theoretically grounded compromise: the ensemble is trained for accuracy, and admissibility is guaranteed afterwards by a minimal-distortion projection. This strategy is compatible with other shape-constrained components (e.g., monotone GAM terms and GBDT constraints) and is consistent with the broader literature on shape-constrained regression used to encode physical priors in data-driven models40,41.

Uncertainty quantification

Reliable decision-making in serviceability-controlled foundation design requires not only accurate point predictions of settlement but also well-calibrated predictive intervals that communicate the range of plausible responses under measurement noise, model misspecification, and geologic variability42. Classical variance-based intervals (e.g., from homoscedastic Gaussian assumptions) are ill-suited for the heteroscedastic and skewed error structure observed in load–settlement data (Section 2), where dispersion typically increases with load. We therefore adopt a two-stage, distribution-free approach that (i) learns conditional quantiles to capture heteroscedasticity and (ii) applies split-conformal calibration to guarantee marginal coverage under weak assumptions28,30,43.

Split-conformal calibration guarantees marginal coverage under exchangeability and is robust to model misspecification30,43. It does not, however, ensure conditional coverage at fixed nor pathwise coverage across an entire curve; those stronger notions are known to be unattainable without strong assumptions. Our group-aware split mitigates dependence within piles and aligns with the experimental design. In return, the final intervals are simple to compute, reproducible, and model-agnostic, which makes them attractive for engineering practice where transparent uncertainty bands are needed for serviceability assessments.

Evaluation protocol

Robust evaluation of data-driven settlement predictors must reflect both engineering relevance (serviceability at working loads) and the functional nature of load–settlement response curves. Following best practice in geotechnical reliability and structural performance assessment, we therefore combine pointwise accuracy metrics, curve-level similarity measures, plausibility checks against basic mechanics, and uncertainty diagnostics, all computed under a pile-wise grouping scheme that avoids information leakage42,46. Unless stated otherwise, all statistics are reported on the held-out test set with 1,000-sample nonparametric bootstrap confidence intervals (BCa) at the pile level47, and multiplicity is controlled using the Holm procedure where multiple pairwise comparisons are made48.

In addition to point estimates, we evaluate the 90% predictive intervals by empirical coverage (fraction of observations inside), average interval width (AIW), and quantile (pinball) loss at for sharpness; each is reported overall and in the service region. Conformal calibration ensures marginal coverage under exchangeability, so deviations from nominal coverage primarily reflect finite-sample variability and residual dependence43.

All metrics are aggregated pile-wise to respect grouping, then averaged across piles. For interval estimates of metrics (e.g., RMSE, AUC-Error), we use BCa bootstrap over piles47. For comparing model variants (Section Ablation and sensitivity), the null hypothesis of equal mean pile-wise metric is tested via bootstrap p-values with Holm correction48; in addition, we report the Hodges–Lehmann median difference and its bootstrap confidence interval as a robust effect size.

Evaluation splits (GroupKFold folds and the 20% pile-wise test holdout) are fixed by seed; all scripts export tabulated metrics and figures to versioned folders. This enables exact reproduction of the tables and plots cited in Section Results.

Extrapolation diagnostic

To quantify extrapolation capability, an additional top-load holdout diagnostic was defined at the pile-curve level. For every pile with at least four load steps, the records were sorted by load and the lower-load portion was used for fitting, while the highest-load portion was held out for evaluation. Specifically, the upper 20% of load steps in each eligible pile, with at least one point per pile, formed the extrapolation subset. This setting tests whether the model can infer higher-load settlement behavior from lower-load observations and the available SPT– and geometry descriptors.

The extrapolation subset was evaluated using the same pointwise and curve-level metrics as the main holdout: RMSE, MAE, , normalized AUC error, DTW, and predicted monotonicity violations. This diagnostic is stricter than random row-wise validation because the target points are located near the nonlinear end of the load–settlement curve, where settlement variability and curvature are larger. The extrapolation results are reported separately from the main 20% PileID-wise test set because the two tests answer different questions: the grouped holdout measures generalization to unseen piles, while the top-load diagnostic measures within-curve extrapolation toward higher load levels.

Uncertainty coverage and interval analysis

Reliable decisions for serviceability-controlled foundations require not only accurate point predictions but also calibrated uncertainty estimates that reflect data sparsity, heterogeneity across sites, and increasing dispersion at higher load levels. In PILE-Stack, uncertainty is produced by quantile meta-models and then calibrated by split-conformal adjustment, which–by construction–targets distribution-free coverage under exchangeability. Here we evaluate the empirical coverage and sharpness of the resulting prediction intervals (PIs) on the hold-out set, with a focus on per-pile behavior, load dependence, and practical implications for design checks.

Figure 14 summarizes the empirical coverage (fraction of observed points lying inside the nominal 90% PI) computed per pile. The distribution is strongly right-skewed toward high coverage: most piles cluster near unity, with a small minority between 0.5 and 0.8. Importantly, no pile exhibited systematic under-coverage below 0.50, which is encouraging for engineering use where missed exceedances at service levels are costly.

Interpretability: SHAP/ALE (global and per-pile)

Model credibility is a central requirement in geotechnical decision-making, where designers must trace predictions back to measurable quantities and established mechanics. We therefore interrogate PILE-Stack with additive feature attributions and dependence analyses. Specifically, we compute Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) on the meta-learner to decompose each prediction into contributions from its inputs (base-expert OOF predictions and selected physics features)44,51. SHAP combines desirable properties (local accuracy, consistency, missingness) and has been widely adopted for diagnostic scrutiny in structural and geotechnical ML. To complement attribution scores with response curves that are less confounded by feature correlation, we evaluate Accumulated Local Effects (ALE) profiles for key variables. Together, SHAP (how much each variable moves the prediction) and ALE (the sign and shape of effects) provide a coherent interpretability protocol for stacked ensembles.

Table 17 ranks meta features by mean absolute SHAP. Two observations stand out. First, the most influential inputs to the meta-learner are the OOF predictions from the monotone MLP and the monotone LightGBM experts (mean and 5.87), followed by the raw load P (X24) and the spline-smoothed GAM. This indicates that (i) strongly nonlinear experts capture diverse aspects of load–settlement behavior and (ii) the meta-learner leverages them more than any single physics descriptor. Second, the stress and toe descriptors (SIG_toe, SPT_toe) and the geometric ratio L/D have consistent but smaller contributions, acting as modifiers of the load-driven response rather than primary drivers. The negligible contribution from KRR (near zero) suggests limited incremental value once tree-based and neural experts are present. These results validate the design choice of assembling heterogeneous experts and then enforcing monotonicity at both base and meta levels.

Impact of monotone constraints

Monotonicity of the load–settlement response is a first-principles property: for quasi-static compression the measured settlement cannot decrease with increasing load, up to measurement noise. Purely data-driven regressors may, however, introduce local reversals in regions with scarce training points or high leverage, which is undesirable for serviceability checks and capacity extrapolation. The PILE–Stack design therefore enforces monotonicity in two complementary ways: (i) base learners with built–in monotone shape constraints in the load coordinate (GAM spline with increasing constraint; tree ensembles with a monotone vector on P) and (ii) a post–hoc per–pile isotonic projection of the meta prediction onto the cone of non–decreasing functions). On the external test set this strategy eliminates all monotonicity violations (MonotoneViolations_pred_sum), while two ground–truth curves still exhibit small non–monotone segments due to experimental scatter (see curve metrics list). This guarantees physically plausible predicted curves without sacrificing accuracy.

These diagnostics validate the proposed two-tier monotonicity strategy: the constrained base experts and meta-learner successfully capture the global physical trend, while the isotonic post-processor provides a lightweight, mathematically rigorous safeguard that ensures mechanical admissibility without washing out learned subsurface interactions.

In PILE-Stack, level-0 experts span smooth additive structure (GAM), tree-based local interactions (LightGBM, CatBoost), kernel smoothers (KRR, SVR), and a small monotone neural branch, which are then fused by a constrained meta-learner.

Robustness to outliers and distributional shifts

Full–scale pile tests can contain occasional instrumentation glitches, reaction frame slippage, or soil heterogeneities that yield heavy–tailed residuals at the point level and rare deviations from ideal trends at the curve level. Beyond using robust metrics (WAPE/SMAPE at service) and the physics–guided constraints, we probed robustness in two ways. First, we examined the residual distribution against a Gaussian reference; second, we inspected performance heterogeneity across load/response strata and piles.

Figure 23 shows that the central 90% of residuals conform closely to a light–tailed distribution (trimmed curve nearly linear), whereas the extreme upper quantiles bend upward, signaling a modest heavy–tail due to a few high–load, toe–dominated tests (cf. the AUC–DTW outliers in Fig. 13). This behavior is expected in geotechnical load tests where end bearing mobilization and setup effects can trigger large settlements over short load increments. Because prediction intervals are produced by split–conformal calibration on a held–out fold, coverage remains valid under such distributional quirks. Indeed, the reliability diagram (Fig. 15) stays near the nominal 90% line, and the per–pile coverage histogram (Fig. 14) shows most piles between 0.7 and 1.0 coverage, with wider intervals automatically appearing at higher loads (sharpness vs. load in Fig. 16).

Finally, GroupKFold by PileID ensures that the model never sees fragments of the same curve during validation, mimicking site–level shifts encountered in practice. The error–by–quantile analysis indicates that while RMSE increases with settlement magnitude–as unavoidable when moving from service to near–capacity regimes–the monotone meta–learner avoids catastrophic extrapolation. Taken together, the QQ analysis, conformal reliability, and group–wise validation provide convergent evidence that PILE–Stack is resilient to outliers and modest distribution drift, an essential property for deployment in heterogeneous site portfolios.

Discussion

Engineering implications for serviceability

Serviceability verification in axial piles is governed by reliable estimates of settlement at working loads and by credible uncertainty bands that reflect site heterogeneity and limited test data. The present study set out to deliver curve-consistent, monotone predictions from ubiquitous SPT– profiles while quantifying uncertainty in a distribution-free manner. The results in Section Results indicate that the proposed PILE-Stack achieves high fidelity on an external, PileID-wise holdout (RMSE mm, ; Table 12), reproduces full curve shapes with small AUC and DTW discrepancies (Fig. 13), and eliminates non-physical reversals via two layers of monotonicity control. The prediction intervals (PIs) are near-nominal at the pile level and widen sensibly with load (Figs. 14–16), which is essential for decision making.

The following recommendations translate the findings into design practice:

Report point and interval at the service load. For each pile, query the ensemble at the project’s service load (or at when only proof loads are available) and report the triplet . On the holdout set, service-region errors are modest (SMAPE , WAPE ), and median PI widths remain mm up to the 70th load percentile (Fig. 16), providing a tight band for SLS checks.

Deploying it alongside routine SPT logging can reduce the scope of costly to-failure tests while maintaining defensible safety margins.

Limitations and scope of applicability

While the results are encouraging, several limitations bound the domain of validity and suggest caution when extrapolating:

Dataset size, geological diversity, installation metadata, and SPT correction limits. The harmonized database contains 848 load-step records from 156 unique pile tests compiled from published sources. Although this size is useful for pile-wise curve modelling, it remains limited relative to the full diversity of soil deposits, stress histories, installation methods, groundwater conditions, pile construction procedures, and regional ground conditions encountered in practice. The source studies did not consistently provide complete soil-type labels, construction-method categories, hammer energy ratios, borehole diameter, sampler details, groundwater correction procedures, or standardized SPT correction metadata. Therefore, the present model uses the reported SPT values and effective-stress descriptors without claiming soil-type- or construction-method-stratified validation. The results should be interpreted as pile-wise generalization within the available SPT– and geometry domain, not as universal geotechnical generalization across all geological and construction regimes.

Outside this scope, it should be viewed as a baseline that warrants site-specific recalibration.

Future work: multi-task curve models, site-transfer learning

Two avenues emerge naturally from the findings.

(i) Multi-task and functional curve models. Rather than predicting each (P, S) pair independently, future models can treat the entire S(P) trajectory as a task and learn shared, monotone function bases across piles. Examples include (a) monotone spline dictionaries with pile-specific coefficients learned via sparse multi-task regression; (b) Gaussian process or neural-ODE surrogates constrained to ; and (c) sequence-to-sequence architectures that ingest depthwise profiles and emit full curves. Such functional learners can natively regularize poorly sampled regions, reduce PI inflation near the last step, and support direct extraction of service stiffness or hyperbolic parameters. Extending conformal calibration to functional coverage (e.g., band conformal or Mondrian/stratified variants) would provide pathwise uncertainty guarantees tailored to curve design.

(ii) Site-transfer and data-efficiency. A practical challenge is adapting to a new site with few or no full-scale tests. The stacked architecture is amenable to transfer by (a) freezing global experts and fine-tuning a lightweight, site-specific meta-layer; (b) using hierarchical pooling that shares information across geologically similar sites while allowing local offsets; and (c) employing active learning to suggest the next most informative load step or borehole depth segment. When richer in-situ data are available (CPT/PMT), multi-modal fusion at the feature level can further reduce epistemic uncertainty. Finally, developing site-level conformal calibration–using a small calibration set from the target site–would turn marginal into locally valid intervals with minimal additional testing effort.

Overall, these directions aim to turn PILE-Stack from an accurate predictor into a portable, design companion: one that reasons over full curves, adapts quickly to new ground conditions, and communicates uncertainty at the level engineers make decisions.

Conclusions

This paper presents PILE-Stack, a physics-guided learning framework that predicts full axial pile load–settlement curves from SPT– profiles and pile geometry. The method targets a key practical gap: routine investigations provide rich depth information, yet design workflows often lack reliable curve-level predictions when full load tests are not feasible. A monotone stacked ensemble, combined with pile-wise isotonic projection, enforces the essential mechanical requirement that settlement does not decrease with increasing load. The resulting predictions remain physically admissible without sacrificing accuracy.

Leakage-safe testing using GroupKFold by PileID and a disjoint 20% pile-wise holdout shows strong generalization within the analyzed database.

Future work should extend the framework toward earthquake engineering use-cases by incorporating cyclic and dynamic effects, including degradation under repeated loading, soil–pile interaction changes, and lateral demand coupling. Adding inputs such as CPT, shear-wave velocity (), groundwater conditions, and liquefaction-related indicators can further improve robustness across seismic site classes. A field-oriented validation on independent projects, including site-to-site transfer tests, can strengthen confidence for deployment in performance-based foundation assessment and post-event serviceability screening.
