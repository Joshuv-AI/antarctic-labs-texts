WABIP/AABIP statement on therapeutic indications of medical thoracoscopy for benign pleural disease
Authors: Udit Chaddha, Abhinav Agrawal, Uzair Ghori, Bertin D. Salguero, Nick A. Maskell, Kazuhiro Yasufuku, Samira Shojaee, Labib Gilles Debiane, Horiana B. Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Medical thoracoscopy (MT) is a minimally invasive procedure with a well-established role in the diagnosis and management of malignant pleural disease; however, its therapeutic applications in benign pleural conditions remain variably adopted and inconsistently defined. In response to increasing clinical utilisation and emerging evidence, the World Association for Bronchology and Interventional Pulmonology (WABIP) and the American Association for Bronchology and Interventional Pulmonology (AABIP) convened a multidisciplinary, international expert panel to develop a consensus statement examining the therapeutic role of MT in benign pleural disease. Six clinically relevant PICO (patient, intervention, comparison and outcome) questions were formulated to guide a systematic literature review of PubMed, Scopus and Cochrane databases from 1980 through 2025. Evidence was appraised using standardised risk-of-bias tools and consensus was achieved using a modified Delphi methodology. This consensus statement summarises the available evidence regarding MT across a spectrum of benign pleural conditions, including recurrent benign pleural effusions, primary and secondary spontaneous pneumothorax, empyema, and tuberculous pleural disease. Emphasis is placed on procedural outcomes, complication profiles and comparative effectiveness relative to alternative medical and surgical approaches. The document also highlights key considerations related to patient selection, procedural complexity and the influence of operator expertise and local resources on clinical outcomes. Given the heterogeneity of underlying disease processes and the predominance of observational data, this work is presented as a consensus statement rather than a formal clinical practice guideline. Overall, it aims to consolidate current knowledge, identify gaps in evidence and provide a practical framework to inform multidisciplinary decision-making and future research on the therapeutic use of MT in benign pleural disease.

Introduction

Medical thoracoscopy (MT), also referred to as pleuroscopy, has long been established as a minimally invasive, effective and well-tolerated procedure for the diagnosis and management of pleural diseases. It is typically performed under local anaesthesia and conscious sedation, allowing for both diagnostic and therapeutic interventions within the pleural cavity [1]. While its role in malignant pleural effusions is well-defined and widely practiced [2–4], its therapeutic utility in benign pleural diseases remains under-recognised and variably applied across regions. Recent evidence and evolving clinical experience suggest that MT offers significant advantages in the treatment of a range of benign pleural conditions [5–8], including complicated parapneumonic effusions, empyema, persistent air leak and potentially other causes of recurrent transudative effusions, amongst others. This joint statement by the World Association for Bronchology and Interventional Pulmonology (WABIP) and the American Association for Bronchology and Interventional Pulmonology (AABIP) aims to consolidate current knowledge, standardise indications and foster evidence-based guidance on the therapeutic applications of MT in benign pleural disease. Given the limited high-quality evidence around the discussed topics, the panel has decided to present this work as a consensus statement and not as guideline recommendations. We acknowledge that MT is vastly skill- and training-dependent with wide variation in local expertise and that each clinical scenario is unique in and by itself. In addition, the availability of rigid thoracoscopy maybe necessary for therapeutic interventions, which may not be feasible with semi-rigid instruments alone. Therefore, the extrapolation of our recommendations to each specific clinical scenario can only be best evaluated by qualified providers at the patient's bedside, in multidisciplinary settings.

Methodology

A multidisciplinary international expert panel of 18 members participated in the consensus development process. The panel composition included 15 pulmonologists and three thoracic surgeons representing 18 institutions across nine countries. This diverse geographic and specialty representation ensured comprehensive expertise in thoracoscopy and benign pleural disease management while enhancing the international applicability of the final recommendations. The expert panel formulated six clinically relevant patient, intervention, comparison and outcome (PICO) questions to guide the systematic literature review and evidence synthesis. These questions were aimed to address key therapeutic indications for MT across the spectrum of benign pleural diseases. A core writing group consisting of seven members (U. Chaddha, A. Agrawal, U. Ghori, B.D. Salguero, L.G. Debiane, C. Lamb and P. Lee) coordinated the systematic review process, synthesised evidence into summary tables (supplementary table S1), drafted initial recommendations and prepared the manuscript for panel review. The systematic review was conducted by two primary reviewers (U. Ghori and B.D. Salguero) who independently performed study selection, data extraction and risk of bias assessments. A third reviewer (U. Chaddha) served as adjudicator when disagreements arose regarding risk of bias ratings or study inclusion decisions. Randomised controlled trials were evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool. Nonrandomised studies, including observational and comparative effectiveness studies, were assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale.

A comprehensive and systematic literature search was conducted across four electronic databases, namely PubMed (n=7888), Scopus (n=816), Cochrane Reviews (n=2) and Cochrane Trials (n=271), covering the period from 1980 to 2025. Two medical librarians independently developed database-specific search strategies using appropriate controlled vocabulary (medical subject heading (MeSH) terms, Emtree) and text word combinations relevant to MT, pleurodesis, pneumothorax, empyema and tuberculous effusions. Search strategies were peer-reviewed following the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies guidelines to ensure comprehensiveness and methodological quality. A systematic search and exclusion process resulted in the inclusion of 138 studies from database searches, with an additional 46 studies identified through grey literature sources, yielding a total of 184 studies for evidence synthesis (figure 1).

The expert panel employed a modified Delphi technique to achieve consensus on clinical recommendations. An initial draft of recommendations with supporting rationales based on the evidence summaries was circulated amongst the panel on 19 October 2025. The draft underwent iterative revision based on panel feedback, to which all members of the panel contributed. A virtual consensus conference was convened on 19 November 2025 with 74% of panellists participating. During this meeting, real-time suggestions were collected and incorporated into the recommendations. Subsequently, an electronic survey was distributed to all panel members to rate each recommendation using a five-point Likert scale. Consensus was defined a priori as achieving at least 70% agreement (ratings of 4 or 5) with a minimum response rate of 80%. If consensus was not achieved, the plan was to conduct a second meeting to discuss only the unresolved questions. The survey achieved 100% participation and consensus was reached on all recommendations after the first meeting (table 1).

Rationale for recommendation

Most pleural effusions are caused by benign (nonmalignant) conditions [9]; however, the term “benign” is somewhat misleading, as effusions from end-organ failure are associated with a significant 1-year mortality rate [10]. The term BPE encompasses a wide range of aetiologies, including heart failure, end-stage renal disease, hepatic hydrothorax, post-surgical effusions and asbestos-related effusions without evidence of malignancy. Given the diverse causes and clinical scenarios associated with BPEs, a single set of generalised recommendations is insufficient. A more detailed diagnostic and management framework is available in a 2024 European Respiratory Society (ERS) statement [11]. Chylothorax, which involves the accumulation of chyle in the pleural space, represents a particularly challenging subtype of BPE. This specific PICO question does not explore the definitive management of recurrent chylothorax, as that topic has already been addressed in a separate 2024 consensus statement [12].

Generally, definitive treatment for organ-failure related BPEs is considered in patients with medically refractory effusions that are frequently recurrent. “Refractory” typically refers to effusions that persist despite the use of optimal medical therapy and decisions regarding this classification should be made collaboratively in a multidisciplinary setting involving the relevant specialists, such as cardiologists for heart failure. The definition of “frequent recurrence” should also consider the patient's preferences, symptoms and quality of life.

Among commonly employed strategies for managing recurrent BPEs are IPCs and pleurodesis using talc, either in the form of slurry instilled through a chest tube or as talc insufflation (poudrage) performed during a thoracoscopy. This PICO question specifically evaluates the efficacy and safety of talc slurry instillation via a chest tube versus talc insufflation via MT. To date, there are no high-quality studies directly comparing the two approaches in the context of BPEs. Available data are limited to small, largely retrospective studies, which suggest comparable success rates and similar complication profiles. These findings are consistent with higher-quality evidence in malignant pleural effusions [13, 14]. In clinical practice, either talc slurry or insufflation may be appropriate depending on the patient's specific condition. Talc insufflation is generally preferred when MT is being performed for pleural biopsy in case where there is diagnostic uncertainty or when imaging (such as computed tomography (CT) or bedside ultrasound) suggests that full evacuation of the pleural space by chest tube alone may be inadequate. In the presence of extensive septations on thoracic ultrasound, MT for adhesiolysis maybe preferred. Conversely, in cases without diagnostic uncertainty, particularly when an existing standard or tunnelled chest tube has already achieved adequate drainage and pleural apposition, talc slurry is typically favoured for its simplicity and effectiveness. It is essential to use graded talc to minimise the risk of systemic and pulmonary inflammatory reactions [15].

Certain BPE subtypes require additional considerations. Hepatic hydrothorax poses a unique challenge, as complication and recurrence rates appear higher regardless of intervention, based on low-quality evidence. In such cases, pleurodesis should be reserved for patients who have received an adequate trial of medical therapy and after other management options, such as TIPS, liver transplantation or IPC, have been discussed in a multidisciplinary context [16]. Post-surgical BPEs, which may develop early or late after cardiac or thoracic procedures (including transplantation), are typically not recurrent. When recurrence does occur and definitive intervention is considered, management is often complicated by adhesions or altered anatomy. These cases require coordination with the original surgical team, as MT may be technically difficult. Recurrent BPEs associated with biopsy-confirmed nonspecific pleuritis (NSP) warrant follow-up for at least 1 year to ensure that underlying malignancy, particularly mesothelioma, has not been missed. Studies indicate an approximate 8% risk of missed malignancy, depending on regional prevalence of mesothelioma [11]. Until this observation period has passed, pleurodesis should generally be deferred. The use of immunosuppressive therapy in NSP-related BPEs remains unclear and is still under investigation. However, in connective tissue disease-related pleural effusions, commonly seen in conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus, immunosuppressive medications are often used alongside diuretics as part of optimal medical therapy [17]. The role of pleurodesis in these cases may be considered, in consultation with the treating rheumatologist, when effusions are recurrent despite appropriate medical management.

Rationale for recommendation

This document does not delve in detail into the precise timing of recurrence prevention strategies in patients with primary spontaneous pneumothorax. However, the panel broadly supports existing guideline recommendations, which suggest considering recurrence prevention in individuals experiencing a first recurrence, in those with high-risk occupations (such as pilots or divers) or when a persistent air leak is present [18–20].

Given the more robust evidence base supporting VATS and that patients with primary pneumothorax are usually young and otherwise healthy good surgical candidates, the panel recommends offering VATS as the first-line option for recurrence prevention, despite its slightly higher morbidity compared to MT. VATS is preferred over thoracotomy due to its similar efficacy and lower reported morbidity. When access to thoracic surgery is limited, talc insufflation during MT may be considered. However, pain, which is the most frequently reported complication [21], often occurs soon after talc insufflation. Therefore, if MT is pursued, appropriate analgesia sedation should be provided. For patients with recurrent primary spontaneous pneumothorax who are hospitalised with a chest tube in place and are being considered for pleurodesis, talc slurry instillation is also a reasonable alternative when thoracic surgical access is limited. This approach is supported by extrapolating evidence from MPE studies suggesting comparable efficacy between talc slurry and insufflation [13, 14].

While pleurodesis and pulmonary resection are commonly performed during VATS, current evidence does not clearly establish the individual benefit of either intervention or whether combining them provides superior outcomes. As such, the panel does not issue a specific recommendation regarding the exact type of intervention to be performed during VATS. This position aligns with the 2023 clinical practice statements from the ERS, the European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery and the European Society of Thoracic Surgeons [22], as well as the 2023 British Thoracic Society guidelines [20]. The relative merits of pleurectomy versus pleurodesis also remain uncertain. Reflecting our panel's approach, a 2023 guideline from the French Speaking Society of Respiratory Diseases, the French Society of Emergency Medicine, the French Intensive Care Society, the French Society of Anesthesia and Intensive Care Medicine and the French Society of Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgery recommended mechanical and/or chemical pleurodesis as a first-line procedure over pleurectomy [19]. A 2023 meta-analysis suggested that combining mechanical and chemical pleurodesis may be more effective than mechanical pleurodesis alone [23]. However, whether combined pleurodesis offers advantages over chemical pleurodesis alone is still unclear. Talc should be used as the agent of choice for chemical pleurodesis [24], but it is important to remember that it can cause dense adhesions, which may complicate future thoracic surgeries, increasing the likelihood of converting to thoracotomy. Additionally, granulomatous reactions from talc can lead to false-positive fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography findings [25].

Smoking cessation remains a critical component of pneumothorax management. A clear and dose-dependent relationship exists between smoking and the risk of developing pneumothorax [26, 27]. A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that smoking cessation was associated with a four-fold reduction in recurrence risk (OR 0.26, 95% CI 0.10–0.63) [28]. Although it remains unclear whether smoking cessation at the time of VATS reduces recurrence rates, providing support and resources for smoking cessation should be a routine component of patient care.

Rationale for recommendation

In general, the least invasive and safest approach should be prioritised, in line with patient preferences and clinical judgment. Our recommendation of talc slurry over talc insufflation in patients with SSP is based on several considerations. First, while the recurrence rate in SSP cannot be generalised due to its dependence on underlying lung parenchymal architecture, including the type and extent of injury, as well as factors such as smoking status (current or former), it is generally high. Other clinical guidelines suggest that recurrence prevention may be considered during or after the first episode [20], a position with which this panel agrees. Most patients experiencing an episode of SSP are hospitalised with a chest tube in place, providing an opportunity to administer talc slurry without requiring additional invasive procedures. Second, there is currently no comparative data evaluating the efficacy of MT with talc insufflation versus talc slurry specifically for recurrence prevention in SSP. However, based on pleurodesis success rates in both benign and malignant pleural effusions, the overall effectiveness of these approaches may be similar in patients with SSP [13]. Third, many patients with SSP have reduced pulmonary reserve and even the minimal sedation required for MT carries some risk in this population. All treatment options, including VATS for patients who are fit to undergo general anaesthesia and single-lung ventilation, should be thoroughly discussed with the patient. In patients with a persistent air leak undergoing surgery to fix the leak concomitant pleurodesis for recurrence prevention must also be performed. As pain is the most common complication with talc slurry instillation or talc insufflation, adequate pain management should be ensued in all cases.

Rationale for recommendation

MT has been utilised to evacuate the pleural space in patients with empyema; however, reported success rates vary significantly, ranging from 75% to 95%, even in cases limited to stage II (fibrinopurulent) empyema [29–35]. Importantly, the definition of “success” across studies is inconsistent, and a notable proportion of patients ultimately require further intervention, including VATS, repeat MT or IET following an initial MT (detailed in PICO 5). The panel acknowledges that MT for empyema is inherently more complex, and outcomes depend heavily on both appropriate patient selection and the technical skill of the operator. If MT is considered, it should be performed early during stage II empyema, before the development of a significant visceral pleural rind, a decision which necessitates timely and accurate imaging with both ultrasound and chest CT. Nevertheless, the boundary between stage II and III empyema remains indistinct and reliably differentiating these stages remains a clinical challenge.

In contrast, IET is a noninvasive, readily available and well-studied alternative with consistently high success rates. Multiple trials and observational series, including MIST-2 and subsequent prospective and retrospective studies, have demonstrated approximately 90% success in avoiding a surgical intervention [31, 36–42]. While pain is a common side-effect, major bleeding occurs in only around 4% of patients [43]. A single small, randomised trial comparing IET with MT found a shorter length of stay in the MT group (2 versus 4 days, p=0.026) [31]; however, this result should be interpreted cautiously, as length of stay was calculated from the time of intervention rather than from randomisation. Future multi-centre, randomised trials should compare the efficacy of MT versus IET in patients with stage II empyema, in avoiding surgery and improving long-term patient-centred outcomes. Given its higher efficacy, ease of administration and favourable safety profile, the panel recommends IET over MT in patients with empyema who fail initial management with tube thoracostomy and antibiotics. In this context, “failure” is a subjective determination, often characterised by persistent sepsis or significant residual pleural collection on imaging. Importantly, the decision to initiate IET should be made early and response to therapy should be assessed within 36–48 h. Delayed intervention is a major contributor to VATS-to-open conversion, which is associated increased morbidity [44].

When choosing a fibrinolytic to pair with DNase, we suggest tPA, since it is the only agent studied in combination therapy [42]. In certain countries where DNase is unavailable, treatment becomes more complex. While a Cochrane review suggested that fibrinolytic monotherapy may reduce surgical referral rates, this finding was not upheld in analyses limited to studies with low or unclear risk of bias (OR 0.48, 95% CI 0.18–1.25; four studies; 599 participants; I2=58%) [45]. Furthermore, both the MIST-1 (streptokinase versus placebo) and MIST-2 (alteplase versus placebo) trials demonstrated no significant difference in surgical referral rates between fibrinolytic monotherapy and placebo [42, 46]. As such, the panel does not recommend fibrinolytic monotherapy in patients who have failed initial tube thoracostomy and antibiotic therapy. In situations where DNase is unavailable or contraindicated, early referral for VATS should be prioritised. Where thoracic surgical access is limited, MT may be considered as a possible option or as a salvage option after IET nonresponse, e.g. persistent fever/sepsis, inadequate drainage or no radiographic improvement 24–48 h after initiation. When surgery is not an option and DNase is unavailable, fibrinolytic monotherapy may be attempted, but clinicians should note that this practice is not backed by randomised evidence. DNase monotherapy is not recommended, given that the MIST-2 trial [42] reported higher surgical referral rates compared with placebo.

Despite the lack of high-certainty evidence in this domain, the panel agreed that the strength of the available data, highlighting the superior efficacy and safety of IET compared to MT, supports a strong recommendation in favour of IET based on the clear potential for benefit and the apparent lack of significant harm. While cost considerations are an essential component of decision-making, costs vary substantially across institutions and geographic settings. Rationale for recommendation

The utility of MT in empyema management is largely confined to breaking adhesions, evacuating pleural fluid or pus, and performing limited decortication of fibrinopurulent exudate from the parietal pleura. In patients with a well-formed visceral pleural rind requiring decortication, VATS performed by a thoracic surgeon is necessary. Although MT has the advantage of being a single-port procedure that can be performed under conscious/moderate sedation, its reported success rates in empyema range between 75% and 95%, even in patients with stage II disease [29–35]. Patients who fail MT invariably require a VATS, or, in nonoperative candidates, a re-do MT or a trial or IET (if not already done). In contrast, VATS has historically been regarded as the gold standard surgical intervention for empyema, with generally reported high success rates in noncomparative, predominantly retrospective studies [34, 47–62]. Based on this, despite the lack of high-certainty evidence in this domain, the panel supports a strong recommendation in favour of VATS. The goal of VATS is to break adhesions, completely evacuate the pleural space and facilitate lung re-expansion. However, the morbidity associated with decortication can increase significantly when conversion to open thoracotomy is required. Delayed surgical referral is a major contributor to such conversions [44]. Therefore, the panel emphasises the importance of early determination of medical management failure, ideally within 3 days of initiating tube thoracostomy. The definition of treatment failure in this context is inherently subjective but is typically based on persistent sepsis or the presence of significant residual pleural collections despite tube drainage and antibiotics or IET. VATS, however, requires general anaesthesia and single-lung ventilation, limiting its use in some patients. Early MT may be considered in those patients with suspected stage II empyema who are not suitable for VATS, particularly when performed by experienced operators and when IET has failed.

This PICO question specifically addresses the role of MT versus VATS in patients with empyema requiring a surgical intervention, but it does not explore whether VATS or IET should be the preferred first-line salvage strategy following failure of antibiotics and tube thoracostomy. The optimal choice between IET and VATS as first salvage remains unresolved, with limited comparative data currently available from only two feasibility randomised controlled trials [53, 63]. Until more definitive evidence emerges, the panel suggests initiating a trial of IET with early surgical referral for VATS in patients who do not respond to initial tube thoracostomy and antibiotics. This approach mirrors the rationale for preferring IET over MT, as outlined in PICO 4: IET is easier to administer, widely available in healthcare settings and has demonstrated high efficacy (∼90% success in avoiding surgery) across multiple studies [31, 36–42].

Rationale for recommendation

The paucibacillary nature of tuberculous pleural effusions presents significant diagnostic challenges. Conventional pleural fluid tests, including Mycobacterium tuberculosis PCR, acid-fast bacilli (AFB) staining, cultures and biomarkers, have limited sensitivity and specificity. As a result, pleural biopsy is often required to establish a definitive diagnosis in patients with suspected tuberculous pleural effusions [64]. Image-guided pleural biopsies using Abrams or cutting needles have demonstrated high diagnostic yields [65, 66]. However, thoracoscopic biopsies performed under direct visualisation offer yields approaching 90–100% [65, 67–71]. An added benefit of biopsy is the ability to culture AFB for drug sensitivity testing, which is especially critical for patients with prior tuberculosis (TB) treatment or in regions with high rates of multidrug-resistant TB.

Given the high diagnostic yield, favourable safety profile and less invasive nature of MT compared to VATS, the panel recommends MT over VATS for patients with suspected tuberculous pleural effusions in whom a biopsy is necessary. While high-certainty evidence is limited, the volume of supporting literature and clinical experience justifies a strong recommendation due to the clear potential for benefit and minimal associated harm. Uniportal VATS and nonintubated VATS may be suitable alternatives in such settings. When MT is not available, image-guided pleural biopsies also are a reasonable alternative [72]. Blind pleural biopsies, particularly when performed by inexperienced operators, should be avoided due to lower diagnostic yield and increased risk of complications [73].

In cases where ultrasonographic assessment reveals free-flowing pleural fluid, suggesting a simple pleural space, thoracoscopic clearance is easy to achieve. In such scenarios, especially when needle drainage is inadequate, in symptomatic patients or those who need a diagnostic biopsy is indicated, the panel suggests using MT over VATS. However, when tuberculous empyema is suspected or confirmed, and thoracoscopic intervention is necessary, the panel recommends VATS over MT. This preference is particularly relevant when imaging or clinical assessment suggests the presence of a thick visceral pleural rind with significant trapped lung or a persistent air leak. In this context, “significant” trapped lung is a subjective determination, often characterised by, but not limited to, persistent sepsis or a symptomatic residual pleural collection on imaging. These cases are often surgically complex and are best managed with VATS, which has demonstrated success rates around 90% in this context [55, 74, 75].

Future directions

Despite growing interest and supportive observational data, the therapeutic use of MT in benign pleural disease warrants further high-quality research to refine its indications, optimise patient selection and define long-term outcomes. Randomised controlled trials comparing MT with traditional surgical approaches and nonsurgical modalities are essential to establish its efficacy, safety and cost-effectiveness across diverse healthcare settings. In addition, the development of international registries and consensus-based procedural protocols will facilitate greater standardisation and quality improvement. Future research should also explore innovations in thoracoscopic instrumentation, sedation techniques, and real-time imaging to expand the scope and accessibility of this valuable intervention. The WABIP/AABIP encourages ongoing collaboration between interventional pulmonologists, thoracic surgeons, and allied disciplines to ensure that MT achieves its full therapeutic potential in managing benign pleural disease.
