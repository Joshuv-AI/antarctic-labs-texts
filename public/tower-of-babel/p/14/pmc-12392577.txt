The risk factors of obsessive-compulsive disorder: a cross-sectional study in Southwestern China
Authors: Haibing Chen, Jie Feng, Mengling Wang, Huifang Han, Yue Deng, Kesu Qin, Jiangyu Liao, Chengzhi Chen, Jingfu Qiu, Hongyang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn recent years, obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) has emerged as the fourth most prevalent mental disorder. However, the relationship between OCD and various factors, including demographic characteristics, lifestyles, and dietary habits remains uncertain. This study aims to explore the connection between various factors and OCD.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted from January 14 to February 15, 2023, targeting the general population through an online platform. Demographic characteristics, lifestyles, dietary habits, and OCD symptoms were assessed using a validated classification scale. To identify potential risk factors for OCD, Lasso and logistic regression analyses were performed. Subsequently, the relative importance of these factors was evaluated through machine learning models, including Support Vector Machine, Random Forest, and Back Propagation Neural Network.ResultsAmong the 1572 participants, 30.40% were categorized as high-risk for OCD. Logistic regression analysis revealed that having a medical history (OR = 1.709, 95% CI: 1.064–2.738), sleep disorders (OR = 1.460, 95% CI: 1.005–2.137), gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms (OR = 1.562, 95% CI: 1.057–2.311), and abnormal defecation (OR = 1.431, 95% CI: 1.013–2.018) significantly increased the risk of OCD (all P < 0.05). These findings were consistent with the results from machine learning models, further supporting the critical role of these factors in OCD risk.ConclusionThis study identified medical history, GI symptoms, sleep disorders and picky eating as significant risk factors for OCD. These findings offer valuable insights into the etiology of OCD and suggest potential avenues for targeted interventions and preventive strategies aimed at reducing OCD risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07281-6.

Introduction

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is a chronic and disabling mental disorder, primarily characterized by the recurrent occurrence of intrusive obsessive thoughts and compulsive behaviors [1]. Over the past few decades, the prevalence of OCD has increased to approximately 2.0–3.0%, ranking it as the fourth most common mental disorder in the world, comparable to that of asthma and diabetes [2]. Despite the rising prevalence, conventional treatment methods have shown limited effectiveness [3, 4]. Therefore, identifying the risk factors associated with OCD and developing effective prevention strategies are critical for enhancing public health and supporting societal development.

Previous studies have suggested that the onset of OCD was mainly associated with genetic factors [5, 6] and life experiences [7].Studies converge on family [8] and twin [9] have consistently demonstrated a hereditary component to OCD, with first-degree relatives of OCD patients exhibiting a higher risk of developing the disorder. However, the complex interplay between genes and the environment has hindered a comprehensive understanding of the genetic mechanisms underlying OCD. Results regarding the association between specific genes [10], such as 5-HTT [11] and DRD2 [12], and OCD remain controversial, necessitating further longitudinal studies and in-depth exploration. In terms of life experiences, childhood trauma [11], such as physical [13] and sexual abuse, is associated with a significantly increased risk of developing OCD. Moreover, several studies have suggested that major life stressors, particularly those involving substantial emotional burden [14] (e.g., the death of a loved one, unemployment), may act as triggers for the onset of OCD.

In recent years, growing attention has been focused on the potential impact of gastrointestinal (GI) conditions on mental disorders. Some studies have reported that approximately 40% of patients with OCD experience GI symptoms such as diarrhea, abdominal pain, and inflammation, which may exacerbate their psychiatric symptoms to some extent [15]. GI health is significantly influenced by lifestyles and dietary habits. Imbalanced nutrition, lack of regular physical activity, and poor sleep quality may disrupt gut microbial homeostasis, potentially contributing to GI symptoms that consequently affect mental health [16, 17]. Although preliminary studies support a potential association between the two, systematic and rigorous empirical research is still lacking to clarify the underlying causal mechanisms. Further in-depth investigation in this area is urgently needed.

In brief, the pathogenesis of OCD is widely considered to result from a multifactorial interaction, with genetic susceptibility and different life experiences jointly influencing an individual’s vulnerability and clinical presentation. However, most studies have focused on specific populations (such as pregnant women [18], university students [19], psychiatric patients [20], etc.), with few studies investigating the correlation between multiple factors of the general population and OCD. Thus, this study aimed to explore the association between demographic characteristics, lifestyles, and dietary habits and the risk of OCD, using a cross-sectional survey of the general population in southwestern China. The findings are intended to provide novel insights and a potential basis for the development of targeted prevention strategies for OCD.

Method

Ethics

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of Chongqing Mental Health Center (2023-004). Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the survey, and participation was entirely voluntary. All data collected were used exclusively for research purposes and were handled with strict confidentiality. Participants were assured that any information they provided would be used solely for scientific research, and their personal information would be kept strictly confidential.

Study design and sample selection

A total of 1,634 questionnaires were collected through online platforms in Chongqing, China, between January 14 and February 15, 2023, using a snowball sampling approach. After excluding 62 questionnaires with irregular responses or apparent logical errors, 1,572 participants who completed the survey were included in the analysis (platform name: the Questionnaire Star program, URL: https://www.wjx.cn). Additionally, every 7 days, the electronic questionnaire was reposted on social media platforms until the conclusion of the survey to enhance both the sample size and diversity of the obtained data. Participants who completed the questionnaire by visiting the link were required to read and agree to the electronic informed consent statement before answering. All data were stored and managed by the Questionnaire Star program (Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Ltd, Changsha, China).

Sample size calculation

The required sample size for this cross-sectional study was estimated using the standard formula: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:N\:=\:K\:\times\:\:(1\:-\:P)\:/\:P,$$\end{document} where P represents the anticipated prevalence and K is determined by the allowable relative error. Based on existing literature, the prevalence of OCD is reported to be around 2.0–3.0%, and a 10.0% margin of error corresponds to K = 400. Under these assumptions, the required sample size would range from 12,934 to 19,600 participants. Prior to the formal survey, a pilot study employing the OCI-R scale was conducted. A total of 40 questionnaires were distributed and all were returned. The observed detection rate of individuals at high risk for OCD in the pilot was 22.5%. Using this observed prevalence and applying the same margin of error, the recalculated required sample size was approximately 1,378 participants. This study used the pilot-based prevalence figure for its sample size determination. Additionally, considering an expected 15.0% rate of invalid responses, the final target sample size was set at 1,585.

Quality control

Several quality control measures were implemented to ensure the quality of the questionnaire’s results. First, during the distribution process, individuals were purposively selected to ensure diversity in gender, age, and educational level, thereby enhancing the representativeness of the sample. Second, to reduce the influence of network bias on sample composition, each participant was allowed to forward the questionnaire to no more than three new respondents. Third, to prevent duplicate submissions of questionnaires from the same participant, each mobile phone number could only be used once for questionnaire completion. Finally, a set of quality control questions—such as “What is your father’s gender?” and “What is 7 minus 2?”—was embedded in the questionnaire. Each question had a few alternative answers, of which only one was correct. The online questionnaire system would mark the questionnaire as invalid if incorrect answers to the quality control questions appeared.

Instrument and measurement

The content of the electronic questionnaire included participants’ demographic characteristics, lifestyles, dietary habits, and the OCI-R scale (Supplemental material). Specifically, the demographic characteristics of participants included gathering data on gender, age, education background, occupation, and medical history. Lifestyles included investigating smoking habits, alcohol drinking, sleep patterns, and GI conditions. Dietary habits included exploring eating patterns, eating speed, taste preferences, and food consumption frequency.

The severity of participants’ OCD was assessed using the OCI-R scale in its Chinese version. This scale consists of 18 items. Those items were rated on a 4-point like scale, ranging from 1 (not at all), 2 (a little), 3 (a lot), and 4 (extremely). The total score ranges from 0 to 72, with higher scores indicating greater severity of obsessive-compulsive symptoms. This study used the following criteria to determine the risk level: a total score of ≤ 27 was considered a low risk for OCD, while a score > 27 indicated a high risk for OCD [21].

Lasso regression for variable selection

To enhance model performance and improve the interpretability of the results, this study applied \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathcal{l}}_{1}$$\end{document} regularization using the Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (Lasso) regression. The optimal tuning parameter lambda was determined through 10-fold cross-validation. Based on the optimal lambda, relevant variables with non-zero regression coefficients were identified and subsequently included in further modeling. Variables with coefficients shrunk to zero were considered non-informative and were excluded from the final models.

Lasso-assisted variable selection for Logistic regression

Logistic regression model was constructed to explore the factors associated with OCD risk based on variables selected through Lasso regression, using the backward likelihood ratio (LR) method. The entry and removal criteria were both set at P < 0.05. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) and Tolerance; VIF < 10 and Tolerance > 0.1 were considered indicative of no significant multicollinearity. A receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) was plotted, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the overall discriminative power of the model. Finally, the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was conducted to assess the model’s calibration.

Model construction and variable importance

Based on the variables selected through Lasso regression, three machine learning models were constructed to explore the relative importance of factors associated with the risk of OCD. These included: a linear model represented by the Support Vector Machine (SVM) model, a classification tree model represented by the Random Forest (RF) model, and an artificial neural network model represented by the Back Propagation (BP) Neural Network model. The importance values range from 0 to 1, with the total importance of all variables summing to 1. Furthermore, the importance scores of all included independent variables were standardized and converted into a relative scale ranging from 0.0 to 100.0%, allowing for a comparative analysis across models. To prevent overfitting, the dataset was randomly split, with 70.0% used for model training and 30.0% for validation. A random seed of 1234 was set to ensure reproducibility of results.

Moreover, to assess the predictive performance of the three machine learning models, ROC curves were plotted, and the AUC values were compared. In addition, key performance metrics including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), true positive accuracy (TPA) score, and Kappa coefficient were calculated to comprehensively evaluate model performance. To improve the robustness and reliability of the evaluation, 10-fold cross-validation was conducted for all models. Statistical analysis

In this study, analyses were conducted using SPSS version 23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R version 4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ basic demographic and clinical characteristics, presented as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Chi-square (χ²) tests were employed to examine whether differences in OCD prevalence across subgroups were statistically significant. Additionally, machine learning models were constructed to rank the importance of selected variables and to identify their relative contributions to the risk of OCD. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

Participants’ demographic characteristics

Among the 1,572 participants included in this survey, 852 were females (54.2%) and 720 were males (45.8%), indicating a slightly higher proportion of females. Most participants were in the younger age group (30 years and below), totaling 1,266 individuals (80.5%). Most participants held a bachelor’s or higher degree, accounting for 1,303 individuals (82.9%). In terms of occupational distribution, 643 participants (40.9%) were engaged in mental or physical labor, 591 (37.6%) were students, and 392 (21.5%) were unemployed or having other occupations, students and mental laborers constituted the majority part of participants in this study. A total of 267 participants (17.0%) reported a history of previous illnesses, with the highest prevalence being GI diseases, affecting 98 individuals (6.2%). Additionally, 299 participants (19.0%) were smokers, and 96 participants (6.1%) reported regular alcohol consumption. Refer to Table 1 for specific details.

Characteristics associated with OCD risk level

Comparative analysis between variables and the risk factors of OCD

In this survey, a total of 478 individuals (30.4%) had OCI-R composite scores > 27, indicating a high risk of OCD. Among demographic characteristics, males had a higher proportion in the high-risk group compared to females (χ² = 8.230, P < 0.005), suggesting that gender may play a role in the development of OCD. Regarding education background, respondents with a bachelor’s degree were more prevalent in the high-risk group (χ² = 11.415, P < 0.005), indicating a possible association between educational attainment and OCD risk. Furthermore, individuals with a previous medical history exhibited a significantly higher risk of OCD compared to those without (χ² = 76.275, P < 0.001). No statistically significant differences were observed with respect to age and occupation. In terms of lifestyle factors, smokers were more prevalent in the high-risk group (χ² = 48.954, P < 0.001), indicating that smoking may increase the risk of OCD. Similarly, respondents who consumed alcohol regularly had a higher risk of OCD (χ² = 17.335, P < 0.001). Sleep quality was significantly poorer in the high-risk group (χ² = 74.856, P < 0.001). Significant differences were also found in GI symptoms, frequency of GI symptoms, abnormal bowel movements, and frequency of abnormal bowel movements (all P < 0.05), while no significant differences were observed in early awakening sleep disorder and bowel movement frequency. Regarding dietary habits, significant differences among OCD risk groups were found in regular diet, daily meal times, eating speed, picky eating, overeating, food allergy, and preferred food temperature (all P < 0.05). Significant differences were also observed in the consumption frequency of fresh fruits, fresh vegetables, pickled vegetables, meat, haslet, processed meat, fried foods, leftover foods, and beverages (all P < 0.05). However, no significant differences were noted in the consumption frequency of whole grains, poultry, seafood, nuts, tubers, eggs, dairy products, and legumes. Assessment of multicollinearity among independent variables

Multicollinearity among independent variables was then assessed. A Tolerance value < 0.100 and a VIF > 10.000 were used as indicators of multicollinearity. The results showed that all variables had Tolerance values > 0.100 and VIF values < 10.000, suggesting that multicollinearity did not significantly affect the model estimation. Variable selection via Lasso regression

Lasso regression was applied to screen the initially included variables. Figure 1A and B depict the relationship between the coefficients of the 63 variables and the Lambda values. The vertical dashed line in both figures represents the optimal Lambda value (0.00947), selected via 10-fold cross-validation. In Fig. 1B, variables positively and negatively associated with the risk of OCD are shown in red and blue, respectively, while variables with coefficients shrunk to zero are shown in gray. These gray variables were considered non-informative and excluded from subsequent analyses. The results identified 26 variables positively associated with the risk of OCD, including: frequency of abnormal defecation, burning sensation, overeating, dreaminess, light sleep, sour regurgitation, alcohol drinking, fruits, diarrhea, picky eating, other-colored stools, vegetables, restless sleep, insomnia, cardiopulmonary diseases, frequency of GI symptoms, abnormal defecation, GI symptoms, animal meat, sleep disorders, smoking, medical history, coronary heart diseases, red or black stools, nausea and vomiting, and GI diseases. An additional 10 variables were found to be negatively associated with OCD risk: age, daily meal times, haslet, defecation frequency, regular diet, fried food, pickled vegetables, beverages, stomachache, and diabetes. Variables with zero coefficients were excluded from further statistical analysis. Refer to Fig. 1C for detailed information.

Under the same conditions, compared to individuals without a previous medical history, those with a previous medical history had an average 70.9% increase in the likelihood of developing OCD (OR = 1.709, 95% CI: 1.064–2.738). In contrast, individuals with a history of diabetes had an average 77.4% decrease in the likelihood of developing OCD (OR = 0.226, 95% CI: 0.096–0.512) compared to those without. Likewise, compared to individuals without a history of sleep disorders, those with a history of sleep disorders had an average 46.0% increase in the likelihood of developing OCD (OR = 1.460, 95% CI: 1.005–2.137). What’s more, picky eaters had a 41.4% higher likelihood of developing OCD compared to non-picky eaters (OR = 1.414, 95% CI: 1.037–1.925). With respect to meat consumption, those who occasionally or rarely ate meat were 81.8–102.9% more likely to develop OCD than individuals who frequently consumed meat (OR = 1.818, 95% CI: 1.401–2.360; OR = 2.029, 95% CI: 1.238–3.327). Conversely, individuals who occasionally or rarely drank beverages were 29.1–41.0% less likely to develop OCD than those who frequently consumed beverages (OR = 0.709, 95% CI: 0.511–0.983; OR = 0.590, 95% CI: 0.403–0.865).

The associations between risk factors and OCD

Based on the results of Lasso regression, 36 variables with statistically significant associations were used as input features for three machine learning models: SVM model, RF model, and BP Neural Network model. The outcome variable was the risk of OCD. The dataset was randomly split into training and testing sets at a 7:3 ratio, resulting in 1,100 participants in the training set and 472 participants in the testing set. To begin with, the performance of the three machine learning models was compared, with the ROC curves presented in Fig. 2. Based on the AUC and several other stability metrics (see Table 3 for details), the RF model demonstrated the best overall performance among the three. Furthermore, 10-fold cross-validation was conducted to evaluate the generalizability and stability of the models. After comparing the results of three models, the RF model exhibited the best generalizability and stability with AUC ranged from 75% (95% CI: 72%−78%) to 71% (95% CI: 69%−74%). Discussion

This study found that 30.4% of participants were at high risk for OCD, a significantly lower prevalence than reported in similar studies from Saudi Arabia (61.6%) [22], Iran (71.0%) [23], and the United Kingdom (76.5%) [24]. These differences may stem from the timing of the previous studies, which were conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, a period marked by heightened anxiety and psychological distress, potentially inflating OCD prevalence. In contrast, our study, conducted in 2023, reflects a time when the pandemic had largely subsided, and public anxiety had decreased. As this study employed the OCI-R, a self-report scale designed for population-level screening rather than clinical diagnosis tools such as the Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale, its measurement properties may differ from those of clinically administered instruments. These two factors may help explain the relatively lower detection rate observed in the present study. Furthermore, while past research generally indicates higher OCD susceptibility in females [25–27], our study found a lower risk among females compared to males. This discrepancy warrants further investigation. Cultural background likely influenced the findings. In many Asian societies, including China, males face higher expectations and societal pressures from an early age, potentially leading to increased psychological distress and a heightened risk of OCD. Additionally, male participants in this study may have encountered specific stressors, such as academic competition or job instability, particularly prevalent during the survey period. These factors may have contributed to the observed higher OCD risk among males.

Participants with a history of GI symptoms were identified as a high-risk group for OCD compared to those without such symptoms, consistent with previous studies [28, 29]. This may be due to gut microbiota dysbiosis caused by GI symptoms, which can disrupt the body’s internal environment. Given the gut-brain axis’s critical role in both physical and mental health, these disruptions may be associated with the onset or worsening of OCD [15, 30]. A Canadian study similarly found higher prevalence and severity of GI symptoms in OCD patients compared to the general population, highlighting their potential clinical relevance in OCD contexts [15]. This aligns with findings in other neurological disorders, where GI symptoms are common. For instance, a large-scale survey by the National Institutes of Health Autism Coordinating Committee across 14 academic health centers reported that 50% of children with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) experienced eating and GI symptoms. These children had significantly higher levels of general GI symptoms, diarrhea, abdominal pain, and constipation, with a risk over three times greater than children without ASD [31].

Emerging evidence suggests a potential connection between specific dietary habits and OCD. This study found that participants who reported being picky eaters exhibited a higher risk of OCD compared to those with more diverse dietary preferences. One plausible explanation is that picky eaters tend to consume a limited range of foods, potentially leading to deficiencies in key nutrients, such as omega-3 fatty acids, B vitamins, and tryptophan [32]. These nutrients play a crucial role in the synthesis of neurotransmitters like serotonin and dopamine, which are closely linked to emotional regulation and OCD. Furthermore, several studies have indicated that individuals with unhealthy dietary habits may be at an increased risk of developing OCD [33], which may contribute to the rising prevalence of the disorder worldwide. Additionally, research has shown that picky eaters are more likely to experience GI symptoms more frequently than non-picky eaters [34]. In the context of these GI issues, individuals might further restrict their diet, potentially leading to the development of avoidant/restrictive food intake disorders and other mental health conditions. While a direct causal relationship between picky eating and OCD has not been definitively established, these findings offer a novel and clinically relevant perspective on how dietary behaviors may influence mental health.

Sleep disorders should be considered a significant factor influencing the prevalence of OCD. Participants with sleep disorders exhibited a higher likelihood of developing OCD compared to those without such issues. Insomnia, light sleep, frequent awakenings, and poor sleep quality are associated with an impaired mental state during the day, resulting in increased errors in task handling and subsequent psychological stress. A study indicated that the high global prevalence of OCD among adolescents might be attributed to excessive smartphone use, which leads to late-night activities and disturbed sleep patterns, increasing the incidence of sleep disorders in this population [35]. Additionally, an increasing number of patients use medications to manage sleep disorders, but the addictive properties of these medications may exacerbate OCD and contribute to a decline in mental health among affected individuals [36]. Furthermore, studies have shown that poor sleep quality is associated with GI symptoms [37], and the combination of these two factors might further increase the risk of developing OCD. These findings suggest the importance of considering sleep health in both the understanding and addressing OCD.

This study identified several protective factors against OCD, alongside risk factors. Logistic regression analysis revealed that individuals who abstain from beverages have a lower risk of developing OCD compared to those who consume them. This may be due to non-beverage drinkers maintaining more balanced diets and healthier consumption habits, whereas frequent intake of sugar-sweetened or caffeinated beverages can destabilize blood glucose and overstimulate the central nervous system [38], which may in turn be associated with emotional dysregulation, heightened anxiety sensitivity, and sleep disruption—all closely linked to OCD risk [39, 40]. Among the three machine learning models used, the RF model exhibited the best and most consistent performance, prompting further examination of the variable importance ranking it generated. Besides significant predictors from logistic regression, smoking also emerged as a key factor associated with OCD risk. A study from Turkey noted rising smoking rates among adolescents [41], and early exposure to smoking, coupled with underdeveloped cognitive abilities, may be link to inadequate stress coping strategies, possibly associated with the increasing prevalence of OCD among smokers.

This study’s primary strength is its use of a general population sample, contrasting with most OCD-related research [42, 43], which often targets specific groups such as students, pregnant women, or individuals with mental illnesses. This approach enhances the generalizability of the findings, offering a more accurate reflection of OCD’s prevalence and characteristics across the broader population. Additionally, the study focuses on southwestern China, a region with limited epidemiological research on OCD, thereby addressing a critical gap in understanding OCD prevalence in this area. This contributes valuable data to the Asian context and provides insights for future research. However, this study has several limitations. Its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between OCD and associated risk factors, so the reported associations should be interpreted with caution. Longitudinal or experimental research is needed to confirm these potential causal links. The use of snowball sampling via the Questionnaire Star platform may have introduced sampling bias by attracting younger, more educated participants, thus limiting the generalizability of findings to broader populations. Additionally, psychiatric comorbidities such as depression and anxiety were not assessed, making it difficult to evaluate their potential associations with high-risk OCD groups. This represents an important direction for future research. Finally, external validation of the machine learning models was not conducted due to data constraints. Without testing on independent datasets, the models’ performance may be overly optimistic, and their generalizability to other populations cannot be assured. Future studies should include external validation to enhance model reliability and applicability.

Conclusions

In conclusion, OCD has become a widely prevalent mental disorder and is significantly associated with individuals’ demographic characteristic (medical history, GI symptoms), lifestyles (sleep disorders, smoking), and dietary habits (particular about food and beverages). Targeted interventions, a more regular lifestyle and routine, such as quitting smoking, along with a balanced diet, may contribute to improving the physical and mental health of individuals while potentially reducing the likelihood of developing OCD.
