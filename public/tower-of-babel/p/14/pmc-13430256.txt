The anatomical conditions of the venous perforators of the medial side of the thigh in progressive chronic venous disease
Authors: Gabriela Hnátková, Lukas Hnátek, David Kachlík
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of the study is to map the anatomical conditions of the venous perforators of the medial thigh to determine their functional relationship to the development of chronic venous disorder and the position of perforators based on their relationship to fixed structures of the lower limb, to map their branching in terms of communication with superficial veins, to evaluate their functional status, and to determine the indication for interventional surgery by ultrasound.MethodsA total of 371 venous perforators of the medial aspect of the thigh in 306 limbs were mapped. The examination was performed in a defined anatomical area of the ventromedial thigh, and the following boundaries were established: proximally, the saphenofemoral junction level; distally, the distal margin of the adductor canal; ventrally, a line running perpendicular to the anterior superior iliac spine; and dorsally, a line running perpendicular to the ischial tuberosity. To determine the location, the area under observation was divided into four equal zones in ascending order.ResultsThe highest number (274/73.9%) was observed in the second zone, whereas the lowest number (5/1.3%) was observed in the first zone; 47% of perforators branched from the great saphenous vein (GSV), 2% from the anterior accessory GSV, 1% from the posterior accessory GSV, 9% from the superficial accessory GSV, and 41% from a separate suprafascial vein. In 28%, the caliber was ≥3.5 mm with reflux >0.5 seconds; in 17%, the caliber was <3.5 mm with reflux >0.5 seconds; in 7%, the caliber was ≥3.5 mm with retrograde flow <0.5 seconds; and in 48%, the caliber was <3.5 mm with retrograde flow <0.5 seconds. During the application of foam sclerotherapy for the treatment, the echocontrast of the foam made the previously invisible subfascial branching of perforators visible.ConclusionsThe venous perforators of the medial aspect of the thigh can become a separate source of reflux into the superficial venous system, either as a primary source or as a secondary source of reflux in addition to the primary reflux in the superficial stem vein. An insufficient perforator may also prolong the duration of reflux in the stem vein. The caliber of the perforator does not affect the duration of reflux in itself.Keywords: Perforating veins, Chronic venous insufficiency, Endovenous laser ablation, Foam sclerotherapy

Modern management of chronic venous disease (CVD) requires an accurate anatomical description of individual structures, which diagnostic modalities enable us to do.1, 2, 3

The venous perforators (perforating veins; venae perforantes) are numerous and highly variable vessels in size, number, location, and other attributes.4 Concerning specifically the venous perforators of the medial side of the thigh, these structures are inseparably associated in clinical practice with the names of William Harold Alfred Dodd (1899-1987) and John Hunter (1728-1793). Although these anatomical structures are traditionally called by their names (as eponyms), the reality is that John Hunter did not actually describe any perforator. He brought the first description of the adductor canal (canalis adductorius) in 1789. However, we did not find any mention of venous perforators there.5 Two centuries later, in 1956, William Dodd depicted and defined the venous perforators of the medial side of the thigh,6 but if we review his drawings in detail, we can note several inaccuracies (Supplementary Fig 1, online only).

The aim of this study is to map the anatomical relationships of the venous perforators on the medial aspect of the thigh. For lesions that have not yet been treated with an intervention, the study will monitor changes over time following the procedure in relation to the development of CVD. Another objective is to verify whether the standard definition of an insufficient venous perforator (lumen >3.5 mm and reflux >0.5 seconds) corresponds to real-world situations.

We believe that this helps prevent any potential misunderstandings. This is because, in cases where a procedure has already been performed at another facility in the past, it is not possible to objectively assess the progression of the disease.

Methods

Between November 2019 and September 2023, a total of 171 individuals, 129 females and 42 males, of Central European origin, were examined and imaged. Females had a mean age of 50.5 years, a median age of 43 years, and spanned 36 to 83 years. Males had a mean age of 57.8 years, a median age of 62.5 years, and spanned 37 to 73 years. Venous perforators were diagnosed on a total of 306 limbs. A total of 371 medial thigh venous perforators were mapped by duplex ultrasound using GE Logiq 7, with a linear probe (frequency range, 3-12 MHz), and Hitachi Aloka Noblus, with a linear probe (frequency range, 5-18 MHz). These structures were described in 149 cases on the right lower limb and in 157 cases on the left lower limb. Simultaneous (bilateral) findings of at least one venous perforator on both the right and left limbs were observed in 135 individuals (135 right and 135 left limbs). In 36 limbs (14 left and 22 right), at least one venous perforator was detected on one limb, and no perforator was detected on the contralateral limb. This situation can be explained by the too-small size of the venous perforator, the limitations of the examination conditions, which were influenced by swelling, excessive subcutaneous tissue, or the resolution of the device. All these examinations were performed with the patient in the vertical (standing) position by one examiner (G.H.; as a standard routine procedure), and in the case of any ambiguity, the examination was performed independently by the other examiner (L.H.), and the result was consulted. In case of discrepancy in the result, the examination was performed again, and the third examiner (D.K.) was consulted. The examination was performed in a defined anatomical area of the ventromedial region of the thigh, and the following boundaries were established: proximally, the level of the saphenofemoral junction; distally, the distal margin of the adductor canal; ventrally, a line drawn perpendicularly from the anterior superior iliac spine; and dorsally, a line drawn perpendicularly from the ischial tuberosity. In the text, when describing the veins, we distinguish between the suprafascial and superficial veins. A superficial vein is one that courses above the muscular fascia, that is, a vein featuring its own superficial compartment (eg, the saphenous compartment) or a vein arising in the adipose tissue of the subcutaneous tissue, without its own superficial compartment. A suprafascial vein is one that is within the subcutaneous adipose tissue and does not feature its own surface compartment. Separately, suprafascially located veins with their own surface compartment are referred to as superficial stem veins.7

For a practical description of the venous perforator localization, the distance between the skin crease in the popliteal fossa and the anterior superior iliac spine was divided into four zones corresponding to each quarter of that distance. The individual sections were numbered in ascending order, derived from the direction of venous blood flow.

The anatomical description of the venous perforator was complex and included the following aspects:

The nature of the course and branching of the venous perforator was schematically mapped as a part of the protocol, and in processing the results, we then formed our own modified classification based on the previously published classification of Le Dentu,8 which is also referred to in the studies by Dortu and Dortu9 and Mozes10 as very clearly discussed by Kachlik et al.4 This classification divides the venous perforators generally into direct (connecting directly the superficial vein with the deep, axial vein), indirect (connecting the superficial vein with the deep vein via a muscular vein), mixed (connecting both directly the superficial vein with the deep vein and the muscular vein at the same time), and atypical (connecting multiple superficial veins with the deep vein).4

If we found any insufficiency of the superficial stem vein or reflux into the suprafascial vein during the ultrasound examination of perforating veins, these sources of reflux were indicated for elimination. The endovenous technique was preferred for eliminating reflux associated with clinical symptoms. Ultrasound-guided foam sclerotherapy and endovenous laser ablation with water-specific wavelengths and radial beam distribution were used to eliminate reflux. A completely occluded incompetent vein was considered a successful procedure. In the case of a venous perforator, occlusion was required at the fascia border and suprafascially.

The procedure was indicated in 145 subjects. The indications for the procedure were as follows: (1) the insufficiency of the stem vein without insufficiency of the venous perforators themselves; (2) the insufficiency of the stem vein and also of one or more venous perforators; and (3) the insufficiency of the venous perforator itself. In some individuals, two stem veins in one limb were also addressed. The characteristics of the group of patients undergoing the procedure are discussed in more detail in Table I.

The effect of the procedure and the evolution of clinical CVD status were evaluated at 1-month follow-up in all 145 subjects, 1-year follow-up in all 145 subjects, and 4-year follow-up in a total of 53 subjects. Follow-up of the CVD evolution in terms of risk of CVD recurrence was assessed according to the recurrent varices after surgery (REVAS) classification.12 The criteria include:

1.Residual varicosities after surgery. These are varicose veins that persist or appear within 1 month of the surgery and meet the condition that they are present in the areas where the surgery was performed.

It was important to distinguish whether the recurrence was a true recurrence, that is, either a situation in which the venous perforator not originally affected by the procedure was the cause of the recurrence, or a situation when the portion of the venous perforator communicating with the stem vein was eliminated by the procedure itself and originally a sufficient slender branch was retained but in the period from the procedure became insufficient and served as a new source of reflux into the suprafascial veins and thus the cause of varicosity recurrence. Recanalization of the addressed venous perforator or part of it was then labeled as a failure of the original procedure.

All the data were anonymized. This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Ethical approval was obtained from the Local Ethics Committee (EK-1067.2/23).

We do not go into detail about the laser generators used, the types of fibers, the energy applied, the application time, nor do we further discuss the sclerosing agents used, the volumes applied, and their concentrations, as this information is beyond the scope of this article.

Results

The localization of the venous perforator during the primary examination

Most frequently, in 207 of 306 (67.6%) cases, one venous perforator was primarily detected in one limb within the examined area; in 79 of 306 (25.8%) cases, two venous perforators were observed in one limb (158 perforators), and in two cases (1%), within the primary examination, three venous perforators were found in one limb. Thus, the average detection rate was 1.21 venous perforators per limb.

The mean distance of a venous perforator from the plane passing through the popliteal skin crease was 19.59 cm (median, 19 cm; range, 5-47 cm). The mean distance of a venous perforator from the apex of the patella was 13.61 cm (median, 13 cm; range, 3-41 cm). The mean distance of a venous perforator from the anterior superior iliac spine was 36 cm (median, 36 cm; range, 18-50 cm).

The course of the venous perforator and its branching

In order to describe the course of the venous perforators, the 371 venous perforators described were excluded from the total number of venous perforators described in individuals who had a history of documented surgery within the superficial venous system. This was always a surgery involving complete or partial stripping of the great saphenous vein (GSV). It would be misleading to describe the course in these individuals because the nature of such venous perforator course may have been influenced by the procedure already performed. Thus, 89 venous perforators were excluded, corresponding to 24% of the total number of venous perforators detected. The following types of branching patterns were then described in the remaining 282 venous perforators. Five types of branching were observed. Type 1—subfascial inflow, visible coiled vein coming from the muscle; type 2—sling-like subfascial branching—one tributary arising from the stem vein and the other from the suprafascial nonstem vein; type 3—the communicating branch with the stem vein is at the border of the muscular fascia or just above it; type 4—the branch has the character of a bifurcated outflow from the suprafascial vein to the stem vein and through the muscular fascia to the deep vein, flow from the stem vein to the deep vein is unlikely; and type 5—the lack of communication with the stem vein, the suprafascial vein drains directly into the deep vein. The frequency of occurrence of individual types of venous perforator branching was type 1 in 68 cases (24%), type 2 in 20 cases (7%), type 3 in 90 cases (32%), type 4 in 25 cases (9%), and type 5 in 79 cases (28%). Venous perforators ensure communication between superficial and deep veins. The following veins drain into the deep veins: GSV in 133 cases (47%), anterior accessory GSV in 6 cases (2%), posterior accessory GSV in 3 cases (1%), superficial accessory GSV in 25 cases (9%), and other suprafascial veins in 115 cases (41%). A special group consists of procedures in which the stem vein was removed because of its insufficiency, but the venous perforators themselves were sufficient. A total of 142 stem veins and 158 primarily described venous perforators were affected in this way. The description of these structures is particularly important in long-term follow-up, where preserved sufficient suprafascial branches represent a potential risk of varicose vein recurrence. We place particular emphasis on the description of the primary venous perforators because, during the procedure, especially in the case of foam sclerotherapy, when the stem vein was affected, foam bubbles created an echocontrast that illuminated other venous perforators in the area and any connections between them. This phenomenon will be described further in the text. Such a description is also important for the correct classification of varicose vein recurrence in accordance with the REVAS classification.12

After the application of sclerotherapy foam, ideal echocontrast is created. This makes it possible to detect venous structures that were not observed during the primary examination. Additional perforators and their subfascial communication as well as additional drainage from superficial veins were thus recorded. The echo contrast produced during foam sclerotherapy is visible in communicating venous structures, regardless of their functional status. This type of echo contrast cannot be used to assess functional conditions. In our case, these were tributaries to the GSV and the anterior accessory GSV. The number of detected venous perforators increased to three venous perforators per limb in 53 cases, two venous perforators per limb in five cases, and four venous perforators per limb in seven cases. A total of 197 venous perforators were detected on 65 limbs, that is, 3.03 venous perforators per limb. The finding creates an image where the main deep veins communicate with the superficial veins through a venous network of interconnected perforators. However, it was not possible to create a more detailed correlation with the findings prior to the procedure.

Discussion

To correctly understand the specific characteristics of the venous perforators, it is necessary to have a good understanding of their general structure14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21 (for more information, see Supplementary material).

We know from the literature that venous perforators usually feature bicuspid valves. There are usually 0 to 3 of these, and their location depends on the type of perforator.10,14,20,22, 23, 24 During our ultrasound mapping, we observed 1 to 2 bicuspid valves. However, their detection was rather accidental and was not the aim of our study.

The topic of a more detailed examination of venous perforators on the medial side of the thigh caught our attention because of discrepancies in many literary sources, including major anatomical atlases.4,25, 26, 27 Unfortunately, a more detailed description of the course and connection of venous perforators on the medial side of the thigh is not available.4,28, 29, 30 Uhl and Gillot30 published three-dimensional computed tomography reconstructions of individual connections, which are unique in that they link anatomical conditions with the clinical environment. They represent the most common sources of reflux in the superficial venous system of the lower limbs. Venous perforators in the area of interest are also mentioned. Their prevalence is not reported; only individual cases/examples are presented.30

According to the current anatomical nomenclature, we refer to the studied group of venous perforators as medial thigh perforators (venae perforantes femoris mediales; “venae saphenofemorales”). They are scattered throughout the medial side of the thigh, connecting the GSV or accessory GSV and the femoral or common femoral veins, and form three subgroups4:

1.Adductor vein perforators (venae perforantes canalis adductorii; “Hunter's/Hunterian/lower Dodd perforators”; incorrectly “femoral canal perforators”).31,32

Adductor vein perforators (venae perforantes canalis adductorii; “Hunter's/Hunterian/lower Dodd perforators”; incorrectly “femoral canal perforators”).31,32

2.Subsartorial venous perforators (venae perforantes subsartoriae; “Dodd's perforators”; incorrectly “femoral canal perforators”) are often confused with the previous group.9,25,32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38

Subsartorial venous perforators (venae perforantes subsartoriae; “Dodd's perforators”; incorrectly “femoral canal perforators”) are often confused with the previous group.9,25,32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38

3.Inguinal venous perforators (venae perforantes inguinales) connect the GSV or its tributaries and the common femoral vein in the proximal part of the femoral triangle in close proximity to the inguinal ligament.33

Inguinal venous perforators (venae perforantes inguinales) connect the GSV or its tributaries and the common femoral vein in the proximal part of the femoral triangle in close proximity to the inguinal ligament.33

Based on our results, some venous perforators, referred to as anterior femoral venous perforators, penetrating through the quadriceps femoris muscle could potentially be included in this group. Anatomical classification is more accurate but more difficult to use for quick orientation in clinical practice.

We find our division of perforator location into individual segments defined by location to be more practical. It retains the necessary yield, especially if the description includes the veins with which the perforator communicates and what function it performs. Such a description is also useful for evaluating findings in cases of varicose vein recurrence. In these cases, thigh perforators are involved in 19% to 25% of cases.39, 40, 41, 42

In European Society for Vascular Surgery guidelines, an insufficient perforator is defined as having reflux greater than 0.5 seconds and a diameter greater than 3.5 mm, measured in an upright position at the level of its passing through the muscular fascia.43 However, we detected some that had long reflux and a lumen smaller than 3.5 mm and were thus a significant source of reflux into the superficial veins. Thus, our primary criterion for excluding the perforator was reflux within the perforator, which was detected in the superficial vein immediately distal to the perforator as well.

Determining recurrence according to the REVAS classification12 is difficult because it is not possible to determine with certainty whether new varicose veins are caused by dilation and newly developed insufficiency of originally healthy veins, failure of the primary procedure, or neovascularization, which appears to be the least likely.

Conclusions

The venous perforators of the medial side of the thigh are underestimated anatomical structures that are of considerable clinical importance. As described in the Discussion section above, these structures contribute between 7% and 40% to the varicosity recurrence. Studies using duplex ultrasound to evaluate the findings report the varicosity recurrence to be about 20%, which correlates with our results. These veins should not be neglected in the primary mapping of the superficial venous system. Their description and characterization should be based on the precise anatomical nomenclature, and it is recommended to avoid simplified clinical descriptions using eponyms, which are sometimes misinterpreted. Accurate descriptions in the diagnostic process allow targeted interventions that reduce the risk of recurrence. However, recurrence must always be taken into account as it is impossible to detect small details, especially branches that are <1 mm in real life, using standard procedures. If the described venous perforators are indicated for intervention, endovenous surgery is the method of choice to eliminate them,43 as was done in our study.

Limitations

Ultrasound detection of perforating veins is limited by the physical constitution of the person. This is mainly obesity or edema in the subcutaneous tissue. These factors may also make the procedure less accurate. Endovenous thermal ablation of perforating veins should be performed by a skilled surgeon. A limitation of this study is the overall complicated classification system for venous perforators, which is rather difficult to apply in routine clinical practice. Furthermore, the assessment of the clinical conditions of individual patients is limited to the Clinical, Etiologic, Anatomic, and Pathophysiologic classification.
