Downregulation of hepatic sulfotransferase 1E1 expression associated with decreased expression of multidrug resistance–associated protein 2
Authors: Chieri Fujino, Satoshi Ueshima, Tatsuki Fukami, Miki Nakajima, Toshiya Katsura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Changes in the expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters can alter the pharmacokinetics of drugs, potentially affecting their efficacy and safety. In this study, we investigated the effects of decreased multidrug resistance–associated protein (MRP) 2 expression on the gene expression of other drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters. Variations in the mRNA expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters were observed in MRP2-knockdown human hepatocellular carcinoma HepG2 cells and the liver of MRP2-deficient Eisai hyperbilirubinemic rats (EHBR). Both models showed decreased mRNA and protein expression of sulfotransferase (SULT) 1E1, a phase II drug-metabolizing enzyme, suggesting a relationship between the transcriptional regulation of MRP2 and SULT1E1. The plasma levels of bilirubin, bile acids, and cholesterol were higher in EHBR than in control Sprague-Dawley rats. Treatment with chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), a primary bile acid, reduced SULT1E1 mRNA expression in HepG2 cells and suppressed human SULT1E1 promoter activity in a luciferase reporter assay using HepG2 cells. CDCA is a known agonist of the farnesoid X receptor (FXR), and transcriptome analysis of the EHBR liver also suggested FXR activation, as inferred from changes in its target gene expression. These findings suggest that decreased MRP2 expression causes coordinated changes in the SULT1E1 gene expression via FXR activation by endogenous substances. These indirect changes in the expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes or transporters should be considered during drug development and in clinical practice.Significance StatementThis study investigated compensatory or coordinated changes in gene expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters in multidrug resistance–associated protein (MRP) 2–knockdown HepG2 cells and in the liver of MRP2-deficient rats. Decreased expression of MRP2 affects the gene expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters, including a decrease in SULT1E1, likely through nuclear receptor activation by endogenous molecules.

Introduction

Changes in the gene expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters can alter the pharmacokinetics of certain drugs and affect drug efficacy and safety. The expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters is regulated by transcription factors and nuclear receptors, including aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), constitutive androstane receptor (CAR), farnesoid X receptor (FXR), glucocorticoid receptor (GR), hepatocyte nuclear factor (HNF) 4α, liver X receptor (LXR), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) α, and pregnane X receptor (PXR).1, 2, 3 Activators of these transcription factors and nuclear receptors simultaneously alter the expression of multiple drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters.1,3,4

Knockout animals lacking certain drug-metabolizing enzymes or transporters have been reported to show compensatory changes in the gene expression of other drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters. For example, in cytochrome P450 (P450) subfamily Cyp3a-knockout mice lacking all functional Cyp3a genes, the mRNA expression levels of hepatic Cyp2b10, Cyp2c29, Cyp2c55, multidrug resistance (Mdr) 1a, multidrug resistance–associated protein (Mrp) 3, and organic anion transporting polypeptide (Oatp) 1a4 are higher than those in wild-type mice.5 In bile salt export pump (Bsep)-knockout mice, hepatic Mrp3, Mrp4, Cyp2b10, and Cyp3a11 protein and mRNA expression levels are higher than those in wild-type mice, whereas Oatp, sodium taurocholate cotransporting polypeptide (Ntcp), and Mrp2 protein expression levels are lower than those in wild-type mice.6 In Mrp4-knockout mice, sulfotransferase (Sult) 2a1 expression is reduced in the liver.7 Therefore, compensatory or coordinated relationships between the transcriptional regulation of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters have been suggested; however, the importance and mechanisms of this phenomenon remain largely unelucidated.

MRP2 (gene symbol: ABCC2) is an efflux transporter that accepts various chemicals and endogenous substances as substrates, such as glucuronide and sulfate conjugates generated by UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT) and SULT, respectively.8,9 MRP2 plays an important role in the excretion of bilirubin glucuronides from hepatocytes into the bile canaliculus. Genetic variants in the MRP2 gene exist and genetic polymorphisms that cause defects in MRP2 function are responsible for Dubin–Johnson syndrome, which results in hyperbilirubinemia.10,11 Hepatic MRP2 expression has been reported to be decreased in patients with diseases such as cholestasis,9,12 chronic hepatitis C with hepatocellular carcinoma,13 and acute liver failure with sepsis.14 A mutant strain of Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats with jaundice and hyperbilirubinemia was discovered, which was later found to result from an MRP2 deficiency. These rats are known as Eisai hyperbilirubinemic rats (EHBR).15, 16, 17 EHBR has been used as an in vivo model for studies in hyperbilirubinemia and the transport mechanisms of various compounds.18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23 Therefore, the phenomena associated with the altered expression of MRP2 must be explored because MRP2 may be coordinately regulated with other drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters, including UGT and SULT. In this study, we focused on MRP2 and investigated the effects of its decreased expression on the gene expression of other drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters in vitro and in vivo.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The human hepatocellular carcinoma HepG2 cell line was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. HepG2 cells were maintained in DMEM (high glucose) (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich Co), 1% sodium pyruvate (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), and 1% nonessential amino acids (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Transfection of MRP2 siRNA into HepG2 cells

Three kinds of Stealth RNAi siRNA for MRP2 were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific, and these sequences (5´–3´) are listed as follows: 1, sense, GAUCAUGAAUGAGAUUCUUAGUGGA, antisense, UCCACUAAGAAUCUCAUUCAUGAUC, 2, sense, CCAGCAAAGGCAAGAUCCAGUUUAA, antisense, UUAAACUGGAUCUUGCCUUUGCUGG, 3, sense, ACCAAGACAUUAGUGAGCAAGUUUG, antisense, CAAACUUGCUCACUAAUGUCUUGGU. Stealth RNAi siRNA Negative Control Med GC Duplex #2 (12935112; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used as a negative control of siRNA. Twenty-five pmol siRNA was transfected into HepG2 cells (passage number: 34–45) using ScreenFect siRNA (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. HepG2 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells/2 mL per well in 6-well plates. The medium consisted of DMEM (high glucose) supplemented with 10% pooled human serum (12181201; Cosmo Bio Co), 1% sodium pyruvate, and 1% nonessential amino acids. The medium was replaced 24 hours after transfection. After another 24 hours and 48 hours, the cells were collected to evaluate mRNA and protein expression, respectively.

Animals

Male EHBR and SD rats (control) were purchased from Japan SLC, Inc. The animals were housed under a 12-hour light/dark cycle and had free access to tap water and food. All experiments involving rats were approved by the Animal Research Committee of Ritsumeikan University and conducted in accordance with the Ritsumeikan Animal Experiment Rules. Five-week-old rats were anesthetized with isoflurane, and their blood and livers were collected. The body weight of EHBR and SD rats was 126.6 ± 10.48 and 154.0 ± 4.449, respectively. Blood collected with heparin was centrifuged at 3000 × g to obtain the plasma. Plasma biochemical tests were outsourced to Oriental Yeast Co, Ltd.

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

The details of real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) have been described in our previous study.24 HepG2 cells were collected, and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Rat liver tissues were collected in RNAlater Solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. Reverse transcription was performed using a high-capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). qPCR was performed using a StepOne Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The relative gene expression levels of each drug-metabolizing enzyme and transporter (HepG2 cells: 12 genes, rats: 23 genes) were calculated by the ΔΔCT method using β-actin as the reference gene. To verify the robustness of the qPCR analysis, 2 additional reference genes, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and β-2 microglobulin (B2M), were examined in a subset of samples from HepG2 cells and rat livers. The results were similar to those obtained using β-actin and did not affect the interpretation of the gene expression patterns (data not shown). The primers used are listed in Supplemental Table 1. All primers were confirmed the specificity by in silico and in vitro agarose gel electrophoresis, no amplification with genome DNA, and 100 ± 20% amplification efficiency based on the slope of the standard curve.

SDS-PAGE and Western blotting

Whole cell lysates of HepG2 cells were prepared using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS in 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer [pH 8.0]). The rat livers were homogenized using a Dounce homogenizer with 250 mM sucrose in 50 mM Tris-HCl buffer. The details of SDS-PAGE and Western blotting have been described in our previous study.24 Briefly, whole cell lysates (20 μg protein) or liver homogenates (40 μg protein) were electrophoretically separated on 8 or 10% polyacrylamide gels. After transfer to the PVDF membrane and blocking, the membranes were incubated overnight or 2 hours at 4 °C with primary antibodies, anti–MRP2 rabbit polyclonal antibody (1/600; 24893-1-AP; Proteintech Group, Inc), anti-SULT1E1 rabbit polyclonal antibody (1/1000; 12522-1-AP; Proteintech Group, Inc), anti-SULT2A1 rabbit polyclonal antibody (1/1000; 16348-1-AP; Proteintech Group, Inc), and anti–β-actin rabbit polyclonal antibody (1/1,000; PM053; Medical & Biological Laboratories Co, Ltd). The secondary antibody used was an antirabbit IgG goat polyclonal antibody–peroxidase conjugate (1/10,000; A0545; Sigma-Aldrich Co). The protein bands were visualized and quantified using ImmunoStar LD (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) and Amersham Imager 680 (GE Healthcare Japan). Protein expression was normalized to that of β-actin and calculated relative to that of the control.

SULT1E1 activity in rat liver cytosol

To prepare the cytosol, the livers were homogenized using a Potter–Elvehjem homogenizer with 4 volumes of 1.15% potassium chloride, and the homogenate was centrifuged at 9000 × g for 20 minutes. The supernatant was centrifuged at 105,000 × g for 60 minutes. The supernatant was used as the cytosolic fraction. Protein concentrations were determined using the Bio-Rad Protein Assay Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc). The incubation mixture consisted of 0–70 nM of β-estradiol (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), 20 μM adenosine 3′-phosphate 5′-phosphosulfate lithium salt hydrate (PAPS, A1651; Sigma-Aldrich Co), and rat liver cytosol (20 μg protein) in a final volume of 200 μL of 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). The mixture without PAPS was preincubated for 2 minutes. The incubation was continued for 5 minutes at 37 °C after the addition of PAPS and stopped by adding 200 μL acetonitrile on ice. The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 minutes. The concentration of β-estradiol and its sulfated metabolite, β-estradiol 3-sulfate, in the supernatant was quantified by an LC-MS/MS system, LCMS-8060NX (Shimadzu Corporation). For LC-MS/MS measurements, a COSMOCORE 2.6C18 Packed Column (2.1 mmI.D. × 50 mm; Nacalai Tesque Inc) was used for LC separation. The mobile phase comprised 0.1% formic acid in ultrapure water (A) and acetonitrile (B). The gradient conditions (A:B) at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min were as follows: 0–2 minutes (65:35), 4–5 minutes (10:90), and 5.01–7 minutes (65:35). The precursor and product ions (m/z, precursor ion → product ion) under positive-ion mode were described as follows: β-estradiol (255.2→159.25) and β-estradiol 3-sulfate (351.1→271.2). For quantity, an absolute calibration method was used and a standard product of β-estradiol 3-sulfate sodium salt (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc) was used for the standard. The β-estradiol 3-sulfate formation rate was characterized with the Michaelis–Menten equation [v = (Vmax [S])/(Km + [S])], where v is the formation rate of β-estradiol 3-sulfate, Vmax is the maximum formation rate, Km is the substrate concentration producing half of Vmax, and [S] is the β-estradiol concentration. Parameter estimates were calculated using the nonlinear least squares method in Microsoft Excel. Intrinsic clearance (CLint) was calculated as Vmax/Km.

Sulfation of fulvestrant and resveratrol by rat liver cytosol

The incubation mixture consisted of 0–600 nM of fulvestrant (Sigma-Aldrich Co) or 0–6 μM of resveratrol (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co, Ltd), 20 μM PAPS, and rat liver cytosol (200 μg protein for fulvestrant, 100 μg protein for resveratrol) in a final volume of 200 μL of 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). The mixture without PAPS was preincubated for 2 minutes. After the addition of PAPS, the incubation was continued at 37 °C for 10 minutes for fulvestrant, and for 5 minutes for resveratrol, and was stopped by adding 200 μL acetonitrile on ice. The mixture was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 minutes. The formation of sulfated metabolites was estimated from the peak areas corresponding to the expected mass transitions in LC-MS/MS analyses, as previously reported,25, 26, 27 because standard products of these sulfated metabolites were unavailable. The appropriate formation of these metabolites was further confirmed by comparing incubations conducted in the presence or absence of PAPS. For LC-MS/MS measurements using LCMS-8040 (Shimadzu Corporation), a ZORBAX Eclipse XDB-C8 (4.6 mmI.D. × 150 mm; Agilent Technologies Japan, Ltd) was used for LC separation. The mobile phase comprised 5 mM ammonium acetate in ultrapure water (A) and acetonitrile (B). For resveratrol, the pH of a 5 mM ammonium acetate solution was adjusted to 5.5 using acetic acid. The gradient conditions (A:B) at a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min were as follows: 0 minutes (50:50), 11–13 minutes (5:95), and 13.01–15 minutes (50:50) for fulvestrant, 0 minutes (80:20), 8–10 minutes (5:95), and 10.01–12 minutes (80:20) for resveratrol. The precursor and product ions (m/z, precursor ion→product ion) under negative-ion mode were described as follows: fulvestrant (605.25→427.25), fulvestrant-3-sulfate (685.1→525.1), resveratrol (227.25→185.15), and resveratrol-3 or 4′-sulfate (307.25→227.25).

Treatment of endogenous substances in HepG2 cells and rat primary hepatocytes

Primary rat hepatocytes were isolated from the livers of male SD rats using a two-step collagenase liver perfusion method.28 The medium for primary hepatocytes consisted of DMEM/F12 with 10% fetal bovine serum, 520 μM L-ascorbic acid, 100 nM dexamethasone, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 μg/mL insulin, 50 μM 2-mercaptoethanol, 10 mM nicotinamide, 100 units/mL penicillin, 100 μg/mL streptomycin, and 10 mM HEPES. The medium for HepG2 cells consisted of DMEM (high glucose) with 10% pooled human serum, 1% sodium pyruvate, and 1% nonessential amino acids. Primary hepatocytes and HepG2 cells (passage number: 2–10) were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 2.5 × 105 cells/500 μL per well. After 4 hours (primary hepatocytes) or 24 hours (HepG2 cells), cells were treated with bilirubin (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA, Tokyo Chemical Industry Co, Ltd), and cholesterol (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation) at selected concentrations dissolved in DMSO (bilirubin and CDCA) or isopropanol (cholesterol). Control cells were treated with an equivalent volume of the vehicle. After 48 hours, the cells were harvested for RNA extraction using TRIzol reagent, as described above.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis of the livers of SD rats and EHBR were conducted by Rhelixa, Inc. Total RNA was extracted for RT-qPCR as described above. The RNA quality and quantity were assessed using a bioanalyzer. Library preparation was carried out using NEBNext poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module and NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep. The constructed libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 using paired-end sequencing with a read length of 150 bp. From the raw sequence reads, low-quality bases and adapter sequences were trimmed using Trimmomatic software (version 0.38), and the quality was assessed using FastQC (version 0.11.7). Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome rn6 using HISAT2 (version 2.1.0). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 (version 1.24.0) after relative log normalization. Enrichment analysis of upregulated and downregulated genes identified from the RNA sequencing data was conducted using Metascape (http://metascape.org/). The genes were listed based on thresholds of |log2 fold change (EHBR/SD rats)| >1 and adjusted P value <.05, using the Benjamini and Hochberg method. In addition, the fold changes in the target gene expression of nuclear receptors and transcription factors were visualized on heatmaps to evaluate their activation. Target genes were selected from previously reported genes (AhR,29, 30, 31 CAR,32, 33, 34 estrogen receptor [ER],35, 36, 37, 38 FXR,39, 40, 41 GR,42, 43, 44, 45, 46 HNF1α,47,48 HNF4α,48, 49, 50, 51 LXR,52,53 PPARα,54, 55, 56 PXR,32, 33, 34,57 retinoic acid-related orphan receptor α [RORα]58, 59, 60) based on the criteria, such as specificity, abundance, and variety. The raw data and processed data have been deposited in NCBI’s Gene Expression Omnibus and are accessible through GEO Series accession number GSE288234 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc= GSE288234).

Luciferase reporter gene assays

To prepare the reporter plasmid containing the human SULT1E1 promoter region, a fragment from −3000 to +100 bp of the human SULT1E1 gene was amplified by PCR using PrimeSTAR GXL Premix (Takara Bio Inc) with genomic DNA from HepG2 cells as the template, and the following primers: 5′-CTCGGCGGCCAAGCTTAGCTGGTAGGAAAACAAAATGGTG-3′ and 5′-CCGGATTGCCAAGCTAACCTGTTTAGTTGATCCTGTG-3′. The purified PCR product was subcloned into the luciferase reporter vector pGL4.17 (Promega Corporation) using In-Fusion Snap Assembly Master Mix (Takara Bio Inc). Internal control plasmid pNL1.1.PGK[Nluc/PGK] Vector was obtained from Promega Corporation.

HepG2 cells (passage number: 15–24) were plated in 96-well plates at 2 × 104 cells/100 μL per well and transfected simultaneously with 100 ng/well of the reporter plasmid and 10 pg/well of the internal control plasmid using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 24 hours, the cells were treated with CDCA, obeticholic acid (OCA, MedChemExpress), guggulsterone (GS, Selleck Chemicals LLC), dexamethasone (DEX, Tokyo Chemical Industry Co, Ltd), hydrocortisone (HC, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), and mifepristone (Mife, also known as RU486 or RU-38486, Tokyo Chemical Industry Co, Ltd) at selected concentrations dissolved in DMSO. After another 24 hours, the firefly and NanoLuc luciferase activities were measured using the Nano-Glo Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega Corporation) and a luminometer. Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to NanoLuc luciferase activity, and the background signal from the empty reporter plasmid was subtracted.

Statistical analysis

All data are expressed as the mean ± S.D. Statistical analysis was performed in GraphPad Prism version 8.4.3 (GraphPad Software) using one-way ANOVA with a Dunnett’s test or in Microsoft Excel using Student’s t test. A P value less than 0.05 was set to be statistically significant.

Results

Effects of MRP2 knockdown on gene expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters in HepG2 cells

To investigate the effects of decreased MRP2 expression on the expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters, MRP2 siRNA was transfected into HepG2 cells. MRP2 knockdown in HepG2 cells was confirmed by Western blotting (Supplemental Fig. 1). In control HepG2 cells, the expression of some drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters was detectable, whereas that of others, including P450 isoforms, was at the limit of detection (data not shown). MRP2 knockdown decreased SULT1E1 and SULT2A1 mRNA expression (Fig. 1A). Conversely, no significant changes in the mRNA expression levels of UGTs were observed, although off-target effects were observed in HepG2 cells transfected with siRNA_3 (Fig. 1B). MRP2 knockdown decreased the protein expression of SULT1E1 (Fig. 1C), however, did not change SULT2A1 protein expression (Fig. 1D). Expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters in the liver of EHBR

To investigate the effects of decreased MRP2 expression on the gene expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters in vivo, EHBR (genetically MRP2-deficient rats) were used. In the EHBR group, the mRNA expression levels of hepatic Mdr1b, Mrp3, Cyp1a2, Cyp3a1, Sult2a1, Ugt1a6, and Ugt2b4 were higher than those in the control SD rats (Table 2). Cyp2b, Cyp2c11, and Sult1e1 mRNA expression levels in the livers of EHBR were lower than those in control SD rats (Table 2). As the decreased Sult1e1 expression in the liver of EHBR was consistent with the results in MRP2-knockdown HepG2 cells (Fig. 1A and C), we subsequently focused on Sult1e1. The protein expression of SULT1E1 in the livers of EHBR was also lower than that in control SD rats (Fig. 2A). SULT1E1 enzymatic activity, assessed by the sulfation of β-estradiol, in the liver cytosol of EHBR was significantly lower than that in control SD rats (Fig. 2B). The estimated Km, Vmax, and CLint values were significantly lower in EHBR than those in control SD rats (Table 3). To investigate the changes in the metabolism of exogenous substrates, the sulfation of fulvestrant and resveratrol was evaluated using the liver cytosol. Fulvestrant is an ER antagonist used in the treatment of hormone receptor–positive breast cancer, and resveratrol is a naturally occurring polyphenolic compound found in grapes and red wine, known for its potential antioxidant, anticancer, and anti-inflammatory effects. The formation of fulvestrant sulfate in the liver cytosol of EHBR was lower than that observed in SD rats (Fig. 2C). Transcriptome changes in the liver of MRP2-deficient rats

To investigate the differences in transcriptional regulation in the presence and absence of MRP2, RNA sequencing was performed on the livers of control SD rats and EHBR. RNA sequencing also showed decreased Sult1e1 expression in the liver of EHBR (Supplemental Fig. 2), consistent with the RT-qPCR data in Table 2. Enrichment analysis was performed to characterize the EHBR transcriptome, using 241 downregulated and 158 upregulated genes. In the enrichment analysis of downregulated genes, 20 pathways were significantly enriched. Among these pathways, the pathways of “sulfation” and “organic cation transport” were included in addition to MRP2-associated pathways, such as “steroid metabolic process,” “response to xenobiotic stimulus,” and “ABC-family proteins mediated transport” (Fig. 5A). Fourteen pathways were significantly enriched in the enrichment analysis of the upregulated genes. The enriched pathway “steroid metabolic process” was common to the enrichment analysis of downregulated genes. Pathways associated with the kinetics of endogenous substances were enriched, including “fatty acid metabolism,” “retinol metabolism,” and “lipid transport” (Fig. 5A). Activation of transcription factors and nuclear receptors was evaluated based on changes in target gene expression. Among the 10 target genes of FXR and GR, 6–7 genes showed changes in expression, with negatively regulated genes decreasing, and positively regulated genes increasing (Fig. 5B). Regarding AhR, CAR, and LXR, an increasing trend in the expression of 4–5 of the 10 target genes was observed. Discussion

We investigated the changes in the expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters in MRP2-knockdown HepG2 cells and MRP2-deficient rats to clarify the relationship between the transcriptional regulation of MRP2 and drug-metabolizing enzymes or transporters. Various drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters were altered in MRP2-knockdown HepG2 cells and MRP2-deficient rats (Figs. 1 and 2; Tables 1 and 2). SULT1E1 expression decreased in both MRP2-knockdown HepG2 cells and MRP2-deficient rats (Figs. 1 and 2), suggesting a relationship between the transcriptional regulation of MRP2 and SULT1E1. SULT1E1 is a phase II drug-metabolizing enzyme that catalyzes the sulfation of exo- and endogenous substrates, including estrogen. Some sulfates generated by SULT1E1, such as estrone 3-sulfate and dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate, are substrates of MRP2.8,9,61,62 MRP2 also transports conjugated bile acids, and the absence of MRP2 leads to bile acid accumulation, resulting in cholestasis. Cholestasis-induced bile acids elevate estrogen levels by suppressing SULT1E1.63 Conversely, excess estrogen can lead to bile acid accumulation, causing cholestasis, and MRP2 contributes to the development of estrogen-induced cholestasis.64,65 Therefore, MRP2 and SULT1E1 are considered functionally closely related through bile acids and estrogens.

In MRP2-deficient EHBR, hyperbilirubinemia was observed and plasma levels of bile acids and cholesterol were high, whereas other parameters examined in the study were normal (Fig. 3 and Table 4), in agreement with those in previous reports.66, 67, 68, 69 The mRNA expression levels of SULT1E1 were decreased by CDCA treatment in HepG2 cells and showed a decreasing trend in rat primary hepatocytes (Fig. 4). Our luciferase reporter assay showed that CDCA suppressed the human SULT1E1 promoter activity in HepG2 cells (Fig. 6A). These results suggest that decreased MRP2 expression may lead to an increase in bile acids, resulting in reduced SULT1E1 expression. Further studies are needed to clarify the transcriptional regulation of rat Sult1e1 and to investigate whether species differences exist. Previous reports showed that transcriptional regulation of SULT1E1 is associated with ER, GR, PPAR, LXR, FXR, PXR, CAR, HNF4α, and RORα.61,62,70, 71, 72 Among these transcription factors, FXR and PXR activation leads to downregulation of SULT1E1 through recruiting peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-c coactivator 1α and HNF4α, respectively.61,73,74 Our results suggested that FXR and GR are activated in the livers of EHBR (Fig. 5B). In addition, CDCA suppressed the human SULT1E1 promoter activity in HepG2 cells at least in part via endogenously expressed FXR rather than GR under the present conditions (Fig. 6). As bile acids, including CDCA, activate FXR and PXR,75,76 decreased MRP2 expression may cause a decrease in the gene expression of SULT1E1 through the activation of FXR by bile acids. However, not all FXR target genes, such as SULT2A1 and Bsep, were altered in MRP2-knockdown HepG2 cells or in the livers of EHBR (Fig. 1 and Table 2). The selectivity of target genes and the relationship between multiple transcriptional regulators should be considered. Regarding the changes in the expression of other drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters, the mRNA expression of OATP2B1 decreased in MRP2-knockdown HepG2 cells, and the mRNA expression of Mdr1b, Ugt1a6, and Ugt2b4 increased in the livers of EHBR (Tables 1 and 2). Although the cause for the discrepancy between the results in HepG2 cells and those in EHBR remains unclear, in vitro–in vivo differences or species differences may be possible. In addition, the hepatic mRNA expression of several P450 isoforms was altered in EHBR (Table 2). However, the limitation of this study is the use of a single cell line, HepG2 cells, in which the expression of some drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters including P450 isoforms was at the limit of detection (data not shown). Further investigations are needed to clarify the effects of decreased MRP2 expression on the expression of P450 isoforms.

MRP3 and MRP4 levels have been reported to increase in patients with Dubin–Johnson syndrome, MRP2 knockout mice, and EHBR.66,77, 78, 79 Bilirubin administration in rats did not increase the expression of MRP3, indicating that the increase in MRP3 expression in the EHBR liver was not due to an increased bilirubin concentration.66 Although Mrp3 and Mrp4 have been reported to be induced by CAR, the mechanisms underlying the compensatory increase remain unclear. In addition, a previous report showed that increased expression of Cyp2b10, Cyp2c29, Cyp2c55, Mdr1a, Mrp3, and Oatp1a4 in Cyp3a knockout mice was attenuated by feeding a synthetic diet, suggesting that dietary components caused the activation of transcription factors, such as CAR and PXR, to increase their expression.5 In BSEP-knockout HepaRG cells, increased MRP3 protein expression and decreased OATP1B1, OATP2B1, NTCP, and MRP2 protein expression were observed, whereas MRP4 protein expression remained unchanged.80 These findings indicated that compensatory changes in gene expression can occur in vitro. Although these results in BSEP-knockout HepaRG cells differ slightly from those in Bsep-knockout mice, both reports discuss bile acid accumulation and the activation of nuclear receptors, such as CAR.6,80 In this study, we showed bile acid accumulation and activation of FXR and GR in EHBR (Figs. 3B and 5B). Thus, elucidating the coordinated relationship between the expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters would be useful for drug development using in vitro and in vivo models. For example, in knockout cells or mice of certain drug-metabolizing enzymes or transporters, the variable expression of other drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters can mask pharmacokinetic evaluation. In patients with MRP2 polymorphisms, including those with Dubin–Johnson syndrome, previous knowledge regarding the changes in the pharmacokinetics of drugs that are substrates of SULT1E1 is lacking. To investigate potential changes in the metabolism of exogenous substrates, we evaluated the sulfation of fulvestrant and resveratrol in rat liver cytosol. Fulvestrant sulfate formation was reduced in EHBR, whereas resveratrol sulfate formation was not markedly different from that in SD rats (Figs. 2C and D). Fulvestrant is mainly catalyzed by SULT1E1, followed by SULT1A1, whereas the contribution of other isoforms is considered negligible.25 Resveratrol is sulfated by multiple isoforms, including SULT1A1, SULT1A2, SULT1A3, and SULT1E1.81, 82, 83 These results suggest that decreased SULT1E1 enzymatic activity in EHBR may differentially affect the metabolism of its substrates, depending on the relative contribution of SULT1E1 to their sulfation. Overall, indirect changes in the expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes or transporters, such as MRP2 deficiency–associated reductions in SULT1E1 demonstrated in this study, may lead to inter- and intraindividual variability in the pharmacokinetics of certain drugs.

In conclusion, we showed that decreased MRP2 expression changes the expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters, including SULT1E1, in HepG2 cells and the liver of EHBR. In HepG2 cells, treatment with CDCA reduced SULT1E1 mRNA expression and suggested suppression of human SULT1E1 promoter activity via FXR. In EHBR, a high plasma level of bile acids was observed, and FXR activation was also suggested in the liver. These findings suggest that decreased MRP2 expression causes coordinated changes in the gene expression of drug-metabolizing enzymes and transporters, including SULT1E1, via nuclear receptor activation by endogenous substances.
