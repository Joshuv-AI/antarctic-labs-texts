Understanding barriers and facilitators of adherence to lifestyle changes in prediabetes: a qualitative study
Authors: Ana Sofia Carvalho-Azevedo, Raquel Sebastián-Hernández, Manuela Abbate, Sergio Fresneda, María Arias-Fernández, Aina M Yañez, Miquel Bennasar-Veny, Cristina Moreno-Mulet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth interventions for individuals with prediabetes are essential to prevent or delay progression to type 2 diabetes. Adherence to lifestyle modification is complex and influenced by social determinants that act as barriers or facilitators. Gender, social class, social support, and the patient–professional relationship may shape individuals’ capacity to adopt and maintain behavioural changes. This study aimed to explore how these factors influence adherence to lifestyle modification among people with prediabetes.MethodsEthnomethodological study with a critical social perspective. Data were collected through fifteen semi-structured interviews, one focus group, and a support network mapping exercise. Analysis combined thematic content analysis and critical discourse analysis. Rigour was ensured through data saturation, investigator triangulation, and reflexivity. The study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).ResultsWomen across social classes reported significant barriers, including work overload, caregiving responsibilities, and weaker support networks, whereas men often benefited from stronger family support. Participants from lower socioeconomic backgrounds faced additional challenges, such as financial limitations affecting healthy food choices and lack of time for exercise. Trust-based and supportive relationships with healthcare professionals emerged as important facilitators of sustained lifestyle change.ConclusionGender and social class play a key role in prediabetes management. These findings highlight the importance of personalised, equity-focused interventions that address social determinants to improve prevention strategies.Trial registrationThe research protocol was registered in a clinical trial registry prior to data collection (registration number: NCT06488677 on July 5, 2024)Keywords: Adherence to lifestyle interventions, barriers, facilitators, prediabetes, lifestyle modification, social class, gender

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) poses a significant public health burden due to its increasing global prevalence and the associated risk of morbidity and mortality in adults (Saeedi et al., 2019). In 2024, an estimated 589 million people worldwide were living with diabetes, and this figure is projected to exceed 850 million by 2050, highlighting the magnitude of the problem (International Diabetes Federation, 2025). T2D is preceded by prediabetes, a condition characterised by blood glucose levels that are higher than normal but not yet sufficient for a T2D diagnosis (Tabak et al., 2012). Currently, a substantial proportion of the adult population presents intermediate states of hyperglycaemia. According to the International Diabetes Federation, in 2024 approximately 634.8 million adults worldwide (12.0%) had impaired glucose tolerance, and 487.7 million (9.2%) had impaired fasting glucose—conditions that place individuals at high risk of progression to T2D (International Diabetes Federation, 2025). Furthermore, around 70% of individuals with prediabetes will go on to develop the disease, with an annual incidence rate between 5% and 10% (Bennasar-Veny et al., 2020).

Numerous studies have shown that the risk of developing T2D is not solely limited to physiological factors (Ntanda et al., 2024). As a multifactorial condition, its risk extends beyond biological aspects, with the social context playing a crucial role in both the development and management of chronic diseases (Hill-Briggs et al., 2020; Pearson, 2019; Saeedi et al., 2019). Although strong evidence supports the effectiveness of health interventions aimed at promoting healthy lifestyles—such as a balanced diet and regular physical activity (PA)—in preventing T2D (Bennasar-Veny et al., 2020; Dagogo-Jack et al., 2022), adherence to these programmes varies considerably depending on an individual’s social and personal context (Saeedi et al., 2019). Individuals are influenced by a range of factors that can act as either barriers or facilitators in their lifestyle modification process, making adherence and the long-term maintenance of changes particularly challenging (Deslippe et al., 2023; Kerrison et al., 2017). These factors include individual aspects, such as health awareness and knowledge, health beliefs, sex, and the relationship established with healthcare professionals, as well as social determinants, such as socioeconomic status and support networks (both family and social). All these elements play a decisive role in adherence to, and the success of, interventions aimed at promoting healthy habits (Hellebo et al., 2024).

In relation to health awareness and recognition of one’s health status, previous research has shown that being aware of one’s health condition is a crucial facilitator in adherence processes (Skoglund et al., 2022). Various studies have demonstrated that, upon receiving a prediabetes diagnosis, individuals tend to have a relatively low risk perception, which can significantly hinder the adoption of lifestyle changes (Owei et al., 2019; Skoglund et al., 2022).

However, individuals who were more conscious of their prediabetes status and recognised the risk of progression to T2D exhibited greater adherence to behavioural changes, albeit without full commitment (Li et al., 2021). This is closely linked to health beliefs, which play a fundamental role in shaping individuals’ behaviours. In the case of prediabetes, health beliefs influence both awareness of the condition and perceptions regarding the development of T2D, as well as the benefits of lifestyle changes (Shaak et al., 2018).

Regarding gender, the literature highlights differences in self-care behaviours between men and women (Caruso et al., 2020; Tseng et al., 2022). Previous studies indicate that women tend to show higher adherence rates and greater sensitivity towards the need to adopt healthy behaviours, positioning this as a key facilitator (Baroni et al., 2022; Caruso et al., 2020). However, research also points to significant barriers faced by women, such as a lack of time due to work overload—particularly in relation to PA—when compared to men (Jackson et al., 2020).

The patient-healthcare professional relationship is also particularly relevant to self-care adherence, acting either as a barrier or facilitator depending on the quality of the interaction (Moore et al., 2017). Effective communication is essential in disease management and in empowering patients to adopt healthy behaviours. When communication lacks empathy, is not personalised, or remains superficial or distant, therapeutic adherence may be compromised (Babaii et al., 2021).

Social factors, particularly socioeconomic status and social support, have a considerable influence on adherence behaviours. Individuals from disadvantaged socioeconomic backgrounds often face economic constraints, precarious employment, and excessive working hours that hinder the adoption and maintenance of healthy behaviours, such as following a balanced diet or engaging in regular PA (Hill-Briggs et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2023). Conversely, family and social support networks can act as key facilitators of adherence by promoting motivation and supporting positive lifestyle changes, whereas limited social support may represent a significant barrier, for example by reducing motivation to exercise (Ntanda et al., 2024; Zeren & Canbolat, 2023).

Considering the above, adherence to lifestyle modifications extends beyond individual control and responsibility. A comprehensive understanding of the interplay between individual, social, and psychosocial factors is essential for identifying the barriers and facilitators that influence the adoption and maintenance of health behaviours. By doing so, conditions can be created that support and promote effective and sustainable healthy lifestyle changes (Hill-Briggs et al., 2020).

In this context, a qualitative approach enables an in-depth exploration of how individuals experience, interpret, and give meaning to lifestyle recommendations within their everyday social contexts, shedding light on the relational, contextual, and social processes that influence adherence behaviours (Green & Thorogood, 2018).

Theoretical framework

This study is grounded in a critical-social paradigm and adopts an intersectional perspective, highlighting gender and social class through a feminist Marxist lens. The critical-social paradigm provides a valuable lens for understanding how social conditions influence health and drive meaningful change. This approach is particularly relevant when analysing individuals’ lived realities and the contexts that directly shape their lifestyle habits. In this sense, the paradigm allows for an analysis that transcends individual behaviours, situating people within their broader socio-environmental context and identifying the barriers and facilitators they encounter in the adherence process. By adopting this perspective, nurses can empower individuals to critically reflect on their health behaviours, fostering interventions that address inequalities and promote health equity (McCall et al., 2023).

The role of social class was explored through the perspectives of Marx, Weber, and the neo-Marxist approach of Wright. Social class emerges as a fundamental factor in shaping relationships and social processes, thereby creating and perpetuating significant inequalities within society. These deeply rooted disparities are crucial when analysing why individuals either adhere or fail to adhere to healthy behaviours. In essence, social class influences multiple aspects of life, including access to resources and healthcare opportunities, ultimately affecting the capacity to adopt and maintain health-promoting behaviours (Manstead, 2018).

The influence of gender on adherence behaviours was examined through a feminist lens, drawing on Nancy Fraser’s three-dimensional theory and Carme Valls’ middle-range theory on gender and health. Valls’ perspective provided insights into how gender-related injustices manifest in the health domain (Valls-Llobet, 2010), while Fraser’s theory highlights the intersection of gender with other forms of oppression, such as social class and race, in shaping health outcomes and behaviours (Fraser, 2013).

The World Health Organisation’s (WHO) Conceptual Framework for Action on the Social Determinants of Health (SDH) offered further insights into how social determinants, particularly social class and gender, contribute to health inequalities (WHO, 2008). This framework underscores the interconnectedness of these factors in shaping health behaviours and outcomes. Additionally, the patient-professional relationship and its impact on health behaviours were analysed using Jürgen Habermas’ Theory of Communicative Action (TCA) and Paulo Freire’s Learning Theory, particularly his concept of Popular Health Education (Farsura de Oliveira & Santos Cota, 2018; Habermas, 1991). These theories were applied to the healthcare context to examine how patient-professional communication can enhance self-care practices. In this context, TCA was used to explore how nursing communication can empower patients and influence health behaviours in both rational and compassionate ways. Effective communication in healthcare is aimed at building trust, fostering autonomy, and providing continuous support, while ensuring that patients’ rights to express themselves and be heard are upheld (Walseth & Schei, 2011). The concept of Popular Health Education was employed to examine the role of nurses in recognising and valuing patients’ existing knowledge, ultimately contributing to a transformation of reality by promoting autonomy and fostering critically aware individuals who can identify and articulate their own health needs (Farsura de Oliveira & Santos Cota, 2018).

Methodology

This qualitative study was conducted within the context of the PREDIPHONE randomised controlled trial, a personalised telephone-based lifestyle intervention led by nurses and aimed at promoting lifestyle changes—specifically recommendations consistent with a Mediterranean diet and regular PA—among individuals with prediabetes (Abbate et al., 2021). Adherence to the PREDIPHONE trial was operationalised through a previously published composite adherence index and categorised into quartiles. For this qualitative study, participants from the highest (first quartile) and lowest (fourth quartile) adherence groups were purposively selected to explore factors underlying adherence and non-adherence experiences (Carvalho-Azevedo and Abbate, 2025).

The aim of the present study was to explore the perceived barriers and facilitators influencing adherence behaviours among participants with high and low adherence in the PREDIPHONE clinical trial (Abbate et al., 2021). Specifically, the study examined individual and social factors—including awareness of prediabetes, health beliefs, gender, social class, support networks, and the quality of the patient-professional relationship—and their impact on adherence to lifestyle modification recommendations.

Design

An ethnomethodological study conducted from a critical-social perspective. This design enabled an analysis of how participants interpreted and responded to their social environment, identifying power dynamics and social factors influencing their adherence to lifestyle changes (Hammersley, 2018). While ethnomethodology examined how individuals make sense of social interactions and everyday practices, critical discourse analysis was used to explore language as a means of uncovering the sociocultural and political realities embedded in discourse (Bowers, 1992; Riyanti, 2023).

Scope, participants and recruitment

In the PREDIPHONE trial, 86 participants with prediabetes assigned to the intervention group completed the nine-month follow-up. Among them, 21 participants (13 women) exhibited high adherence to the intervention, while another 21 (11 women) showed low adherence. Through theoretical and purposive sampling, 20 individuals (10 women and 10 men) were selected to participate in this study from the highest (first quartile) and lowest (fourth quartile) adherence groups, as defined using a previously published composite adherence index (Carvalho-Azevedo and Abbate, 2025). These participants were further segmented according to sex and social class (white- and blue-collar), defined according to occupational position as white-collar (non-manual occupations) or blue-collar (manual occupations) (Domingo-Salvany et al., 2013), in order to ensure the greatest possible diversity in participant discourse.

The sample size was determined based on the concept of ‘information power’, which evaluates the participants’ ability to provide rich and relevant data by considering the following factors: (a) the study objectives, (b) the specificity of the sample, (c) the application of established theory, (d) the quality of dialogue, and (e) the analytical strategy employed (Malterud et al., 2016). Participants were chosen for their capacity to provide detailed narratives about their health behaviours.

Recruitment was carried out primarily by the principal investigator, with the support of other researchers who had also participated in the PREDIPHONE clinical trial, and was conducted via telephone. After identifying potential participants according to the segmentation criteria established for the qualitative study and their narrative capacity, the principal investigator contacted and invited them to take part in the study. Participants were offered the option of conducting the interview at their primary care centre in order to facilitate accessibility and attendance. Recruitment for the focus groups was conducted in the same manner, through telephone contact. To facilitate attendance, the focus group sessions were held at university facilities located in the city centre, with easy access on foot and by public transport.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews and one focus group were conducted. Data were collected by the principal investigator (AC-A.). To minimise potential power dynamics and avoid influencing participants' responses regarding health behaviour recommendations, the researcher (AC-A.) deliberately chose not to disclose her background as a nurse during data collection.

All interview and focus group questions were based on the previously described theoretical framework and covered various topics related to T2D and prediabetes (Supplementary Table S1). Initially, the study design envisaged the use of focus groups as a method to promote interaction and collective discussion around experiences of lifestyle modifications (Barbour, 2018). However, given the sensitive nature of low adherence and in order to create a safe environment in which participants could express themselves freely, without pressure or judgement, semi-structured individual interviews were planned exclusively for participants with low adherence. Focus groups were intended for participants with high adherence in order to foster interaction and shared reflection on lifestyle changes. However, due to recruitment difficulties, it was not possible to conduct focus groups with men with high adherence; therefore, individual interviews were carried out with this subgroup as an alternative.

In total, 11 interviews were conducted with low-adherence participants (5 women and 6 men). Among high-adherence participants, a focus group was held with 5 women, and 4 high-adherence men were interviewed individually. Participants were encouraged to share their understanding of prediabetes, their perception of their health status, the lifestyle changes they had implemented, and the factors that facilitated or hindered adherence to a healthy diet and regular PA. Additionally, perceived family and social support were explored.

The interviews were conducted in rooms at the primary care facilities where participants were registered. Each interview lasted between 40 and 60 minutes. The focus group took place at the University of the Balearic Islands and lasted approximately 90 minutes. This duration was not predetermined or strictly timed, but rather allowed participants sufficient time to freely express and discuss their experiences in relation to the objectives of the session.

Data collection was carried out between 27 September 2023 and 24 April 2024. Initially, four interviews were conducted, followed by a thorough review of the data collection process to refine the interview format and ensure a representative participant profile. Discourse analysis was conducted concurrently with data collection. Ultimately, after completing 15 interviews and one focus group, data saturation was reached.

All interviews and the focus group were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Before concluding each session (both individual interviews and the focus group), participants were asked to graphically represent their personal support networks related to the lifestyle change process (Sanz-Menéndez, 2003). This mapping exercise made it possible to identify the main resources and supports perceived by participants, as well as to explore how these networks had influenced their adherence-related behaviours to the recommendations received. Throughout the data collection process, the principal investigator (AC-A.) maintained a field diary to document reflections and observations, including descriptive, methodological, and theoretical notes, which were essential for data interpretation and analysis (Phillippi & Lauderdale, 2018).

Data analysis

An abductive content analysis and critical discourse analysis were conducted. Critical discourse analysis explores the complex relationship between language, power, and society, aiming to uncover and challenge how discourse both shapes and is shaped by social structures (Riyanti, 2023). In this study, this analytical approach was applied to understand how social factors influence adherence to lifestyle modifications.

The data analysis process involved several steps: (1) familiarisation with the data through repeated readings of the transcribed interviews, (2) identification of codes and categories using both inductive and deductive strategies, and (3) analysis and interpretation of the data in alignment with the theoretical framework (Calderón, 2002).

Data were independently coded and analysed by three investigators. To ensure accuracy and consistency, triangulation was performed, allowing the comparison of codifications, the identification of convergences and divergences, and the establishment of a consensus. The field diary was also integrated into the interview transcripts and used as part of the analysis. To protect participant confidentiality, each individual was assigned a unique code. The processes of coding, categorisation, and analysis were systematically organised and optimised using ATLAS.ti software (version 23).

Scientific rigour

To ensure methodological rigour and validity, various techniques were employed, including data saturation, triangulation among researchers, multiple data collection methods, and the application of theoretical frameworks (Guba & Lincoln, 1994; Renjith and Yesodharan, 2021).

The principal investigator maintained reflexivity and positionality throughout the research process by consistently documenting reflections in a field diary (Jamieson and Govaart, 2023; Palaganas et al., 2017). The study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) 32-item checklist for interviews and focus groups, which guided the reporting of methodological and analytical procedures (Tong et al., 2007).

Results

Our findings revealed emergent categories related to the barriers and facilitators perceived by participants regarding adherence to the lifestyle modification intervention. Several categories were identified, as detailed in Supplementary Table S2. However, for the presentation of results, only the major categories were selected: 1) (Mis)understandings and Beliefs about the Condition of Prediabetes; 2) the Influence of Social Determinants on Adherence; and the 3) Patient-Professional Relationship.

(Mis)understandings and beliefs about the condition of prediabetes

(Mis)understandings and beliefs about the condition of prediabetes

The majority of interviewed participants were aware of their prediabetes diagnosis, even among those with low adherence. This awareness emerged as an important facilitator in the adoption of healthier lifestyle habits.

‘I’m prediabetic... If you don’t take care of yourself now, later on, when you eventually wake up, it might be too late. You’ll need injections or something… insulin. It’s better to address it now, at least while they’ve managed to catch it early.’ LBM7

‘Sugar is very important, much more than you might think. You say “OK, so I have high sugar levels”, but sugar can cause so many problems, so many. When you see it going over the limit, you need to start cutting down—that’s the first thing, and there are ways to do it.’ LWF5

‘This study was really good for me, and it helped me a lot. Since I have prediabetes, it prompted me to change how I eat, exercise, and pay more attention to myself. It really helped improve my lifestyle.’ HBF1

However, for some participants, a lack of proper recognition of prediabetes emerged as a barrier to lifestyle modifications. The absence of a clear diagnosis contributed to difficulties in prioritising the necessary changes, as expressed by one participant:

‘It didn’t affect me much, really, because my levels weren’t high. Erm… It didn’t affect me then, but I’m keeping it in mind, I am aware of it. I just don’t act on it, though I am definitely aware.’ LWM2

Furthermore, the asymptomatic nature of prediabetes and the absence of visible symptoms made it more challenging to follow dietary and PA recommendations. One participant emphasised that, because they did not experience any symptoms, they did not perceive their health condition as something requiring particular care or attention.

‘Diabetes is known as the silent disease. It eats away at you little by little and you don’t notice. That’s why I don’t think people take it seriously enough, because you don’t feel it… as I said… I can’t feel anything, I’m not suffering at all.’ LBM3

Regarding health beliefs, regardless of participants’ education level or adherence to the intervention, these beliefs acted as both barriers and facilitators to behavioural change.

Participants who had a better understanding of their prediabetes condition demonstrated greater self-efficacy, which facilitated the adoption of healthier habits.

‘Well, I already knew my blood sugar was a bit high, and I needed to start taking better care of myself…’ HBM1

However, even among low-adherence participants, there was an awareness of the risk of developing T2D, and they recognised the health benefits of lifestyle changes, valuing the recommendations they had received, as reported by two participants.

‘I’ve been taking it more seriously since I was warned that I am at risk of diabetes.’ LBM3

‘I’ve learned a lot and now I know I need to take better care of myself in every way and not let things get as bad as they used to.’ LBF6

Moreover, the identification of personal strategies, perceived as ‘keys to action’, facilitated the implementation of changes. During the interviews, some participants highlighted personal motivations that they considered essential for initiating change and explained how these beliefs provided them with the strength needed to transform their lifestyle habits and integrate modifications into their daily routines, as expressed by one participant.

‘I thank God that I recently had a granddaughter. She kind of gives me the strength to keep going. God sent her to me and I have to keep living for her. I need to have the strength to move forward and make it happen.’ LBF1

On the other hand, some participants expressed misconceptions about sugar consumption and its impact on health, which emerged as a significant barrier. Specifically, one participant believed that sugar was essential for providing daily energy and improving performance in daily activities.

‘But I feel that if I cut out all sugar and fat, my body and muscles will collapse. If I stop for a while, I don’t think I’ll be able to get going again…’ LBF6

Additionally, a lack of self-efficacy was a key factor that hindered both the initiation and maintenance of lifestyle changes. Some participants demonstrated a limited perception of their ability to implement and sustain these changes. Similarly, a lack of confidence in their ability to manage their own health made it difficult for them to begin modifying their habits.

‘So, to be honest I have tried a bit, but I keep failing. Food is my weakness… and I don’t exercise because of work.’ LBM8

Furthermore, one participant also failed to perceive the risk of developing T2D, which contributed to their reluctance to adopt lifestyle modifications.

‘Since I haven’t felt any illness or anything that has affected my health, I’ve stuck with my usual diet.’ LBM3

2. The influence of social determinants on adherence

The influence of social determinants on adherence

Gender

Key gender-related aspects revealed differences in the lived experiences of men and women, particularly regarding the adoption of a healthy diet and engagement in PA. Notably, women faced substantial barriers due to their gendered roles.

Most of the women interviewed reported difficulties in self-care, which they attributed to family responsibilities and work overload. Many stated that their multiple responsibilities significantly limited their available time, making it difficult to adhere to the recommended lifestyle changes.

‘I work non-stop all day… And I handle everything at home. I go without eating the whole day, and there are days when I just don’t have time to eat. You know, if I have to go shopping, I skip meals—I go shopping and then straight to work, I’m not going to exercise—I have more important things to do. Maybe I should be more selfish.’ LBF6

This work overload was also associated with feelings of exhaustion and demotivation, which negatively impacted their ability to adopt behavioural changes, particularly in relation to PA, as expressed by one participant.

‘It’s just… I get home and collapse on the sofa. I don’t have the energy to go for a walk.’ LWF9

Furthermore, many women reported a lack of family support. One participant specifically mentioned that she received no help from her spouse, describing a lack of support and cooperation in following her prescribed diet. As a result, she often had to prepare two separate meals, as her spouse refused to follow the same dietary plan.

‘My husband says he’s not on a diet—only I am. He doesn’t do diets… I [have to] cook separate meals for him because he refuses to eat mine, so I end up making his and then mine.’ HBF1

‘What I really need is for my husband to share this burden with me.’ LBF6

Despite these challenges, the majority of women interviewed demonstrated autonomy and self-efficacy in self-care, taking on a proactive role in managing their health.

‘Even though I work shifts and don’t have much time to exercise, I still do it whenever I can… I’m really happy—I’ve learned to take better care of myself, and I’ve already seen results. Even after the project, I’m sticking to the changes.’ HBF5

Regarding men, the majority reported receiving significant support from the women in their families in relation to self-care, which facilitated the adoption of health-related behaviours. This support often involved delegating responsibility for tasks such as grocery shopping and meal preparation to their wives. Additionally, some participants stated that they did not engage in PA unless accompanied by their wives.

‘My wife takes care of all the cooking—I always say, God didn’t make me for the kitchen. She handles the shopping too, because apparently I don’t know how to do it properly.’ LBM3

‘It’s my wife who does the cooking and meal prep. She also joins me for walks—we go for walks in the mornings and afternoons. My wife is great for things like that.’ HBM1

Social class and employment status

Participants had different experiences in modifying their lifestyle habits depending on their social class, which emerged as either a barrier or a facilitator in shaping health behaviours. White-collar participants reported greater financial resources, enabling them to invest in healthy eating and engage in PA.

‘To be honest, money isn’t an issue for me. If I need to spend more on something expensive, I’m fine with it—really, it doesn’t bother me. I even take private Pilates classes that cost 150 euros.’ LWM2

On the other hand, blue-collar participants reported facing challenges, such as limited financial resources to purchase healthy food, which made it difficult to adopt a balanced diet. Two participants explicitly expressed this difficulty.

‘When your mortgage goes up by 40%, it’s scary—really scary. So, to be honest, you end up neglecting your diet. It gets really difficult when prices start going up… Everything is so expensive, unless you find a special offer, and even then, I’d rather save that for my kids.’ LBF6

‘Eating healthily definitely puts a strain on your budget. If you want to buy lean meats, for instance, they come with a higher price tag. Salads and other healthy options are costly too—they’re just not within reach financially.’ LBM7

Regarding employment status, it was found to be a key factor in adherence to lifestyle modification recommendations. Among high-adherence participants, the majority were retired, as shown in Table I, which allowed them to have more free time for self-care, as one participant expressed.

‘I retired a year ago, and now I have more time to take care of myself.’ HWF3

In the work context, time emerged as a critical factor for self-care, widely identified by participants as a barrier, although the underlying causes varied according to social class. Lower social class participants reported a lack of time due to work overload, whereas higher social class participants attributed it to demanding work conditions and professional responsibilities.

‘Often, I go without eating the whole day, and there are days when I just don’t have time to eat. You know, if I have to go shopping, I skip meals—I go shopping and then straight to work.’ LBF6

‘I don’t have time. I work shifts, so I work mornings, afternoons, evenings, and nights, and I’ve got two school-age children. The gym is for people who have time.’ LWM10

Family and social support

Most high-adherence participants reported having adequate family support, which contributed to greater motivation and assistance in implementing lifestyle changes. The presence of family members who encouraged and supported these changes facilitated adherence to the recommended modifications.

‘I have a lot of support. My wife helps me with everything, and then the kids also look out for me…’ HBM4

However, a lack of family support was also mentioned by some participants. Those with low adherence to the intervention reported not receiving encouragement or assistance from family members in adopting and maintaining the recommended lifestyle changes, as one participant expressed.

‘At home, my wife brings in all kinds of… She doesn’t really worry about my prediabetes and brings, well, because I have two kids, she brings all sorts of biscuits… I don’t get any support from my wife in terms of the type of food in the house, because she prioritises the kids. There are a number of foods we shouldn’t have at home, especially sugary ones… I get no cooperation at all, and she has no intention of changing either… and for me, it’s a struggle, because sometimes, well, I end up eating them.’ LWM10

Moreover, social support and cohesion were found to be fundamental in shaping participants’ health behaviours. The majority stated that having a strong social network acted as a facilitator to implementing lifestyle changes. Participants with close-knit social groups and strong friendships highlighted their importance in maintaining motivation and consistency when pursuing a healthy lifestyle.

‘Going on hiking trips with friends is the physical activity I do… I go with a group of friends… 8, 10, 14 kilometres. Honestly, I really enjoy it; it motivates me.’ HBF5

Conversely, the absence of social support was identified as a barrier by some low-adherence participants, who reported lacking the necessary encouragement to implement desired changes. This lack of a support network was particularly evident among lower social class participants, especially women, who perceived their networks as more distant and less engaged. ‘Sometimes I feel alone in this, I don’t have any friends… I don’t have anyone to help me with this…’ LBF1

3. The patient-professional relationship

The patient-professional relationship

Most participants reported maintaining a good relationship with the healthcare professional during the intervention, which proved to be a key facilitator in their engagement with the programme and adherence to lifestyle change recommendations.

‘The people who contacted me have kept their commitment to supporting and guiding me… It’s reassuring to know that someone genuinely cares about my health. …And I feel truly satisfied because, for example, the way I’ve been treated has been really pleasant—it’s nothing like the typical medical consultation experience.’ LBM3

Some participants also highlighted the ability of professionals to listen and address their concerns, emphasising that the encouragement received was fundamental in helping them implement lifestyle modifications. This type of support was considered an essential factor in increasing motivation and strengthening commitment to the desired changes.

‘...I really liked it because they listened to me, explained how I should do things... They gave me a lot of information, and that helped me—it motivated me.’ LWF5

However, some participants expressed concerns about a lack of follow-up and ongoing support during the intervention. They identified the absence of continuous and regular monitoring as a barrier to their engagement during the adherence process. One participant specifically stated that they felt abandoned by healthcare professionals throughout the intervention.

‘They would see you once every three or four months… I felt like I needed more help, just a bit more support. Because, think about it—they tell you, “I’ll see you in three or four months,” and that’s it. It makes you feel alone, like you have to get through it on your own. And that’s really tough, because if there were just a little more support, I think it would make a big difference.’ LBF1

Another barrier observed was related to the recognition of the healthcare professional’s role during the intervention. The nurse’s presence often remained invisible, with participants frequently referring to her informally as ‘the girl’, which also highlighted a gender-related issue.

‘It was the girl who followed up with me—I can’t remember her name, just that it was the girl. She was the only one who saw me. She was really, really good, a very kind girl. And she was the only one who made me understand the condition I had and that I needed to do my part! She was very friendly and kind. I felt really comfortable talking to her.’ LBF1

‘The girls, every time they called me or came to the facility here… such lovely young women.’ HBF5

Additionally, a power dynamic was evident in the relationship between health professionals and participants. Some participants followed recommendations and increased adherence primarily as an act of obedience towards the healthcare professional’s prescriptions. In some cases, this dynamic led participants to feel committed to the study itself, adhering to recommendations not for their own benefit, but rather for the success of the research project.

‘At that time, I told myself, well, since I’m doing this voluntarily, I might not follow it to the letter, but I’ll try to help you as much as I can… So, of course, if I say I’m going to try to help you, then I have to make an effort to do so.’ LBM8

Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to analyse the perceived individual and social factors that influenced adherence to lifestyle modification recommendations among individuals with prediabetes. Specifically, it sought to explore how knowledge of a prediabetes diagnosis, health beliefs, gender, social class, the patient-professional relationship, and family and social support impacted adherence to the Mediterranean diet and regular PA.

Our findings reveal that adherence to lifestyle modifications among individuals at risk of T2D is shaped by a complex interaction of factors that influence behaviour in different ways (Ntanda et al., 2024). This study highlights that certain barriers and facilitators play a crucial role in the adoption of lifestyle changes, with varying impacts depending on an individual’s contextual circumstances (Skoglund et al., 2022). A lack of recognition of prediabetes, as well as an absence of perceived risk of developing T2D, emerged as key barriers to adherence among participants. This difficulty in recognising prediabetes was largely due to the absence of a clear diagnosis and the lack of visible symptoms (Harcke et al., 2023), as reported by many participants, which led to a failure to prioritise the necessary lifestyle changes (Skoglund et al., 2022). Despite this, many participants acknowledged the potential risk of progression to T2D if they did not adopt the recommended changes, even if they did not fully adhere to the prescribed lifestyle modifications (Gopalan et al., 2015; Heidemann et al., 2019). Thus, individual knowledge of one’s health status is closely linked to health beliefs and the lived experiences of participants (Kroke & Ruthig, 2022). Even when faced with the invisibility of prediabetes, many expressed an awareness of the risk and an understanding of the benefits of lifestyle interventions. However, some participants exhibited misconceptions about sugar consumption, erroneously believing that sugar provided the energy necessary for daily activities, which was associated with low adherence to the intervention. Previous studies also highlight the role of health beliefs in self-care behaviours (Nolan & Jenkins, 2019; Vassou et al., Jul 2021).

With regard to gender, our findings indicate that women, particularly those from lower social classes, faced greater challenges in following lifestyle modification recommendations due to their caregiving roles and the burden of family and professional responsibilities. This result aligns with other studies showing how traditional gender roles can limit the time women have to dedicate to self-care (Lim et al., 2020; Mussida & Patimo, 2021). This finding can be understood through Nancy Fraser’s three-dimensional theory of justice, in which the dimensions of redistribution, recognition, and representation interact to explain how gender influences economic, cultural, and political justice (Dahl et al., 2004). In the context of adherence to health behaviour modifications, the unequal redistribution of resources such as time and family support places women at a disadvantage. Moreover, the lack of recognition of domestic caregiving renders their work invisible, further exacerbating this disparity, particularly among women from lower social classes who have fewer support networks, ultimately impacting their self-care (Hendryx et al., 2020; Hill-Briggs et al., 2020; Shin & Park, 2023). This also aligns with Carme Valls’ perspective, which, through an intersectional lens, argues that the social structure and expectations imposed on women negatively affect their health and, consequently, their adherence to healthy lifestyle habits (Valls-Llobet, 2010).

In the case of men, our findings showed that they found it easier to adhere to lifestyle modification recommendations, largely due to the support provided by women in the family setting. This finding is consistent with previous studies highlighting women’s role in providing family support in contexts requiring adherence to therapeutic plans (Macias, 2023; Miller & Dimatteo, 2013). This may suggest that women act assume a protective role regarding the health of their families. To address this, it is essential to empower men to take responsibility for their own health and well-being. Breaking away from the traditional gender roles in which men rely on women for caregiving is necessary to provide them with effective strategies to improve adherence.

Adherence to dietary and PA recommendations was also influenced by the quality of the relationship between participants and healthcare professionals, supporting findings from previous studies (Davis-Ajami et al., 2021; Skoglund et al., 2022; Stonerock & Blumenthal, 2017). Most participants highlighted that a positive interaction with healthcare professionals had a significant impact on their self-care behaviours. In particular, clear, empathetic, and motivational communication was essential to the behaviour change process (Kwame & Petrucka, 2021). Additionally, many participants acknowledged the ability of most healthcare professionals to listen to their concerns and respond in an understanding and encouraging manner, which was key to maintaining their motivation and commitment to the proposed changes (Breuing et al., 2021; Sharkiya, 2023). However, some participants expressed a lack of regular follow-up, as the intervention was low-intensity and telephone-based, resulting in an unsatisfactory level of engagement (Montanes Muro et al., 2023).

Analysing these results through the lens of Habermas’ TCA, the relationship between patients and healthcare professionals should be understood as a space for dialogue and mutual understanding, where assertive and clear communication recognises the patient as an active partner, thus serving as a facilitator of adherence (Walseth & Schei, 2011). In the context of our study, the empathetic and motivational communication valued by many participants was crucial at promoting and facilitating adherence to lifestyle changes. Conversely, paternalistic relationships and a lack of regular follow-up can, through Habermas’ theory, be seen as an example of communication breakdown, where power is unequally distributed (Walseth & Schei, 2011). In such cases, healthcare professionals assume an authoritative role, while patients take on a passive role, which may interfere with adherence to therapeutic plans. Furthermore, Freire’s Popular Health Education theory helps to contextualise these findings, highlighting the importance of dialogic communication, where the patient is an active participant (Wiggins, 2012). When healthcare professionals engage patients collaboratively and encourage critical awareness, they enhance self-efficacy and awareness of prediabetes, facilitating commitment to lifestyle changes (Torre et al., 2017). Additionally, some participants shifted the responsibility for behavioural change onto the nurse, stating that they attempted to follow recommendations more out of a sense of obligation to satisfy the healthcare professional than out of a personal motivation driven by the benefits to their own health. This transfer of responsibility may suggest a power dynamic in which the healthcare professional is perceived as an authority figure, while patients adopt a passive role (Cody, 2003). In this regard, it is important to recognise that while this leadership style may appear to facilitate adherence, if it is not grounded in self-care knowledge and empowerment, but rather merely used to meet the professional’s objectives, it will not be sustainable in the long term and may even be ethically questionable. It is therefore essential to assess the motivations behind patients’ lifestyle modifications to ensure that they empower and dignify them, enabling them to maintain these changes over time.

Our findings also revealed that many participants perceived a lack of recognition of the gender of healthcare professionals, frequently referring to the nurse informally as ‘the girl’, in contrast to how they referred to male nurses. Once again, the lack of recognition of women, both in their role as patients and as healthcare professionals, reflects the dynamics of cultural injustice described by Fraser (2013). While the patient-nurse relationship was positive in many respects, this invisibility may affect adherence to self-care, particularly in how patients perceive the seriousness and importance of health advice (Montanes Muro et al., 2023).

Social class also played a significant role in adherence to lifestyle modifications, as understood through the framework of SDH (Braveman & Gottlieb, 2014; WHO, 2008). Lower social class participants reported greater difficulties in accessing healthy food, leading to lower adherence to the intervention. They also faced greater challenges in integrating PA into their routines, whether due to a lack of time, financial constraints, or inadequate access to appropriate spaces. Additionally, higher social class participants reported closer support networks, whereas lower social class participants, particularly women, perceived their networks as more distant, which may have impacted their self-care. The analysis of these results highlights the importance of social class, economic and social resources, and support networks as key determinants of adherence to recommended interventions. These findings confirm previous research on the influence of social class on self-care and health (Liu et al., 2023; Richards et al., 2022; Skoglund et al., 2022).

In the context of adherence behaviours and the influence of social class, the theories of Marx, Weber, and Wright offer insights into how economic position shapes and impacts individuals’ experiences. From Marx’s perspective, lower social class groups face greater difficulties with adherence due to their working conditions and the alienation of the labouring classes, who are often overburdened with work, leaving them with little time or resources for self-care (Milios, 2003). From Weber’s standpoint, this can be understood in terms of social stratification and the unequal access to resources—whether financial or social support networks—which influence adherence. Individuals from higher social classes possess greater economic and social capital, making it easier for them to follow health recommendations (Jones, 2011). Meanwhile, Wright’s neo-Marxist perspective (Becker, 1989) suggests that social classes are shaped by power relations in the workplace. When applying his theory to healthcare, it becomes evident that these power dynamics influence people’s autonomy and control over their free time, which may explain why lower social class individuals struggle to maintain regular PA (Franco et al., 2024). Therefore, from the perspective of the SDH, interventions aimed at modifying health behaviours should not be approached solely from within the healthcare system but must also be addressed through social policies. This highlights the importance of improving working conditions, promoting work-life balance, and reducing working hours for both social classes. Furthermore, equitable access to public health-generating services should be prioritised, rather than leaving healthcare access predominantly to private services.

With regard to support networks, both familial and social, as previously discussed, they are closely linked to gender roles and social class (Keim-Klärner et al., 2023; Rodríguez-Madrid et al., 2018). Our findings underscore the significance of support networks—or the lack thereof—in motivating individuals to initiate and sustain lifestyle changes in the context of prediabetes (Skoglund et al., 2022). In line with previous studies, family emerged as a cornerstone and a key ally in the adoption of healthy behaviours (Michaelson et al., 2021). However, social environments and networks can act as either barriers or facilitators. Limited support networks proved to be obstacles, particularly in relation to PA (Ntanda et al., 2024).

Our findings, consistent with other research, indicate that when implementing health programmes—particularly those aimed at T2D prevention—it is essential to consider the structural and social context of individuals, actively involving families and communities throughout the process (Skoglund et al., 2022). This suggests that the care of individuals with T2D cannot be approached solely on an individual basis but must incorporate family and social environments, recognising the role of caregiving relationships in fostering healthy lifestyle habits.

This study highlights that, due to both the specific characteristics of prediabetes—a multifactorial and often asymptomatic condition—and the interplay between individual factors and social context, adherence to lifestyle changes is a complex phenomenon. Therefore, it is crucial that preventive health programmes are designed with the individual’s context in mind. More holistic and personalised approaches that account for factors such as gender and social class, among others, may contribute to more effective outcomes and greater health equity.

Limitations and strengths

The study on adherence to lifestyle modifications among individuals with prediabetes faced challenges due to the complexity of adherence behaviours and the specific characteristics of prediabetes, a condition that precedes T2D and is often asymptomatic. The research aimed to provide a broad understanding of the factors that acted as barriers or facilitators in adherence to the Mediterranean diet and engagement in PA.

Another limitation was the inability to conduct a focus group with men who exhibited high adherence. Initially, two focus groups were planned—one for men and another for women with high adherence. However, as the men declined to participate, the data collection method was adjusted to individual interviews instead.

A key strength of this study was its ability to highlight the impact of gender and social class, factors that are often underestimated in research on health-related behavioural practices.

Implications for practice, research, and policy

Given the increasing prevalence of T2D, health policies aimed at ensuring regular and more personalised follow-up for individuals with prediabetes should be implemented or reinforced. These interventions must take into account the influence of individual and contextual factors, paying particular attention to vulnerable populations, as T2D follows an unequal distribution pattern, disproportionately affecting individuals from disadvantaged backgrounds. This underscores the need for future qualitative studies focused on identifying barriers and facilitators in the adoption of healthy lifestyles. Such research should explore in greater depth the influence of gender and social class on behavioural changes in health.

Finally, the development of public policies that directly address social determinants within primary healthcare services, particularly in vulnerable populations, could enhance the effectiveness of preventive interventions in prediabetes and reduce progression to T2D.

Conclusion

Adherence to lifestyle recommendations among people with prediabetes is a complex phenomenon influenced by the interaction of personal, social, and environmental factors. This study shows that gender plays a key role in adherence, with women experiencing greater difficulties due to family responsibilities and limited social support, regardless of social class. In contrast, men generally demonstrated better adherence, often supported by women within their family environment in relation to diet and PA.

Social class further conditioned participants’ ability to maintain healthy lifestyles. Individuals from more disadvantaged socioeconomic backgrounds faced greater barriers related to economic constraints and limited time, particularly regarding access to healthy food and opportunities for PA. Notably, participants from higher social classes, despite having greater economic resources, also reported difficulties engaging in PA due to high work demands and lack of time.

Overall, these findings highlight the importance of considering both gender and social class when addressing adherence to lifestyle recommendations in prediabetes, pointing to the need for preventive strategies that are sensitive to these social determinants, particularly for women and socioeconomically vulnerable groups.
