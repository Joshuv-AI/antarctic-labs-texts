Analyzing longitudinal antidiabetic medication patterns: a data-driven clustering framework
Authors: Peng Zhang, Jennifer Mason Lobo, Min-Woong Sohn, Pedro Curi Hallal, Hyojung Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAntidiabetic medications play a critical role in supporting glycemic control, which is essential for preventing or delaying diabetes-related complications. While prior studies have often focused on isolated treatment transitions, such as first- or second-line therapy initiation or single events like intensification, few have examined long-term medication trajectories. This study applied a novel time-series clustering approach to identify common antidiabetic medication use patterns among Medicare beneficiaries over a three-year period and to compare demographic and clinical characteristics across patient clusters with similar treatment pathways.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted among Medicare beneficiaries who initiated metformin monotherapy and received antidiabetic medications during a three-year follow-up period, with a one-year washout period to ensure they were antidiabetic medication–naïve prior to initiation. Five types of transitions (switch, intensification, de-intensification, discontinuation, and re-initiation) were defined based on changes in the number of concurrently used medications to describe longitudinal medication patterns. Dynamic time warping was applied to measure pairwise similarities in medication use sequences, accommodating variations in timing and duration. Partitioning Around Medoids clustering was used to group patients with similar medication use patterns.ResultsAmong 4,616 eligible patients, 222 distinct medication patterns were identified. The most common patterns included continuous metformin monotherapy (36.3%), recurrent cycles of discontinuation and re-initiation (23.1%), one-time discontinuation (14.2%), switching (3.0%), and intensification (2.8%). These patterns were grouped into five clinically meaningful clusters. Cluster-level comparisons indicated that Non-Hispanic Black patients were more likely to experience discontinuation, whereas female patients were more likely to undergo intensification. Patients who switched or re-initiated therapy tended to be older, had more comorbidities, and higher rates of emergency and inpatient visits.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the value of combining sequence analysis and unsupervised clustering to reveal complex medication use patterns over time. Understanding these trajectories and associated disparities can support data-driven decision-making and guide personal interventions to improve medication adherence and equitable diabetes care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03539-5.

Background

Diabetes remains a significant public health challenge in the United States. As of 2022, around 10% of the population lives with diabetes, with 90–95% of cases being type 2 diabetes [1]. Poor glycemic control is a major contributor to diabetes-related complications, leading to severe outcomes such as cardiovascular disease, kidney failure, nerve damage, and vision loss. Research has shown that each 1% increase in HbA1c is associated with a 25% higher risk of cardiovascular events and a significant increase in the likelihood of neuropathy and retinopathy [2, 3]. Beyond lifestyle modifications like increased physical activity, patients with type 2 diabetes need to take antidiabetic medications to support glycemic control and improve long-term outcomes.

Antidiabetic medications fall into seven primary categories: metformin, sulfonylureas, insulin, DPP-4 inhibitors (DPP-4i), GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RA), SGLT2 inhibitors (SGLT-2i), and thiazolidinediones (TZD). These medications not only help control blood sugar levels but also provide additional benefits. For instance, SGLT-2i and GLP-1RA have been shown to reduce cardiovascular risks [4], while TZD has demonstrated potential benefits in preserving β-cell function [5]. Managing diabetes is a dynamic and evolving process, requiring timely adjustments to medication regimens as the disease progresses. The American Diabetes Association (ADA) provides medication regimen guidelines based on factors like comorbidities, cardiovascular risks, and weight control [6]. While these guidelines provide a structured framework, real-world prescription decisions often extend beyond standardized recommendations depending on patient-specific factors, such as age, socioeconomic status, medication tolerance, and prior treatment history, leading to greater variability in prescribing patterns [7–9].

Research has provided valuable insights into common prescription patterns of antidiabetic medication through descriptive statistics, particularly for first- and second-line therapies. Metformin remains the preferred first-line treatment, typically followed by sulfonylureas and insulin [10]. Traditional second-line therapies such as TZD and sulfonylureas have seen declining use with the introduction of newer options like DPP-4i, which is now a popular second-line choice [10]. Studies also highlight the influence of demographic and clinical factors, such as age, comorbidities, and race, on medication selection [11].

Beyond first- and second-line therapies, other studies have examined specific transitions, such as switching or intensification of treatment. One study revealed that among patients who switched or intensified their therapy within 12 months of initiating metformin, sulfonylureas and DPP-4i were the most common choices [12]. Another study observed that patients were more likely to intensify therapy than switch when using third-line medications, with insulin being the most common choice, followed by DPP-4i and SGLT-2i [13]. Moreover, these transitions are linked to varying risks of medication discontinuation and differences in glycemic control outcomes [12, 13].

While previous studies have provided valuable insights into medication use patterns, they have typically focused on isolated segments of patients’ medication histories, such as first- or second-line therapies, or single transitions like intensification. However, in practice, treatment pathways continue to evolve and become increasingly complex, especially with longer follow-up periods. In this respect, studying only pre-defined, specific medication use patterns limits our ability to fully capture the dynamic nature of long-term medication trajectories. A holistic, data-driven framework can enhance our understanding of long-term medication pathways and enable more robust analysis of outcomes associated with these trajectories.

To address this gap, our study seeks to develop an analytic framework that systematically examines and categorizes patients’ medication transition histories across multiple years. More specifically, this study aims to investigate long-term antidiabetic medication usage patterns among Medicare beneficiaries using clustering approaches. By identifying clusters of patients with similar medication use histories, we aim to uncover common characteristics within these groups, examine disparities in outcomes, and identify opportunities for targeted interventions. This approach has the potential to reveal hidden patterns, inform equitable care strategies, and improve health outcomes for individuals living with diabetes.

Methods

Data source and study cohort

This study is a retrospective cohort analysis using Medicare claims data from 2011 to 2015. Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by University of Virginia Health Sciences Research institutional review board (IRB-HSR #21127). The dataset includes Medicare Beneficiary Summary Files (MBSF), Chronic Condition segments, and Medicare Part D prescription data. The study population consists of individuals aged 65 or older who were continuously enrolled in Medicare during the study period. Individuals who died during the study period were excluded, as their incomplete follow-up could introduce bias in evaluating longitudinal medication use.

From the MBSF Chronic Condition segment, we identified individuals with type 2 diabetes who met at least one of the following criteria during 2011 to 2015: (1) at least one inpatient or two outpatient visits with diabetes-related ICD-9 or ICD-10 codes [14], or (2) prescriptions for antidiabetic medications.

Medication history data

Prescription data from Medicare Part D were used to extract details on the dispensing date, generic name, and medication days supply. Medications were grouped into seven categories: metformin, sulfonylureas, insulin, DPP-4i, GLP-1RA, SGLT-2i, and TZD, which collectively accounted for 99.2% of all antidiabetic prescriptions in 2015 in our Medicare Part D data. Daily medication possession was calculated from January 1, 2011, to December 31, 2015, accounting for medication rollovers, which allowed unused medication supply from an earlier prescription period being carried forward into subsequent prescription periods. Unused medications remaining after December 31, 2015, were excluded from analysis.

To focus on antidiabetic medication patterns among individuals who newly started metformin as a first-line therapy, we included only participants who initiated and maintained metformin monotherapy for at least 30 consecutive days, with no antidiabetic medication use in the past 12 months prior to their first prescription. We used the earliest dispensing date of the first metformin prescription as the baseline for each participant, with start dates ranging from January 1, 2012, to January 1, 2013. Medication use patterns

Medication transitions between the seven antidiabetic medication classes were categorized into five types, as adapted from the literature. A switch occurs when a new medication is added to the therapy while the total number of co-used medications remains unchanged [12, 15]. Intensification involves increasing the total number of co-used medications to enhance therapy [12, 16, 17], whereas de-intensification reduces the total number of co-used medications [17–19]. Discontinuation is defined as stopping all antidiabetic medications for at least 90 days [20, 21], and re-initiation refers to resuming one or more medications following a discontinuation period [21, 22]. If there was no medication use change during the follow-up period, the pattern would be defined as continuous metformin monotherapy. Detailed operational definitions, including timing thresholds and duration requirements, are provided in Appendix A (Table A1).

To extract a medication pathway for each patient, we identified every transition in their medication use history and represented their whole history with the combination of these transitions. For example, a patient adding sulfonylureas to metformin and subsequently discontinuing sulfonylureas would have a pathway defined as intensification followed by de-intensification.

When coding medication pathways, we excluded periods of medication use lasting fewer than 30 days to minimize the influence of short-term medication use on patterns changes. For instance, if a patient used metformin for 15 days before switching to insulin, this metformin use period was ignored from the history. This approach allowed us to focus on stable, long-term medication patterns.

Distance measure and clustering method

Our goal was to classify individuals into groups with similar medication use patterns over a three-year period. To achieve this, we followed a three-step process: (1) encoding daily medication use as three-dimensional vectors, (2) calculating pairwise similarities between patients based on these vectors, and (3) clustering patients with high similarity scores (close distance) to identify distinct medication use patterns.

Encoding: We encoded daily medication use sequences into three dimensions. The first two dimensions capture daily medication use: one represents the number of distinct medication classes a patient used each day, while the other reflects cumulative medication use (i.e., the total number of distinct medication classes a patient had used up to a given day). However, different transition types may produce similar changes in one of these dimensions. For example, switching and intensification can yield comparable cumulative medication counts. To better distinguish these transitions, we introduced a third dimension defined as the squared sum of the first two dimensions. Using these three dimensions, we constructed medication use sequences for each patient to capture the numerical changes in co-used medications over time. For example, if a patient switches from metformin to sulfonylureas, the cumulative number of medications used over time increases, but the number of medications taken each day stays the same. Later, if the patient intensified with metformin and continue both drugs, the daily count increases, while the cumulative number of medications used does not change. Calculating similarities

To identify individuals who have similar medication use patterns between all pairs of individuals, we computed similarities between sequences of medications using Dynamic Time Warping (DTW). It is a non-Euclidean distance measure that accommodates differences in timing or speed of transitions [23]. For medication use sequences with three dimensions, DTW distances were first computed separately for each dimension, and a weighted sum of these distances was used as the final similarity measure. Further details and examples of the DTW calculation are provided in the Appendix B.

Clustering

We identified clusters of medication use patterns using Partitioning Around Medoids (PAM) based on the similarity measure computed with DTW. Unlike centroid-based methods, PAM selects representative medoids which are actual data points having the smallest total distance to all other data points within each cluster, which makes it well-suited for handling non-Euclidean distances [24]. To determine the number of clusters, we evaluated models ranging from three to five clusters. Models with fewer than three clusters were not considered because they collapsed substantially different patterns into overly broad group. In contrast, models with more than five clusters produced smaller subgroups with highly similar medication transition patterns, which increased interpretive complexity without yielding meaningful distinct patterns. For each model, we calculated the silhouette score to assess within-cluster cohesion and between-cluster separation [25]. Because statistical criteria alone may not fully capture the substantive relevance of cluster structures, we also examined the clinical interpretability of each model by assessing whether the resulting clusters represented distinct and clinically meaningful medication transition patterns. The final number of clusters was selected based on a balance between clustering performance and clinical interpretability. All analyses were conducted in Python (version 3.8) using the packages dtaidistance (version 2.3.5) and kmedoids (version 0.5.3.1) [26, 27].

Demographic and clinical characteristics

We analyzed and compared cluster characteristics based on individuals’ demographic factors and comorbidity status from MBSF data. These factors included age, race/ethnicity (Non-Hispanic (NH) Black, NH White, and Others), and the comorbidity level (none, 1–2 comorbidities, and 3 or more comorbidities based on the Elixhauser Comorbidity Index) [28].

We also incorporated access to primary care physicians (PCPs) and the rurality status of individuals’ residential county. PCP access was categorized into low (0–5 providers), moderate (6–15 providers), and good access (16 or more providers) based on the number of Medicare providers reachable within a 15-minute drive of the zip code’s centroid. Rural counties were identified based on USDA RUC code and any country having code from 7 to 9 would be classified as rural counties. These covariates were then linked to patients’ antidiabetic medication prescription history and analyzed within the clustering framework to examine potential disparities in healthcare access and treatment patterns. We used Chi-square test for categorical covariates and one-way ANOVA for continuous covariates to access statistical significance across clusters.

Results

Frequent population patterns

Initially, 49,653 patients were included in the dataset. After excluding patients who had antidiabetic medication use in the prior year before their metformin monotherapy, a total of 4,616 patients were included in the study sample. Across this sample, 222 unique medication use patterns were identified. Table 1 presents the top 10 most frequent patterns observed in the study sample. Within three years of metformin initiation, 36.3% maintained continuous metformin monotherapy throughout the follow-up period, and the remaining 63.7% patients experienced at least one of the five medication transitions defined in Sect. 2.3. Of the top 10 most frequent patterns, discontinuation was the second most common pattern (14.2%), followed by cycles of recurrent discontinuation and re-initiation, which were observed in 23.1% of patients. Patterns involving one-time intensification were identified in 3.0% of patients, while 2.8% switched to alternative therapies once.

Clustering results

The silhouette scores were 0.54 for three clusters, 0.59 for four clusters, and 0.58 for five clusters. Although the four-cluster solution achieved the highest silhouette score, the numerical difference between the four- and five-cluster solutions was minimal (0.01). Moreover, in the four-cluster solution, switching-dominant and intensification-dominant patterns were grouped together, reducing the ability to distinguish clinically meaningful transition types. In contrast, the five-cluster solution separated continuous monotherapy, discontinuation patterns, switching patterns, single-step intensification, and multi-step intensification into distinct groups. Therefore, the five-cluster solution was selected as it preserved clearer differentiation between major transition mechanisms while maintaining comparable clustering performance. Figure 3 illustrates the detailed breakdown of medication patterns observed within each cluster. In Cluster 1, which consisted of 1,680 patients, nearly all individuals (99.6%) continuously used metformin monotherapy throughout the study period. In Cluster 2, encompassing 1,676 patients, all individuals used metformin monotherapy, with 39.2% discontinuing metformin entirely and the remaining 60.8% exhibiting patterns of recurrent discontinuation and re-initiation. Cluster 3 included 615 patients, of whom over three-quarters intensified their medication during the follow-up period. Among these, sulfonylureas were the most common choice (42.4%), followed by DPP-4i (19.5%) and insulin (8.8%). Cluster 4, comprising 202 patients, was characterized by intensifications involving multiple medications. Of these, 66.3% underwent intensifications with multiple medications, with 10.4% intensifying multiple medications in a single transition. The most frequent intensification combinations included sulfonylureas and DPP-4i (2.5%). Additionally, 55.9% of patients intensified over multiple steps, with sulfonylureas (25.7%), DPP-4i(18.3%), and insulin (6.9%) being the most common first-step choices. Subsequent intensifications frequently involved DPP-4i (5.9% for patients who initially intensified with sulfonylureas, and 1.0% for those who started with insulin) or sulfonylureas (8.5% for patients who began with DPP-4i). Cluster 5 included 443 patients, of whom 47.0% switched medications. The most common medications switched to were sulfonylureas (27.3%), DPP-4i (9.5%), and insulin (7.2%). Additionally, 44.9% of patients in this cluster experienced a period of discontinuation and subsequently re-initiated therapy, predominantly with medications other than metformin. The most frequent re-initiation medications were sulfonylureas (15.4%), DPP-4i (7.0%), and insulin (2.7%).

Table 2 summarized the demographic and clinical features of each cluster. Compared to NH White patients, NH Black patients were significantly more likely to belong to Cluster 2 (22.4%), which includes individuals who experienced one or more metformin discontinuation periods. Compared to male patients, female patients were significantly more frequently observed in Cluster 3 and Cluster 4 demonstrating higher probability receiving medication intensification. Patients in Cluster 5 where patients tended to switch their therapy or reinitiate with medications other than metformin were on average older and had a greater burden of comorbidities compared to those in other clusters. This cluster also faced notable challenges in accessing primary care, as evidenced by the higher proportion of patients in areas with low access to primary care physicians. In terms of healthcare utilization, patients in Cluster 5 demonstrated the highest levels of inpatient and emergency department visits, with an average of 0.6 inpatient admissions and 1.4 emergency department visits per year. Discussion

This study proposed a novel framework to group antidiabetic medication-naïve Medicare beneficiaries based on their three-year medication use and transition histories. Among 4,616 beneficiaries, we identified 222 unique medication transition pathways. By applying PAM clustering model with DTW as the distance measure, these pathways were grouped into five distinct clusters, each characterized by the most prevalent patterns. Comparisons across clusters revealed demographic and clinical differences.

The method we propose in this paper offers several advantages over the previous methods used in detecting medication utilization patterns. First, the results offered a comprehensive view of multi-year treatment trajectories by following individual patients for a three-year period after metformin initiation. Second, compared to prior studies that primarily focused on the most common prescribing trends or simplified individual medication histories to one or two transition events [10, 12, 13], this study captured more detailed and complex individual medication use pathways. The use of unsupervised clustering provided a scalable and automated method to identify meaningful subgroups of patients, overcoming the limitations of manually defined transition types, especially as patterns become increasingly complex over extended follow-up periods. Moreover, analyzing characteristics across distinct medication use pathways could help identify potential clinical and sociodemographic disparities. These disparities could be addressed through targeted interventions to improve the quality of diabetes care.

Clusters 1 and 2 accumulatively accounted for over 70% of the whole study population, and patients in these two clusters exclusively used metformin throughout the follow-up period. This high proportion reflected the widely recommended and prevalent use of metformin in diabetes management [29]. Patients in Cluster 1 demonstrated consistent adherence to basic metformin monotherapy and showed the lowest rates of inpatient admissions and emergency department visits. This result implies that patients in Cluster 1, who received stable diabetes care management with metformin monotherapy, had a relatively low risk of diabetes-related adverse health outcomes.

Unlike individuals in Cluster 1 (n = 1,680), those in Cluster 2 (n = 1,676) experienced one or more discontinuation periods during the follow-up period. The similar number of patients in Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 indicates that approximately half of all patients exclusively using metformin experienced medication discontinuation at least once during follow-up. Compared to patients in Cluster 1, those in Cluster 2 were older, more likely to be NH Black or other minority racial/ethnic groups, had more comorbidities, and had higher rates of emergency department and inpatient visits. Potential factors contributing to discontinuation include lower health literacy, cultural perceptions, or side effects of metformin [30–32]. Limited health literacy may reduce patients’ ability to understand dosing instructions and the long-term benefits of treatment, thereby increasing the likelihood of treatment interruption [33]. Cultural perceptions or misconceptions about medication necessity and side effects may further contribute to non-adherence [34]. Recurrent discontinuation is clinically concerning, as medication non-adherence has been consistently associated with higher risks of mortality, hospitalization, and emergency department utilization in patients with chronic diseases [5, 35, 36]. Therefore, clusters characterized by repeated discontinuation may represent patients at elevated risk of adverse outcomes and may warrant closer follow-up and adherence-focused interventions.

Our study showed that approximately 15% of all patients in this study intensified their medication therapy during the follow-up period, and these patients were primarily classified into Clusters 3, characterized by intensification with one additional medication, and Cluster 4, consisting of patients who intensified therapy with multiple medications. The most frequently used medications for intensification included sulfonylureas, DPP-4i, and insulin. These medications were also the most common choices when patients adopted intensification involving multiple drugs. In Cluster 4, more patients (55.9%) chose to intensify their medications step by step rather than intensified multiple medications at the same time (10.4%). The frequent use of sulfonylureas may reflect their availability as low-cost generics during the study period, when they were commonly placed in lower Medicare Part D formulary tiers [37]. In contrast, newer agents such as GLP-1RA and SGLT-2i were brand-only therapies typically associated with higher cost-sharing, potentially limiting early adoption among older adults [38]. Although insulin was also largely brand-only, its longstanding role as an effective intensification therapy could have contributed to its frequent use [39]. DPP-4i may have been preferred for their lower risk of hypoglycemia, which is especially relevant in older populations [40].

As shown in Table C1, medication patterns in Clusters 3 and 4 were highly diverse. The most common sequences included the transition from metformin alone to metformin plus sulfonylureas in Cluster 3 (4.7%), and the progression from metformin alone to metformin plus DPP-4i, followed by metformin plus sulfonylureas in Cluster 4 (1.5%). However, these patterns represented only a small portion of patients in these two clusters. The substantial diversity in medication use patterns may reflect the varied clinical needs and health conditions among patients. The known compatibility of sulfonylureas, DPP-4i, and insulin with other therapies, along with their generally manageable side effect profiles, makes these medications suitable for addressing such diverse patient scenarios and treatment requirements and could be another reason leading to frequent use of these medications in these patients [40–42].

Existing literature offers mixed evidence regarding gender-related disparities in medication intensification. One study conducted by Liss et al. (2023) investigated antidiabetic medication transitions after patients initiated their second-line therapy [12]. The study reported that female patients were more likely to experience various medication transitions, including intensification, compared to male patients [12]. Conversely, another study conducted by Oktora et al. (2023) found that male patients were more likely than females to receive therapy intensification following metformin initiation [43]. In our study that evaluated medication use patterns over three-year time periods, the proportions of females in Clusters 3 and 4 (49.6% in Cluster 3, 45.5% in Cluster 4) were lower than the proportions of males. However, these proportions were higher compared to female patients who stuck to metformin monotherapy in Cluster 1(38.4%). The reduced sex disparities observed in the intensification clusters may reflect that female patients experience greater challenges in achieving glycemic control with metformin monotherapy, such as more frequent adverse events or reduced treatment effectiveness [43].

Less than 10% of the study population were categorized in Cluster 5, characterized by switching medications or reinitiating treatment with agents other than metformin. Similar to Cluster 3 and 4, where sulfonylureas, DPP-4i, and insulin were the most commonly used medications for treatment intensifications, these same three medications were also most frequently used by individuals in Cluster 5 for switching or reinitiating therapy. Patients in Cluster 5 tended to be the oldest, had the highest comorbidity burden, and faced the most limited access to primary care. They also had the highest rates of emergency department and inpatient utilization among the five clusters. Switching or re-initiating therapy may reflect several underlying mechanisms, including medication intolerance, adverse effects, renal function decline, or inadequate glycemic control [44, 45]. Inadequate access to primary care could hinder timely medication adjustments and monitoring, while high emergency department utilization may reflect poor overall health status. Interventions such as telephone-based follow-up could improve care continuity and medication management for this vulnerable group.

From a clinical and policy perspective, the observed variation in medication use pattern along with their associated demographic and clinical characteristics highlight needs for targeted interventions to promote patient-centered diabetes care. For example, patients with recurrent metformin discontinuation may benefit from health literacy programs and counseling on medication adherence. For those undergoing intensification, financial assistance programs may help expand access to more effective but costlier therapies such as GLP-1RA and SGLT-2i. Meanwhile, patients who switch or reinitiate therapy and have limited access to outpatient care may benefit from mobile health interventions, such as remote monitoring or phone-based care coordination, to reduce care fragmentation and prevent complications.

This study has several limitations. First, we defined transitions (e.g., intensification or switching) based on the number of concurrent medications used and did not incorporate dosage changes or the timing of transitions. Including these variables in future studies could provide deeper insights in treatment pathways and be more precise in making clinical decisions. Second, prescription records did not fully reflect actual consumption, meaning that adherence and use of medication may have been overestimated. Incorporating real-time medication tracking or self-reported adherence data could improve accuracy. Third, several unmeasured factors, including health literacy, cultural perceptions, and glycemic control, may also contribute to disparities in medication use patterns. Future studies incorporating these variables could better quantify their influence on treatment trajectories. Fourth, the thresholds and duration criteria used to define medication transitions were based on prior literature. For example, discontinuation was determined as no medication use for 90 + days [20, 21]. Alternative threshold specifications (e.g., discontinuation as no medication use for 60 + days or 120 + days) could influence the size and composition of the identified clusters. Therefore, interpretations of the clustering results should be made within the context of the predefined transition criteria used in this study.

Although our dataset spans 2011–2015 and does not reflect the widespread use of GLP-1RA and SGLT-2i, the proposed clustering framework can be applied to more recent data. This approach allows researchers to compare medication use patterns across different time periods and examine how guideline changes and new therapies influence treatment trajectories. With more recent data, we would expect intensification and switching clusters to include a higher proportion of GLP-1RA and SGLT-2i. However, uptake of these agents has been gradual among Medicare beneficiaries due to higher cost-sharing, comorbidity burden, and tolerability concerns [46]. Traditional agents may therefore continue to play a substantial role in older populations.

In addition, this framework can be extended to evaluate the relationship between medication trajectory clusters and clinical outcomes. In the present study, we used emergency department and inpatient visits as exploratory proxy indicators of adverse outcomes. Future studies incorporating validated complication outcomes could apply regression or survival models to formally assess how different treatment patterns are associated with long-term health outcomes.

This study identified diverse patterns of antidiabetic medication use among Medicare beneficiaries following metformin initiation, using a data-driven clustering approach. The resulting clusters revealed clear differences in demographic and clinical characteristics and highlighted potential disparities in diabetes care. Understanding these patterns can support more targeted interventions to promote medication adherence, optimize glycemic control, and improve equity in chronic disease management.
