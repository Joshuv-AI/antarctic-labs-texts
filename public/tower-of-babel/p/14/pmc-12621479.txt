Prevention and control of dengue and Aedes mosquitoes in South and Southeast Asia: Interventions, challenges, and future recommendations
Authors: Iskandar Arfan, Lilis Sulistyorini, Muji Sulistyowati, Ayu Rizky, Saliza Mohd Elias
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Dengue fever, caused by a virus transmitted through Aedes mosquitoes, has become a major health issue in South and Southeast Asia. This review explores various preventive and control interventions for dengue implemented in South and Southeast Asia, the challenges faced, and recommendations to enhance the sustainability and effectiveness of these control efforts. The scoping review was conducted by searching articles published between 2014 and 2024 through databases such as PubMed, Scopus, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, and ProQuest. A total of 1599 studies were identified, and 25 articles were eventually analyzed. The findings indicate various intervention approaches in both regions. Key challenges identified in this review include community engagement and participation, resource, logistics, and data management issues, public perceptions and knowledge, control methods and evaluation of effectiveness, and social and economic issues. These challenges hinder the effectiveness of interventions. Recommendations to strengthen future interventions include: enhancing community involvement through continuous training, improving monitoring and evaluation with technology-based solutions, and expanding the use of technologies like Wolbachia-infected mosquitoes and spatial repellents. Expanding public awareness campaigns, strengthening school and community-based education, and improving training for health workers are also essential. Lastly, adopting a combined approach is crucial for achieving more effective and sustainable results in dengue prevention.

Introduction

Dengue fever is an infectious illness caused by one of the four serotypes of the dengue virus and is transmitted to humans by female Aedes mosquitoes [1]. Outbreaks of dengue fever are widespread in numerous countries across the Americas, Africa, the Middle East, Asia, and the Pacific Islands [2]. Between 1990 and 2019, the incidence of dengue fever increased significantly, with new cases nearly doubling from 30.7 million in 1990 to 56.9 million in 2019, while deaths rose from 28,151 in 1990 to 36,055 in 2019 [3]. Currently, approximately 3.9 billion people are at risk across 128 countries, with over 100 countries experiencing endemic dengue transmission. The majority of those affected are located in Africa, Asia, and Latin America [4].

The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified dengue as a key disease to focus on in the effort to meet the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) by 2030. The goal is to decrease the number of cases to 2.35 million and achieve a 0% Case Fatality Rate (CFR). One of the five strategic components in the WHO Global Strategy for Dengue Prevention and Control 2021–2030 involves engaging and mobilizing communities [5].

The risk factors for dengue disease are associated with social and environmental factors such as population density, human mobility, access to water sources, water storage practices, climate conditions conducive to the proliferation of the dengue virus vector such as high temperature, high humidity, higher rainfall [[6], [7], [8]]. Recent studies have demonstrated that rising temperatures and changing rainfall patterns significantly increase mosquito density, extend larval survival, and expand suitable habitats for Aedes aegypti, leading to higher dengue incidence in previously non-endemic regions [9]. For example, Abbasi (2024) reported an 87% increase in mosquito density and a doubling of dengue cases under warmer climate conditions in Iran, emphasizing the urgent need for climate-resilient and integrated vector control strategies [10]. Recent evidence also emphasizes that climate change, globalization, and rapid urbanization have further accelerated the spread of Aedes mosquitoes across regions, contributing to the transboundary nature of dengue transmission in tropical countries [11,12]. There are other factors too, like knowledge, attitudes, and practices of mosquito control in the community, as well as the implementation of sustainable vector control activities in the community [13,14]. Other studies have also identified factors related to the presence of the causative agent of the disease Ae. aegypti, such as housing conditions that support vector presence, negative risk perceptions of dengue fever, and impoverished and slum areas [[15], [16], [17]].

The World Health Organization (WHO) promotes the implementation of an Integrated Vector Management (IVM) program to control mosquito populations. The main components of the IVM program include: (i) selecting methods based on knowledge of local vector biology, disease transmission, and morbidity; (ii) utilizing a range of interventions such as biological and chemical controls, in combination with community mobilization, often in a synergistic approach; (iii) fostering collaboration within the health sector and with both public and private sectors; (iv) engaging local communities and stakeholders; (v) creating a regulatory and legislative framework for public health; (vi) ensuring the judicious use of insecticides; and (vii) applying effective management practices. The proper execution of the IVM program can help countries tackle challenges related to dengue and Aedes mosquitoes [18]. However, increasing insecticide resistance among Aedes populations poses a growing challenge for vector control programs, reducing the effectiveness of conventional interventions and underscoring the importance of adopting integrated and innovative strategies [19].

The main goal of this scoping review is to gather, summarize, and present evidence (from scholarly journal articles and literature) on the effectiveness of dengue/Aedes prevention and control interventions implemented in South and Southeast Asia over the past decade (2014–2024). This review will also document the challenges faced, lessons learned, and recommendations for overcoming practical difficulties in implementing these interventions. The findings from this scoping review can assist countries in the Asian region, particularly in South and Southeast Asia, in evaluating their integrated vector management (IVM) programs, comparing their prevention and control strategies with neighboring countries, and applying the lessons learned to create more sustainable approaches to Aedes control.

Materials and methods

This scoping review also follows the checklist for Scoping Reviews from the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-ScR). The review was created in the following stages: 1) identifying the research question; 2) identifying the relevant studies; 3) study selection; 4) charting the data; and 5) collating, summarizing, and reporting results [20]

Research question

The research question for this study is: "What prevention and control interventions have been implemented to address dengue and Aedes mosquitoes in South and Southeast Asia between 2014 and 2024? What challenges have been encountered during their implementation, and what recommendations have been proposed to enhance their effectiveness and sustainability?"

Study selection

Duplicate studies were removed from the set of papers uploaded to the reference manager for abstract screening. Relevant studies were selected based on inclusion criteria aligned with the research question. To ensure that the chosen criteria accurately identified articles pertinent to the study, a two step screening process was employed. In the first step, titles and abstracts were reviewed to identify relevant sources. These sources were then fully retrieved and assessed using inclusion and exclusion criteria. The final list of included articles was determined by conducting a full-text screening of the selected sources in the second step. All stages of the screening process were independently conducted by two reviewers. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion and consensus, and when necessary, a third reviewer was consulted to reach a final decision.

The inclusion criteria were based on the Population–Concept–Context (PCC) framework. The population included all age groups from South and Southeast Asia. The concept focused on dengue and Aedes mosquito prevention and control interventions, including physical, chemical, biological, environmental, digital, educational, and community-based approaches, with outcomes such as improved knowledge, practices, entomological indices, and dengue incidence reduction. The context covered studies conducted in South and Southeast Asian countries, and only original research articles published between 2014 and 2024 were included. Excluded were studies conducted outside the target regions, those focusing solely on laboratory or molecular aspects, non-original papers (reviews, commentaries, or conference abstracts), and low method quality (insufficient methodological information).

Data charting

The data chart was created based on the research questions. Each included study was examined, and relevant information was added to the chart. The data chart contained the following details: region, country of research, authors and year, study objective, design, type of intervention, outcome, and key findings. This chart served as a tool for data analysis, guiding the process of addressing the research questions. The complete data extraction table is provided in (supplementary material 2).

Collating, summarizing, and reporting the results

Data were synthesized narratively according to the main outcomes, including the types of interventions, their differences across Southeast and South Asia, implementation challenges, and recommendations for improvement. Each item on the checklist is scored "1" for Yes and "0" for No or Unclear. The total score for each study is expressed as a percentage of the maximum possible score to facilitate a descriptive comparison of methodological quality. Studies with scores above 70% are considered to demonstrate stronger methodological rigor. The quality assessment is used to identify potential sources of bias and to describe the overall strengths and limitations of the included evidence. Ethical considerations

This scoping review was conducted in accordance with ethical principles of research integrity and transparency. As this study relied solely on secondary data extracted from publicly available articles and databases, Ethical approval was not required for this scoping review as it relied solely on published data. The review adhered to the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews) framework and all original sources were properly cited to maintain academic integrity.

Result

A total of 1,599 relevant articles were identified through searches in various databases, including PubMed (375 articles), Scopus (344 articles), ScienceDirect (226 articles), Web of Science (401 articles), and ProQuest (253 articles). After removing 816 duplicate records, 783 articles remained and were screened based on titles and abstracts. A total of 750 articles were excluded due to irrelevance to the research topic. From the remaining 783 articles, 33 were selected for full-text evaluation. Following a thorough assessment, 9 articles were excluded for the following reasons: 2 were not original research, 4 did not meet the inclusion criteria, and 2 had low methodological quality. Ultimately, 25 articles were included in this review.

The included articles were published between 2014 and 2024. In South Asia, five studies were conducted in India [[21], [22], [23], [24], [25]], two in Sri Lanka [27,28], and one in Pakistan [26]. A total of twenty-five studies were included in this review, comprising eight from South Asia and seventeen from Southeast Asia. The pre–post (before–after) design was the most dominant in South Asia (62.5 %), while cluster randomized trials (41.2 %) and pre–post studies (47.1 %) were most frequently applied in Southeast Asia. The most commonly implemented intervention was health education and community empowerment (25.0 % in South Asia; 17.6 % in Southeast Asia), followed by biological (23.5 %) and chemical approaches (17.6 %) in Southeast Asia. Several studies also applied combined interventions such as B + F (chemical and educational) or C + F (biological and educational), reflecting the adoption of Integrated Vector Management (IVM) strategies.

In terms of outcomes, most studies assessed improvements in knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) (25 % in South Asia; 11.8 % in Southeast Asia) and entomological indicators (25 % and 5.9 %, respectively), while reductions in dengue incidence were most frequently reported in Southeast Asia (35.3 %). Overall, these findings indicate that community education approaches remain the primary strategy in South Asia, whereas Southeast Asia demonstrates a stronger focus on epidemiological impact through the combination of biological and chemical interventions. 1)Physical interventions

Physical interventions

Two studies evaluated physical intervention methods to control Aedes mosquito populations, the primary vectors of dengue. The first study in Sri Lanka used the low-cost, locally-made Gravid Female Trap (GFT), which demonstrated a significant decrease in the Aedes population, with a 100 % reduction in the Breteau Index and a 50 % reduction in adult mosquitoes. GFT was effective in reducing dengue transmission by preventing the reproduction of gravid female mosquitoes, as evidenced by a decline in dengue cases and the detection of mosquitoes infected with DENV [28]. The second study in Malaysia employed the Malaysia Mosquito Autocidal Trap (MyMAT), which captured 97 % of Aedes mosquitoes and reduced dengue cases by 78 % over 42 weeks. MyMAT, using green technology without chemicals, proved effective in trapping mosquitoes and reducing dengue transmission, with a strong correlation between mosquito population and rainfall. Both studies demonstrate the effectiveness of physical interventions in controlling Aedes mosquito populations and dengue transmission, offering sustainable solutions without chemicals [38].2)Chemical Interventions

2)Chemical Interventions

Chemical Interventions

Four studies on chemical interventions to control Aedes mosquitoes showed varying results in reducing mosquito populations and their impact on dengue infection. The first study by Reza et al. (2016) in West Sumatra, Indonesia, tested copper-based ovitraps with a larvicide concentration of 10 ppm, which proved effective in killing Aedes spp. larvae up to 98.3 %. This research suggests that copper can be an affordable and effective larvicide, especially in resource-limited areas [30]. The second study by Ligsay et al. (2022) in the Philippines used ovitraps treated with the larvicide pyriproxyfen. Entomological results showed a significant reduction in mosquito populations, but no significant decrease in dengue seroconversion rates, indicating that while larval control was successful, its impact on dengue infection was limited [39]. The third study by Tissera et al. (2016) in Sri Lanka evaluated the effectiveness of Mosquito Shield, a spatial repellent containing transfluthrin. Although this repellent successfully reduced Aedes mosquito populations, no significant difference in Aedes-borne virus infection rates was found between the Mosquito Shield and control groups, suggesting limited effects on reducing infections [27]. The final study by Kittayapong et al. (2017) in Thailand tested school uniforms impregnated with permethrin. Entomological findings showed a reduction in Aedes mosquitoes in classrooms during the first month, but no significant serological evidence was found regarding protection against dengue infection. The reduced effectiveness of permethrin after several washes may be a factor contributing to the lack of protection against infection [44]. Overall, while these chemical interventions can reduce mosquito populations, their impact on reducing dengue infections and long-term effectiveness requires further research.3)Biological Interventions

3)Biological Interventions

Biological Interventions

Four studies on the use of Wolbachia to control Aedes mosquito populations and dengue transmission showed significant results. The first study in Yogyakarta, Indonesia (Anders et al., 2018) was a cluster-randomized controlled trial (RCT) testing the effectiveness of using Aedes aegypti mosquitoes infected with Wolbachia [35]. The results showed that the application of Wolbachia significantly reduced dengue incidence in the intervention area, with reductions observed across all age groups and increased effectiveness over time. In Singapore (Lim et al., 2024), Wolbachia was applied using the Incompatible Insect Technique (IIT) combined with the Sterile Insect Technique (SIT). The results showed a significant reduction in Aedes mosquito populations and dengue incidence, with a protection effectiveness of 61 % after 12 months of application. The longer the intervention was applied, the greater the reduction in dengue incidence [42]. In Malaysia, a study by Chow et al. (2024) assessed the spillover effects of Wolbachia interventions, which showed that, in addition to the reduction in dengue incidence in the intervention area, there was also a reduction in dengue incidence in surrounding areas that were not directly exposed to the intervention [36]. Lastly, a study in Singapore by Ong et al. (2022) used the combined IIT-SIT method, which successfully reduced Aedes mosquito populations by 98 % and dengue incidence by 88 %. These four studies demonstrate that the application of Wolbachia is effective in controlling Aedes mosquito populations and reducing dengue transmission, both in areas directly exposed to the intervention and in surrounding areas through spillover effects, offering great potential for sustainable vector control strategies [40].4)Health Education and Community Empowerment Interventions

4)Health Education and Community Empowerment Interventions

Health Education and Community Empowerment Interventions

Five studies on Health Education and Community Empowerment in dengue control highlight the importance of community-based approaches to enhance knowledge, attitudes, and preventive practices. A study in Pune, India (Narendran et al., 2024) demonstrated a significant increase in knowledge and preventive practices following an intervention based on the Health Belief Model [21]. In East Kalimantan, Indonesia (Ridha et al., 2023), the One House One Jumantik (G1R1J) program successfully increased knowledge and preventive actions through health cadre mentoring [32]. In Selangor and Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia (Dapari et al., 2024), the iDEAL education module was shown to be effective in reducing dengue incidence in schools [37]. A study in Puducherry, India (Jency et al., 2024) found that education for sanitation workers improved their knowledge and practices, although participation was low [22]. A study in Yogyakarta, Indonesia (Sulistyawati et al., 2019) emphasized the importance of community empowerment in vector control, despite low participation in the use of control cards. Overall, community-based education proved effective in dengue control, but a bottom-up approach is needed to increase motivation and broader community participation [34].5)Integrated Interventions

5)Integrated Interventions

Integrated Interventions

In several studies, the Integrated Approach (Biological + Chemical), which combines the release of Wolbachia-infected mosquitoes with chemical control through insecticide fogging, has been shown to significantly reduce dengue incidence and insecticide use, as observed in the study in Yogyakarta by Indriani et al. (2023) [31]. Another approach, the Integrated Approach (Biological + Health Education and Community Empowerment), which combines the release of Wolbachia-infected mosquitoes with community-based health education and empowerment, also successfully raised public awareness and reduced dengue incidence, as found in Singapore by Lim et al. (2024) and in Yogyakarta by Indriani et al. (2020) [33,42].

Moreover, the Integrated Approach (Biological + Physical + Environmental Management + Health Education + Digital Technology), as implemented in Cambodia by Bigio et al. (2022), demonstrated effectiveness by combining physical and environmental management, health education, and digital technology to monitor larvae and educate the public [29]. The Integrated Approach (Chemical + Environmental Management + Health Education), which combines chemical control, environmental management, and community-based health education, as applied in Delhi by Nagpal et al. (2016), also successfully reduced larval indices and controlled disease spread [24]. Meanwhile, the Integrated Approach (Health Education + Digital Technology), which utilizes digital technology in health education, proved effective in enhancing community outreach and vector surveillance, as demonstrated in studies in Thailand by Suwanbamrung et al. (2018, 2024) [43,45].

The Integrated Approach (Health Education and Community Empowerment + Environmental Management), carried out in Delhi by Lachyan et al. (2023), as well as the Integrated Approach (Health Education and Community Empowerment + Physical + Environmental Management + Digital Technology), implemented in Kerala by Gopalan et al. (2021), showed positive results in reducing mosquito populations through community empowerment, environmental management, and digital technology [23]. Lastly, the Integrated Approach (Health Education and Community Empowerment + Chemical + Biological), which combines health education, community empowerment, and both chemical and biological control, proved effective in reducing larval numbers and dengue incidence, as found in the research in Pakistan by Younas et al. (2021) [26].

Overall, the results of various interventions emphasize that an integrated approach involving various aspects of biology, chemistry, education, community empowerment, environmental management, and technology, as well as combined interventions, is a highly effective strategy in controlling the Aedes aegypti mosquito population and reducing the incidence of dengue disease. These approaches not only succeeded in reducing the number of mosquitoes but also increased community awareness and active participation in disease prevention efforts.

Challenges in implementation

Several key themes have emerged, reflecting the various challenges encountered in this scoping review, including:1)Community Engagement and Participation

1)Community Engagement and Participation

Community Engagement and Participation

Community Engagement and Participation has emerged as a significant challenge, with many vector control programs facing issues related to the low public knowledge of dengue and limited active participation in prevention programs [21,23]. Despite widespread awareness of the importance of control efforts, uncooperative attitudes and a lack of motivation remain major barriers [26,45]. Additionally, the diverse responses to prevention methods, such as the use of spatial repellents or community-based control techniques, highlight challenges in building awareness and ensuring compliance with established preventive practices [27].2)Resource, Logistics, and Data Management Issues

2)Resource, Logistics, and Data Management Issues

Resource, Logistics, and Data Management Issues

Resource, Logistics, and Data Management Issues are also major obstacles in intervention implementation. Many studies point to limitations in resource distribution, both human and material, which hinder the effectiveness of vector control [28,31]. For example, uneven distribution of Wolbachia mosquitoes or a shortage of personnel to monitor and maintain mosquito traps present significant challenges to ensuring intervention success. Accurate data collection is often impeded by logistical constraints and difficulties in ensuring complete and valid data from all intervention sites [28,42].3)Public Perceptions and Knowledge

3)Public Perceptions and Knowledge

Public Perceptions and Knowledge

Public Perceptions and Knowledge of vector control methods also act as hindrances. While the public may be aware of the dengue threat, they often lack a deeper understanding of Aedes mosquito behavior or effective prevention measures [21,28]. Misconceptions about the effectiveness of methods such as fogging and larvicides contribute to low participation in vector control efforts [23]. Many studies have found that, despite improvements in knowledge, achieving long-term behavior change remains challenging [25]. A lack of deep understanding regarding the importance of vector prevention among the community poses a barrier to maintaining active participation in vector control programs.4)Control Methods and Evaluation of Effectiveness

4)Control Methods and Evaluation of Effectiveness

Control Methods and Evaluation of Effectiveness

Control Methods and Evaluation of Effectiveness have also emerged as significant issues in this research. Difficulties in measuring spillover effects or the impact of interventions on areas that do not receive direct treatment highlight barriers in assessing the benefits of control technologies such as Wolbachia or other mosquito traps [36,41]. Evaluating the effectiveness of interventions becomes more complex due to challenges in isolating the influence of the intervention from other external factors, such as changes in community behavior or weather effects [30,35,38]. External factors, such as weather conditions and human mobility, have been identified as major challenges in maintaining the consistency and sustainability of vector control interventions. Weather variability, such as rainfall or extreme temperatures, affects the effectiveness of traps or other control systems, often diminishing the desired control outcomes [24,38].5)Social and Economic Issues

5)Social and Economic Issues

Social and Economic Issues

Social and Economic Issues also influence the success of vector control programs. Social factors such as population density and limited resources exacerbate the challenges faced in mosquito control implementation. Inequalities in access to proper sanitation facilities and inadequate environmental management often hinder the success of interventions [25,26]. Socio-economic conditions, such as poverty and inequality, further amplify the spread and impact of dengue. Economic limitations can restrict individuals' ability to adhere to recommended preventive practices, such as purchasing insecticides or maintaining a clean environment [46].

In conclusion, disease control faces a variety of interrelated challenges. Active community engagement and participation have proven to be key factors influencing the success of interventions, with many programs encountering difficulties related to low public knowledge and attitudes towards prevention. Additionally, resource and logistical constraints, both in terms of material distribution and workforce availability, remain significant barriers that slow the implementation and maintenance of programs. Another challenge identified is the uncertainty regarding the effectiveness of control methods, influenced by various external factors such as weather, data management variability, and difficulties in measuring the impact of control efforts in areas not directly exposed to interventions. Inconsistent data management and difficulties in ensuring equitable distribution of control measures also emphasize the need for strengthening monitoring and evaluation systems to enhance data accuracy and improve intervention effectiveness. Social and economic factors, along with inadequate infrastructure, also hinder the success of interventions in some regions, given the reliance on voluntary participation and imbalances in access to sanitation facilities. This underscores the importance of cross-sector collaboration to strengthen dengue prevention efforts at the community level.

Recommendations for improving effectiveness

Based on the research conducted by various authors on vector control and dengue prevention, several recommendations have emerged that can strengthen the interventions already in place. Most studies emphasize the importance of:1)Community Involvement

1)Community Involvement

Community Involvement

Several studies in this scoping review recommend increasing community involvement through continuous training and strengthening local capacity to independently manage vector control activities [21,23,25]. Therefore, implementing ongoing training programs to enhance knowledge and skills, as well as empowering local leaders in vector control programs, is highly recommended.2)Strengthening Monitoring, Ongoing Evaluation, and Broader Implementation of Technology, Including New Technologies, in Interventions

2)Strengthening Monitoring, Ongoing Evaluation, and Broader Implementation of Technology, Including New Technologies, in Interventions

Strengthening Monitoring, Ongoing Evaluation, and Broader Implementation of Technology, Including New Technologies, in Interventions

Several studies highlight the importance of strengthening monitoring, ongoing evaluation, and the broader implementation of technology, including new technologies, in interventions. Research by Wanasinghe et al. (2024), Suwanbamrung et al. (2024), and Lim et al. (2024) recommends the use of technology for real-time data analysis and more frequent and comprehensive monitoring of larval indices and mosquito populations [28,41,45]. Indriani et al. (2020) and Indriani et al. (2023) also suggest increasing the frequency and distribution of Wolbachia-infected mosquito releases, along with greater involvement in monitoring and evaluating sustainability [31,33]. Tissera et al. (2023), Ong et al. (2022), and Shahar et al. (2022) recommend using new technologies such as Wolbachia-infected mosquitoes, Gravid Female Traps (GFT), and spatial repellents to strengthen mosquito control efforts. They suggest the wider application of technology-based interventions, including increased frequency of Wolbachia mosquito releases or spatial repellent use, along with large-scale trials to assess their effectiveness [27,38,40]. Additionally, the use of pyriproxyfen-treated ovitraps by Reza et al. (2016) and copper-based ovitraps by Ligsay et al. (2022) highlights the importance of controlling mosquito larvae with copper solutions at a concentration of 10 ppm. These vector control technologies need to be improved with tight monitoring and equitable distribution to increase their effectiveness. They also recommend increasing community training to ensure more effective use of these control tools [30,39]. These recommendations are crucial for ensuring the long-term success of dengue vector control efforts. They emphasize the importance of involving various parties to strengthen existing interventions and expand the reach and effectiveness of dengue control programs at the community level. The movement of people, which can affect the distribution of Wolbachia exposure, also needs to be considered. Anders et al. (2018) propose strengthening data collection related to human mobility in secondary analysis to estimate its impact on the reduction of dengue incidence [35]. More intensive monitoring of larval indices and the use of computer technology for real-time data analysis are also recommended by Suwanbamrung et al. (2018) to enhance the response to potential dengue outbreaks [43]. Lastly, the effectiveness of school uniforms treated with permethrin should be monitored, as suggested by Kittayapong et al. (2017), to address the reduced efficacy due to rapid washing of uniforms. Developing new technology to enhance permethrin durability on uniforms and reduce the decline in protection against mosquito bites is an important step to support disease control efforts [44].3)Increasing Public Awareness and Education

3)Increasing Public Awareness and Education

Increasing Public Awareness and Education

Narendran et al. (2024), Tissera et al. (2023), and Younas et al. (2021) emphasize the need for broader educational campaigns to increase public knowledge about the causes and prevention of dengue, as well as the importance of active public participation in mosquito control. They recommend utilizing social media and other digital platforms to raise public awareness about dengue [21,26,27].4)Expanding Interventions Through School and Community-Based Education and Training

4)Expanding Interventions Through School and Community-Based Education and Training

Expanding Interventions Through School and Community-Based Education and Training

Researchers such as Dapari et al. (2024), Suwanbamrung et al. (2024), and Sulistyawati et al. (2019) suggest expanding school-based interventions by involving more students, parents, and community stakeholders to strengthen the impact of vector control [34,37,45].5)Involvement and Training of Health Workers

5)Involvement and Training of Health Workers

Involvement and Training of Health Workers

Jency et al. (2024) and Ridha et al. (2023) highlight the importance of training sanitation workers and health cadres to enhance their capacity to manage vector control and educate the public [22,32]. Therefore, providing structured training for health workers and cadres, involving effective communication on dengue prevention, is crucial.6)Use of a combined approach

6)Use of a combined approach

Use of a combined approach

Bigio et al. (2022) and Gopalan et al. (2021) show that combining bio-physical control approaches, environmental management, and community capacity building can lead to more effective results [23,29]. Therefore, integrating a more comprehensive vector control approach is essential.

Discussion

This scoping review synthesizes the results of various vector control interventions for dengue, with a focus on the effectiveness of interventions implemented over the past one decades. One of the key findings from physical interventions is the use of mosquito traps, such as the Gravid Female Trap (GFT) in Sri Lanka and MyMAT in Malaysia, which significantly reduced Aedes mosquito populations and dengue incidence [28,38]. Both studies highlight that physical approaches, using simple and chemical-free technology, can be a long-term solution to mosquito vector control without harmful environmental impacts. The use of MyMAT in Malaysia, which successfully captured 97 % of Aedes mosquitoes, shows great potential for sustainable approaches without relying on insecticides. Physical interventions often focus on eliminating or managing mosquito breeding sites, such as water containers, which can significantly reduce mosquito populations. For example, community-led efforts in Chennai, India, resulted in a significant reduction in the pupal index and other entomological indices [47]. Physical interventions, especially those involving community mobilization and environmental management, are often more cost-effective than chemical control. This approach can be sustained over time with community support, reducing the need for repeated chemical treatments [48]. Although physical interventions have clear benefits, several factors need to be considered to ensure their effectiveness, including reliance on sustainability and community involvement [47]. Integration with other intervention actions or an integrated approach by combining physical interventions with other interventions such as chemical, biological, environmental, and others [[49], [50], [51], [52]], as well as environmental and socio-economic factors [53,54].

Chemical interventions also play an important role in control efforts. Studies on the use of larvicides such as copper, pyriproxyfen, and permethrin show varied results. While some larvicides successfully reduced mosquito populations, their impact on reducing dengue infections remains limited [30,39]. This suggests that chemical interventions cannot work optimally without strict supervision and integration with other approaches. Therefore, mosquito control should involve a combination of chemical, physical, and biological approaches to ensure long-term success.

The use of Wolbachia in biological interventions, as found in various studies in Indonesia, Singapore, and Malaysia, has shown significant results [31,33,35,36,[40], [41], [42]]. The application of Wolbachia not only reduced dengue incidence in areas directly exposed to the intervention but also demonstrated spillover effects, reducing incidence in surrounding areas. This highlights the potential of Wolbachia as a highly effective and sustainable vector control solution. The Wolbachia program, especially when combined with techniques such as the Incompatible Insect Technique (IIT) and Sterile Insect Technique (SIT), has consistently shown effectiveness in reducing Aedes mosquito populations and dengue transmission, particularly in Singapore and Yogyakarta [35,41].

Furthermore, health education and community empowerment approaches have proven to be key factors in increasing public awareness and participation in vector control. Research in India and Indonesia has shown that community-based interventions involving education through models such as the Health Belief Model or the One House One Jumantik (G1R1J) program have successfully improved knowledge and preventive practices.

Integrated interventions in several studies have also been widely implemented, combining biological + chemical, biological + health education and community empowerment [31], biological + health education and community empowerment [33,42], biological + physical + environmental management + health education + digital technology [29], chemical + environmental management + health education [24], health education + digital technology [43,45], health education and community empowerment + environmental management [25], health education and community empowerment + physical + environmental management + digital technology [23], and health education and community empowerment + chemical + biological [26]. Integrated interventions, which combine multiple strategies, have proven to be more effective in preventing and controlling dengue and reducing entomological indices compared to single interventions [[55], [56], [57]]. In addition to enhancing effectiveness, the combination of interventions or strategies can also improve sustainability [58].

Overall, while various interventions have proven effective in reducing Aedes mosquito populations and dengue incidence, several challenges or barriers were identified in their implementation, along with recommendations for improving interventions in the future to ensure their effectiveness and sustainability. Some of the issues identified in this scoping review include a lack of community involvement and participation, resource and logistical challenges and data management issues, public perceptions and knowledge, the methods of control used, and the complex evaluation of effectiveness due to difficulties in separating the impact of interventions from other external factors. Social and economic issues also influence the success of interventions. Based on the findings of this review, several recommendations to strengthen future dengue vector control interventions include: first, increasing community involvement through continuous training and empowering local capacity to independently manage vector control activities. Second, strengthening monitoring approaches, ongoing evaluation, and the broader implementation of technology-based interventions. Several studies emphasize the importance of using technology for real-time data analysis and more frequent and comprehensive monitoring of larval indices and mosquito populations, as well as the application of new technologies such as Wolbachia-infected mosquitoes, Gravid Female Traps (GFT), and spatial repellents. Research also recommends increasing the frequency of Wolbachia mosquito releases and broader use of technology-based mosquito control interventions, including large-scale trials to assess their effectiveness. Additionally, controlling mosquito larvae with copper solution and ensuring tight monitoring and equitable distribution are also suggested to improve vector control effectiveness. Third, enhancing public awareness and education through wider campaigns, utilizing digital platforms such as social media to spread information on dengue prevention. Fourth, expanding interventions through school and community-based education and training, involving students, parents, and community stakeholders to strengthen the impact of vector control. Fifth, improving the training and involvement of health workers and cadres, providing structured training to enhance their capacity in managing vector control and educating the public. Finally, employing a combined approach that integrates various strategies such as bio-physical control, environmental management, community capacity building, or other combinations to achieve more effective and sustainable results.

Conclusion

This review of 25 studies on dengue vector control in South and Southeast Asia highlights regional differences. South Asia primarily uses health education and community empowerment (25 %), with pre-post designs (62.5 %) focused on knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP). In Southeast Asia, biological and chemical interventions (23.5 % and 17.6 %, respectively) are more common, alongside a focus on reducing dengue incidence (35.3 %). Southeast Asia also employs more cluster randomized trials (41.2 %) and integrated vector management (IVM) strategies. Several issues identified in this scoping review include a lack of community involvement and participation, resource and logistical challenges, data management issues, public perceptions and knowledge, control methods used, and the complex evaluation of effectiveness due to difficulties in isolating the impact of interventions from other external factors, as well as social and economic issues that also affect the success of interventions. Based on the review's findings, several recommendations to strengthen future dengue vector control interventions include: enhancing community involvement through continuous training and empowering local capacity, improving monitoring and evaluation with technology-based solutions for real-time data analysis and frequent mosquito population monitoring, and increasing the use of technologies like Wolbachia-infected mosquitoes and spatial repellents. Additionally, expanding public awareness campaigns through digital platforms, strengthening school- and community-based education, and improving training for health workers and cadres are essential. Lastly, adopting a combined approach that integrates bio-physical control, environmental management, and community capacity building is recommended to ensure more effective and sustainable results.

Limitation and strengths

Although this scoping review has aimed to provide a comprehensive overview of the effectiveness of dengue vector control interventions, there are limitations that should be noted. One limitation is the use of varying outcome measures across studies, both in terms of mosquito population control and dengue infection reduction, which may reduce the consistency of the results. This scoping review has several significant strengths. One of its strengths is the broad literature search sourced from five major databases, which allowed us to ensure that relevant publications on dengue vector control have been included in this review, providing a more comprehensive view of existing interventions. Another strength is the methodology used in the search, screening, and data extraction process. Each phase in this process was tested and cross-checked to minimize bias, which enhances the reliability of the results of this review. Additionally, the quality assessment conducted for each study ensures that the included studies are of sufficient relevance and quality to make meaningful contributions to this review.
