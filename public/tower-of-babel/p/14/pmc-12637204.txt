The Indian Ocean Dipole drives imported-dominated dengue outbreaks in China: Mechanisms and predictions
Authors: Jian Ma, Lei Xu, Qian Han, Jing Gao, Huihui Yuan, Kaixing Dong, Cunrui Huang, Cui Zhou, John S Ji, Chutian Zhang, Huatang Zeng, Yongman Guo, Lexuan Luo, Xiangliang Zhang, Yong Luo, Qiyong Liu, Nils Chr Stenseth, Wannian Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDengue fever, influenced by climate dynamics and human mobility in nonendemic regions, remains poorly understood. We assessed the effects of large-scale climate features on domestic dengue outbreaks using data from China (2013–2021) and projections for 2023–2028, incorporating climate, human mobility, and environmental factors. A significant positive correlation (r = 0.89, P < 0.01) was found between domestic dengue incidence and overseas imported cases. Significant associations were also noted with domestic (F2.96, 25.78 = 518.03, P < 0.01) and international human mobility (F2.77, 25.78 = 66.84, P < 0.01), the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) index (F2.98, 25.78 = 522.84), and vectorial capacity (F2.92, 25.78 = 338.74, P < 0.01). IOD is the most influential climate feature, with a trend of intensification over time. The Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithm incorporating IOD (r2 = 0.56) predicted outbreaks in 2025, 2026, and 2028. Our findings reveal that large-scale climate phenomenon, notably the IOD, and human mobility significantly influence dengue domestic outbreaks in China through a “dislocated impact.”

The rapid global spread of dengue poses a significant health threat, especially in nonendemic countries like China where overseas imported cases are continuing to increase. Our study highlights the significant impact of the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) on domestic dengue outbreaks in China, intensified by imported cases and human mobility, especially from Southeast Asia. Integrating regional climate with human movement patterns, our predictive model not only enhances dengue forecasting but also informs strategies for other infectious diseases. This work advances understanding of imported disease dynamics and underscores the IOD's role in domestic dengue management, offering innovative prevention and control approaches amid global changes.

Introduction

Dengue fever, a climate-sensitive mosquito-borne disease caused by the dengue virus (DENV) and transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes, annually impacts an estimated 390 million individuals across 129 countries (1, 2). Its spread is primarily influenced by climatic factors and human mobility, and the disease poses a growing global threat (3). In China, dengue outbreaks are primarily triggered by imported cases from Southeast Asia (4), but it is the subsequent local transmission that drives large-scale epidemics within affected cities. Notably, the mechanisms governing the importation of cases differ substantially from those driving local transmission, reflecting distinct ecological and epidemiological dynamics. Understanding the transition from imported to locally sustained transmission is therefore critical for accurate risk forecasting and the development of targeted intervention strategies.

Local dengue transmission is closely tied to climatic conditions that influence mosquito abundance and virus replication dynamics (5–7). Local climate conditions, such as temperature and precipitation, are critical in determining mosquito behavior and the viral life cycle (7, 8). These elements not only affect transmission efficiency and the virus's incubation period but also modify the breeding environments for mosquitoes (9 ). Further compounding this issue is climate change, which alters temperature and rainfall patterns, potentially expanding risk zones into previously unaffected latitudes, including southeastern China (5, 10–12 ). In addition to climatic factors, population density and environmental indicators such as the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and the Water Index (NDWI) play significant roles in the transmission dynamics of dengue (13–15). High population densities can facilitate faster spread of the disease through increased human contact, while vegetation indices are crucial in defining the habitats suitable for mosquito breeding (16).

At broader temporal and spatial scales, large-scale climate oscillations modulate regional weather systems and may exert indirect yet substantial influences on local dengue dynamics (17). Local climate primarily affects the timing of dengue outbreaks, while large-scale climate patterns influence long-term prevalence trends, while phenomena such as the El Niño Southern Oscillation (ENSO) and the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) influence long-term prevalence trends (17). Originating in the tropical Pacific Ocean, ENSO interacts with the ocean and atmosphere to create extreme weather conditions that heighten infectious disease risks (18–21). The warm phase of ENSO, known as the El Niño index, is strongly linked to increased dengue activity, especially in regions like Guangdong Province, China (22). Similarly, the IOD, marked by sea surface temperature variations in the Indian Ocean, significantly impacts regional climates and dengue transmission, particularly in India, Southeast Asia, and Taiwan in China (22–27). Recent studies also highlight how the Indian Ocean Basin-Wide (IOBW) index, which tracks tropical Indian Ocean temperature anomalies, can influence local temperatures and, consequently, the global incidence of dengue (28). Additionally, other climatic patterns such as the Atlantic Niño (AN) and the Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation (AMO) play roles in regional and global climate dynamics, with the AN influencing weather patterns during the boreal summer and the AMO impacting health outcomes such as rickets incidence in the United Kingdom by altering atmospheric conditions (29, 30). Notably, the impact of the IOD on regional climate are more pronounced in Southeast Asia and southwestern China than in southeast China (31), contrasting with ENSO's wider geographic influence (32, 33). During the positive phase of IOD, increased moisture from the Indian and Pacific Oceans converges over Southeast Asia, traveling through the Bay of Bengal and the South China Sea before entering southwestern China under warm, humid monsoon currents (34–36). Investigating the impacts of large-scale climate phenomena like IOD and ENSO on dengue transmission in mainland China is of great importance and warrants further research.

Human mobility substantially complicates the epidemiological landscape of dengue fever. The proliferation of air travel has significantly facilitated the emergence and re-emergence of dengue cases (4, 37–40). This dynamic illustrates how globalization and increased human movement exacerbate public health vulnerabilities by accelerating the spread of infectious diseases. Building on this context, the perturbations induced by the COVID-19 pandemic have further highlighted the critical role of human mobility in the propagation of infectious diseases, including dengue (41, 42). A compelling example of this was observed in Guangzhou, a major urban center in China, where a drastic reduction in local dengue cases was reported in 2020, with only two cases compared to 1,385 in the previous year (42). This illustrates the significant potential of monitoring human movement to enhance our understanding of dengue transmission patterns and to inform the development of effective public health surveillance and intervention strategies (35, 43, 44).

In this study, we focus exclusively on locally transmitted dengue incidence in mainland China during the period 2013–2021. Our objective is to elucidate the environmental and climatic drivers that shape the spatiotemporal dynamics of local dengue outbreaks, with particular emphasis on the indirect influence of large-scale climate oscillations, such as the IOD. To this end, we develop an integrative modeling framework incorporating climate variables, vectorial capacity, environmental indicators, population density, and overseas dengue activity as potential predictors. Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) serves as the primary tool for predictive modeling, enabling the capture of complex nonlinear interactions and the quantification of feature importance. Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) are further applied to characterize smooth nonlinear relationships between key predictors and dengue incidence. Finally, these approaches aim to enhance precision forecasting and inform early warning systems for climate-sensitive vector-borne diseases in China and similar settings.

Environmental factors, climate factors, and human mobility driving dengue incidence (2013–2021)

Our optimal GAM effectively explains dengue incidence from 2013 to 2021, achieving an r2 value of 0.33 and explaining 32.87% of the deviance. The spatiotemporal distribution of the model residuals is detailed in the Supplementary material (Fig. S3). This model demonstrates the impacts of local climate and environmental factors, VC, human mobility, and large-scale climate features on dengue incidence. Concerning each factor's specific influence, the model demonstrated a U-shaped impact of minimum temperature on dengue incidence (F2.85, 25.78 = 9.18, P < 0.01, Fig. 3A); a negative impact of cumulative precipitation (F2.87, 25.78 = 26.14, P < 0.01, Fig. 3B); an N-shaped impact of the NDWI index (F2.93, 25.78 = 17.65, P < 0.01, Fig. 3C); and positive impacts of population density (F2.49, 25.78 = 3.52, P < 0.01, Fig. 3D), the VC index with a lag of 2 months (F2.92, 25.78 = 338.74, P < 0.01, Fig. 3E), the IHM index (F2.77, 25.78 = 66.84, P < 0.01, Fig. 3F), and the DHM index (F2.96, 25.78 = 518.03, P < 0.01, Fig. 3G). Among large-scale climate phenomena, the strongest correlation was observed for the IOD index with a lag of 0 months (F2.98, 25.78 = 522.84, P < 0.01, Fig. 3H, Tables S1 and S2), with a positive impact on dengue incidence. This correlation surpassed those of ENSO index (Δewts-CV-3year = 0.0038), the IOBW index (Δewts-CV-3year = 0.0057), AN index (Δewts-CV-3year = 0.0061), and the AMO index (Δewts-CV-3year = 0.0045) (Table 1 and Table S1). Our cross-correlation function (CCF) results further validate that the time-lagged correlation between IOD and overseas imported cases, as well as the correlation between overseas imported cases and dengue incidence, both peak within the current month or 1 lag month (Fig. S4). Furthermore, we constructed two sets of GAM for comparing with the optimal GAM (Tables S3 and S4). For with overseas imported cases GAM, IOD had an explanatory power of 33.76%, outperforming ENSO, which had an explanatory power of 30.40%. For without overseas imported cases GAM, IOD also had a higher explanatory power of 20.36% compared to ENSO's 18.41% (Table S5). Notably, IOD maintained its robustness even after with and without overseas imported cases (Figs S5 and S6, Table S5). Additional, both phases of the IOD showed positive effects, but the effect in the positive phase (F2.61, 28.18 = 986.83, P < 0.01, Fig. S7h) was significantly higher than in the negative phase (F3.00, 28.18 = 2.93, P = 0.03, Fig. S7i). All four seasons of the IOD exhibited positive effects, with the effects in summer (F2.95, 34.38 = 533.41, P < 0.01, Fig. S8h) and autumn (F2.91, 34.38 = 65.50, P < 0.01, Fig. S8i) being significantly higher than in spring (F2.98, 34.38 = 76.29, P < 0.01, Fig. S8j) and winter (F2.95, 34.38 = 65.50, P < 0.01, Fig. S8k).

Predictive models for dengue outbreaks incorporating the IOD (2023–2028)

The spatiotemporal prediction model that incorporated IOD (r2 = 0.56) demonstrated enhanced efficacy compared to the model that excluded IOD considerations (r2 = 0.44) in predicting major dengue outbreaks over the subsequent 6 years (Fig. 4A). In comparison to GAM, our XGBoost approach demonstrated superior performance in spatiotemporal predictions, with trends in the relationships between various factors and dengue incidence aligning closely with those observed in the GAM (Fig. S9, Table S6). Residual analysis indicated no significant temporal differences between years; however, notable spatial differences were observed among provinces, with detailed residual analysis provided in the Supplementary material (Figs. S10 and S11). Temporally, our XGBoost model predicts a higher incidence in intermediate- and high-risk areas (exceeding 20 cumulative cases from 2023 to 2028), totaling 87 cities (Fig. 4B). Spatially, our XGBoost model predicts major dengue outbreaks in the upcoming years, notably in October 2025 (predicted 1,502 cases), September 2026 (predicted 1,705 cases), and September 2028 (predicted 1,774 cases), with the highest number of cases expected in Guangdong Province, followed by Yunnan and Chongqing (Figs. 4A and C).

Discussion

This study provides compelling evidence that the IOD plays a role in domestic dengue outbreaks in China. Despite the ENSO–IOD relationship (ENSO is one of the main triggers of IOD events) (27), our findings indicate that the IOD has a stronger correlation and greater explanatory power in accounting for domestic dengue cases compared to other large-scale climate indices, including the ENSO. The increasing explanatory power of the IOD in recent decades highlights its growing influence on dengue dynamics, likely due to the intensification of global climate anomalies and increasing human mobility.

Our analysis revealed a strong positive correlation between overseas imported cases and domestic dengue incidence, particularly in high-risk TFcities such as Guangzhou, Shenzhen, and Xishuangbanna. This underscores the dominant role of imported cases in triggering local outbreaks. By XGBoost machine learning, we confirmed that the IOD's influence on dengue transmission operates through a “dislocated impact” mechanism. This indirect but critical climate-disease linkage highlights the urgent need for cross-border disease monitoring and intervention strategies.

Key drivers of domestic dengue incidence

The IOD emerged as a more potent predictor than the ENSO, the IOBW index, and other global climate oscillations. Local environmental factors such as temperature, precipitation, the NDWI, and VC also significantly impact dengue transmission. However, their influence is overshadowed by the effects of large-scale climate events and human mobility. For instance, temperature displayed a U-shaped relationship with domestic dengue incidence, reflecting an optimal range for mosquito activity, while excessive rainfall, indicated by negative correlations with precipitation, potentially disrupts mosquito breeding habitats. The NDWI's N-shaped pattern suggests complex interactions between water presence and mosquito breeding sites, highlighting urban water storage as a significant factor exacerbating dengue risk, as observed in studies from regions like Vietnam (45).

The analysis accentuates the IOD as a critical driver of regional climate patterns affecting domestic dengue dynamics in China. During positive IOD phases, increased rainfall heightens domestic dengue transmission risks (46, 47). The complex interplay between IOD and ENSO complicates their individual contributions to domestic dengue outbreaks, as extreme IOD events can amplify or modulate ENSO effects (48). This interaction is supported by significant correlations between IOD and ENSO, suggesting a synergistic impact on regional climate and subsequently on domestic dengue transmission (Fig. S12). Furthermore, while other climate models such as the AMO (49) and the AN (50) also affect the global climate, their impact on dengue incidence in our study region is less pronounced compared to the IOD (Table 1 and Table S1). Unlike the IOBW mode, which exhibits decadal oscillations (51, 52), the IOD's interannual oscillations closely align with the short-term fluctuations in local dengue outbreaks, especially during positive IOD events and in the warmer seasons (53) (Figs. S4 and S5, Table S7). Our analysis also underscores the impact of the IOD through overseas imported cases leading to local transmission in China, evidenced by the correlation between the IOD index from the previous year and the annual number of overseas imported dengue cases through TFcities (Fig. S13). The rising trend in the monthly IOD index from 1980, particularly from 2013 to 2021 (Fig. S14), corresponds with an increased relationship between the IOD and domestic dengue incidence (Fig. S15), potentially exacerbated by the global rise in surface temperatures which intensifies climate anomalies including those associated with the IOD (52–54). This observed trend necessitates enhanced surveillance and preventive strategies to address the changing dynamics of domestic dengue transmission influenced by these important climate variations.

Human mobility plays a pivotal role in shaping dengue dynamics in China, with a substantial proportion of local cases attributable to imported infections (35). Phylogenetic analysis further bolsters this perspective by demonstrating that dengue cases in regions like Guangdong exhibit multiple serotypes and genotypes of DENV with external origins (55). This highlights the importance of regional climate conditions in Southeast Asia and their impact on disease transmission dynamics in China. Future strategies for dengue control must consider these dynamic and interlinked factors, emphasizing the need for enhanced surveillance and integrated disease management approaches that account for both local environmental conditions and global climate influences.

Long-term climate-dengue associations

Our wavelet analysis provides a long-term perspective on the relationship between climate variability and dengue incidence, revealing a strengthening correlation between the IOD and dengue outbreaks in recent decades (Figs. S14, S15, and S16). While the association was relatively weak before 2004, it became more pronounced in the 2005–2012 period and further intensified between 2013 and 2021. This suggests that climate-driven dengue fluctuations have become more prominent over time, likely due to increasing globalization, trade, and human mobility. The observed periodicity of ∼1 year in the wavelet coherence analysis aligns with seasonal dengue transmission cycles, suggesting that annual climate variations, particularly those associated with the IOD, exert a recurrent influence on outbreak patterns (51). Our findings support the hypothesis that large-scale climate anomalies in the Indian Ocean region can modulate dengue transmission dynamics by altering weather patterns in endemic regions like Southeast Asia, which subsequently impact imported cases in China. Furthermore, the increasing explanatory power of IOD over time implies that its role in dengue variability is becoming more critical. This may be partly attributed to climate change, which has been linked to shifts in IOD variability and an overall intensification of extreme climate events (56, 57). Future research should investigate how long-term changes in the IOD and other climate modes could influence dengue risk at a global scale.

Spatiotemporal prediction of domestic dengue outbreaks

Our predictive model accurately described both the temporal dynamics and the broad geographical distribution of historical dengue incidence. With the conclusion of the COVID-19 pandemic and resumption of human mobility, we anticipate a reversal of the decline in case numbers following domestic outbreaks in 2020–2021, especially in Guangdong and Yunnan provinces (38–40). Notably, our predictions align with previous research suggesting that future dengue risk will be concentrated in China's southern, eastern, and central regions (4). Furthermore, our comparative analysis of prediction models with and without the IOD index demonstrated the former to provide a better fit to the actual situation. In particular, it better captured the seasonality of dengue, especially in Yunnan, and more accurately identified high-risk areas (Fig. 4A). Importantly, our final model's prediction of dengue cases in 2023 is consistent with the number of observed cases, which confirms its accuracy (Fig. 5A and Fig. S17). Further predictions indicate that the emergence of future positive IOD events may lead to Guangdong Province experiencing major domestic outbreaks between 2023 and 2028 (Figs. 5A, Figs. S18 and S19).

Global influences and preventive strategies for dengue control

The recent escalation of the dengue epidemic in both China and Southeast Asia, notably observed after 2005 and intensifying from 2013 onwards, is attributed to the continuous expansion of international trade and tourism. Given the ongoing expansion of international air travel in the foreseeable future, the “dislocated impact” of climatic conditions is expected to become even more pronounced. Our empirical modeling approach, particularly incorporating information on the IOD, can facilitate the development of practical strategies for the early prevention and control of imported diseases. It is imperative to prioritize efforts towards comprehending the local climate and environment conditions in key endemic regions to stop outbreaks from becoming pandemic (58).

Our study on domestic dengue outbreaks has several notable limitations. Firstly, we lacked data on DENV serotypes, which limits our epidemiological insights. Changing immunity levels to dengue within China's population may mask the effects of environmental factors on transmission dynamics. Research from endemic regions indicates that infection with one dengue serotype offers pronounced short-term cross-protection against other serotypes (59), suggesting that growing immunity could lessen the influence of environmental factors on transmission, potentially undermining the associations identified in our analysis. Furthermore, disease prevalence estimates may be biased by underreporting asymptomatic cases. Our focus on air travel overlooks land-based transmission routes, which may be especially important in border regions like Yunnan Province. Additionally, a recent study (60) identified a long-term weakening trend on a millennial time scale of IOD variability due to greenhouse gas emissions, which is crucial for understanding long-term changes and impacts. However, our study primarily focuses on short-term variations of the IOD, particularly its interannual variations and their impact on dengue transmission. These long-term impacts warrant further exploration in future research. Integrating more data sources and longer time scales will be essential for comprehensively understanding the long-term impacts of IOD and other climate factors on dengue transmission. Addressing these gaps is crucial for improving the accuracy and scope of future predictive modeling in dengue research.

In conclusion, our study provides a comprehensive analysis and forecasts domestic dengue outbreaks in China, highlighting the significant impact of the IOD. This influence is notably more pronounced when compared to other large-scale climate phenomena. By integrating regional climate influences, human mobility, and VC within the framework of “dislocated impact,” we refined the accuracy of our predictions. Our comprehensive methodology redefines the benchmark for predicting vector-borne diseases, delivering a flexible and scalable model that can be adapted to study additional vector-borne diseases and applied to diverse global health challenges.

Materials and methods

Summary of analytical steps performed in the study

This study employed multiple models to analyze the driving mechanisms of the IOD on dengue outbreaks and predict future spatiotemporal outbreak trends (Fig. 5). First, we collected monthly dengue case data at the city scale across mainland China (2013–2021) and matched covariates including corresponding spatiotemporal-scale VC, local/large-scale climatic indices, population density, and human mobility. Using XGBoost, we evaluated the nonlinear effects of these covariates on dengue outbreaks, identifying the optimal covariate combination through cross-validation value comparisons. And projected monthly dengue cases at the urban scale in China (2023–2028) using future covariate datasets. Subsequently, we investigated long-term relationship variations between IOD and dengue base on national monthly incidence data (1980–2021) using wavelet analysis. To enhance predictive capacity, we developed an XGBoost model based on the optimal covariates identified by GAM. To assess IOD's contribution, we compared model performance and predictions between configurations with and without IOD. To mitigate uncertainties from different future climate scenarios, final projections were derived from ensemble means of three climate models. Detailed computational and analytical methodologies are presented below.

Data collection

Dengue case data

Based on travel history, dengue cases are classified as imported dengue cases for those who traveled abroad within the 14 days prior to onset, or domestic dengue cases for those with no international travel during that period, with all patients having a history of mosquito bites.

Monthly records of both locally transmitted and overseas imported dengue cases across 366 cities in mainland China (excluding Hong Kong, Macau, Taiwan, and Sansha) from 2013 to 2021 were obtained from the Chinese National Notifiable Infectious Disease Reporting Information System, based on standardized epidemiological survey data (Supplementary Methods, Fig. S20). For predictive modeling, these city-level incidence data were primarily incorporated into the XGBoost framework to ensure temporal consistency with available climate, environmental, and human mobility covariates during the same period. GAMs were also employed as a complementary approach to capture nonlinear relationships. To investigate long-term associations between climate variability and dengue activity, we additionally utilized a national-level dengue incidence time series spanning 1980 to 2021, sourced from Chinese CDC archives, for wavelet analysis. This allowed us to examine the temporal coherence between large-scale climate oscillations and dengue trends. Moreover, the five cities with the highest volumes of overseas imported dengue cases, Guangzhou, Shenzhen, Xishuangbanna, Dehong, and Lincang were collectively referred to as “TF cities” for focused subgroup analyses (Supplementary Methods, Figs. S21 and S22).

Climate data

We selected different climate datasets based on two key criteria: (1) availability of high temporal resolution (daily scale) spanning at least 2013 to the present, and (2) preference for datasets that incorporate observational corrections when possible. Specifically, MSWEP was chosen for precipitation because it integrates satellite, gauge, and reanalysis data, and has been shown to provide improved rainfall estimates compared to reanalysis-only datasets like ERA5, particularly in regions with complex precipitation patterns. ERA5, on the other hand, was used for temperature, humidity, and wind-related variables due to its comprehensive and physically consistent reanalysis framework. Importantly, DCCP was incorporated not for retrospective modeling but to extend the analysis into future projections, as it includes climate variables under multiple scenarios up to 2028 and beyond.

Local climate data

Daily maximum, average, and minimum temperature data spanning 2005 to 2022 with a spatial resolution of 0.1° were collected from the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts Reanalysis v5 (ERA5) dataset (https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/cdsapp#!/dataset/reanalysis-era5-land?tab=form). Daily precipitation spanning 2005 to 2022 with a spatial resolution of 0.1° was collected from the Multi-Source Weighted-Ensemble Precipitation (MSWEP) dataset (https://climatedataguide.ucar.edu/climate-data/global-high-resolution-precipitation-mswep). Daily maximum, average, and minimum temperature along with precipitation from 2005 to 2028 with a spatial resolution of 0.1° were collected from the Decadal Climate Prediction Project (DCPP) contribution to the sixth Coupled Model Intercomparison Project dataset (https://esgf-node.llnl.gov/search/cmip6/), inclusive of three climate models: HadGEM3-GC31-MM, MIROC6, and CMCC-CM2-SR5. To align local climate data from different time periods, we applied bias correction and downscaling to the future data (2023–2028) using the historical data (2005–2022) (61). Monthly daily maximum, average, and minimum temperatures and precipitation were aggregated for each month and city.

Large-scale climate data

Monthly SST data from 2005 to 2028 with a spatial resolution of 0.1° were collected from the DCPP dataset (https://esgf-node.llnl.gov/search/cmip6/). This dataset was initialized using observations forced by the SSP245 scenario, and it includes simulations from three climate models: HadGEM3-GC31-MM, MIROC6, and CMCC-CM2-SR5 (Fig. S23). IOD index was calculated from differences in SST values averaged over the western (50°E–70°E; 10°S–10°N) and the eastern (90°E–110°E; 10°S–0°) tropical Indian Ocean (62). ENSO index including Niño3, Niño3.4, and Niño4 index (63). These indexes are defined as SST averaged over the following regions: Niño3 (5°N–5°S, 150°W–90°W), Niño3.4 (5°N–5°S, 170°W–120°W), and Niño4 (5°N–5°S, 160°E–150°W). For each of these indexes, we used both the average SST values and the anomalies calculated against the 1981–2020 period averages. IOBW index was calculated as the SST averaged over the Indian Ocean (40°E–110°E; 20°S–20°N) (64). The AN index was calculated as the SST averaged over the Atlantic region (20°W–0°; 3°S–3°N) (65). Finally, the AMO index was calculated as the SST averaged over the north Atlantic (75°W–6°W and 0°–60°N) (66).

Seasonal classification of IOD phases

To further differentiate the different phases of the IOD and their seasonal impacts, we categorized the IOD based on its phase and the season. Specifically, an IOD value >0 is defined as positive IOD, while a value <0 is defined as negative IOD (29). The seasons were defined as follows: March, April, and May were categorized as spring; June, July, and August as summer; September, October, and November as autumn; and December of the previous year, January, and February as winter (67).

Human mobility data

Domestic mobility data

Collected annual passenger traffic amounts (PT) from transportation Statistical Yearbooks (all used data was extracted from http://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/, e.g. data from 2021 can be found at https://www.stats.gov.cn/sj/ndsj/2021/indexch.htm, Fig. S24). The actual migration index (AM) was obtained from Autonavi Migration data (https://trp.autonavi.com/migrate/page.do). The number of passengers moving out through TFcities (NPM) was extracted from AM data. The monthly city-to-city DHM was determined through adjusting PT by the monthly proportion in yearly NPM index following the formula: DHMi,t = PTt × proportional of NPMi,t. Future values of the DHM index to 2028 were predicted using the ARIMA model (Fig. S25). For detailed information, please see Supplementary methods.

International mobility

Collected monthly air passenger volumes (AP) from Southeast Asia to China; these were predicted by Mao et al. (68). Annual international revenue passenger-kilometers (RPKs) from 2013 to 2025 were collected from the International Air Transport Association (https://www.iata.org/en/publications/economics/, Fig. S26) (69). The monthly city-to-city international human mobility index (IHM) was determined as AP adjusted by the yearly growth rate of RPKs following the formula: IHMi.t = APi,t × growth rate of RPKst. Future values of the IHM index to 2028 were predicted using the ARIMA model (Fig. S27). For detailed information, please see Supplementary methods. International mobility reflects direct mobility from Southeast Asia, while domestic mobility reflects indirect mobility through TFcities.

Vectorial capacity data

We calculated monthly VC from daily temperature data according to the method provided by Rocklӧv et al. (7). The VC describes the potential of a vector to transmit a pathogen and is defined as the number of new mosquito infectious bites that arise from one infected person introduced into a population of entirely susceptible hosts in a day (7, 12, 70) (Supplementary methods). Detailed model parameters were referred to Rocklӧv et al. (71).

Population density and environment data

Population density

From 2013 to 2028 with a spatial resolution of 1 km2 was collected from hybrid gridded demographic data, taking the scenarios of medium fertility rates (72) (Supplementary methods). Due to the lack of monthly population density data, we assumed consistent population density for each month throughout the year (73). The monthly population density was determined by averaging the scenarios and aggregated for each city.

Environment data

The monthly NDVI with a spatial resolution of 0.25 km2 was derived from the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) MOD13Q1 product (74), while the monthly NDWI with a spatial resolution of 0.5 km2 was derived from the MODIS MCD43A4 product (75). Monthly NDVI and NDWI data were collected for the period 2013–2021. For the post-2021 period, we assumed these variables remained constant at their 2021 levels. Both measures were aggregated for each month and city (Supplementary methods).

Data analysis methods

GAMs for dengue incidence estimation (2013–2021)

To assess the impact of multiple environmental, climatic, and human mobility factors on dengue incidence, we employed GAMs, which allow for flexible modeling of nonlinear relationships. The GAM framework incorporated key local climatic variables (temperature and precipitation), large-scale climate indices (IOD and ENSO), and human mobility indices (IHM and DHM) to capture the dynamics of disease importation and local transmission. This approach ensured consistency with available data and provided robust statistical inference on the factors influencing dengue outbreaks in China (5). Modeling was performed using the mgcv package (version 1.8–36) of the R statistical programming environment (76), following the methodological framework of Xu et al. (77). A two-step variable selection process was applied, integrating expanding window time series cross-validation (ewts-CV) (78) and leave-one-year-out cross-validation (loyo-CV) (5) to identify the optimal model configuration (Supplementary methods, Fig. S28). Variables were selected based on minimizing CV scores and maximizing the percentage of deviance explained (5). The selected predictors included local climate factors (monthly maximum, average, and minimum temperatures; cumulative precipitation), environmental factors (NDVI or NDWI, selecting the superior indicator), VC (with an optimal lag of 0–3 months), human mobility indices (IHM and DHM), and large-scale climate indices (IOD, IOBW, ENSO, AN, and AMO with an optimal lag of 0–3 months) (Tables 1 and S1). Model accuracy was evaluated using the coefficient of variation (r2). The final selected GAM with a Gaussian distribution can be written as Eq. (1):

To further refine our analysis, two sets of GAMs were constructed. The first set modeled overseas imported dengue cases as the response variable to evaluate the influence of IOD and ENSO, while the second set focused on local and domestic imported cases to distinguish their relative contributions (Supplementary method). Additionally, to assess seasonal and phase-specific effects of the IOD, two additional GAMs were developed: one incorporating an interaction term between IOD and its positive and negative phases, and another modeling the interaction between IOD and seasonal variations (Table S7). Finally, to examine temporal shifts in the role of IOD in dengue incidence, three separate GAMs were constructed for different historical periods (1980–2004, 2005–2012, and 2013–2021) to compare changes in the percentage of deviance explained by IOD over time (Table S8).

Extreme gradient boosting model for dengue prediction using 2013–2021 data and forecasting for 2023–2028

To quantify the dominant risk factors for dengue incidence in 366 cities, we initially fitted the dengue incidence to an XGBoost model using climate, NDWI, domestic, and international human mobility as the main factors. Subsequently, we calculated the contribution of each factor in the 366 cities by computing their respective SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values as a proportion of the total SHAP values of all factors on GAM model selection. To conduct expanding window time series cross-validation (ewts-CV), we configured testing windows of 3 years and 1 year, respectively.

To enhance predictive accuracy and assess the future trajectory of dengue outbreaks, we employed XGBoost, a machine learning algorithm designed to handle complex, nonlinear, high-dimensional datasets (79). The model was trained on 2013–2021 data, incorporating climate predictors, human mobility, and environmental variables, ensuring alignment with available data. To project future dengue incidence, the trained model was applied to forecast outbreaks from 2023 to 2028, integrating climate projections and predicted mobility trends. By leveraging iterative learning from previous model errors, XGBoost provided robust predictions, complementing the insights obtained from GAMs. XGBoost modeling was implemented using the xgboost Python package (version 1.6.2) (https://xgboost.readthedocs.io/en/stable/python/python_intro.html).

To interpret model predictions, we employed SHAP, which enables the decomposition of model outputs to quantify the contribution of individual features and assess spatiotemporal heterogeneity in disease transmission (80). We adopted the ewst-CV and the loyo-CV method consistent with the GAM approach, splitting the data into training and testing sets, and then using grid search to determine the best hyperparameter values. The parameters examined included learning rate (ranging from 0.05 to 0.2 with an interval of 0.05) and maximum tree depth (ranging from 4 to 11 with an interval of 1). As the dependent variable dengue was log-transformed, reg:squarederror was selected for the objective function. Training ceased when additional iterations no longer enhanced performance on the validation dataset. Model accuracy was evaluated using the coefficient of variation (r2) and root mean square error (RMSE). Each covariate included data for the period 2013–2028. The 2013–2021 data were used for model training and testing, while the 2023–2028 data were used for prediction. The optimal model incorporated temperature, precipitation, and IOD, and was employed to project dengue incidence from 2023 to 2028 under three climate scenarios (81).

Other statistical analyses

We examined the correlation coefficients between predictor variables and included only those with absolute values ≤0.7 (82).

Long-term climate-driven patterns in dengue incidence using wavelet analysis (1980–2021)

To examine the long-term association between the IOD index and dengue incidence from 1980 to 2021, we conducted wavelet cross-correlation and coherence analyses (Supplementary methods, Figs. S14, S15, and S16). These methods identified multidecadal cyclical patterns and revealed coherent temporal relationships between climate variability and dengue outbreaks across the time–frequency domain. The cross-wavelet transform was employed to characterize the interaction between the wavelet transforms of the IOD index and dengue incidence, quantifying their shared power and phase lag across different time scales (83, 84). This method facilitated the detection of periods where strong coherence was observed, indicating potential climatic influences on dengue outbreaks. Furthermore, arrows in the cross-wavelet and coherence spectra illustrated relative phase dynamics, providing insights into whether variations in IOD preceded or lagged fluctuations in dengue incidence (85, 86).

The CCF (1980–2021)

To further explore temporal dependencies between dengue incidence and its potential drivers, we applied the CCF using the stats package (version 4.1.1) of the R statistical programming environment (76). The CCF quantifies lagged correlations between time series data, allowing us to determine whether IOD variations or imported cases precede or follow fluctuations in total dengue cases. By assessing short-term and delayed associations across multiple time lags, this analysis provided a deeper understanding of the temporal relationships between key predictors and dengue transmission dynamics (Fig. S4).

Residual analysis (2013–2028)

To analyze the fitting of our GAM and XGBoost model, we employed several statistical techniques to assess temporal and spatial variations of residuals. Specifically, using the stats package (version 4.1.1) of the R statistical programming environment (76), we calculated the F-statistic to compare the between-group variance to the within-group variance, with significance assessed at the α = 0.05 level. Following a significant ANOVA result, we performed Tukey's HSD test to identify which specific group means differed significantly from each other. This post hoc analysis controls for the chance of finding differences by mistake when comparing multiple groups by calculating the HSD value, which is then used to compare all possible pairs of group means. The analysis was conducted using the agricolae package (version 1.3-7) of the R statistical programming environment (76), and the results were interpreted based on the q-statistic and corresponding critical values.
