Tricuspid valve endocarditis caused by Campylobacter fetus bacteraemia in a 48-year old female: a case report
Authors: Magnus Schmitt, Jan Schröder, Lorenz Scheit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Campylobacter infections are very common overall. They typically affect the gastrointestinal tract and are usually caused by consumption of contaminated meat or water. Older people, men, and people with weakened immune systems are significantly more likely to be affected. Endocarditis caused by Campylobacter fetus, especially of the tricuspid valve, is rarely reported and not easy to diagnose.Case summaryA 48-year-old female patient presented to the emergency department with pain in the upper abdomen and left flank and tenderness in the left chest. After initially ruling out pyelonephritis, within the scope of focus search, vegetation on the tricuspid valve with rapidly progressive tricuspid valve insufficiency was observed. Campylobacter fetus grew in the initial blood cultures. Antibiotic treatment was initiated with ceftriaxone. Due to additional unclear renal insufficiency and further treatment, the patient was transferred to an interdisciplinary centre, where tricuspid valve replacement was performed. The patient was discharged home with a peripherally inserted central catheter for prolonged intravenous antibiotic therapy with meropenem.DiscussionDiagnosis was difficult based on the variety of symptoms, as the patient was a younger female who did not present with typical symptoms of endocarditis. Follow-up examinations and consultation with an interdisciplinary centre were necessary to ensure adequate treatment. The patient initially received intravenous cephalosporin, aminopenicillin, and finally carbapenem antibiotics. Due to a lack of recommendations, antibiotic therapy was based on empirical evidence. Amoxicillin–clavulanic acid and imipenem have been described as effective antibiotics against almost all strains of C. fetus.

Antibiotic therapy for Campylobacter fetus bacteraemia continues to be empirical, and carbapenem therapy also appears to be very effective in cases of resulting tricuspid valve endocarditis.

Introduction

Campylobacter infections have increased in recent years.1–3 It commonly infects the mucosal surfaces of the gastrointestinal tract and less frequently causes bacteraemia.3 Already 25 years ago, Campylobacter jejuni and coli were considered the most common bacterial enteropathogens in industrialized countries.4 Campylobacter is often transmitted to humans through meat and contaminated drinking water, partly due to contaminated manure. Travel-related infections with Campylobacter are also often attributable to contaminated meat and drinking water.1 Campylobacter bacteraemia is mostly caused by C. fetus and is particularly common in older people, males, and those who are immunocompromised or have implanted medical devices.3,5

Although Campylobacter infections are common, endocarditis caused by Campylobacter, and especially C. fetus, is rare. In a large-scale French study, only 2% of patients with Campylobacter infection were found to have endocarditis, with no cases involving tricuspid valve.6 Another systematic review identified 26 patients with infectious endocarditis caused by C. fetus in 22 studies, but neither any tricuspid valve affection.7 To our knowledge, only three cases of C. fetus-induced endocarditis of the tricuspid valve, have been reported since 2000.8–10 One patient died after discontinuing therapy at his own request.9 The other two patients received several weeks of antibiotic therapy and surgical treatment.8,10 All three cases involved men. To the best of our knowledge, there have been no reports of female patients with infectious tricuspid valve endocarditis caused by C. fetus, suggesting that this case could be the first of its kind.

Case presentation

A 48-year-old female patient presented to the emergency department with upper abdominal pain, left-sided flank pain, and tenderness in the left thorax. She also reported recurrent vomiting after eating, intermittent fever, and coughing up blood for several days. Previous illnesses included bronchial asthma, pneumonia in 2024, and pyelonephritis in 2018 with subsequent shrinkage of the right kidney and right-sided urolithiasis. Known cardiovascular risk factors included nicotine abuse (30 pack-years) and dyslipoproteinaemia. No long-term medication was taken.

The clinical examination only detected pressure-sensitive tenderness over both renal areas. Laboratory tests showed an isolated increase in C-reactive protein (89 mg/L), elevated renal retention parameters (creatinine 1.76 mg/dL) and liver enzymes (alanine aminotransferase 89 U/L, γ-glutamyltransferase 316 U/L), and decreased haemoglobin (10.1 g/dL) and thrombocyte counts (146/nL). Initially, further diagnostics were performed on suspicion of pyelonephritis. A calculated antibiotic therapy with ceftriaxone was initiated. Abdominal ultrasound revealed an enlarged left kidney with a blurred delineation between the pyelon and the parenchyma consistent with left-sided pyelonephritis. In addition, a previously known right-sided shrunken kidney was observed. The liver (ventrodorsal diameter 188 mm) and spleen (volume 547 mL) were enlarged. Despite morphological evidence of pyelonephritis in computed tomography (CT) and ultrasound, no corresponding clinical correlation was found in the urological examination. From a urological perspective, pyelonephritis was therefore considered unlikely. As no other causes of renal insufficiency were initially identified, the suspected diagnosis of pyelonephritis was maintained.

Computed tomography angiography to rule out pulmonary artery embolism revealed multiple bi-pulmonary lesions, some nodular and some cord-like, with partial cavitation in the lungs. There were no signs of septic emboli. Additionally, there was no evidence of pulmonary tuberculosis. During a bedside echocardiogram on Day 1, a vegetation on the tricuspid valve (19 × 10 mm) without relevant insufficiency was detected. Enterococcus faecalis was detected in the urine on Day 3; the first two sets of blood cultures, taken on Day 0, were positive for Campylobacter fetus on Day 4 with a time to positivity of 71.6 h (further details, including susceptibility interpretation, are given in Table 1). The second two sets of blood cultures, which were collected on Day 1, also tested positive for C. fetus with a time to positivity of 106.4 h. Because a C. fetus infection was still considered an unlikely cause of the symptoms and the patient was clinically stable at that time, the medical team initially opted against escalating the antibiotic treatment. According to our infectious disease specialists, ceftriaxone had been described in the literature as potentially effective against C. fetus. Therefore, treatment with ceftriaxone was initially continued. Rheumatology blood tests showed positive results for c-ANCA (1:160) and PR3 antibodies (qualitative). Transthoracic echocardiography on Day 4 showed echogenic structures (19 × 9 mm) most consistent with past endocarditis of the tricuspid valve; a differential diagnosis of rheumatic degenerative change of the valve without relevant insufficiency was considered (Figure 1). Transoesophageal echocardiography on Day 7, to more accurately classify the findings, already revealed mild to moderate insufficiency and a vegetation size of 24 × 11 mm.

Based on a review of the clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings, along with interdisciplinary consultation with the infectious disease department, an autoimmune process, such as vasculitis, was discussed as the most likely cause. A university hospital was contacted as a large interdisciplinary centre to discuss the case. At their request, the patient was transferred to the centre for further diagnosis and treatment.

Antibiotic therapy was changed to intravenous ampicillin at that facility; however, the reason for this change was not explained. It was probably due an equal or better effectiveness, and less selective against other microorganisms, thereby reducing the risk of resistance development.11 Repeated transthoracic and transoesophageal echocardiography then demonstrated severe tricuspid valve insufficiency with tricuspid valve endocarditis. The CT scan performed as part of the surgical planning showed new septic pulmonary embolisms, which supported suspicion of right-sided endocarditis. Clinically, the patient showed increasing signs of right heart failure. Serological tests were performed to rule out immunodeficiency, such as HIV and hepatitis B and C. After an interdisciplinary case review by the endocarditis team, the decision was made to proceed with urgent cardiac surgery involving heart valve replacement without attempting valve repair. At that time, one major and three minor modified Duke criteria had been met. Following median sternotomy, the tricuspid valve was replaced with a biological valve prosthesis of porcine origin (St Jude Medical Epic™ porcine bio-prosthesis). Intraoperative inspection confirmed infectious tricuspid valve endocarditis; therefore, antibiotic therapy was escalated to meropenem based on previous clinical experience. Postoperative transthoracic echocardiography showed normal function of the implanted valve prosthesis.

After being transferred back to the Bundeswehr Hospital, transthoracic and transoesophageal echocardiography showed regular prosthetic valve function. Microbiological detection of C. fetus by PCR on the removed tricuspid valve was confirmed by the university hospital. Since only the initial antibiotic susceptibility test results (Table 1) were available at that time, the infectious disease specialists recommended continuing treatment with meropenem for a total of 6 weeks to protect the new heart valve.

Outpatient parenteral anti-infective therapy with meropenem was organized for further treatment. Culture-based detection of C. fetus on the removed tricuspid valve was only successful after bacterial enrichment and reported after discharge (Table 1). Unfortunately, no subspecies identification was performed. After Day 1, all subsequent blood cultures showed negative results.

Independent of a planned follow-up examination of the patient at our hospital, the patient presented at our emergency room with community-acquired pneumonia and prerenal acute-on-chronic kidney damage after a few months. No pathogen was detected in the blood cultures. Transoesophageal echocardiography showed no evidence of heart valve involvement and no indication of recurrent endocarditis (Figure 2).

Discussion

This case shows a rare case of a right-sided infectious endocarditis caused by C. fetus. Diagnosing endocarditis is always complex and requires presence of several major and/or minor criteria according to the modified Duke criteria.12 Initial examinations only revealed vegetation on the tricuspid valve and fever as criteria. Therefore, endocarditis was considered a possible cause at an early stage. However, based on clinical impressions and in consultation with infectious disease specialists, a systemic disease, such as vasculitis due to an autoimmune process, was considered the most likely diagnosis. While the positive rheumatism criteria supported this, they also represented a minor criterion for endocarditis. Only repeated echocardiography and an additional CT scan revealed rapidly progressing tricuspid regurgitation and septic emboli, ruling out rheumatic degenerative changes of the tricuspid valve. With one major and three minor criteria met, infectious endocarditis could be diagnosed based on clinical criteria. Due to the rapid progression of the insufficiency with subsequent heart failure, the decision was made to proceed with urgent surgery. Progressive right heart failure that does not respond well to diuretics is a guideline-based indication for surgery.13 The infected heart valve was completely replaced. This is the case for most patients with infective tricuspid valve endocarditis, and repair does not lead to better outcomes.13

The patient initially received intravenous cephalosporin antibiotics due to suspected pyelonephritis, followed by intravenous aminopenicillin and carbapenem antibiotics. Therapy with a third-generation cephalosporin is not recommended for Campylobacter bacteraemia and is considered a risk factor for fatal outcomes.5 However, this does not apply to bacteraemia caused by C. fetus.5 Since there are no specific recommendations from guidelines or recognized limits for resistance testing of C. fetus, treatment was also empirically based. After completion of the treatment, no bacteria were detected in the blood cultures, and no changes were observed in the prosthetic valve. Treatment with meropenem is consistent with recommendations and experiences described in the literature and was also effective in this patient.5,6,9,10,14

This case demonstrates that the correct diagnosis and treatment of a complex set of symptoms were ultimately only possible through interdisciplinary collaboration, repeated testing, and ongoing discussion and evaluation of the findings.
