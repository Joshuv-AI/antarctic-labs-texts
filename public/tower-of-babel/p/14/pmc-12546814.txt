Magnitude and determinants of adverse perinatal outcomes of pregnancies complicated with preeclampsia and eclampsia at a teaching hospital in the Tigray region of Ethiopia
Authors: Hale Teka, Mussie Alemayehu, Awol Yemane, Bisrat Tesfay Abera, Habtom Tadesse, Ephrem Berhe, Hiluf Ebuy Abraha, Fanos Gebru, Mohammedtahir Yahiya, Marta Abraha, Ytbarek Tadesse, Daniel Gebre, Ashenafi Tekle, Tsega Gebremariam, Birhane Amare, Yibrah Berhe Zelelow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreeclampsia–eclampsia (PRE-E) syndrome remains a major global health issue because of its implications as an important cause of severe maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality. Determining the magnitude of the problem and identifying factors that negatively affect outcomes is vital for clinical decision-making. Identifying high-risk pregnant women who need intensive observation and devising context-specific interventions to reduce unfavorable perinatal outcomes relies on understanding the magnitude and determinant factors. Hence, this study aimed to quantify the magnitude and determinant factors of adverse perinatal outcomes among pregnant women with preeclampsia–eclampsia syndrome at Ayder comprehensive specialized hospital in the Tigray region of Ethiopia.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted that included 934 pregnant women who had PRE-E syndrome at Ayder comprehensive specialized hospital from January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2021. Data were collected using EpiData (version 4.6.0.2) and analyzed using Stata version 16. A composite adverse perinatal outcome including fetal growth restriction, preterm birth, low Apgar score at the 5th minute, stillbirth, and neonatal death was calculated and used for analysis. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify determinant factors of adverse perinatal outcomes; results are described using adjusted odds ratio (AOR). A p-value less than 0.05 was used to declare a statistically significant difference.ResultsThe prevalence of adverse perinatal outcomes among the study participants was 65.1% (95% CI 61.2, 68.1). The overall perinatal mortality rate was 166 per 1000 live births. Multifetal gestation (AOR = 10.6, 95% CI 4.38, 25.69), primigravidity (AOR = 1.74, 95% CI 1.01,3.00), onset of PRE-E syndrome before 34 weeks (AOR = 29.75, 95% CI 12.8,68.7), and having severity features (AOR = 2.66, 95% CI 1.92,3.66) were significant determinants of adverse perinatal outcomes.ConclusionThe overall prevalence of adverse perinatal outcomes was consistent with countrywide findings and studies done elsewhere in low-resource settings. Early-onset preeclampsia, severe features, multifetal gestation, and primigravidity negatively affected perinatal outcomes. Thus, clinical profiles such as gravidity, gestational age at diagnosis, multifetal gestation, and severity features should be considered in clinical decision-making in low-resource settings. Perinatal outcomes may be improved through optimizing antepartum, intrapartum, and postpartum context- and gestational age-specific interventions, and adhering to established protocols with proven perinatal benefits such as administration of steroids, neuroprotection, and neonatal surfactant use.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20802-3.

Introduction

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy complicate approximately 5–8% of pregnancies globally1,2. In Africa, these disorders complicate 10% of pregnancies3. They remain a major global health issue because of their association with severe maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality4,5. These heterogeneous disorders include gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, eclampsia, and chronic hypertension that can be complicated by superimposed preeclampsia6,7. Preeclampsia is a common and severe form of hypertensive disorder of pregnancy, complicating an average of 4% of pregnancies worldwide.⁸,⁹ Preeclampsia and eclampsia are considered clinical syndromes rather than single disease entities, and these syndromes are associated with significant maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality10. Fetal and neonatal complications of PRE-E syndrome include fetal growth restriction (FGR), intrauterine fetal death (IUFD), prematurity, low birth weight (LBW), poor Apgar scores, and neonatal death11.

Globally, nearly half a million babies die annually due to preeclampsia–eclampsia-related complications12,13. Perinatal mortality associated with PRE-E syndrome is remarkably high, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMIC)14–16. In Ethiopia, previous studies show that perinatal morbidity and mortality associated with PRE-E syndrome is unacceptably high17. Since etiologies and established preventive mechanisms of preeclampsia remain elusive, identifying factors that negatively affect perinatal outcomes helps dictate context-specific interventions to ameliorate the high burden of adverse outcomes associated with PRE-E syndrome. Furthermore, understanding the magnitude and determinant factors of preeclampsia in low-resource settings can guide context-specific management plans. However, limited data exist on the magnitude and determinant factors of perinatal outcomes among pregnant women with PRE-E syndrome, particularly in low-resource settings. Thus, this study aimed to examine the magnitude and determinant factors of adverse perinatal outcomes among pregnant women with PRE-E syndrome at Ayder comprehensive specialized hospital, an academic hospital setting in the Tigray region of Ethiopia.

Methods

Study design

A cross-sectional study design was used to examine the magnitude and determinant factors of perinatal outcomes of PRE-E syndrome in Ayder comprehensive specialized hospital. This involved reviewing records of pregnant women and their offspring who had PRE-E syndrome over a 7-year period between January 1, 2015, and December 31, 2021.

Study setting

This study was conducted in Ayder comprehensive specialized hospital, a teaching hospital in the Tigray region of Ethiopia. It is the largest referral center in the region for a catchment of over 8 million people from Tigray and neighboring districts of Afar and Amhara regions. The hospital provides comprehensive specialized care, with obstetrics and gynecology services among the main services. It houses two separate antenatal care clinics for low- and high-risk patients, and the number of deliveries has been increasing, currently averaging 5000 deliveries per annum. Throughout the study period, both high- and low-risk pregnant women in labor were followed with intermittent auscultation. The hospital also has a neonatal care unit that receives referrals from both the maternity ward and other institutions.

Study population

Records of pregnant women with PRE-E syndrome who received antenatal care, intrapartum care, and postnatal care at Ayder comprehensive specialized hospital, and those referred there for delivery, were included in the study. Since fetal viability is considered to be at ≥ 28 weeks of gestation in Ethiopia, pregnant women whose pregnancies were terminated before 28 weeks of gestation were excluded from the study18.

Data collection tool and procedures

Data were obtained from the medical records of pregnant women with PRE-E syndrome who gave birth at Ayder comprehensive specialized hospital. The collected data include baseline clinical information such as laboratory investigations, antepartum and intrapartum fetal status, and neonatal status at discharge. The maternal (pregnant women’s) data include place of residence, age, blood pressure, gravidity, parity, gestational age, order of pregnancy (singleton, twin, or triplet), history of abortion, duration of hospital stay in days, and mode of delivery. If any clinical or laboratory severity feature was found, the pregnant woman was labeled as having preeclampsia with severity features. For perinatal data, parameters included fetal growth restriction, antepartum or intrapartum fetal status, neonatal status at discharge, Apgar score at the 5th minute, and birth weight.

Measurement

Adverse perinatal outcome was measured by reviewing the chart for low birth weight (LBW), poor Apgar score at the 5th minute, stillbirth, preterm birth, fetal growth restriction, and neonatal death. LBW was measured by taking the birth weight in grams; newborn weight < 2500 g was categorized as LBW. The Apgar score was labeled poor if the 5th minute score was < 719. Fetal growth restriction was recorded by checking the chart for the presence or absence of FGR during pregnancy follow-up. Preterm was defined as delivery before 37 completed weeks of gestation. Babies who had no sign of life at birth were categorized as stillbirths (IUFD). Those born alive who died up to the date of discharge were categorized as neonatal deaths. Hence, adverse perinatal outcome was categorized as “adverse perinatal outcome” for newborns who had either LBW, stillbirth, FGR, an Apgar score < 7 at 5 min, preterm birth, or neonatal death until discharge. If the newborn did not have any of these poor perinatal outcomes, the condition was categorized as “no adverse perinatal outcome.”

Perinatal mortality was calculated by dividing the sum of stillbirth and neonatal death by 1000 live births.

Duration of stay in the health facility was measured by counting the number of days from admission to discharge and treated as a continuous variable.

Onset of preeclampsia was categorized as “early” if it began before 34 weeks, and “late” if onset was at or after 34 weeks of gestation.

Gestational age at delivery was classified as “early preterm” for gestational age < 34 weeks, “late preterm” for gestational age 34–36 weeks, and “term” for ≥ 37 weeks.

Parity was labeled as “primipara” if no prior delivery, “parous” if history of previous delivery (2–4), and “grand multipara” if there were five or more previous deliveries.

Data quality control

To ensure the quality of the data, a standard tool was developed to capture all the necessary variables from patient charts. The questionnaire was designed in Open Data Kit (ODK), which increased the efficiency and accuracy of data collection process. ODK provided mechanisms for data validation during data entry, reducing errors and ensuring data quality. The ability to enforce skip patterns, constraints, and data types helped maintain the integrity of collected data. We pre-tested the tool in health facilities not included for this study. Extensive training was provided for the data collectors and supervisors for two continuous days. The research team also checked the data rigorously for logical responses and flow, data inconsistencies and incompleteness on daily basis.

Data analysis

Data were collected using EpiData (v4.6.0.2) and analyzed using Stata version 16.1. Descriptive statistical analysis was performed to describe the distribution of data across selected factors related to adverse perinatal outcomes. For categorical variables, frequencies and percentages were calculated, and comparisons were made using the chi-squared test. For normally distributed continuous variables, mean (sd) was estimated, and an independent t-test was employed for comparisons. Before proceeding to the final model, univariate analysis was done to select candidate variables for multivariable logistic regression. Predictors with a p-value of 0.25 or less were included in the final model20. At the end, independent variables associated with the presence of adverse perinatal outcomes were entered into the final model, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was used to assess the final model’s fitness, and Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) checked for possible collinearity between predictors.

Ethical clearance

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Mekelle University, College of Health Sciences (study protocol: MU-IRB 2016/2022). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was not obtained from the pregnant women for review of their charts since this was a retrospective study. Care was taken to de-identify patient health information in the medical records, and after reviewing the study protocol, the IRB office waived the need for informed consent.

The majority (575, 61.5%) were urban residents, and 442 (43.9%) were primigravida. In addition, more than nine in ten (93.15%) had at least one ANC visit in their index pregnancy. Of the total, 162 (17.4%) had a history of abortion. Prevalence of adverse perinatal outcome

As shown in Fig. 1, the prevalence of adverse perinatal outcomes among the study participants was 65.1% (95% CI 61.2, 68.1). Specifically, the prevalence of stillbirth was 13.06% (95% CI 11.04, 15.38), while neonatal death accounted for 6.02% (95% CI 4.19, 8.59). Predictors of adverse perinatal outcome

The multivariable logistic regression analysis indicates that pregnant women with twin or triplet gestation (multifetal gestation) were about eleven times more likely to develop adverse perinatal outcomes compared to those with a singleton gestation (AOR = 10.6, 95% CI 4.38,25.69). Similarly, the odds of having adverse perinatal outcomes were two times higher among primigravidae (AOR = 1.74, 95% CI 1.01,3.00) compared to multigravidae. An increased odds of adverse perinatal outcome was observed among pregnant women who had early-onset PRE-E syndrome (AOR = 29.75, 95% CI 12.8,68.7) compared to those with late-onset disease. Discussion

This study provides information about the magnitude and determinants of adverse perinatal outcomes among pregnant women who experienced preeclampsia–eclampsia syndrome at Ayder comprehensive specialized hospital in the Tigray region of Ethiopia. In the present study, the overall adverse perinatal outcome was 65.1% (95% CI 61.2, 68.1). Gestational age at onset of disease, disease severity, parity, and twin gestation were found to be significant determinants of adverse perinatal outcomes of PREE syndrome.

Preeclampsia–eclampsia syndrome is associated with a high risk of adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes21,22. As compared to normotensive pregnant women, preeclampsia and eclampsia result in increased risks of perinatal demise11. The prevalence of adverse perinatal outcomes is particularly staggering in LMIC5,17,23,24. In this study, the prevalence of adverse perinatal outcomes was 65% (95% CI 61.2, 68.1), which was in line with studies done elsewhere in Ethiopia, where prevalence ranges from 61.7–66.4%25,26. Early identification of the disease through optimal antenatal care, anticipation of complications, and instituting context-specific individualized interventions could mitigate the high burden of adverse perinatal outcomes in high-risk populations, such as pregnant women with preeclampsia–eclampsia syndrome. This can be achieved by empowering women through increased awareness of pregnancy complications, decentralizing advanced care, equipping health facilities in remote areas with appropriate medical equipment such as blood pressure cuffs, and training rural healthcare staff on early warning signs (triggers) to enable timely referral to healthcare institutions capable of handling such high-risk women.

The proportion of perinatal mortality (14.6%) was in line with the national prevalence of 16.3% (95% CI 13.4, 19.6) found in the Ethiopian national survey reported among pregnant women with preeclampsia–eclampsia syndrome. This finding was consistent with those of other studies in LMIC25. The prevalence of perinatal mortality, however, was extremely high when compared with high-income countries. Studies in Norway, Sweden, and China showed perinatal mortalities of 3.0, 5.2, and 11.0 per 1000 live births, respectively28,29. This discrepancy can be attributed to several factors. First, due to both the poor health-seeking behavior of pregnant women and a lack of readily accessible health services, many arrive late in the disease course, resulting in higher perinatal morbidity and mortality, 30,31. Second, antepartum interventions such as steroid administration for lung maturity, prophylaxis for neuroprotection in extremely preterm infants, and nonstress tests or biophysical profiles—which are proven to decrease perinatal mortality—are often lacking or used less frequently in low-resource settings32–34. Third, preeclampsia–eclampsia is a high-risk condition that necessitates electronic fetal monitoring, which is limited in the developing world; in resource-poor settings, high-risk pregnant women in labor are generally followed using a fetoscope33. Fourth, the frequency of preterm birth is high in PREE syndrome due to indicated terminations of pregnancy, which leads to neonates requiring comprehensive postpartum care such as resuscitation, neonatal ICU, and surfactant use36,37. However, in resource-constrained settings, such measures remain limited, leading to worse outcomes. Delayed institution of management has also been reported as a major contributor to the high prevalence of adverse outcomes associated with PRE-E syndrome38.

Compared to late-onset PRE-E syndrome, an earlier gestational age at diagnosis has been associated with more significant adverse perinatal outcomes39. This trend is consistent in both high- and low-income countries40–43. Similarly, in the present study, pregnant women with early-onset preeclampsia had higher odds of adverse perinatal outcomes. A previous study suggested that small-for-gestational-age (SGA) neonates might be a partial explanation for worse outcomes in early-onset versus late-onset preeclampsia41. The other reason may be that early-onset preeclampsia tends to be more severe, thus necessitating earlier termination and resulting in complications related to prematurity36. Gestational age at delivery is indeed a key determinant of neonatal outcome44. Some studies have asserted that early- and late-onset disease may differ in terms of clinical presentation and pathophysiology45,46. Late-onset disease is mostly associated with normal or slightly increased uterine resistance and a lower rate of fetal involvement, with more favorable perinatal outcomes compared to early-onset disease47,48.

Compared to singleton pregnancies, preeclampsia is often diagnosed earlier in twins and results in more severe maternal morbidities requiring earlier termination49,50. In this study, pregnant women carrying twin gestations were at nearly 11-fold higher odds of experiencing an adverse perinatal outcome. This finding is likely tied to preterm deliveries associated with severe disease in twin gestations.

Consistent with other studies from both low- and middle-income countries, this study revealed higher odds of adverse perinatal outcomes in pregnancies complicated by severe preeclampsia/eclampsia compared to those without severity features11,12. The excess risk in severe cases may be partly linked to increased occurrence of fetal growth restriction and IUFD11,12.

In general, first pregnancies are considered high risk, with significant maternal and neonatal morbidity and mortality, and complications proportionally become higher in primigravidae with advanced maternal age52,53. In agreement with these assumptions, this study showed that primigravidae were at nearly twice the risk of having an adverse perinatal outcome.

Our study attempts to quantify the magnitude and identify the factors associated with adverse perinatal outcomes using retrospective data. However, it has some limitations. First, substantial sociodemographic variables were missing from patient charts (e.g., educational status, marital status, occupation). Second, neonatal status was not routinely recorded in postpartum follow-up notes; thus, neonatal mortality recorded here primarily reflects deaths until the mother was discharged. Third, as the data was collected from hospital records, common limitation such as missing values, misclassification risks, and documentation bias could have occurred. Fourth, measuring or categorizing the outcome variable (adverse perinatal) as “adverse perinatal outcome” and “no adverse perinatal outcome” could have a potential to loss an information that treating as continuous variable. However, the outcome variable doesn’t satisfy the assumption of linearity. We recommend prospective studies that encompass more detailed sociodemographic, reproductive, and obstetric variables.

Conclusion

The overall prevalence of adverse perinatal outcomes was consistent with findings in other low-resource settings, at 65.1%. Earlier gestational age at disease onset, severe features, multifetal gestation, and primigravidity negatively affected perinatal outcomes. Early identification of disease through optimal antenatal care, anticipation of complications, and instituting context-specific individualized interventions could mitigate the high burden of adverse outcomes in high-risk groups such as pregnant women with preeclampsia–eclampsia syndrome. This goal can be achieved by empowering pregnant women with increased awareness of pregnancy complications, decentralizing advanced care, equipping remote health facilities with essential medical equipment, and training rural healthcare staff on early warning signs for prompt referral. Since most pregnancies complicated by PRE-E syndrome risk termination at preterm gestations, proven gestational-age-specific interventions such as administering neuroprotective drugs, steroids, and surfactant use postpartum cannot be overemphasized.
