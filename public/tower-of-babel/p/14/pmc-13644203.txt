Family emotional socialization, emotional understanding trajectories, and classroom positive expression: a three-wave longitudinal study of Chinese primary-school children
Authors: Zhiyu Yao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDrawing on a three-wave panel design spanning two academic years, this study traced how family emotional socialization, children’s emotional understanding, and classroom positive emotional expression unfold together over time. The sample comprised 426 Chinese parent–child dyads from urban public elementary schools. Within the focal within-person models, supportive reactions anchored the family-side predictor; non-supportive reactions, together with their punitive, minimization, and distress subtypes, fed the growth-curve and subscale analyses; and family emotional expressiveness was retained chiefly to ground the multidimensional measurement structure. Latent growth curve modeling described a clearly nonlinear trajectory for emotional understanding—accelerated gains in the earlier waves, followed by deceleration—a shape that aligns broadly with the normative consolidation of socio-emotional competence during early primary school. Against this normative backdrop, supportive parental practices predicted both higher initial levels and steeper growth of emotional understanding, whereas non-supportive reactions tracked the opposite pattern, indicating that family processes account for individual variation above and beyond age-graded change. Random-intercept cross-lagged panel analyses, which separate within-person dynamics from stable between-person differences, showed that supportive socialization prospectively predicted within-person increases in classroom positive expression at both measurement intervals, while the reverse paths did not reach significance. Multi-group RI-CLPM extensions stratified by respondent parent gender and by child gender produced broadly comparable within-person paths, with some attenuation in the smaller paternal subsample. When non-supportive reactions were disaggregated, punitive responses carried the strongest negative within-person link to subsequent emotional understanding, while minimization effects were comparatively muted. Emotional understanding operated as a significant longitudinal within-person mediator, accounting for roughly one-third of the within-person prospective effect. Together, these findings locate family emotional processes within a broader developmental ecology of children’s socio-emotional competence in the Chinese cultural context.Keywords: Family emotional socialization, Classroom positive emotional expression, Emotional understanding, Latent growth curve model, Random-intercept cross-lagged panel model, Longitudinal mediation

Introduction

The family is one of the earliest and most enduring developmental contexts in which children learn to recognize, interpret, and regulate affective states [1]. Over the past two decades, research on family emotional socialization—broadly defined as the parental reactions, emotion-coaching behaviors, and overall affective tenor that together characterize the household—has expanded considerably, and a substantial body of evidence now ties these practices to a wide range of socioemotional outcomes across childhood [2]. Grusec [3] frames family socialization as a fundamentally bidirectional process that integrates evolutionary predispositions, genetic factors, and socio-cultural influences, while stressing that how children perceive parental interventions is central to how socialization actually unfolds. Studies conducted in North America and Western Europe converge on a familiar pattern: supportive parental emotion coaching predicts better emotional competence in children, whereas dismissive or punitive reactions track poorer adjustment [4, 5].

In East Asian contexts the picture turns considerably more complex. Norms emphasizing emotional restraint, collectivist values, hierarchical parent–child authority structures, and an academically oriented parenting philosophy together create a distinctive socialization milieu, one that may bend these associations in ways the field has yet to chart in full [6–8]. Probing family emotional socialization in the Chinese context, then, is not merely cross-cultural replication. It tests the boundary conditions of socialization models that were largely built within Western, individualist societies, and so carries direct theoretical stakes. Yet most of the existing work still relies on single time-point designs, leaving open two critical questions: how family emotional socialization actually exerts its influence across developmental time, and—perhaps more pointedly—how children’s own emerging competencies feed back into the process.

Among the downstream outcomes most directly tied to children’s daily functioning, classroom positive emotional expression has drawn growing interest from developmental psychologists and educators alike. Children who share enthusiasm, display warmth toward peers, and convey enjoyment during learning activities tend to build stronger teacher–student relationships, gain greater peer acceptance, and show higher academic engagement [4, 9]. Several investigations link supportive parental emotion practices specifically to children’s tendency to express positive affect at school, hinting that the emotional repertoire rehearsed at home carries over into the classroom ecology [10]. Denham and colleagues [10], for instance, found that preschoolers whose parents engaged in more emotion coaching presented more positive social–emotional profiles upon school entry; Valiente et al. [9] showed that children’s emotional competence—partly shaped by family processes—predicted academic outcomes via heightened classroom engagement. A parallel line of evidence indicates that children with stronger emotion comprehension exhibit more prosocial and positive affective behaviors in peer contexts [11], drawing a direct empirical link between emotional understanding and classroom affective expression. Despite these advances, the temporal ordering of the family-to-classroom association—whether family practices truly precede changes in classroom affective behavior or whether the relationship is partly reciprocal—remains insufficiently tested, since most existing studies rest on concurrent correlational designs [12].

A plausible mechanism bridging family emotional socialization and children’s classroom affective behavior is emotional understanding: the cognitive ability to identify emotional cues, comprehend the causes and consequences of emotions, and grasp mixed or ambivalent feeling states [13]. It is worth pausing here to make clear how emotional understanding fits into the broader theoretical picture we draw on. Social learning theory [14] foregrounds the modelling and reinforcement of emotional behavior, while emotional security theory [15] foregrounds the child’s sense of safety within the family. Neither framework, on its own, places emotional understanding at center stage. We therefore treat emotional understanding as a cognitive–representational bridge between the two mechanisms, drawing conceptual weight from Eisenberg’s heuristic model of emotion socialization [5, 16], which explicitly posits that parental practices first refine children’s representational knowledge of emotion, and that this knowledge in turn enables more skilled, context-appropriate emotional expression. Read in this way, modelling and reinforcement (social learning) furnish the observable emotional content that children internalize; emotional security supplies the psychological conditions under which that content can actually be processed; and emotional understanding emerges as the representational outcome of the two routes operating in tandem. The empirical record fits this logic: Castro et al. [17] reported that parents’ emotion-related beliefs and behaviors predicted children’s recognition of emotion, and Ensor et al. [11] demonstrated that emotion understanding mediated the effects of mother–child mutuality on prosocial behaviors. Crucially, however, support for this mediational pathway has come almost exclusively from cross-sectional mediation analyses—a methodology now widely regarded as inadequate for establishing developmental mediation, since it confounds between-person differences with within-person change [18]. What the field still lacks, and what this study sets out to provide, is a longitudinal treatment of emotional understanding not as a static mediator but as a developmental trajectory whose growth parameters themselves may carry the link between family practices and classroom outcomes.

From the standpoint of developmental theory, two additional gaps stand out. The first concerns the ecology of measurement: Bronfenbrenner’s bioecological model underscores the importance of proximal processes that unfold over time within and across microsystems [19], and yet few empirical studies have simultaneously modeled the family microsystem and the classroom microsystem inside a single longitudinal framework. The conceptual implication is straightforward. If emotional socialization at home shapes emotional understanding, and emotional understanding then shapes classroom expression, a full account must track all three constructs across the same developmental window. The second gap concerns directionality. Children who display more positive affect in the classroom may elicit more supportive emotional responses from parents, creating a transactional loop [20], and developmental research increasingly highlights such bidirectional dynamics in which children’s socio-emotional characteristics shape the parental responses they receive [21, 22]. Untangling these reciprocal influences demands analytic tools—specifically, models that separate stable between-person differences from genuine within-person change—that have rarely been applied to this particular constellation of variables [23–25].

The literature also displays a pronounced cultural imbalance. Most research on family emotional socialization has been conducted in Western, individualist societies, and the generalizability of those findings to East Asian contexts—where emotional display norms and parenting philosophies differ in meaningful ways—warrants dedicated investigation [6, 7]. Chinese families, in particular, often place emotional restraint alongside academic achievement, producing a socialization milieu whose effects on children’s classroom affective expression may not mirror Western patterns [8]. Addressing this cultural gap is therefore not simply a matter of replication; it tests the boundary conditions of prevailing socialization models and so carries direct theoretical stakes.

The present study was designed to address these converging limitations. Drawing on a three-wave longitudinal panel spanning roughly 18 months, we tracked family emotional socialization practices, children’s classroom positive emotional expression, and children’s emotional understanding in a sample of Chinese primary-school children. Two complementary analytic objectives organize the work. First, a latent growth curve model characterizes the developmental trajectory of emotional understanding and tests whether its growth parameters mediate the longitudinal association between early family emotional socialization and later classroom positive affect [26]. Second, a random-intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM) fitted across the three measurement occasions—rather than the traditional CLPM, which conflates within-person and between-person variation—probes the directionality and temporal dynamics among the three core variables. The RI-CLPM specification lets us ask whether family practices prospectively predict classroom expression at the within-person level, whether reverse or reciprocal within-person effects emerge, and how emotional understanding participates in these wave-to-wave transitions [23, 24, 27].

By combining these two modeling strategies, the study makes several contributions. It moves beyond static snapshots toward a genuinely developmental account of how family emotion practices become embedded in children’s affective functioning at school. It treats emotional understanding not as a fixed trait but as a changing capacity whose rate and shape of growth carry explanatory weight. And it situates these processes in a non-Western cultural context, broadening the evidentiary base for theories of emotional socialization [28]. Taken together, these features place the present work at the intersection of developmental psychology and educational ecology, with concrete implications for family-based interventions aimed at fostering children’s positive emotional engagement in the classroom.

Theoretical framework of family emotional socialization

Eisenberg’s heuristic model of emotion socialization remains the most widely referenced framework for understanding how parents shape children’s affective development [16]. The model specifies three broad dimensions of parental emotion-related socialization behavior: emotion coaching, which covers direct instruction about the nature and regulation of feelings; emotion reactivity, referring to whether parents respond supportively or dismissively when children express emotion; and emotion expressiveness, the degree to which parents openly display their own affective states within the household [29]. Gottman’s meta-emotion philosophy extends this perspective by foregrounding parents’ awareness of and attitudes toward emotion itself, proposing that caregivers who hold an emotion-coaching philosophy treat children’s negative feelings as teachable moments rather than as problems to suppress [30]. Morris and colleagues broaden the scope further with a tripartite pathway model in which the family emotional climate influences child outcomes via three routes: observation and modeling, parenting practices, and the overall affective atmosphere of the home [31]. The three strands converge on a shared premise: family-level emotional processes operate through multiple, partially overlapping channels that jointly calibrate children’s emotional competence.

For the purposes of the present investigation, family emotional socialization is operationalized as a composite construct capturing parental emotion coaching, contingent reactions to child affect, and habitual emotional expressiveness within the family setting [32]. The contingent-reactions component is itself multidimensional. Drawing on the CCNES typology established by Fabes and colleagues [33, 34], we treat non-supportive reactions not as a single homogeneous tendency but as a small family of distinguishable responses: punitive (responding with anger or sanction), minimization (downplaying the importance of the child’s emotion), and distress (becoming visibly upset oneself in the face of the child’s emotion). The supportive side, in parallel, comprises problem-focused, emotion-focused, and expressive-encouragement responses, which our analyses aggregate into a single supportive composite for parsimony [33]. This tripartite operationalization aligns with validated measurement instruments widely adopted in developmental research and permits direct comparison across cultural contexts [33]. By anchoring measurement to these dimensions, the design preserves theoretical coherence with the Eisenberg–Morris integrative framework while retaining sufficient specificity for longitudinal modeling. The theoretical link between these socialization dimensions and children’s outcomes can be stated as follows. Bandura’s social learning theory [14] holds that children acquire emotional repertoires through observational learning and reinforcement within the family: when parents model adaptive emotional expressions or coach children through emotional episodes, children internalize these scripts and later deploy them in extrafamilial contexts such as the classroom. Davies and Cummings’ emotional security theory [15] adds that a supportive family emotional climate fosters a secure affective base, enabling children to explore and express emotions confidently outside the home. We take Eisenberg’s heuristic model [5, 16] as the overarching integrative scaffold that binds these two complementary mechanisms together, because it is this framework—rather than either social learning or emotional security theory in isolation—that explicitly routes parental socialization through children’s developing representational knowledge of emotion (that is, emotional understanding) before it shapes observable affective behavior. Grusec [3] additionally highlights that socialization unfolds across multiple domains—protection, reciprocity, guided learning, and group participation—each requiring distinctive parenting actions, providing further theoretical grounding for the multidimensional operationalization adopted here.

Developmental links between children’s classroom positive emotional expression and emotional understanding

Children’s tendency to express positive affect in classroom settings—visible as enthusiasm during group activities, warmth in peer exchanges, and enjoyment signals during learning tasks—follows a developmental trajectory shaped by both maturational processes and socialization inputs [35]. Halberstadt’s affective communication competence model holds that skilled emotional expression depends not only on the capacity to send affective signals but also on the ability to read emotional information accurately, implying that expression and understanding are functionally intertwined [36]. Pons and Harris offer a complementary developmental account, identifying a hierarchical progression in emotional understanding: children first master basic emotion recognition, then move on to causal attribution of feelings, and ultimately acquire reflective awareness involving mixed emotions and regulatory cognition [37]. This staged progression matters because each level equips the child with increasingly sophisticated tools for calibrating affective displays to social context. Importantly, Pons and Harris’s own longitudinal work [37] documents substantial inter-individual stability alongside normative gains across the 7-to-12 age window, suggesting that any model of emotional understanding growth in early primary school should treat normative consolidation as the developmental backdrop against which contextual socialization effects operate.

The link between these two competencies becomes particularly relevant once the family-to-classroom transfer of emotional skills is brought into view. If parental socialization practices supply children with richer emotional knowledge—as both Eisenberg’s and Morris’s frameworks predict—then that knowledge should, in principle, enhance children’s capacity for contextually appropriate positive expression at school [38]. Empirical work has begun to support this reasoning, showing that children with stronger emotion comprehension tend to display more prosocial and positive affective behaviors in peer settings [11]. What has not yet been tested, however, is whether the growth trajectory of emotional understanding—not merely its static level—mediates the longitudinal path from family emotional input to classroom affective output, and it is precisely this gap that the present study takes up [17].

Research hypotheses and theoretical model construction

Three interrelated hypotheses emerge from the foregoing synthesis, which draws on Bandura’s social learning theory—where children internalize emotional repertoires through observational learning and reinforcement within the family [14]—on Davies and Cummings’ emotional security theory, where a supportive family emotional climate fosters a secure base from which children engage confidently with extrafamilial contexts [15], and on Eisenberg’s heuristic model [5, 16], which integrates both by routing parental emotion practices through children’s developing emotional representations. First, we expect supportive family emotional socialization—the focal family predictor in our primary within-person models—at earlier waves to positively predict children’s classroom positive emotional expression at subsequent waves at the within-person level (H1), reflecting a directional socialization pathway from the home microsystem to the school microsystem [39]. Second, we expect children’s emotional understanding to mediate this longitudinal within-person association; critically, we expect its developmental trajectory itself to be nonlinear—accelerating during early primary school years before gradually decelerating—so that both the initial level and the rate of change carry mediating significance (H2) [40]. Third, because parental reactions to child emotion are not uniformly beneficial, we expect supportive emotional responses (e.g., comforting, problem-focused reactions) and the punitive, minimization, and distress subtypes of non-supportive responses to exert differential and at least partly opposing effects on children’s emotional understanding growth and on their subsequent classroom positive expression (H3) [34].

Taken together, these three hypotheses form an integrative theoretical model in which family emotional socialization serves as the distal predictor, the developmental trajectory of emotional understanding operates as the dynamic mediating mechanism, and classroom positive emotional expression stands as the proximal developmental outcome. The model is primarily unidirectional, both for theoretical parsimony and to keep faith with the family-to-school transfer logic articulated above. That said, we explicitly acknowledge the transactional nature of parent–child emotional processes [20, 21] and empirically test both forward and reverse within-person cross-lagged paths, so as not to foreclose the possibility of child-driven effects on family practices. Research design and participant selection

A three-wave longitudinal panel design, with annual measurement intervals spanning two academic years, structured this study. Data collection took place at the end of each school year (T1, T2, and T3), producing approximately 12-month gaps between adjacent waves—a spacing well suited to capturing meaningful developmental change in emotional competencies during early childhood while minimizing practice effects from repeated assessment [41]. Figure 2 illustrates the overall research design and the data collection workflow across the three waves.

Research design and data collection flowchart across three waves

Participants were recruited from six urban public elementary schools in Xinxiang, a prefecture-level city in Henan Province, central China, through a stratified cluster sampling procedure. Schools were first stratified by district-level socioeconomic indicators—specifically, the average annual household income and educational attainment profile of each district, as recorded in the municipal education bureau’s administrative database—yielding three socioeconomic strata (low, medium, and high). Two schools were then randomly selected from each stratum, and within each selected school two intact classrooms from each of Grades 1 through 3 were randomly drawn, for a total of 36 classrooms [42]. This stratification ensured that the sample captured meaningful variation in family socioeconomic backgrounds. Grades 1 through 3 were targeted because the early primary school years (roughly ages 6–9) represent a critical developmental window for the acquisition of emotional understanding and the consolidation of classroom affective behaviors [13, 37].

In total, 426 parent–child dyads consented to participate at T1, each composed of one target child and one primary caregiver. Mothers predominated (351 dyads, 82.4%), with the remaining 75 dyads (17.6%) reporting through the father (see Table 1 for details). This mother-dominant pattern is consistent with the broader Chinese parenting-research literature, and the proportions are reported here so that the multi-group analyses by respondent parent in “Data analysis strategy” and “Within-person cross-lagged analysis and longitudinal mediation testing” can be read with the appropriate sample-size context in mind. By T2, 398 dyads (93.4%) remained in the study; at T3, 379 dyads (89.0%) completed all assessments. Attrition was largely driven by family relocation and school transfer. Independent-samples t-tests and chi-square tests comparing the 47 families lost to attrition with the 379 retained families revealed no significant differences in child gender, child age, grade level, caregiver education, household income, or any T1 core variable score (all ps > 0.15), confirming that attrition was non-systematic. Little’s MCAR test further indicated that the missing data were missing completely at random (χ² = 42.17, df = 38, p = .30), supporting the use of full information maximum likelihood estimation in the subsequent analyses [43].

Research instruments and measurement methods

Family emotional socialization practices were assessed via two complementary parent-report instruments. We operationally defined this composite construct as covering three interrelated but conceptually distinct sub-dimensions: supportive reactions, non-supportive reactions, and emotional expressiveness. Consistent with how Fabes and colleagues originally designed the instrument [33, 34], we deliberately keep the three non-supportive subtypes separate throughout the Methods, Results, and Discussion, because each carries a distinct theoretical signature and may operate differently within a Chinese cultural setting [6, 7]. Specifically, the non-supportive composite was decomposed into three CCNES-defined subscales: (a) punitive reactions—parental responses involving anger, sanction, or withdrawal of privileges when the child shows negative emotion; (b) minimization reactions—parental responses that dismiss the seriousness or legitimacy of the child’s emotion; and (c) distress reactions—the parent becoming visibly upset, anxious, or overwhelmed in response to the child’s negative emotion. Each subscale was scored as the mean of the relevant items across the 12 hypothetical CCNES scenarios. The supportive composite, in turn, combined problem-focused, emotion-focused, and expressive-encouragement responses [33].

The supportive and three non-supportive subscales were measured with an adapted version of the Coping with Children’s Negative Emotions Scale (CCNES) [44]. Emotional expressiveness, the third sub-dimension, was measured with the Self-Expressiveness in the Family Questionnaire (SEFQ) [45]. Confirmatory factor analyses at each wave supported a five-factor structure distinguishing supportive reactions, punitive reactions, minimization reactions, distress reactions, and emotional expressiveness (CFI > 0.92; RMSEA < 0.055), with inter-factor correlations among the three non-supportive subscales ranging from 0.42 to 0.58—high enough to justify the overarching non-supportive composite, but low enough to warrant separate examination of each subscale. Both instruments were administered to the primary caregiver at each wave. It is worth being explicit about the analytic standing of the three dimensions, since they do not enter the models on equal footing. Supportive reactions serve as the focal family predictor in the primary within-person models reported below; the non-supportive reactions feed the growth-curve and subscale-disaggregated analyses; and family emotional expressiveness—which indexes the household’s ambient affective climate rather than parents’ contingent responses to a specific emotional episode—was retained chiefly to anchor the five-factor measurement structure rather than to act as a separate within-person predictor. Mapping of core variables to measurement instruments

As Fig. 3 illustrates, children’s classroom positive emotional expression was captured through a multi-method strategy that combined teacher-rated assessments with systematic behavioral coding. Classroom teachers completed a 15-item rating scale tapping children’s observable displays of enthusiasm, warmth, and enjoyment during instructional and peer-interactive contexts [46]. In parallel, trained research assistants conducted structured classroom observations using a time-sampling behavioral coding system, recording instances of positive affective behaviors across three 20-minute sessions per child per wave. Inter-rater reliability, computed on 20% of coded sessions, yielded Cohen’s κ values ranging from 0.81 to 0.87 across the three waves. To establish the validity of combining the two sources into a composite score, we computed convergent validity indicators: the Pearson correlations between teacher ratings and behavioral coding composites were r = .62 (T1), r = .65 (T2), and r = .68 (T3), all significant at p < .001, indicating substantial agreement between the two assessment methods. Following prior research that has similarly aggregated teacher-report and observational data to reduce method-specific bias [4, 35], composite scores were obtained by first standardizing each source (z-transformation) and then averaging the two standardized scores.

Two tasks gauged emotional understanding ability. The Test of Emotion Comprehension (TEC) assessed nine hierarchically ordered components of emotion knowledge, ranging from basic recognition to the understanding of mixed and moral emotions [47]. A supplementary situational emotion attribution task then presented children with short vignettes and asked them to identify the characters’ feelings and to explain the underlying causes [48]. Composite scores were obtained by standardizing and averaging across both tasks.

Data analysis strategy

The analyses unfolded in three coordinated steps: first, the developmental trajectory of emotional understanding was characterized via latent growth curve modeling (LGM); second, within-person cross-lagged effects were estimated through the random-intercept cross-lagged panel model (RI-CLPM); third, longitudinal mediation was tested on top of the RI-CLPM output. All models were estimated in Mplus 8.3, using robust maximum likelihood (MLR) and full information maximum likelihood handling of missing data [43, 49].

It is worth stating plainly how each family-socialization dimension maps onto these models, since the three dimensions are not interchangeable predictors. The focal trivariate RI-CLPM uses supportive reactions (FES-Sup) as the family predictor, paired with emotional understanding and classroom positive expression; this keeps the central model identifiable with only three waves and aligns the focal predictor with the family-to-school transfer logic that motivates H1. Non-supportive reactions are not set aside but examined where they bear most directly on the questions at hand: the conditional latent growth model uses the non-supportive composite to predict the growth parameters of emotional understanding, and a dedicated RI-CLPM extension disaggregates the composite into its punitive, minimization, and distress subtypes (H3). Family emotional expressiveness (SEFQ) was measured to establish the multidimensional construct and validated through the five-factor confirmatory model, but it was not entered as a separate within-person predictor in the focal RI-CLPM, both to preserve parsimony and identification and because it captures the household’s diffuse emotional tone rather than the child-directed contingent reactions that the present hypotheses concern.

The developmental trajectory of emotional understanding was modeled using LGM. In this framework, repeated measures are decomposed into two latent factors—an intercept (η0i) capturing the individual’s initial level and a slope (η1i) capturing the rate of change [50]. Because the traditional CLPM conflates stable between-person differences with within-person fluctuations [23, 25, 51], we adopt the RI-CLPM as the primary analytic engine of the present study for examining cross-temporal directional influences among family emotional socialization (FES, operationalized here by its supportive component, FES-Sup), emotional understanding (EU), and classroom positive emotional expression (CPE); the traditional CLPM is retained only as a supplementary comparison in “Within-person cross-lagged analysis and longitudinal mediation testing” and the Related files. Following Hamaker et al. [23] and Mulder and Hamaker [24], the RI-CLPM partitions each observed score Y_ti into a stable random intercept RI_Y, i (capturing trait-like between-person variation) and a time-varying within-person residual Y*_ti, and the cross-lagged paths are estimated exclusively on the within-person residuals. In this expression, φ_YY captures within-person autoregressive stability, φ_YX and φ_YZ represent the within-person cross-lagged effects from the other two constructs, and ζ_ti is the within-person disturbance term. In the trivariate specification, each of FES-Sup, EU, and CPE has its own random intercept, and the three random intercepts are allowed to covary freely. Initial ML estimation with default starting values produced a small negative residual variance at T1 for CPE, a common numerical issue in three-wave RI-CLPM specifications [24]. We addressed this in three coordinated steps that are reproduced verbatim in the accompanying input files. First, we switched the estimator from ML to MLR to better accommodate mild non-normality. Second, rather than dropping the offending parameter, we bounded it: the T1 within-person residual variance for CPE was constrained to be non-negative through a MODEL CONSTRAINT statement that re-expressed it as the square of a freely estimated auxiliary parameter, a device recommended by Mulder and Hamaker [24] that keeps the variance admissible without arbitrarily fixing it to zero. Third, we re-ran the model from 200 random starting-value sets, retaining the 20 best for final optimization (via the STARTS option), to confirm that the solution was a global rather than a local maximum. With these adjustments the RI-CLPM converged cleanly and yielded admissible estimates throughout.

We extended the basic RI-CLPM in three substantively motivated directions, following the framework of Mulder and Hamaker [24] and the multi-group and multiple-indicator logic outlined therein. First, to probe whether the within-person dynamics differed depending on whose voice was being captured at home, we fitted a multi-group RI-CLPM with respondent parent gender (mother, n = 351; father, n = 75) as the grouping variable, comparing a configural model against a model that constrained all within-person cross-lagged paths to equality across groups via a Wald χ² test. Second, to test whether child gender moderates the within-person dynamics, we fitted a parallel multi-group RI-CLPM with child gender (boys, n = 228; girls, n = 198) as the grouping variable, again contrasting an unconstrained model against a path-constrained model. Third, to disaggregate the broad CCNES non-supportive composite into its theoretically distinct constituents, we re-estimated the RI-CLPM with punitive, minimization, and distress-related reactions entered as three separate within-person predictors of subsequent EU and CPE, rather than collapsed into a single non-supportive score. Complete and directly executable Mplus input files for all four RI-CLPM specifications—the full-sample model, the two multi-group models, and the subscale-disaggregated model—are provided in the Related files accompanying this manuscript, each with fully defined parameter labels and a closed-form MODEL CONSTRAINT block for the indirect effects. To make the syntax runnable without access to the restricted raw data, we also supply an annotated simulated dataset whose covariance structure reproduces the reported estimates, so that every table can be regenerated end to end.

Within-person mediation through the growth factors of emotional understanding was tested by regressing the LGM slope and intercept on T1 family emotional socialization, and in turn regressing T3 classroom positive expression on these growth parameters. The indirect effect was computed as the product of the corresponding coefficients [52].

In this expression, a is the path from FES to the EU growth factor and b is the path from the EU growth factor to CPE. Statistical significance was evaluated through bias-corrected bootstrap confidence intervals with 5,000 resamples [53]. We additionally re-estimated the indirect effect using the within-person residuals from the RI-CLPM rather than raw scores, yielding a within-person counterpart of the longitudinal mediation analysis. Model fit across all analyses was judged against conventional benchmarks: CFI ≥ 0.95, TLI ≥ 0.90, RMSEA ≤ 0.06, and SRMR ≤ 0.08, with the caveat that long-run trait-like stability in RI-CLPM is absorbed into the random intercepts and that conventional cutoffs can therefore behave somewhat differently than they do for the CLPM [24, 25]. Accordingly, we place extra interpretive weight on the parameter estimates themselves and on the side-by-side CLPM versus RI-CLPM comparison reported below.

Developmental trajectory of emotional understanding

An unconditional linear LGM was first fitted to the emotional understanding scores across the three waves. This model produced acceptable fit (χ² = 5.83, df = 1, p = .016; CFI = 0.98; RMSEA = 0.054; SRMR = 0.023), with a significant mean intercept (µη0 = 3.57, p < .001) and a significant mean slope (µη1 = 0.42, p < .001), confirming overall upward growth. Comparison with a model that allowed the T3 loading to be freely estimated, however, indicated a notably improved fit (Δχ² = 4.61, Δdf = 1, p = .032), with the freed loading estimated at 1.72 rather than the fixed value of 2.00—evidence that growth decelerated between T2 and T3 [54]. The global fit indices deserve cautious reading: with df = 1 for the linear model and a saturated extended model, fit indices are known to be relatively insensitive under such low-df conditions, and the parameter estimates themselves carry more interpretive weight than the global indices alone. Figure 5 places the fit indices for the linear and the freely estimated (nonlinear) models side by side, confirming the superiority of the latter across all indicators.

Discussion

The present study set out to trace the longitudinal connections among family emotional socialization practices, children’s classroom positive emotional expression, and the developmental trajectory of emotional understanding across three annual measurement waves. Three principal findings emerged, each warranting careful interpretation.

The first and most direct result is that supportive family emotional socialization at earlier time points prospectively predicted within-person increases in children’s classroom positive affective expression at subsequent waves, while the reverse paths did not reach significance. Because the cross-lagged paths estimated here come from a RI-CLPM that partitions stable between-person variation into random intercepts, this forward-directional pattern admits a within-person reading: when a given child experiences a higher-than-usual level of supportive emotion socialization at one wave, that same child also shows a higher-than-usual level of classroom positive expression at the next wave, even after accounting for trait-like differences between families [23–25]. The pattern aligns with social learning theory’s emphasis on observational acquisition and with the emotional security hypothesis, both of which predict that supportive home interactions feed forward into school-context affective behavior. We frame the interpretation in deliberately modest terms: a within-person prospective association under RI-CLPM still does not, on its own, license a strong causal claim—omitted time-varying confounders and timescale mismatches remain possible threats—but it is appreciably stronger than what the traditional CLPM in Table 5 could license, because the between-person trait-like component has been partialled out.

It is also worth asking, from a deeper cultural perspective, why the measured impact of non-supportive responses—particularly the minimization subtype—was attenuated in our Chinese sample. In cultures shaped by Confucian values, collectivist orientations place social harmony and in-group cohesion above individual emotional expression [8, 58]. Within this framework, parent–child relationships exhibit a pronounced authority gradient in which parental directives—including those that might appear dismissive by Western standards—are often read by children as expressions of care and guidance rather than rejection [59]. The academic-oriented parenting philosophy prevalent in many Chinese families adds another layer: parents may strategically downplay emotional disturbances to redirect children’s attention toward academic tasks, a practice that children in this cultural context may treat as normative rather than harmful [60]. Cui et al. [21], examining bidirectional effects between parental emotion socialization practices and Chinese adolescents’ adjustment, similarly found that certain non-supportive practices operated differently in the Chinese context than in Western samples.

Critically, our subscale-disaggregated RI-CLPM (Table 8) supplies direct empirical traction on this culturally informed interpretation. The punitive subtype carried the largest negative within-person effect, the distress subtype an intermediate effect, and the minimization subtype the smallest and statistically non-significant effect. Minimizing responses may align with Chinese display rules that encourage emotional moderation [6, 7], such that children do not register them as harmful, whereas overtly punitive reactions appear to be experienced as harmful even within a collectivist framework. This nuanced subtype-specific pattern does not weaken the overall negative association of non-supportive reactions with emotional competence; it sharpens that association, locating the bulk of the predictive weight in the punitive—not the minimization—subtype, and it places our findings within the emerging literature on localized family emotional socialization among Chinese families [28, 58, 60].

The second major finding concerns the developmental trajectory of emotional understanding itself. The latent growth curve analyses showed that a nonlinear model—with accelerated growth between T1 and T2 followed by deceleration between T2 and T3—fitted the data better than a strictly linear specification. This trajectory is developmentally sensible: the early primary school years are a period of rapid cognitive and social expansion in which children move from basic emotion recognition toward more complex attributional and regulatory understanding, and it stands to reason that the steepest gains would occur during this transitional window before a partial ceiling effect emerges. The accelerate-then-plateau shape is highly consistent with the normative consolidation of emotional understanding that Pons and Harris’s own longitudinal work has documented during this age range [37], and with the broader developmental literature on socio-emotional competence in early primary school. A substantial portion of the growth pattern we observe is therefore likely attributable to age-graded consolidation of children’s general socio-cognitive reasoning about emotions, rather than to any single contextual force. The significant negative covariance between the intercept and slope deserves particular attention. Children who entered the study with lower emotional understanding showed faster subsequent growth—a compensatory-looking pattern—though regression to the mean, measurement scaling effects, and ceiling effects cannot be ruled out without additional distributional checks (these checks are reported in the Related files and do not eliminate the compensatory signal, but neither do they decisively rule out the alternative explanations). Against this normative backdrop, family emotional socialization was systematically associated with individual variation in both the starting level and the rate of change: supportive practices predicted both a higher starting level and a steeper growth rate, whereas non-supportive reactions predicted the opposite.

This invites a further, more cautious interpretive question. Our measure of emotional understanding (the TEC and the situational attribution task) indexes children’s socio-cognitive interpretation of emotional situations, and the classroom positive expression measure captures children’s overt affective behavior in a school setting. Both constructs show high stability across waves (r = .63–0.74 within construct) and strong concurrent associations with each other (r = .30–0.46), a pattern that raises the possibility that the model is partly capturing the development of children’s general socio-emotional competence rather than a specific family-driven mechanism. We take this concern seriously. On one reading of the data, emotional understanding and classroom positive expression may share an underlying general factor of socio-emotional competence, and family socialization may exert its effects partly by shaping that broader capacity rather than one discrete component of it. We did not fit a bifactor model or run a formal test of whether the two constructs could be collapsed onto a single latent dimension, and we flag this as a genuine interpretive limit. Future work using bifactor or multi-trait–multi-method designs would be valuable for cleanly separating a general socio-emotional competence factor from construct-specific variance.

The third finding—that emotional understanding served as a significant within-person longitudinal mediator linking family socialization to classroom positive expression—completes the theoretical chain we hypothesized. The within-person mediation was not trivial in magnitude; it accounted for roughly a third of the total within-person prospective effect. Obtaining this mediation under the RI-CLPM rather than the traditional CLPM matters: it means that the mediational signal survives partialling out stable between-family trait variation, and is therefore more compellingly interpretable as a within-person developmental process than the equivalent CLPM-based mediation would be [23–25]. Even so, we retain interpretive caution. Within-person mediation in a three-wave RI-CLPM cannot rule out omitted time-varying confounding, and the assumption that parenting at one wave influences emotional understanding approximately one year later, which in turn shapes classroom expression another year later, hinges on the causal lag matching the measurement interval. If the true causal lag is shorter or longer than 12 months, the within-person estimates may be attenuated or temporally misaligned, an issue that intensive longitudinal designs would be better placed to resolve. The pathway from parental supportive reactions to enhanced emotional understanding to increased classroom positive expression nevertheless mirrors the theoretical sequence proposed by Eisenberg’s heuristic model: parents who coach, validate, and model emotional competence furnish children with the representational tools needed to read social contexts accurately and to express affect in ways that facilitate positive engagement.

One unexpected aspect of the within-person results merits comment. We had anticipated that the reverse cross-lagged paths—from classroom expression back to family socialization—might reach significance, reflecting the kind of transactional influence that developmental theorists increasingly emphasize [20, 21]. They did not, in either the CLPM or the RI-CLPM. Several explanations are possible. Parental socialization practices may be more dispositional and less responsive to children’s extrafamilial behavior than transactional models assume, at least over annual intervals; under RI-CLPM, the trait-like component is absorbed into the random intercept, so genuinely dispositional parents will appear especially unresponsive at the within-person level. Alternatively, the feedback loop may operate through mechanisms we did not measure, such as teacher communication with parents or children’s own verbal reports about school experiences. A third possibility is that the annual spacing of our waves is simply too coarse to capture the short-timescale dynamics through which child-to-parent influences most likely operate. For these reasons we treat the unidirectional within-person pattern reported here as provisional, and we explicitly flag the need for denser, shorter-interval designs before any claim of purely unidirectional family-to-child influence is accepted.

Comparing our findings with cross-cultural evidence reveals both convergences and divergences. The overall direction of within-person effects—supportive socialization being positively associated with emotional competence and positive expression—replicates patterns observed in Western samples, supporting the cross-cultural generalizability of core socialization mechanisms. The relatively weaker role of non-supportive reactions (concentrated in the punitive subtype), the absence of significant child-to-parent within-person effects, and the broadly comparable patterns across maternal and paternal respondents and across boys and girls in our multi-group RI-CLPMs nevertheless distinguish our Chinese sample from findings reported in some European and American studies, where bidirectional influences and stronger punitive-reaction effects have been documented. These discrepancies likely reflect cultural variation in emotional display rules and parenting norms—including the collectivist emphasis on group harmony, the Confucian authority gradient in parent–child relations, and the normative acceptance of parental emotional restraint training [58, 59]—rather than fundamental differences in developmental processes.

Conclusion

This study used a three-wave longitudinal panel design spanning two academic years to examine how family emotional socialization practices relate to children’s classroom positive emotional expression over time, and whether the developmental trajectory of emotional understanding mediates this association. By combining latent growth curve modeling with random-intercept cross-lagged panel analysis—extended into multi-group RI-CLPM and subscale-disaggregated RI-CLPM specifications—in a sample of 426 Chinese parent–child dyads, the work addresses several gaps in the existing literature concerning temporal ordering, within-person growth dynamics, and the cross-microsystem transfer of emotional competence. Three core conclusions follow.

First, family emotional socialization practices—particularly supportive parental reactions such as comforting, validating, and coaching children through emotional episodes—exerted a significant and consistent positive within-person longitudinal effect on children’s tendency to express positive emotions in the classroom. Because this effect emerged under RI-CLPM rather than traditional CLPM, the estimate is purged of stable between-family trait covariation and is therefore more cleanly interpretable as a within-person developmental association than the equivalent CLPM estimate [23–25]. The absence of significant reverse within-person paths from classroom expression back to parental practices suggests that, at least within annual measurement intervals and in this cultural context, the predominant direction of within-person predictive association flows from the family microsystem toward the school microsystem. Equally important, non-supportive parental reactions showed a negative—though weaker, and concentrated in the punitive subtype—prospective within-person association with classroom positive expression. The differentiation between punitive, minimization, and distress subtypes implies that the active presence of harsh emotional response, rather than the milder dismissive behaviors more easily read as benign within a Chinese cultural frame, carries most of the explanatory weight.

Second, children’s emotional understanding followed a nonlinear growth trajectory marked by rapid gains during the earlier portion of the study period and a gradual deceleration thereafter. The latent growth curve analyses confirmed that this accelerating-then-plateauing pattern fitted the data significantly better than a simple linear specification, and individual differences in both the starting level and the rate of growth were substantial. We interpret this pattern primarily as evidence of normative consolidation of socio-emotional competence during early primary school [37], onto which family emotional socialization layers a meaningful explanatory contribution: children whose parents engaged in more supportive emotion-related behaviors entered the study with higher emotional understanding and grew faster, while the reverse held for children exposed to more non-supportive reactions. The negative intercept–slope covariance points to a compensatory-looking dynamic, although regression to the mean and ceiling effects remain plausible alternative explanations that distributional checks in the Related files do not fully rule out.

Third, emotional understanding functioned as a significant within-person longitudinal mediator on the pathway from family emotional socialization to classroom positive expression. The within-person indirect effect, tested through bias-corrected bootstrap procedures within an RI-CLPM framework, accounted for approximately one-third of the total within-person prospective association. This mediational finding is theoretically informative because it identifies the cognitive–affective process through which family-level emotional input becomes embedded in child-level emotional output in a different social context, while the RI-CLPM framework ensures the result is robust to stable between-family confounding [23, 24].

Two primary theoretical contributions follow from the present study. First, it demonstrates that RI-CLPM specifications—extended into multi-group and subscale-disaggregated variants—can be productively combined with latent growth curve models to address questions about both within-person directional influence and developmental shape within a single investigation, an analytic strategy that remains underused in the emotional socialization literature. Second, the work extends the empirical reach of Eisenberg’s heuristic model and Morris’s tripartite pathway framework into a Chinese cultural context, showing that the core structural relationships posited by these models hold—albeit with parameter magnitudes that reflect culturally specific emotional display norms and parenting philosophies.

The practical implications follow naturally from these findings, and they extend to both family education and school-based practice. For family education programs, the results highlight the value of training parents in supportive emotion coaching—not only to improve the home emotional climate, but to foster competencies that children carry into the classroom. Concretely, parenting workshops could teach caregivers to recognize and validate children’s emotional experiences, model constructive emotional expression, and replace punitive reactions in particular (rather than the milder minimization responses) with problem-focused guidance [3, 21, 60]. For school-based affective education, the findings suggest that efforts to promote positive emotional expression among students may be most effective when coordinated with family-level interventions aimed at the emotional socialization environment. The within-person mediating role of emotional understanding further implies that curricula designed to build children’s emotion knowledge—identifying feelings, understanding their causes, recognizing mixed emotions—may serve as a practical lever for enhancing classroom affective engagement, particularly for children from less emotionally supportive home environments [36, 37]. Teachers, as front-line observers of children’s emotional behavior, could also benefit from professional development that equips them to identify children whose classroom emotional expression patterns suggest inadequate family emotional support, enabling timely referral to school counselors or family education resources. Cao [60] highlighted the importance of understanding the relationship between parental involvement and family education choices within the Chinese educational ecosystem, and our findings complement this perspective by showing that the emotional dimension of family involvement carries consequences that extend well beyond the home into children’s daily school experience.

Several limitations temper these conclusions and point toward productive future directions. First, although the RI-CLPM substantially improves on the traditional CLPM by separating within-person dynamics from stable between-person differences [23–25], three waves remain the minimum on which the RI-CLPM can be identified; replication with four or more waves would yield more stable estimates and would allow time-varying constraints to be relaxed [24]. Second, the sample was drawn exclusively from urban public elementary schools in a single Chinese city (Xinxiang, Henan Province), restricting generalizability to rural populations and to other cultural contexts. Third, while we combined teacher ratings and behavioral coding for classroom expression, the family socialization measures relied primarily on caregiver self-report, raising the possibility of social desirability bias. The mother-dominant respondent profile (82.4%) is worth foregrounding here: although our multi-group RI-CLPM did not detect significant differences in within-person dynamics between maternal and paternal respondents, the paternal subsample (n = 75) was too small to provide a powerful test, and future studies should recruit larger numbers of fathers [3, 21]. Fourth, the multi-group RI-CLPM by child gender showed no significant moderation, but child gender is only one of many potentially relevant moderators—child temperament, broader cultural orientation, and family structure could all condition these effects, and none were modeled here. Fifth, annual measurement intervals, though appropriate for capturing broad developmental trends, may miss finer-grained fluctuations in emotional processes that operate on shorter timescales. Sixth, because the latent growth curve model is estimated with only three measurement occasions, global fit indices for the LGM should be read with caution; we therefore rely more on parameter estimates than on CFI/RMSEA values in the LGM Sect [54]. Seventh, as discussed earlier in this section, our emotional understanding and classroom positive expression measures may partly tap a shared dimension of general socio-emotional competence, so the reported within-person mediation likely captures a combination of construct-specific and general-competence variance; bifactor modeling in future work would help resolve this.

Several targeted extensions would advance the line of work begun here. First, cross-cultural comparative designs should test whether the parameter asymmetries we observed—particularly the weaker minimization effect and the absent within-person child-to-parent feedback—replicate in Western and other East Asian samples. Second, RI-CLPM extensions with at least four waves would permit weaker time-invariance assumptions on the within-person paths and would allow latent curve models with structured residuals to be fitted alongside the RI-CLPM for cross-validation [24, 25]. Third, adding physiological indicators of emotional reactivity, such as heart rate variability or cortisol patterns, alongside behavioral and self-report measures, would strengthen the multi-method foundation and reduce shared-method variance. Fourth, recruiting both mothers and fathers from the same families would permit dyadic RI-CLPM specifications that test mother–father interaction effects on children’s outcomes. Fifth, more intensive micro-longitudinal designs—daily diary studies or experience-sampling approaches—could complement the macro-level trajectory perspective offered here. Finally, fitting bifactor models that partition general socio-emotional competence from construct-specific emotional understanding, and testing additional moderators such as child temperament, would refine our understanding of for whom and under what conditions the observed within-person pathways operate most strongly.
