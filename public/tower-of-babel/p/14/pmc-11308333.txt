Early childhood risk factors for later onset of pediatric chronic pain: a multi-method longitudinal study
Authors: Maria Pavlova, Melanie Noel, Serena L. Orr, Andrew Walker, Sheri Madigan, Sheila W. McDonald, Suzanne C. Tough, Kathryn A. Birnie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPediatric chronic pain (i.e., pain lasting ≥ 3 months) is prevalent, disabling, and costly. It spikes in adolescence, interrupts psychosocial development and functioning, and often co-occurs with mental health problems. Chronic pain often begins spontaneously without prior injuries and/or other disorders. Prospective longitudinal cohort studies following children from early childhood, prior to chronic pain onset, are needed to examine contributing factors, such as early pain experiences and mental health. Using data from a longitudinal community pregnancy cohort (All Our Families; AOF), the present study examined the associations between early developmental risk factors, including early childhood pain experiences and mental health symptoms, and the onset of pediatric chronic pain at ages 8 and 11 years.MethodsAvailable longitudinal AOF data from child age 4 months, as well as 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, and 11 years, were used. Mothers reported their child’s pain experiences (e.g., hospitalizations, vaccinations, gut problems) at each timepoint from 4 months to 8 years, child chronic pain at age 8, and child mental health symptoms at ages 5 and 8 years. Children reported their chronic pain frequency and interference at age 11. Adaptive least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regressions were used to select predictor variables. Complete case analyses were complemented by multiple imputation using chained equation (MICE) models.ResultsGut problems, emergency room visits, frequent pain complaints, and headaches at age 5 or earlier, as well as female sex, were associated with increased risk of maternal reported child chronic pain at age 8. Maternal reported chronic pain at age 8 was associated with higher levels of child-reported pain frequency and pain interferences at age 11. Boys self-reported lower levels of pain interference at age 11.ConclusionsSome, but not all, painful experiences (e.g., gut problems, ER visits, pain complaints) in early life contribute to pediatric chronic pain onset and should be considered for screening and early intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-024-04951-4.

Pediatric chronic pain (i.e., pain lasting ≥ 3 months) is a prevalent condition that affects 1 in 5 youth [1] and imposes high societal costs estimated at $19 billion USD annually [2]. Pediatric chronic pain tends to spike in adolescence [1], and can interrupt cognitive, psychological, social, and physical development and functioning [3]. Chronic pain often co-occurs with, and is exacerbated by, internalizing mental health symptoms (e.g., anxiety and depressive symptoms), which tend to persist into adulthood [4–6].

Youth and families with lived experience of chronic pain, as well as policymakers and healthcare professionals, have identified prevention and early treatment of chronic pain as a top priority [7]. As chronic pain often spontaneously begins without any prior injuries [1], prospective longitudinal cohort studies that follow children from early childhood, prior to chronic pain onset, are essential to identify, and differentiate between, contributing risk factors and co-occurring issues. Yet, such cohort studies are exceedingly rare [8].

Pediatric chronic pain often initially presents in primary care settings. Pain complaints are one of the most common problems seen in primary care accounting for up to 15% of pediatric primary care appointments [9]. In the average 3.3 years it takes for youth to access tertiary care for pediatric chronic pain [10], their pain symptoms continue to be primarily managed by family physicians and pediatricians. Therefore, recognizing, screening for, and addressing early risk factors that are associated with the onset of chronic pain are important for primary care providers.

Retrospective reports of adults with chronic pain and cross-sectional data suggest that premature birth, as well as painful procedures and injuries earlier in life, may result in physiological changes to the nervous system (e.g., abnormal synaptic connectivity, decreased pain threshold, hyperinnervation) [11, 12] and, thus, contribute to chronic pain onset. Internalizing and externalizing (e.g., aggression, impulsivity) symptoms commonly co-occur with pediatric chronic pain at rates higher than their peers without pain [5, 13]. Emerging evidence from prospective studies with clinical samples suggests that chronic pain in adolescence predicts the onset of depression, suicidality, and anxiety symptoms, as well as opioid use and risk for socioeconomic disparities [14–17]. However, more population-based prospective cohort studies that employ multiple informants (e.g., youth, parents) are needed to determine what early risk factors may confer risk to children’s chronic pain onset and maintenance.

To-date, only a few population-based prospective studies have examined whether or which early life painful experiences and mental health symptoms may contribute to the development of pediatric chronic pain [18, 19]. Using multi-informant (mothers, children) data from an existing Canadian longitudinal pregnancy cohort, we aimed to address this gap. Specifically, we examined the associations between maternal reported early child developmental risk factors (i.e., early pain experiences and mental health symptoms) and the later onset of pediatric chronic pain at age 8 and 11 years, as reported by mothers and children, respectively. Given the associations between mental health symptoms and pediatric chronic pain [13], as well as potential contributions of early painful experiences to chronic pain onset [11, 12], we hypothesized that higher levels of internalizing and externalizing mental health symptoms, as well as higher rates of early painful experiences (e.g., repeated injuries, hospitalizations) would be associated with higher risks of chronic pain at age 8 and 11.

Methods

Study design and participants

The All Our Families (AOF) cohort is a well-characterized ongoing longitudinal pregnancy cohort that began in May 2008 with 3,387 pregnant women in a large Western Canadian urban centre. Community-based sampling was used to recruit pregnant women at primary care clinics and through routine blood screening at the sole provider of laboratory services in the region; the study information was also available via posters to allow for passive recruitment. Recruitment and cohort baseline characteristics have been previously reported [20, 21]. Data were collected using online surveys at multiple time points between 2008 and 2022. Mothers completed questionnaires twice during pregnancy and 7 times since their child’s birth (i.e., 4 months, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, and 11 years). Children completed self-report questionnaires at the age of 11 years. Eligible participants for this study included mothers who were recruited into the AOF cohort and were not lost to follow-up/discontinued their participation, as well as their children who provided assent to participate in the study. The institutional research ethics board approved the study (REB13-0868). Participants provided consent/assent using an online survey form.

Procedures and measures

Available data from 4 months, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, and 11 years timepoints were used. The data were collected between May 2008 and January 2022. Participants were contacted to complete each timepoint’s surveys based on their child’s birthdate.

Outcomes

Chronic pain at age 8

Children were categorized as having chronic pain at age 8 if their mothers reported their child having pain for ≥ 3 months at any point since Grade 1, and/or often or always complaining of pain, having headaches, and/or stomach problems. Using a conservative approach, a mother was required to have provided a negative (“No”) response to all four items to confirm the absence chronic pain. An affirmative response to any of the four items indicated the presence of chronic pain. Mothers who did not respond to all four items of the composite variable were excluded from analysis. Data on the absence or presence of chronic pain at age 8 years were available for 1,583 participants (out of 2,074 mothers who completed at least a portion of the study’s questionnaires at that timepoint) and included in the analyses (Fig. 1). Chronic pain at age 11

We used youth reports of pain frequency and pain interference to determine the presence of chronic pain. Youth self-reported the frequency of pain using an item from the Pain Questionnaire [22]. Youth were prompted to report pain frequency regardless of maternal reports of children’s pain at age 8. In line with previous research [23], frequency of pain at age 11 was dichotomized into those with moderate to high frequency (weekly, almost every day, every day) and low to none (none, once per month, 2–3 times per month). A total of 1,019 youth reported pain frequency at age 11 (out of 1,034 youth who completed at least a part of the study questionnaires). All youth who reported pain frequency of at least once per month (n = 818) were prompted to report their pain interference using four of the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS)-25 Pediatric Profile items [24]. The PROMIS-25 instrument assesses children’s physical and socio-emotional functioning; only items assessing pain interference were used. The four items assess how much pain interfered with daily activities (e.g., sleeping, walking, running, paying attention). The raw scores were converted into t-scores. Based on a population-based pediatric sample, t-scores between 50 and 58 indicate moderate levels of pain interference, whereas t-scores ≥ 59 suggest severe pain interference levels[25]. The PROMIS instruments have demonstrated excellent psychometric qualities and have been previously used in research on pediatric chronic pain [24]. A total of 813 youth reported pain interference at age 11 years (Fig. 1). Additionally, youth reported their pain location, pain intensity (0, no pain-10, worst pain possible numerical rating scale), and whether their pain was present for 3 months or longer. Past population-based studies (e.g., [26, 27]) used a similar approach of assessing the presence of chronic pain, that is a combination of questions regarding pain chronicity, intensity, location, and frequency. To our knowledge, this is the first population-based study to also assess pain interference.

Predictors

Early life painful experiences

At each timepoint, mothers reported on a variety of potentially painful childhood experiences (e.g., neonatal intensive care stay, surgeries, hospitalizations, vaccinations, medical procedures, gut problems). A list of 86 potentially painful experiences was compiled and reviewed by pediatric pain researchers (KAB, MN, SLO, MP) (see Table S1, Supplementary Materials). Through expert opinion supported by existing research, the research team reduced the list to 70 variables representing experiences that are likely painful as supported by previous research [27–34]. The variables were subsequently grouped according to the type of painful experience (see Table S1, Supplementary Materials). To further reduce the number of potential predictors, each variable group was coded as a single variable (e.g., a reported hospitalization at any point between 4 months and 8 years resulted in a “1 = yes” hospitalization variable). To reflect a repeated nature of multiple vaccinations, a total number of vaccine injections was recorded and used in the analyses.

Children’s mental health

At the 5- and 8-year timepoints, mothers also reported children’s mental health symptoms (i.e., depression and anxiety symptoms, behavioural problems) using parent rating scales of the Behavioural Assessment System for Children—Second Edition (BASC-2) [35]. The BASC-2 was normed using general and clinical samples and demonstrated high levels of reliability and validity [35]. The BASC-2 t-scores normed in samples of both boys and girls were used for analyses. T-scores ≥ 60 were considered elevated and indicative of clinically significant levels of depressive/anxiety symptoms and behavioural problems.

Sociodemographic variables

Mothers reported their age, education, race/ethnicity, and household income, as well as their child’s gestational age at birth and sex.

Statistical analyses

Continuous demographic and early life pain experience variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and presented as median (interquartile range). Binary variables were presented as count (percentage). The BASC-2 subscale t-scores were dichotomized using clinically significant elevations (i.e., t-scores ≥ 60) as a cut-off point [35]. Based on clinical importance and literature review, up to 24 variables were considered for multivariable regression models of chronic pain at age 8 and pain frequency and pain interference at age 11 (discussed above and presented in Supplementary Materials). Variables were omitted from model inclusion consideration if they presented with > 40% missingness [36] or near-zero variance [37]. Hospitalizations and ear tube insertion variables were omitted due to missingness; painful dental procedures and neonatal intensive care variables were omitted due to near-zero variance. Continuous predictors (i.e., number of vaccinations, maternal age, gestational age) were mean centered prior to model development.

We used adaptive least absolute shrinkage and selection operation (LASSO) regression for final variable selection. Adaptive LASSO’s oracle property assigns specific weights to each coefficient based on the correlation structure of the data, thus allowing for the identification of the correct nonzero coefficient variables [43]. Weights were derived using initial binomial (chronic pain age 8, pain frequency age 11) and gaussian (pain interference age 11) ridge regressions with tenfold cross-validation. The weights represented the absolute reciprocal values of the ridge regression coefficients and were subsequently transferred to LASSO regression and incorporated penalty terms. All included variables were subjected to penalization. LASSO regressions (binomial or gaussian) were completed for each outcome using tenfold cross-validation. We selected the tuning parameter (lambda) that minimized the cross-validation prediction error. Non-zero coefficients found via LASSO were transferred to unrestricted multivariable logistic (chronic pain age 8, pain frequency age 11) or multivariable linear regression (pain interference age 11) models for final presentation of adjusted associations between predictors and pain outcomes at ages 8 and 11. Adjusted associations were presented as either odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) and p values for binary outcomes, or coefficient with 95% CI and p values for pain interference. In line with Althouse’s [38] and Rothman’s [39] recommendations for analysing large datasets with multiple variables, p values were not adjusted.

Imputed data models of each outcome (i.e., chronic pain at age 8, pain frequency at age 11, pain interference at age 11) were generated for the analyses. Little’s test was initially run to determine if data was missing completely at random (p < 0.05). We imputed up to 58 models of the data set (number of imputations were set to the percentage of incomplete cases) with 20 iterations using multiple imputed chained equations (MICE) with predictive mean matching and logistic regression. Specific to each outcome, variables with > 40% missingness were not considered for imputation [36]. Following imputation, pooled multivariable logistic and linear regression models were constructed using the same variables as our complete case adaptive LASSO models. We chose to present our imputed associations for our primary analyses as the use of multiple imputation, versus listwise deletion, has been argued to produce less biased results [40]. Presented OR and coefficients, 95% CI, and p values represent the pooled estimates from individual models completed on each of the imputed datasets. Analyses were conducted using SPSS 25.0 and R Studio version 2022.2.3.492 with R statistical software version 4.2.1 using packages glmnet (version 4.1–7) [41] and mice (version 3.15.0) [42] for adaptive LASSO and MICE, respectively.

Chronic pain at age 8 years

Data on chronic pain was available for 1,583 children, and 18% (n = 288) of children were reported by their mothers to have chronic pain at the age of 8 years. Among those experiencing pain at 8 years, most children had stomach (47%) and leg (22%) pain, with most (72%) experiencing pain multiple times per week.

Imputed case analysis demonstrated that children with gut problems (adjusted [a]OR = 3.19 [2.36 to 4.31], p < 0.001) at the age of 5 years or younger were more likely to develop chronic pain by the age of 8 years (Table 2). Children who were reported to have headaches sometimes (aOR = 1.65 [1.14 to 2.41], p = 0.009) or often/always (aOR = 5.42 [1.93 to 15.20], p = 0.001) and complain of pain often/always (aOR = 2.59 [1.15 to 5.83], p = 0.022) at age of 5 years were more likely to develop chronic pain by the age of 8 years, as compared to children who were reported to never have headaches or never complain of pain. Children who visited an emergency room at least once before the age of 8 years were more likely to develop chronic pain at the age of 8 years (aOR = 1.99 [1.31 to 3.02], p = 0.001), as compared to children who never visited an emergency room. Boys were less likely to develop chronic pain at the age of 8 years, as compared to girls (adjusted OR = 0.75 [0.56 to 0.99], p = 0.043). Chronic pain, pain frequency, and pain interference at age 11

At the age of 11 years, 1,019 children reported their pain frequency. Eighty percent of children (n = 818) self-reported experiencing pain and aches at least once a month, with 31% of children reporting having weekly or daily pain. Thirty-nine percent of children reported having chronic pain (i.e., pain lasting ≥ 3 months). Of these, children reported having muscle and joint pain (24%), headaches (22%), leg pain (21%), and abdominal pain (18%). Children, who endorsed having pain at least once per month, reported an average pain intensity level of 4.05/10 (SD = 1.65) and an average pain interference score of 48.94/74.00 (t-score; SD = 8.36), which is indicative of normal pain interference (i.e., when a child experiences pain, it does not stop them from participating in daily activities for a long period of time) Ten percent of youth (n = 81) reported severe levels of pain interference (i.e., t-scores ≥ 59), and 34% of youth endorsed moderate levels of pain interference (i.e., t-scores between 50 and 58).

Out of the 818 youth who reported having pain at least once per month, 689 youth had mothers who reported their child’s pain at age 8. Of the 689 youth with data available at both ages 8 and 11, 129 were reported chronic pain at age 8 and self-report having pain at least once per month at age 11. 560 youth reported having pain at age 11 at least once per month, while their mothers did not report them having chronic pain at age 8.

Chronic pain at the age of 8 years was associated with 1.91 increased odds of experiencing frequent pain at the age of 11 (aOR = 1.91 [1.30 to 2.81], p = 0.001; Table 3). Chronic pain at the age of 8 years was also associated with 2.58-point increase in the level of pain interference at age 11 (2.58 [0.95 to 4.20], p = 0.002) (Table 4). Boys, as compared to girls, reported lower levels of pain interference (-1.87 [-3.02 to -0.72], p = 0.001). Complete case analyses revealed a different set of significant associations. Specifically, the complete case analyses showed that children with a gut problem at age 8 or earlier in life (aOR = 1.57 [1.01 to 2.46], p = 0.047), children who complained of pain sometimes (aOR = 1.60 [1.03 to 2.47], p = 0.035; as compared to children who did not complain of pain), and children with clinically elevated depressive symptoms (aOR = 2.05 [1.16 to 3.64], p = 0.014) reported frequent pain at age 11 (Table 3). Children who complained of pain sometimes at the age of 5 or 8 years, as compared to children who did not complain of pain, also reported higher levels of pain interference, (1.85 [0.11 to 3.58], p = 0.037) (Table 4). Higher gestational age was associated with 0.78-point decrease in the level of pain interference at age 11 (-0.78 [-1.28 to -0.28], p = 0.002).

Discussion

At age 8, approximately 18% of children in our population-based sample experienced chronic pain (pain lasting for 3 months or longer), as reported by mothers. At age 11, 39% of children self-reported chronic pain. This study extends previous research and aligns with patient-identified research priorities [7] by examining associations between early life (from birth to age 8) painful experiences, as well as children’s mental health symptoms, and chronic pain in late childhood and emerging adolescence. Results of the imputed case analyses are discussed below. We found that recurrent gut problems, headaches, pain complaints, emergency room visits, as well as being born female, were all uniquely associated with increased risks of child chronic pain at the age of 8 years. Children’s mental health (i.e., anxiety and depressive symptoms, behavioural problems) and other types of painful experiences (e.g., ear infections) were not associated with chronic pain at age 8. Moreover, chronic pain at the age of 8 years was associated with frequent self-reported pain and higher levels of pain interference at the age of 11. Boys reported lower levels of pain interference than girls. Other predictors (i.e. gut problems, serious injuries, anxiety and depressive symptoms, pain complaints, child sex, and gestational age) were not associated with pain interference or frequency at age 11.

Gut problems (e.g., functional abdominal pain, constipation) are often painful [34] and are common in children with prevalence increasing from early childhood (4–7% at age 2–3) up to 11% at age of 6 [43]. Recurrent abdominal pain at age of 3 has been associated with pediatric headaches and musculoskeletal pain, as well as higher levels of emotional problems (i.e., anxiety, depressive, and somatic symptoms), later in childhood (i.e., at the age of 3.5 and 6) [43]. Genetic factors, neonatal painful experiences, and psychological factors (e.g., stress, exposure to abuse) are thought to contribute to gut pain in childhood [44]; these are similar factors that are hypothesized to contribute to the onset of pediatric chronic pain [8]. Thus, gut problems and later-onset chronic pain may share similar etiological factors, with gut problems manifesting earlier in life. At the age of 8 years, gut problems may transform into recurrent abdominal pain or serve as a precursor of other types of pain (e.g., headaches, musculoskeletal pain). Central nervous system sensitization is present in children with recurrent abdominal pain and may contribute to the onset of recurrent headaches and musculoskeletal pain [45].

Injuries, which are usually associated with pain, are the second most common reason for pediatric emergency room visits [46]. Despite chronic pain often arising in the absence of injuries [1], a painful injury much earlier in life may serve as the starting point for some types of chronic pain (e.g., post-traumatic brain injury [TBI] headaches). Indeed, over a half of children who suffered a TBI reported persistent pain in multiple body locations, in addition to headaches, 3 years after the initial TBI [47]. Further, injuries may be associated with acute stress and post-traumatic stress symptoms (PTSS) [48]. PTSS often co-occur with and maintain pediatric chronic pain [6, 49]. Some studies demonstrated bidirectional relationship between persistent post-injury pain and PTSS [50]. Other studies, however, showed that baseline PTSS predicted pain levels 3 and 6 months after the initial injury [51]. Thus, it is possible that if most of the emergency room visits in the present study were related to injuries, children could have experienced traumatic stress, which, in turn, may have created psychological vulnerability for the onset of chronic pain. Alternatively, emergency room visits could have been related to recurrent pain concerns (e.g., headaches, stomach aches). However, the present study did not ask mothers to report the reason for the emergency department visits. Therefore, it would be premature to associate emergency room visits with later onset of chronic pain.

Pain predicted pain, such that headaches and frequent pain complaints at the age of 5 years were associated with reports of chronic pain at the age of 8 years. In turn, reports of chronic pain at the age of 8 predicted frequent pain and higher levels of pain interference at the age of 11 years. If pain is unaddressed and unmanaged, pain intensity and duration may contribute to chronic pain later in childhood and adolescent years [52]. For example, in a population-based prospective study, abdominal pain and pain in other locations (e.g., musculoskeletal pain, headache) at the age of 12 years predicted the recurrent abdominal pain at the age of 14 years [53]. In another epidemiological study, recurrent headaches and/or abdominal pain persisting throughout childhood and adolescence (i.e., at ages 7, 11, and 15) predicted physical symptoms (e.g., headache, back pain, abdominal pain) in adulthood (i.e., at the ages of 33 and 36 years) [54, 55].

The results of the study are in line with the identified sex differences in pediatric chronic pain with more girls, versus boys, reporting chronic pain [56]. Sex differences become more pronounced during puberty [1], and co-occurring anxiety has been associated with higher levels of pain in girls [56]. The differences are likely due to dissimilar pain mechanisms in males and females and the influence of sex hormones on pain signalling mechanisms [57].

The imputed case analyses did not reveal any significant associations between internalizing mental health symptoms at the age of 5 years and chronic pain later in childhood and in early adolescence. Anxiety, depression, somatic, and post-traumatic symptoms often co-occur with pediatric chronic pain likely due to shared neurobiological (e.g., inflammation, genetic influences) and psychological (e.g., avoidance, catastrophizing) vulnerabilities [5]. Externalizing mental health symptoms (e.g., aggression, impulsivity) are comorbid with chronic pain in children and intensify the impact internalizing mental health symptoms on chronic pain [13]. However, the temporality of the symptoms’ onset is debated [58], which is likely due to paucity of rigorous longitudinal studies. The lack of significant associations between internalizing and externalizing symptoms and the later development of chronic pain in the present sample is intriguing and requires future investigations, particularly given that chronic pain predicted the onset and severity of suicidality in youth [14]. The lack of association may also be the result of dichotomizing mental health variables into the categories of below and above clinically elevated symptoms, which reduced the variables’ variance.

Our study identified some of the early life factors that are associated with the development of chronic pain in childhood and adolescence (i.e., sex, gut problems, ER visits, persistent pain complaints). These factors may serve as potential targets for early screening and preventive interventions, that can be implemented within primary care systems, where early pain symptoms are first seen and continuously managed. Salamon and Cullinan highlighted the need for early screening of children to identify those at risk of developing chronic pain, tracking pain-related visits, and integrating secondary prevention interventions (e.g., brief targeted cognitive-behavioural strategies delivered individually or in a group format) within primary care settings [59].

Of note, our study demonstrated that many common childhood painful experiences (e.g., vaccines, ear infections) were not associated with development of chronic pain later in life. One possible explanation could stem from continuing progress made in pediatric pain assessment and management locally where the cohort was recruited [60]. Several variables (i.e., admission to neonatal intensive care unit, hospitalizations, painful dental procedures) were excluded from the analyses due to missingness or near-zero variance. Some of these variables (e.g., neonatal intensive care) have been associated with altered sensory processing [61] and have a potential to contribute to later onset of chronic pain. Further, social determinants of health variables included in the models (e.g., ethnicity, income) also were not associated with the onset of chronic pain later in childhood/adolescence. This is in contrast to existing findings demonstrating associations between socioeconomic variables (e.g., lower socioeconomic status, lower levels of maternal education, and racialized identity of children) and increased prevalence of chronic pain and higher levels of pain-related disability [62, 63]. Future examination of these variables in population-based samples is warranted.

Given our choice to use the adaptive LASSO method requiring complete case data for all considered variables, our available sample size represents those that provided responses to our outcomes of interest (i.e., pain outcomes). The degree of missingness in predictor variables (i.e., painful experiences, mental health symptoms) reduced the number of complete cases available for adaptive LASSO by 52% to 57%. To compensate for this, we used multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) to impute missing data within model datasets. We chose to focus our interpretations on presented imputed effect sizes, as opposed to complete case analyses. Caution in this approach is advised, however. The use of MICE imputation assumes that data is missing at random. However, to confirm that data is missing at random, as opposed to missing not at random, requires access to the very data that is missing within the dataset. Indeed, it is possible that failure to reply to our questionnaires may be due to systematic reasons (e.g., participants with worse outcomes were less likely to respond). If such a mechanism is associated with our cohort, one must be cautious in their interpretation of imputed effect sizes. However, it has been argued that even under a missing not at random assumption, multiple imputation reports less biased results to complete case analysis [40].

The study had limitations that should be addressed in future research. First, although chronic pain was assessed by self-report at age 11, only maternal report based on four items was obtained at the age of 8. Of the four items, only one of those items directly asked about the presence of chronic pain; the other items focused on the presence of frequent pain complaints. Compared to existing cohort studies (e.g., 54, 55), pain assessment used in the AOF cohort was more detailed (particularly at the age of 11) and in line with the International Association for the Study of Pain and the International Classification of Diseases definitions of chronic pain [64]. However, given the high prevalence [1] and associated cost of pediatric chronic pain [2], future cohort studies should incorporate a more nuanced self- and parent-report assessment of pain symptoms starting with earlier ages. Additionally, only maternal reports of children’s internalizing and externalizing symptoms were collected up to age 8. Given the discrepancies between parental and self-reports of mental health symptoms [65], it is possible children’s early-life internalizing and externalizing symptoms were underreported. Future cohort studies should include children’s self-report of their mental health and pain symptoms at earlier points (e.g., late childhood). Second, the present study reports on youth’s chronic pain at age 8 and 11, whereas chronic pain tends to peak later in adolescence [1]. It is possible that there are additional risk factors contributing to the development of chronic pain later in adolescence. Future cohort investigations should include pain reports later in adolescence. Of note, 39% of youth reported having pain present for 3 months or longer, which is line with other epidemiological reports [1, 23]. Further, compared to existing population-based studies, the present investigation used an expanded pain assessment that included a pain interference measure. Ten percent of youth reported severe levels of pain interference, which is in line with previous studies [66]. Third, the study focused on some of the individual predictors of chronic pain (e.g., sex, internalizing symptoms). Other individual predictors that may be contributing to chronic pain onset were not assessed in the present study. For example, early life adverse events (e.g., childhood maltreatment) have been linked to poor physical outcomes, including frequent pain [67, 68]. Future cohort studies should consider how adverse childhood events may contribute to the onset of pediatric chronic pain [69]. Beyond individual predictors, biopsychosocial models of pediatric chronic pain [8] highlight family and social factors (e.g., parent chronic pain and mental health) that contribute to the onset and maintenance of chronic pain [70–72]. Future studies should examine the contributions of parental factors in early childhood to the development of pediatric chronic pain. Additionally, the study focused on factors that predict chronic pain later in childhood/adolescence. Protective factors that could buffer against the onset of chronic pain (e.g., parenting style, social support) should be examined in future studies. Finally, compared to provincial and national statistics, women who chose to participate in the AOF study were older and reported higher income [20]. Further, mothers that continued their study participation tended to be White and reported higher education and income levels, as compared to those who discontinued their participation [20]. Therefore, it is possible that the identified associations may not generalize to more diverse samples.

Conclusions

Pediatric chronic pain remains prevalent, costly, and debilitating for a considerable portion of youth. Prevention remains one of the key research objectives on the pediatric pain research and clinical agenda. Using a multi-informant prospective cohort data, our study identified potential factors (i.e., female sex, gut problems, persistent pain complaints, ER visits) that may increase the risk of chronic pain development in late childhood and early adolescence.
