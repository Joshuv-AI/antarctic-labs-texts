Lessons From the Past
Authors: Matthew J Budoff
Published: 2026

This article by Ozdemir et al,1 although of clinical interest, sadly ignores decades of work done with coronary artery calcium and angiography. This study, despite its claims, is neither the first nor the largest to evaluate coronary artery calcification (CAC) and angiography. This work was largely conducted in the 1990s when CAC was first introduced as a diagnostic test, dozens of similar studies with CAC and angiography were reported, and large multicenter studies were published demonstrating the sex-specific sensitivities and specificities of CAC for obstructive disease.2,3 As for larger cohorts, Knez et al4 studied more than 2115 symptomatic persons with both CAC and invasive angiography, and Budoff et al5 reported on 1851 persons with invasive angiography and CAC. These studies, conducted 2 decades earlier, determined that CAC scanning provides incremental and independent power in predicting the severity and extent of angiographically significant coronary artery disease (CAD) in symptomatic patients over risk factors and clinical variables, demonstrating sex-specific sensitivity, specificity, and post-test probabilities for obstructive CAD. Guideline recommendations for the use of CAC to establish the presence of obstructive disease go back to 2006.3

The current paper nicely replicates work performed by Rumberger et al3 in 1997, where they found a CAC of 711 was associated with a 90% specificity, but only a 42% sensitivity. The optimal CAC for a sensitivity of >70% and specificity of >70% was 123 for that cohort.

The issue with CAC scores is that if you maximize specificity, similar to virtually all testing, you compromise sensitivity, and higher CAC score cut points will lead to many missed cases of obstructive CAD. Thus, the main utility of CAC has always been to maximize sensitivity, then do a more specific test (ie, computer tomography angiography) to improve specificity. With a high sensitivity, you can rule out disease with great confidence and eliminate that patient from the need for further evaluation. CAC acts as an excellent screening tool, and thus, it can be used earlier in the atherosclerotic process, in which 0 scores or low scores can be deferred, and high scores can be referred for further testing.

Most importantly, and it cannot be stressed enough, is that these cut points for obstructive disease only work in symptomatic persons, and if applied to asymptomatic persons, they will lead to many unnecessary angiograms that do not show obstructive disease. The current study was performed in patients who underwent angiography, an incredible referral bias. Post-test probability only works in conjunction with pretest probability, and it has been shown numerous times already that in asymptomatic persons, even with very high CAC scores (>1000), there is a low prevalence of obstructive disease (20%-40%).2
