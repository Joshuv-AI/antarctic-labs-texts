Unveiling the relationship between aesthetic experiences and attention through a cross-experiment validation of their processing biomarkers
Authors: Paolo Barbieri, Martina Berto, Pietro Sarasso, Jacopo Frascaroli, Giacomo Handjaras, Francesca Piovesan, Giorgio Gnecco, Davide Bottari, Irene Ronga, Stephen Fleming
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA centuries-old tradition encompassing philosophy, psychology, and artistic practice describes aesthetic experiences as characterized by a special state of heightened attention toward external stimuli (i.e. an “aesthetic attitude”). In recent years, this view has motivated wide-ranging claims about the nature of our aesthetic encounters and the cognitive benefits of exposure to art. Despite sustained efforts from a growing stream of interdisciplinary research, however, it is still unclear whether aesthetic experiences can be systematically linked to observable attentional enhancements. In this study, we address this long-standing question using electroencephalography (EEG) and advanced machine learning (ML) techniques. We performed a series of EEG experiments measuring brain activity elicited by synthetic and natural images during an aesthetic (beauty judgments) and a pragmatic (symmetry judgments) task. Visual evoked potentials and neural oscillations were used to assess whether the aesthetic task induces attentional enhancements. In line with our hypotheses, the power of alpha and beta prestimulus oscillations significantly decreased in the aesthetic vs. pragmatic task. Furthermore, larger late positive potentials and N170 responses (the latter for natural images only) were found in the aesthetic vs. pragmatic task. ML analyses further showed that prestimulus neural oscillations and N170 responses were systematically able to predict the type of task. Overall, our results highlight the presence of a perceptual processing enhancement and a heightened state of attention in aesthetic contexts. The upshot is a clearer understanding of the dynamics and neural underpinnings of our aesthetic experiences.Keywords: neuroaesthetics, aesthetic attitude, attention, event-related potential (ERP), machine learning

Our study addresses a question that has haunted scholars for centuries: Do experiences of beauty make us more focused on the world around us? By looking at the neural correlates of attention in an aesthetic vs. a pragmatic task (judging the beauty vs. the symmetry of visual stimuli), we detected neural indices of amplified attention in the former. Through machine learning, we demonstrated that the tasks were distinguishable based on electroencephalography features, corroborating our results. By revealing a systematic attentional enhancement in aesthetic tasks, our findings may have a transformative impact on all contexts in which individuals could benefit from better employment of their attentional resources, such as learning, rehabilitation, psychotherapy, and the promotion of adaptation in times of distress.

Introduction

A centuries-old tradition encompassing philosophy, psychology, and artistic practice describes our experiences of beauty in art and nature as characterized by a special state of heightened attention toward external stimuli (i.e. an “aesthetic attitude,” as some have called it) (1). In philosophy, this idea has been common currency at least since Kant, who describes judgments of taste as involving the careful engagement with the formal features of an object, with the sole purpose of prolonging its contemplation (2–4). In line with this view, a recent strand of research suggests that aesthetic experiences demand high levels of attentional and working memory capacities, providing further support for the link between aesthetic appreciation and a heightened deployment of our attentional resources (5, 6). Previous neuroimaging studies have also indicated that aesthetic appreciation often corresponds to an increased activity along the neural perceptual hierarchy (7–10) similar to that underlying exogenous and endogenous attentional modulations (9–11). Other neurophysiological studies found a magnification of well-established indices of attentional amplification (i.e. the alpha event-related desynchronization—alpha ERD—and the early waves of the visual evoked potentials—VEPs) (11, 12) in subjects contemplating more appreciated abstract images (7).

So far, however, the attentional enhancement found in response to aesthetically appreciated stimuli has mainly been regarded as a bottom-up, transient effect produced by the features of the stimulus. There are, however, reasons to think that the same attentional enhancement can be prompted by top-down, task-related modulations (13). In other words, the anticipation of having an aesthetic experience might have the same effects on our attention as having an aesthetic experience itself. Consistent with this latter hypothesis, in a series of functional MRI (fMRI) studies, greater activations in visual areas were found when the same pictures were presented as artworks than when they were not (13). Moreover, a series of electroencephalography (EEG) studies (14–16) ) found a difference in the late components of the ERP when participants were asked to make aesthetic (beautiful/not-beautiful) as opposed to descriptive (symmetric/not-symmetric) judgments about a set of geometric shapes, suggesting that there might be a functional dissociation of the neural processes involved in the two kinds of judgments (see also (17) for an fMRI study pointing in a similar direction). Differences in EEG responses (coinciding with the latency of the late positive potential—LPP) were also found in a study where the subjects were asked to evaluate their liking as opposed to the correctness of the last chord of a sequence (18). Importantly, however, while there are several studies examining whether aesthetic judgments might be underpinned by specific neural processes distinct from those involved in descriptive judgments, experimental studies designed to test whether aesthetic/evaluative tasks can systematically induce observable attentional enhancements are still lacking.

The present research fills this gap by comparing different electrophysiological indices of attention, both prestimulus and poststimulus and unfolding at different timescales, during an aesthetic vs. a pragmatic evaluation task (Fig. 1). In experiment 1, we asked participants to evaluate either the beauty (aesthetic task) or the symmetry (pragmatic task) of 180 abstract visual stimuli (10) containing different profiles of spectral frequency. In experiment 2, the same participants evaluated the beauty or symmetry of 180 photographs of natural landscapes. Using synthetic and natural images, we aimed to test the generalizability of findings across different stimuli. During the task, we registered the EEG signal to compute prestimulus and poststimulus attention-related indices. More specifically, we analyzed prestimulus alpha oscillations, poststimulus alpha ERD, and the early components of the VEP (expressed in the first 200 ms following stimulus onset) to assess the presence of endogenous attentional processes associated with the aesthetic evaluation.

Experimental questions and summary of the main results.

In this respect, we made two hypotheses (Fig. 2B and Table 1). If we could induce an “aesthetic attitude” (i.e. a top-down, tonic attentional enhancement during the aesthetic task as compared with the pragmatic task), we expected to observe in both experiments decreased prestimulus inhibitory alpha power and increased poststimulus alpha ERD and VEP early components in the aesthetic task compared with the pragmatic task (hypothesis 1). Furthermore, to validate and generalize our findings, we used all relevant electrophysiological measures obtained in experiment 1 (the one employing synthetic images) as biomarkers to classify whether participants were judging the beauty or the symmetry of natural images in experiment 2. We hypothesized (hypothesis 2) that the task (aesthetic vs. pragmatic) performed on both natural and synthetic images could be distinguished based on electrophysiological signals with both high sensitivity and specificity.

Methods

Ethics information, sampling plan, and pilot data

Thirty-six subjects with normal or corrected-to-normal vision participated in the study. Participants performed both experiments 1 and 2 in two separate sessions (aesthetic and pragmatic) and in a randomized order, counterbalanced across participants. All participants gave their written informed consent to participate in the study, which complied with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki (2006) and was approved by the university ethics committee (Prot. n. 0433380 dated 21/07/2023; University of Turin, Italy). The number of participants was estimated via simulations. We used the procedure described in Wang et al. (19) to simulate a dataset with two conditions (aesthetic and pragmatic) for VEP data, as this is known to provide a good signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). First, based on the effects observed on the five subjects of a pilot study identical to experiment 1, we selected 32 electrodes of interest at posterior locations (see SI Section S2.1). For the simulation, we chose a time window within the first 0.2 s of the VEPs, as it comprised both C1 and N170 waves (10). The amplitude values at the electrodes of interest for the two conditions were sampled from a bivariate normal distribution (within-subject design) where mean and SD were chosen based on the results of the five participants who performed the pilot study (mean voltage aesthetic = 0.87; mean voltage pragmatic = 1.41; SD aesthetic = 0.79; SD pragmatic = 0.94). As hypothesized, participants collected in the pilot study showed a significant magnification of the early and middle-latency components of the VEP response (including C1 and N170 components) during the aesthetic compared with the pragmatic evaluation task. We then ran a cluster-based permutation on simulated datasets to test whether any statistical cluster (t-values) exhibited a significant difference between the two conditions with an alpha level of 0.05 (see SI Section S2.2 for more details). The simulation results showed that, to obtain power above 0.80, a sample size of n = 36 was required. The original algorithm used to perform such analyses can be downloaded from this link: https://osf.io/rmqhc/files/osfstorage.

If, during data collection, technical problems occurred or registration was interrupted for any reason, we planned to discard the affected participants, and replace them with others to meet the required sample size (n = 36). Furthermore, an SNR analysis was performed to discard noisy data. Specifically, we planned to exclude a participant's dataset if >30% of the epochs in one condition were marked for rejection (see the “Analysis plan” section for details on the procedure). Details on EEG data analysis (including all information on artifact rejection) are included in the “Analysis plan” section and Supplementary material. During the pilot phase, no participant was discarded.

Stimuli

In experiment 1, we employed 180 2D black-and-white random noise images, with different profiles of spectral frequency, created with the IDL software (Harris Geospatial Inc., USA). All stimuli were generated with the same resolution (300 dpi), dimensions (960 × 540), luminance, and contrast (see also SI Section S1.2). In Fig. 2A, we show some representative examples of these stimuli.

In experiment 2, we employed 180 2D black-and-white photographs of natural landscapes (e.g. mountains and seashores). We chose images of natural landscapes for their ecological validity and because, with this kind of stimuli, judgments of symmetry and aesthetic appreciation seem to diverge more than with other stimulus categories (20). We included a total of 180 stimuli homogenized for color spectrum (black and white), dimensions, resolution, contrast, and light reflection (see SI Section S1.2). Since some studies have shown that curved visual features generate a different response in the visual cortex compared with rectilinear stimuli (21) (something relevant to our examination of VEPs), stimuli in both sets were controlled for curvilinearity with the MLV Toolbox (22) (see Supplementary material for more details).

In Fig. 2A, we show five examples (two from experiment 1 and three from experiment 2) of representative stimuli from the adjusted image set. Stimuli are available on OSF (https://osf.io/uzh8r/).

Experimental procedures

The setup and the procedures were identical for experiments 1 and 2 and are represented in Fig. 2B (please refer to SI Sections S1.1 and S1.3 for further details on the apparatus). Participants sat at a table in a fixed position and were asked to look at and evaluate the abstract images (90 images, experiment 1) or photographs (90 images, experiment 2) presented on the screen while EEG data were recorded. Participants were presented with two different blocks (40 min in total) for aesthetic and pragmatic evaluation, and both the order of presentation of the experiment (synthetic and natural images) and the task (aesthetic and pragmatic evaluation) were randomized across participants. Visual stimuli were presented in a random order (stimulus duration 500 ms (23)), preceded by a cue (cue duration 500 ms (23)), preparing for the required evaluation. The experiment included a cue-stimulus delay period of 1 s (23). A second delay period of 2 s followed the stimulus (Fig. 2B). A question mark then appeared on the blank screen, indicating to participants that they should evaluate the beauty (during the aesthetic evaluation) or the symmetry (during the pragmatic evaluation) of the presented images on a 1 to 9 Likert scale (10, 24) by pressing the corresponding numerical key on the computer keyboard. The evaluation scale ranged from 1 (“I completely dislike it” or “it was completely asymmetric”) to 9 (“I like it very much” or “it was very symmetric”). As soon as they responded, the next trial started.

Analysis plan

Electrophysiological recordings and preprocessing

EEG activity was recorded using 64 Ag–AgCl electrodes placed on the scalp of the participant according to the international 10–20 system and online referenced to FPz. Electrode impedances were kept below 5 kΩ. The electrooculogram was recorded from two surface electrodes placed over the lower eyelid and lateral to the outer canthus of the right eye. Signals were recorded and digitized by using a 64-channel EEG system BrainAmp DC (Brain Products Italia srl, Putignano, Italy) with a sampling rate of 1,000 Hz.

Processing pipeline and analysis strategy

The analysis was performed with a previously validated preprocessing pipeline (25, 26). Raw EEG data were preprocessed with EEGLAB (27) (see SI Section S3.1). To detect stereotypical artifacts (eye movements, blinks, and heartbeat), we ran independent component analysis (ICA) (28) based on the extended Infomax (29, 30). Before computing ICA, data were low-pass filtered with a cutoff frequency of 40 Hz (windowed-sinc FIR filter, filter order 50), downsampled to 250 Hz, and high-pass filtered at 1 Hz (windowed-sinc FIR filter, filter order 500) (31). ICA weights were then attributed to the original raw EEG (continuous and unfiltered signal with original sampling rate). The ICA procedure is fully described in the Supplementary material (SI Section S3.2). The cleaned signal was low-pass filtered at 40 Hz (windowed-sinc FIR filter, filter order 50), and downsampled to 250 Hz to further reduce computational load. A high pass filter of 0.1 Hz (windowed-sinc FIR filter, filter order 5,000) was applied to remove DC offset and slow drifts. In order to attenuate the 50 Hz line noise artifact, we used the Zapline-plus plugin (32, 33). Finally, noisy channels were interpolated using an automatic custom algorithm by Lalor et al. (34) and Diliberto (2015; https://cnsp-workshop.github.io/website). Bad channels were rejected via spherical interpolation based on high- or low-threshold criteria; that is, a channel was marked for rejection when its SD was higher than three or lower than six times the mean SD across all channels.

The preprocessed EEG data were then divided into epochs of 3.5 s (total duration), including a 1-s precue interval, a 500-ms cue presentation, a 1-s cue-stimulus interval, and a 1-s poststimulus interval. Epochs with a joint probability of activity greater than three SDs were automatically rejected. If for one experiment more than 30% of epochs were rejected, the data for that participant were excluded from further analysis.

Subsequently, electrophysiological data were analyzed using the FieldTrip toolbox (http://fieldtriptoolbox.org) (35) for Matlab (Mathworks, Inc., USA).

Two analyses were performed on epoched data: (i) a time–frequency decomposition and (ii) a calculation of the VEPs. VEPs were generated by averaging at the individual level the activity across trials and applying a baseline correction (−100 to 0 ms). To avoid biasing classification results, the VEPs of each participant in each experiment were normalized (36).

Time–frequency analyses were conducted per channel using a wavelet transform, known to represent a compromise between time and frequency resolution (37). The procedure is fully described in the Supplementary material (SI Section S3.3). The wavelet transform was conducted on an interval from −1 to 2.5 s after stimulus onset, in time steps of 20 ms. The data transformation was performed before averaging across trials, separately for each frequency band. The total power of each frequency band represents both the phase-locked and the nonphase-locked signal with respect to the event. The resulting power was baseline corrected to obtain the relative signal change (Pcorr) which constituted the input of subsequent group analyses. The prestimulus period between −300 and −200 ms was used as a baseline for all spectral analyses. This baseline avoids inclusions of slow frequency leakage occurring immediately before the stimulus onset (see SI Section S3.4 for further information).

Statistical analyses

Each of the data analyses described below was repeated for experiments 1 and 2. Analyses were focused on exploring VEPs and time–frequency modulation as a function of the different evaluation tasks (aesthetic vs. pragmatic).

Statistical analyses in the time–frequency domain

Oscillatory fingerprints (38) of aesthetic vs. pragmatic evaluations were separately assessed for the (i) prestimulus and (ii) poststimulus onset periods with the same statistical approach. To investigate the possible effects of the evaluation task (aesthetic vs. pragmatic) separately on prestimulus (from −1 to 0 s) and poststimulus (from 0 to 1 s) oscillations, we employed a cluster-based permutation approach that accounts for the multiple comparison problem (39). The effect at the sample level was quantified by means of two-tailed t tests for dependent samples comparing the time–frequency oscillation between conditions (aesthetic vs. pragmatic) in a wide spectrum (2–40 Hz) and at each time point and channel (n = 62). Further details on the statistical approach (i.e. the two-tailed t test corrected by cluster-based permutations) are included in the Supplementary material (SI Section S3.5). We hypothesized that aesthetic and pragmatic evaluations affected alpha oscillations in both the prestimulus onset period and the poststimulus onset period.

Statistical analyses in the time domain

To investigate the effects of the evaluation task (aesthetic vs. pragmatic) on the magnitude in time of the brain response, first, we extracted two average waveforms (VEPs) for each participant, one corresponding to the aesthetic judgments and the other to the symmetry ones. Group-level statistics and correction for multiple comparisons were performed via cluster-based permutation testing (39), analogously to the time–frequency analyses described above. Specifically, differences between conditions (aesthetic vs. pragmatic) in the time domain were quantified by means of two-tailed t tests for dependent samples. We compared the baseline-corrected evoked response between tasks (aesthetic and pragmatic) at all latencies (from 0 to 2.5 s) and channels (n = 62). Clusters were identified based on temporal and spatial proximity, and the algorithm selected the t-values exceeding a specified threshold (cluster alpha = 0.05), as explained in the previous section and the Supplementary Material (SI Section S3.6).

Machine learning approach

In the binary classification problem, our goal was to employ the features of alpha power measured prestimulus and poststimulus onset (prestimulus inhibitory alpha power and poststimulus alpha ERD) and the VEP recorded in the first 200 ms poststimulus onset (including the C1 and the N170) to predict the aesthetic vs. pragmatic nature of the task. The classification was performed first within experiment 1 and then using the data of experiment 1 as the training set and the data of experiment 2 as the test set. The precise mathematical model that we used in the machine learning (ML) analysis is fully described in the Supplementary Material (SI Section S4).

Results

All participants (n = 36) recruited in the study were included in the group-level analysis. As descriptive analyses of the images employed, we investigated the aesthetic judgments provided during the aesthetic task. The statistical comparison between the average aesthetic appreciation of synthetic (mean = 4.53; SD = 1.155) and natural (mean = 5.47; SD = 0.900) images revealed that they differed significantly. More specifically, natural images showed significantly higher appreciation than synthetic ones (paired-sample t test results: mean = −1.10; SD = 0.179; t35 = −6.16; P < 0.001; Cohen's dz = 1). With the aim of exploring possible determinants of participants’ aesthetic judgments beside other low-level statistical features (such as contrast and luminosity, which were counterbalanced across blocks and conditions—see the “Methods” and “Stimuli” sections), through Mid-level Vision ToolBox (22), we quantified the curvature value of each image, since image curvature has often been associated with image beauty (40). In the present study too, we found a significant relationship between natural (but not synthetic) images and curvilinearity in the aesthetic task (for further details on these descriptive analyses, please refer to the Supplementary material).

Experiment 2 (natural images)

Point-by-point, group-level analyses over the poststimulus time window (0–1 s) revealed two different clusters of significance (aesthetic vs. pragmatic). To sum up, for hypothesis 1, we expected to observe in both experiments decreased prestimulus inhibitory alpha power and increased poststimulus alpha ERD and VEP early components in the aesthetic task compared with the pragmatic one. Results on the prestimulus activity fully confirmed our hypothesis in both experiments, demonstrating the presence of different preparatory activity across tasks. However, we did not find any difference in the neural oscillatory activity of the poststimulus. Results on VEPs (0–1 s time window) revealed a significant difference across the two tasks encompassing the LPP time window. Significant differences within VEP early components were found for experiment 2 selectively, within the N170 time window.

Testing hypothesis 2: ML approach

To assess whether the aesthetic and pragmatic tasks could be predicted within conditions and across experiments by the neural activity, we implemented four different analytical approaches that exploited in different manners information contained across participants, features, and electrodes, and using two different classifiers (linear binary classifier and Support Vector Machine—SVM).

First, as described in the Supplementary Material, we employed a linear binary classifier across participants (using a half-split approach) and across features, to investigate the possibility of distinguishing between pragmatic and aesthetic judgments separately for each experimental condition (i.e. synthetic and natural images), as well as across conditions through cross-decoding (i.e. training on synthetic images and testing on natural ones).

More specifically, features were employed in a multivariate classifier, neglecting the spatial informativeness of the channels. Given the visual nature of the task, we averaged the activity of evoked and oscillatory neural responses across two posterior channels (O1 and O2), which well capture visual responses for both ERPs and time–frequency data. For the whole set of the aforementioned features (i.e. C1, N170, alpha prestimulus, and alpha poststimulus, which were the average of the activity within the relative time windows), we trained and tested a linear binary classifier across participants (half the subjects as training and half as test), based on the data associated with synthetic images coming from experiment 1, using the procedure described in the Supplementary material, hence including |H|=161,051 candidate classifiers in the optimization process. For completeness, we repeated the procedure using data associated with natural images coming from experiment 2. Then, based on the same procedure, we trained a linear binary classifier using data associated with synthetic images coming from experiment 1 for the training set (including data from all subjects in that set), and data associated with natural images coming from experiment 2 as test set (including data from all subjects in that set). For each analysis, to assess the performance of the best classifier on the test set, we applied again Hoeffding's bound, this time with the empirical risk evaluated on the test set, and with the statistical learning theory (SLT)-based confidence half-interval ε evaluated using |H|=1, since only one classifier had to be tested. Finally, for the first two analyses, the procedure was repeated, exchanging the training and test sets. In this way, it was possible to estimate the (best-classifier) classification error rate per subject. For the third (cross-experimental) analysis, this exchange was not needed, as it was already possible to estimate the (best-classifier) classification error rate per subject. Significance was tested against chance level (i.e. 50%) using a one-sample, one-tailed t test (n = 36, right tailed).

Discussion

The present study aimed to explore, with solid a priori assumptions and a fully preregistered design, the age-old intuition that aesthetic experiences are characterized by a state of heightened attention toward external stimuli (i.e. an “aesthetic attitude”). Specifically, we examined whether engaging in sustained aesthetic evaluation, as opposed to a pragmatic one, generates a top-down, tonic attentional enhancement observable through prestimulus and poststimulus attention-related indices in the EEG signal. Overall, our findings in both the time and time–frequency domains support this hypothesis, revealing differences in evoked responses and oscillatory activity across tasks, even though not all our predictions were confirmed (see “Testing hypothesis 1” in the Results section). The ML approach solved the binary classification problem only when run at the single participant level, showing that the N170 component and alpha oscillatory activity before stimulus onset can predict the assigned task within the same experiment, but not across different experiments (see “Testing hypothesis 2” in the Results section), suggesting highly consistent effects across trials, within the same experiment.

Neural oscillatory dynamics during aesthetic evaluation

The prestimulus time–frequency results of both experiments (synthetic and natural images) revealed a significant tonic decrease in alpha (8–12 Hz) and beta (20–30 Hz) frequency power during aesthetic compared with pragmatic evaluation, fully supporting our first hypothesis. This result shows that participants’ oscillatory activity differs significantly even before the onset of the image, suggesting the emergence of a specific “attitude” during the evaluation task. Importantly, prestimulus alpha power reduction has been associated in the literature with attentional engagement (41) and perceptual processing facilitation (enhanced gain of the visual stream (41)). It is therefore possible that one of the processes characterizing aesthetic evaluation is an enhanced allocation of neural resources toward the processing of attended stimuli. Moreover, the observed prestimulus tonic reduction in alpha and beta power has frequently been reported during preparatory activity and is typically associated with the so-called readiness potential (RP) (42–44). The RP, traditionally linked to motor preparation (42), has more recently been observed in cognitive tasks as well (42–45), such as preparing for mental operations on numbers (43). Therefore, in line with previous studies investigating the preparatory activity indicated by RP (42, 44, 46) in motor and cognitive tasks, it is possible that the reduction in beta power during the aesthetic task reflects an enhanced preparatory process, while the accompanying decrease in alpha power may be interpreted as the complementary attentional engagement, both promoting a sensory-oriented engagement with the upcoming stimuli. To sum up, our results on the prestimulus interval reveal a distinct neural signature for the aesthetic task, likely reflecting attentional and preparatory processes.

On the other hand, the poststimulus time–frequency analysis of experiments 1 and 2 did not support our a priori hypothesis. We observed no significant difference in alpha ERD between the aesthetic and pragmatic evaluation tasks. This suggests that the oscillatory activity following stimulus onset was not modulated by the specific evaluation task. However, when considered alongside the significant differences observed in VEP responses (see below), our findings indicate that poststimulus neural activity differs markedly in terms of amplitude and latency across tasks, even though not in the frequency band hypothesized. Several factors may account for the discrepancies observed between time domain and time–frequency responses. One possibility is that the significant prestimulus difference in the alpha and beta bands affected poststimulus analyses, potentially limiting the detection of significant ER% effects. Future studies could be directed to disentangling prestimulus and poststimulus time–frequency dynamics by designing both the experimental protocol and the statistical approach to address this issue more effectively.

VEP response modulation between aesthetic and pragmatic tasks

The time domain results partially confirmed our first hypothesis, revealing significant differentiation of the VEPs between aesthetic and pragmatic tasks (see Results and Fig. 4). Aesthetic vs. pragmatic evaluation of synthetic images (experiment 1) elicited a significant modulation of the LPP, predominantly over the left hemisphere. However, the earlier time window (0–200 ms) did not reveal a significant difference between tasks, suggesting that the task did not modulate the C1 and N170 early components with synthetic images. On the other hand, VEPs elicited by natural images (experiment 2) revealed three clusters of significant difference, encompassing a larger and distributed scalp topography, and involving both the LPP (from its onset) and the N170 component. The findings from experiment 2 support our first hypothesis, suggesting that during the aesthetic evaluation of natural images participants engaged in distinct neural processes as early as 200 ms poststimulus. To sum up, aesthetic evaluation consistently enhanced the LPP across both experiments, confirming previous findings and extending them by revealing an earlier onset and a broader scalp distribution (14, 15). The LPP is a well-established ERP component emerging between 300 and 1200 ms after stimulus onset and is associated with sustained attentional and evaluative processes, especially in response to emotionally salient stimuli (47). Notably, both the latency and scalp distribution of the LPP provide valuable information for distinguishing its functionally distinct temporal components (48). The early LPP (300–600 ms), typically associated with a posterior topography, is thought to reflect automatic attentional engagement and emotional reactivity (48–51); the late LPP (600–1200 ms), characterized by a broader anterior-posterior distribution, is generally connected with prefrontal cortical involvement and is associated with cognitive regulation and deliberative evaluation (48, 52). Crucially, task instructions, such as providing information about stimulus valence, have been shown to differentially modulate these LPP components, affecting both appraisal and reappraisal processes (48, 53, 54). Our findings revealed an earlier onset of LPP differentiation compared with prior studies on aesthetic evaluation (14, 16, 18), indicating that our design might have induced modulation of both the early and late LPP components. Moreover, while Jacobsen and colleagues (14) reported a predominantly right-hemisphere distribution, our results showed a left-lateralized topography in experiment 1 and a broader, more distributed activation in experiment 2. We speculate that our paradigm, specifically designed to involve sustained aesthetic evaluation over time, successfully elicited a stronger aesthetic attitude, which persisted throughout the entire experimental block. This enduring state likely modulated both early and late LPP components and thus influenced both affective and higher-order cognitive evaluative processes.

As indicated in the Results, image type (synthetic vs. natural) influences the VEP responses during aesthetic and pragmatic evaluation, leading to distinct temporal and spatial clusters of significance across experiments. Specifically, the sustained aesthetic evaluation of natural images when compared with synthetic images elicited a broader (i.e. bilateral) and earlier differentiation, also involving the N170 component. This finding suggests that the aesthetic attitude effect was amplified by natural images. One contributing factor may be the generally higher aesthetic appreciation of these stimuli compared with synthetic ones (see Results). Participants’ clear preference for natural images may have enhanced the engagement with the aesthetic evaluation task. Additionally, the semantic richness typically associated with landscapes (and absent in synthetic images) may have further enhanced the effect observed in experiment 2. Future studies should investigate the influence of aesthetic appreciation on the establishment of a strong aesthetic attitude, ideally by controlling for both stimulus features and subjective preferences.

Neural signatures differentiate between aesthetic and pragmatic tasks

The ML approach successfully addressed the binary classification problem, but only partially fulfilled the a priori predictions. Specifically, the N170 component and prestimulus alpha oscillatory activity were able to predict the assigned task within the experiment but failed to generalize across experiments (Fig. 5). Note that this finding emerged only once the analysis was performed at the single participant level, suggesting variability of the effects across individuals

This finding reinforces our previous discussion, suggesting that neural signatures in both prestimulus and early poststimulus windows (within 200 ms) are reliable task predictors. Notably, the ML also identified a role for the N170 in the experiment with synthetic images, an effect not detected by the VEP cluster-based statistical analysis, thereby highlighting the enhanced sensitivity of the ML approach. The lack of predictive accuracy across experiments provides confirmation of the observed differences in the neural signals between experiments 1 and 2 and thus between the neural activity associated with the use of synthetic and natural images. Variations in prestimulus oscillatory activity, VEP latency and amplitude, as well as differences in scalp distribution, make it impossible to generate predictions across tasks. Nevertheless, the ML approach provided further evidence of the possibility to use biomarkers to differentiate aesthetic experiences from other forms of visual judgments.

The neural underpinnings of the aesthetic attitude

Overall, our results shed some light on the age-old philosophical idea that our experiences of beauty are characterized by the adoption of a special mode of attention—heightened, contemplative and focused on the object's formal or expressive qualities (i.e. the “aesthetic attitude”) (1, 2, 13). This idea, central to classical aesthetics especially in the Kantian tradition, is increasingly gaining traction in psychology and neuroscience, where evidence for a distinctive attentional state in aesthetic contexts is beginning to accumulate. Previous neuroscientific findings have suggested that a state of heightened attention during engagement with art may be supported by alpha-band oscillation (55, 56). Distinctive neural responses in aesthetic contexts appear to be generable through top-down processes, such as engaging in aesthetic evaluation (15, 16, 18) or being exposed to aesthetically appealing stimuli that generate expectations of encountering other appealing stimuli (55–57). However, many aspects of the relationship between aesthetic experiences and attention remain unclear. In particular, it is unclear whether aesthetic evaluation tasks can systematically induce observable attentional enhancements.

Our results contribute to filling this gap by offering a rigorous, data-driven exploration of how an aesthetic evaluation task can produce a top-down modulation of well-established EEG indices of perceptual and attentional enhancement. The systematic modulation of neural oscillatory activity in both alpha and beta frequency bands prior to stimulus presentation, the predictive role of early VEP components in distinguishing the task (aesthetic vs. pragmatic), and the observed enhancement of both early and late components of the LPP all support the idea that participants were adopting a distinctive processing mode when evaluating images for their aesthetic appeal. This extends previous findings on the neural correlates of evaluative tasks (14–16, 18) and sheds new light on the neural underpinnings of the aesthetic attitude.

In this respect, our study may represent experimental evidence for a growing body of research linking aesthetic experience to enhanced perceptual processing and attentional magnification. According to several recent theoretical frameworks, in fact, we experience aesthetic pleasure when we are particularly effective in processing sensory information (i.e. we process information fluently, find new patterns in our sensorium, make larger updates to our models of the world, experience faster reduction in uncertainty about the causes of our sensations) (7, 58–60). For these frameworks, aesthetic pleasure acts as a kind of hedonic feedback that signals which portions of the world are more epistemically profitable and worth exploring further (7). Interestingly for our purposes, according to several of these approaches, aesthetic pleasure does not merely act as a feedback of effective perceptual processing but can also generate the expectation that future perceptual encounters will also be profitable, making us more attentive, curious, and inclined to learn. Previous studies have shown that exposure to aesthetically pleasing stimuli enhances attention, perceptual learning processing and curiosity in subsequent tasks (55, 57, 61, 62). Our study extends these results by showing that the mere framing of a task in terms of aesthetic appreciation can lead to appreciable perceptual and attentional enhancements. In particular, our findings suggest that an aesthetic attitude can be induced by a purely top-down instruction and then sustained and amplified by the pleasantness of the presented stimuli, in a sort of nonlinear, exponential process. We speculate that this may be particularly relevant in the context of profound aesthetic experiences, such as those elicited by nature, museum visits, music, cinema, and theater, where both the expectation to encounter beauty and the sustained encounter with beautiful stimuli contribute significantly to the overall experience.

For these reasons, we believe that our findings have the potential to inform different fields that could benefit from a better understanding of the way in which we deploy our attentional resources, such as education, psychotherapy (63), neurocognitive rehabilitation (64), and social adaptation in times of distress (65). Adopting an aesthetic attitude may prepare the neural system to embrace the sensory, cognitive, and affective complexity of the environment, effectively tuning the perceiver to its informativeness.
