Association between angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor use and clinical outcomes in patients with heart failure: a 1-year prospective cohort study from Jordan
Authors: Rama S Odeh, Mariam Abdel Jalil, Mohammad Ali Qudah, Hanna K Al-Makhamreh, Akram Saleh, Oriana Awwad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesHeart failure (HF) is associated with complex symptoms and frequent hospitalisation that reduce patients’ quality of life (QoL). This study aims to assess the association between angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI) use and changes in QoL and disease-related outcomes among patients with HF in Jordan.DesignProspective observational cohort study.Setting and participantsThe study was conducted among patients with HF attending the outpatient cardiology clinics at Jordan University Hospital, a tertiary care centre in Amman, Jordan. Patients either initiated on ARNI or receiving angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI)/angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) were included in the study at a 1:2 ratio. All participants were followed up for up to 1 year after recruitment. The study period was from 4 February 2024 to 29 May 2025.Primary and secondary outcome measuresData on QoL, New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) were collected at baseline and after 3 months of treatment. Hospitalisation data were collected for the preceding year and the year following participants’ recruitment. Medication adherence and ARNI side effects were assessed after 3-month of follow-up period.ResultsA total of 227 patients with HF were included; 74 were initiated on ARNI, and 153 were receiving ACEIs/ARBs. At baseline, significantly lower QoL scores and LVEF were observed in the ARNI group compared with the ACEIs/ARBs group. After 3-month, the ARNI group showed improvements in all QoL scores, NYHA functional class and LVEF (p<0.05). Worsened QoL scores (symptom stability, symptom burden, self-efficacy domains and clinical summary score) were detected within the ACEIs/ARBs group (p<0.05). One-year post-recruitment a significant reduction in cardiovascular and all-cause hospitalisations (p<0.05) was observed in the ARNI group compared with the ACEIs/ARBs group. Adherence levels assessed after 3 months of treatment were shown to be high in both study groups (p=0.558). The main ARNI side effect was hypotension.ConclusionsARNI use was associated with favourable QoL, NYHA class, and LVEF as well as lower hospitalisation rates among patients with HF in Jordan. The safety profile was consistent with previous studies.Keywords: Heart failure, Quality of Life, Medication Adherence

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a worldwide public health concern and a multifaceted clinical condition.1 It is defined by symptoms and signs arising from anatomical and/or functional cardiac abnormalities.2 HF is associated with high mortality rates, frequent hospitalisations and a negative impact on patients’ quality of life (QoL).3 The global prevalence of HF is estimated to be 64 million individuals. In Jordan, HF affects approximately 100 000 people, with an annual incidence of 8251 cases.4 In a study conducted between 2021 and 2023, involving 1861 patients with HF from 21 centres in Jordan, 70.3% of the patients were identified as having HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF; EF<40%), indicating its predominance among the Jordanian population.5 HF contributes to 8.7% of the cardiovascular (CV)-related deaths in Jordan and poses a serious prognostic concern in the Middle East, with mortality rates reaching 25% within 1 year and 50% within 5 years.6

Breathlessness, fluid retention, fatigue and reduced exercise capacity are the primary manifestations of HFrEF.2 Various processes affect the key pathways involved in HFrEF development, including the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, natriuretic peptide system and autonomic nervous system.7 Understanding the underlying mechanisms of HFrEF led to significant progress in its treatment, resulting in the development of medications that alleviate symptoms and prolong life expectancy.8 Standard treatments for HFrEF include a combination of evidence-based beta-blockers (BBs), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors (angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs) or angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNIs)) and sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 inhibitors.9 Among them, a noteworthy addition was the combination of the angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) valsartan with the neprilysin inhibitor sacubitril, a food and drug administration (FDA)-approved ARNI.10 Clinical trials highlighted ARNI’s superiority over ACEIs and ARBs in reducing CV mortality and hospitalisations while improving QoL among patients with HFrEF,11,13 leading cardiology organisations such as the American College of Cardiology (ACC)/American Heart Association (AHA)/HF Society of America (HFSA) and the European Society of Cardiology, to recommend, from 2016, valsartan/sacubitril in their management guidelines.9

While the superiority of ARNI over ACEIs/ARBs has already been established in large randomised controlled trials such as PARADIGM-HF, in Jordan, no previous studies have ever investigated the therapeutic outcomes of ARNI in patients with HF, and local real-world data remain limited. The key aim of this study was to provide such knowledge by assessing the association between ARNI use and QoL scores among patients with HFrEF in comparison to the more traditional treatments (ACEIs/ARBs) after 3 months of therapy. Additionally, the study aims to assess secondary outcomes, including improvements in patients’ New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification and left ventricular EF (LVEF) at 3 months of follow-up as well as hospitalisation rates after 1 year of follow-up. Medication adherence and the occurrence of certain ARNI-related side effects were also evaluated after 3 months of treatment.

Methods

Study design

This observational prospective cohort study was conducted from 4 February 2024 to 29 May 2025 in patients with HFrEF attending the outpatient cardiology clinics at Jordan University Hospital (JUH) in Amman, Jordan. The clinical site is one of the largest tertiary hospitals in Jordan, receiving patients from the central urban, rural and surrounding areas.14

Participants and study procedures

Patients with HF attending the outpatient cardiology clinics were invited to participate in the study. A list of these patients was obtained from the hospital system prior to the start of clinic visits. Patients who consented to participate were screened for eligibility and subsequently enrolled into one of the study groups. The study included two groups of adult participants (≥18 years of age). The first comprised patients receiving ARNI for the first time, either newly initiated on ARNI for the treatment of HF or switched to it from ACEIs or ARBs (never received ARNI before the study recruitment). The second group consisted of patients who had been treated with ACEIs or ARBs for at least 9 months before recruitment. This latter group served as control group for comparative analyses against the ARNI group. Patients were excluded from the study if they had HF with preserved EF, a condition interfering with the primary study aim (eg, active cancer, dialysis, lung fibrosis, leg amputation or mental health disorders), an acute cardiac event (such as acute coronary syndrome, stroke or arrhythmias) within the 3 months before recruitment or if they refused to participate in the study. In addition, patients who experienced changes in their medication regimen or had an acute cardiac event after recruitment were excluded from the final analysis. Participants were enrolled at a ratio of 1:2 (ARNI:ACEIs/ARBs) to increase the power of the study. This means that for each participant recruited and allocated to the ARNI group, two participants were recruited in the ACEIs/ARBs group. A consecutive (non-random) sampling approach was used. Eligible patients who consented to participate in the study were invited to attend two face-to-face interviews with a clinical pharmacist, each lasting approximately 20 min, one on study enrolment (baseline) and the other after 3 months of treatment (follow-up). All data were collected by the same clinical pharmacist (MSc holder) with formal training in Good Clinical Practice.

Data collection

A structured data collection form was used to obtain information from both patients and their medical records. These included information on the participants’ demographics, clinical characteristics, medications, laboratory data (serum creatinine and potassium), echocardiograms and hospitalisations. To assess the study outcomes, information about the patients’ QoL, NYHA class and LVEF was also collected both at baseline and after a 3-month follow-up period, while the number of hospitalisations was recorded during the 12 months prior to and following the recruitment date. Additionally, adherence to the study medications (ARNI, ACEIs and ARBs) and ARNI’s side effects were evaluated after 3 months of treatment. Adherence to medication was assessed using the validated Arabic version of the Morisky-Green-Levine Scale (MGLS).15 This tool consists of 4 items, each scored as ‘Yes’=1 or ‘No’=0, with lower total scores indicating higher adherence and adherence levels classified as either adherent (score 0) or not adherent (score 1–4) to treatment.15 16 Adverse effects of ARNI were evaluated according to the findings from the PARADIGM-HF trial,11 which identified hypotension, angioedema, hyperkalaemia, changes in renal function and cough as the most common associated adverse effects. Hypotension was defined as systolic blood pressure <100 mm Hg or experiencing dizziness, syncope or blurred vision, while hyperkalaemia was defined by a potassium level ≥5.5 mmol/L.17 Changes in renal function were evaluated by comparing baseline and follow-up serum creatinine levels, with a 30% increase indicating worsening in renal function.18

Study outcomes

Quality of life (QoL)

To evaluate the association between ARNI use and participants’ QoL, the validated Arabic version of the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ) was used.19 The KCCQ is a 23-item questionnaire designed to assess health-related QoL in patients with HF by evaluating seven distinct domains (physical limitation, symptom stability, symptom frequency, symptom burden, self-efficacy, QoL and social limitations) and three summary scores (the total symptom score (KCCQ-TS), the clinical summary score (KCCQ-CS) and the overall summary score (KCCQ-OS)). The domains and summary scores provide in-depth insights into the various aspects of a patient’s life impacted by HF, with the tool being sensitive to changes in health status over time.19 Permission to use the instrument and its scoring system was provided by the original authors (https://www.cvoutcomes.org/). QoL changes were analysed within each study group (baseline vs follow-up) and between groups (ARNI vs ACEIs/ARBs) at both baseline and after 3 months of follow-up.

NYHA classification and LVEF

Secondary study outcomes included the detection of changes in participants’ NYHA functional class and LVEF after 3 months of treatment by comparing baseline and follow-up readings within and between the study groups. According to the NYHA functional classification for HF, patients can be categorised into one of four different classes (I, II, III or IV) based on their symptoms and physical activity limitations. Higher classes indicate more severe symptoms, greater limitations and worse health status.20 Patients’ files were screened using the JUH archiving system to collect LVEF measurement records, both at baseline and at 3 months of follow-up. Higher readings indicated better cardiac function and health status.9 LVEF readings were obtained from transthoracic echocardiography performed by skilled echocardiography laboratory technicians. At the study site, these technicians were not involved with patients’ clinical care and were not informed of the medications prescribed, ensuring that LVEF acquisition was independent of treatment assignment. All echocardiographic images and measurements were subsequently reviewed and validated by an attending cardiologist in accordance with the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines.

Hospitalisation rates

Hospitalisation data were also collected using the JUH archiving system. For each patient, the total number of CV-related hospitalisations and all-cause hospital admissions were recorded in the 12 months before and after participation in the study. CV hospitalisations encompassed admissions associated with HF, arrhythmia and ischaemic heart disease as documented by the treating physicians, while all-cause hospitalisations included any hospital admission even if not linked to CV causes. Comparisons were conducted within each treatment group and between groups.

Statistical analysis and sample size calculation

The study’s targeted sample size was calculated using the ClinCalc automated sample size calculator (https://clincalc.com/stats/samplesize.aspx). Based on published data, the mean±SD QoL score (key outcome) among Jordanians with HF was 50±21, using the validated Arabic 36-Item Short Form Survey tool.3 Assuming an increase in the QoL scores by 20%, a statistical power of 90%, an alpha value of 0.05 and an enrolment ratio of 1:2 (ARNI: ACEIs/ARBs), the estimated sample size was 70 patients for the ARNI group and 140 patients for the ACEIs/ARBs group.

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS (V.23). Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were reported as frequency (percentage) for categorical variables and median (IQR) for continuous variables. Group differences were assessed using χ2 or Fisher’s exact test for categorical/ordinal variables and the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables. Changes in outcomes over time were analysed using the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test for continuous/ordinal variables. A p value<0.05 indicated statistical significance. Additionally, a comprehensive multivariable analysis using rank-based analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed to account for the non-normal distribution of KCCQ scores. The dependent variable was the ranked post-intervention KCCQ domains and summary scores. The model included baseline ranked KCCQ score (continuous covariate), NYHA class at baseline (categorical factor), age (continuous covariate), gender (categorical factor) and treatment group (ARNI vs ACEIs/ARBs) (categorical factor). This model directly adjusted for baseline disease severity, demographics and clinical confounders.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Participants’ demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 227 patients were included in the study and completed the follow-up period. Among them, 74 (32.6%) were in the ARNI group and 153 (67.4%) in the ACEIs/ARBs group. The participants’ flow diagram is presented in figure 1. Within the ACEIs/ARBs group, 73 patients (47.7%) and 80 patients (52.3%) were treated with ACEIs and ARBs, respectively. The median (IQR) age of the study population was 65 (56–74) years, with the majority being male (67.8%). Most of the participants were married (84.6%), unemployed (77.1%), overweight or obese (78.4%) and smokers (66.1%). In addition, most of the patients were neither performing physical activity (79.3%) nor adopting a low-sodium diet (74%). Almost half of the study population had a university/college degree (52.4%). The study sample showed a median (IQR) HF duration of 4 (2–8) years and a median (IQR) of 2 (1–3) comorbidities. No significant differences were observed in the demographic/clinical characteristics between the ARNI and ACEIs/ARBs groups, except for the NYHA class (table 1).

Study outcomes

Quality of life (QoL)

Significant differences were detected between the two study groups in all QoL domains at baseline (p<0.05), except for the self-efficacy domain. Lower scores (indicating worse QoL) were observed among ARNI participants. Accordingly, the KCCQ-TS, KCCQ-CS and KCCQ-OS were also lower in the ARNI group compared with the ACEIs/ARBs group (p<0.05). This indicated that patients initiated on ARNI had greater physical and social limitations, with their HF symptoms being less stable, more frequent and of greater burden, reflecting poorer QoL (figure 2 ; online supplemental table S2).

At follow-up, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test results showed significant improvements (p<0.001) in the QoL within the ARNI group across all KCCQ domains. Higher post-treatment median scores were observed, indicating better clinical condition (figure 3; table 2). Changes in NYHA classification and LVEF readings

The patients’ distribution among the four NYHA Classes at baseline and follow-up is shown in the online supplemental table S4. Significant improvements in NYHA classification were observed in the ARNI group after 3 months of treatment compared with the ACEIs/ARBs group (p=0.001) (table 4). At follow-up, most of the participants treated with ARNI (94.6%) showed a lower NYHA class compared with baseline, indicating improvement in HF symptoms, with no improvements observed in the ACEIs/ARBs group.

The LVEF was evaluated at baseline and at follow-up in a total of 221 patients out of 227 (97.4%); among them, 73 were treated with ARNI, while 148 were in the ACEIs/ARBs group. At baseline, significant differences were observed in the LVEF records between the two groups (p=0.001), with the ARNI group showing a lower LVEF median (26; IQR: 25–35) compared with the ACEIs/ARBs group (45; IQR: 40–50). At follow-up, participants treated with ARNI showed significantly higher EF readings compared with baseline, reporting a median of 30 (IQR: 25–37), which indicated improvements in cardiac function (p=0.001). On the other hand, treatment with ACEIs/ARBs did not reveal any change in LVEF after 3 months of treatment, with the median remaining at 45 (IQR: 40–50) (p=0.167).

Hospitalisation rates

Of all the included participants, 72 (31.7%) and 96 (42.3%) experienced at least one CV and one all-cause hospitalisation in the 12-month period preceding the study recruitment, respectively. No significant differences were observed in the number of hospitalisations between the ARNI and ACEIs/ARBs groups (p>0.05) (table 5). One year after being enrolled, a total of 53 (23.3%) and 23 (10.1%) participants had at least one CV and one all-cause hospitalisation, respectively. A between-group comparison showed significantly lower hospitalisation rates in the ARNI group compared with the ACEIs/ARBs group (p=0.001) (table 5). The ARNI group also experienced significant reductions in the number of CV and all-cause hospitalisation (p=0.001) after treatment, while no statistically significant changes were observed in the ACEIs/ARBs group (p>0.05) (table 5). These findings highlight an association between ARNI treatment and a reduction in hospitalisation rates among the study population.

Medication adherence

Among all the study participants, the majority (n=180, 790.3%) were found to be adherent to their medication at follow-up. Similar adherence rates were observed between participants in the ARNI (n=57, 77%) and ACEIs/ARBs groups (n=123, 80.4%) (p=0.558), suggesting that the improvements observed in the measured outcomes might be associated with the treatment rather than differences in the medication adherence between the study groups (online supplemental table S5).

Safety outcomes in the sacubitril/valsartan group

Assessment of the main side effects associated with ARNI treatment showed that 31.08% (n=23) of the participants suffered from symptomatic hypotension, with fewer patients showing worsening in the renal function (n=5, 6.76%) or hyperkalaemia (n=4, 5.41%). There were no reported cases of cough or angioedema in the study population.

Discussion

This study provides the first real-world evidence on the use of ARNI among patients with HFrEF in Jordan and its association with disease-related clinical outcomes. The key measured outcome included changes in patients’ QoL, with significant improvements observed across all KCCQ domains and summary scores after 3 months of treatment. These results are consistent with previous large clinical trials confirming the greater efficacy of ARNI in managing HF compared with ACEIs/ARBs, providing a comprehensive benefit encompassing the disease’s physical and psychosocial aspects.21 22 In the PARADIGM-HF trial, patients treated with ARNI experienced a better QoL compared with those treated with enalapril. Particularly, improvements were observed over a period of 36 months across all KCCQ domains and summary scores, except for the symptom stability domain. In line with these study results, patients treated with enalapril also showed declines in most of the domains’ scores.13 Similarly, the TRANSITION study also reported better symptom control and fewer HF-related events among patients treated with ARNI, suggesting improved symptom stability.23 On these bases, the 2022 AHA/ACC/HFSA HF guidelines recommended ARNIs as first-line treatment for patients with HFrEF due to proven benefits in improving symptoms, reducing hospitalisations and achieving stable symptom management.9

In this study, improvements in the NYHA functional class were also observed among patients treated with ARNI. This aligns with those observed in the participants’ QoL and with evidence from previous studies, including the PARADIGM-HF11 and the EVALUATE-HF trials,24 which also support these results.

Interestingly, the ARNI group also showed significant amelioration in the LVEF readings, indicating better heart function, which might also reflect the amelioration observed in the QoL and the NYHA functional classe observed after 3 months of ARNI treatment. In this regard, previous studies similarly found patients treated with sacubitril/valsartan to have higher LVEF compared with those treated with enalapril.25,28 The ARTIM-HF trial, for instance, reported no changes in the LVEF records over 6 months of ACEIs/ARBs therapy, whereas a significant increase in the mean LVEF was observed in patients receiving ARNI. 29Overall, our findings provide real-world evidence from Jordan on the use of ARNI in routine clinical practice, complementing data from larger randomised trials and suggesting potential benefits on patient-reported outcomes, functional status and cardiac performance.

Consistent with previous research findings, ARNI treatment was associated with a significant reduction in hospitalisation rates among the study participants. A 21% reduction in HF hospitalisation was observed in the PARADIGM-HF trial after treatment with sacubitril/valsartan compared with enalapril.11 Similarly, shorter-term studies, such as PIONEER-HF and TRANSITION, also demonstrated that early ARNI initiation leads to lower rehospitalisation rates and improved clinical outcomes.23 30

At the study site, prescription of ARNI was reserved for patients with more severe HF. This aligns with the study findings, as patients initiated on ARNI had significantly poorer QoL and lower LVEF compared with those treated with ACEIs/ARBs and is also consistent with previous reports. For instance, in the TRANSITION study, patients started on ARNI had lower baseline LVEF and higher symptom burden compared with patients treated with ACEIs/ARBs. Similarly, the PROVE-HF study reported patients initiated on ARNI to often have impaired QoL and elevated biomarkers at baseline, reflecting higher disease severity.11 23 31

To better associate the observed improvements in the patients’ health status with ARNI treatment, participants in both groups were monitored closely to confirm that no new treatments or dosage regimen changes were introduced during the study period. In addition, appropriate adherence to medications (ARNI or ACEIs/ARBs) was also confirmed at the follow-up visit, with no significant differences observed between the two study groups, further suggesting that the observed measure outcomes were likely linked to the use of medications rather than to differences in adherence levels. The high adherence rates observed among the study population contrast with previous results from Jordan, showing lower rates among Jordanians with chronic diseases.32 33 On the same line, and more specifically, Jarrah et al34 reported suboptimal medication adherence among patients with HF, showing that almost half of them had poor adherence to their disease-related medications.34 This might be attributed to differences in the demographic characteristics of the study participants and the study setting. For instance, the participants’ median age in this study showed a prevalence of older adults who usually have more frequent interactions with healthcare providers and are typically more motivated to take their medications correctly.33 35 Furthermore, the high educational level of the study participants might have also played a role in the observed finding, with studies from Jordan highlighting the impact of knowledge and educational attainment on medication adherence.33

Additionally, patients attending a tertiary university hospital might demonstrate higher engagement compared with broader populations.36 Assessment of ARNI side effects showed findings aligning with the safety profile observed in the PARADIGM-HF trial, which also reported a relatively low risk of worsening renal function and hyperkalaemia, as well as a higher incidence of hypotension, attributed to the combined effects of neprilysin inhibition and ARB.11 17 No cases of cough or angioedema were observed in the ARNI group, in line with the PARADIGM-HF trial.11 Overall, this finding contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting the safety profile of sacubitril/valsartan, making ARNI a viable option for the treatment of patients with HF with careful monitoring and management of potential side effects.

This study represents the first in Jordan to evaluate the use of ARNI among patients with HFrEF. It employed the KCCQ, a robust tool encompassing several domains and summary scores to evaluate the HF-related QoL comprehensively, with sensitivity to health status changes over time. The prospective design of the study enabled a forward-looking assessment of the association between ARNI treatment and the key outcomes while minimising missing data. The study faced some challenges in recruiting patients naïve to ARNI, as the population at JUH consisted predominantly of individuals with long-standing HF treated with ACEIs/ARBs therapy. Despite this, a representative sample was recruited and completed the follow-up period. Conducting the study at a single tertiary care centre with its local character might have restricted the international generalisability of the findings, and the open-label non-randomised design limited the ability to fully control for potential confounding and bias. Blinding of participants was not feasible, as patients needed to be informed about their treatment allocation, which is a recognised limitation of observational research. This design reflects routine clinical practice, allowing the capture of real-world outcomes while acknowledging that awareness of treatment could have introduced performance bias. Adherence was assessed using the MGLS self-reported questionnaire. While this represents an indirect method of measuring adherence, it is widely used in real-world clinical studies and provides a practical estimate of patient-reported adherence. Direct methods, such as serum drug level monitoring, were not feasible in our real-world setting. The relatively short 3-month follow-up period might have not fully captured the longer-term changes associated with ARNI use on the patients’ QoL and cardiac function. However, the inclusion of 12-month hospitalisation data provides an indication of the potential sustained favourable benefit of ARNI use in real-world HF management.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study demonstrated the benefits of ARNI in ameliorating the QoL, NYHA functional class and LVEF among patients with HFrEF as well as reducing the number of hospitalisations. These findings address the positive changes observed across multiple aspects of patients with HF conditions during ARNI therapy, further proving evidence of its effectiveness in managing their symptoms and cardiac function, significantly enhancing the overall patient well-being in Jordan. High levels of medication adherence were reported among the study participants, further suggesting that the observed measured outcomes were associated with the treatment rather than with differences in medication adherence. Evaluation of ARNI-related side effects after 3 months of therapy revealed a safety profile similar to that observed in previous research. Despite the positive changes associated with ARNI use, its prescription remains constrained to patients with more severe conditions, limiting its benefits to only a subset of Jordanians suffering from HF.
