Potential Application of Cocoa Extract in Preventing Skin Cancer Through Inhibition of Adenosine Receptor A2, VEGFR-2, and BCL-2: in vivo and Docking Molecular Studies
Authors: Siswanto WAHAB, Khairuddin DJAWAD
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSkin cancer is a growing global health issue. Natural compounds with multi-pathway activity are promising chemopreventive agents. Cocoa extract contains bioactive components that may inhibit cancer-related molecular pathways, such as flavonoids, polyphenols, and alkaloids. This study aims to assess the chemopreventive potential of cocoa extract against skin cancer by targeting the adenosine A2 receptor and vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-2 (VEGFR-2), and by modulating B-cell lymphoma-2 (BCL-2) expression, using both in vivo and molecular docking methods.MethodsThirty female Swiss mice were treated with (7,12-dimethylbenz[a]anthracene) and (12-O-tetradecanoylphorbol-13-acetate) to induce squamous cell carcinoma. Cocoa extract (1600 ppm) was administered as a preventive treatment for 12 weeks. Measurements included flavonoid and polyphenol content, malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, BCL-2 protein expression, and histopathological evaluation. Molecular docking was performed to assess interactions between cocoa compounds and adenosine A2 and VEGFR2 receptors.ResultsThe cocoa extract contained 118.72 µg of flavonoids and 66.24 mg of polyphenols. Theobromine inhibited adenosine A2 receptor activity and reduced interleukin-8 expression. Procyanidin and epicatechin bound to VEGFR2, potentially suppressing angiogenesis. Immunohistochemistry showed significant reductions in both BCL-2 expression (p=0.035) and MDA levels (p=0.001), indicating decreased oxidative stress. Histological analysis revealed milder dysplasia in the cocoa-treated group compared to controls (p=0.002).ConclusionsCocoa extract demonstrates potential as a natural chemopreventive agent against skin cancer through antioxidant activity, anti-angiogenesis, and apoptosis modulation pathways.

INTRODUCTION

Skin cancer is a form of malignancy whose prevalence continues to increase globally1. An estimated 104,960 new cases of skin cancer are expected to occur in the United States in 2025, with 8,430 deaths2. This condition has been reported to increase among populations exposed to long-term ultraviolet (UV) light. Chronic exposure to UV radiation can trigger DNA mutations, oxidative stress, and dysregulation of apoptotic mechanisms, ultimately initiating cellular transformation and neoplasm growth3, 4. One important pathway involved in this process is the activation of the adenosine A2 receptor (A2AR) and the vascular endothelial growth factor receptor-2 (VEGFR-2), both of which play a role in creating a pro-survival and pro-angiogenic microenvironment5.

A2AR, a member of the G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) family, is known to be overactivated in tumor tissue due to adenosine accumulation in hypoxic microenvironments. Activation of this receptor triggers increased cAMP/PKA/CREB signaling, which induces the expression of various anti-apoptotic genes, including B-cell lymphoma-2 (BCL-2). Thus, skin cancer cells acquire resistance to apoptosis, enabling them to survive adverse environmental conditions. Furthermore, VEGFR-2 is the primary receptor tyrosine kinase for VEGF, which plays a crucial role in angiogenesis. VEGFR-2 activation initiates the PI3K/Akt and ERK/MAPK pathways, which not only increase endothelial cell proliferation and migration but also enhance BCL-2 expression. This finding indicates that VEGFR-2 is crucial link between angiogenesis and resistance to apoptosis in skin carcinogenesis. The combination of A2AR and VEGFR-2 activation synergistically promotes BCL-2 expression, resulting in more resistant and angiogenic tumor tissue6.

The pathogenesis of skin cancer involves a complex interaction between oxidative stress, inflammation, angiogenesis, and apoptosis resistance. Following exposure to carcinogens such as DMBA, excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) are generated, leading to lipid peroxidation and increased malondialdehyde (MDA) formation, a well-established marker of oxidative stress7, 8. Elevated oxidative stress activates redox-sensitive transcription factors, particularly NF-κB and HIF-1α, which stimulate the production of pro-inflammatory mediators including interleukin-8 (IL-8) and VEGF9, 10. Increased VEGF subsequently activates VEGFR-2 signaling, promoting angiogenesis and tumor cell survival through the PI3K/Akt and ERK/MAPK pathways11. Simultaneously, A2AR activation within the tumor microenvironment enhances inflammatory signaling and further contributes to IL-8 secretion6. These pathways ultimately converge on the upregulation of anti-apoptotic proteins such as BCL-2, allowing damaged cells to evade apoptosis and progress toward dysplasia and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) development12, 13. Therefore, MDA, IL-8, VEGFR-2, A2AR, and BCL-2 represent interconnected molecular events involved in skin carcinogenesis.

With increasing interest in natural therapies, cocoa (Theobroma cacao) has emerged as a potential candidate for skin cancer prevention. Cocoa is rich in flavonoids, particularly epicatechin, catechin, and proanthocyanidins, which possess potent antioxidant properties and cellular signal-modulating activity14. Previous in vitro and in vivo studies have shown that cocoa polyphenols can inhibit cancer cell proliferation, induce apoptosis, and reduce the expression of angiogenic factors. This mechanism is thought to be related to the ability of cocoa flavonoids to inhibit tyrosine kinase receptor activation and disrupt GPCR signaling7, 13, 15.

Considering the pathogenesis pathways of skin cancer involving A2AR, VEGFR-2, and BCL-2, as well as the potential of cocoa bioactives in modulating these signals, this research is crucial16. The in vivo studies aim to provide a realistic biological picture of the effects of cocoa extract on suppressing skin cancer development, whereas molecular docking studies strengthen molecular evidence by analyzing ligand-protein interactions. The integration of these two approaches is expected to elucidate the potential of cocoa extract as a phytotherapy-based chemopreventive agent for skin cancer, while opening opportunities for the development of safer and more affordable cancer prevention strategies.

MATERIALS and METHOD

Research Type and Design

This study employed a pure experimental, post-test control-group design. The study was conducted in the Animal Laboratory of the Faculty of Medicine. Histopathological and immunohistochemical preparations were performed in the Anatomical Pathology Laboratory, while MDA levels were examined in the Microbiology Laboratory.

Ethical Approval

All procedures and experimental protocols involving animal subjects in this study were conducted in strict accordance with the internationally accepted principles for the care and use of laboratory animals. The research design was reviewed and formally approved by the Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Hasanuddin (approval number: 281/UN4.6.4.5.3.1/PP66/2024, date: 03.05.2025). Throughout the 12-week intervention period, every effort was made to minimize animal distress and to ensure that the housing, feeding, and sacrifice procedures followed the standard ethical guidelines for biomedical research.

Informed Patient Consent

Informed patient consent was not applicable to this study, as the research methodology did not involve human participants, human clinical records, or any human biological materials. The scope of this investigation was strictly confined to an in vivo experimental model using female Swiss mice and computational molecular docking simulations. Consequently, neither clinical trial registration nor human ethical consent forms were required for this study.

Cocoa Extract Preparation and Phytochemical Examination

Cocoa beans (Theobroma cacao L.) were obtained from a certified local plantation in Bantaeng Regency, South Sulawesi, Indonesia and were botanically authenticated by the Faculty of Mathematics and Natural Sciences at Universitas Hasanuddin, Makassar. The phytochemical profile of cocoa, particularly the concentration of flavonoids and polyphenols, can vary significantly depending on geographical origin, altitude, soil composition, and post-harvest processing methods. In this study, the selected beans were extracted by maceration with 96% ethanol (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). The resulting extract was thickened by solvent evaporation; then a 2000 ppm stock solution was prepared and diluted to 1600 ppm. The extract was prepared as a topical lotion for application to mouse skin. The total phenolic content was determined spectrophotometrically using gallic acid as a standard, and phytochemical screening was performed to identify flavonoids and polyphenols.

Molecular Docking Method

Molecular docking analysis was conducted to evaluate the interaction of the main bioactive compounds in cocoa (theobromine, epicatechin, and procyanidin) with the A2AR and VEGFR-2, which are receptors involved in the BCL-2 production pathway. The three-dimensional structures of the ligands were obtained from PubChem, while the structures of the target proteins were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank. Target protein identification was performed using the similarity ensemble approach. Protein and ligand preparation were performed using Avogadro Software, and protein preparation was performed using Swiss PDB Viewer. Docking was performed using the Vina Wizard in PyRx 0.8. The best results were selected based on the lowest binding energies and were visualized using PyMOL and Discovery Studio.

Experimental Animal Design and Intervention

Sample size was determined using the Federer equation. The sample comprised three groups of 10 female Swiss mice (total=30), with mice randomly assigned to control or treatment groups. The experimental animals were kept under standard conditions (temperature 28±2 °C, humidity 50±10%, 12-hour light-dark cycle) with a minimum adaptation period of one week.

7,12-dimethylbenz[a]anthracene (DMBA) (50 µg) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was administered three times during the first week (initiation), while 12-O-tetradecanoylphorbol-13-acetate (TPA) (4 µg) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), dissolved in acetone (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) was administered three times per week from the second to the twelfth week (promotion). The cocoa extract was applied to the shaved dorsal skin of the mice 20 minutes before application of DMBA or TPA. Twenty-four hours after the last treatment, the mice were sacrificed for tissue examination. The details of each group are as follows:

Group A (Negative Control): 10 mice without treatment.

Group B (DMBA + TPA): 10 mice with ethanol control + DMBA followed by TPA.

Group C (Cocoa + DMBA + TPA):10 mice divided into mice treated with 1600 ppm cocoa extract + DMBA followed by TPA.

MDA and BCL-2 Measurement

MDA levels were measured in epidermal microsomes using a commercial MDA Colorimetric Assay Kit (Elabscience Biotechnology Inc., Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol; absorbance was read at 532 nm. The decrease in MDA compared with the control indicated the antioxidant activity of the extract. BCL-2 expression was analyzed by streptavidin-biotin peroxidase immunohistochemistry using the EnVision+ Dual Link System-HRP (Dako, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and a primary mouse monoclonal anti-BCL-2 antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and was assessed based on the proportion of positive cells and staining intensity, with scores of negative, +1, and +2.

To visualize the spatial distribution of lipid peroxidation, immunohistochemical staining for MDA-protein adducts was conducted alongside the quantitative colorimetric assay. Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded skin sections (4 µm thick) were deparaffinized and rehydrated. Antigen retrieval was performed using a citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a microwave for 15 minutes. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H2O2. The tissue sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary rabbit polyclonal anti-MDA antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at a 1:200 dilution. Detection was achieved using the EnVision + Dual Link System-HRP (Dako, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and visualized with the 3,3’-diaminobenzidine chromogens, which produces a brown precipitate at sites of lipid peroxidation. Finally, the slides were counterstained with hematoxylin.

Statistical Analysis

Data on MDA levels, BCL-2 expression, and histopathological features were tabulated and analyzed using SPSS v.25. A normality test was performed before selecting statistical tests. Numerical data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by a post-hoc LSD test, whereas ordinal data were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. A p-value of <0.05 was considered significant.

RESULTS

Phytochemical Screening

Phytochemical screening was conducted to identify active compounds in cocoa extract with potential anticancer activity. The results showed that cocoa extract at a concentration of 1600 ppm contained 118.72 µg of flavonoids and 66.24 mg of polyphenols.

Molecular Docking

A molecular docking analysis was conducted to determine the anticancer potential of cocoa extract. We evaluated proteins, native ligands, and potential ligands from cocoa for their ability to interact with anticancer target proteins. The results of the analysis are shown in Table 1. The results of the docking validation show that A2AR and VEGFR-2 are target proteins. Their native ligands are theophylline and axitinib, with binding affinities of -6.0 and -11.9, respectively. The evaluation of phytochemical compounds in cocoa extract as ligands is presented in Table 1. The results show that Theobromine has binding affinities of -5.9 and -4.7 to A2AR and IL-8, respectively, indicating its potential inhibitory activity. In addition, procyanidin and (+) epicatechin have binding affinities (-8.3 and -8.4) similar to that of the native ligand of VEGFR-2.

Theobromine may inhibit A2AR through the same mechanism as theophylline, namely by forming a hydrogen bond with ASN253. A similar interaction was found with caffeine, a xanthine compound capable of inhibiting adenosine receptor A2. The binding affinities of theophylline and theobromine are similar (Figure 1A-C).

Procyanidin (Figure 1 D-F) and (+)-epicatechin (Figure 2 A-C) can bind to CYS919, the anchoring site of the reference ligand axitinib, a VEGFR2 inhibitor. Similar interactions with axitinib that may strengthen the binding of procyanidin and (+) epicatechin, including LEU840 and PHE1047. No crystallographic structure of IL-8 bound to inhibitors is available, so the docking results are compared with those from other molecular docking studies. Similar results were obtained by Boreak et al.10 A hydrogen- bond interaction with THR35 was observed. However, the binding affinity of Theobromine is much lower than other confirmed inhibitors (Figure 2 D-F).

Cocoa Extract Inhibits BCL-2 Expression

This study demonstrated that administration of 1600 ppm cocoa extract before induction with DBMA and TPA reduced BCL-2 immunohistochemical expression, and the difference between groups was significant (p=0.01). Figure 3 shows that in the 1600 ppm cocoa extract group, there were 6 samples with negative BCL-2 expression (0) and 4 samples with BCL-2 +1; these counts are significantly lower than those in the DMBA + TPA group, which had 2 BCL-2 negative samples, 4 samples with BCL-2 +1, and 4 samples with BCL-2 +2 (p=0.035). The reduction of this anti-apoptotic protein is evident as decreased intensity and frequency of brown cytoplasmic staining in the cocoa-treated epidermal cells (Figure 4).

Cocoa Extract Inhibits MDA Levels

Analysis of MDA levels showed a significant difference between study groups (p<0.001). Further analysis using post-hoc LSD showed that cocoa extract significantly inhibited MDA levels in the intervention group (1600 ppm cocoa) compared to the DMBA + TPA group, with a ratio of 1.688±1.638 versus 3.67±0.606 (p=0.001), as shown in Figure 5 below. The biochemical suppression of lipid peroxidation was further corroborated by immunohistochemical spatial analysis targeting MDA-protein adducts. Microscopic evaluation revealed a marked decrease in intensely stained cells (+1, low score) in the epidermal layer of the cocoa-extract group, indicating reduced structural oxidative damage. This contrasted sharply with the extensive and intense brown staining (+2, high score) observed across the hyperplastic epidermis in the DMBA + TPA positive control group (Figure 6).

Post-hoc analysis of Supplementary Table 1 revealed significantly higher tissue MDA levels in the DMBA group compared with the Negative Control group [mean difference (MD)=2.512, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.441-3.583, p<0.001], corresponding to a very large effect size (Cohen’s d=2.15). Similarly, the DMBA group exhibited significantly higher MDA levels than the cocoa-treated group (MD=2.028, 95% CI: 0.957-3.099, p=0.001), with a very large effect size (Cohen’s d=1.74). In contrast, no significant difference was observed between the cocoa-treated and Negative Control groups (MD=0.484, 95% CI: -0.587 to 1.555, p=0.362), indicating a small-to-moderate effect size (Cohen’s d=0.41).

Cocoa Extract in Repairing Histopathological Tissue Damage

The results of this study indicate that samples with cocoa extract have a lower percentage and lower severity of tissue dysplasia, with the intergroup comparison statistically significant (p<0.001). A Mann-Whitney analysis showed that the 1600 ppm cocoa group had a lower extent of tissue dysplasia than the DMBA + TPA group without cocoa, which had 6 samples with severe dysplasia and 4 samples with SCC (p=0.002), as shown in Figure 7. These protective effects are visually evident in the tissue architecture, where the cocoa-treated group maintained a more orderly epidermal structure with mild dysplasia, sharply contrasting the marked hyperplasia, nuclear pleomorphism, and invasive characteristics seen in the DMBA + TPA group (Figure 8).

Macroscopic Appearance

Macroscopic examination of the dorsal skin 24 hours after the final TPA application revealed severe erythema, scaling, and multiple papillomas or nodules characteristic of SCC in the DMBA + TPA group. In contrast, mice pretreated with 1600 ppm cocoa extract exhibited only mild erythema with minimal or no visible nodular lesions, as shown in Figure 9.

DISCUSSION

The results of this study demonstrated clear, sequential mechanism by which cocoa extract may interrupt the carcinogenic cascade in the skin. The process of skin carcinogenesis is initiated by severe DMBA-induced oxidative stress, which was reflected in the elevated MDA levels observed in the DMBA + TPA group8. As reported in previous studies, oxidative stress not only causes direct cellular damage but also promotes a tumor-supportive microenvironment characterized by inflammatory and angiogenic signaling6, 7, 11. Within this interconnected network, activation of surface receptors such as VEGFR-2 and A2AR, together with increased IL-8 activity, contributes to tumor progression by enhancing cell survival and resistance to apoptosis through downstream mediators including BCL-26, 7, 11.

Cocoa extract appears to exert a multitarget protective effect across this pathogenic axis. First, its rich flavonoid and polyphenol content acts as a potent antioxidant, neutralizing ROS and suppressing lipid peroxidation, thereby reducing MDA levels8, 17, 18. In parallel, our docking analysis demonstrated that cocoa bioactive compounds, particularly epicatechin, procyanidin, and theobromine, interact favorably with VEGFR-2 and A2AR11, 19. Through modulation of these signaling pathways, cocoa may attenuate the inflammatory, angiogenic, and anti-apoptotic responses that support carcinogenesis, including pathways associated with IL-8 activity and BCL-2 overexpression10, 12. This interconnected mechanism is consistent with the findings of Bhosale et al.13(who highlighted that natural polyphenols can simultaneously alleviate oxidative stress and modulate multiple cancer-related signaling pathways, resulting in suppression of downstream survival mechanisms. Therefore, the observed reduction in MDA and BCL-2 expression, together with the attenuation of dysplastic changes, may represent the biological consequence of cocoa extract interfering with several interconnected processes involved in skin carcinogenesis12, 20.

Our phytochemical analysis confirmed that the 1600 ppm cocoa extract is rich in flavonoids (118.72 µg) and polyphenols (66.24 mg). These flavan-3-ols, including epicatechin and procyanidin, act as primary defenses against DMBA-induced oxidative stress by scavenging ROS and stabilizing peroxyl radicals21. The direct clinical impact of this antioxidant capacity was evident in our in vivo results, in which cocoa extract significantly suppressed MDA levels compared to the DMBA + TPA group (p=0.001). This is consistent with Zulkifli et al.8, who demonstrated that cocoa extract potently reduced MDA levels by inhibiting lipid peroxidation propagation and enhancing endogenous antioxidant enzymes.

Beyond direct ROS scavenging, our molecular docking analysis revealed that theobromine, a key cocoa alkaloid, effectively binds to A2AR (binding affinity -5.9) and to IL-8. A2AR overactivation during tissue hypoxia typically accelerates cancer cell proliferation via the cAMP-PKA pathway and induces pro-inflammatory IL-8 secretion. By antagonizing A2AR, theobromine suppresses this inflammatory and proliferative signaling. This finding aligns with Cheng et al.6 validating the potential of xanthine derivatives to modulate purinergic receptors in neoplastic microenvironments22, 23, 24.

Furthermore, pathological angiogenesis is a critical step in SCC progression, primarily mediated by VEGFR-2. Our molecular docking studies demonstrated that procyanidin and (+)-epicatechin bind robustly to the CYS919 anchoring site of VEGFR-2, exhibiting binding affinities of -8.3 and -8.4, comparable to the reference inhibitor axitinib. By physically obstructing VEGFR-2, these polyphenols prevent the VEGF-induced endothelial proliferation that tumors rely on for nutrient supply. These computational findings are strongly supported by in vitro evidence from Moyle et al.11, which showed that epicatechin successfully inhibits VEGFR-2 phosphorylation and endothelial migration.

The successful inhibition of these upstream inflammatory (A2AR/IL-8) and angiogenic (VEGFR-2) pathways ultimately results in the regulation of cellular apoptosis. Our immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated that cocoa extract significantly downregulated the anti-apoptotic protein BCL-2 (p=0.035). Because upstream signaling from VEGFR-2 and A2AR normally upregulates BCL-2 to confer tumor survival, the observed interaction of cocoa bioactive compounds with these receptors may partially explain the reduction of BCL-2 expression observed in vivo. This pro-apoptotic shift is corroborated by Sitarek et al. (2024), who reported that flavonoid interventions successfully decrease BCL-2 expression while increasing Bax, thereby restoring apoptotic sensitivity in mutated cells12, 18, 25.

Taken together, the synergistic effects of upstream receptor blockade, oxidative stress reduction, and apoptosis restoration culminated in significant tissue protection. Histopathological evaluation showed that pretreatment with cocoa extract restricted DMBA/TPA-induced damage to mild dysplasia, thereby completely preventing the progression to SCC observed in the positive controls (p=0.002). This robust in vivo outcome strongly supports previous studies, such as Kim et al.20, highlighting the comprehensive protective properties of cocoa bioactives against environmental skin damage and malignant transformation.

Study Limitations

A limitation of this study is that detailed in vitro and in vivo analyses of potential mediators involved in the pharmacodynamic mechanisms of cocoa extract against skin cancer have not been conducted; studies of mediators such as VEGFR, IL-8, and A2AR remain limited to in silico studies. A comprehensive evaluation of the phytochemical compounds present in cocoa extract, other than flavonoids and polyphenols, has not been conducted. Further research is expected to improve upon this study.

CONCLUSION

Cocoa extract at a concentration of 1600 ppm has been shown to exert chemopreventive activity against skin cancer through several mechanisms, namely significant flavonoid and polyphenol content, theobromine-mediated inhibition of A2AR activation and IL-8 expression, and procyanidin- and epicatechin-mediated inhibition of VEGFR-2 signaling. In addition, cocoa extract suppresses BCL-2 expression, reduces MDA levels, and prevents more severe tissue dysplasia compared with the DMBA + TPA control group. These results indicate that cocoa extract has the potential to act as a natural chemopreventive agent against skin carcinogenesis through antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and pro-apoptotic mechanisms.
