Genetic diversity and host-symbiont cophylogenetic analysis of Neorickettsia endobacteria of Fasciola hepatica
Authors: Pawan Kumar, Young-Jun Choi, Martha V Fernandez-Baca, Maria Luisa Morales, Pedro Ortiz, Cristian Hoban, Miguel M Cabada, Makedonka Mitreva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeorickettsia are obligate intracellular bacteria that persist as endosymbionts of digenean trematodes and include several vertebrate pathogens. However, many digenean-associated lineages remain poorly characterized, including Neorickettsia associated with the liver fluke Fasciola hepatica (nFh). Here, we present a large-scale population genomic analysis of nFh to assess its prevalence, genetic diversity, evolutionary history, and transmission dynamics. We screened whole-genome sequencing data from 502 F. hepatica samples collected across Peru, the UK, Uruguay, and the USA, detecting Neorickettsia infection in all regions and an overall prevalence of approximately 17% in Peru. From these data, we generated 77 reference-guided nFh consensus genomes. nFh exhibited low nucleotide diversity (π = 9.1 × 10−5), consistent with an obligate intracellular lifestyle. Phylogenetic analyses revealed a clear divergence between UK and American nFh lineages and two closely related haplotype clusters within South America. Cophylogenetic analyses showed significant congruence between nFh and host nuclear phylogenies, but not mitochondrial phylogenies, supporting long-term host association with mixed transmission modes beyond strict transovarial inheritance. Analysis of host kinship and endosymbiont genotype sharing further supported both vertical inheritance and horizontal transmission within vertebrate hosts. nFh infection was significantly associated with triclabendazole resistance in F. hepatica from Cusco, Peru, but not from Cajamarca, indicating region-specific associations between endosymbiont infection and drug resistance. Together, these findings establish nFh as a widely distributed endosymbiont of F. hepatica with low genetic diversity, geographic population structure, and mixed transmission modes, with potential implications for the epidemiology of fascioliasis and anthelmintic resistance.Keywords: Neorickettsia, Endosymbiosis, Fasciola hepatica, Fascioliasis, Population genomics, Cophylogeny, Triclabendazole resistance, One health

Introduction

Members of the genus Neorickettsia (Rickettsiales: Anaplasmataceae) are obligate intracellular Proteobacteria that exist as endosymbionts of digenean trematodes. These flukes act as both reservoirs and vectors, facilitating vertical transmission of Neorickettsia through all trematode developmental stages and horizontal transmission to vertebrate hosts (Vaughan et al., 2012). Following transmission, Neorickettsia infects macrophages, monocytes, and other cell types, including intestinal epithelial cells, where it can cause systemic disease (Vaughan et al., 2012; Greiman et al., 2014).

The genus Neorickettsia was established after Philip (1955) demonstrated that salmon poisoning disease in dogs is caused by Neorickettsia helminthoeca, an endosymbiont of the digenean Nanophyetus salmincola (Philip, 1955; Vaughan et al., 2012). Since then, several pathogenic species have been described, including Neorickettsia risticii, the etiological agent of Potomac horse fever (PHF), Neorickettsia findlayensis, which also causes PHF and elicits higher serological titers than N. risticii in experimentally infected horses, Neorickettsia elokominica, responsible for Elokomin fluke fever disease in bears, and Neorickettsia sennetsu, the causative agent of Sennetsu neorickettsiosis in humans (Dawson et al., 1987; Madigan and Pusterla, 2000; Dittrich et al., 2015; Greiman et al., 2016; Teymournejad et al., 2020). Collectively, these species demonstrate that digenean-associated species of Neorickettsia can infect a broad range of vertebrate hosts, including humans, horses, bears, and dogs.

In contrast to these well-characterized taxa, multiple provisional Neorickettsia lineages have been detected in diverse digenean hosts but remain taxonomically undescribed. These include genotypes associated with Fasciola hepatica, Plagiorchis elegans, and members of the family Lecithodendriidae (Greiman et al., 2014, 2017). For these lineages, fundamental questions regarding pathogenicity, host specificity, transmission efficiency, and evolutionary persistence remain unresolved, representing a major gap in our understanding of Neorickettsia diversity and epidemiological significance (Greiman et al., 2017). We previously reported the first genomic characterization of a Neorickettsia symbiont associated with F. hepatica (hereafter nFh) (McNulty et al., 2017). Using PCR and immunohistochemical labelling (PeNsp-3), we demonstrated the presence of nFh across multiple life stages of F. hepatica and within reproductive and ingestion-associated tissue, consistent with vertical maintenance and potential transmission to vertebrate hosts. Phylogenetic analysis based on 16S rRNA gene sequences and 473 conserved single-copy proteins placed nFh closer to N. risticii and N. sennetsu than to N. helminthoeca (McNulty et al., 2017). While this work established the existence of a stable trans-kingdom symbiosis between F. hepatica and Neorickettsia, it did not assess the prevalence, geographical distribution, or population genetic diversity of this association.

Here, we address these gaps by screening 502 F. hepatica isolates from diverse geographical regions for nFh and by generating 77 nFh consensus genomes. Using comparative genomics and phylogenetic analyses, we assess the prevalence, genetic diversity, and population structure of nFh. We further apply host-symbiont cophylogenetic approaches to test for patterns of co-divergence between F. hepatica and nFh. Together, these analyses provide new insights into the transmission dynamics, evolutionary history, and epidemiological significance of this symbiosis.

Materials and methods

Identification of Neorickettsia DNA in Fasciola hepatica whole-genome sequencing data

To investigate the prevalence of Neorickettsia infection in F. hepatica, we screened whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data from previously published studies spanning multiple geographical regions. In total, 496 individual adult samples and 6 pooled egg samples of F. hepatica from Peru (Choi et al., 2020, 2025; Jung et al., unpublished data), the UK (Cwiklinski et al., 2015; Beesley et al., 2023), Uruguay (Choi et al., 2020), and the USA (McNulty et al., 2017) were included (Supplementary Table S1). Raw sequencing data were assessed for quality using FastQC v.0.11.8 (Andrews, 2010). Adapter sequences and low-quality bases were removed using Trimmomatic v.0.39 (Bolger et al., 2014). Trimmed reads were aligned to the reference Neorickettsia genome (GenBank: NZ_LNGI01000001.1) (McNulty et al., 2017) using BWA v.0.7.17 (Li and Durbin, 2009). PCR and optical duplicate reads were identified and removed with Picard v.2.27.5 (http://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/). To minimize false-positive alignments, reads containing soft- or hard-clipped regions were excluded using Samtools v.1.21 (-e 'sclen == 0 && hclen == 0') (Danecek et al., 2021). Additional filtering was performed using Bamtools v.2.5.1 (http://github.com/pezmaster31/bamtools), retaining reads with an alignment score (AS tag) greater than read length minus 20 (-tag 'AS:>read length - 20') and a minimum mapping quality of 10 (-mapQuality '>=10'). For each sample, the proportion of the Neorickettsia reference genome covered at a minimum depth of 5× was calculated using Samtools v.1.21 (Danecek et al., 2021).

Consensus calling of Neorickettsia genomes

Consensus genotypes were generated for nFh genomes from 77 samples with greater than 95% genome coverage at a minimum depth of 5× using Samtools v.1.16 (-a --show-del yes --show-ins no --min-depth 5) (Danecek et al., 2021) (Supplementary Table S1). One high-coverage nFh sample (QAN053D1) was excluded from consensus calling because excessive heterozygosity in the F. hepatica nuclear genome (inbreeding coefficient = −0.045) suggested that the sample likely contained DNA contamination from another fluke sample (Choi et al., 2025). The distribution of non-ATCG characters at homologous positions across all consensus genomes was assessed using a custom Python script (Supplementary Text S1). Positions containing any non-ATCG characters were excluded from multiple-sequence alignments. Gap regions present in the reference assembly (NZ_LNGI01000001.1:63978-64166 and 571508-571509) were masked prior to downstream analyses.

Neorickettsia phylogenetic and F. hepatica-Neorickettsia cophylogenetic analyses

Maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenetic trees of nFh were constructed using IQ-TREE v.2.4.0 (Minh et al., 2020) with ultrafast bootstrap approximation (-B 10000). The best-fit nucleotide substitution model was selected using ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al., 2017). Phylogenetic trees were visualized using FigTree v.1.4.4 (Rambaut, 2018). Multiple sequence alignments in FASTA format were converted to NEXUS format using trimAl v.1.4.1 (Capella-Gutierrez et al., 2009), and median-joining haplotype networks were constructed and visualized using PopArt v.1.7 (Leigh and Bryant, 2015). To investigate phylogenetic congruence between nFh and F. hepatica, cophylogenetic analyses were conducted using both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes of F. hepatica. Nuclear single nucleotide variants were called and filtered as previously described (Choi et al., 2025; Jung et al., unpublished data). Samples identified as outliers in the original publications due to high genotype missingness or excessive heterozygosity were excluded, leaving 488 samples for phylogenetic analysis (Supplementary Table S1). Pairwise nuclear genetic distances among samples were calculated using VCF2Dis (Xu et al., 2025) after site filtering in PLINK v.1.90 (Purcell et al., 2007) with --geno 0.05 --mac 1. A neighbor-joining (NJ) tree was constructed from the resulting distance matrix to represent nuclear genomic relationships among F. hepatica isolates. Kinship analysis was performed using the KING method (Manichaikul et al., 2010), implemented in AKT v.0.3.3 (Arthur et al., 2017), as previously described (Choi et al., 2025). Phylogenetic tree and haplotype network construction based on F. hepatica mitochondrial genomes followed the approach described previously (Kumar et al., 2025). Host-symbiont cophylogenetic analyses were conducted using ParaFit (Legendre et al., 2002) (nperm = 99999, seed = 10, correction = “lingoes”, test.links = TRUE) to assess patterns of genetic co-divergence between F. hepatica hosts and nFh symbionts and to test the statistical significance of congruence between host and symbiont phylogenies.

Results

Prevalence and distribution of Neorickettsia infection in Fasciola hepatica

The presence of Neorickettsia DNA in F. hepatica samples from diverse geographical regions (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S1) was assessed by aligning WGS data to the nFh reference genome (GenBank: NZ_LNGI01000001.1). The analyzed datasets comprised WGS data generated from adult F. hepatica specimens collected from animal and human hosts in Peru (n = 475), the UK (n = 11), Uruguay (n = 9), and the USA (n = 1), as well as pooled egg samples collected from animal infections in the UK (n = 6). Across all datasets, 88 F. hepatica samples contained Neorickettsia-derived reads covering more than 10% of the reference genome at a minimum depth of 5×. These samples were classified as Neorickettsia-positive (nFh+). Based on this criterion, Neorickettsia prevalence was 81/475 in Peru, 4/17 in the UK (including adult and egg samples), 1/1 in the USA, and 2/9 in Uruguay (Table 1). Within Peru, the difference in nFh prevalence between Cajamarca (18.90%) and Cusco (16.08%) was not statistically significant (χ2 = 0.63, P = 0.43), with an overall prevalence of 17.05% across the two regions (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S2). Neighbor-joining phylogenetic analysis based on F. hepatica nuclear genetic variation (Fig. 1A) revealed a tree topology consistent with a highly structured population, with limited gene flow between Cusco and Cajamarca, in agreement with principal component and admixture analyses (Jung et al., unpublished data). Within each region, nFh-infected samples did not form distinct clusters but were distributed across host nuclear genotypes, providing no evidence for lineage-specific clustering of nFh infection. In addition, Neorickettsia infection was not associated with host mitochondrial haplogroups in either Cajamarca (χ2 = 0.50, P = 0.92) or Cusco (χ2 = 3.22, P = 0.52) (Supplementary Table S2). This lack of association was also evident in the mitochondrial haplotype network, in which nFh-infected and uninfected samples were interspersed across haplotypes (Fig. 1B).

Neorickettsia genetic diversity

Of the 88 nFh+ samples, 77 contained sufficient nFh-derived reads to achieve more than 95% coverage of the 859,205 bp Neorickettsia reference genome at a minimum depth of 5×, thereby supporting reference-guided consensus calling and genotyping of individual nFh genomes (Table 1). Across these samples, 430 sites exhibited single nucleotide polymorphisms (0.05%), of which 185 were parsimony informative (0.02%). Maximum-likelihood phylogenetic analysis and a median-joining haplotype network (Fig. 2) based on the full genome showed that nFh samples from the UK formed an outgroup to all remaining samples originating in Americas and were separated by 129 single nucleotide variants. Haplotypes from the Americas clustered around two central nodes, which we designated as Cluster I and Cluster II (Fig. 2). Cluster I comprises 32 haplotypes from Cajamarca, Peru (n = 20), Cusco, Peru (n = 9), Uruguay (n = 2), and the USA (n = 1). Cluster II contains 28 haplotypes, all originating from the Cusco region of Peru. Although the central ancestral nodes of the two clusters differ by only 1 bp, the central node of Cluster I is closer to the UK samples than that of Cluster II.

Average pairwise nucleotide diversity (π) across the 77 nFh samples was 9.1 × 10−5. Nucleotide diversity was compared across F. hepatica nuclear genomes, F. hepatica mitochondrial genomes, and Neorickettsia genomes from Cajamarca and Cusco, Peru. Neorickettsia showed low nucleotide diversity in both Cajamarca (π = 1.2 × 10−5) and Cusco (π = 1.2 × 10−5), relative to F. hepatica nuclear genomes from Cajamarca (π = 6.4 × 10−3) and Cusco (π = 6.6 × 10−3) and mitochondrial genomes from Cajamarca (π = 1.8 × 10−3) and Cusco (π = 3.2 × 10−3) (Table 2). Nucleotide diversity in F. hepatica nuclear and mitochondrial genomes was comparable between nFh-infected and uninfected groups (Table 2).

Association between Neorickettsia infection and triclabendazole resistance

We evaluated whether the triclabendazole (TCBZ) resistance phenotype in F. hepatica was associated with nFh infection. Adult samples with known TCBZ sensitivity phenotypes from Cusco (Choi et al., 2025) and Cajamarca (Jung et al., unpublished data) were analyzed to assess associations between drug resistance and nFh infection status. TCBZ sensitivity was assessed using the same in vitro assay and phenotypic criteria for both Cusco and Cajamarca samples (Fernandez-Baca et al., 2022), allowing direct comparison between regions. No significant association was detected in samples from Cajamarca (χ2 = 0.062, P = 0.81) (Table 3). In contrast, samples from Cusco showed a significant association between nFh infection and the TCBZ-resistant phenotype (χ2 = 10.1, P = 0.0015) (Table 3). Consistent with this regional pattern, Neorickettsia haplotype clusters also differed significantly in their distribution of TCBZ phenotypes. Cluster I contained 13 TCBZ-sensitive and 15 TCBZ-resistant haplotypes, whereas Cluster II contained 4 TCBZ-sensitive and 24 TCBZ-resistant haplotypes (Fig. 3). This difference in TCBZ phenotype distribution between the two clusters was statistically significant (χ2 = 6.84, P = 0.0089).

Phylogenetic relationship between the Neorickettsia endosymbiont and its host Fasciola hepatica

To investigate the evolutionary history of Neorickettsia in relation to its digenean host, we conducted a cophylogenetic analysis comparing the nFh phylogeny with that of F. hepatica. ParaFit analysis revealed significant congruence between the nFh phylogeny and the nuclear genome phylogeny of F. hepatica (P = 2 × 10−5, ParaFitGlobal = 0.11) (Fig. 4). In contrast, no significant congruence was detected between the mitochondrial phylogeny of F. hepatica and the nFh phylogeny (P = 0.4, ParaFitGlobal = 7.0 × 10−5) (Fig. 5).

Neorickettsia transmission pathways inferred from genotype sharing patterns

Using kinship coefficients calculated for pairs of F. hepatica samples, we classified each pair according to its expected genetic relationship and quantified the genetic distance between their corresponding Neorickettsia endosymbionts (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Table S3). Two F. hepatica sample pairs exhibited kinship coefficients (φ) greater than 0.354, consistent with self-relationships. Both pairs were collected from the same vertebrate host animals, indicating that they likely represent clonemates arising from clumped transmission. In both cases, the paired F. hepatica samples carried identical Neorickettsia genotypes, consistent with a vertical transmission model in which endosymbionts are inherited from a common infected parent. Three F. hepatica sample pairs displayed φ values between 0.022 and 0.354, consistent with first-to third-degree relationships. Among these, one pair carried identical Neorickettsia genotypes, potentially reflecting shared ancestry through vertical transmission. All remaining F. hepatica sample pairs showed φ values below 0.022, consistent with the samples being unrelated. Among unrelated sample pairs collected from different vertebrate host animals, the probability of carrying identical Neorickettsia genotypes was low at 0.51%. In contrast, when unrelated F. hepatica sample pairs were collected from the same vertebrate host animal, 38.9% of pairs (7 out of 18) carried identical Neorickettsia genotypes, despite the absence of a recent familial relationship between the host flukes. This marked difference in the likelihood of sharing identical Neorickettsia genotypes between unrelated sample pairs collected from the same versus different vertebrate hosts was statistically significant (binomial test, P = 2.0 × 10−14) and is consistent with a transmission model that includes both vertical inheritance and horizontal transmission of Neorickettsia within vertebrate hosts.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study provides the first large-scale population genomic assessment of Neorickettsia infection in F. hepatica across multiple geographical regions, including Peru, the UK, Uruguay and the USA. Based on WGS data (Cwiklinski et al., 2015; McNulty et al., 2017; Choi et al., 2020; Beesley et al., 2023; Choi et al., 2025; Jung et al., unpublished data) and conservative coverage criteria, we detected Neorickettsia-positive flukes in all regions represented in the dataset, with an overall prevalence of 17.05% in Peru (Table 1). This prevalence falls within the range reported for Neorickettsia infecton in other digenean systems. For example, 17.9% of cercariae-infected snails from Chungcheong and Jeolla provinces, Korea, tested positive for N. risticii (Chae et al., 2003), and 8% of Plagiorchis elegans from North Dakota, USA, were positive for N. risticii (Greiman et al., 2013). Collectively, these studies suggest that Neorickettsia prevalence among digeneans is variable and generally low, consistent with incomplete vertical transmission during asexual reproduction within snail hosts, possibly driven by low bacterial loads within sporocysts and limited bacterial replication (Greiman et al., 2013).

Within Peru, nFh prevalence did not differ significantly between Cajamarca and Cusco, despite pronounced nuclear genetic structuring of F. hepatica populations between these regions. This similarity in prevalence is notable given that Cajamarca (2750 m above sea level) and Cusco (3399 m above sea level) are separated by ∼1900 km, differ in climatic conditions, and have been reported to differ in human fascioliasis prevalence, at 9.6% and 5.1%, respectively (Murga-Moreno et al., 2025). Consistent with this pattern, nFh-infected samples did not cluster within host nuclear phylogenies (Fig. 1) and showed no association with host mitochondrial haplogroups (Supplementary Table S2), offering little support for lineage-specific susceptibility. Together, these findings suggest that nFh infection is broadly distributed across host genetic backgrounds and does not appear to be largely determined by host genotype.

Across geographically diverse samples, nFh genomes exhibited remarkably low nucleotide diversity (π = 9.1 × 10−5). This low diversity is broadly consistent with the reduced effective population size and limited recombination expected of obligate intracellular endosymbionts. However, it may also reflect the effects of relatively recent clonal expansion, purifying selection, or a long generation time coupled to the host life cycle (Akter et al., 2017). Despite this overall low diversity, phylogenetic analyses revealed a deep split between nFh from the UK and those from the Americas, with UK samples forming a clear outgroup separated by more than 100 single nucleotide variants (Fig. 2). This pattern is consistent with historical geographical isolation and limited intercontinental gene flow in host F. hepatica populations (Choi et al., 2020, 2025). Within the Americas, nFh haplotypes clustered into two closely related but distinct groups, designated Cluster I and Cluster II. Although the central haplotypes of these clusters differ by only a single nucleotide, their contrasting geographical distributions suggest recent diversification following colonization or expansion within South America. Population structure has also been observed in N. risticii, where South American strains form a cluster distinct from North American genotypes (Paulino et al., 2020), supporting the role of geographical isolation in shaping Neorickettsia genetic diversity at broader spatial scales. Nevertheless, within Peru, genetic differentiation among nFh was less pronounced than host population structure (Fig. 1, Fig. 2), suggesting that additional processes, such as population bottlenecks associated with vertical transmission, incomplete vertical transmission, or occasional horizontal transmission events, may contribute to homogenizing Neorickettsia diversity at regional scales.

Triclabendazole, widely used in veterinary practice, is currently the only WHO-recommended treatment for human fascioliasis. Its extensive use has contributed to reduced drug efficacy, posing a growing threat to both public health and the livestock industries in endemic regions (Esteban et al., 1999; Kelley et al., 2016; Gandhi et al., 2019; Morales et al., 2021). Previous work from our group indicated that TCBZ resistance in F. hepatica is associated with nuclear genetic variants and evolved independently in geographically diverse populations (Choi et al., 2025; Jung et al., unpublished data), but is not correlated with mitochondrial haplogroups (Kumar et al., 2025). A key finding of this study is the region-specific association between Neorickettsia infection and TCBZ resistance in F. hepatica. While no association was detected in Cajamarca, a statistically significant association was observed in Cusco, where Neorickettsia-positive flukes were more likely to be resistant to TCBZ (Table 3). This association was further supported by differences in resistance phenotypes between nFh haplotype clusters, with Cluster II showing an enrichment of resistant parasite samples (Fig. 3). These results do not establish a causal role for Neorickettsia in TCBZ resistance, but they raise several non-mutually exclusive hypotheses. First, Neorickettsia infection may directly or indirectly influence metabolic pathways, detoxification processes, cytoskeletal dynamics, or stress responses in F. hepatica that affect drug efficacy. One possible mechanism could involve effects on F. hepatica microtubules, the presumed target of TCBZ. Several intracellular bacteria are known to stabilize host microtubules to promote their survival and persistence, either through effector proteins that interact with microtubule subunits or by recruiting host cellular proteins that alter microtubule dynamics (Radhakrishnan and Splitter, 2012). If nFh similarly affects microtubule stability or associated pathways in F. hepatica, it could influence TCBZ susceptibility. Secondly, TCBZ-resistant parasites may also be more likely to harbor Neorickettsia. For example, resistant parasites may be more permissive to Neorickettsia internalization or replication, thereby facilitating horizontal transmission. Resistant parasites may also support higher bacterial loads, which could increase detection probability and reduce loss of the endosymbiont during vertical transmission. Studies of N. risticii have shown that host intracellular Ca2+ signaling (Rikihisa et al., 1995) and protein tyrosine phosphorylation (Zhang and Rikihisa, 1997) play essential roles in bacterial internalization, replication, and cell-to-cell spread. If TCBZ-resistant flukes differ from sensitive flukes in these host biological processes, these differences could contribute to the observed association between resistance and nFh infection, even in the absence of drug pressure. Thirdly, Neorickettsia infection and TCBZ resistance may be jointly favored under shared ecological or management conditions, such as repeated drug exposure or intensive livestock production. Because microtubule function is important for F. hepatica survival and influences Neorickettsia replication and intercellular spread (Rikihisa et al., 1994), resistant flukes that preserve microtubule function under drug pressure could favor both parasite survival and endosymbiont persistence. Lastly, the observed association may reflect hitchhiking of Neorickettsia lineages with particular F. hepatica nuclear backgrounds under selection in Cusco, even though nFh infection itself is not host-lineage specific at broader scales. However, unless the association is biologically favored or maintained by local epidemiological conditions, such hitchhiking would be expected to weaken over time when vertical transmission is incomplete and horizontal transmission can occur. The absence of a similar association in Cajamarca underscores the importance of local context and cautions against generalizing the Cusco pattern without further investigation. Because both the Cusco and Cajamarca genomic datasets were obtained from adult flukes collected from cattle, the region-specific association between nFh infection and TCBZ resistance does not appear to be driven by differences in the vertebrate host species from which the flukes were collected. Nevertheless, vertebrate host species remain an important variable for future study because cattle, sheep, and humans may differ in parasite exposure, triclabendazole use, infection intensity, and opportunities for within-host nFh transmission. Controlled experiments and functional studies will be necessary to determine whether the observed association reflects a causal biological relationship, an indirect correlation, or a confounding epidemiological pattern.

Interestingly, among the six UK egg samples from a field F. hepatica population collected from sheep showing TCBZ treatment failure (Beesley et al., 2023), four samples were nFh+, three of which were post-treatment samples (Supplementary Table S1). This pattern suggests enrichment of Neorickettsia DNA after TCBZ treatment and may indicate a possible association between nFh infection and TCBZ resistance in the UK dataset. In contrast, nine of the 11 UK adult samples were from TCBZ-resistant populations, yet Neorickettsia DNA was not detected in any of them. This contrasting pattern may reflect the small sample size, differences in detection sensitivity, or both. The egg samples were pooled, whereas the adult samples represented individual flukes or portions of individual flukes (Cwiklinski et al., 2015; Beesley et al., 2023). Pooling eggs from multiple parasites could increase the probability that at least one nFh-infected maternal lineage is represented. In addition, nFh may be unevenly distributed across adult tissues. Our previous immunolocalization study detected nFh in reproductive tissues, eggs, the oral sucker, and the tegument (McNulty et al., 2017). Thus, DNA extraction from only a portion of an adult fluke may miss infected tissues or produce a lower bacterial-to-host DNA ratio than an egg pool enriched for material derived from reproductive tissues. More broadly, sample sizes in the non-Peruvian datasets remain limited, constraining interpretation of country-specific patterns. Expanded geographical sampling will therefore be important for assessing whether these patterns are generalizable beyond the Peruvian datasets.

Cophylogenetic analyses revealed significant congruence between the Neorickettsia phylogeny and the nuclear, but not the mitochondrial, phylogeny of F. hepatica (Fig. 4, Fig. 5). This pattern supports a long-term association between nFh and host nuclear lineages and is consistent with repeated vertical transmission over evolutionary time. In contrast, the lack of mitochondrial congruence suggests that endosymbiont evolutionary history is not strictly governed by maternal inheritance through transovarial transmission. These findings are consistent with observations from other bacterial symbiont systems, where mixed transmission modes complicate expectations of strict cospeciation (Russell et al., 2017). Accordingly, the observed nuclear congruence likely reflects population-level coupling rather than complete host and symbiont codiversification, permitting episodic horizontal transmission while maintaining long-term host association.

Horizontal transmission has been documented for several Neorickettsia species, including N. risticii and N. helminthoeca, which cause disease in vertebrate hosts (Nyberg et al., 1967; Gibson et al., 2005). Using immunolocalization, we previously detected Neorickettsia in the eggs and reproductive tissues of adult F. hepatica, consistent with vertical transmission, as well as in the oral sucker and tegument, tissues that directly interface with the vertebrate host and may permit horizontal transfer (McNulty et al., 2017). Similar localization patterns have been reported in P. elegans, where Neorickettsia is detected in reproductive and somatic tissues of the fluke and in the small intestinal tissues of the hamster host, supporting the plausibility of mixed transmission pathways, and heavy colonization of male reproductive tissues has further suggested the possibility of fluke-to-fluke transmission via mating (Fischer et al., 2017). In light of this evidence, our genomic analyses integrating host kinship estimates with endosymbiont genotype comparisons indicate that Neorickettsia transmission in F. hepatica likely involves both vertical and horizontal components. Neorickettsia genotype sharing among clonemates and closely related flukes is compatible with inheritance from a common infected parent, whereas the elevated frequency of identical Neorickettsia genotypes among unrelated flukes collected from the same vertebrate host is not readily explained by host fluke genetic relatedness alone and is consistent with transmission occurring within the vertebrate host environment. Together, these observations are compatible with a transmission system in which vertical inheritance contributes to long-term host association, while occasional horizontal transmission may influence local persistence and spread. They also raise questions regarding the host range of the Neorickettsia endosymbiont of F. hepatica, including its occurrence in other digenean or vertebrate hosts, and why documented infections in animal or human fascioliasis remain limited. Further clarification of nFh transmission ecology will be important for evaluating its potential relevance to animal and human health within a One Health framework.

Conclusions

This study provides evidence that nFh is a genetically structured and widely distributed endosymbiont of F. hepatica with complex transmission dynamics and possible relevance to drug resistance. By integrating population genomic analyses, cophylogenetic comparisons, and host kinship information, we offer insights into how microbial symbionts may interact with parasitic helminths across ecological and evolutionary scales. Future work will be needed to experimentally validate transmission routes, functionally characterize host-endosymbiont interactions, and expand geographical sampling to assess the generality of these observations. Given the medical and veterinary importance of F. hepatica, improved understanding of microbial endosymbionts may inform parasite control strategies and contribute to understanding the evolution of anthelmintic resistance.
