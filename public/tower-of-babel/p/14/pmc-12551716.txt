Crystallization-activated moisture barrier for high-tolerance manufacturing of perovskite solar cells
Authors: Yabin Ma, Chao Luo, Changling Zhan, Keli Wang, Jiandong He, Peng Gao, Zhuye Bi, Yingzhuang Ma, Qing Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Moisture promotes perovskite crystallization during annealing but only within a narrow humidity range, hindering the stable mass production of perovskite solar cells, especially for formamidinium lead iodide. To overcome this, we integrated a “crystallization-activated moisture barrier” into the perovskite film, which can exist in a liquid state/crystallization phase during the initial annealing to allow moisture to enter and promote crystallization, then migrate upward, and form a dense hydrophobic layer on the film surface to protect the perovskite from high humidity–caused damage. Such a “moisture barrier” enables the manufacturing of perovskite films to be insensitive to a wide range of relative humidity from 20% to over 90%. The barrier also regulates bulk crystallization and passivates surface defects. The crystallization-activated moisture barrier effectively reduces the sensitivity of perovskite films to humidity during annealing, which paves the way for high-tolerance manufacturing of perovskite photovoltaic devices.

INTRODUCTION

Metal halide perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have gained extensive attention from both academia and industry due to their exceptional optoelectronic properties (1, 2). Solving the challenges associated with mass manufacturing from the laboratory to industrialization is the key for PSCs toward commercialization (3). Currently, because of the ideal bandgap of formamidinium lead iodide (FAPbI3), the certified power conversion efficiencies (PCEs) of FAPbI3-based PSCs have reached 27% (4). Conventional processes involve an annealing step of the perovskite film under controlled humidity conditions [relative humidity (%RH) = 30 to 40%], as appropriate moisture can facilitate perovskite crystallization (5–9). However, moisture is known to act as a double-edged sword during annealing. Excessive moisture will lead to the escape of organic cations and collapse of the inorganic framework, which will, in turn, worsen the properties of the perovskite films (7). Furthermore, the collapse of the crystal structure can cause iodide ions (I−) to migrate and transform into iodine (I2) under light and thermal stress, further contributing to irreversible degradation and performance loss of the perovskite structures (10). Since annealing is a necessary step in the preparation of perovskite films, it is therefore essential to address the challenges faced in perovskite film fabrication in laboriously creating a narrow annealing humidity window to facilitate the commercialization of PSCs.

To address this challenge, many additives, such as ionic liquids, polymers, ester solvents, and alkyl ammonium halide have been introduced into perovskite precursors to mitigate the effects of moisture (11–15). Yan et al. (16) eliminated vacancy sites through the addition of guanidine benzyl acetate based on the critical role of vacancies in the hydrolysis of perovskite. Meng et al. (17) introduced the ionic pair stabilizer dimethylammonium formate to inhibit the oxidation of iodide ions and the deprotonation of organic cations, notably enhancing the efficiency of PSCs under ambient conditions. Liu et al. (5) developed a cover layer by introducing a chlorine-containing organic molecule, which effectively prevented water infiltration and preserved the stability of the dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)–PbI2 complex, even at RH levels of up to 80%, thus enabling the reproducible fabrication of PSCs. Kim et al. (18) used long alkyl chain octylammonium iodide (OAI) for posttreatment of perovskite films, resulting in the formation of out-of-plane low-dimensional perovskite on their surfaces (mainly distributed near grain boundaries), which plays a role in blocking electrons and improving the moisture resistance of perovskite films. In addition, Kim et al. (18) explored the interrelationship between structural features and their functions in surfactant-modified perovskites. They introduced OAI into the precursor solution of CH3NH3PbI3 and found that OA cations could encapsulate individual perovskite particles to form three-dimensional perovskite domains. This armor-like effect can effectively protect individual perovskite particles from moisture erosion and maintain charge transfer characteristics between particles (19). However, these efforts were mainly devoted to the preparation of perovskite film in atmospheric environment at a certain humidity, while a study on preventing the decomposition of perovskite from the outset of annealing process remained largely unexplored. Addressing the dual nature of humidity, using it to promote the crystallization of perovskite films while preventing high humidity from causing damage to the films is crucial but rarely reported.

Here, a barrier molecule, dodecyltrimethylammonium bromide (DTAB), was introduced into the perovskite precursor solution as a moisture barrier. During the annealing of the spin-coated perovskite precursor film in ambient air, moisture could freely enter the perovskite film to promote the transformation into the α-perovskite phase. Meanwhile, the barrier molecules inside the film could also effectively regulate the perovskite crystallization. Once the process of promoting perovskite crystallization is completed, the α-perovskite phase forms within seconds. Because of their large molecular size, DTAB molecules cannot incorporate into the perovskite lattice and therefore segregate toward grain boundaries and the film surface during crystallization. Therefore, a hydrophobic layer will form to close the channel for high-humidity moisture to enter the perovskite film, thereby protecting the perovskite film from degradation. As a result, the optimized PSC achieved a 25.33% PCE and an 84.93% fill factor (FF). The unencapsulated PSC exhibited notably improved stability by maintaining 94.6% of its initial PCE when aged under ambient air (ISOS-D-1) for 2602 hours. The device also maintained 86.5 and 84.9% of its initial PCE under exposure to sunlight (ISOS-L-1) for 566 hours and under conditions of 85°C and 85% RH (ISOS-D-3) for 350 hours, respectively.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Effect of humidity on the preparation of perovskite films

First, perovskite films were annealed in two different environments—a nitrogen environment and an ambient atmosphere with 40% RH—to examine the effect of humidity on the preparation of perovskite films. The perovskite film annealed in humid air exhibited a smoother surface, slightly larger grains, and a lower defect density, as evidenced by a narrower full width at half maximum (FWHM) in x-ray diffraction (XRD), increased photoluminescence (PL) intensity, and a prolonged time-resolved PL (TRPL) lifetime compared to the sample annealed in nitrogen (more details are provided in fig. S1 and table S1). Therefore, it is essential to anneal the perovskite film in a humid atmosphere to achieve PSCs with enhanced performance. However, its effectiveness could be largely influenced by seasonal fluctuations in environmental humidity. Consequently, the development of an annealing strategy with strong environmental adaptability is of considerable research value and practical significance for achieving the reproducibility and stability of PSCs.

Because of variations in latitude, longitude, terrain elevation, and seasonal climate fluctuations across different regions, it is highly challenging to maintain consistent air humidity conditions. Usually, the most suitable humidity condition for air annealing of perovskite films is ~40% RH (5, 6, 20–24). Next, the perovskite films were annealed at different RH levels (the methods for creating these humidity conditions are shown in fig. S2) to simulate different seasonal environments. From scanning electron microscope (SEM) images (Fig. 1D), it can be observed that the quality of the perovskite film deteriorates considerably as the humidity increases. Obvious voids appear at higher humidity levels due to the erosion caused by moisture. The XRD patterns also show that increasing humidity leads to a decline in the crystal quality of the perovskite film, as indicated by the gradual increase in the FWHM value (Fig. 1B). The shift in the XRD characteristic peaks of the (100) crystal plane at different humidity levels indicates that the distortion of perovskite structure is influenced by moisture (fig. S3A). As expected, higher humidity resulted in an increase in defect density and thus increased nonradiative recombination loss of carriers (Fig. 1, A and E, fig. S3B, and table S2). The average TRPL lifetimes of perovskite films annealed at 20, 60, 80, and 93% RH are 3060, 2688, 2427, and 1795 ps, respectively. This is because, after completing its function of promoting crystallization, moisture further reacts with hygroscopic organic ammonium salts [formamidinium iodide (FAI)] and induces lattice decomposition of perovskite (Fig. 1C) (16).

Therefore, effectively using moisture to facilitate the crystallization of perovskite films, while simultaneously mitigating the damage caused by high humidity after crystallization is complete, has become a critical challenge in accelerating the industrial production of perovskite-related optoelectronic devices. The necessity of suppressing moisture during the subsequent crystallization process of perovskite films was discussed in note S1. This issue could be addressed if a moisture barrier were constructed, which could be opened to allow moisture to enter the perovskite film to advance crystallization but automatically close the channel for moisture at the appropriate time. If a moisture barrier molecule were incorporate into the perovskite film (Fig. 1F), during the annealing process, then moisture would facilitate the transformation of the α phase. Once the main crystallization process is completed, the hydrophobic moisture barrier molecules migrate to the surface of the perovskite film, forming a dense hydrophobic layer to protect the film from high-humidity damage (Fig. 1F and fig. S4).

Design the moisture barrier molecule

First, the crystallization process of perovskite films annealed at 40% RH with different annealing times was investigated by XRD characterization (fig. S5). The results show that the δ phase transformed almost completely into the α phase in the initial annealing stage, and after this initial stage, the crystallization of perovskite film was essentially complete. Therefore, it is necessary to screen appropriate molecules to close the entry channel of moisture at an appropriate stage.

Choosing an appropriate molecule is a prerequisite for forming a moisture barrier, which requires that certain conditions to be met: (i) It should have a sufficiently long alkyl chain, and its hydrophobicity can effectively prevent the collapse of the perovskite structure caused by moisture; (ii) it must contain both hydrophilic and hydrophobic groups, so that it is spontaneously repelled to the surface of the perovskite film after the moisture-promoted crystallization is largely complete; (iii) it should have a large molecular size so that it cannot be incorporated into the perovskite lattice; (iv) it should have strong interactions with I− to inhibit its migration (25).

Figure 2A presents a design roadmap for the barrier molecule. The first step is to maintain consistency in functional groups [(CH3)3N+], while varying the alkyl chain length. SEM analysis revealed that longer alkyl chains correlate with less PbI2 residue on the surface of the perovskite film, indicating that the film is less susceptible to moisture erosion and has a more intact crystal structure (Fig. 2B). However, excessively long alkyl chains, such as cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), will result in the formation of voids because their increased self-assembly tendency disrupts perovskite crystallization (26, 27). Therefore, DTAB with 12 carbon atoms offers optimal hydrophobicity, while also avoiding the adverse effects of longer alkyl chains on the quality of perovskite films. The second step involves maintaining a constant alkyl chain length while varying the functional groups (Fig. 2A). SEM results show that only DTAB (Fig. 2B), with its quaternary ammonium functional groups, could preserve the integrity of the perovskite structure.

In the third step, to investigate whether halide ions affect the morphology of perovskite films, both the alkyl chain length and the hydrophilic functional group [the (CH3)3N+ headgroup] are held constant while changing the halide ions. The experimental results demonstrate that dodecyltrimethylammonium iodide (DTAI) (I−), dodecyltrimethylammonium chloride (DTAC) (Cl−), and DTAB (Br−) achieve comparable effects (fig. S6), with similar surface morphology. To identify the optimal molecule, we performed the density functional theory calculations (note S2 and fig. S7, A to C and E), revealing that the DTAB molecule is the most suitable choice (28, 29). Considering that DTAB is a type of ionic salt and may separate into DTA+ and Br− when dissolved in solvents such as N,N′-dimethylformamide (DMF) and DMSO (30), the electrostatic potential of DTA+ and its adsorption energy (Ea) on the Pb-I–terminated perovskite surface (fig. S7, D and F) were calculated. The positive electrostatic potential around the N atom of DTA+ indicates that it can passivate perovskite defects through ionic binding.

Liquid state/crystallization phase

After the barrier molecule (DTAB) was added to the perovskite precursor solution, it was found that DTAB could favorably reduce surface tension, decrease zeta potential, and promote more uniform size distribution of colloids (note S3 and fig. S8), which would be favorable for film crystallization. After the wet precursor film containing barrier molecules was placed on a hot plate for the initial annealing period, moisture could enter the film as the moisture barrier was not yet formed, and the barrier molecules were expected to promote perovskite crystallization together with the moisture. Next, an in situ PL method was used to investigate the crystallization of the precursor film during annealing in an air environment (Fig. 3, A and C). The initial crystallization process for both the control and target perovskite films exhibits a four-stage “up-down-up-down” process (Fig. 3, B and D) with notable differences (31). These differences indicate that (i) the target film had more nucleation sites (fig. S9) and a faster nucleation rate, (ii) the barrier molecules promote the transformation of the inorganic frameworks into perovskite, (iii) the growth process of perovskite film was delayed in the target film, and (iv) the quenching of PL was largely inhibited in the target film. These advantages indicate that the target perovskite film has lower defect density and higher crystal quality. For detailed discussion, please refer to note S4 and figs. S9 to S12.

To further demonstrate the advantages of our proposed strategy, we annealed the perovskite films for different durations. As the annealing time increased, more and more sheet-like crystalline structures at the boundaries were exposed on the surface of the control perovskite film (Fig. 3E), which has a morphology similar to the PbI2 crystal reported in previous studies. This indicates the decomposition of perovskite due to FA+ evaporation (32). In contrast, PbI2 did not appear on the surface of the target perovskite film containing barrier molecules, where clear and intact perovskite grains were visible (Fig. 3F). This indicates that the presence of barrier molecules effectively promotes film crystallization, maintains the integrity of perovskite structure, and prevents erosion by moisture.

More characterization results revealed that the presence of barrier molecules helps obtain perovskite films with higher quality (note S5 and figs. S13 to S17). The SEM images show that the target perovskite film is smoother and flatter compared to the control film due to the favorable promotion of crystallization. Moreover, the roughness of perovskite film was reduced from 21.2 to 16.0 nm (Ra: arithmetic mean roughness) (Fig. 3, G, I, and O). The introduction of DTAB renders the surface of perovskite films smoother, facilitating the formation of good contact between the hole transport layer and perovskite (PVK) layer, reducing series resistance and improving FF. The grain surface concavities, bijunction grain boundary grooves (GBGs), and triple-junction GBGs were also reduced (Fig. 3, I to N, and fig. S18) (33). This is because of the fact that barrier molecule interacts with FAI (PbI2), reducing the free energy at surface and grain boundaries of the perovskite films, as well as their difference, which is reported to be beneficial for obtaining better surface morphology (33). This is conducive to forming good interface contact, thereby notably improving the performance and stability of the device (34). Laser scanning confocal fluorescence microscopy (CLSM) revealed an overall increase in luminescence intensity in the target film, indicating a decrease in the overall defect density (Fig. 3, P and Q) (35). In addition to chemical passivation, DTAB may also form dipole layers on perovskite film surface that increase the work function and promote interface hole transport (36). This was verified by Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM) characterization, which revealed a higher and more uniform surface potential in target perovskite film (fig. S19) (25, 37). Overall, when the moisture barrier was in the liquid state/crystallization phase, barrier molecules, together with moisture, promoted the high-quality crystallization of perovskite.

Solid state/protective phase

The barrier molecules are expected to spontaneously close the channel for moisture entry into the perovskite film at the appropriate time. For the target perovskite film, the α phase has mainly formed by the ~5th second (Fig. 4B), meaning that the moisture barrier must be in its solid state/protective phase at about that time to form a hydrophobic layer on the film surface. Then, the water contact angles of the perovskite films prepared with different annealing times was measured. The water contact angle of the control film does not change further with time (fig. S20), and the film remained hydrophilic, posing a risk of subsequent moisture damage. For the target perovskite film containing barrier molecules, before annealing, it maintains the same contact angle of about 42° with the control film (Fig. 4A). In contrast, the water contact angle of the target perovskite film increased with increasing annealing time and reaches as high as 84° by the 5th second, indicating that the barrier molecules had spontaneously migrated from the bulk to the film surface. As a result, the hydrophobic layer had formed, meaning that the moisture barrier was functional. Moreover, the water contact angle showed no notable further change with the annealing time (Fig. 4C). The time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) analysis further confirmed that the barrier molecules underwent a migration process (Fig. 4D), initially being predominantly distributed within the bulk of the perovskite film and gradually migrating to the surface as annealing progressed.

From an energy perspective, the surface free energy of the perovskite film is higher than its bulk free energy. To reduce surface energy, nonpolar long alkyl chains, which have low surface energy, would prefer to accumulate on the surface of the perovskite film, thereby lowering the system’s overall free energy—a phenomenon known as upward segregation (38). In addition, the unfavorable enthalpic interaction between DTAB and perovskite components provides a driving force for DTAB segregation (39). After annealing the perovskite film for 5 s, the α phase is largely formed. Because of the large molecular size (steric hindrance), DTAB cannot enter the perovskite lattice and migrates upwards to minimize the free energy (fig. S21). The crystallization-driven migration enables the migration to be appropriately timed under various humidity conditions. The higher the humidity, the faster the DTAB molecules migrate upward (fig. S22 and note S6).

To verify the effectiveness of the moisture barrier, we prepared perovskite films incorporating a barrier molecule under varying humidity. Because of the moisture barrier, the quality of the perovskite film remained consistent in a very wide humidity range from 20% RH to as high as 93% RH (Fig. 4E) (40). XRD and PL results further support this conclusion (see fig. S23, A to D, and table S3 for more detailed analysis). Even at 93% RH, the surface of the perovskite film surface remained intact and smooth, with no signs of PbI2 formation. TRPL mapping reveals that perovskite films obtained at different humidity levels maintain similar lifetimes (Fig. 4F). The average TRPL lifetimes of perovskite films annealed at 20, 60, 80, and 93% RH were 4236, 3959, 3770, and 3764 ps, respectively. In addition, the XRD characteristic peak positions and intensities of the (100) crystal plane can maintain notable consistency at different humidity levels, indicating that the introduction of DTAB effectively prevents distortion of the perovskite structure (fig. S23A). These results confirmed the effectiveness of the moisture barrier, which is promising for manufacturing, due to its robust resistance to humidity damage and seasonal moisture fluctuations.

Furthermore, to emphasize the significance of moisture barrier molecule design, we selected the 4-propoxy-4-cyanobiphenyl (3OCB) molecule for a comparative study with DTAB. It has a similar molecular structure to DTAB and could also migrate to the surface of perovskite film during annealing (fig. S24) but with a notably lower hydrophobicity than DTAB (fig. S25). The results show that although it migrates to the surface of the perovskite film, it cannot form an effective moisture barrier to protect the film from moisture damage (more detailed analysis is provided in figs. S26 and S27 and table S5), highlighting the importance of molecular design for a moisture barrier.

Moisture barrier enhances photovoltaic performance

The versatility of the barrier molecule encouraged us to evaluate its impact on device performance. Normal PSCs were prepared to investigate their photovoltaic performance (fig. S28A). Under optimal humidity conditions (40% RH), the highest PCE of the control PSCs is 23.97%, with an open circuit voltage (VOC) of 1.16 V, a short-circuit current density (JSC) of 25.22 mA/cm2, and an FF of 82.07% (Fig. 5A). In contrast, the target PSCs exhibited a PCE of 25.33% (VOC = 1.17 V, JSC = 25.50 mA/cm2, and FF = 84.93%). The improved PCE is attributed to the barrier molecule promoting perovskite crystallization with reduced lattice defects. The optimization of DTAB concentration and statistical distributions of photovoltaic parameters are also presented (Fig. 5, C and G; fig. S28, B to F; and tables S6 and S7). A much narrower distributions in box plots demonstrate outstanding uniformity and repeatability of target devices compared to control devices (Fig. 5, C and G, and fig. S28, C and D). In addition, the characterization results of related devices strongly suggest that the introduction of barrier molecules greatly contributes to obtaining PSCs with superior photovoltaic performance (note S7, figs. S29 to S31, and table S8).

The photovoltaic performance of PSCs prepared under different annealing humidity levels (Fig. 5B and fig. S32) was further evaluated. We counted 20 devices and found that the SDs of PCE for target devices at different humidity levels (0.00105, 0.00128, 0.00299, and 0.00324) were smaller than those for control devices (0.0015, 0.0067, 0.00443, and 0.01269). The target device demonstrates superb performance and a much narrower distribution of PCE compared to the control device prepared at these four tested humidity levels, which mimic different seasonal stages. Unexpectedly, the PCE of target PSCs achieved 23.55% when the humidity increases to as high as 93%, while PCE of control PSCs drops markedly to 19.71%. To our knowledge, this value is among the highest reported efficiencies for perovskite cells manufactured in these high RH environments (fig. S33 and table S9). This indicates that the constructed moisture barrier could effectively broaden the annealing humidity window of the perovskite film, resulting in notably enhanced reproducibility of PSC performance. This distinct feature completely alleviates the limitation of maintaining the so-called proper annealing humidity window in perovskite manufacturing, which is highly favorable for industrial large-scale production even under high-humidity conditions.

The stability of the perovskite film was studied, encouraged by the great improvement of the quality of the target perovskite films. In situ PL tracking results under 40% RH conditions revealed a severely decreased PL intensity of the control perovskite film at the beginning, indicating that moisture deteriorated the perovskite structure and increased the defect density early on (Fig. 5D). In contrast, the target perovskite film maintained high PL intensity even after aging for up to 400 min (Fig. 5H). After heating at 85°C for about 150 min in ambient air (RH = 30%), no diffraction peaks of δ phase or PbI2 were observed in the target perovskite film compared to the control film (Fig. 5, E and I). This indicates that the target perovskite film has excellent thermal stability and is not easily damaged by moisture. SEM results showed that the target perovskite film retained with a better morphology after 52 days of storage under a 20% RH condition (figs. S34 and S35). High-energy regions are formed at grain boundaries due to atomic mismatches and dangling bond defects. DTAB preferentially adsorbs at grain boundaries and passivates defects through electrostatic interactions, suppressing nonradiative recombination and improving device performance. The perovskite films were also directly immersed in water to assess their stability (fig. S36). The control perovskite film completely turned into a yellow phase, while the target perovskite film retained its black phase after 3 s. This indicates that DTAB molecules effectively prevent the erosion of water vapor and the phase transition of perovskite films. The stability of the perovskite film under an electric field coupled with humidity (RH = 30%) was intuitively examined using a self-built CLSM system based on a lateral structure (fig. S37, A and B). Under an electric field of 0.25 V/μm, the control perovskite film suffered severe damage after 150 s (Fig. 5F and fig. S37, C to E). However, the target perovskite film showed strong resistance to electric field–coupled humidity by remaining largely intact under the same aging conditions (Fig. 5J and fig. S37, F to H). This result indicated suppressed ion migration due to fewer defects, more complete lattice, and strong interaction between DTA+ and I− in target films (41). Ion migration tends to occur along grain boundaries, leading to observed microstructural changes and phase degradation. The introduction of DTAB effectively suppresses ion migration, thereby preventing changes in the microstructure and phase uniformity of perovskite and improving device stability (42). Moreover, it can be found that DTAB exhibits a stronger interaction with I2 compared to starch (fig. S38), as evidenced by the introduction of DTAB changing the color of the starch + I2 solution from purple to yellow (43).

To investigate the effect of DTAB on the long-term stability of PSCs, we monitored the evolution of the PCE under different aging conditions using the unencapsulated PSCs. All stability tests were conducted in strict accordance with ISOS standards, with specific details and condition controls shown in fig. S40. First, a harsh test examination was performed by subjecting PSCs to aging at 85% RH and 85°C (ISOS-D-3). The target PSCs exhibited a T85 > 350 hours under damp heat testing, while the control PSCs showed rapid decay (Fig. 5K). Second, PSCs were aged under continuous one-sun irradiation (ISOS-L-1) (Fig. 5L). The results showed that the target PSCs retained 86.5% of their initial PCE after 566 hours of aging, whereas the control PSCs maintained only 60.4% of the initial PCE, indicating greatly improved stability of PSCs. Last, after aging in ambient air (ISOS-D-1), the control PSCs degraded to 68.4% of their initial PCE, while the target PSCs maintained 94.6% of the initial PCE after 2602 hours, demonstrating substantially enhanced environmental stability (Fig. 5M). This enhanced stability can be attributed to effective defect passivation and the hydrophobic barrier effect of DTAB. Overall, the introduction of DTAB ensures high-quality film formation under high-humidity annealing environments and remarkably improves the stability of PSCs, revealing its great potential and compatibility for the commercialization of PSCs.

In summary, a crystallization-activated moisture barrier was constructed to overcome the double-edged sword effect of moisture on perovskite films during the annealing stage in air. During the initial annealing, DTAB resides within the film and allows moisture-assisted crystallization. Upon completion, DTAB migrates to the surface, forming a hydrophobic barrier. This strategy enables high-quality perovskite film preparation under different humidity conditions, even under very harsh, high-humidity environments (93% RH) amidst seasonal fluctuations. In addition, because of its defect passivation ability and hydrophobicity, the barrier molecules could act as a strong shield against moisture, leading to notable enhanced stability of the optimized perovskite film under light, heat, and high-humidity conditions. The optimized PSCs achieved a PCE of 25.33% with an FF of 84.93%. The unencapsulated device demonstrated superior long-term stability, maintaining 84.9% of its initial PCE after 350 hours of aging under damp heat conditions (ISOS-D-3) and 94.6% of its initial PCE after 2602 hours (under ISOS-D-1 conditions). This research has greatly enhanced the PSC preparation process’ tolerance to high humidity, has alleviated the impact of seasonal humidity fluctuations on the preparation process, and is of great significance for accelerating the industrial mass production of stable perovskite photovoltaic devices.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Lead iodide (PbI2; 99.999%), lithium bis(trifluoromethyl sulfonyl)imide salt (Li-TFSI), 4-tert-butyl pyridine, DMF (99.8%), DMSO (99.9%), isopropyl alcohol (IPA; 99.7%), and chlorobenzene (CB) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Ether (AR) was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. Acetonitrile (AR) was purchased from Energy Chemical. Methylammonium chloride (MACl; 99.99%), 2-fluorophenylethylammonium iodide (o-F-PEAI), and poly(3-hexylthiophene-2,5-diyl) (P3HT; Mn, ~30,000) were purchased from Xi’an Yuri Solar Co. Ltd. FAI (99.9%) and Spiro-OMeTAD were purchased from Advanced Electron Technology Co. Ltd. SDS, sodium laurylsulfonate (NDP), trimethylammonium bromide (TAB), trimethylphenylammonium bromide (TTAB), butyltrimethylammonium bromide (BTAB), n-octyltrimethylammonium bromide (OTAB), DTAB, DTAC, DTAI, and CTAB were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co. Ltd. The above materials were used without further purification.

Solution preparation

The perovskite precursor solution was obtained by combining PbI2 (1.2 mmol, 553.2 mg), FAI (1.2 mmol, 206.3 mg), and MACl (0.42 mmol, 28.3 mg) in 1 ml of mixed solvent (DMF:DMSO, 4:1) to yield FAPbI3. Different amount of DTAB powder (0.5, 0.2, 0.1, and 0.05 mg) was added into 1 ml of precursor solution. For surface passivation solutions, 5 mg of o-F-PEAI was dissolved in 1 ml of IPA. For Spiro-OMeTAD solution, 90 mg of Spiro-OMeTAD, 22 μl of Li-TFSI solution (520 mg in 1 ml of acetonitrile), and 36 μl of 4-tert-butylpyridine were dissolved in 1 ml of CB. The solutions were stirred at room temperature for 6 hours. For P3HT solution, 10 mg of P3HT was dissolved in 1 ml of CB and stirred at 60°C for 6 hours. All solutions were filtered through a 0.45-μm syringe filter.

Device fabrication

Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass was chosen as the substrate. The FTO was cleaned by ultrasonication sequentially in cleaning concentrate, deionized water, and ethanol for 30 min. The TiO2 electron–transfer layer was prepared by chemical bath deposition on FTO/glass substrate, as reported previously. First, the cleaned FTO/glass substrate was treated with ultraviolet (UV) ozone cleaner for 15 min. Next, the substrate was placed in the 0.2 M TiCl4 aqueous solution at 70°C for 60 min, and then the substrate was washed three times with deionized water and ethyl alcohol alternately. The substrate was then annealed at 200°C for 30 min in air and treated in UV ozone for 15 min to enhance the wettability. Approximately 80 μl of perovskite precursor was dropped onto the TiO2/FTO/glass substrates, and the antisolution method was used to form perovskite films with diethyl ether as the antisolvent. The solution was spin coated at 3000 rpm for 10 s and then at 5000 rpm for 30 s. Fifteen seconds before the end of the 5000-rpm step, 600 μl of diethyl ether was quickly dropped onto the surface. Next, the film was annealed at 150°C for 15 min in air atmosphere. After the PVK film was cooled, 100 μl of surface passivation solution was dynamically spin coated on the PVK film at 2000 rpm for 30 s for top surface passivation without thermal annealing. Then, 50 μl of Spiro-OMeTAD solution was spin coated at 5000 rpm for 30 s to form the hole transfer layer. When conducting light testing and testing under high-temperature and -humidity conditions, removing the o-F-PEAI layer and the Spiro-OMeTAD layer was changed to P3HT layer. For P3HT layer, 70 μl of P3HT solution was spin coated at 3000 rpm for 30 s. The gold electrode (~80 nm) was thermally evaporated onto the surface of the Spiro-OMeTAD layer through a shadow mask, and the active area was 0.049 cm2.

Device characterization

A field-emission SEM (JEOL SU-8020) was used to acquire the surface morphology of perovskite films. The nucleation process of perovskite films was observed in situ on a hot table at 50°C by an optical microscope with a lens of 50 times. UV-visible absorption spectra were conducted on the diluted perovskite precursor solution at a 100-fold dilution using a UV3600 system. XRD patterns were acquired using a Bruker D8 general area detector diffraction system diffractometer with Cu Kα (λ = 1.54 Å) radiation. Surface tension was determined using the hanging drop method with the DataPhysics OCA20 contact angle system. Zeta potential and dynamic light scattering tests were conducted on the diluted perovskite precursor solution at a 100-fold dilution using the NanoBrook 90Plus phase analysis light scattering system. In situ PL spectroscopy characterization of the sample was performed using a spectrophotometer (QE65 Pro) at an excitation wavelength of 405 nm. The TOF-SIMS was determined by TOF-SIMS 5 (IONTOF). The 1-keV O2− and Cs+ beams were used for sputtering to detect the positive and negative ions, respectively. The beam current is 100.00 nA for O2− and 36.00 nA for Cs+, and the sputter area is 300 μm by 300 μm. The 30-keV Bi3+ primary ion beam was used for analysis to avoid severe beam damage. The beam current is 0.50 pA, and the analysis area is 100 μm by 100 μm. The water contact angles were measured with an OCA20 instrument. Grazing incidence XRD measurements were conducted using Bruker D8 DISCOVER 25 x-ray diffractometer with a power of 1.6 kW. Scanning speed is 2 s per step and 0.01° per step, with ψ angle from 5° to 50°. The incident x-ray beam was set to a fixed grazing angle of 1°, ensuring surface-sensitive analysis. The atomic force microscopy (AFM) were performed using a Bruker Dimension Icon (Bruker Nano Inc.) in the single-pass frequency-modulated mode under ambient conditions. The confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM) characterizations were performed on a homemade Olympus FV3000 CLSM system with a ×40 objective lens. Steady-state PL spectra were acquired on an F-320 spectrofluorometer (Gangdong Sci. & Tech., China) under 450-nm laser excitation. TRPL measurements were performed using a HORIBA Jobin Yvon DeltaPro system (Edison, NJ) equipped with a 506-nm pulsed diode laser (1-MHz repetition rate) and time-correlated single-photon counting detector. The samples for PL and TRPL were prepared on glass. The TRPL mapping test uses a laser with a wavelength of 405 nm and a frequency of 20 MHz to irradiate the sample. After reflection from the sample, it is received and focused onto the sample through a 60× oil-immersed objective lens (numerical aperture, 1.4; Olympus UPLanSApo). The laser intensity was adjusted through a neutral density filter and measured using a power meter (PM100D S16VC, Thorlabs, USA). The sample was scanned 15 times, with a total time of 141 s. The KPFM were determined by Oxford Instruments Asylum Research/Cypher S. The J-V curve was also captured with Keithley 2400 source but under Air Mass 1.5 Global (AM 1.5 G) illumination at 100 mW/cm2. The external quantum efficiency of the PSCs was recorded by the QE-R 3011 system (Enlitech, Taiwan) in the range of 300 to 900 nm. A reference silicon detector was calibrated to adjust the monochromatic light intensity. The space charge–limited current measurement and dark I-V test were performed using a Keithley 2400 source measurement unit under dark conditions. The C-V and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy measurements were stimulated by the Zahner electrochemical workstation (IM6ex, Germany).
