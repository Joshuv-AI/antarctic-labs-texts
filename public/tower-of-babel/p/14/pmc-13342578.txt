Decoding bone grafting preferences: a cross-sectional survey of patient perspectives and influence of treatment cost
Authors: Ahsan Khawaja, Zara Pervaiz, Nighat Naved, Fahad Umer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to evaluate patients’ preferences for different bone graft materials and assess the influence of treatment cost on these preferences in the Pakistani population. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among adult patients across three tertiary-care hospitals in Karachi. A validated bilingual questionnaire assessed demographic characteristics, initial graft preferences, reasons for refusal, and changes in preference after introducing treatment cost. Descriptive statistics were reported and data were analyzed using chi-square test, and Bowker’s test of symmetry. A total of 101 participants were included. Autologous grafts, particularly those derived from intraoral sites, were the most preferred type, while xenografts showed the highest refusal rates. Pain and discomfort were the primary concerns associated with autologous grafts, fear of disease transmission influenced allograft refusal, and religious considerations were the dominant factor for xenograft rejection. No statistically significant association was found between demographic variables and graft preference (p value > 0.05). Furthermore, introduction of treatment cost did not result in a significant preference shift (p value = 0.42). Cultural and safety-related perceptions significantly influenced decision-making. Although cost was acknowledged as an important factor, it did not significantly affect preferences, highlighting the need for culturally sensitive and patient-centered counseling.Subject terms: Dentistry, Dental treatments

Introduction

Guided bone regeneration (GBR) is a well-established surgical modality designed to augment bone volume in cases of alveolar bone deficiency [1, 2]. With consistent long-term success [3–6], GBR has significantly increased implant survival rates up to 95%, underscoring its reliability and effectiveness [7–9]. While GBR offers superior clinical benefits, the availability of a wide variety of graft sources such as autologous grafts, allografts, xenografts, and alloplast may complicate the decision-making process.

Patients and clinicians are the two key stakeholders in this decision-making process [10–12], and understanding their perspectives and preferences is of utmost importance. Patients’ autonomy, shaped by cultural values, religious beliefs, ethical concerns, and philosophical views, significantly influence their treatment choices [13–15].

Moreover, treatment cost has been cited as one of the most influential factors affecting patients’ decision-making, particularly when financed out-of-the-pocket [16, 17]. In procedures such as bone grafting, this consideration becomes even more important, as the cost can vary considerably depending on the type of graft material used and the surgical technique required [18].

This highlights the importance of understanding all the possible factors that may influence patients’ preferences regarding bone grafting [19]. For this purpose, conducting surveys has proven to be an effective strategy [20]. Previously, survey-based studies have been conducted in Christian-majority countries such as Chile, Italy, Spain [21], and France [22], as well as in Muslim-majority countries like Saudi Arabia [23], Kuwait [24], and Turkey [14] focusing on patients’ preferences regarding the sources of bone grafts. However, most of these studies were conducted in countries with superior healthcare infrastructure without adequately considering the potential influence of financial constraints.

Conducting this study in Pakistan, known for its religious diversity and distinct economic challenges will explore all the factors influencing bone grafting preferences for patients. Therefore, the present study aimed to explore patients’ preferences regarding different bone grafting materials and to identify the factors influencing their acceptance or refusal. Additionally, the study sought to evaluate whether variations in treatment cost influence patients’ preferences when choosing between different graft options.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted in Karachi, Pakistan after obtaining approval from the Ethical Review Committee of Aga Khan University Hospital (ERC No. 2025-11531-35568). Data were collected across three tertiary-care institutions, including two public sector hospitals, Karachi Metropolitan University, and Sindh Institute of Oral Health Sciences, and one private sector hospital, The Aga Khan University Hospital. Prior administrative permission was obtained from the respective public institutions before initiating data collection. To minimize institutional sampling bias, an equal number of participants were recruited from each center.

Sample size

The required sample size was calculated using OpenEpi software, assuming a population size of 1,000,000 for finite population correction and an anticipated response frequency of 50%. With a 95% confidence level and 10% absolute precision, the minimum sample size was calculated to be 97 participants recruited using a non-probability convenience sampling technique until the required sample size was achieved.

Eligibility criteria

Participants were included in the study if they were 18 years of age or older, able to understand and communicate in English or Urdu languages, capable of making independent medical decisions, and willing to provide informed consent. Conversely, individuals were excluded if they had previously undergone a bone grafting procedure, exhibited any cognitive impairment affecting decision-making ability, or had a history of substance abuse that could potentially compromise the reliability of their responses.

Questionnaire structure and validation

A self-administered, structured questionnaire was specifically designed for this study. The instrument was divided into four distinct sections to systematically capture participant responses. The first section elicited participant demographics, including age, gender, education level, and religious affiliation. The second section assessed initial preference towards different bone graft materials (autologous intraoral graft, autologous extraoral graft, allograft, xenograft, and alloplast) prior to the provision of any cost-related information. In the third section, participants who expressed refusal for specific graft types were asked to indicate their reasons for refusal, allowing for identification of key barriers to acceptance. The final section reevaluated participant preferences to determine their final preference after the introduction of treatment cost, thereby assessing the impact of financial considerations on decision-making. To facilitate informed decision-making, a brief explanatory cover letter accompanied the questionnaire, outlining the different types of bone graft materials along with their respective advantages and disadvantages (Fig. 1).

Explanatory cover letter for patients.

The questionnaire underwent a rigorous content validation process by seven subject experts, who evaluated the instrument for relevance, clarity, and representativeness. Content validity was assessed using the Content Validity Index (CVI). The scale-level CVI values demonstrated excellent validity with S-CVI scores of 0.94 for relevance, 0.92 for clarity, and 0.97 for representativeness. All item-level CVI (I-CVI) values exceeded 0.78, indicating acceptable content validity for individual items. Minor revisions were made based on expert feedback to improve clarity and conceptual coverage. To enhance accessibility and cultural relevance, the questionnaire was translated into Urdu language, resulting in a bilingual instrument suitable for the study population.

Data collection

Data were collected through in-person administration of the questionnaire in dental outpatient clinics during routine consultations or while patients waited for treatment. Eligible participants were provided with a brief explanation of the study objectives, and written informed consent was obtained prior to participation. Each participant completed the questionnaire under the supervision of a trained research assistant who was available to assist with reading or clarification of questions when required. Questionnaires were reviewed at the time of collection to ensure completeness.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted in Python 3.12.11 using Google Colab (Google LLC, USA). Descriptive statistics were reported as frequencies and percentages. Associations between demographic variables and patients’ graft preferences were evaluated using Pearson’s chi-square test, with p value < 0.05 considered statistically significant. To assess whether the introduction of treatment cost influenced patients’ graft preferences, the initial preferences were compared with the final preferences after cost disclosure using Bowker’s test of symmetry.

Results

A total of 101 patients participated in the survey. The majority of the participants were young adults aged 18–30 years (n = 91, 90.1%). In terms of gender distribution, females comprised 62.4% (n = 63) of the sample, whereas males accounted for only 37.6% (n = 38). Regarding educational status, most respondents held a bachelor’s degree (n = 75, 74.3%). With respect to religious affiliation, majority of participants identified as Muslims (n = 100, 99%), while one participant (1%) identified as Hindu. Initial preferences for bone graft materials

The most preferred graft material among patients was autologous bone graft derived from an intraoral site. This was followed by autologous bone graft obtained from extraoral sites. Other grafting options, in decreasing order of preference, included alloplast, allograft, and xenograft (Fig. 2).

Reasons for refusal of specific graft types

Pain and discomfort were the most frequently reported concerns associated with autologous bone graft derived from both intraoral and extraoral sites, as well as alloplastic grafts. For allografts, the most common concern was potential disease transmission, whereas religious considerations were the predominant factor influencing refusal of xenografts (Fig. 3).

Reasons for refusal for each graft type.

Influence of treatment cost on graft preference

When participants were asked whether knowledge of treatment cost would influence their graft preference, 25.5% of respondents reported that their preference would change, 28.6% reported that their preference would remain unchanged, and 45.9% indicated that their preference might or might not change after being informed of the cost of different graft materials.

To evaluate whether cost introduction resulted in a change in graft preference, initial preferences were compared with preferences after disclosure of treatment cost using Bowker’s test of symmetry. The analysis demonstrated no statistically significant shift in preferences (p value = 0.42), indicating that overall graft preferences remained largely unchanged after introduction of treatment cost (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The present study aimed to explore patients’ preferences regarding different bone graft materials and to evaluate the influence of treatment cost on these preferences within a subset of the Pakistani population. The findings demonstrated a clear preference for autologous bone grafts, while xenografts were the most frequently refused option. Religious beliefs, concerns regarding disease transmission, and anticipated pain were identified as key factors influencing patient decision-making.

The preference for autologous bone grafts despite the need of an additional surgical site is consistent with existing literature [14, 23, 24]. Previous studies, including the multicenter analysis by Bucchi et al., have similarly reported a higher acceptance of autologous grafts compared to other graft types [21]. One possible explanation for this preference is the concept of psychological ownership, whereby patients demonstrate a natural inclination toward using their own biological tissues. Autologous grafts are often perceived as more biocompatible, safer, and free from external risks, which enhances patient trust and acceptance. However, consistent with previous studies, pain and discomfort, particularly related to donor site morbidity, were commonly cited concerns associated with autologous grafts.

In contrast to studies conducted in predominantly Christian populations [21, 22], where xenografts have shown relatively higher acceptance, the present study observed markedly higher refusal of xenografts. However, this finding is supported by studies from Muslim-majority populations. Güngörmüş and Güngörmüş, as well as Nadershah et al., reported that the majority of Turkish and Saudi participants, respectively, refused xenografts specifically due to religious reasons. The consistency of these findings across Muslim-majority countries suggests a pattern influenced by shared religious and cultural values, reinforcing the importance of culturally sensitive patient counseling [14, 25].

It is important to note that, despite providing participants with information that xenografts may be derived from both porcine and bovine sources, refusal rates remained consistently high. This may indicate that patients often generalize xenografts as religiously non-permissible, possibly due to limited awareness or preconceived beliefs. It is plausible that presenting bovine and porcine graft sources separately could have resulted in different acceptance patterns, which represents an important consideration for future research.

The present study also found that fear of disease transmission was the predominant concern associated with allografts, which is in agreement with findings reported by Bucchi et al., where disease transmission and ethical concerns were major reasons for rejection. This reflects a persistent perception of risk associated with human-derived grafts, despite advancements in sterilization and processing techniques.

A key and counterintuitive finding of this study was that treatment cost did not significantly influence patients’ graft preferences, despite being widely recognized as an important factor in healthcare decision-making, particularly in low and middle-income countries such as Pakistan. Several explanations may account for this observation. First, autologous grafting, the most preferred option was also among the least costly, which may have reduced the likelihood of preference shifts following cost disclosure. Second, the study population predominantly consisted of well-educated individuals, who may place greater emphasis on risk-benefit considerations over financial factors. Third, bone grafting is often perceived as a one-time investment, and patients may be more willing to prioritize long-term outcomes and safety over immediate cost considerations. Nevertheless, the role of cost cannot be entirely disregarded. Although it did not produce a statistically significant change in preferences in this study, it remains an important factor, particularly for financially constrained populations, and may influence treatment accessibility rather than preference alone.

The strength of the study includes a validated bilingual questionnaire ensuring both reliability and accessibility of the data collection instrument. Additionally, the multi-center study design, incorporating both public and private sector hospitals, enhanced sample representativeness. This approach prevented over-representation of affluent patients typically seeking care at private facilities, thereby providing a more accurate reflection of Pakistan’s broader population and helped to mitigate the limitations associated with convenience sampling. Finally, the application of strict exclusion criteria, particularly excluding individuals with prior bone grafting experience, reduced the likelihood of potential bias based on previous treatment experience.

This study also has certain limitations including the inherent limitation of a cross-sectional design. Although Pakistan is home to diverse religious groups, the study sample predominantly comprised Muslim participants (99%), which limits the generalizability of the findings to minority populations. A more balanced representation of different religious groups may have provided deeper insight into the role of religion in shaping graft preferences. Additionally, the predominance of young, educated adults in our sample limits generalizability to older and less-educated populations. However, this distribution reflects Pakistan’s demographic reality where 64% of the population is under 30 years of age [26].

Additionally, the cost estimates used in the questionnaire were based on average market values, which may vary across institutions depending on the brand and quality of graft materials. This variability could influence patient preferences in real clinical settings. Furthermore, recording individual-level socioeconomic status would have enabled a more precise assessment of how cost sensitivity influences preferences across different income groups. The reliance on self-reported data may introduce response bias, as participants may provide socially desirable answers, particularly regarding financial sensitivity or religious beliefs. Furthermore, the study was conducted in an urban setting, which may not fully represent the perspectives of rural or underserved populations.

The findings of this study have important clinical implications for dental practice in Pakistan. When counseling patients for guided bone regeneration procedures, clinicians should prioritize discussions regarding graft origin, religious permissibility, and safety profiles, as these appear to be the primary determinants of patient choice. However, this should not overshadow the reality that, for economically disadvantaged patients, treatment cost may still represent a significant barrier to accessing grafting procedures.

Conclusions

Patients demonstrated a marked preference for autologous bone grafts, while xenografts were most frequently refused, largely due to religious considerations. Despite being widely acknowledged, treatment cost did not significantly alter graft preferences, suggesting that perceived safety, biological compatibility, and cultural acceptability outweigh financial considerations in decision-making. These findings underscore the need for comprehensive, culturally sensitive, and transparent patient counseling, while recognizing that cost may still act as a barrier to access for economically disadvantaged populations.
