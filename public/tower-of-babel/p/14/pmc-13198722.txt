A stepped-care strategy for cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia in chronic spinal pain—protocol of a randomized clinical trial (the StepUp study)
Authors: Anneleen Malfliet, Zoë Maebe, Luka Mattelin, Olivier Mairesse, Jo Nijs, Astrid Lahousse, Nicole K Y Tang, Aisha Cortoos, Eva Huysmans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic spinal pain (CSP) and insomnia frequently co-occur in a mutually reinforcing relationship that amplifies symptom severity and reduces treatment efficacy. Despite the robust evidence supporting cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-i) as the first-line treatment, accessibility remains critically low due to workforce limitations, insufficient training in primary care, and the high burden of traditional treatment formats. This paper is the protocol of the StepUp study which aims to evaluate a stepped-care approach to CBT-i designed to improve access, efficiency, and clinical outcomes for individuals with CSP and chronic insomnia. In this three-arm randomized controlled trial, participants with CSP will be allocated to stepped-care CBT-i, standard CBT-i, or usual care. The primary outcome is remission of chronic insomnia at 6 months post-treatment. Secondary outcomes include cost-effectiveness, medication use, treatment adherence, sleep quality, pain intensity, and physical functioning assessed up to 12 months follow-up. By integrating precision medicine principles and resource optimization, this study addresses a critical gap in the management of comorbid insomnia and pain. Findings are expected to inform scalable, evidence-based care pathways, and enhance the delivery of effective behavioral sleep interventions within real-world healthcare systems. Statement of SignificanceThis trial addresses an important knowledge gap in the management of chronic insomnia in individuals with CSP. By evaluating (cost-) effectiveness within a pragmatic, scalable framework, the study has the potential to inform evidence-based care pathways, improve patient outcomes, and guide resource allocation in healthcare systems more efficiently for people with CSP and comorbid chronic insomnia.

Introduction and rationale

Chronic spinal pain (CSP) is significantly influenced by various factors, among which insomnia plays a pivotal role [1, 2]. Chronic insomnia is often considered a result of CSP, yet research findings suggest a bidirectional relationship [3, 4]. Sleep problems can both trigger and sustain pain [5], indicating a complex interplay where each can amplify the intensity of the other [6, 7]. Moreover, the presence of chronic insomnia can hinder treatment effectiveness in CSP [8, 9], and chronic insomnia is also associated with decreased daily functioning and life satisfaction in this population [5]. Notably, people with chronic pain spontaneously engage in more physical activity after one night of better sleep [10]. Hence, given the importance of physical activity in pain management, targeting sleep-related issues in people with CSP and comorbid chronic insomnia is suggested.

Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-i) is internationally recognized as the gold standard, first-line treatment for insomnia disorder [11–13]. CBT-i is a non-pharmacological treatment, and aims to rectify unhelpful sleep-related beliefs and behaviors [14], by targeting those factors that maintain insomnia with a multicomponent approach [15]. CBT-i is known for its long-term, clinically meaningful effectiveness [16], and its favorable impact extends to people with chronic pain and comorbid chronic insomnia [17, 18]. In the context of CSP, an Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT) from our group investigating the effectiveness of physiotherapist-led CBT-i evidences substantial enhancements in sleep-related parameters and the impact of pain on daily function (moderate to large, and clinically meaningful effect sizes) [19]. CSP represents one of the most prevalent and burdensome chronic pain conditions worldwide, posing a major public health challenge and underscoring the urgent need for effective and accessible treatment strategies [1, 20]. Focusing on this population also allows us to build directly on our prior work by now evaluating an evidence-based organization of care that facilitates broader and sustainable implementation in clinical practice.

The compelling evidence outlined above underscores that the challenge for CBT-i is no longer to prove its credentials. However, despite its demonstrated efficacy, CBT-i remains largely inaccessible to many patients in routine clinical care [21, 22]. Limited accessibility is primarily driven by a shortage of licensed CBT-i practitioners, insufficient training among primary care providers in recognizing sleep-related issues and the appropriate referral for such complaints, and substantial demands on patient’s time, energy, and financial resources [23, 24]. As a result, a considerable proportion of individuals with CSP and comorbid insomnia do not receive timely or adequate treatment. Addressing these barriers requires innovative care delivery models that preserve clinical effectiveness while improving accessibility.

A stepped care model could offer a solution by operationalizing principles of precision medicine and resource efficiency, whereby more intensive and costly interventions are reserved for patients who do not benefit sufficiently from lower-intensity care [25].

Step 1 (“entry level care”) is characterized by low costs, easy access, minimal therapeutic intensity, least inconvenience for patients, and requiring minimal specialist time [26]. For CBT-i, an evidence-based approach meeting these criteria [25, 27] extending well beyond sleep hygiene alone, involves replacing sleep-interfering behaviors with sleep-promoting ones through education and behavior change counseling [28], which can aid sleep initiation and maintenance [4, 5, 28]. Essentially, as step 1 can be delivered by nurses, physicians, and physiotherapists, it substantially enhances accessibility and facilitates patient engagement. The stepped care approach implies that not benefiting sufficiently from the initial “entry level” gives access to step 2: comprehensive, specialist-delivered standard CBT-i [27].

Preliminary, uncontrolled studies and feasibility research suggest promising outcomes for stepped-care CBT-i in people with insomnia, with 69 per cent improvement in sleep efficiency [29], and a 45 per cent response-rate (with 41 per cent remitted) in the context of comorbid chronic insomnia and other chronic conditions [30]. This evidence positions stepped care as a valuable and accessible alternative to standard interventions [31]. However, high-quality controlled trials evaluating non-inferiority of stepped-care CBT-i to standard CBT-i in people with CSP are currently lacking.

Therefore, the primary aim of the StepUp study is to examine whether stepped care CBT-i is non-inferior to standard CBT-i (delivered in a hybrid format) with respect to remission from chronic insomnia among people with CSP at 6 months post-treatment, and superior to usual care. Secondary objectives are to evaluate cost-effectiveness, changes in pain- and sleep-related medication use, treatment adherence, and whether stepped-care CBT-i leads to greater improvements than usual care in sleep quality, pain, and physical function at 6-month follow-up.

Materials and methods

This protocol follows the CONSORT [32] guidelines, the SPIRIT [33] statement, and the TIDieR [34] checklist. The trial was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06771856) in January 2025, and recruitment started in February 2025. The study is carried out at the Vrije Universiteit Brussel (Brussels, Belgium). The first participant was enrolled on the May 2, 2025, and currently, 25 participants are enrolled. It is expected that recruitment will be finalized mid-way 2026.

Study design

To examine the research aims, a three-arm randomized controlled trial in people with CSP will compare stepped care CBT-i, standard hybrid (h)CBT-i, and a usual care group. Outcome assessments will take place at baseline, and after 1 month (T1 = during the intervention period), 3 months (T2 = directly post-treatment), 9 months (T3 = 6 months follow-up), and 15 months (T4 = 12 months follow-up). T1 is the time point where participants in the stepped care group who have not remitted after the entry level treatment (step 1, see later) will receive standard hCBT-i (step 2). If however, they are remitted at T1 (i.e. after only receiving step 1), they will directly move into the follow-up period and thus will not receive step 2. T2 is the immediate post-treatment assessments after completion of standard hCBT-i (both for stepped care CBT-i and the standard hCBT-i groups). Participants will be considered treatment responders if their Insomnia Severity Index (ISI) score improves by ≥8 points [33] and will be considered as having remitted if their posttreatment ISI score is <12 [30, 35]. Participants randomized to the stepped care CBT-i group whose ISI remained ≥12 after step 1 will be referred to step 2 [30]. The organization of the research methodology is visualized in Figure 1.

Participants and recruitment

This study will include 129 participants. People are eligible for study participation if they meet the following criteria: individuals (18–65y/o), suffering from chronic insomnia (i.e. at least moderate insomnia, ISI ≥ 15) and non-specific CSP (i.e. pain severity of ≥3/10 on the Brief Pain Inventory) of at least 3 month’s duration, currently seeking care for CSP. In the absence of other intrinsic sleep disorders and shift work, chronic insomnia is defined as >30 min of sleep latency and/or minutes awake after sleep onset for >3 days/week for >3 months. Exclusion criteria are: having been diagnosed with severe underlying sleep pathology, being pregnant or given birth in the preceding year, suffering from a chronic condition which is currently causing other pain complaints (e.g. fibromyalgia), or suffering from severe psychological or psychiatric diseases (e.g. depression, anxiety disorder, burnout), dementia or cognitive impairment. Eligible participants are asked not to start new treatments or medication, and continuing non-physical therapy care as usual for 6 weeks prior to and during study participation to obtain a steady state.

Participants are recruited from the Dutch-speaking region of Belgium using multiple recruitment strategies designed to reach a diverse pool of potential participants (see Supplementary File 1 for a detailed description of the recruitment approach).

Screening for inclusion procedure

Potential participants will be invited to contact the research team via the study’s general email address in response to the study advertisement or after receiving a study flyer from their general practitioner. Initial screening will involve the completion of an online questionnaire designed to assess inclusion and exclusion criteria. Researchers will evaluate the responses, and any additional clarifications will be sought during a follow-up telephone or e-mail conversation. This conversation will also provide participants with a detailed explanation of the study procedures. Subsequently, eligible participants will receive the Informed Consent Form via email to review at their convenience. One week later, those who remain interested in participating will be provided with all practical details required for study participation.

Screening for underlying sleep pathologies will follow a two-step approach. First, the online questionnaire will gather information regarding potential sleep disorder diagnoses and any prior sleep assessments. Participants who have previously undergone sleep evaluations will be asked to share the results of this examination. Additionally, the screening process will incorporate two specific questionnaires to assess the risk of sleep apnea (i.e. STOP-Bang Questionnaire, high risk if answering “yes” to ≥5 questions [36]) and restless legs syndrome (RLS) (i.e. RLS Diagnostic Index, high risk if score ≥11 [37]). Individuals identified as being at higher risk will be referred to specialized care for further evaluation.

Participants with low risk based on the screening tools will be included in the study, even though certain conditions, such as sleep apnea, cannot be definitively ruled out. Emerging evidence suggests that individuals with comorbid sleep apnea and insomnia can benefit from CBT-i, even without targeted interventions like continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP). Several research groups have suggested that these patients should initially receive treatment for their insomnia before the sleep apnea is targeted [38]. Research indicates that clinical outcomes, including sleepiness and daytime fatigue, may improve following CBT-i, regardless of the presence of untreated sleep apnea [39]. Moreover, in cases where CPAP alone is insufficient to address symptoms such as fatigue, cognitive-behavioral mechanisms often play a contributing role, and these are effectively addressed through CBT-i. Although the presence of sleep apnea might warrant combined interventions (CBT-i and CPAP), participants unaware of concurrent sleep apnea are likely to benefit from CBT-i compared to receiving no intervention at all—an outcome more probable in the absence of study participation.

Randomization

Consented participants are randomized to one of the three study arms: (1) stepped care, (2) standard care, or (3) usual care. The randomization sequence was computer-generated by an independent researcher not involved in any other aspect of the trial. Block randomization was used with stratification factors for sex, dominant pain problem (neck or low back pain), and level of insomnia (moderate, with ISI score 15–21; or severe, with ISI score ≥22). The independent researcher placed papers indicating the group allocation in sequentially numbered opaque and sealed envelopes that are stored safely with no access for anyone aside from the independent researcher responsible for the randomization. Upon participant inclusion, the participant’s initials and study ID are written on the next envelope, after which the allocation is revealed. This way, concealed allocation is ensured.

Intervention

Standard care hCBT-i

Standard care hCBT-i will take 10 weeks, and will be delivered in a hybrid format, i.e. combining information transfer using a digital, online platform, self-management strategies, and two online, one-on-one sessions with a certified sleeptherapist (psychologist). The 10-week schedule differs in timing, yet non in content, from 6-weeks schedule often seen in literature to match the 10-week schedule in the stepped care group (see below). Although traditional CBT-i is often delivered in a fully face-to-face format, accumulating evidence suggest that digitally delivered and blended CBT-i interventions can achieve meaningful outcomes. Meta-analytic findings indicates that digitally delivered or blended CBT-i demonstrate similar effectiveness at the group level to in-person CBT-i, supporting their use as a viable alternative, particularly in contexts where accessibility to “offline” treatment is limited [40, 41]. For the online lessons, the online intervention BrainWise (www.Brainwise.be) will be used. The construction of this online intervention was based on self-help materials, research of the literature, and patient interviews. Brainwise consists of weekly online webinars and/or exercises and incorporates elements commonly used in face-to-face CBT-i. The 10-week program covers the following topics: (1) sleep education; (2) bedtime restriction incl. sleep diaries; (3) reprogramming of the “internal clock”; (4) sleep hygiene; (5) stress management; (6) daily life integration of sleep principles; (7) long-term relapse prevention. Each morning, participants are asked to fill out their sleep diary, of which the input is integrated into the program. Details on the content can be found in Supplementary File 2.

The intervention is supported by online coaching: at two specific time points (around week 3 and week 8) the therapist and participant will have a face-to-face online meeting. The therapist providing the support is experienced, trained, and certified in CBT-i and in delivering online feedback.

Stepped care CBT-i

The stepped care approach in CBT-i entails a two-step approach. Participants in this group all receive an initial entry-level session of 1 h delivered by a trained physiotherapist. Whether or not they are afterwards referred to the second step (standard care hCBT-i) depends on their remission status. In this way, the stepped-care pathway encompasses the complete CBT-i intervention when indicated. The content of this entry-level session was developed by two involved researchers (A.M. and O.M).

The “entry-level” (Step 1) in this stepped care approach will imply a single, 1-h long session, administered by a physiotherapist. We chose to integrate this session into physiotherapy care (i.e. delivered by a physiotherapist), as people with CSP are commonly and readily referred to physiotherapists by their doctors as an initial, easily accessible step [42]. Importantly, a systematic review has demonstrated that, with appropriate additional training, physiotherapists are able to competently and effectively deliver CBT [43]. This is further supported by multiple studies, including a RCT conducted by our group, which suggest that non-sleep experts (e.g. physiotherapists or nurses) can also successfully deliver CBT-i [19, 44]. However, it should be noted that, outside our own program of research, the literature has primarily examined the integration of specific CBT-informed techniques into physiotherapy practice rather than the administration of a full CBT-i protocol. Moreover, in our trial, physiotherapists received extensive specialized training prior to delivering the intervention. This single session will cover following aspects using a Socratic dialogue, an information leaflet and a workbook [30]: (1) quick checklist on sleep hygiene (incl. caffeine consumption, smoking, eating habits, use of smartphone, etc.); (2) introduction of several entry-level CBT-i principles including strategies to optimize them (e.g. what is sleep, what can I control and what not, process S and process C, sleep pressure, etc.) [4, 5, 28, 45]; (3) pinpointing 2–3 of the most relevant strategies for the participant; and (4) setting goals and creating an action plan for consistent implementation of these strategies over the next month. Step 1 goes beyond conventional sleep hygiene or general sleep education. It entails more than the transfer of knowledge alone, as it explicitly includes behavioral guidance and structured strategies to effectively modify sleep-related behaviors and cognitions. Importantly, Step 1 as a stand-alone intervention should not be considered a standardized or complete CBT-I protocol. Rather, it represents an initial, low-intensity introduction to core CBT-I principles. While this level of intervention may be sufficient for some patients, others will require progression to a comprehensive CBT-I program (i.e. the core principle of stepped care organization). Details on the content can be found in Supplementary File 3.

One month after “Step 1” the participants fill out the ISI again, and their score further guides decision making. Participants will be considered as having remitted if their posttreatment ISI score is <12, [30, 35] and will enter the follow-up phase immediately without receiving step 2 (standard care hCBT-i). Participants randomized to the stepped care hCBTi group whose ISI remained ≥12 after step 1 will be referred to step 2 [30], which involves standard care hCBT-i. In this case, participants will be granted access to the Brainwise platform and the online face-to-face consultations with the psychotherapist, where they will receive the same care as the standard care hCBT-i group, but within the remaining 6 weeks. This implies that the content and one-to-one therapist contact provided in Step 2 were identical to the CBT-i program delivered in the standard care group. No treatment sessions were omitted. Rather, the same number of sessions was delivered over a condensed period of 6 weeks instead of ten (see Supplementary Material for details).

Usual care

In the usual care group, participants will be asked to continue as usual regarding their sleep behavior and pain-related medical use. They can follow regular care for their pain- and/or sleep problem as recommended by their general practitioner. Their medical use and behavior will be monitored. Once data collection has ended in a usual care participant (i.e. at 12 months follow-up), they are offered a Standard Care hCBT-i intervention.

Involved therapists

The sleep therapist involved in the standard care hCBT-i trajectory, is an experienced and certified sleep therapist. No additional training on CBT-i content was provided. However, the therapist was trained for the methodological aspects of the study. This training took about 1 h, and afterwards, the therapist was provided with the detailed protocol, checklists, and flow charts to ensure adherence to the study’s protocol.

The physiotherapist involved in the stepped care treatment arm was trained in CBT-i by O.M., and expert in sleep and certified sleep therapist.

Treatment fidelity and adherence

The standard care hCBT-i program consists of two components: (1) fixed online content (including videos and other educational materials), and (2) online one-on-one sessions with a sleep therapist. While the fixed nature of the online materials ensures a degree of content consistency, the two one-on-one sessions allow the therapist to confirm whether participants engage with or comprehend the material as intended. Moreover, the digital platform allows the therapist to check the progression of the participant throughout the digital content, allowing to check the participants exposure to the content.

Importantly, for the therapist-led online one-on-one sessions, there is no standardized structure imposed. Therapists use these sessions primarily to assess participant motivation, monitor adherence, and troubleshoot difficulties with the program. Therefore, since the content of these sessions is neither fixed nor manualized and is primarily supportive in nature, no fidelity measures such as session audiotaping or independent rating are implemented. For these sessions, fidelity is monitored at the structural level through tracking of session frequency, timing, and duration.

The first session of the stepped care CBT-i arm adheres to a structured manual. The assumption is that rigorous manual adherence by a highly trained provider ensures fidelity. Consequently, these sessions are not audiotaped. To ensure ongoing verification of therapist competence and to mitigate the risk of fidelity drift over time, an independent researcher (Z.M.) will observe one session every 4 months. This periodic assessment serves to evaluate the therapist’s adherence to the information leaflet and workbook, and maintain consistency in delivery. Participants who do not remit after the stepped care session and subsequently transition into the standard care hCBT-i will follow the same procedure, including periodic fidelity checks as outlined.

Adverse events

Adverse events are recorded and classified in a systematic way (Supplementary File 4). Given the low level of expected risks, no rule for trial discontinuation was defined. After the study period and in the usual care arm, participants are not prohibited to have relevant concomitant care, but healthcare utilization will be documented.

Outcome assessment

Data collection procedures

Collected data include self-reported and objective data. Self-reported data are collected at baseline, immediately post-intervention, and at 6- and 12-month post-intervention using the REDCap online system (REDCap 12.4.13, Vanderbilt University, US). Objective sleep data are collected at home at baseline, immediately post-intervention, and 6-month post-intervention. For participants receiving standard CBT-i, a sleep diary and Experience Sampling Methods will ensure daily data collection on sleep quality, nocturnal experiences (e.g. pain, fatigue, restlessness, worrying, and perceived rest), daily burden, and health status.

Demographic data

At baseline, information on age, height, weight, sex, educational level, employment status, and marital status will be collected.

Primary outcome: rate of insomnia remission

The primary outcome is the rate of insomnia remission at 6 months post-treatment (= primary endpoint). Rate of insomnia remission will be assessed using the ISI, a self-report questionnaire assessing the nature, severity, and impact of insomnia [35, 46]. Participants will be considered treatment responders if their ISI score improves by ≥8 points and will be considered as having remitted if their post-treatment ISI score is <12 [30, 35].

Secondary outcomes

Different secondary outcomes will be assessed, including pain-related outcomes, sleep-related outcomes, symptoms of anxiety and depression, health-related quality of life, and healthcare use, and productivity (loss). Blinding

As data collection is self-administered at home (questionnaires and sleep assessments), blinding of researchers to randomization is not required. Participants are aware of their allocation but not of the study hypothesis. Therapists are also unblinded; however, each therapist is assigned to a single treatment arm to prevent cross-contamination. The statistician will remain blinded during analysis, with group allocation information removed from the dataset beforehand.

Analytic strategies

Sample size estimation

The required sample was estimated using G*Power 3.1.9.6 (F-tests, Analysis of Variance (ANOVA): repeated measures, within-between interaction), relying on the primary endpoint (i.e. number of measurements = 2), comparison between three groups, correlation among repeated measures of 0.3 (conservative approach), and effect size (f) of 0.2 based on Zhou et al. [30]. Alpha was set at 0.05 (two-sided), power was 0.80. A total sample size of n = 90 at the primary endpoint (T3—6 months post intervention) was determined. Considering a 30 per cent potential dropout rate based on our prior studies [19], the sample target is adjusted to n = 129 (43 per group) to ensure at least 90 participants at the primary endpoint.

Handling of missing data and drop-outs

Specific efforts will be made to minimize missing data and participant drop-out. Participants who withdraw will be contacted by e-mail or telephone to collect their reasons, enabling better interpretation of missingness patterns and to check the assumption of missing at random.

Statistical analysis to answer the primary research question: “Is stepped care CBT-i non-inferior to standard CBT-i with respect to remission from chronic insomnia among people with CSP at 6 months post-treatment, and superior to usual care?”

Remission from insomnia is defined as a binary variable (remission: yes/no). Group differences in remission rates will first be explored descriptively, with Chi-square tests to examine overall associations between treatment allocation and remission status. The primary analysis will be conducted according to the intention-to-treat-principle, using binary logistic regression, with remission status as the dependent variable and treatment group as the independent variable (categorical, with usual care as the reference category). This will allow estimation of odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for remission in each CBT-i condition relative to usual care. Planned contrasts will also test stepped care CBT-i versus standard CBT-i directly. Potential confounders will be explored and added to the model if needed. Results will be presented as absolute remission rates by group and adjusted ORs. Where appropriate, absolute risk differences and numbers needed to treat will also be reported for clinical interpretability. Participants with missing outcome data at 6 months will be handled using multiple imputation under the missing-at-random assumption; sensitivity analyses using complete-case data will be performed to assess robustness.

In addition to the primary 6-month analysis, longitudinal effects of treatment on remission from insomnia across all follow-up assessments will be examined using Generalized Estimating Equations, including treatment group, time, and their interaction as predictors.

Statistical analysis to answer the secondary research question focused on cost-effectiveness (i.e. cost-utility analysis)

The cost-utility analysis (CUA) will be conducted using the Belgian Health Care Knowledge Centre guidelines [60], EUnetHTA guidelines [61] and the handbook developed by Drummond et al. (fourth edition) [62], and reported in line with the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards [63]. CUA assessments entail the Medical Consumption Questionnaire [53], the Productivity Cost Questionnaire [53], and the EQ-5D-5L [54, 55], and will take place at baseline, immediately post-intervention and at 6- and 12-months follow-up, resulting in a time horizon from baseline until 12 months post-intervention. Quality-adjusted life years will be calculated from utility scores derived from the EuroQol EQ-5D using the value set for Belgium [64] as the health effect. A healthcare payer—necessary for reimbursement requests—and societal perspective will be evaluated, with the latter including both direct healthcare costs (intervention cost plus post-intervention healthcare expenditure) and indirect costs (productivity loss costs). Healthcare use will be valued using unit reference prices as published by RIZIV-INAMI [65, 66] and BCFI-CBIP [67]. Incremental cost-effectiveness ratios will be calculated, and probabilistic sensitivity analysis will be performed. Impact of methodological choices will be evaluated by relevant scenario analyses. All details on this analysis can be found in Supplementary File 6.

Statistical analysis to answer the secondary research question focused on clinical effectiveness (i.e. changes/differences in pain, sleep quality, physical function, medication use, and treatment adherence)

For medication use data, longitudinal group differences will be examined using Generalized Estimating Equations with time as a repeated measure and treatment group as a fixed factor. ORs with CIs will be reported for group-by-time effects.

Treatment adherence across the treatment period will be analyzed either as a binary outcome (meeting a predefined adherence threshold, e.g. ≥80 per cent of sessions) or as a continuous measure (percentage of sessions attended). For repeated binary adherence data, generalized estimating equations will be used. For continuous adherence, linear mixed models will be applied, including participant-level random intercepts and fixed effects for treatment group and time.

Last, clinical patient-reported outcomes (like pain, sleep quality, and physical function) will be analyzed longitudinally using linear mixed-effects models, with the outcome at each follow-up as the dependent variable, treatment group and time as fixed factors, and baseline value as a covariate. Random intercepts will account for within-subject correlation across repeated measurements. Group-by-time interactions will test whether treatment effects differ across follow-up points. Adjusted mean differences with 95% CIs and standardized effect sizes (Cohen’s d or partial η2) will be reported.

All secondary analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle. Missing data will be handled using maximum likelihood estimation within linear mixed models/generalized estimating equations models or via multiple imputation under a missing-at-random assumption, with complete-case analyses conducted as sensitivity checks.

Additional analyses

Depending on the distribution of the data, different post-hoc analyses may be conducted after finalization of the trial. These can include exploratory moderator and mediation analyses.

Discussion

This randomized controlled trial represents the first study to evaluate the (cost-)effectiveness of stepped care CBT-i compared to standard CBT-i and usual care in individuals with CSP and comorbid chronic insomnia. Given the high prevalence of both CSP and chronic insomnia, together with the limited availability of specialized care, a stepped care approach—if proven clinically and cost-effective—has the potential to significantly enhance treatment accessibility, optimize healthcare resource utilization, and reduce associated costs for this patient population. Due to its proven efficacy, clinical impact, cost-effectiveness, and feasibility in other populations and for other psychological interventions, the stepped care approach is put forward by experts as a promising strategy [68–70], which can increase access to high quality interventions [71–75].

The findings of this study are expected to have wide-ranging implications for clinical practice, policy, and healthcare planning. Demonstrating the (cost-)effectiveness of stepped care CBT-i may inform clinical decision-making, facilitate the development of evidence-based treatment pathways, and support the implementation of a scalable stepped care model across diverse healthcare settings. In particular, the stepped care model allows for broader dissemination of initial intervention steps, as it can be delivered by a variety of healthcare professionals, thereby expanding patient access without compromising quality. While empirical validation of stepped care CBT-i in CSP is currently lacking, preliminary qualitative evidence from oncology clinicians (n = 42) and cancer patients (n = 10) supports its feasibility and acceptability in real-world oncology practice, underscoring the potential for successful translation into routine care [31] and the need to study it in non-oncological patient populations.

Economic evaluation is a critical component of this study. By assessing both direct healthcare costs and broader cost-effectiveness outcomes—including productivity loss—the trial provides a comprehensive evaluation of the intervention’s socioeconomic impact. This is particularly relevant given that productivity loss constitutes a major cost driver for individuals with CSP [76, 77]. Integrating cost-effectiveness analyses alongside clinical outcomes allows for a more holistic understanding of the value of stepped care CBT-i and strengthens the evidence base for its potential implementation in routine practice.

Methodologically, the trial is designed to maximize rigor and generalizability. The inclusion of three arms (stepped care CBT-i, standard CBT-i, and usual care) enables robust causal inference and allows differentiation of effects attributable to each intervention. Recruitment strategies aim to capture a diverse and clinically relevant population, enhancing external validity. The primary outcome, insomnia remission, is clinically meaningful, dichotomous, and readily interpretable, while secondary outcomes capture a multidimensional profile of patient benefit, including medication use, treatment adherence, sleep quality, pain, and physical function. Longitudinal assessments across baseline and three follow-up time points, combined with appropriate statistical models, ensure accurate modelling of treatment effects over time. Moreover, efforts to ensure methodological rigor include blinding of outcome assessors and statisticians, standardized delivery of interventions, and the incorporation of objective sleep measures to complement self-reported outcomes and mitigate bias. Although self-reported data may still introduce recall or social desirability bias, these measures strengthen internal validity and provide a robust assessment of both clinical and patient-centred outcomes.

Conclusion

In summary, this trial addresses an important knowledge gap in the management of chronic insomnia in individuals with CSP. By evaluating both (cost-)effectiveness within a pragmatic, scalable framework, the study has the potential to inform evidence-based care pathways, improve patient outcomes, and guide resource allocation in healthcare systems more efficiently for people with CSP and comorbid chronic insomnia.
