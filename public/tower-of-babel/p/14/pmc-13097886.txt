Prediction of bowel resection in acute irreducible inguinal hernia: a retrospective analysis of preoperative computed tomography and clinical risk factors
Authors: Nurettin Sahin, Turgut Donmez, Ferman Tevfik Ozyalvac, Emine Ayca Sahin, Muge Yurdacan, Mahmut Said Degerli, Sezer Bulut, Elif Hocaoglu, Mustafa Orhan Nalbant, Ozden Canoz, Alpen Yahya Gumusoglu, Mert Ozdemir, Suna Nur Gundogan, Ahmet Surek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIn acute irreducible inguinal hernias, the presence of bowel strangulation is the most critical determinant of clinical urgency and prognosis. Preoperative identification of strangulation can prompt urgent surgical intervention and potentially prevent bowel resection and its associated complications. This study aimed to identify preoperative risk factors associated with bowel resection in these patients.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included patients who underwent emergency surgery for acutely irreducible inguinal hernia at our institution between 2017 and 2024. Patients were categorized into two groups based on whether bowel resection was performed. Clinical, laboratory, and radiological variables were compared between the groups.ResultsA total of 182 patients met the inclusion criteria. Bowel resection was performed in 45 cases (24.7%). These patients had significantly older age, female predominance, ASA and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) scores, longer symptom and operative durations, and higher rates of femoral hernia, peritonitis signs, elevated CRP, leukocytosis, and increased neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR). CT findings associated with resection included smaller defect and hernia diameters, reduced sac volume and bowel enhancement, increased bowel wall thickness, and a higher incidence of ileus. Multivariate logistic regression identified CCI, NLR, peritonitis signs, intraoperative bowel obstruction, bowel wall thickness, defect diameter, and lack of enhancement as independent predictors of resection.ConclusionThis study identified specific preoperative clinical, laboratory, and CT features that may help identify patients at increased risk of bowel resection in acute irreducible inguinal hernias. These findings may support early risk stratification and assist operative planning in emergency settings.Keywords: Bowel resection, Computed tomography, Irreducible inguinal hernia, Risk factors

Introduction

Inguinal hernia is defined as the protrusion of the peritoneum through a weakened area in the groin, forming a hernia sac. As it may contain intra-abdominal organs or structures, the definitive treatment is surgical [1]. It is a common condition, with approximately 20 million inguinal hernia repair operations performed worldwide each year. Elective repairs of inguinal hernias are generally safe and have a low risk profile [2]. However, in cases where complications such as incarceration, strangulation, or bowel obstruction occur, emergency surgical intervention may be required [3].

In previous HerniaSurge guidelines, the term incarceration was used to describe an irreducible hernia, whereas strangulation referred to compromised vascular perfusion within the hernia sac. However, recent updates to the guidelines argue that the term incarcerated hernia inadequately characterizes acute hernias, and instead propose three new definitions. Hernias that were previously reducible but become irreducible at the onset of acute symptoms are now referred to as acute irreducible hernias. In contrast, chronic irreducible hernias are defined as those present for a long time without new symptoms and are not reducible on physical examination. The definition of strangulated hernia remains unchanged but is now strictly applied to cases confirmed through intraoperative findings or preoperative imaging that demonstrates compromised perfusion [4].

In both acute and chronic irreducible hernias, the presence or absence of strangulation is the critical determinant. In cases where the hernia sac contains small bowel or colon and strangulation is present, bowel resection may be necessary. Several factors have been identified as predictors of intestinal necrosis or the need for resection, including female sex, advanced age (particularly > 65 years), femoral hernia type, longer duration of symptoms, presence of bowel obstruction, elevated white blood cell count, and increased neutrophil count [2, 3].

Approximately 5–15% of patients with inguinal hernias present with an acutely irreducible form, and among these, up to 15% develop bowel ischemia requiring urgent surgery and bowel resection [5]. While the mortality rate in elective hernia surgeries is around 0.1%, it increases to 7.0% in emergency cases and up to 25% in patients undergoing bowel resection [6]. Conversely, in the absence of strangulation, successful manual reduction followed by planned elective repair may help avoid the risks associated with emergency surgery [7]. If the hernia can be successfully reduced back into the abdomen and the patient stabilized, one can then proceed with an elective hernia repair soon afterward, rather than an emergent operation under suboptimal conditions. Studies have shown that manual reduction followed by delayed repair is a feasible strategy in select patients, with successful reduction rates on the order of 60–70% in experienced hands. This approach can avert the need for emergency bowel surgery and thereby avoid the higher perioperative risks, especially in elderly or comorbid patients. Alongside its potential benefits, manual reduction may be associated with complications such as reduction of the entire hernia sac together with the incarcerated bowel (reduction en masse), reduction of ischemic or necrotic intestine, and perforation of necrotic bowel [8].

The ability to preoperatively distinguish a strangulated hernia from a simple irreducible hernia is of critical clinical importance. Therefore, this study aimed to identify preoperative indicators—including clinical findings, laboratory parameters, and computed tomography features—associated with strangulation in acute irreducible inguinal hernias. Specifically, we sought to determine factors related to bowel ischemia or necrosis requiring resection, with the goal of supporting early surgical decision-making and improving the management of patients with incarcerated groin hernias.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

This study is a retrospective observational analysis of patients with acute irreducible inguinal hernia who underwent emergency surgery at a tertiary care hospital. The study period spanned from January 2017 through August 2024. All patients presenting to the emergency department with an acute irreducible inguinal hernia and taken for urgent surgical intervention were reviewed. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to define the study cohort:

Inclusion criteria: (1) age 18 years or older; (2) emergency surgery performed for an acute irreducible (incarcerated) inguinal hernia, including both primary and recurrent cases; (3) availability of a preoperative intravenous (IV) contrast-enhanced abdominopelvic computed tomography (CT) scan; and (4) available results for preoperative laboratory tests, including complete blood count (CBC) and C-reactive protein (CRP).

Inclusion criteria: (1) age 18 years or older; (2) emergency surgery performed for an acute irreducible (incarcerated) inguinal hernia, including both primary and recurrent cases; (3) availability of a preoperative intravenous (IV) contrast-enhanced abdominopelvic computed tomography (CT) scan; and (4) available results for preoperative laboratory tests, including complete blood count (CBC) and C-reactive protein (CRP).

Exclusion criteria: (1) age under 18; (2) no preoperative CT scan obtained; (3) CT scans that were non-contrast or did not demonstrate any bowel content within the hernia sac (e.g., hernia containing only omentum, bladder or fluid); and (4) incomplete medical records or missing key data.

Exclusion criteria: (1) age under 18; (2) no preoperative CT scan obtained; (3) CT scans that were non-contrast or did not demonstrate any bowel content within the hernia sac (e.g., hernia containing only omentum, bladder or fluid); and (4) incomplete medical records or missing key data.

Using these criteria, eligible cases were identified from a total of 536 patients who underwent emergency surgery for acute irreducible inguinal hernia during the study period. Patients were stratified into two groups based on the primary outcome of whether bowel resection was required: a non-resected inguinal hernia (NRIH) group and a resected inguinal hernia (RIH) group. In the NRIH cohort (initial n = 451), 22 patients were excluded due to missing data and 183 patients who did not have a preoperative CT scan; one patient was further excluded due to significant CT image artifacts (hip prothesis that precluded evaluation of the inguinal region). Of the remaining 245 cases with available CT imaging, 42 patients had only non-contrast CT studies and 66 patients had no bowel loops within the hernia sac on CT (omentum in 57 patients, adnexa in 3, urinary bladder in 4, appendix in 1, and ascitic fluid in 1 patient), and these cases were excluded as well. This yielded a final NRIH sample of 137 patients who met all inclusion criteria.

Similarly, in the RIH cohort (initial n = 85), 25 patients were excluded for lack of a preoperative CT and 15 were excluded for having a non-contrast CT, resulting in a final RIH group of 45 patients for analysis. Thus, a total of 182 patients (137 NRIH and 45 RIH) were included in the study. The patient selection process is illustrated in Figure 1.

Demographic and clinical data were collected from patient records, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, history of previous abdominal surgery, smoking and alcohol use, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). The duration of symptoms (day), time from hospital admission to surgery, and the month and season in which surgery was performed were also documented. Clinical findings at the time of emergency admission were evaluated, including the presence of fever (body temperature ≥ 38.0 °C), hypotension, tachycardia, and signs of peritonitis on physical examination (rebound tenderness, guarding, or abdominal rigidity). The type (inguinal or femoral), side, and recurrence status of the hernia were recorded. Laboratory data included preoperative CRP levels, leukocyte count, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), lymphocyte-to-CRP ratio (LCR), and serum lactate values. Intraoperative findings such as bowel perfusion status, duration and type of surgery, use of mesh, presence of bowel obstruction or perforation and anesthesia type were recorded. Postoperative outcomes included reoperation, morbidity (Clavien–Dindo classification) and mortality. All data were compiled using Microsoft Excel 2019, and variables were compared between the RIH and NRIH groups to identify potential predictors of bowel resection.

Intraoperative confirmation of strangulation was made by the surgical team. Bowel resection was performed in cases with frank necrosis, visible perforation, persistent dusky or ischemic coloration despite warm saline application, or absent motility upon tactile stimulation. Histopathological examination of the resected specimens confirmed the presence of ischemia and/or necrosis.

In addition, intraoperative findings suggestive of mechanical obstruction were recorded. A proximal-to-distal luminal diameter difference of twofold or greater observed intraoperatively in the incarcerated bowel loop was interpreted as a sign of mechanical obstruction.

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration. Approval was obtained from the local ethics committee prior to data collection (Approval Date: 25/12/2024, Decision No: 2024-15-19). Due to the retrospective nature of the study, informed consent was not obtained from all patients.

Imaging protocol and analysis

All included patients underwent preoperative contrast-enhanced abdominopelvic CT scanning prior to surgery. CT scans were performed using a helical multi-detector scanner (Siemens Healthineers, Germany) with patients in the supine position, with arms raised above the head. No specific fasting was required due to the emergency setting. Scan parameters included a tube voltage of 120 kVp, tube current of 150–200 mAs, and a slice thickness of ≤ 5 mm. The scan field of view (FOV) encompassed from the diaphragm to the pubic symphysis (approximately 30–40 cm).

Both non-contrast and contrast-enhanced phases were obtained in appropriate patients. A total of 100–120 mL of non-ionic contrast agent (300 mg/mL) was administered intravenously at a rate of 2–3 mL/s using an automated injector. Scanning delay was 70 s for venous phase and 30–35 s for arterial phase, depending on the protocol.

Soft tissue window settings were used for interpretation (Window width: 350 [200–600]; Window level: 50 [30–60])

All CT images were retrospectively re-evaluated by an experienced radiologist specializing in abdominal imaging, who was blinded to surgical outcomes. The following CT variables were analyzed for each case: the hernia defect diameter, the maximal hernia sac diameter and the hernia sac volume. In addition, several radiologic signs indicative of bowel compromise or obstruction were recorded. These included bowel wall thickening within the herniated loop, the degree of contrast enhancement of the bowel wall (categorized qualitatively as absent, reduced, or normal enhancement), the presence of intramural gas within the bowel wall, the presence of free air or free fluid in the abdominal cavity (pneumoperitoneum or ascites) suggestive of perforation or peritonitis, the presence of fluid within the hernia sac, signs of ileus or small bowel obstruction on imaging (such as proximal intestinal dilation with distal collapse), and the maximal diameter of dilated proximal bowel loops on the CT scan. All measurements were performed using the radiology workstation’s software tools. The radiologist’s CT analysis was conducted retrospectively and documented for subsequent correlation with surgical findings.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics included means, standard deviations, medians, minimums, maximums, frequencies, and percentages. Distribution of variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests. For normally distributed quantitative variables, the independent samples t-test was used; for non-normally distributed variables, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to assess effect size. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 182 patients were included in the study, with 137 patients (75.3%) in the NRIH group and 45 patients (24.7%) in the RIH group. The mean age of the cohort was 67.7 ± 13.3 years. Of the patients, 75.3% (n = 137) were male and 24.7% (n = 45) were female. The mean body mass index (BMI) was calculated as 25.4 ± 4.0. A total of 57.2% of the patients were classified as ASA class III or IV. The average Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score was 3.4 ± 2.0.

Emergency department evaluation

The mean duration of symptoms prior to presentation was 1.9 ± 1.8 days. The average time between hospital admission and surgery was 528.0 ± 209.8 min. Most hernias were located on the right side. The recurrence rate was 14.3%. At the time of emergency admission, fever (≥ 38 °C) was detected in 3 patients (1.6%), tachycardia in 22 patients (12.1%), hypotension in 14 patients (7.7%), and hypertension in 28 patients (15.4%). Signs of peritonitis on physical examination were present in 87 patients (47.8%).

Preoperative abdominal CT findings

Preoperative CT scans were re-evaluated for all included patients. The mean defect diameter was 27.5 ± 9.7 mm, mean hernia sac diameter was 57.5 ± 19.9 mm, and mean hernia sac volume was 179.3 ± 260.1 mL. Ratios such as hernia diameter-to-defect diameter, hernia volume-to-defect diameter, and hernia volume-to-hernia diameter were calculated accordingly. Bowel wall thickening was observed in 92.9% of patients, with a mean wall thickness of 4.6 ± 1.2 mm. Radiological signs of ileus were present in 61.5% (n = 112), and the mean diameter of dilated bowel loops in these patients was 35.7 ± 5.9 mm. Normal contrast enhancement of the bowel wall was observed in 60 patients (33.0%), decreased in 108 (59.3%), and absent in 14 (7.7%). Fluid in the hernia sac was detected in 83.5% of patients, while 33.5% had free intra-abdominal fluid. Intramural air was present in 14 patients (7.7%), and intra-abdominal free air was noted in one patient (0.5%).

Perioperative and postoperative outcomes

Bowel resection was performed in 45 patients (24.7%), while 137 patients (75.3%) underwent hernia repair without resection. The mean duration of surgery was 96.2 ± 36.2 min. Open surgery was performed in 93.4% of cases. In these patients, the surgical techniques included Lichtenstein mesh repair, primary suture repair for inguinal hernia, plug mesh repair for femoral hernia, and primary repair for femoral hernia. Among patients who underwent laparoscopic surgery, the transabdominal preperitoneal (TAPP) approach was exclusively utilized. All 45 patients in the resection group underwent bowel resection as an additional procedure. These resections were performed through the inguinal incision in 15 patients, via midline laparotomy in 25 patients, laparoscopically in 2 patients, and by extending the camera trocar site following laparoscopy in 3 patients. Furthermore, diagnostic laparoscopy was performed in 51 patients who initially underwent open surgery. Mesh was not used in 25 patients (13.7%). Among them, 21 cases were excluded from mesh repair due to suspected intestinal contamination during bowel resection, while in 4 cases, severe edema and concern for bacterial translocation led to the decision to avoid mesh placement.

Signs of bowel obstruction were present intraoperatively in 57.7% of patients, and signs of perforation in 7.1%. Bowel resection was performed in all patients with irreversible ischemia or necrosis. Morbidity occurred in 18.7% of patients, and 6 required reoperation. In the RIH group, four patients (8.9%) required reoperation, all due to wound-related complications. In the NRIH group, two patients (1.5%) required reoperation; one due to postoperative ileus and one due to delayed bowel perforation. The mortality rate was 7.7%.

Comparison between RIH and NRIH groups

Patients in the RIH group had significantly higher age (p = 0.035), female sex ratio (p < 0.001), ASA scores (p < 0.001), CCI scores (p < 0.001), and prevalence of hypertension (p = 0.003) compared to the NRIH group. Smoking prevalence (p = 0.027) was significantly lower in the RIH group, likely due to the older age of this cohort. No significant differences were found between the groups in terms of BMI, alcohol use, other comorbidities (such as heart failure, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, malignancy, chronic kidney disease, benign prostatic hypertrophy, cirrhosis), prior abdominal surgeries, hernia side, recurrence rate, or season of surgery (all p > 0.05). Duration of symptoms (p < 0.001) and operative time (p < 0.001) were significantly higher in the RIH group, while the time from admission to surgery (p = 0.150) was similar. Femoral hernia was significantly more common in the RIH group (p < 0.001). Signs of peritonitis on physical examination were also significantly more frequent in the RIH group (p < 0.001). At presentation, normotension was more frequent in the RIH group (p = 0.008), whereas rates of fever (p = 1.000) and tachycardia (p = 0.177) were similar. CRP (p < 0.001), leukocyte count (p = 0.007), and NLR (p < 0.001) were significantly higher in the RIH group, while LCR (p < 0.001) was significantly lower. No significant difference was found in lactate levels (p = 0.143). Mesh usage during hernia repair was significantly lower in the RIH group (p < 0.001). Rates of bowel obstruction (p < 0.001), perforation (p < 0.001), reoperation (p = 0.034), morbidity (p < 0.001), and mortality (p < 0.001) were all significantly higher in the RIH group. Comparison of CT findings between groups

When comparing CT findings between the two groups, defect diameter (p < 0.001), hernia diameter (p = 0.003), hernia sac volüme (p = 0.011), and hernia volume-to-hernia diameter ratio (p = 0.024) were significantly lower in the RIH group, while the hernia diameter-to-defect diameter ratio was significantly higher (p = 0.001). No significant difference was found in the hernia volume-to-defect diameter ratio (p = 0.369). The mean bowel wall thickness was significantly greater in the RIH group (p < 0.001). The presence of ileus was also more frequent in the RIH group (p < 0.001), although the diameter of dilated loops did not differ significantly (p = 0.074). The presence of bowel wall enhancement was significantly lower in the RIH group (p < 0.001), whereas the presence of intramural air was significantly higher (p < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in the presence of fluid within the hernia sac (p = 0.512) or intra-abdominal free air (p = 0.247). However, intra-abdominal free fluid was significantly more common in the RIH group (p = 0.012). A full comparison of CT findings is provided in Table 2.

Discussion

Strangulation is the most common and serious complication of inguinal hernias, occurring in approximately 1.3–3% of cases and significantly increasing both morbidity and mortality. Identifying the presence of strangulation and determining whether the incarcerated hernia is associated with a simple mechanical obstruction or with complications such as ischemia, necrosis, or perforation is quite difficult [9]. If strangulation can be ruled out and the hernia is reducible, emergency surgery and even hospitalization may be avoided, and elective hernia repair can be planned. This is particularly beneficial in patients using anticoagulants, those who are obese, have comorbidities, or in situations where surgical intervention is challenging (e.g., during a pandemic). Manual reduction is a simple and non-invasive method suitable for patients with a short history of incarcerated inguinal hernia, particularly within the first 24 h after symptom onset [10]. In the study by Kohga et al., which compared elective hernia surgery after reduction versus emergency surgery, patients in the reduction group had shorter hospital stays, higher mesh usage rates, and lower complication incidences [11]. In our clinical practice, manual reduction is routinely attempted when clinically appropriate; however, all patients included in this study ultimately required emergency surgical exploration due to persistent irreducibility or concern for strangulation.

In incarcerated hernias, the contents of the sac cannot be returned to their original compartment. Strangulation occurs when blood flow to the herniated contents is compromised, eventually leading to ischemia and necrosis. Therefore, surgical repair is the only definitive treatment method for emergency inguinal hernias [12]. Early diagnosis is known to be the primary factor that improves prognosis [13]. Morbidity and mortality were found to be significantly higher in the resection group in this study. The higher morbidity and mortality observed in the resection group may be associated with advanced bowel ischemia and necrosis, longer operative times, and an increased systemic inflammatory response related to potential contamination risk. This finding may also suggest that patients requiring resection present at a more advanced clinical stage, which may adversely affect prognosis.

Preoperative diagnosis and classification of adult inguinal hernias are important for determining the indication for surgical repair and selecting the appropriate surgical technique. The European Hernia Society recommends physical examination as the standard diagnostic approach. However, this may be insufficient in certain populations. In obese patients, physical examination can be inconclusive, and in female patients, the coexistence of femoral and inguinal hernias necessitates accurate differentiation. In such cases, additional diagnostic methods may be required. Herniography is an invasive procedure, whereas ultrasonography is operator-dependent [14]. In the systematic review by Piga et al., which included 27 studies, ultrasound was shown to be more effective than Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) and CT in detecting inguinal hernias [15]. In the meta-analysis by Robinson et al., CT ranked third after herniography and ultrasound for the detection of occult inguinal hernias, with a reported sensitivity of 80% and specificity of 65% [16]. In the study by Miller et al., most CT and MRI reports were found to be inaccurate for assessing occult inguinal hernias [17]. For these reasons, prone-position CT with the Valsalva maneuver has been proposed to increase CT accuracy in detecting inguinal hernias. In the study by Ghafoor et al., high specificity but moderate sensitivity was reported when CT was performed with the Valsalva maneuver, noting that sensitivity was influenced by hernia size [18]. In a separate study, Kamei et al. reported a sensitivity of 98.3% and specificity of 100% for prone-position abdominal CT in detecting inguinal hernias, with accurate classification of hernia type in 95.8% of cases. Notably, 43 patients had incidental non-hernia pathology identified [14]. Some studies have examined the role of CT in distinguishing between inguinal and femoral hernias and have proposed various anatomical landmarks for this distinction. Delabrousse et al. suggested using the pubic tubercle as a reference point on CT [19], while Burkhardt et al. identified the course of the inferior epigastric vessels and the common femoral vein as critical markers [20]. Kitami et al. indicated that multidimensional CT (MDCT) can differentiate hernia types based on their relationship to the inguinal ligament [21]. In this study, CT imaging was performed in the supine position without the Valsalva maneuver, reflecting routine emergency department practice. Although prone positioning or Valsalva-enhanced CT protocols have been reported to improve hernia detection rates under elective conditions, these techniques may be difficult to apply in patients presenting with acute pain, suspected strangulation, or clinical instability. Supine contrast-enhanced CT may allow rapid assessment of bowel perfusion, obstruction, and potential complications without delaying surgical decision-making. However, the absence of dynamic maneuvers may limit sensitivity for small or occult hernias, and this limitation should be considered when interpreting radiologic findings.

The role of preoperative CT in emergency inguinal hernias remains controversial. Some authors advocate for its use to determine hernia type and identify ischemia or strangulation, while others caution that imaging may delay surgical intervention in patients requiring urgent repair. CT can also reveal non-hernia pathologies [22]. In the study by Knewitz et al., at least one additional abnormal finding was identified in 64% of patients who underwent preoperative CT scanning. Although the time from emergency admission to surgery was longer in patients who underwent CT, the non-CT group had a higher rate of bowel resection. This was attributed to surgeons opting for faster surgery in the presence of signs of bowel necrosis or perforation. As a result, CT imaging was advised in cases of diagnostic uncertainty, while clinical evaluation should remain the principal decision-making tool in other scenarios [22]. Similarly, Ibrahim et al., it was suggested that preoperative CT scanning in emergency hernia cases may delay surgical intervention and increase the likelihood of bowel resection [23]. In this study, the mean duration from hospital admission to surgery was 540.8 ± 216.2 min in the NRIH group and 488.9 ± 185.5 min in the RIH group. Although this difference was not statistically significant, only patients who underwent contrast-enhanced abdominal CT were included in the study, and therefore no definitive conclusions can be drawn regarding whether CT imaging contributed to surgical delay.

The most important issue in acute irreducible inguinal hernias is whether intestinal ischemia is present. Intraoperative evaluation of bowel microcirculation and viability is generally based on the color of the serosal surface, presence of peristalsis, palpable mesenteric pulsation, and bleeding from marginal arteries. These are subjective criteria and dependent on surgical experience [24]. Various methods have been proposed to standardize this evaluation. Among them, indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence imaging has emerged as the most widely used and validated method, and it is currently recommended by the World Society of Emergency Surgery (WSES) [25]. In this study, bowel viability was assessed intraoperatively based on color, peristalsis, mesenteric pulsation, and bleeding. However, the critical issue is the preoperative identification of intestinal ischemia or necrosis. For this purpose, preoperative CT has been suggested as a supportive tool for surgeons, and limited studies have evaluated CT findings. Most such studies were not specific to hernia patients but investigated bowel ischemia of other causes. In the study by Kohga et al., mean CT attenuation values of the intestine, presence of mesenteric fluid, and free peritoneal fluid were proposed as predictors of irreversible ischemic changes in strangulated ileus [26]. In the study by Millet et al., reduced bowel wall enhancement was reported as the most specific finding, increasing the likelihood of ischemia by 11-fold. The mesenteric fluid sign was identified as the most sensitive finding, considered reliable for ruling out strangulation [27]. In another study by Millet et al., CT findings in adhesive small bowel obstruction were examined. Decreased wall enhancement, diffuse mesenteric haziness, and a closed-loop mechanism were associated with strangulation. In strangulated cases, reduced wall enhancement and mesenteric fluid were predictors of bowel resection [28]. Zhou et al. evaluated incarcerated inguinal hernias and reported significant differences in internal ring diameter and the ratio of hernia sac volume to ring diameter between patients who underwent bowel resection and those who did not [29]. In this study, several CT parameters (including defect diameter, hernia diameter, hernia-to-defect diameter ratio, bowel wall thickness, bowel wall enhancement, presence of intramural gas, intra-abdominal free fluid, and signs of ileus) were found to be significantly associated with bowel resection. In a multivariate logistic regression model using only CT findings, four variables independently predicted resection: smaller defect diameter, increased bowel wall thickness, absence or reduction of wall enhancement, and the presence of ileus. Among these, the presence of ileus had the highest odds ratio (OR: 3.766, 95% CI: 1.255–11.306). The association between smaller defect diameter and bowel resection in this study may be explained by a narrow hernia neck impairing venous return at an early stage, thereby facilitating bowel edema and ischemia. Increased bowel wall thickness may reflect venous congestion and inflammatory edema, representing an indirect sign of circulatory compromise, whereas reduced or absent bowel wall enhancement and the presence of intramural air may be considered earlier radiologic indicators of impaired perfusion. In contrast, intra-abdominal free fluid and signs of ileus may represent later findings requiring time for bowel dilatation and third-space fluid sequestration to develop. Taken together, these observations suggest that some CT parameters may directly reflect vascular insufficiency, while others may represent secondary changes related to the temporal progression of strangulation.

Various biomarkers have been studied to predict strangulation or bowel resection in acute irreducible inguinal hernia cases. Among these, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and lymphocyte-to-CRP ratio (LCR) have been the most commonly studied. In the study by Zhou et al., NLR > 6.5, femoral hernia type, and incarceration of small bowel were significantly associated with strangulation [13]. In another study by Zhou et al., NLR was significantly higher in patients who underwent resection, although it was not an independent risk factor [29]. In the study by Yildirim et al., neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, CRP, platelet count, NLR, and LCR values were all significant in predicting strangulation in incarcerated inguinal hernias, with LCR being more prominent [30]. In this study, preoperative laboratory values obtained at the time of emergency department admission were evaluated in this study. CRP, leukocyte count, and NLR were significantly higher in the bowel resection group, while LCR was significantly lower. According to multivariate logistic regression, only NLR was identified as an independent predictor factor for bowel resection in acute irreducible inguinal hernias. The finding that only NLR remained independently associated with bowel resection may suggest a role of systemic inflammatory response in the pathophysiology of strangulation. Increased neutrophil counts together with relative lymphopenia may reflect the stress response and inflammatory burden related to bowel ischemia.

Several previous studies have investigated the risk factors associated with bowel resection in patients with incarcerated inguinal hernias. Chen et al. identified increased age (≥ 70 years), elevated body temperature (≥ 37.3 °C), high systemic immune-inflammation index (SII ≥ 1230.13), presence of bowel obstruction, and clinical signs of peritonitis as independent predictors of bowel resection [31]. In a study by Beji et al. on incarcerated femoral hernias, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), serum urea level, and the time interval from symptom onset to surgery were found to be significant independent variables for predicting bowel necrosis [32]. Similarly, Zhou et al. reported that clinical signs of bowel obstruction, peritonitis, prolonged duration of obstruction, and low total protein levels were independent risk factors for bowel resection; although computed tomography (CT) findings were included in their dataset, they were not analyzed as risk predictors [29]. Wang et al. demonstrated that strangulation duration exceeding nine hours was an independent predictor of ischemia and necrosis in incarcerated femoral hernias [33]. In a meta-analysis conducted by Chen et al., which included seven studies on incarcerated groin hernias, the authors identified female sex, advanced age, femoral hernia type, presence of bowel obstruction, incarceration duration, elevated white blood cell count, and neutrophilia as significant predictors of bowel resection [2]. In this study, a multivariate model including all statistically significant variables showed that the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), NLR, clinical signs of peritonitis, intraoperative findings of bowel obstruction, CT-based defect diameter, bowel wall thickness, and absence or decrease of bowel wall enhancement were all independent predictors distinguishing NRIH from RIH patients in acute irreducible inguinal hernia cases.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to combine a comprehensive preoperative clinical evaluation, laboratory findings, intraoperative observations, and detailed preoperative contrast-enhanced computed tomography assessments to predict strangulation and bowel resection in patients with acutely irreducible inguinal hernias. By systematically re-evaluating all CT scans with a focus on strangulation markers, clinically valuable predictors were identified to assist surgeons in early risk stratification and surgical decision-making.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design and single-center setting represent inherent methodological constraints. Additionally, the limited time frame and relatively small sample size further may affect the generalizability of the results. Furthermore, the study population was limited to patients who underwent preoperative contrast-enhanced CT, which may introduce selection bias and restrict the external validity of the findings.

Conclusion

In acute irreducible inguinal hernia cases, timely recognition of strangulation remains a critical factor for patient outcomes. The findings of this study suggest that combining clinical indicators (such as peritonitis signs and CCI score), laboratory markers (particularly NLR), and preoperative contrast-enhanced CT findings (notably bowel wall thickness, defect diameter, and lack of enhancement) provides a robust framework for identifying patients at risk for bowel ischemia and resection. The integration of CT-based evaluation into routine emergency assessment protocols may enhance surgical decision-making and allow for better preoperative planning. Prospective multicenter studies are warranted to validate these predictive markers and to develop standardized algorithms for the management of acute irreducible inguinal hernias.
