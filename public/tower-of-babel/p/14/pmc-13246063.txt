Consistency in staging, grading, and extent of periodontitis diagnosis: A cross-sectional online-based assessment among Indonesian periodontics specialist students
Authors: I.Komang Evan Wijaksana, Nova Andriani Hepitaria, Irma Josefina Savitri, Shahabe Saquib Abullais, Saad AlQahtani, Ghadah Khaled Bahamdan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Periodontitis remains a significant global health burden, and its accurate diagnosis is critical for effective management. The 2018 classification system for periodontitis introduces staging, grading, and extent parameters to enhance diagnostic consistency, but its application among trainees remains underexplored. This study evaluates the consistency of staging, grading, and extent diagnosis of periodontitis among Indonesian periodontics specialist students using the 2018 classification system, highlighting challenges and variability in interpretation. A total of 41 students across different training levels participated in the study, classifying 5 clinical cases based on comprehensive diagnostic materials, including radiographs, periodontal charts, and medical histories. Diagnostic concordance with gold-standard diagnoses was assessed and using Fleiss Kappa for inter-reliability analysis. Agreement rates were 56.10% for staging (Fleiss’ Kappa = 0.170, 95% CI: 0.145–0.196), 76.10% for grading (Kappa = 0.210, 95% CI: 0.185–0.235), and 74.15%. for extent (Kappa = 0.168, 95% CI: 0.144–0.192). Overestimation of severity occurred in 35.12% of staging decisions, while 20.93% of cases were underestimated in grading. Diagnostic variability was greatest in borderline cases. Indonesian periodontics specialist students demonstrated fair consistency in grading but poor agreement in staging and extent compared with expert consensus. These findings highlight the need for enhanced calibration exercises and curriculum reform to improve diagnostic reliability. Multi-institutional studies with larger cohorts are recommended to further validate these findings.

1. Introduction

Periodontitis, a major contributor to global oral and systemic disease burdens, is projected to affect over 1.56 billion people by 2050, marking a 44% increase from 2021.[1] With its links to noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) through shared risk factors like aging and lifestyle, the disease imposes significant health and economic challenges,[2] accounting for 48% of dental disease burdens and over USD 104 billion in annual losses.[3] Indonesia ranks among the top 4 countries for severe periodontitis prevalence, with 38 million cases in 2019, representing 3.5% of the global caseload[4] and affecting 74.1% of its population,[5,6] underscoring the need for improved diagnostic frameworks.

Despite advancements in classification systems, periodontitis diagnosis remains inconsistent.[7] The 2018 periodontitis classification system introduced a multifaceted staging and grading system aimed at improving diagnostic precision and treatment predictability.[8,9] However, its implementation reveals challenges,[10,11] particularly in countries like Indonesia, where access to preventive measures and awareness remains low. This study investigates whether the new classification system had been implemented for diagnostic consistency among Indonesian periodontics specialist students, a critical step in improving patient outcomes.

The 2018 classification system integrates phenotypic and systemic factors to refine periodontitis diagnosis, facilitating personalized treatment.[12,13] Evidence suggests its efficacy in structured clinical settings, where gold-standard panels have validated its use.[14,15] However, limited research exists on its application among emerging clinicians, especially in resource-constrained regions like Indonesia, where disease prevalence and socio-economic barriers complicate standardization efforts.

While the new classification system holds promise, its adaptability among less experienced clinicians and its practical efficacy in high-burden,[10,11] diverse populations remain underexplored. Previous studies have primarily focused on its application in controlled environments,[7] neglecting real-world challenges faced by specialists in training. This creates a gap in understanding how effectively these students can apply the system under varied clinical scenarios, particularly in diagnosing and staging severe periodontitis cases.

Indonesia provides a relevant setting due to its high prevalence of severe periodontitis, the large population of dental students, and the limited literature on how global classification systems are applied in local clinical education. Therefore, the present study aimed to evaluate the consistency of staging, grading, and extent of periodontitis diagnosis among Indonesian periodontics specialist students using the 2018 classification system. We hypothesized that periodontics specialist students would demonstrate lower diagnostic agreement compared to expert consensus, particularly in complex or borderline cases, reflecting the need for enhanced calibration and educational support.

2. Materials and methods

This cross-sectional, online-based study was conducted among periodontics specialist students enrolled at Universitas Airlangga, Indonesia. Eligible participants were those actively registered in the specialist training program and who had previously received formal instruction on the 2018 classification of periodontitis. Students who were on academic leave or declined participation were excluded. A total of 41 students (30 females and 11 males; aged 25–40 years) completed the study. Participation was voluntary, with no financial incentives provided.

The study protocol was approved by the Health Research Ethical Clearance Commission of the Faculty of Dental Medicine, Universitas Airlangga (469/HRECC.FODM/VII/2021). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and confidentiality of responses was maintained.

Five clinical periodontitis cases were selected from published studies, with gold-standard diagnoses established by international periodontology experts. These cases were chosen to reflect a range of disease severities and complexities, challenging the diagnostic capabilities of the participants. Cases were adapted from Ravida et al,[7] and Sirinirund et al,[16] selected for their validated diagnoses and clinical relevance (Supplementary file). Comprehensive diagnostic materials were provided to each participant, including intraoral photographs, complete periodontal charts, dental radiographs, patient demographic details (e.g., age and sex), and pertinent medical histories, such as smoking and diabetes status. The materials were designed to replicate real-world clinical scenarios, offering participants the opportunity to classify cases accurately based on the 2018 classification criteria.

The 5 cases used in this study encompassed varying complexities of periodontitis. Case 1 involved 6 teeth with clinical attachment loss (CAL) ≥ 5 mm, indicating a localized extent of disease, supported by radiographic bone loss (RBL) measurements. Case 2 represented a borderline scenario with horizontal bone loss limited to the coronal third of the root and CAL of 5 to 6 mm at specific sites associated with crown restorations and recession. Challenges included staging ambiguities due to CAL measurement errors and reliance on RBL for a holistic diagnosis. Case 3 involved limited tooth mobility (Grade 1–2) and tooth loss within 5 teeth, aligning with Stage III, despite posterior radiographic challenges. Case 4 presented with severe features, including probing depths of 5 to 11 mm, RBL to the mid-root or beyond, and generalized tooth mobility, indicative of Stage IV periodontitis. Case 5 demonstrated extensive circumferential attachment loss near the apex and hypermobility (Grade III), distinguishing it as Stage IV due to its complexity factors like localized trauma.

These cases were selected to reflect diverse diagnostic challenges and test participants’ application of the 2018 classification system.

Participants accessed the cases through a secure online platform (Google Forms). Each participant independently classified the 5 cases according to the 2018 classification, specifying the stage, grade, and extent/distribution of periodontitis. To minimize bias, the order of case presentation was randomized for each participant. Students were not allowed to consult external resources, but a copy of the official 2018 classification criteria was made available for reference during the assessment.

Diagnostic agreement was calculated by comparing participant responses with the expert-defined gold standard. Proportions of correct, overestimated, and underestimated diagnoses were reported for staging, grading, and extent. Inter-rater reliability was analyzed using Fleiss’ Kappa with 95% confidence intervals. Interpretation followed Altman benchmark scale: <0.20 = poor, 0.21 to 0.40 = fair, 0.41 to 0.60 = moderate, 0.61 to 0.80 = good, and > 0.80 = very good agreement.

Additional descriptive statistics, including raw counts and percentages, were provided for transparency. Subgroup analysis was performed by training year to explore trends in diagnostic performance. No formal sample size calculation was conducted due to the exploratory nature of the study; however, the sample represented the entire cohort of periodontics residents available at the institution. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Base V27.

4. Discussion

This study revealed inconsistencies in the staging, grading, and diagnostic extent of severe periodontitis cases among periodontics specialist students when applying the 2018 classification system. Overall agreement rates were 56.10% for staging, 76.10% for grading, and 74.15% for extent, substantially lower than the agreement rates reported for experienced clinicians (76%, 82%, and 84.8%, respectively).[7] These findings underscore the challenges faced by students in applying a complex, multidimensional classification system, particularly in borderline and severe cases.

Staging demonstrated the lowest agreement among participants, with a Fleiss Kappa value of 0.170, indicating poor inter-reliability. This was particularly evident in Case 2, which had the lowest staging agreement at just 12.20%. Borderline characteristics, such as horizontal bone loss confined to the coronal third of the root and mixed CAL patterns, likely contributed to the difficulty.[17] Errors in CAL measurement, often due to reliance on crown margins instead of the cemento-enamel junction, further compounded the challenges. These findings are consistent with previous studies emphasizing the challenges of consistent periodontitis diagnosis, particularly in staging, which requires nuanced interpretation of clinical data.[7] This study demonstrated that specialist students were able to differentiate advanced stages of periodontitis (Stages III and IV) from early stages (Stages I and II). However, they faced challenges in distinguishing between Stage III and Stage IV. These findings are consistent with those reported by Marini et al,[17] who similarly observed difficulties among participants in differentiating these stages. In our cohort, most errors reflected overestimation, suggesting that trainees tended to classify cases as more severe than experts, possibly due to uncertainty or overemphasis on radiographic features. Nonetheless, direct comparisons with Marini et al study are limited due to differences in study design and population characteristics. The inability to consistently distinguish between Stage III and IV carries significant clinical implications, particularly in effectively communicating the complexity of advanced periodontitis cases to patients, which is essential for informed decision-making and treatment planning. These findings highlight the subjectivity of staging and the need for improved training in interpreting complex clinical and radiographic data.

Grading exhibited the highest agreement (76.10%), supported by a fair Fleiss Kappa value of 0.210. This may reflect the relative simplicity of grading, which is based primarily on RBL progression and risk factors such as smoking and diabetes. These criteria may be easier for students to apply consistently compared with staging, which involves more subjective judgments.[10] While fewer misclassifications were observed compared to staging, discrepancies persisted, particularly in cases with ambiguous RBL progression. Case-specific variability was apparent, with straightforward cases achieving higher agreement rates, while mixed or intermediate bone loss patterns posed greater challenges. These results align with previous studies emphasizing the need for structured training in radiographic interpretation to enhance grading consistency.[7]

Agreement for diagnosing the extent of periodontitis was 74.15%, with notable misclassifications involving the molar/incisor pattern in 8.29% of assessments, despite its absence in the gold-standard diagnoses. This highlights potential gaps in participants’ training on distribution patterns and reliance on incomplete clinical impressions. Cases with detailed periodontal charts, such as Cases 3 and 5, achieved better agreement, illustrating the importance of structured diagnostic tools. However, less agreement was observed in cases with early-stage presentations, where clinical ambiguities were more pronounced.[17]

Subgroup analysis suggested a trend toward improved agreement among senior trainees, particularly in grading (82.1% in third-year students vs 69.3% in first-year students). Although these differences were not statistically significant, the trend indicates that diagnostic performance improves with training and cumulative clinical exposure. Similar findings have been reported in dental education research, where progression through training correlates with increased diagnostic accuracy.[10,18] These results underscore the importance of structured calibration throughout the training period rather than a single exposure to the classification system.

The findings emphasize the importance of enhancing training protocols for periodontics students to reduce diagnostic variability. Incorporating structured calibration exercises, detailed case-based learning, and robust radiographic analysis into educational curricula could significantly improve consistency.[18–20] Additionally, addressing common errors, such as misusing crown margins for CAL measurements, and emphasizing accurate periodontal chart evaluations are critical for better alignment with gold standards.[7]

Future training strategies could benefit from integrating digital tools, such as simulated case evaluations and AI-assisted diagnostics, to reinforce learning and provide immediate feedback. Such innovations have the potential to improve diagnostic accuracy and confidence, particularly in complex cases.[21–23]

Beyond the online setting, this study has several limitations. First, the sample was restricted to a single institution, limiting generalizability. Selection bias may have occurred, as only students who consented participated, and their performance may not represent all trainees. The Hawthorne effect cannot be excluded, as participants knew their responses were being evaluated for research. Cultural and educational factors unique to Indonesia may also influence diagnostic behavior and should be considered when comparing these findings to other contexts. Furthermore, the absence of a formal power calculation limits the strength of statistical inferences.

These findings have practical implications for clinical education. Curricula should emphasize repeated calibration exercises, detailed case-based discussions, and targeted training on differentiating advanced stages of periodontitis. Integration of digital diagnostic support systems, including AI-based feedback, may enhance accuracy and confidence.

Future studies should be multi-institutional with larger sample sizes, enabling more robust subgroup and correlation analyses. Longitudinal designs could track how

skills evolve across training years, providing insight into the effectiveness of educational interventions. Additionally, research should explore whether structured digital tools or simulation-based learning environments can improve long-term retention of diagnostic skills.

In conclusion, this study highlights the diagnostic challenges faced by periodontics specialist students, particularly in staging and extent assessments. Improved educational frameworks and innovative training tools are essential to align student diagnoses with established standards, ultimately improving patient outcomes.
