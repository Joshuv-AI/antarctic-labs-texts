A scoping review of rapid-onset climate-related disasters & gender-based violence against women
Authors: Ashley M Ruiz, Leilany Muñoz, Trinity Walsh, Graham Hardy, Bahareh Ranjbari, Maria Mendoza Contreras, Sharon L Leslie, Mark Nevitt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe effects of climate-related disasters hold a syndemic or multiplicative effect on exacerbating pre-existing social epidemics of gender-based violence against women (GBVAW). With increasing patterns in severity and frequency of rapid-onset climate-related disasters anticipated to worsen due to climate change, GBVAW poses a serious threat to exacerbating women’s socioeconomic and health disparities. However, limited literature exists that synthesizes understanding of factors that shape women’s vulnerability to gender-based violence in relation to rapid-onset climate-related disasters. In this paper, we aim to identify key factors that shape women’s vulnerability to gender-based violence induced by rapid-onset natural disasters through the use of scoping literature review. Using content analysis, ten key factors were found to shape women’s vulnerability to GBVAW through the syndemic effects of rapid-onset climate-related disasters including: 1) pre-existing gender inequalities, 2) rigid gendered roles and labor, 3) overwhelmed systemic infrastructure capacity, 4) displacement, 5) loss of social network and social capital, 6) cyclical poverty traps, 7) water and food insecurity, 8) gendered stereotype depictions of women in mass communication, 9) male-centered disaster relief efforts, and 10) weathering conditions to women’s health. Based on the key factors identified, the current state of existing literature and gaps in integrating mitigation of GBVAW within climate risk management frameworks are discussed, as well as recent legal pathways and remedies that may support the mitigation of GBVAW in times of rapid-onset climate-related disasters.Keywords: Women’s health, Health disparities, Risk reduction, Environment, Social vulnerability

Introduction

Purpose of paper

The syndemic effects of rapid-onset climate-related disasters place women at a heightened vulnerability to pre-existing social epidemics of gender-based violence (GBVAW) (Giudice et al., 2021; World Health Organization, 2023; Ruiz et al., 2023; UN Environment Programme, 2022; United Nations, 2022; Bhutta et al., 2023; Thomas and Thomas, 2004; Murphy et al., 2023; Thurston et al., 2021; Desai and Mandal, 2021; Riyad Fatema et al., 2019; UNFCCC, 2022; Kerimova et al., 2003; Anastario et al., 2009; Berberian et al., 2022; UN High Commissioner for Refugees, 2009; Refugee and Agency, 2022; Kelly et al., 2021; Vu et al., 2014; Wirtz et al., 2014; Rothschild and Haase, 2023; United Nations, 2023; Leeuwen, 2023). Globally, GBVAW is recognized as one of the most pervasive and prevalent human rights violations (Women, 2024; World Health Organization, 2018). A preventable public health issue, the World Health Organization estimates that 1 out of 3 women will experience physical and/or sexual forms of violence from either an intimate partner or non-partner throughout their lifetime (World Health Organization, 2018). The term GBVAW encompasses any form (i.e. emotional, physical, sexual, or financial) or type of actions that are intended to cause harm or suffering to women based on socially ascribed expectations of gender (World Health Organization, 2018). With increasing patterns in frequency, magnitude, and severity, rapid-onset climate-related disasters are anticipated to worsen with the direction of climate change (Giudice et al., 2021; World Health Organization, 2023; Ruiz et al., 2023; UN Environment Programme, 2022; United Nations, 2022; Bhutta et al., 2023; Thomas and Thomas, 2004; Riyad Fatema et al., 2019; United Nations, 2023). The effects of rapid-onset climate-related disasters have an exhaustive effect on irreversible damage to life, assets, and health (UNFCCC, 2022; Kerimova et al., 2003; Anastario et al., 2009; Berberian et al., 2022; UN High Commissioner for Refugees, 2009; Refugee and Agency, 2022; Kelly et al., 2021; Vu et al., 2014; Wirtz et al., 2014; Rothschild and Haase, 2023; United Nations, 2023; Leeuwen, 2023; Heltberg et al., 2009). These effects disproportionately impact and disadvantage women’s attainment of optimal health outcomes as a human right through GBV (Giudice et al., 2021; World Health Organization, 2023; Ruiz et al., 2023; UN Environment Programme, 2022; United Nations, 2022; Bhutta et al., 2023; Thomas and Thomas, 2004; Murphy et al., 2023; Thurston et al., 2021; Desai and Mandal, 2021; Riyad Fatema et al., 2019; UNFCCC, 2022; Kerimova et al., 2003; Anastario et al., 2009; Berberian et al., 2022; UN High Commissioner for Refugees, 2009; Refugee and Agency, 2022; Kelly et al., 2021; Vu et al., 2014; Wirtz et al., 2014; Rothschild and Haase, 2023; United Nations, 2023; Leeuwen, 2023). This poses a serious threat to diminishing advances towards health equity and instead exacerbates pre-existing health disparities among women. However, little is known of the factors that shape women’s vulnerability to GBVAW in times of rapid-onset climate-related disasters. This scoping review fills this knowledge gap by identifying key factors that contribute to women’s vulnerability to GBVAW in times of rapid-onset climate-related disasters. Based on these findings, we present a state of the existing literature to highlight current gaps in climate risk management approaches and international legal pathways that may support the mitigation of GBVAW in times of rapid-onset climate-related disasters.

Rooted in gender inequality, GBVAW continues to be one of the most pervasive human rights violations around the world (UN Environment Programme, 2022; Sri et al., 2021; Ostadtaghizadeh et al., 2023). Globally, nearly 736 million women, or almost one in three women, are estimated to have suffered physical or sexual violence at some point in their lifetime (World Health Organization, 2021). Although GBVAW is primarily recognized in relation to physical, sexual, and psychological forms, GBVAW is an umbrella term that includes all forms of violence, including economic, patrimonial, and moral violence (Fernandes et al., 2025). In this paper, GBVAW is defined as any attempts, threats, harm or suffering directed towards a person based on socially ascribed differences of being a woman (World Health Organization, 2023; Ruiz et al., 2023; Women, 2024; Fernandes et al., 2025).

The shadow pandemic of GBVAW

Significant mortality and morbidity outcomes have been found to be associated with GBVAW (García-Moreno Henrica et al., 2005; Heise, 1994; Jewkes, 2002; Kiss et al., 2012). Globally, gender-related intentional killing is pervasive among women (Lewis et al., 2024; UN Women, 2023). In the US, approximately 4,970 women were murdered in 2021, although these reports are anticipated to be higher due to issues of categorization of cases as homicide (Lewis et al., 2024; Anguiano, 2019). Recent studies show that 60% of all female homicides are committed by someone close to them, either an intimate partner or a family member (UN Women, 2023). For women who survive, the toll of GBVAW carries a myriad of negative short and long-term mental, physical, sexual, and reproductive health outcomes (Heidari and Moreno, 2016; García-Montes et al., 2022; Rees et al., 2011; Magruder et al., 2017; Palermo et al., 2013).

The mental, physical, sexual, and reproductive consequences of GBV on women’s health are often witnessed or felt, at any point throughout the life course of women (Heidari and Moreno, 2016; García-Montes et al., 2022; Rees et al., 2011; Magruder et al., 2017). Acute and chronic psychological outcomes of GBVAW have been associated with sleep disorders, often a result of nightmares, as well as panic attacks, anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) (García-Montes et al., 2022; Rees et al., 2011; Chandra and Satyanarayana, 2010; Campbell et al., 2002; Ellsberg et al., 2008; Klein, 2004; Karakurt et al., 2014). Acute and chronic physical burdens of GBV to women’s health are associated with injuries, pain syndromes, coronary heart disease, stomach ulcers, spastic colon, and digestive disorders (Kiss et al., 2012; García-Montes et al., 2022; Ellsberg et al., 2008; Karakurt et al., 2014). GBVAW is also associated with sexual and reproductive health issues including increased incidence to sexually transmitted infections including human immunodeficiency virus, miscarriage, painful sexual intercourse, preterm birth, neonatal death, and unintended pregnancy (Heise, 1994; Heidari and Moreno, 2016; Heise et al., 2002).

While these health disparities of GBVAW outlined are not fully comprehensive, the consequence of GBVAW to women’s health has been shown to be three times the effect of war, as well as cardiovascular disease, cancer, HIV, and tuberculosis worldwide (Heise, 1994; Kiss et al., 2012; Klein, 2004; Heise et al., 2002). The burdens of GBV on women’s health and well-being hinder women’s quality of life (difficulty enjoying life, making decisions, loss of interest, feeling worthless) (Ganster-Breidler, 2010; García-Moreno et al., 2015). Overall, GBVAW limits women opportunities to participate as an equal in society, as well as social and economic development (García-Moreno et al., 2015). Studies show out-of-pocket expenses for women seeking health care following an incidence of violence ranged from $29.72 in South Africa to $156.11 in Romania (Vyas et al., 2021); while other studies average the cost of health care following sexual assault to be $516 (Chrisler and Ferguson, 2006). Women with histories of rape average 12.4 mental health visits, which is estimated to cost in total $978 (Chrisler and Ferguson, 2006). The overall cost to women for lost productivity averaged $73.84 to $2,151.48 in South Africa per healthcare visit (Vyas et al., 2021).

Previously recognized as an epidemic, the onset of the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic led to intensified GBVAW being recognized as a shadow pandemic (Ruiz et al., 2023; Ostadtaghizadeh et al., 2023; Dlamini, 2021; Guidorzi, 2020; Khanlou et al., 2020; UN Women, 2020). Although GBVAW differs among cultures and contexts, global patterns demonstrate the disproportionate harm directed towards women, even when considering age and socioeconomic backgrounds differences (Fernandes et al., 2025; Kiss et al., 2012; Rees et al., 2011; Scheer et al., 2020). The phenomenon of GBVAW stems from androcentric domination (systems and ideologies centered on men as the norm) which results in unequal power relations that disadvantage women based on gender within everyday social functions (World Health Organization, 2023; Ruiz et al., 2023; Fernandes et al., 2025).

Rapid-onset climate-related disasters

GBVAW and increasing patterns of rapid-onset climate-related disasters due to climate change are both global public health crisis (Giudice et al., 2021; World Health Organization, 2023; UNDRR, 2020; Sauerborn and Ebi, 2012; Smith and Katz, 2013; Smith, 2024). Rapid-onset climate-related disasters are defined by the United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR) as “sudden-onset” climate-related disasters of hazardous events that develop quickly within a few minutes or hours, and are often unexpected, that stem from atmospheric and oceanic changes (Anastario et al., 2009; National Centers for Environmental Information, 2025). Rapid-onset climate-related disasters have a short preimpact or warning phase, and often occur without warning (Bedi et al., 2023; UNICEF, Sudden Onsets, UNICEF., 2025). Examples of rapid-onset climate-related disasters include hurricanes, flash floods, tsunamis, cyclones, landslides, and tornadoes (Anastario et al., 2009; Bedi et al., 2023). Rapid-onset climate-related disasters often result in unexpected and substantial economic losses in income, assets, as well as private and public asset losses (Hill et al., 2024). A global assessment on household welfare found that rapid-onset climate-related disasters resulted on average a 43% loss in incomes in Asia and small island states (Hill et al., 2024).

Globally, patterns of rapid-onset climate-related disasters have sharply intensified in frequency, severity, and magnitude (Smith and Katz, 2013; National Centers for Environmental Information, 2025). The UNDRR reports that between 1980 and 1999, 3,656 climate-related disasters were recorded worldwide; compared to 6.681 climate-related disasters between 2000–2019 (Sauerborn and Ebi, 2012). The increased frequency of individual rapid-onset climate-related disasters coupled with increased severity and magnitude have contributed to intensifying the global death toll by 73% (UNDRR, 2023). In the US, rapid-onset climate-related disasters have drastically increased from an average of 3.3 rapid-onset climate-related disaster events a year in the 1980′s, compared to 27 rapid-onset climate-related disaster events in 2024 (National Centers for Environmental Information, 2025).

Climate-related disasters that result from weather and climate extremes are a component of the Earth’s system (United Nations, 2025; Centre for Research on the Epidemiology of Disasters, 2015). However, these increasing patterns of frequency, intensity, and magnitude of disasters due to the impact of human activities are not ‘natural’ (United Nations, 2025). Rather, to date, 90% of disasters are classified as weather- and climate-related, and are recognized to occur from the exacerbation of human activity (Centre for Research on the Epidemiology of Disasters, 2015). The term ‘natural’ disasters suggests the origin of disasters to be natural, or not impacted by the consequences of human activity; the term climate-related disasters questions the ‘naturalness’ of atmospheric and oceanic changes contributing to increased frequency, severity, and magnitude of climate-related disasters (UNDRR, 2020; Sauerborn and Ebi, 2012; United Nations, 2025; Wisner et al., 2003). Rather, since 2005, there has been a growing body of research documenting worsening patterns of rapid-onset climate-related disasters aggravated by anthropogenic drivers, and the subsequent socioeconomic conditions that result globally (Nardulli et al., 2015). With 2005 marked an unprecedented turning point, with the largest number of climate-related disasters fostered by the highest recorded year of global warming at the time, (United Nations International Strategy for Disaster Reduction, 2005; Munich Re, 2005). This led to over 90,000 deaths and unprecedented economic damage (Munich Re, 2005). In turn, these developments shifted the socioeconomic and political context as global nations began to respond to the consequences of climate change, the first climate treaty being the Kyoto Protocol under the United Nations Framework for Convention on Climate Change (UNFCC) (Brown, 2005).

The syndemic effects of climate change, rapid-onset climate-related disasters & GBVAW

Rapid-onset climate-related disasters have an exhaustive effect on irreversible damage to life, assets, and health, but hold a syndemic or multiplicative effect on exacerbating pre-existing social issues of GBVAW (UNFCCC, 2022; Kerimova et al., 2003; Anastario et al., 2009; Berberian et al., 2022; UN High Commissioner for Refugees, 2009; Refugee and Agency, 2022; Kelly et al., 2021; Vu et al., 2014; Wirtz et al., 2014; Rothschild and Haase, 2023; United Nations, 2023; Leeuwen, 2023; Heltberg et al., 2009). As a concept, ‘syndemic’ describes the relationship of upstream factors that worsen two or more health conditions during heightened adversity (Willen et al., 2017; O’Rourke et al., 2024; Vahedi et al., 2025). This involves three processes: 1) the concentration of two or more diseases or clustering of multiple epidemics, 2) the creation of vulnerability among populations due to large-scale sociopolitical and economic factors, and 3) the interactive effects of a disease or multiple epidemics on exacerbating overall health outcomes (Willen et al., 2017; O’Rourke et al., 2024; Vahedi et al., 2025; Khanlou et al., 2022). Women are 14 times more likely to die and experience injuries compared to men due to climate-related disasters, but growing evidence indicates sustained increases in GBVAW following rapid-onset climate-related disasters (Riyad Fatema et al., 2019; UNFCCC, 2022; United Nations, 2023; Segal and Giudice, 2022; Neumayer and Plümper, 2007; Capasso et al., 2021). For example, women displaced following Hurricane Katrina not only reported encountering four times more daily experiences of GBV following Hurricane Katrina, but daily experiences of GBV remained elevated for two years following the rapid-onset climate-related disaster among women (Anastario et al., 2009).

Weather-related hazards are already the leading cause of displacement globally, with the Americas being the third highest recorded climate-related disaster-induced displacement rates (UN Environment Programme, 2022; United Nations, 2022). Displacement is defined as persons or groups who have been obligated or forced to leave their residence to avoid natural or human-made harm or disasters (Thomas and Thomas, 2004). Experts anticipate substantial patterns of displacement to worsen with the direction of climate change and climate-related disasters (Giudice et al., 2021; World Health Organization, 2023; Ruiz et al., 2023; UN Environment Programme, 2022; United Nations, 2022; Bhutta et al., 2023; Thomas and Thomas, 2004; Riyad Fatema et al., 2019; United Nations, 2023). Displacement induced by climate-related disasters holds dire health consequences for women (Kelly et al., 2021; Neumayer and Plümper, 2007; Simeonova, 2011; Joshi and Fayyad, 2015; Wood et al., 2020). Women who are displaced have disproportionately higher rates of mortality; often due to higher risk of acquiring communicable diseases and developing chronic health problems compared to men (Kelly et al., 2021; Joshi and Fayyad, 2015; Wood et al., 2020). Recent evidence estimates that approximately 80% of those displaced due to climate-related disasters globally are women (UN Environment Programme, 2022; Riyad Fatema et al., 2019; Neumayer and Plümper, 2007).

Climate-related disasters amplify overarching power differential dynamics within pre-existing social systemic infrastructures. However, populations of women most impacted and vulnerable to the effects of rapid-onset climate-related disasters are systemically marginalized populations of women (Thurston et al., 2021; Desai and Mandal, 2021; Anastario et al., 2009; Berberian et al., 2022; Leeuwen, 2023; Heltberg et al., 2009; Women, 2024; Segal and Giudice, 2022; Neumayer and Plümper, 2007; Crear-Perry et al., 2021). For instance, although one study found that across 141 countries between 1981 and 2002, women’s life expectancy was significantly lower than men’s (Petrucci et al., 2019; Fan and Huang, 2023). The higher socioeconomic status of women included in this study, the weaker this effect was on life expectancy differences based on gender (Petrucci et al., 2019; Fan and Huang, 2023). This is similar to other studies that demonstrate the disproportionate vulnerability of displacement induced by climate-related disasters to racial and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations of women (Anastario et al., 2009; Berberian et al., 2022; Refugee and Agency, 2022; Kelly et al., 2021; Segal and Giudice, 2022; Neumayer and Plümper, 2007; Luetke et al., 2020). This is particularly concerning as these effects pose a serious burden to further exacerbating existing health disparities among women’s (Berberian et al., 2022; Neumayer and Plümper, 2007; Mak and Thomas, 2022).

Given the anticipated direction of climate change on worsening patterns of rapid-onset climate-related disasters, understanding key factors that shape women’s vulnerability to GBV due to the syndemic effects of rapid-onset climate-related disasters carries a pronounced urgency (Min et al., 2011). –-With 2005 marking an unprecedented global socioeconomic and political shift due to the impact of rapid-onset climate-related disaster patterns, understanding the impact of rapid-onset climate-related disasters on GBVAW within this shifted context is critical to informing future directions in climate and disaster risk mitigation efforts. In addition, with GBVAW being a broad term, no existing evidence presents the full range of GBVAW (including different forms and types) impacted by rapid-onset climate-related disasters. The purpose of this scoping literature review is to fill this knowledge gap by presenting the current state of existing evidence as to the effects of rapid-onset climate-related disasters on the full range, including different types and forms of GBVAW from 2005 to the present. Specifically, this scoping literature review aims to identify key factors of rapid-onset climate-related disasters that shape women’s vulnerability to GBVAW by conducting a scoping literature review. Based on these key factors, we present novel insights into the current state of existing literature, as well as gaps in integrating mitigations of GBVAW within climate risk management approaches and frameworks. Lastly, we discuss current international legal pathways and remedies that may offer support to mitigating GBVAW in times of rapid-onset climate-related disasters.

Literature review methods

A scoping literature review was conducted utilizing Mak & Thomas’s guidelines and following the PRISMA-ScR Extension for Scoping Reviews (Tricco et al., 2018; Canavan and Ide, 2024). A scoping review was chosen to allow for inclusion of studies from various methodology types, to report broadly on the literature in the field, identify gaps in the literature, and inform subsequent research.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive literature search strategy was developed to identify articles meeting the study criteria. An initial search strategy was drafted for testing in PubMed. Pre-identified sentinel articles were hand-searched for keywords relating to the study objectives. Those results were assessed, and additional terms were selected from the titles, abstracts, and MeSH terms. A second draft search was created with the added terms and again tested in PubMed. Confirmation that the search identified the sentinel articles was considered validation of the search. The searches combined controlled vocabulary supplemented with keywords related to the concepts of climate-related or natural disasters (e.g., weather, hurricanes) and women (e.g. female, gender). Records were limited to English language. No study types or document types were excluded during the searches. Seventeen bibliographic databases were searched: Agricola (EBSCOhost), APA PsycInfo (EBSCOhost), CINAHL (EBSCOhost), Cochrane Library, Embase.com, Environment Complete (EBSCOhost), Health Source: Nursing/Academic (EBSCOhost), Google Scholar via Publish or Perish, Global Health (CABI), GreenFile (EBSCOhost), ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Citation Index via Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus, Sociological Abstracts (ProQuest), Violence and Abuse Abstracts (EBSCOhost), Web of Science Core Collection and Women’s Studies International (EBSCOhost). Searches were undertaken on October 1, 2024. The full literature search strategies can be found in Supplemental Digital Material 1.

Study selection

This review included both qualitative and quantitative studies. Articles included primary and secondary sources of data, such as in-depth interviews, ethnographic approaches, and participant observation. Sources of grey literature, such as dissertations, presenting primary or secondary data analysis, were included. Clinical and research protocols, conference or government reports, and research evaluating specific interventions or trauma exposure broadly were not included. Sources that focused on violence against youth, including girls, were not included. In addition, articles that did not specify or focus on a specific form of rapid-onset climate-related disaster were excluded, including infectious disease outbreaks, conflict, acts of terrorism, or humanitarian disasters. Classification as a disaster followed the Emergency Events Database criteria used by the Centre for Research on the Epidemiology of Disasters (CRED) criteria, which requires at least one of the following to be classified as a disaster: 1) ten or more people killed; 2) 100 or more people affect, 3) declaration of a state of emergency, or 4) international assistance called (Delforge et al., 2025).

A total of 6,611 articles were identified through the database searches and imported into EndNote X20. These were uploaded to the web-based systematic review software Covidence, which identified 2,604 duplicates, leaving 4,007 records. Titles, abstracts, and full-text were screened by three trained reviewers based on the following inclusion and exclusion criteria. Articles were determined to be eligible for inclusion in this scoping review if they met the following criteria: (1) published with full-text available in English, (2) present findings based on primary or secondary data, (3) focus only on rapid-onset climate-related disasters occurring in 2005 or after, and (4) identify outcomes of GBVAW. Studies were also eligible to be included if both a rapid- and slow-onset disaster were examined, providing findings reported were relevant to GBVAW in times of rapid-onset climate-related disasters. To provide a comprehensive representation of the literature, all types and forms (i.e., psychological/emotional, physical, sexual, or financial) of GBVAW as an outcome were included in this review.

Studies were excluded in this review if one or more of the following exclusion criteria were met: (1) full-text unavailable in English, (2) focused on slow-onset disasters or disasters measured from gradual changes occurring over more than 24 h, (3) focused on rapid-onset geophysical disasters and climate-related disasters occurring before 2005, (4) did not contain primary or secondary data, (5) did not focus on GBVAW, and (6) focused on disasters unspecific to at least one rapid-onset climate-related disaster. Due to practical limitations, full-text sources available in English were included in the criteria as the majority of the research team is monolingual in English language and lack of funding to support professional translation.

Initially, all four reviewers reviewed 20 titles and abstracts in pairs collectively against the inclusion or exclusion criteria, followed by assessment of inter-rater reliability. This process was repeated until a high average inter-rater reliability was established (greater than 0.8) (Cooper and Hedges, 1993). Once this was achieved, four members of the research team engaged in dual-independent screening of the remaining 3,987 titles and abstracts of articles. Following initial title and abstract screening, discrepancies were addressed by the pair of reviewers and an additional reviewer through open discussion to reach consensus with at least 80% agreement across reviewers. Open discussions focused on the rationale for decisions leading to disagreement. Reviewers then evaluated rationales based on the inclusion or exclusion criteria to determine inclusion or exclusion. If the initial pair of reviewers were unable to reach consensus, a third reviewer determined whether the article was included or excluded based on the inclusion or exclusion criteria. This process was repeated for screening titles and abstracts, as well as for screening the full-text of articles.

Following title and abstract screening, a total of 3,428 articles were removed. Of these, 3,401 articles did not meet the inclusion criteria or met one or more of the exclusion criteria, and 27 articles were identified as duplicates. The remaining 579 articles were subjected to full-text review followed by the process of inter-rater reliability assessment as outlined previously. t Following full-text screening, 546 articles were excluded. Of the 546 articles excluded during full-text review, 250 articles did not focus on rapid-onset climate-related disasters from 2005 or after; 178 articles did not include primary or secondary data; 78 did not focus on GBVAW; 42 were unavailable in full-text; 8 were duplicate sources; and 3 were not available in English translation. After full-text screening, 33 articles that met the eligibility criteria for inclusion were included for quality appraisal. Quality appraisal and data extraction

Five co-authors engaged in quality appraisal and extracted data from the 33 sources included in this review. Quality appraisal and data extraction of included articles were completed independently by two co-authors, followed by an inter-rater reliability assessment. To ensure consistency of quality appraisal and data extraction, the first 10 studies included were assessed and extracted by two reviewers individually and compared. Differences were resolved through open discussion among the dual pair of reviewers and a third reviewer. Discussion involved reflection of the expectations of quality appraisal and data extraction to understand the rationale leading to disagreement. This was repeated until a high average inter-rater reliability was established in the process of quality appraisal (greater than 0.75) and data extraction (greater than 0.71) (Cooper and Hedges, 1993). Five co-authors total, using dual-independent coding, engaged in the process of quality appraisal and data extraction of the remaining 28 sources. After quality appraisal and data extraction of all sources, inconsistencies in quality appraisal and data extraction were discussed and resolved with a third co-author.

Quality appraisal to assess the trustworthiness, relevance, and results involved the utilization of a custom risk of bias assessment tool guided by using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools specific to the study design of sources in narrative form (Aromataris et al., 2024). The custom risk of bias assessment tool assessed the following four domains: 1) sequence generation, 2) incomplete outcome data, 3) selective outcome reporting, and 4) other sources of bias. The level of risk of bias for each source was scored as either unclear, low, or high risk of bias across the four domains discussed. All studies were included for analysis to gain a comprehensive representation of the current state of the literature on GBVAW in times of rapid-onset climate-related disasters. No studies included in the analysis were found to be at high risk of bias across two or more domains. Data extraction was conducted independently by two pairs of co-authors through the use of a standardized data extraction template. The data extraction template guided gathering key components and characteristics related to study identification, methods, population, intervention, outcomes, and results data. Content analysis

The text of extracted data from included sources was analyzed using latent pattern content analysis (Kleinheksel et al., 2020; Erlingsson and Brysiewicz, 2017; Potter and Levine-Donnerstein, 1999).. Latent content analysis allows for the examination of patterns of characteristics based on text, while simultaneously leveraging the incorporation of the researcher’s expertise and interpretations of the meaning underlying the text (Erlingsson and Brysiewicz, 2017; Potter and Levine-Donnerstein, 1999). In this review, the lead author, with clinical and scholarly expertise in GBVAW, as well as the coding teams’ professional background in epidemiology, environmental, and global public health, informed the analysis process. Latent pattern content analysis is a systematic process of analyzing data across three phases (preparation, organization, and reporting) (Kleinheksel et al., 2020; Erlingsson and Brysiewicz, 2017; Potter and Levine-Donnerstein, 1999). Latent pattern content analysis followed the following steps in this review: 1) identification of units with meaning based on the data, 2) creation of a codebook, 3) application of codes to data, 4) refinement of codes and patterns in the data, 5) development of categories to inform organization of grouping similar codes 6) identifying underlying meanings of categories, 7) generation of themes, and 8) drawing conclusions (Kleinheksel et al., 2020; Erlingsson and Brysiewicz, 2017).

In preparing the data for analysis, the textual findings of data extracted from the included sources were compiled as a whole into a single document. Three members of the research team engaged in immersion through rereading the findings of data extracted from the included sources. Members of the research team then line-by-line highlighted and documented initial thoughts and insights to segments of text, which on first impression, appeared to be related to the aim of this review independently (Kleinheksel et al., 2020; Erlingsson and Brysiewicz, 2017). This initial approach to inductive code and category development allowed for the comparison of emerging labels, as well as identifying discrepancies and missed segments of text among the coding team (Kleinheksel et al., 2020; Erlingsson and Brysiewicz, 2017). Using inter-coder reliability, the highlighted and documented initial thoughts were compared and discussed by three members of the team to resolve discrepancies and missed text segments through open discussion. Initial highlights and thoughts informed the development of labels as units with meaning.

The development of units of meaning informed the coding schemes and categories that were compiled into a codebook with corresponding definitions. Initial codes that were related were grouped together as categories, and a portion of the textual findings from extracted data was tested by three members of the research team independently. The three members of the coding team then compared the initial categorization and coding tested on a sample of text and refined the developed categories and codes to ensure consistency of categories and codes to the data extracted. The refined coding schemes were then applied by the three members of the coding team to all data extracted independently. The coding team then compared the applied revised categories and coding schemes to the text of findings extracted from the data through open discussion to resolve discrepancies. The generation of themes emerged through the organization of at least two refined categories that held deeper underlying meanings of describing the factors that shape women’s vulnerability to GBVAW in relation to rapid-onset climate-related disasters (Erlingsson and Brysiewicz, 2017).

Findings

Ten key factors were identified as contributing to women’s increased vulnerability to GBV in relation to rapid-onset climate-related disasters: 1) pre-existing gender hierarchy and inequalities, 2) rigid gendered roles and labor, 3) overwhelmed systemic infrastructure capacity, 4) displacement, 5) loss of social network and social capital, 6) cyclical poverty traps, 7) water and food insecurity, 8) gendered stereotype depictions in mass communication, 9) male-centered disaster relief efforts, and 10) weathering conditions to women’s mental health. Based on the analysis, an overview of themes with corresponding sources is included in the Supplementary Materials.

Pre-existing gender hierarchy & inequalities

Pre-existing gender hierarchy and inequalities were found to be a key factor that contributed to women’s vulnerability to GBVAW (Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Jewkes et al., 2022; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Safajou et al., 2023). In this review, 17 studies describe the impact of pre-existing gender hierarchy and inequalities on the collective social practices that subjected women to being devalued based on patriarchal ideologies that existed prior to a rapid-onset climate-related disaster (Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Jewkes et al., 2022; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Safajou et al., 2023). While a majority of studies describe the pre-existing cultural entrenchment of patriarchal ideologies on intensifying outcomes of GBVAW to maintain the social status of men following a rapid-onset climate-related disaster, outcomes of intensified GBVAW differ among populations of women, and the pre-existing systemic protections offered for women (Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Safajou et al., 2023). For instance, systemic infrastructure protections include responses designed to offer access to distributing aid and resources (Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2019; Gibb, 2022; Madhuri, 2016; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008). Within this review, the denial of GBVAW within systemic infrastructure negated opportunities of offering protection to women, a response that previously existed before the disaster. In times of rapid-onset climate-related disasters, these denials resulted in added harm through psychological/emotional violence that trivialized or denied women’s suffering, pain, loss and grief from the devastation of the disaster on assets, relationships, as well as traumatic births and miscarriages (Severson et al., 2023; Parkinson, 2019; Madhuri, 2016). In turn, the embedded pre-existing gender hierarchies and inequity further extended the social practice of ‘collective shaming’, isolation, and control among women, and heightened among populations of women navigating trafficking, IPV, and DV (Severson et al., 2023; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2019; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016).

Rigid gendered roles & labor

Rigid gendered roles and labor entail expectations in divisions of labor based on socially ascribed ideas of gender. In this review, 15 studies describe the syndemic effects of rapid-onset climate-related disasters and rigid gendered roles and labor as a contributing factor shaping women’s vulnerability to GBV (Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Gibb, 2022; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Safajou et al., 2023; Finley, 2019; Richter, 2007; Azad et al., 2013). For women, rigid gendered roles are based on discriminatory gendered beliefs and norms that attribute women as inferior and inherently by nature as shy, weak, fragile, unstable, easily stressed or scared compared to men (Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Gibb, 2022; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Safajou et al., 2023; Finley, 2019; Richter, 2007; Azad et al., 2013). These gendered discriminatory beliefs perpetuate harmful sociocultural practices that deepen gender inequalities following rapid-onset climate-related disasters (Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Gibb, 2022; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Safajou et al., 2023; Finley, 2019; Richter, 2007; Azad et al., 2013). For women, this includes sociocultural practices such as early childhood marriage, polygamy, and wife beating (Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Parkinson, 2019; Bermudez et al., 2019; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Safajou et al., 2023; Finley, 2019; Richter, 2007). The gendered power differentials created position for women as inferior and subordinate to males’ domination, who are assumed to be inherently in positions of power due to nature (Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Gibb, 2022; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Safajou et al., 2023; Finley, 2019; Richter, 2007; Azad et al., 2013). In times of rapid-onset climate-related disasters, these rigid gendered roles manifest within gendered power differential dynamics in ways that further disadvantage women in personal and public spaces through the use of violence (Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Gibb, 2022; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Safajou et al., 2023; Finley, 2019; Richter, 2007; Azad et al., 2013). As a result, women are forced into social positions that require dependence on men to gain access to resources during and following rapid-onset climate-related disasters.

For example, four studies discuss women’s lack of control and inclusion in decisions related to evacuation, the allocation of funds or resources, including telecommunications (Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson, 2019; Bermudez et al., 2019; Richter, 2007). Sources discuss that men’s control of resources resulted in women having limited to no access to information on disaster relief funds to support recovery and rebuilding post-disaster (Ullah et al., 2024; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Gibb, 2022; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Safajou et al., 2023). In consequence, women were vulnerable to exploitation perpetuated by men in rebuilding assets and housing initiatives while unpaid (Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Gibb, 2022). This included men’s control in decision-making processes related to the use of insurance and disaster aid funds (Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Gibb, 2022). In situations of IPV and DV, this led men to use funds for themselves, while the family lived in homes requiring repair without the means to repair damages (Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008). The forced dynamics of dependence shaped through gendered roles foster predatory social conditions during times of disasters for women (Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008). These predatory conditions included perpetrators targeting vulnerable women, such as women who were alone or isolated (Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Bermudez et al., 2019). This included predators checking abandoned houses to target women displaced and alone, as well as female-led households in which husbands are far away for work during a disaster (Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Bermudez et al., 2019). The rigidity of gendered roles contributed to shaping sociocultural expectations of women’s responsibilities in terms of labor (Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Parkinson, 2019; Jewkes et al., 2022; Gibb, 2022; Madhuri, 2016; Richter, 2007). For women navigating rapid-onset climate-related disasters, these responsibilities involved increased demands and expectations of providing emotional work, caregiving, and domestic work with worsening living conditions and without compensation (Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Parkinson, 2019; Jewkes et al., 2022; Gibb, 2022; Madhuri, 2016; Richter, 2007). With the combination of increased demands and support, increased tensions arise due to sociocultural taboos between worsening living conditions in which women are unable to maintain hygiene, meet demands, and be in positions of control related to decisions and resource allocation (Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Jewkes et al., 2022; Bermudez et al., 2019; Richter, 2007). These tensions transcended across interpersonal relationships, households, families, and surrounding communities post-disaster, due to shifting or unmet gendered roles and labor (Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Jewkes et al., 2022; Bermudez et al., 2019; Richter, 2007). This included further societal marginalization and stigma when women attain means of generating income post- disaster, but also require childcare when employment is attained (Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Jewkes et al., 2022; Bermudez et al., 2019; Richter, 2007). Three studies discuss men’s feelings of inadequacy and their inability to cope to justify worsening abuse towards women, as men believe they are no longer the ‘provider and protector’ of the family. This is often accompanied by men’s use of maladaptive behaviors such as alcohol, substance use, and gambling (Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019). Some studies discuss women describing these violent behaviors that violated their rights as an inevitable reality, as men are described as being “prone” to engaging in violence (Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020).

Overwhelmed & diminished infrastructure capacity

Overwhelmed and diminished infrastructure capacity refers to formal structures and systems designed to support the public safety of communities impacted by rapid-onset climate-related disasters. In this review, nine sources describe diminished organizational infrastructures capacity (including law enforcement, healthcare, and shelters) to attend to supporting women’s needs following rapid-onset climate-related disasters (Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Picardo et al., 2010; Minakshi and Joshi, 2012). The diminished infrastructure capacity due to the disaster contributed to worsening outcomes of GBVAW, particularly through silencing women reporting DV (Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Picardo et al., 2010; Minakshi and Joshi, 2012).

In this review, three studies describe multiple theories related to the issue of reporting after a disaster as a result of overwhelmed and diminished infrastructures, which are discussed (Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Picardo et al., 2010; Minakshi and Joshi, 2012). One theory theorizes that the prevalence and incidence of GBVAW do not change but rather reports within formal infrastructures changes (Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019). For example, one study focused on a DV shelter in New Zealand navigating a snowstorm found that only 4 new cases of DV were reported, which was beneath a monthly average of 14 new clients from June 2005 to May 2007 (Parkinson and Zara, 2013). However, when communication was restored, the number of new clients increased to 25, almost double monthly average compared to the previous 2 years (Parkinson and Zara, 2013).

Another theory presented is that the increased demand for resources for all clients following the disaster exposes women to heightened vulnerability to all forms of violence, as infrastructures are unable to keep up with the demand (Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Picardo et al., 2010; Minakshi and Joshi, 2012). Two sources discuss how the overwhelmed and diminished infrastructure contributed to pressures from police and healthcare professionals that encouraged women to deny or forgive men of their violence (Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019). Instead of providing resources, these interactions accused women of over-reacting or not caring for men, which led to women being reluctant to report and being re-traumatized while seeking help within formal institutions (Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019).

The decreased capacity of infrastructure and increased demand due to rapid-onset climate-related disasters overwhelm existing systems and foster unsafe conditions for women, particularly related to security. For women, issues of security were of heightened concern for populations of women previously ostracized prior to the onset of the disaster, such as women of specific vulnerability due to age, ethnicity, caste, sexuality, economic status, and caregiver status (Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Picardo et al., 2010; Minakshi and Joshi, 2012). For example, one study in this review discusses perpetrators taking advantage of women’s vulnerability within a disaster to gain access to women or their families when police were distracted by the disaster (Houghton et al., 2010).

Displacement

The displacement of women who were obligated or forced to leave their residence to avoid harm from rapid-onset climate-related disasters or violence was also a factor found to shape women’s vulnerability to GBV (Thomas and Thomas, 2004). In this review, nine studies discuss displacement induced by rapid-onset climate-related disasters as a key factor shaping women’s vulnerability to GBV (Anastario et al., 2009; Ullah et al., 2024; Singh, 2020; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Azad et al., 2013; Schumacher et al., 2010; Anastario et al., 2008; Amoo et al., 2018). Women displaced were prone to environments of overcrowding that lacked security, particularly in supporting privacy and safety for women (Ullah et al., 2024; Singh, 2020; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Azad et al., 2013; Schumacher et al., 2010; Anastario et al., 2008; Amoo et al., 2018). This led to predatory conditions in which women encountered threats, attempts, and completed harassment, physical torture, sexual violence (Anastario et al., 2009; Ullah et al., 2024; Singh, 2020; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Azad et al., 2013; Schumacher et al., 2010; Anastario et al., 2008; Amoo et al., 2018).

The displacement of women induced by post-disasters creates heightened social conditions that contribute to women’s vulnerability to trafficking (Anastario et al., 2009; Ullah et al., 2024; Singh, 2020; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Azad et al., 2013; Amoo et al., 2018). Women displaced and in migration navigated dangerous routes that were dependent on informal networks to survive (Gibb, 2022; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Amoo et al., 2018). This contributed to systemic violence in gendered zones of control at the border of countries, in which impunity was provided to perpetrators perpetuating acts of violence against women, ranging from extortion, sexual abuse, and trafficking (Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016). Women’s vulnerability to violence and displacement created patterns of migration for women which entailed exposure to SV and trafficking, in both public and private settings (Ullah et al., 2024; Singh, 2020; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Azad et al., 2013; Schumacher et al., 2010; Anastario et al., 2008; Amoo et al., 2018). Four studies discuss the prevalence of sexual attacks towards women occurring in tents, streets, and public places (Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Madhuri, 2016; Azad et al., 2013; Amoo et al., 2018).

Internal displacement of women immediately following a disaster, in conjunction with IPV and DV, was also found to be prone to extended periods, or protracted phases of displacement and new incidences of violence (Ullah et al., 2024; Singh, 2020; Gibb, 2022; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Schumacher et al., 2010; Amoo et al., 2018). For example, one study found elevated and sustained rates of new incidences of GBV across all types of violence among women displaced for two years following Hurricane Katrina (Anastario et al., 2008). Housing is a critical aspect to protecting women from SV, trafficking, and exploitation, particularly among women leaving abusive situations (Singh, 2020; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Amoo et al., 2018). The lack of housing or housing insecurity contributed to women’s vulnerability to increased exposure and severity of all forms of violence following displacement induced by disasters (Singh, 2020; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Azad et al., 2013; Amoo et al., 2018).

Loss of social network & social capital

Loss of social network and social capital entailed the breakdown of social cohesion, structures, and dynamics that provide resources and support as a mechanism for protection towards GBVAW impacted by rapid-onset climate-related disasters (Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Safajou et al., 2023; Farmer et al., 2017; Barcelona de Mendoza et al., 2015; Harville et al., 2010; Fagen et al., 2011). 16 sources in this review discuss the impact of rapid-onset climate-related disasters on women’s loss of social network and social capital and subsequent vulnerability to GBV (Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Safajou et al., 2023; Farmer et al., 2017; Barcelona de Mendoza et al., 2015; Harville et al., 2010; Fagen et al., 2011). The loss of social networks and social capital for women resulted from deaths or separation from social supports that had previously offered protection, as well as escalated tensions and stressors between remaining social structures and interpersonal dynamics around women post-disaster (Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Safajou et al., 2023; Farmer et al., 2017; Barcelona de Mendoza et al., 2015; Harville et al., 2010; Fagen et al., 2011). The broken social networks that result diminish access to spaces that had previously offered safe refuge, sources to generate income, and information on resources available to support disaster recovery (Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019). The isolation that transpires escalates women’s vulnerability to emotional, physical, and economic violence, including trafficking (Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Madhuri, 2016). The combination of disrupted and diminished social networks, as well as economic instability and increased stressors, led to increased tensions within surrounding social dynamics and functions (Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Madhuri, 2016).

Women discussed the added stressors due to their concerns for children’s safety post-disaster due to issues of GBVAW in public and private settings (Ullah et al., 2024; Houghton et al., 2010; Ponder, 2016; Harville et al., 2010). Women often feared the safety of children being harmed, as well as the risks to confidentiality, if reporting abuse from men (Severson et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019). Women also discussed loss in social network and social capital related to issues of betrayal within intimate relationships post-disaster (Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Ponder, 2016; Harville et al., 2010). Studies in this review discuss escalated tensions within intimate relationships related to refraining from sexual activities, spouses’ lack of helpfulness to the functions of the household, and destabilized gendered roles post-disaster (Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019). The escalated social tensions destabilized intimate relations and led to relationship destabilization, including divorce, as well as DV escalation in verbal, physical, and sexual violence towards women by spouses after disasters (Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Harville et al., 2010).

The loss of social network and social capital also extended into heightened communal tensions with the breakdown of social support systems, contributing to women’s vulnerability to all forms of GBV (Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Farmer et al., 2017; Barcelona de Mendoza et al., 2015). Seven studies discuss land disputes, neighborhood violence, and community conflict without protection mechanisms for women (Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Farmer et al., 2017; Barcelona de Mendoza et al., 2015; Fagen et al., 2011). In consequence, women were prone to all forms of GBV within the broader community, as well as within systemic responses including evacuation, relief distribution efforts, and law enforcement (Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Fagen et al., 2011).

Cyclical poverty traps

Cyclical poverty traps entail self-perpetuating generational and inherited poverty that prevents women’s ability to escape. In this review, 10 studies describe cyclical poverty traps as a contributing factor to women’s vulnerability to GBV due to rapid-onset climate-related disasters (Luetke et al., 2020; Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Houghton et al., 2010; Jewkes et al., 2022; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Azad et al., 2013; Schumacher et al., 2010; Amoo et al., 2018). Cyclical poverty traps entailed denied opportunities for women to generate, attain, and sustain adequate compensation to support humane living conditions. The effects of rapid-onset climate-related disasters had a disproportionate impact on women’s livelihoods throughout their life course trajectories, which further contributed to socioeconomic vulnerability to SV, DV, IPV, and trafficking across women’s lifespans (Luetke et al., 2020; Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Houghton et al., 2010; Jewkes et al., 2022; Ponder, 2016; Azad et al., 2013; Schumacher et al., 2010; Amoo et al., 2018).

The aggravated economic hardship that ensues from a rapid-onset climate-related disaster results in the need for additional income to repair and replace houses and basic necessities and to recoup assets. For women, the effects of the disaster jeopardized means of employment and basic necessities (Luetke et al., 2020; Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Houghton et al., 2010; Jewkes et al., 2022; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Azad et al., 2013; Schumacher et al., 2010; Amoo et al., 2018). For instance, women lacked adequate clothing after a disaster and were unable to find means of income to purchase more clothes (Azad et al., 2013). The disaster also required additional economic resources for women to attain the means to alleviate illnesses and injuries, such as healthcare, transportation, and food (Luetke et al., 2020; Ullah et al., 2024; Madhuri, 2016; Amoo et al., 2018). In conjunction with increased needs for economic income, women were also most likely to lose their means of generating income. Women’s loss of income due to the disaster had negative effects for women and their children’s attainment of education, adequate sanitation facilities, and sources of food and water (Luetke et al., 2020; Singh, 2020; Amoo et al., 2018). In addition, the overall workload increases for families affected by disaster and in need of financial support (Houghton et al., 2010). The added workload of domestic work that women were responsible for fulfilling contributed to missed opportunities for women to earn income while being exposed to unsafe social conditions in public (Houghton et al., 2010).

Over the life course of women, the effects of rapid-onset disasters restricted or denied opportunities for women to attain livelihoods and education (Nguyen, 2019; Schumacher et al., 2010). In this review, the attainment of education was found to be a strong predictor of women’s exposure to post-hurricane IPV (Tricco et al., 2018; Aromataris et al., 2024; Amoo et al., 2018; Anastario et al., 2008). Women were often in positions that required permission from men to leave for work, school, or to receive disaster aid, but were also used as labor to help save money for men in rebuilding (Singh, 2020; Madhuri, 2016). The economic shocks that ensue contribute to women’s vulnerability to engaging in transactional sex, early childhood marriage, and labor exploitation, particularly among those unable to absorb the economic shocks from the disaster (Luetke et al., 2020; Singh, 2020; Jewkes et al., 2022; Madhuri, 2016).

Water & food insecurity

Another factor contributing to women’s vulnerability to GBV was water and food insecurity, which entailed the restriction of access to food and water to support women’s needs. In this review, ten sources discussed the effects of water and food insecurity on women’s vulnerability to GBVAW (Luetke et al., 2020; Ullah et al., 2024; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson, 2015; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Azad et al., 2013). Five studies found the scarcity of food and water for increased women’s risk for exposure to molestation, harassment, DV, SA, and transactional sex (Luetke et al., 2020; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson, 2015; Ponder, 2016; Azad et al., 2013). The lack of water contributed to unsafe social dynamics between women and shelter staff and community members (Ullah et al., 2024; Parkinson, 2015; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Azad et al., 2013). With the lack of water due to the disaster, women are unable to maintain hygiene related to bathing, menstruation, pregnancy, and lactating mothers (Ullah et al., 2024; Parkinson, 2015; Ponder, 2016; Azad et al., 2013). In addition, women are often in positions that require traveling through unsafe social conditions to attain food and water to support caregiving and domestic roles (Parkinson, 2015; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Azad et al., 2013). The issue of impure water causes health issues for women related to headaches and stomach pains, as well as living and sleeping problems (Parkinson, 2015; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016). The issue of food and water insecurity required women to ration portions; the lack of food and support may cause women to contact their abuser under the impression that the situation with the abuser may be better for them (Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010).

Gendered stereotype depictions of women in mass communications

Gendered stereotype depictions of women entailed inaccurate and harmful representations of women through sources of mass communication. In this review, six sources described the syndemic effect of media stereotype depictions through mass communications as contributing factors of women’s vulnerability to GBV (Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Ponder, 2016; Harris, 2011). This included sources of mass communication that depicted gendered stereotypes of women through social media, speeches, and news media (Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Ponder, 2016; Harris, 2011). While sources of mass communication acknowledge aspects of GBVAW, the representations of women within these forms of communication are depicted through sensationalized reports and characterized through imagined descriptive and prescriptive stereotypes.

In this review, descriptive stereotypes involved poor racialized minoritized women in shelters as being portrayed as prostitutes, while men were depicted as “looting” or criminal gang members (Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019). The result is the perpetuation of negative images results in the reduction of people’s full humanity. For instance, in this example, Black men are cast as criminals and sexual predators, while Black women are represented as hyper-sexual and not legitimate victims (Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019). In consequence, these gendered stereotype depictions through mass communication create a representation that delegitimizes and devalues women’s experiences within government and non-governmental organizations (NGO), delegating aid and services (Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Ponder, 2016; Harris, 2011). One source in this review analyzed speeches by public figures about Hurricane Katrina (Harris, 2011). Sources describe women as being described as only being capable to suffering, inflicting injury and violence, or depicted through prescriptive stereotypes in which women are used as symbols expected to attain whiteness, femininity, and middle-class (Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Ponder, 2016; Harris, 2011).

Male-centered disaster relief efforts

Male-centered disaster relief efforts refer to disaster relief efforts responding to rapid-onset climate-related disasters that overlook women’s realities based on and shaped by gender. In this review, 11 sources describe the exclusion of women that heightened their risk to violence due to the prioritization of men’s needs within disaster relief efforts following rapid-onset climate-related disasters (Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Parkinson, 2015; Madhuri, 2016; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Safajou et al., 2023; Richter, 2007; Azad et al., 2013; Minakshi and Joshi, 2012; Farmer et al., 2017). This led to the privileging of men within disaster aid structures that neglect the needs of women and deepened gender inequality in rapid-onset climate-related disaster contexts (Ullah et al., 2024). Men were found to often be in control of making decisions about the use of disaster aid funds (and often misused funds for alcohol or substance use), which further shaped the unsafe conditions for women (Ullah et al., 2024; Parkinson, 2015; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Minakshi and Joshi, 2012; Farmer et al., 2017). Even when women did have the chance to access relief, they remained highly vulnerable to men collecting disaster aid funds, as well as exposure to verbal, physical, and sexual violence within overcrowded settings (Nguyen, 2019; Parkinson, 2015; Madhuri, 2016; Safajou et al., 2023; Richter, 2007; Azad et al., 2013). The mismanagement of relief supplies intensified domestic tensions, placing women at even greater risk of violence (Nguyen, 2019; Parkinson, 2015; Madhuri, 2016).

Patriarchal disaster aid structures severely limited women’s participation in decision-making, not only through access to disaster aid but also resources necessary to support women’s health, including sexual and reproductive health needs (Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Parkinson, 2015; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Safajou et al., 2023; Richter, 2007; Azad et al., 2013). For example, one study found that 53% of respondents reported that their healthcare needs were not addressed in post-disaster settings (Richter, 2007). Menstruating girls and women were recognized to face multiple challenges in post-disaster settings due to a lack of privacy, stigma, inadequate disposal facilities, limited access to warm water, menstrual and sanitary supplies management, which all heightened women’s vulnerability to GBV (Nguyen, 2019; Madhuri, 2016; Safajou et al., 2023; Richter, 2007; Azad et al., 2013). In shelters, some women had no access to feminine hygiene products, while others had to pay out-of-pocket for birth control, as immediate reproductive services were often unavailable (Safajou et al., 2023; Richter, 2007; Azad et al., 2013; Minakshi and Joshi, 2012). These conditions posed significant health risks, including nutritional deficiencies and infections from unhygienic toilet facilities, which were especially severe for pregnant and lactating mothers (Jewkes et al., 2022; Madhuri, 2016; Safajou et al., 2023; Richter, 2007).

The normalization of centering men’s needs within 1,006,374 disaster relief efforts extends into temporary shelter and camp environments for women. Studies reveal women’s high exposure to widespread harassment, SA, and rape linked to unsafe sanitation facilities, overcrowding, and lack of privacy within temporary shelters and camps (Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Parkinson, 2015; Madhuri, 2016; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Safajou et al., 2023; Richter, 2007; Azad et al., 2013; Farmer et al., 2017). Women also described the profound consequences of a loss of social status and dignity that fostered predatory shelter environments, in which perpetrators slashed tents, threatened women with weapons, and exploited displacement and poverty for trafficking (Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Azad et al., 2013).

Weathering to Women’s health

Weathering refers to the impact of rapid-onset climate-related disasters on amplifying negative health outcomes among women due to previous exposure, as well as increased and sustained exposure to stressors and trauma (Severson et al., 2023). 19 studies in this review found heightened negative mental health outcomes among women due to stressors of GBVAW in times of rapid-onset climate-related disasters (Anastario et al., 2009; Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Singh, 2020; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Jewkes et al., 2022; Madhuri, 2016; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Safajou et al., 2023; Azad et al., 2013; Picardo et al., 2010; Minakshi and Joshi, 2012; Schumacher et al., 2010; Anastario et al., 2008; Amoo et al., 2018; Barcelona de Mendoza et al., 2015; Harville et al., 2010; Molyneaux et al., 2019). The findings of the studies included in this review show the weathering effects of rapid-onset climate-related disasters on women’s mental health outcomes due to GBVAW. The majority of perpetrators to these forms of violence were intimate partners, family members, or those close to women (Anastario et al., 2009; Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Singh, 2020; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Jewkes et al., 2022; Madhuri, 2016; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Safajou et al., 2023; Azad et al., 2013; Picardo et al., 2010; Minakshi and Joshi, 2012; Schumacher et al., 2010; Anastario et al., 2008; Amoo et al., 2018; Barcelona de Mendoza et al., 2015; Harville et al., 2010; Molyneaux et al., 2019). For example, following Hurricane Katrina, nearly 25% of women reported being hit or verbally threatened; psychological/emotional IPV rose from 33.6% before the disaster to 45.2% after, and physical IPV nearly doubled from 4.2% to 8.3% (Ullah et al., 2024; Harville et al., 2010; Fagen et al., 2011). These rising rates of IPV were not only alarming in prevalence but also in their consequences to women’s mental health outcomes. Women experiencing higher levels of IPV had increased risks of depression, PTSD, and panic attacks (Barcelona de Mendoza et al., 2015; Harville et al., 2010; Fagen et al., 2011). Although sexual IPV was reported less frequently, 6% of women who disclosed forced sex following disaster, in the context of displacement and household instability (Ullah et al., 2024).

In addition, women’s restriction from decision-making and inserted societal expectations based on gender, limited opportunities for women to escape abusive environments, restricted access to health services, and amplified conditions conducive to mental health deterioration, and, in extreme cases, lead to anticipated homicide, suicidal thoughts, or even self-harm (Anastario et al., 2009; Ullah et al., 2024; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Anastario et al., 2008). Women in disadvantaged social circumstances are more likely to lack preparation for childbirth and to experience psychological/emotional violence during disasters, which can isolate them from family, community, and relief support, thereby escalating physical and psychological risks (Ullah et al., 2024).

Discussion

Our analysis identified ten key factors that were found to contribute to women’s vulnerability to GBVAW in times of rapid-onset climate-related disasters. These findings demonstrate distinct factors that occur in interactions between socioeconomic, political, and environmental factors that further amplify GBVAW and health disparities among women in times of rapid-onset climate-related disasters (Luetke et al., 2020; Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Jewkes et al., 2022; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Safajou et al., 2023; Finley, 2019; Richter, 2007; Azad et al., 2013; Picardo et al., 2010; Minakshi and Joshi, 2012; Schumacher et al., 2010; Anastario et al., 2008; Amoo et al., 2018; Farmer et al., 2017; Barcelona de Mendoza et al., 2015; Harville et al., 2010; Fagen et al., 2011; Harris, 2011; Molyneaux et al., 2019). These novel insights provide crucial understandings of the state of the literature across the full range of GBVAW as a contagion in times of rapid-onset climate-related disasters. With the syndemic effects of rapid-onset climate-related disasters on GBVAW, these insights serve to inform future climate risk management approaches, as well as legal pathways and remedies that govern implementation of climate and disaster management efforts.

In this review, fifteen sources discuss the effects of hurricanes on SV, primarily in relation to DV and IPV in the United States (Anastario et al., 2009; Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Jewkes et al., 2022; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Finley, 2019; Richter, 2007; Picardo et al., 2010; Schumacher et al., 2010; Anastario et al., 2008; Farmer et al., 2017; Barcelona de Mendoza et al., 2015; Harville et al., 2010; Fagen et al., 2011; Harris, 2011). Of these sources, 13 studies demonstrate the immediate and sustained effects of Hurricane Katrina on hindering women’s health outcomes related to GBVAW due to systemic barriers to healthcare, security, and housing (Anastario et al., 2009; Ponder, 2016; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Finley, 2019; Richter, 2007; Picardo et al., 2010; Schumacher et al., 2010; Anastario et al., 2008; Farmer et al., 2017; Barcelona de Mendoza et al., 2015; Harville et al., 2010; Fagen et al., 2011; Harris, 2011). Of studies focused on Hurricane Katrina, six studies found reports of increased emotional/psychological, physical, and SV, mostly driven by IPV or DV, among reproductive-aged women in Louisiana, Mississippi, or North Carolina (Richter, 2007; Picardo et al., 2010; Schumacher et al., 2010; Anastario et al., 2008; Farmer et al., 2017; Barcelona de Mendoza et al., 2015; Harville et al., 2010). Among these studies, this included the anticipated fear of victimization within public shelters and neighborhoods that contributed to poorer mental health outcomes (including depression and post-traumatic stress disorder). For women impacted by Hurricane Katrina, the reinforcement of power imbalances based on gender within systemic responses created unequal access to receiving disaster aid, including housing, which worsened the frequency and severity of DV by trapping women in abusive situations (Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Richter, 2007). Furthermore, studies describe the impact of Hurricane Katrina on transforming neighborhoods through gentrification by offering police protection to new residents, while using police aggression and brutality to target and displace long-term residents who were Black women with children (Ponder, 2016; Finley, 2019).

The impact of rapid-onset climate-related disasters on compounding socioeconomic and health disparities among women due to the acquisition of governed resources, most notably housing, security, disaster aid, and healthcare, is similar across types of disasters and contexts (Luetke et al., 2020; Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Jewkes et al., 2022; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Safajou et al., 2023; Finley, 2019; Richter, 2007; Azad et al., 2013; Picardo et al., 2010; Minakshi and Joshi, 2012; Schumacher et al., 2010; Anastario et al., 2008; Amoo et al., 2018; Farmer et al., 2017; Barcelona de Mendoza et al., 2015; Harville et al., 2010; Fagen et al., 2011; Harris, 2011). For instance, relief camps, public, and makeshift shelters were consistently found to be insufficient in protecting women from SV following floods, cyclones, and wildfires or bushfires in Pakistan, Iran, Australia, Haiti, Bangladesh, the Philippines, and India (Ullah et al., 2024; Nguyen, 2019; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Safajou et al., 2023). In turn, some studies discuss women’s reluctance to access public shelters in times of rapid-onset climate-related disasters (Madhuri, 2016; Farmer et al., 2017). In areas prone to flooding and cyclones in Bangladesh, the effect of floods on patterns of migration and employment shifted the dynamics of women within households and surrounding predators (Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Azad et al., 2013). Due to flooding and cyclones, women were often unemployed and men within shared households migrated for long periods of time for employment (Azad et al., 2013). These changes created social conditions in which perpetrators pretended to be volunteers and preyed upon women found alone in households or seeking shelter in abandoned buildings during cyclones (Rezwana and Pain, 2020).

While some studies suggest the impact of rapid-onset climate-related disasters on worsening women’s dependence on previously abusive partners in relation to mobility, housing, and security (Singh, 2020; Jewkes et al., 2022; Gibb, 2022; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Minakshi and Joshi, 2012), this varied by culture and access to disaster aid responses. For instance, studies in India discuss cultural ideologies that required women to attain men’s permission to move to another location for safety in times of floods in areas of India (Singh, 2020; Madhuri, 2016; Minakshi and Joshi, 2012). Similarly, one study conducted in the Philippines found women used their social location in ways that facilitated receiving critical resources to acquire relocation and housing based on their knowledge of ongoing humanitarian discourse (Gibb, 2022). In addition, there are distinct contextual differences that impact transferability in relation to security through the use of policing within informal and formal settings based on context (Jewkes et al., 2022; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Richter, 2007; Harville et al., 2010). A majority of studies discuss the use of police in formal and informal settings following Hurricane Katrina in the US context (Finley, 2019; Picardo et al., 2010; Farmer et al., 2017; Harville et al., 2010). Most studies discuss reports of DV to police through the use of phone calls or call centers in Australia, New Zealand, and India following bushfires, snowstorms, and flooding (Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019). One study conducted discusses the lack of applicability of police use in the context of South Africa (Jewkes et al., 2022).

Despite differences in social mobility related to caste and class systems among studies, learned behavioral scripts were a central mechanism that worsened socioeconomic disparities and subsequent health outcomes among populations of women in times of rapid-onset climate-related disasters (Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Jewkes et al., 2022; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Safajou et al., 2023; Finley, 2019; Richter, 2007; Azad et al., 2013; Picardo et al., 2010; Minakshi and Joshi, 2012; Schumacher et al., 2010; Anastario et al., 2008; Amoo et al., 2018; Farmer et al., 2017; Barcelona de Mendoza et al., 2015; Harville et al., 2010; Fagen et al., 2011; Harris, 2011; Molyneaux et al., 2019). These learned behavioral scripts become enforced to exclude populations of women from receiving protections based on a set of established and institutionalized social rules within dominant social groups that govern formal and informal spaces in times of heightened uncertainty and shifting climates (Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Jewkes et al., 2022; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Safajou et al., 2023; Azad et al., 2013; Amoo et al., 2018; Farmer et al., 2017; Harris, 2011; Molyneaux et al., 2019). These social rules are institutionalized to govern formal and informal spaces by enforcing learned behavioral scripts, or socially normative expectations that are learned through exposure and observation that program behaviors (Contagion of Violence, 2013; Forsyth and Gibbs, 2020). Populations of women disproportionately vulnerable to worsened health outcomes included women experiencing trafficking, transactional sex, abusing drugs, and living with disabilities, HIV, and AIDS, as well as women new to social networks, including neighborhoods or camps (Severson et al., 2023; Ullah et al., 2024; Vara-Horna et al., 2023; Nguyen, 2019; Singh, 2020; Houghton et al., 2010; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Jewkes et al., 2022; Gibb, 2022; Ponder, 2016; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Jenkins and Phillips, 2008; Safajou et al., 2023; Azad et al., 2013; Amoo et al., 2018; Farmer et al., 2017; Harris, 2011; Molyneaux et al., 2019).

This is consistent with existing literature on syndemics, which recognizes violence as a contagion spread through learned behavioral scripts in relation to female homicides, DV, and slow violence from environmental degradation (Contagion of Violence, 2013; Forsyth and Gibbs, 2020; Castillo and Moscoso, 2022; George, 2014; Hofnung, 2024). The concept of slow violence incorporates time in the process of environmental harm within familiar operations that were initially described as invisible (Davies, 2018; Davies, 2022; Kaufman, 2014). However, current understandings of slow violence argue that slow violence requires the use of slow observation to make visible the effects of uneven distributions of resources through degradations to losses of quality of life over time. Slow violence recognizes multiple dimensions of violence (including forms, intentionality, and directions of victimization) that, if recognized in relation to historical and structural processes on climate, can be made visible to outside observers (Cairns, 2022).

While only one dissertation and qualitative study explicitly discuss slow violence in relation to patterns of SV in relation to the Black Friday Bushfires in Australia and Cyclone Roanu in Bangladesh, (Parkinson, 2015; Rezwana and Pain, 2020), most studies describe patterns of disproportionate slow recovery from rapid-onset climate-related disaster most commonly occurring among populations vulnerable to violence (Anastario et al., 2009; Ullah et al., 2024; Jewkes et al., 2022; Madhuri, 2016; Bermudez et al., 2019; Rezwana and Pain, 2020; Anastario et al., 2008; Amoo et al., 2018; Molyneaux et al., 2019). In addition, a few studies describe women’s shifting tolerances to violence following rapid-onset climate-related disasters (Nguyen, 2019; Parkinson and Zara, 2013; Parkinson, 2015; Parkinson, 2019; Picardo et al., 2010). While three studies describe increases in exposure to DV, IPV, and SV among women immediately and sustained following rapid-onset climate-related disasters (Azad et al., 2013; Anastario et al., 2008; Molyneaux et al., 2019). For instance, 7.4% of women in high-affected bushfire areas in Australia reported experiencing violence sustained 3–4 years following the bushfire (Molyneaux et al., 2019). In turn, this was found to be associated with increased symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder and depression. Similarly, in Bangladesh, in areas prone to cyclones and floods, women reported increased experiences of crimes, such as theft, robbery, and burglary, for up to five years following a flood (Azad et al., 2013).

Notably, three sources in this review did not find increases in either workplace sexual harassment, SV, or IPV (Nguyen, 2019; Jewkes et al., 2022; Fagen et al., 2011). Of these sources, one source focused on outcomes of SV among women in a university setting (Fagen et al., 2011). This is consistent with existing literature in which achieving higher education offers protection from GBVAW for women (Murphy et al., 2023). Another source found that there was no overall increase in IPV after flooding despite escalated stressors, as well as the relationship of household resiliency in mediating IPV outcomes following flood (Jewkes et al., 2022). While these studies do not show increases in these forms of GBVAW in the aftermath of rapid-onset climate-related disasters, these observations contribute to urgently needed slow observations at times of heightened uncertainty in climate and human survival. Currently, slow observations and inquiries of all forms and types of GBVAW on women’s health outcomes are not included as critical aspects to inform climate risk management assessments and models, particularly in times of rapid-onset climate-related disasters.

The complexity of integrating human systems into climate and disaster management models is recognized to be complex by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), the leading body for assessing the science of climate change (IPCC, 2023). While the IPCC report highlights epistemic forms of violence and their risks, no existing assessments or models integrate understanding of shifting cluster patterns across different forms and types of GBVAW with climate shifts, including rapid-onset climate-related disasters (O’Lear et al., 2022; O’Lear, 2024). Additionally, environmental historians and sociologists have called for the critical need to integrate capitalocene as an aspect of human systems within climate and disaster risk management frameworks (O’Lear et al., 2022; O’Lear, 2024). Capitalocene calls for understanding capitalism as the starting point underlying the relationship of human and climate disasters (O’Lear et al., 2022; O’Lear, 2024). As a destructive, divisive system, capitalocene calls for challenging the way in which nature is situated within international climate risk management frameworks, and the impact of capitalism in making disasters (O’Lear et al., 2022; O’Lear, 2024). Developing climate and disaster risk management frameworks designed to understand long-term capitalocene effects could inform climate risk management frameworks to mitigate different forms and types of GBVAW in times prone to syndemics, particularly rapid-onset climate-related disasters. With understanding these effects, climate and disaster risk management approaches hold potential for developing public health interventions tailored to shifting contexts, as well as translating population-specific health interventions to mitigate different forms and types of GBVAW.

Currently, disasters are governed as a threat to human security in relation to climate change; with these climate risk management approaches inherently involve third parties external to directly impacted rapid-onset climate-related disasters (Forsyth and Gibbs, 2020; Taylor et al., 2013; Fagan et al., 2007; Institute of Medicine and National Research Council, 2013; O’Lear et al., 2022; Rothe et al., 2025). Third-party roles refer to entities outside of the directly affected area to the rapid-onset climate-related disaster (Forsyth and Gibbs, 2020; Taylor et al., 2013; Fagan et al., 2007; Institute of Medicine and National Research Council, 2013; O’Lear et al., 2022; Rothe et al., 2025). With these current approaches, the following section presents recent evolving legal pathways and remedies that inform climate risk management approaches at the intersection of rapid-onset climate-related disasters and GBVAW.

Future climate risk management implications to mitigate syndemic effects: Legal considerations & legal remedies

As this paper has demonstrated, the effects of climate-related disasters have a disproportionate effect on women and GBVAW. These effects are only anticipated to worsen, with the direction of climate change and increasing patterns of rapid-onset climate-related disasters, leading to increased vulnerabilities for these discrete populations.

Today, we lack clear legal remedies and concrete pathways to account for these disparate impacts in both US law and international law. Still, possible legal pathways are emerging as evidenced by the rise in climate change litigation in both domestic and international tribunals around the world. For example, the 1979 Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW) has played an important role in addressing discrimination against women, but challenges remain in implementing the treaty’s legal protections and applying them to climate change’s disparate impacts. Outside the US, poorer nations have achieved some notable climate legal wins at both the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea (ITLOS) and the International Court of Justice (ICJ). Nevertheless, legal protections and existing legal doctrines largely fail to provide substantive remedies for vulnerable populations, such as women who disproportionately face climate impacts.

Potential international legal pathways

In what follows, we analyze how two longstanding international treaties could potentially be enforced in protecting GBVAW, mapping each legal instrument on the relevant structured taxonomy factor. Irrespective of seeking legal protections for GBVAW, international law suffers from enforceability complications, as national self-interest can override existing legal commitments. (Bodansky, 2006) Concern over implementing international legal obligations is not unique to GBVAW. Still, two treaties and three recent international legal cases provide potential international legal pathways and a glimpse of how they apply to the ten factors we identified.

First, the 1951 Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees and the 1967 Refugee Protocol have protected refugees fleeing for specific reasons, but have yet to specifically address GBVAW or to protect migrants fleeing environmental disaster (Joint Stipulation and Order Re, 2024). Under international law, protected refugee status may be granted for reasons of persecution (e.g., race, nationality, or membership of a particular social group) (Warren, 2017). The Refugee Convention and Protocol could serve as a potential legal pathway to shape pre-existing gender inequalities and displacement, but the enforceability of legal protections remains a challenge. Under the Refugee Convention’s plain meaning and existing interpretation, environmental migrants fleeing their homeland would not fall under this refugee conception (Convention Relating to the Status of Refugees art. 1, 1951). Nevertheless, the Refugee Convention can be amended with future legal protocols to expand the definition of refugees. While international law fails to provide specific protections for migrants and refugees navigating climate-related disasters, states receiving climate migrants could voluntarily choose to afford them special legal protection.

Second, the 1979 Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW) is a major human rights treaty administered by a separate treaty-monitoring international body (Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination Against Women, 1981). Because of its broad focus on protecting women, CEDAW is known as the “international bill of rights for women” and maps onto all ten key factors. While CEDAW holds some promise in alleviating discrimination against women, the Convention has yet to be successfully employed in an international tribunal addressing discrimination against women suffering from disproportionate climate change impacts. And several key countries, such as the United States, have signed CEDAW, but have not yet ratified CEDAW.

Beyond these two treaties, there have been three recent lawsuits addressing climate change at international tribunals that have upheld climate rights, many of which can be mapped on our structured taxonomy. Evidentiary standards vary from international tribunal to tribunal, with most climate-related tribunals placing the burden on the party bringing the claim to establish the facts alleged. International courts often assess whether the evidence convincingly establishes the facts, rather than applying domestic-law standards like “preponderance” or “clear and convincing. (María and Benjamin, 2025).

First, in July 2025, the International Court of Justice (ICJ) issued a landmark Advisory Opinion on States’ Obligations in Respect of Climate Change. (International Court of Justice, 2025) This ICJ decision maps onto several key factors, including overwhelmed systemic infrastructure capacity, displacement, and water and food insecurity. The ICJ recognizes that a clean, healthy, and sustainable environment is a human right, and environmental harm can undermine the rights of current and future generations. In addition, the ICJ affirmed that states have an affirmative legal duty under international law to combat climate change, and failure to meet these obligations can result in legal consequences, such as compensation (International Court of Justice, 2025). Finally, the ICJ highlighted the importance of protecting vulnerable populations. While the ICJ opinion is a non-binding advisory opinion, the opinion marked a significant step in reinforcing state obligations to act on climate change and protect vulnerable populations, thus setting the stage for future climate-related proceedings (Caroline, 2025).

Second, the International Tribunal for the Law of the Sea (ITLOS) issued an Advisory Panel in 2024 at the request of the Commission of Small Island States on Climate Change and International Law (See Human Rights Committee, 2020). This decision holds promise in shaping two factors: climate displacement and overwhelmed systemic infrastructure capacity. In a 153-page opinion, the court found that state parties to the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea (1) take all necessary measures to prevent, reduce, and control marine pollution from GHG emissions; and (2) to take affirmative steps to protect and preserve the marine environment. Again, while not a binding decision, the ITLOS decision’s focus on taking affirmative steps could influence domestic legislation and future international opinions evaluating the scope of state responsibility in addressing climate change. Still, the United States is not a party to the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea and has not agreed to abide by ITLOS decisions.

Third, in Teitiota v. New Zealand, a citizen of Kiribati (Ioane Teitiota) filed a complaint with the UN Human Rights Committee, claiming that New Zealand’s decision to deport him constituted a violation of the International Committee on Civil and Political Rights (ICCPR) (Teitiota v. New Zealand, 2026). Similar to the ITLOS decision, Teitiota holds promise in shaping two factors: climate displacement and overwhelmed systemic infrastructure capacity. Teitiota argued that climate-driven sea level rise and the lack of fresh water amounted to persecution under the ICCPR. As such, he asserted that he was entitled to a special legal status as a climate refugee within New Zealand (Teitiota v. New Zealand, 2026). New Zealand rejected this argument and deported Teitiota in 2015. While the UN Human Rights Committee found that New Zealand did not violate Teitiota’s rights under the ICCPR, the Court explicitly noted that climate-related harm could trigger state-based legal obligations. Unlike the ICJ and ITLOS decision Teitiota is focused on the international legal obligations for one state, New Zealand, thus making enforcement dependent on the actions of one actor. While New Zealand has failed to update its immigration and visa laws in response to the decision, the ICJ decision cited the Teitiota v. New Zealand decision.

These three international law cases all mark advances in climate-related legal claims. While fundamental questions remain about their enforceability, the trend is for increased climate litigation in the face of growing Greenhouse Gas emissions. Still, none of these enshrine specific legal protections for GBVAW that are immediately binding on all states and enforceable. Time will tell if these cases mark the beginning of a broader conception of positive legal rights that can pave the way for protecting disadvantaged groups, particularly for women navigating the effects of climate change and rapid-onset climate-related disasters.

Conclusion

With the direction of climate change anticipated to worsen patterns of rapid-onset climate-related disasters, understanding the current and shifting climate on GBVAW poses a serious threat to diminishing worsening socioeconomic and health disparities. In this scoping literature review, we identified ten key factors that contribute to shaping women’s vulnerability to GBVAW due to rapid-onset climate-related disasters. These findings call for an urgent and timely integration into ongoing and future climate risk management approaches to mitigate the multiplicative syndemic outcomes of GBVAW in times of heightened uncertainty. While environmental historians and social scientists have recognized the need for slow observation illuminating slow violence, current observations and inquiries lack integration into informing climate risk management approaches and frameworks. If better understood, these integrations provide tailored opportunities for climate risk management approaches and frameworks tailored to context and population-specific needs. Finally, while legal structures have failed to provide adequate protection to confront climate change’s disparate impacts on GBVAW, international courts have seen an uptick in climate litigation asserting novel claims. These novel claims hold the potential to inform future climate risk management capable of mitigating the syndemic effects of climate change through rapid-onset climate-related disasters on GBVAW.
