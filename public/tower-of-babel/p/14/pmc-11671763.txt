Large-scale production of rice haploids by combining superior haploid inducer with PTGMS lines
Authors: Chaolei Liu, Song Yan, Fangming Mao, Tingting Sun, Huan Liang, Qing Liu, Qian Qian, Kejian Wang
Published: 2024

Dear Editor,

Doubled haploid (DH) technology is the fastest and most efficient method for producing completely homozygous lines, commonly used in maize breeding (Ren et al., 2017). The discovery of Stock 6, the first maize haploid inducer (HI) reported in 1959, yields 2%–3% maternal haploids during self-pollination or when used as a male in outcrossing. High-efficiency HIs derived from Stock 6, with a haploid induction rate (HIR) of 7%–15%, have significantly contributed to the commercialization of maize DH breeding. Recently, the genetic basis of haploid induction has been identified with two main molecular players in maize: MTL/ZmPLA1/NLD, which triggers haploid production (Gilles et al., 2017; Kelliher et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2017), and DMP, which enhances haploid induction (Zhong et al., 2019). Importantly, MTL is conserved across cereals, and knockout of OsMATL in rice has produced 2%–6% haploids (Yao et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2024a), presenting promising prospects for rice breeding. However, due to the lack of high-efficiency HIs and limitations in cross-pollination, DH technology based on Osmatl remains an unreliable and inefficient practice for rice breeding. Here, we developed a high-efficiency HI with favorable agronomic performance in rice. The Fix synthetic apomixis system, achieved through simultaneous mutagenesis of OsMATL and three MiMe genes, has exhibited significant variations in clonal seed efficiency among different rice varieties (Liu et al., 2024b). Based on these findings, we hypothesize that haploid induction may also be influenced by genetic background. Despite extensive efforts in recent years, no high-efficiency HIs with an HIR exceeding 8% have been reported in rice. To develop such HIs, we crossed CY84_HI (Wang et al., 2019) with 20 different germplasms, including 12 cultivated varieties, seven landrace varieties, and one wild rice variety (Supplemental Table 1). In the F2 generation, we screened for homozygous Osmatl mutants within different genetic backgrounds and subsequently advanced them using the single-seed descent method (Supplemental Figure 1). Ultimately, a total of 112 Osmatl HIs with diverse genetic backgrounds were developed, and their HIRs were estimated by identifying haploids in the field. Notably, these haploid plants exhibited distinct phenotypic traits, including dwarfism, shortened flag leaves and spikelet lengths, and reduced glume sizes compared to their diploid counterparts (Figure 1B). Overall, most Osmatl HIs from different genetic backgrounds displayed very low HIRs, ranging from 0% to 3.1%; however, four of the 10 lines derived from the cross between CY84_HI and the local variety CaiYuanZhong exhibited HIRs ranging from 9.4% to 12.5% (Supplemental Table 2). Subsequently, these 10 lines from the CY84_HI × CaiYuanZhong cross, along with one line each from the other crosses, were selected for a more accurate HIR assessment through flow cytometry analysis of their selfed seeds (Figure 1C). Consistent with field results, the four high-efficiency HIs exhibited HIRs ranging from 11.8% to 15.1%, whereas the remaining lines showed only 0.7%–4.4% HIRs (Supplemental Table 3). To facilitate field cross-pollination, we selected an elite line, referred to as HI285, which exhibited desirable traits such as a high plant height (141.3 ± 2.2 cm), a short heading date (73–79 days), and a significantly improved HIR of 12.4% ± 0.8% (Figure 1D). Despite a fertility rate of 14.2% ± 2.4% in HI285 (Figure 1D), we produced an abundant supply of seeds for subsequent haploid induction research.

The bisexual flowers of rice pose a significant challenge in emasculation and hybridization, making large-scale haploid induction in rice a labor-intensive process (Qi et al., 2023). In the context of two-line hybrid rice, the development of PTGMS lines is crucial; however, breeding new PTGMS lines using traditional methods is challenging and time-consuming, typically requiring at least 6–10 generations of inbreeding through selfing or sib-crossing. To facilitate easier crossbreeding and explore rapid generation of new PTGMS lines, we collected several PTGMS lines, including PA64S, 638S, LF85S, and XXS, and crossed them in pairs to create heterozygous PTGMS (het-PTGMS) lines under low-temperature conditions when these PTGMS lines were fertile. Similar to the seed production process in hybrid rice, we interplanted HI285 with the het-PTGMS lines, surrounding them with an isolation belt, at high temperatures when the PTGMS lines became sterile (Supplemental Figure 2). We employed two strategies in practice. In strategy I, we monitored the heading dates of the het-PTGMS lines and staggered the sowing of both het-PTGMS and HI285 seeds to synchronize their flowering periods. In strategy II, we initially sowed and planted the HI285 line in stages, and subsequently transferred different het-PTGMS lines with varying flowering dates to the HI285 plots at their respective heading stages (Figure 1E). Furthermore, segregated F2 or advanced-generation het-PTGMS lines with artificially selected desirable agronomic traits were also moved to the HI285 plots. Given the tall stature of HI285 plants, their pollen can easily reach the stigmas of the het-PTGMS lines. At maturity, the het-PTGMS lines from both strategies produced seeds, yielding fertility rates of 6.0% ± 2.9% and 9.1% ± 2.8%, respectively, whereas the self-pollinated seeds of HI285 exhibited a fertility rate of 15.6% ± 3.2% (Figures 1F and 1G). Despite the overall low fertility rates, the large scale of our operations allowed us to obtain a significant quantity of hybrid seeds.

To explore the potential for creating new PTGMS lines, we conducted a germination experiment using hybrid seeds derived from crossing the het-PTGMS line (the F1 of LF85S and XXS) with HI285. We identified putative haploid individuals (or DHs) using 12 insertion/deletion markers distributed across all 12 chromosomes (Supplemental Table 4; Supplemental Figure 3), followed by confirmation of their haploid ploidy through flow cytometry analysis (Figure 1C). Of the 140 progenies analyzed, 18 lines were confirmed as haploids, and three individuals as DH, demonstrating an HIR of 15.0%. The PTGMS haploid plants exhibited typical haploid characteristics, whereas the DH lines displayed variable sterility/fertility under high/low temperature conditions (Figure 1H). To further confirm their genotypes, we randomly selected six haploids and two DHs for sequencing using next-generation sequencing technology (Supplemental Table 5). Analysis of 776 790 SNP markers distributed across all 12 chromosomes, which differed between LF85S and XXS (Supplemental Table 6), revealed that all eight plants were fully homozygous across all chromosomes and contained recombinant regions distinct from those in the parental genomes (Figure 1I). As a result, we successfully generated new PTGMS lines.

In summary, we have successfully developed a high-efficiency HI with an HIR exceeding 10% and desirable agronomic traits, achieved through extensive cross-breeding using diverse germplasm resources. The integration of PTGMS lines has significantly simplified the cross-pollination process when combined with this superior HI, enabling efficient large-scale haploid production and the rapid generation of new PTGMS lines. Consequently, we have established a practical, high-throughput methodology for producing rice haploids using HIs, which is expected to greatly expedite the adoption of DH technology in rice breeding.
