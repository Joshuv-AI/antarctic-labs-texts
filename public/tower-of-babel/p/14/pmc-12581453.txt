Cervical pathology outcomes in the setting of completely normal colposcopic impressions
Authors: Yusha Chen, Suyu Li, Jimiao Huang, Xiqi Huang, Huifeng Xue, Jinwen Zheng, Xiangqin Zheng, Dilin Pan, Jiancui Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the pathological outcomes in patients referred for colposcopy due to abnormal cervical cancer screening results while presenting completely normal colposcopic impressions.MethodsThis retrospective study was conducted at the Cervical Disease Diagnosis and Treatment Health Center of Fujian Maternity and Child Health Hospital (a tertiary referral center in Southeast China). We included 2,515 patients with abnormal screening (cytology/HPV) but completely normal colposcopy. All underwent four-quadrant cervical biopsies. Endocervical curettage (ECC) was added for HPV16/18-positive cases, high-grade cytology, or partially visualized squamocolumnar junctions (SCJ). Conization/hysterectomy was performed per clinical guidelines. Pathological diagnosis relied on the most severe findings across cervical biopsies, ECC, conization and hysterectomy specimens.ResultsHigh-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions or worse (CIN2+) were detected in 7.1% (179/2515), including 7 cervical cancers (all postmenopausal, aged >45, with partially visualized SCJ). Biopsies confirmed 72.1% (129/179) of CIN2+ cases, while 20.1% (36/179) were identified via ECC and 7.8% (14/179) via conization. CIN2+ risk correlated with advanced age (P=0.011) and high-risk screening results (P<0.001). Menstrual status, cervical transformation zone type, gravidity, and parity showed no significant differences between CIN2+ and normal/CIN1 groups.ConclusionThe presence of CIN2+ lesions cannot be ruled out based solely on completely normal colposcopic impressions. High-risk screening results and advanced age are associated with an increased risk of CIN2+. Random biopsies, alongside ECC when the SCJ is partially visualized, play a crucial role in detection, particularly among postmenopausal patients. However, for patients under 25 with low-risk screening results, such procedures may not be imperative.

Introduction

Colposcopy assumes a crucial role in cervical cancer control, serving as the primary triage method for evaluating individuals with abnormal cervical cancer screening outcomes. By applying 5% acetic acid and Lugol’s iodine to the cervix, colposcopy enables a magnified examination to identify features such as acetowhite epithelium, mosaic, punctation, or non-stained Lugol’s iodine areas, which are suspicious for CIN.

However, the reported sensitivity of colposcopy for detecting cervical precancerous lesions is only 56% [1]. Biopies are recommended targeting all areas with acetowhitening even when the colposcopic impression is normal [2]. However, the acceptability of deferring biopsies for individuals with a completely normal colposcopic impression (i.e., no acetowhitening, metaplasia, or other visible abnormalities) remains uncertain and inconsistent. According to the American Society for Colposcopy and Cervical Pathology (ASCCP) [2], in cases where patients exhibit completely normal colposcopic impressions along with negative high-grade cytology and non-16/18 human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, random biopsies are not recommended. However, if screening results indicate HPV 16-positive high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion(HSIL) cytology, immediate treatment without biopsy confirmation is considered an alternative, while guideline recommendations are absent for other screening results. The Canadian colposcopy guideline [3] do not recommend random biopsies when the colposcopic impression is completely normal.

Given the varying guideline recommendations and clinical uncertainty surrounding the management of patients with a normal colposcopic impression across different screening risk strata, this study aims to evaluate the safety and acceptability of deferring random biopsies in this population. To achieve this, we analyzed the pathology outcomes of patients who were referred for colposcopy due to abnormal screening tests yet presented with a completely normal examination. The findings are expected to offer evidence-based insights that can help guide biopsy decisions in this clinically under-studied group.

Materials and methods

Study design

This is a retrospective observational study, Fig. 1 shows the flow chart diagram of patient selection. Between January 2019 and November 2023, a total of 18,908 colposcopies were conducted at the Cervical Disease Diagnosis and Treatment Health Center of Fujian Maternity and Child Health Hospital. Patients with abnormal colposcopic impressions, pregnancy, prior conization or total hysterectomy, as well as those lacking cytology/HPV results and cervical pathology, were excluded. Ultimately, 2515 patients with completely normal colposcopy results were included in the study. The interval between cytology/HPV testing and colposcopy was always less than 3 months. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee at Fujian Maternity and Child Health Hospital, Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University (2024KY014). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. The research findings were presented following the guidelines outlined in the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement.

Cytology and HPV testing

Liquid-based cytology screening was utilized for the testing. Cytology results were interpreted following the 2001 Bethesda system [4]. Abnormal cytology findings were categorized as Atypical Squamous Cells of Undetermined Significance (ASC-US), Low-Grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion (LSIL), High-Grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion (HSIL), Atypical Squamous Cells-Cannot Exclude HSIL (ASC-H), Atypical Glandular Cells-Not Otherwise Specified (AGC-NOS), Atypical Glandular Cells - Favor Neoplasia (AGC-FN), Endocervical Adenocarcinoma In Situ (AIS), Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC), and Adenocarcinoma. Three HPV testing methodologies were employed: Hybrid Capture-2 testing (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) targeting HPV DNA of 13 high-risk oncogenic types (HPV-16, −18, −31, −33, −35, −39, −45, −51, −52, −56, −58, −59, and − 68), PCR‐RDB HPV genotyping (Yaneng Biotech) identifying 16 high-risk HPV types (HPV-16, 18, 31, 33, 35, 39, 45, 51, 52, 56, 58, 59, 66, 68, 73, 82), one borderline risk HPV type - HPV 53, one indeterminate risk HPV type - HPV 83 and five low-risk HPV types (HPV-6, 11, 42, 43, and 81), and Aptima (Gen-Probe Inc., San Diego, CA) for HPV E6/E7 mRNA detection from 14 high-risk oncogenic types (HPV-16, −18, −31, −33, −35, −39, −45, −51, −52, −56, −58, −59, −66, and − 68).

Colposcopy and biopsy protocol

The colposcopy referral criteria comprise the following: positive HPV16/18, non-16/18 HPV infection with abnormal cytology (ASC-US+), persistent non-16/18 HPV infection, two consecutive ASCUS results, LSIL or greater cytology. patients meeting these criteria underwent examination using a dynamic spectral imaging (DSI) colposcope (Leisegang, Germany). Following the application of 5% acetic acid and Lugol’s iodine, if the cervix appeared completely normal (i.e., without acetowhitening, metaplasia, or other visible abnormalities), random biopsies were taken at the squamocolumnar junction (SCJ) in that quadrant at 3, 6, 9, or 12 o’clock for patients with fully visualized SCJ. For patients with SCJ not fully visualized, biopsies were taken at the external cervical ostium in the corresponding quadrant at 3, 6, 9, or 12 o’clock. patients positive for HPV16/18, with cytology results of ASC-H, HSIL, or AGC, and with SCJ not fully visualized underwent endocervical curettage (ECC). An expert colposcopist, who was blinded to the pathological diagnoses, retrospectively reviewed all colposcopic images obtained from the patients included in this study.

Definition of histologic end points

Conization procedures (cold-knife conization or loop electrosurgical excision) were indicated for: biopsy-confirmed CIN 3, most CIN 2 cases, or CIN 1 meeting any of the following criteria: persistent disease ≥ 24 months, patient request post-counseling, or concurrent high-grade cytology (HSIL, AGC-FN, or persistent ASC-H). Total hysterectomy was adjunctively performed when clinically warranted. The pathological evaluations of cervical biopsies, ECC, conization, and hysterectomy tissues were conducted independently by two senior pathologists who were blinded to the patients’ identities. The final pathological diagnosis was based on the most severe findings among the cervical biopsies, ECC, conization, and hysterectomy tissues. Histologic endpoints were defined according to the 2014 WHO classification of tumors of the female reproductive organs (4th edition) [5] and the Lower Anogenital Squamous Terminology (LAST) recommendations [6]. These classifications include Normal cervix; LSIL (low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion), which encompasses CIN1 and p16 negative CIN2; HSIL, comprising p16 positive CIN2 and CIN3; AIS; and invasive cervical cancer. Furthermore, HSIL, AIS, and invasive cervical cancer were grouped as CIN2+.

Statistical analysis

HSIL, ASC-H, AGC, AIS and carcinoma were categorized as high grade cytology. Cases with high grade cytology and/or positive HPV16/18 were categorized as high-risk screening results; all other cases were categorized as low-risk screening results. Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). All P-values were derived from two-sided tests, with statistical significance defined as P <.05. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and chi-square tests were employed for analysis. Normality of numerical variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and analyzed using the t-test. Non-normally distributed data were reported as median with interquartile range and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney test. The Cochran-Armitage trend test was utilized to examine trends.The Wilson method was utilized to compute 95% confidence intervals for the proportions of CIN2 + across different scenarios.

Results

Characteristics of the selected patients

The detailed characteristics of patients with completely normal colposcopic impressions are provided in Table 1. The average age of the patients was 46.82 years, ranging from 19 to 86. Among the patients, 30.3% (764) were positive for HPV16/18, 62.7% (1576) had non-16/18 genotype high-risk HPV infection, and 5.6% (140) were negative for high-risk HPV. Only 10.8% (272) of patients had a fully visualized SCJ. The majority (76.0%, 1912) of patients were menopausal. Furthermore, 97.6% (2455) had no history of cervical lesions, while 2.3% (58) had a history of LSIL, and only 2 patients had a history of HSIL. Regarding cytological results, 86.2% (2167) of patients had low-risk findings (NILM/ASCUS/LSIL), while 9.1% (233) had high grade cytology. The final pathology revealed that 76.5% (1925) of patients had normal results, 16.3% (411) had CINI, 4.2% (106) had CIN2, 2.2% (56) had CIN3, 0.4% (10) had AIS, and 0.3% (7) had cervical cancer. Additionally, among the 2299 patients who underwent ECC, 92.5% (2127) had normal results, 3.9% (90) had CIN I, 3.1% (72) had HSIL, 0.3% (6) had AIS, and 0.2% (4) had endocervical adenocarcinoma. Furthermore, 163 patients underwent conization or hysterectomy, among whom the pathology of 13 conization tissues confirmed HSIL, with random biopsies/ECC indicating CIN1. The detailed clinical information for the 7 cervical cancer cases is provided in Table 2. All cases involved postmenopausal patients aged over 45 years, with the SCJ not visualized. Six cases exhibited high-risk screening results. Among them, random biopsies revealed normal pathology in four cases, while ECC indicated cervical adenocarcinoma, including two cases of cervical clear cell carcinoma. In two other cases, both random biopsies and ECC pathology showed HSIL, and conization revealed cervical squamous cell carcinoma. Only one presented a low-risk screening result. In this particular instance, random biopsies identified CIN1, whereas ECC yielded normal results, subsequent conization revealed cervical adenoid basal carcinoma.

Detection of CIN2 + by random biopsies in different screening risk strata

Table 3 demonstrates that among all patients diagnosed with CIN2+, 72.1% (129/179, 95% CI 65.5–77.7%) were detected by random biopsies, while the remaining cases were identified solely through ECC or conization. Within the high-risk screening group, among those diagnosed with CIN2+, 74.6% (85/114, 95% CI 65.2–81.9%) were diagnosed based on random biopsies. Of these high-risk patients, 92.1% (105/114) had incomplete SCJ visualization, and among those with CIN2+, 72.4% (76/105, 95% CI 62.1–80.7%) were detected via random biopsies. In the subgroup with fully visible SCJ (7.9%, 9/114), all CIN2 + cases were identified through random biopsies. Among patients in the low-risk group who were diagnosed with CIN2+, 67.7% (44/65, 95% CI 55.9–77.8%) were detected by random biopsies. In this group, 84.6% (55/65) had incomplete SCJ visualization, and among those with CIN2+, 63.6% (35/55, 95% CI 50.8–74.4%) were diagnosed via random biopsies. Furthermore, 90% (9/10, 95% CI 63.3–98.6%) of CIN2 + cases in the low-risk screening results group with fully visualized SCJ were detected by random biopsies, while only 10% (1/10, 95% CI 0.2–48.0%) of CIN2 cases were identified solely through conization. Importantly, no statistically significant difference was observed in the detection rates of CIN2 + between the two groups using random biopsies (74.6% vs. 67.7%, P =.325). 20.1% (36/179, 95% CI 14.2–26.8%) of CIN2 + cases were solely detected by ECC, all of these cases had incomplete SCJ visualization. 7.8% (14/179, 95% CI 4.6–13.3%) of CIN2 + cases were solely detected by conization, all of which were in patients above 40 years, postmenopausal, and with CIN I pathology in random biopsies.

Clinical characteristics comparison between normal/CIN1 and CIN2+

Table 4 illustrates that the mean age of the normal/CIN1 group is 47.5 years, which is significantly lower than the mean age of the CIN2 + group, averaging at 51 years; this discrepancy proved to be statistically significant (P =.011). The high-risk screening results group comprised 63.7% (114/179) of the CIN2 + patients, a notably higher proportion compared to the 36.3% (65/179) observed in the low-risk group (P <.001). No statistically significant disparities were identified in menstrual status, cervical transformation zone type, gravidity, and parity between the normal/CIN1 and CIN2 + groups. Table 5 presents a detailed breakdown of the CIN2 + detection rate across various screening risk stratifications and age groups, indicating a progressive rise in the detection rate of CIN2 + with increasing age. Specifically, the prevalence of CIN2 + escalated from 7.9% (5/63) in patients aged 20 to 30 years to 13.9% (59/423) in patients aged 50 years or older (trend, P =.002) among those with high-risk screening results, and from 2.6% (2/77) in patients aged 20 to 30 years to 5.1% (34/665) in patients aged 50 years or older (trend, P =.018) among those with low-risk screening results.

Discussion

The study found that among individuals with abnormal cervical screening results but completely normal colposcopic impressions, the prevalence of CIN2 + was 7.1% (179 out of 2515). Additionally, even among patients with fully visualized SCJ, CIN2 + could be diagnosed through random biopsies, albeit at a low detection rate. Out of the total, 76.6% cases were classified as normal, while 16.3% cases were diagnosed as CIN I. Additionally, seven cases of cervical cancer were identified, all involving postmenopausal patients aged over 45 with incompletely visualized SCJ. Among the 179 CIN2 + cases, 72.1% (129 out of 179) were confirmed via biopsy specimens, 20.1% (36 out of 179) were solely identified through ECC, and 7.8% (14 out of 179) were diagnosed via conization. The occurrence of CIN2 + was found to be correlated with advanced age and high-risk screening results.

The detection rate of CIN2 + among individuals with abnormal cervical cancer screening results and negative colposcopic impressions varies from findings in previous studies [7–11]. One contributing factor to this variation is the wide range of sample sizes across previous studies, spanning from 14 to 822 patients. In comparison, the sample size of the current study was substantially larger (n = 2515). The detection rate of CIN2 + among individuals with colposcopic nonvisible lesions in this study was higher than that reported in the ATHENA study [12], despite similar sample sizes and colposcopy referral criteria. This difference may be attributed to the fact that the ATHENA study only conducted one random biopsy when the colposcopic impression was normal, and biopsies were performed solely on patients with fully visualized SCJ. Additionally, pathology results from ECC were not considered, potentially leading to an underestimation of precancerous lesions in individuals with abnormal cervical cancer screening results and completely normal colposcopic impressions. Beyond procedure-related factors, population characteristics – particularly HPV vaccination coverage – may influence CIN2 + baseline prevalence and thus detection rates. While HPV vaccine types have not been found to significantly impact commonly recognized colposcopic features, they have been shown to reduce the incidence of HPV vaccine-type CIN2 + overall [13, 14]. Although specific data on HPV vaccination in our study were unavailable, the coverage of HPV vaccination among females in mainland China is low, ranging from 2.6% to 3.0% [15, 16]. The ASCCP colposcopy guideline [2] is applicable to populations with high vaccine coverage but may not be suitable for populations with low HPV vaccine coverage.

In this study, only two patients under 25 years old with low-risk cervical screening results were diagnosed with CIN 2. Considering that patients under 25 years old with CIN 2 often experience lesion regression and may be suitable for expectant management [17, 18], the necessity of random biopsies in individuals referred to colposcopy with low-risk screening results and completely normal colposcopic impressions may not impact the final management decision.

Cervical lesions missed by colposcopy are often associated with small lesions [19] and thinner CIN 2/3 [20]. Additionally, not fully visualized SCJ also poses a risk of missed diagnosis. In our study, 20.1% (95% CI: 14.2–26.8) of CIN2 + cases with completely normal colposcopic impressions were solely detected by ECC, all of which had inadequately visualized SCJ, suggesting the importance of ECC for patients with inadequately visualized SCJ. HPV genotype also correlated with colposcopic impression, though evidence was inconsistent. CIN2 + with HPV 16 positivity tended to exhibit abnormal colposcopic impressions, while lesions associated with carcinogenic types other than HPV 16 ere more easier to be missed [21]. Conversely, no difference was observed between the visual appearance of HPV16-related lesions and lesions associated with other HPV types in the study by van der Marel et al. [22]. In this study, the rate of HPV 16/18 infection was 30.4%, smaller than that of non-16/18 HPV genotype infections, despite HPV16 being the most common types in the same database [23]. This suggests that patients with HPV16 infections are prone to have abnormal colposcopic impressions. Similarly, high-grade cytology results accounted for only 9.2% of the included population, much lower than the 86.1% of low-grade or normal cytology results. Despite the lower prevalence, the detection rate of CIN2 + in high-risk screening test results is higher than in low-risk screening test results.

This study benefits from the largest reported cohort of patients with abnormal cervical screening yet completely normal colposcopic impressions, systematically evaluated via four-quadrant random biopsies—a significant methodological strength. However, its single-center retrospective design at a tertiary hospital in Southeast China may introduce regional selection bias. Furthermore, selective ECC application (restricted to HPV16/18+, HSIL + cytology, or non-fully visualized SCJ cases) risks underdiagnosing endocervical canal lesions, particularly in women with fully visualized SCJ where ECC was routinely omitted. These limitations necessitate cautious interpretation of generalizability.

Conclusion

For patients referred to colposcopy due to abnormal cervical cancer screening results, CIN2 + lesions, including cervical cancers, were still identified in a non-negligible proportion (7.1%) of individuals despite completely normal colposcopic impressions. Besides high-risk screening results, advanced age is a significant influencing factor. Random biopsies, along with ECC when the SCJ is not fully visualized, may be crucial for detection, particularly for postmenopausal patients. However, for patients with completely normal colposcopy findings and aged under 25 with low-risk cervical screening results, random biopsies and ECC may not be necessary.
