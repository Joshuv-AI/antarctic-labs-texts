Effective nutritional intervention improves the health-related quality of life (HRQL) of undernourished children with cancer: results of a randomized, controlled phase 3 trial
Authors: Maya Prasad, Savita Goswami, Girish Chinnaswamy, Elena J Ladas, Ronald D Barr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe association of nutritional intervention and health-related quality of life (HRQL) was examined in children with cancer.MethodsUndernourished children with cancer (N = 260) were randomized 1:1 to standard nutritional therapy (SNT) or SNT + Ready to Use Therapeutic Food (RUTF). HRQL was assessed using the Health Utilities Index® (HUI) at study entry and 6 weeks later. HUI has two systems, HUI2 and HUI3, with derived categories of disability. Scores after SNT and SNT + RUTF were compared.ResultsAt study entry there were no significant differences in HRQL scores between the two treatment arms. After 6 weeks of nutritional intervention, median overall HUI2 scores were comparable—0.90 with RUTF and 0.89 with SNT (p = 0.317)—but overall HUI3 scores were 0.90 and 0.79 (p = 0.009), respectively. At study entry and 6 weeks, 87 (35%) and 116 (46.6%) subjects had no or mild disability, and 162 (65%) and 133 (53.4%) had moderate or severe disability (p < 0.001) respectively using HUI2 scores. Corresponding scores with HUI3 were 66 (26.5%) and 103 (41.3%) for no or mild disability and 183 (73.5%) and 146 (58.6%) (p < 0.001) for moderate or severe disability at study entry and 6 weeks, respectively.Children with weight gain >10% had significant improvement in overall HUI2 (0.92 vs 0.84; p = 0.049) and HUI3 scores (0.88 vs 0.78; p = 0.010).ConclusionIn severely and moderately malnourished children with cancer, administration of RUTF and weight gain were associated with improved HRQL. Weight gain can improve HRQL in numerous domains and overall. Introduction

Children with cancer are at risk of poor health-related quality of life (HRQL) during and after treatment [1–7]. The major determinants of HRQL in children undergoing treatment for cancer include type, location, and stage of disease, intensity of therapy, presence of treatment-related symptoms and co-morbidities, and child and family sociodemographic factors (e.g., family cohesiveness, financial burden, etc.) [1–4]. Lifestyle factors, such as physical activity, are associated also with HRQL in survivors of cancer in childhood [5].

There is a considerable literature on HRQL in children with cancer, especially acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, which is the commonest form of malignant disease in this age group worldwide [8]. Studies of HRQL in children with cancer have been reported predominantly from high-income countries [1–4]. Common attributes (domains/dimensions) of health affected by a diagnosis of cancer in children are mobility/ambulation, emotion, cognition, and pain [9]. Undernutrition and weight loss are associated with lower HRQL scores on physical, emotional, and social functioning, while children with obesity may have lower scores on emotional and cognitive functioning [3]. As can be expected, treatment-related toxicities have been associated with worse HRQL [10].

Even in the high-income setting, there are few reports on the association of nutrition and HRQL in children with cancer [3]. There are no studies that have reported on the association of nutrition and HRQL in the low-income setting where the endemic prevalence of undernutrition is high and often accompanies children with newly diagnosed cancer. To address this gap, we examined the association of nutritional status and HRQL in children with cancer undergoing treatment in India. We also examined the impact of a nutritional intervention (ready-to-use-therapeutic-food; RUTF) and weight gain on changes in HRQL in undernourished children with cancer.

Patients and methods

This study is a randomized, controlled, phase 3, open-label trial conducted in the paediatric oncology outpatient clinics and wards of the Tata Memorial Centre (TMC) and Lokmanya Tilak Municipal General Hospital (LTMGH), Mumbai. Details of the study have been published previously [11]. Briefly, children aged 5–15 years with cancer, having either severe or moderate acute malnutrition (SAM or MAM, respectively), defined by body mass index (BMI) Z-scores less than −2 or mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC), for age less than the 5th percentile, were eligible. All enrolled participants provided written assent from children (older than 12 years) and written informed consent from parents or legal guardians.

Nutritional intervention

were randomized to receive either ready-to-use therapeutic food (RUTF) plus standard nutritional therapy (SNT), or SNT alone, stratified by age (5–9 years and 10–15 years) and diagnosis (acute lymphoblastic leukaemia, acute myeloid leukaemia, lymphoma, and solid tumours) Both groups were designed to receive diets with similar caloric content. The SNT arm received an energy- and protein-dense enriched home-based diet. In the RUTF arm, children were given ready-to-use therapeutic food (RUTF) to provide 50% of their total caloric needs, with the remaining calories supplied by SNT. After the 6-week intervention period, all children were advised to continue with SNT and could consume RUTF if they wished. Participants in both groups were monitored by physicians and study nutritionists over a period of 6 months: twice weekly during the first 2 weeks, weekly from weeks 3 to 8, and then monthly from the third to the sixth month. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of both centres, was registered in the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2015/06/005896), and underwent routine monitoring by the institutional Data Safety Monitoring Unit at TMC.

Health-related quality of life

HRQL was assessed using the proxy respondent version of 40-item Health Utilities Index® (HUI) interviewer-administered questionnaire at study entry and 6 weeks later [12, 13]. The questionnaire was administered in Hindi or Marathi by one of two trained clinical psychologists, who also did the scoring as per the HUI manual. The HUI is a family of generic, multi-attribute preference-based instruments for measuring comprehensive HRQL and has been validated in all people age 5 years and older. While the HUI is primarily validated for proxy reports in children under 12, it has been employed in older paediatric populations with appropriate adaptation [12, 13]. It uses both single- and multi-attribute utility scoring systems [12]. There are two systems—HUI2 and HUI3—which are independent of each other but complementary. HUI2 has six attributes: sensation, mobility, emotion, cognition, self-care, and pain, and HUI3 has eight: vision, hearing, speech, ambulation, dexterity, emotion, cognition, and pain. The constructs of emotion and cognition differ between HUI2 and HUI3. HUI2 emotion measures distress and anxiety while HUI3 emotion measures happiness versus depression. Similarly, HUI2 cognition measures learning whereas HUI3 cognition measures ability to solve day-to-day problems [12]. HUI3 is a more detailed descriptive system and generally the measure for primary analyses because it describes 972,000 unique health states compared to 24,000 with HUI2 [12]. Both HUI2 and HUI3 give utility scores for each attribute and also a multi-attribute utility score for overall HRQL (supplementary Table 1). The individual attributes and overall HRQL scores are calculated from multiplicative utility functions for both HUI2 and HUI3 on a scale from 0 to 1 (0.00 = dead; 1.00 = perfect health). HUI2 scores can range from −0.03 to 1.00 and HUI3 scores from −0.36 to 1.00. A negative score implies that the health status is worse than being dead. Overall HUI2 and HUI3 scores are generated by multiplicative, multi-attribute utility functions [12–14].

Categories of disability are assigned based on pre-defined overall HRQL scores, separately for HUI2 and HUI3. The categories of disability in each system are HUI2: none (1.00), mild (0.91 to 0.99), moderate (0.80 to 0.90), and severe (less than 0.80), and HUI3: none (1.00), mild (0.89 to 0.99), moderate (0.70 to 0.88), and severe (less than 0.70).

Statistical analysis

The demographic distribution of patients was characterized by median and interquartile range (IQR) or as percentages where appropriate. Categorical outcomes were compared using chi-square tests and differences in medians between two groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Between-group comparisons of disability scores were analysed using ANCOVA, with the baseline value as a covariate. A probability value of < 0.05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed utilizing IBM® SPSS software version 24.

To assess the impact of nutritional status and the study interventions on HRQL, inter-group temporal differences in individual attributes and overall scores in both HUI2 and HUI3 were compared. Differences in mean absolute values of 0.05 in each attribute and of 0.03 in overall HRQL scores were considered significant [12]. We also assessed the categories of overall disability at baseline and 6 weeks. The endpoint included the number of patients who had no or mild disability compared to the number who had moderate or severe disability.

Results

Study participants

A total of 260 subjects were enrolled between July 21, 2015, and March 21, 2018; 130 in each arm. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients are listed in Table 1. At the end of the 6-week intervention, 123 subjects in the RUTF arm and 126 in the SNT arm were analyzable for HRQL. At study entry, 214 (86%) of the entire cohort had SAM and 35 had MAM, distributed equally between the nutritional treatment arms. After 6 weeks, 98 (79.6%) children on the RUTF arm and 81 (64.2%) on SNT (p = 0.025) experienced weight gain and were reclassified with a healthy nutritional status.

Changes in HRQL from diagnosis to 6-weeks

At study entry there were no significant differences in overall HRQL scores between the two treatment arms; the median scores being 0.87 and 0.78 in HUI-2 and HUI-3, respectively, indicating moderate disability (Table 2). The lowest median values at study entry were emotion and pain, and HUI3 ambulation, emotion and pain (Table 2).

Analyses from study entry to 6 weeks revealed a significant improvement in HRQL scores in the study population as a whole, using both HUI2 and HUI3. Using HUI2 scores, at study entry, 87 (35%) had no or mild disability, and 162 (65%) had moderate or severe disability, and at 6 weeks 116 (46.6%) had no or mild disability, and 133 (53.4%) had moderate or severe disability (p = 0.01). When assessed by HUI3, at study entry, 66 (26.5%) had no or mild disability, and 183 (73.5%) had moderate or severe disability, and at 6 weeks 103 (41.3%) had no or mild disability, and 146 (58.6%) had moderate or severe disability (p < 0.01). As a group, mild disabilities tended to increase during treatment, whereas more severe disabilities decreased as measured by HUI2 and HUI3.

HRQL by treatment arm

At the end of the 6 weeks of nutritional intervention, median overall HUI2 scores were comparable in both arms, 0.90 in the RUTF arm and 0.89 in the control arm (p = 0.317).

However, improvement in the HUI2 pain score in children in the RUTF arm (median 1.00) vs the control arm (median 0.97, p = 0.036) was observed. Children on the RUTF arm had a significant improvement in the median overall HUI3 scores from baseline values compared to the control arm (0.90 vs 0.79; p = 0.009). Specifically, patients in the RUTF arm had significantly higher scores than controls in HUI-3 emotion (1.00 vs 0.95, p = 0.023) and HUI3 pain (1.00 vs 0.96, p = 0.023) but not in other domains (Fig. 1). None of the other individual HUI domains showed a significant improvement in absolute value from baseline to 6 weeks by treatment arm. Additionally, fewer subjects who received RUTF had moderate-severe disability using HUI3 (71, 57.7% vs 85, 67%, p = 0.002) than those on the control arm.

Discussion

This report on the changes in HRQL in undernourished children undergoing treatment for cancer in a low-middle income country (LMIC) setting addresses a significant gap in the data on HRQL and nutrition. We found that the attributes of health most commonly affected in our cohort of children were mobility, emotion, cognition and pain. It is important to highlight that our study measured weight gain among children with undernutrition and becoming a healthy weight as treatment was initiated. In contrast to the HIC setting, we did not observe excessive increases in weight resulting in children developing overweight or obesity during this time, so our results must be interpreted considering this important distinguishing feature. The significant impact of cancer on HRQL is reported in the literature from both HICs and LMICs [1–4, 15–18].

Notably, in our cohort, the lowest HRQL scores at study entry were in the same attributes with similar levels of disability (moderate) in the two arms, suggesting that randomization was effective at obtaining balance between the two arms. Following 6 weeks of nutritional intervention, there was weight gain in 71% of children (more in the RUTF arm) [11]. There was also overall improvement in HRQL and reduction in disability in both arms, with significantly greater benefits (in the form of HUI2 emotion, self-care and pain; HUI3 emotion and pain; and overall HUI3) to children in the RUTF arm. Similar gains in HRQL with less disability were seen in children who gained more than 10% of body weight in the 6-week study interval, viz. overall HUI2 and HUI3 scores; HUI2 mobility, emotion, and self-care; and HUI3 ambulation, pain, and emotion. Weight gain and improved nutrition would be expected to improve the HRQL domains of emotion, ambulation, and mobility. There is increasing evidence that undernutrition, poor dietary intake, and unhealthy dietary behaviours may be involved in the pathogenesis and progression of chronic pain and that nutritional interventions may play a role in chronic pain management [19].

The only other study, performed in a HIC, which examined the effect of nutritional status on HRQL in children with cancer, reported that undernourished children had significantly lower scores in physical and social functioning, as well as cancer summary scale and nausea, compared to well-nourished children [3]. Weight loss of > 5% was associated with lower scores on patient-reported physical, emotional, and social functioning and parent-reported pain and nausea [3]. While that study reported worse social functioning and more pain in children with weight gain, the authors mention that weight gain was more common in already overnourished children, unlike our cohort of undernourished children where weight gain led to a healthy weight. Notably, that study used the child- and parent-report versions of the PedsQL 4.0 Generic scale and the PedsQL 3.0 Cancer Module, not the HUI, and only 10% of children were undernourished at diagnosis, making direct comparison with our study results difficult [3].

In the previous report of our study, there was less treatment-related toxicity from chemotherapy and fewer treatment delays in children whose nutritional status improved in the 6-week interval, irrespective of the nutritional intervention arm [11]. Not only is this significant from a clinical perspective, there are definitive implications on the cost of care. The average cost of six weeks of standard nutritional care at our centre is INR 30,000. Six weeks of RUTF costs an additional INR 2,500. Hence, RUTF added an additional 10% to standard nutritional care, only a fraction of the cost of cancer treatment. While we have not conducted either a formal cost analysis or of Disability-Adjusted Life-Years, and despite higher direct costs, the use of RUTF or similarly effective nutritional interventions should be considered in view of the decrease in treatment-related toxicity and improvement in HRQL.

The strengths of our study are the formal randomized-controlled design utilizing a validated measure of HRQL in children with cancer [12, 13]. In contrast to previous studies, nutritional status and intervention were determined prospectively, overcoming the limitations of retrospective studies. Moreover, our conduct of this study in the LMIC setting establishes a framework to build upon for future HRQL studies in similar settings. The limitations of this study include the use of a proxy-respondent questionnaire from which children’s and parents’ perceptions could not be separately assessed. In our study, caregivers completed the HUI questionnaires, acknowledging the possible differences between proxy and self-reports.

Moreover, this study included the addition of ready-to-use therapeutic food (RUTF) to standard nutritional therapy in the experimental arm rather than a side-to-side comparison.

In conclusion, the results of this study provide guidance for future studies of similar design which should include long-term follow-up to determine the sustainability of the gains in nutritional status, clinical outcomes and HRQL which are achievable with nutritional and possibly exercise interventions in malnourished children with cancer. Such studies would be especially relevant in low- and middle-income countries where malnutrition in children with cancer is prevalent [20].
