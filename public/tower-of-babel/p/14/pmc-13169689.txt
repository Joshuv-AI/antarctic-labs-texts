Single state agency licensure requirements for substance use disorder treatment programs in the United States: results of a national survey
Authors: Brooks Yelton, Melissa A Westlake, Amanda J Abraham, Colleen M Grogan, Olivia Hinds, Christina M Andrews
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubstance use disorder (SUD) remains a critical public health crisis in the United States, with high prevalence and overdose mortality rates. Despite the availability of effective treatments, including medications that significantly reduce overdose risk, fewer than 20% of Americans with active SUD receive treatment annually. Single State Agencies (SSAs), which oversee federal funding and regulate SUD treatment programs, hold significant authority to impose licensure and reporting requirements intended to promote treatment quality.MethodsThis study presents findings from a national survey of SSAs across 50 states and the District of Columbia (94% response rate) conducted between 2020 and 2021, examining their roles in licensing SUD programs and setting requirements to promote evidence-based clinical practice.ResultsResults reveal that 58.3% of SSAs were responsible for licensing SUD treatment programs, with most requiring reporting primarily from publicly funded programs rather than all licensed providers. Staffing and clinical practice requirements varied substantially across treatment settings: opioid treatment programs and inpatient withdrawal management services were most frequently subject to medical staffing and supervision mandates, while outpatient programs were less regulated in these respects. Across treatment settings, treatment and aftercare/discharge planning were commonly required.ConclusionsThe study findings indicate that agency requirements in many states allow for SUD treatment practices and staffing arrangements that are inconsistent with evidence-based care. Background

Substance use disorder (SUD) and related mortality remains an urgent public health crisis in the United States. In 2024, roughly one in six Americans (16.8%) aged 12 or older met criteria for SUD in the past year [1], and over 80,000 died from drug-related overdoses [2]. Yet, fewer than 20% of Americans with an active SUD received any treatment for their condition in the prior year [1]. Among those who receive SUD treatment, the care provided is highly variable. Many SUD treatment programs do not provide medication for treatment of opioid use disorder [3] despite evidence it reduces risk of overdose death by as much as 50% [4]. Moreover, threats to quality resulting from inadequate staffing, absence of clinical supervision, and treatment practices lacking concordance with clinical guidance have been extensively documented in the SUD treatment field [5–7].

Single State Agencies (SSAs)—government entities charged with overseeing federal funds for SUD treatment—possess powers to regulate the provision of SUD treatment. SSAs have authority to require comprehensive reporting on SUD treatment programmatic activities and outcomes and can withhold state and federal block grant funding from programs not in compliance with state requirements. Many SSAs are also responsible for licensing SUD treatment programs, including those not receiving block grant funds, and can require compliance with specified standards as a condition of licensure. However, it remains unclear what steps states have taken to use these powers to promote SUD treatment quality by setting related requirements. In this study, we present the results of a national survey of SSAs to better understand their role in setting requirements to promote the quality of SUD treatment.

Methods

We conducted an internet-based survey of SSAs in all 50 states and the District of Columbia (DC) from September 2020 to April 2022 with assistance from the University of Chicago Survey Lab. SSA directors’ names and contact information were identified through a directory issued by the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration [8]. If we were unable to contact the SSA director after follow-up, additional contact efforts were made with SSA staff members through emails and telephone calls. In total, 47 states plus DC responded (94% response rate). No data were received from Florida, Maine, or New Mexico.

SSAs were asked whether they required reporting of data on services rendered. If answered affirmatively, SSAs were asked to indicate whether reporting was required for all licensed SUD treatment programs or just those receiving public funding. Additionally, we asked whether SSAs were responsible for licensing SUD treatment programs in their state in 2020. SSAs responsible for SUD treatment licensure were also asked whether they imposed any of the following requirements as a condition of licensure: staff requirements for medical directors, SUD treatment medication prescribers, and clinical supervisors; staff-client ratios; patient placement criteria; clinical assessment; and use of treatment plans and aftercare/discharge plans. These licensure requirements were selected as program staffing and professionalization have been linked to higher rates of SUD medication prescribing [9, 10] and better treatment outcomes [4]. In addition, routine clinical assessment and treatment planning improve patient engagement and retention, which are linked to better outcomes in SUD treatment [11]. All measures were adapted from a survey of state agency requirements conducted by the National Association of State Alcohol and Drug Agency Directors [12]. A full list of the survey questions utilized in this study can be found in an additional file (see Additional file1).

To examine state agencies’ approaches to promote quality, we assessed whether SSAs had requirements for each of the following SUD-related services based on the continuum of care established by the American Society of Addiction Medicine: outpatient, intensive outpatient, outpatient withdrawal management, recovery support, opioid treatment programs, short- and long-term residential, inpatient, and inpatient withdrawal management [13]. Among SSAs that indicated they were responsible for licensing SUD treatment programs, we calculated the percentage of SSAs that imposed each requirement for each treatment setting. Our calculations for each requirement excluded states with missing data.

Results

Most required at least some SUD treatment programs in their state to report information on their activities. Data reporting was more often required only for state- and federally-funded treatment programs (68.8%, n = 33) than for all licensed programs (31.3%, n = 15) (see Fig. 1). This was true regardless of whether or not the SSA was responsible for program licensure.

Single state Agency responsibility for substance use disorder (SUD) treatment program licensing and data reporting requirements by state, 2020

SSAs were responsible for licensing of SUD treatment programs in 58.3% (n = 28) of the states in our sample (see Fig. 1). In states in which SSAs were not responsible, these activities were typically handled by their state’s primary health and human services agency or an affiliated office (e.g., Department of Public Health, Department of Community Health). Other responsible agencies included the Office of Inspector General in Kentucky and West Virginia, the Board of Alcohol/Drug Counselor Certification in Idaho, the Department of Licensing and Regulatory Affairs in Michigan, and the state licensing board in Wyoming.

SSAs responsible for licensing SUD treatment programs for which we gathered complete data (n = 27) most commonly reported responsibility for licensing outpatient services, intensive outpatient services, opioid treatment programs, inpatient withdrawal management, and short-term residential services (each 96.3%), followed by long-term residential and outpatient withdrawal management programs (each 92.6%). Almost 78% of SSAs licensed inpatient treatment, and only 40.7% licensed recovery support services. Twenty-six SSAs (96.3%) licensed at least seven of the nine service types, and six SSAs (22.2%) licensed all nine types of care. One state (California) licensed only four of the nine types of care.

Among states with complete data (n = 17), about 42% required a medical director or prescriber on staff across treatment settings, ranging from zero to 16 states per setting. Only four SSAs (23.5%) required either a medical director or weekly prescriber to be staffed by outpatient and intensive outpatient treatment programs (Fig. 2). SSAs were more likely to require short-term and long-term residential treatment programs to employ a medical director or weekly prescriber, with half of SSAs requiring one or both (medical director or weekly prescriber) for short-term residential treatment and 43.8% requiring one or both for long-term residential treatment. Most SSAs required a medical director or weekly prescriber in inpatient settings—82.4% required one or both in inpatient withdrawal management and 69.2% in other inpatient programs. SSAs were most likely to have such staffing requirements in opioid treatment programs: around 65% required a prescribing professional on site at least weekly, while a medical director was required on site by 94.1% of SSAs; neither was required by 5.9% of SSAs.

Percentage of Single state agencies requiring medication prescribing, staffing, patient assessment and treatment licensure requirements by service type, 2020

Requirements for clinical supervision of counseling staff were more common (see Fig. 2). The proportion of SSAs requiring clinical supervision was greatest for outpatient (76.5%) and inpatient (76.9%) services, and lowest for outpatient withdrawal management (64.7%) and recovery support services (42.9%).

Relatively few SSAs required minimum staffing ratios (Fig. 2). However, requirements varied significantly by type of service. Minimum staffing ratios were most common in higher-intensity settings. SSAs required them in 53.8% of inpatient settings, 50% of short- and long-term residential settings, and 41.2% of inpatient withdrawal management programs. Minimum staffing ratios were less common in outpatient settings, with SSAs least likely to require them for recovery support services (14.3%), outpatient withdrawal management and intensive outpatient (17.6%), and outpatient (23.5%).

Few differences were found among SSAs with regard to requirements for use of patient placement criteria and clinical assessment (Fig. 2). Nearly all SSAs required use of some form of patient placement criteria. Clinical assessment requirements were also required by nearly all SSAs for most services. However, fewer SSAs required assessment for recovery support services (57.1%), outpatient withdrawal management services (82.4%), and outpatient and inpatient services (88.2%).

Treatment plans and aftercare/discharge plans were required by the majority of SSAs overall, with equal application of both requirements in intensive outpatient (88.2%), outpatient (94.1%), opioid treatment programs (94.1%), short- and long-term residential (93.8%), inpatient (92.3%), and inpatient withdrawal management services (94.1%) (Fig. 2). Treatment plans were required for 82.4% of SSAs for outpatient withdrawal management and by 71.4% for recovery support services; aftercare/discharge plans were required by 88.2% and 42.9% of SSAs respectively.

Discussion

We found less than one-third of SSAs required data reporting by all SUD treatment programs operating in the state; the rest required reporting only from programs receiving state funding. Limited data reporting requirements impede transparency and quality monitoring, particularly if all licensed treatment programs are not required to report their program data. Stakeholders cannot evaluate the effectiveness of agency requirements intended to drive quality if data on the providers to which those requirements do (and do not) apply cannot be assessed. Moreover, a lack of data makes efforts to enforce requirements especially complicated.

Our results indicate that many SSAs responsible for licensing SUD treatment programs did not impose requirements related to staffing and clinical practice across several treatment settings. Such requirements were most frequently applied to opioid treatment programs and inpatient withdrawal management services. This is not surprising, given that opioid treatment programs are heavily regulated by the Drug Enforcement Agency and must meet requirements for medical supervision of methadone dispensation. Moreover, drug withdrawal includes serious medical risks that require professional supervision in controlled settings to manage safely. Conversely, services delivered in outpatient settings were subject to staffing and supervision requirements far less frequently.

We also found few states required the inclusion of a medical professional on staff in any capacity. Given SUD treatment programs cannot prescribe without a medical professional on staff, the absence of requirements to ensure their presence is highly consequential. This is particularly critical for management of opioid use disorder, as medication substantially reduces use of opioids as well as risk of mortality [14, 15]. Requiring medical staff in some capacity across SUD treatment settings has the potential to greatly expand access to these lifesaving medications.

Our findings also suggest relatively few states set requirements for minimum staffing ratios. Minimum staffing ratios, which are common in many other areas of health care [16, 17], help to ensure staff have time to appropriately treat and monitor their patients. When understaffing results in poor availability of and oversight by SUD treatment providers, quality suffers. While minimum staffing ratios may not be as important in outpatient settings, they are very important in residential and inpatient settings, where patients reside in an institution 24 hours a day and require adequate support to meet their health care needs.

Of the states included in our analysis, only half required minimum staffing ratios in residential settings and just over half (53%) required them for inpatient treatment programs. While appropriate staffing ratios vary greatly depending on urbanicity, facility size, and the number of treatment services and medications offered [18], there is limited guidance at the national level on what these ratios should be. Federal law previously required patient caps for buprenorphine prescribing, but such limits were removed under the Consolidated Appropriations Act of 2023. Instead, much discretion is left to the state.

Finally, while the vast majority of states required SUD treatment programs to conduct patient assessments guided by formal patient placement criteria, a sizable proportion of states did not require any clinical supervision for staff who assess, place, and treat patients. While in some cases lack of a requirement for clinical supervision is quite reasonable—for example, peer recovery services are not by definition a formal clinical intervention—in most settings, clinical supervision is an important part of ensuring quality of care. It is possible that SSAs may not require clinical supervision because states often contract with external licensing and credentialing boards, which already require clinical supervision requirements for SUD treatment provider certification. However, regulation at the professional level rather than the programmatic level allows SUD treatment programs more leeway to hire individuals who are not subject to licensing credentials that are inconsistent with commonly recognized clinical standards for the treatment of SUD.

There are several limitations to this study. First, our study analyses did not include states that do not license SUD treatment through the state SSA. However, as a majority of states rely on SSAs to license SUD treatment, providing evidence regarding the requirements they impose is nonetheless important. Future research should assess the full range of state and federal regulations, in addition to SSA program licensure, in order to best understand how quality-related requirements are applied to a broader range of SUD treatment settings. Relatedly, it is important to note, that other state- and federal-level entities such as the Joint Commission, which accredits hospital, or other state-level provider licensing and credentialing boards may simultaneously also impose requirements that influence the quality of SUD treatment delivered. Therefore, it is important that these results are interpreted as the requirements established by SSAs to receive program licensure but do not necessarily guarantee the licensed programs do not meet these treatment quality requirements.

Second, while our study measures included a broad range of requirements commonly associated with SUD treatment quality, they are not all encompassing. Third, we excluded states for which there was missing data for the requirements examined. Fourth, it is possible some treatment programs may be subject to additional requirements imposed by other state agencies which may not be included in our analysis; however, we included this information if noted by SSAs in the survey. Fifth, the nature of staffing and practice requirements may differ across treatment settings as a result of the unique features of the setting. Lastly, our study relied on self-reported data regarding licensure requirements gathered by a survey of Single State Agencies. It is possible that some statutes or regulations exist that agency representatives who completed the survey did not report. A supplementary investigation into state law could help fill this gap.

Conclusions

Given SSAs have substantial powers to set and enforce requirements aimed at improving quality of SUD treatment through conditions for funding appropriation and licensure, states may be missing opportunities to leverage licensure requirements promoting higher standards of care and better alignment with evidence-based SUD treatment practice. That said, it is important to note some SSAs may have a desire to raise standards but cannot do so without compromising access to care. Because some programs may not have the ability to meet certain requirements due to limitations in staffing or infrastructure, imposing higher standards may force them to lose licensure altogether. Thus, it may be necessary to provide resources and technical assistance to help programs meet requirements aimed at promoting quality as they are established.

Nonetheless, raising standards through licensing requirements may be necessary. SUD treatment programs have been encouraged for over a decade to provide medication for SUD. Given so many still do not do so, it may be necessary to move beyond training and incentives by setting requirements for practice consistent with the evidence.
