Nonlipogenic ABCA1 Inducers (NLAI) for Alzheimer’s Disease Validated in a Mouse Model Expressing Human APOE3/APOE4
Authors: Ganga
Reddy Velma, Megan S. Laham, Cutler Lewandowski, Ana C. Valencia-Olvera, Deebika Balu, Annabelle Moore, Martha Ackerman-Berrier, Pavel Rychetsky, Christopher Penton, Soumya Reddy Musku, Anandhan Annadurai, Maha Ibrahim Sulaiman, Nina Ma, Gregory R J Thatcher
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Therapeutics enhancing apolipoprotein (APOE) positive function are a priority, because APOE4 is the major genetic risk factor for Alzheimer’s disease (AD). The function of APOE, the key constituent of lipoprotein particles that transport cholesterol and lipids in the brain, is dependent on lipidation by ABCA1, a cell-membrane cholesterol transporter. ABCA1 transcription is regulated by liver X receptors (LXR): agonists have been shown to increase ABCA1, often accompanied by unwanted lipogenesis and elevated triglycerides (TG). Therefore, nonlipogenic ABCA1-inducers (NLAI) are needed. Two rounds of optimization of an HTS hit, derived from a phenotypic screen, gave lead compound 39 that was validated and tested in E3/4FAD mice that express human APOE3/4 and five mutant APP and PSEN1 human transgenes. Treatment with 39 increased ABCA1 expression, enhanced APOE lipidation, and reversed multiple AD phenotypes, without increasing TG. This NLAI/LXR-agonist study is the first in a human APOE-expressing model with hallmark amyloid-β pathology.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a degenerative brain disease that with aging populations worldwide is placing an increasing burden on society, affecting millions of people. AD is one of a spectrum of neurodegenerative diseases sometimes termed Alzheimer’s disease and related dementia (ADRD). AD is the most common cause of dementia leading to a decline in memory, language, problem-solving, and other cognitive skills, leaving a person unable to sustain daily functions. AD is defined by the hallmark pathology of amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles and a pattern of cognitive deficits in learning and memory. However, the majority of patients with a confirmed diagnosis of AD, at death, bear other neuropathologies, such as Lewy Bodies, TDP-43 inclusions, microinfarcts associated with vascular dementia, and cerebral amyloid angiopathy.1,2 A major research and clinical goal is to validate biomarkers for subsets of AD with the hope that therapeutics can be tailored to specific patients.

The pathogenesis of ADRD is multifaceted with no clear path to pharmacotherapy outside of hallmark neuropathology. The clinical trial landscape has been dominated by antiamyloid therapies for decades, the success of which has been problematic. Extremely limited treatment options are currently available for patients with AD: acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, and galantamine); an NMDA receptor antagonist (memantine); a memantine-donepezil combination pill; and recently approved antiamyloid antibodies, notably aducanumab. The first five of those treatments may provide temporary, symptomatic relief. Aducanumab is marketed as disease-modifying and received FDA approval because of proven ability to reduce Aβ neuropathology, despite minimal cognitive benefit.3 The need for AD therapies remains high and for ADRD as a whole, the need is unmet.

The major protein constituent of lipoprotein particles that carry cholesterol and other lipids in the brain is apolipoprotein E (APOE). APOE is the dominant genetic risk factor for AD. Expression of one or two copies of the APOE4 allele increases AD risk by 2.8- or 8.0-fold, respectively, marking APOE function as a target for AD therapeutics.4 APOE4 exacerbates many pathologic aspects of sporadic AD, from amyloid and tau pathology to inflammation and blood-brain barrier function.5 APOE is involved in lipid metabolism and trafficking. In the central nervous system (CNS), APOE is required for the formation of lipoprotein particles that transport cholesterol and phospholipids. Three human APOE isoforms exist, E2, E3, and E4, differing structurally by just two residues yet functioning quite differently in the CNS.6 More specifically, APOE4-containing lipoproteins carry less cholesterol, disrupt cholesterol homeostasis, and are susceptible to proteolytic degradation leading to the generation of neurotoxic fragments.7 Another key component to cholesterol homeostasis in the brain is the ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1). ABCA1 is required to add cholesterol and lipids to lipid-poor apolipoproteins, and APOE receives cholesterol and phospholipids via ABCA1.8 Lack of ABCA1 leads to diminished ability for cells to efflux lipids.9 Therefore, increasing expression of ABCA1 is hypothesized to restore perturbed lipid transport associated with AD and to enhance lipidation and stabilization of APOE4,10−12 while also attenuating inflammatory signaling, promoting vascular health, and clearing toxic Aβ species.13,14 Most recently, the effect of ABCA1 upregulation was reported in a mouse model of tauopathy, impacting tau pathology, inflammation, cognition, behavior, and neurodegeneration in a manner correlated with expression of human APOE.15

ABCA1 transcription is controlled by nuclear hormone receptors (NHRs). The liver X receptor (LXR), a member of the NHR family, is the primary receptor that controls ABCA1 expression. Another gene expressed by LXR is sterol-response element binding protein 1c (SREBP1c) which is associated with lipogenesis and triglyceride (TG) synthesis. ABCA1 upregulation targets APOE4 function and cholesterol homeostasis; however, induction of SREBP1c can cause unwanted adverse effects associated with elevated levels of TG, steatohepatitis, and hepatomegaly.7 Different isoforms of LXR have been linked to the expression of different genes: LXRα with SREBP1c and LXRβ with ABCA1.8 Developing an agonist that preferentially binds and activates LXRβ has been a longstanding approach to avoid adverse effects; however, selective LXRβ agonists have yet to be successfully translated to the clinic.16,17

We and a handful of other groups have employed novel, robust techniques to overcome the shortcomings of receptor binding assays to identify and develop small molecules that selectively enhance ABCA1 expression without TG-related side effects. We previously described a high-throughput screening (HTS) campaign to identify selective ABCA1 inducers that avoided TG production in vitro and in vivo.18 We optimized an HTS hit to yield an early lead compound that showed efficacy at reversing diabetic and lipid-related phenotypes in a high-fat diet (HFD), obesogenic mouse model of type-2 diabetes (T2D).19 The link between T2D and AD has been demonstrated,20 with numerous studies showing that insulin resistance worsens cognition and AD pathology in both humans and animals,21 an effect that is potentiated by APOE4.22−24

Herein, we describe the optimization and development of nonlipogenic ABCA1 inducers (NLAIs). The lead compound obtained, 39, has improved potency for ABCA1 induction and retained cellular selectivity for upregulation of ABCA1 vs lipogenic genes. Compound 39 was further profiled for LXR activation and compared to other reported LXR agonists, both in binding to LXR and other related NHRs. Based upon the activity profile of 39 and on previous studies with an earlier lead, 6, we moved this compound forward to testing in a mouse model of AD that expresses human APOE3 and APOE4 on a background of extensive Aβ neuropathology caused by five mutant human transgenes associated with familial AD (FAD).

Although the prevalence of APOE alleles in study populations varies with sex, race and other characteristics, a recent study provides a measure of prevalence across the continuum of cognitively sound to subjectively memory impaired (SMI), to mild cognitively impaired (MCI), and AD.25 The frequency of APOE4 was 9%, 18%, 20%, and 31%. respectively. Importantly, in this study the frequency in the AD group of APOE3/4 and APOE4/4 carriers was 39% and 11%, respectively. In several studies, gene expression in APOE3/4 brains of AD decedents resembles APOE4/4 rather than APOE3/3 carriers.26 Therefore, FAD-Tg mice carrying APOE3/4 (E3/4FAD mice)27 were selected for this pilot study as highly representative of the target human population.

In E3/4FAD mice expressing mutations in APP and PSEN1 and heterozygous for human APOE isoforms, the lead NLAI, 39, increased ABCA1 expression, enhanced APOE lipidation, and reversed multiple AD phenotypes. Importantly, in treatment regimens up to four months, lipid-related side effects such as increased plasma TG and hepatomegaly were not observed. This study represents the first report of an NLAI with in vivo efficacy and selectivity in a mouse model expressing human APOE and AD-hallmark Aβ pathology.

Results and Discussion

Phase 1: Hit to Early Lead NLAI Optimization

The first phase of chemical modifications to generate analogues of the HTS hit, 1, led to early lead 6 (Figure 1). Modifications were made with the initial goal of exploring replacement of the metabolically labile ester group. Each synthesized analogue was screened at three concentrations in an ABCA1-luc reporter assay using astrocytoma CCF-STTG1 cells, stably transfected with an ABCA1-promoter linked to a luciferase response element.18 A cutoff of >1.5-fold increase in luciferase signal was used for analogues to progress to a full 11-point dose–response to determine EC50. Compounds with ≤1.5-fold increase in luciferase signal for 3-point curves were not further tested: nearly 20 analogues demonstrated either dramatically reduced or no activity in the ABCA1-luc assay (Table S2). While the methyl ester, 2, maintained activity (Table 1), the t-butyl ester, 12, was inactive (Table S2). Of note, the carboxylic acid derivative of the ester (9) was inactive as well. Replacement of the ester group with a wide selection of ester and carboxylic acid bioisosteres, including amides (14, 20, 21, and 22), boronate (15), and tetrazole (17) (Figure 1A) was not tolerated, leading to inferior or no activity (Table S2).

Modifications beyond the benzoate functionality were more successful. Notably, repositioning the ethoxy group to the meta position of the N-phenyl sulfonamide moiety (3), or lengthening the alkyl chain (4), maintained or slightly improved potency and/or efficacy (Table 1). Other Phase 1 modifications to the phenyl sulfonamide (e.g., 23, 28, 29, 30) led to loss of activity (Figure 1A; Table S2) showing that truncation, elongation, and substitution of the ethoxy substituent were detrimental to activity. Addition of a second ethoxy group was a logical progression, which led to both improved potency and efficacy for 5 compared to hit compound 1 (Table 1).

Another potential metabolic soft spot, in hit 1, is the exposed thiophene ring. Addition of a methyl group at the 4-position of the thiophene ring (27, Table S1) abolished, but the 5-chloro modification (7, Table 1, Figure 1A) enhanced activity. Advantageous Phase 1 modifications were combined to generate early lead compound 6 with an EC50 of 817 nM, representing a 3-fold leftshift in potency, for ABCA1 induction, compared to the original hit, (Figure 1; Table 1).

Phase 2: SAR and Further Lead Optimization

The second phase of optimization based upon early lead 6 explored the same regions of hit 1 investigated in Phase 1, with the exception of the 2-Cl-thiophene that was retained throughout (Figure 1A). Again, only compounds with improved ABCA1 induction were pursued for full concentration–response, leaving 24 compounds that failed to meet criteria (Table S4). Further attempts to replace the phenyl ester functionality led to more success than in Phase 1, with several compounds meeting criteria: sulfone (31) and ketal (46) induced ABCA1-luc with EC50 of 12.6 and 2.0 μM, respectively (Figure 1A, Table 2). Measured potency did not improve on 6 and in two cases was inferior to the original hit. Incorporating cyclic anilide ring systems (e.g., 63 and 65) was not tolerated, nor was a bicyclic benzimidazole (69), benzothiazole (70), or pyridine (61) (Figure 1A; Table S4). The only replacement that met criteria, but with low potency (3 μM), was 47 containing an oxadiazole ring (Figure 1A; Table 2).

Further modifications to the benzoate moiety retained the ethyl ester to explore substitution and derivatization of the benzene ring (Figure 1A; purple). With some exceptions (52, 56; Table S4), substitutions at the meta position were successful (Figure 1A). In particular, a 3-Cl or 3-F substitution led to improvements of EC50 < 500 nM: 37 (247 nM); 41 (339 nM); 42 (66 nM); 43 (138 nM); 44 (29 nM) (Table 2).

Several replacements and benzene substitutions of the N-phenyl sulfonamide were explored (Figure 1A; yellow). N-containing heterocycles (54, 55, 58, 53, 59) were not tolerated. Combinations of ether substituents were tested. Although it was rational to expect acetal and ketal derivatives (48 and 49) to be successful, these compounds failed (Figure 1A). Rotational flexibility is clearly important for activity in ether substituents. On a more fruitful note, shortening the 3-ethoxy group to a 3-methoxy group (32) further enhanced ABCA1-luc potency (Table 2). Successful modifications were combined in lead 39, producing an increase in maximal efficacy (from 2.3× to 3.5×) and a 25-fold improvement in EC50 (2.5 μM to 100 nM) in ABCA1-luc assay compared to 1. The dose–response for ABCA1-luc potency demonstrates this significant upward and left shift (Figure 1B).

Counterscreen Reporter Assays for SREBP1c Activation

Our search for NLAIs was originally driven by ABCA1 induction assayed in the primary screen used for HTS, measuring the response of a luciferase reporter in human astrocytoma cells to mimic the response in human astrocytes. As a counterscreen we employed a SREBP1c-luciferase reporter (linked to the SREBF1 gene promoter) in human hepatocarcinoma HepG2 cells.18 Unwanted lipogenesis is mediated by LXR agonists via direct transcriptional activation and through upregulation of SREBP1c;28 therefore induction of the gene encoding SREBP1c was used as a model to counterscreen for lipogenesis in hepatocytes.

SREBP1a and SREBP1c are derived from the same gene via the use of alternate transcription start sites, which yields alternate forms of exon 1.29 SREBP1a induces all SREBP-responsive genes mediating synthesis of cholesterol, fatty acids, and TG. SREBP1c enhances transcription of genes required for synthesis of fatty acids and TG, but not cholesterol. SREBP1c is the predominant isoform in liver and therefore is associated most closely with lipogenesis and TG elevation.29 SREBP1 isoforms are inactive and ER-resident, requiring transport by SREBP cleavage-activating protein (SCAP) to the Golgi membrane where sequential proteolysis by S1P and S2P leads to release of the active/mature SREBP1 transcription factors that bind to sterol response element (SRE) inducing gene transcription.

SCAP is both a chaperone and sterol sensor that is inhibited by binding of endogenous sterols and their xenobiotic analogues.30 This feedback mechanism regulates cholesterol, fatty acid, and TG biosynthesis in response to hepatic cholesterol status. Other regulatory mediators of SREBP1 transcriptional activity include lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3). LPCAT3 expression correlates with SREBP1c activation. In vivo studies on 6 revealed reduced hepatic LPCAT3, attenuated TG levels and other related changes in fatty acids.19 Thus, while the SREBP1c-luc assay was useful as a counterscreen for HTS and initial hit selection, we transitioned to a more relevant assay to model lipogenesis in hepatocytes using HepG2 cells stably transfected with a luciferase reporter downstream of a SRE promoter (SRE-luc). The original hit showed no activity in this counter screen. As the potency increased in the ABCA1-luc assay from hit 1 to the first lead compound, 6, we observed activity in the SRE-luc assay (Table 1). This trend continued as more potent compounds were developed such as 39. Among this series, only one compound (46) showed greater potency for SRE-luc activation (Table 2). Noticeably, the new NLAI candidates retained selectivity for ABCA1 induction versus SRE activation. The disconnect between the NLAI activity of 6 in vivo versus effects on lipogenic genes in cultured cells,19 echoes previous observations on LXR agonists.31

Coregulator TR-FRET (CRT) for NHRs

Binding of ligands to the two isoforms of LXR (LXRα and LXRβ) induces a conformational change in LXR allowing recruitment of coregulators, which mimics the physiological function of such ligands. A coregulator time-resolved fluorescence energy transfer (CRT) assay, thus measures binding and coregulator recruitment. This biochemical assay incorporates a GST-tagged LXR ligand binding domain (LBD), anti-GST-terbium, biotin-SRC-1, and streptavidin-XL665. LXR agonist binding induces recruitment of coactivator SRC-1 (steroid receptor coactivator 1; also known as nuclear receptor coactivator 1, NCOA1). When excited at 340 nm the terbium will emit at 620 nm exciting XL665 and emitting a signal reflecting coregulator binding to LXR. The assay measures the EC50 for coactivator recruitment, a measure of ligand binding affinity, and the strength of signal reflects maximal efficacy. For sterol receptors, the endogenous ligands rarely induce efficacy comparable to synthetic ligands. Thus, for LXR, T0901317 (T0) serves as a suitable control (Figure 2)

NLAI binding to LXR and coregulator recruitment. Concentration–response for recruitment of SRC-1 to LXRα (dotted lines) and LXRβ (solid line), determined by CRT assay, showing mean and SD, normalized to T0 as a full LXR agonist.

LXRβ is almost ubiquitously expressed, whereas LXRα has more limited tissue expression, most notably in the liver. Many groups have strived to obtain an LXRβ selective agonist to avoid unwanted side effects that have been ascribed to agonism of LXRα, on the basis that activation of LXRα in the liver, drives lipogenesis. Selectivity has most commonly been pursued based on binding potency (EC50); however, selectivity has also been claimed on the basis of efficacy.32 Thus, 39 shows selectivity for LXRβ based upon both potency and efficacy; whereas, T0 is not selective (Figure 2).

Although LXR binding affinity and isoform selectivity were not used for HTS, nor to drive NLAI optimization, coregulator recruitment and isoform selectivity of our NLAIs were of interest. In the LXR-CRT assay, NLAIs showed at most a two-3-fold selectivity for binding to LXRβ over LXRα and consequent coactivator recruitment (Tables 1,2). Hit 1 and early Phase 1 analogues had affinity for LXR too low to reliably quantify in this assay (EC50 > 25 μM). In Phase 1 optimization, increased potency for ABCA1 induction led to potency for LXR activation measurable by CRT. Early lead compound, 6, is a weak agonist for both LXR isoforms (Table 1, Figure 2). Phase 2 optimization of potency for ABCA1 induction also led to increased potency for binding to both LXR isoforms. Lead compound, 39, showed increased potency for both LXR isoforms compared to 6, with selectivity for LXRβ (Figure 2, Table 2). Potency for SRC-1 recruitment to LXRβ was similar for 39 and literature LXR agonist GW3965.

Class II nuclear hormone receptors include: the NR1 subfamily, LXR, peroxisome proliferator activated receptor (PPAR), retinoic acid receptor (RAR), constitutive androstane receptor (CAR), pregnane X receptor (PXR), farnesoid X receptor (FXR); and the NR2 subfamily retinoid X receptor (RXR). RXRs function as heterodimer binding partners not only with LXRs but also with all class II nuclear hormone receptors. The majority, though not all, of these heterodimers function as permissive pairs, meaning that ligand activation of either RXR or its binding partner can elicit similar effects. Profiling of binding sites across the genome illustrated two key phenomena associated with this function. First, most sites of LXR-DNA binding are shared with RXR but only a fraction of RXR binding sites are shared with LXR. Second, the whole-genome binding profiles are quite different across cell and tissue types.33

Due to the permissive nature of the heterodimer complexes within the sterol family of NHRs, NLAIs potentially can mediate their effects via binding to other NHRs. Cross-reactivity of LXR agonists has been observed;34 for example, T0 is claimed to be an agonist for FXR35 and both T0 and GW3965 are reported as PXR agonists.36,37 Therefore, to further evaluate the possible mechanisms by which 39 may upregulate the gene transcription of ABCA1 and SREBP1c, we developed a panel of lanthascreen assays for relevant NHRs. Each lanthascreen CRT assay measures ligand binding and the conformational change that leads to the recruitment of a coactivator. A terbium donor was used to excite the coactivator tagged with fluorescein as a measure of ligand binding and recruitment. For each receptor, a known agonist was used as a positive control.

RXR is a critical NHR for LXR-mediated gene transcription. RXR forms homo- and heterodimeric complexes with other permissive and nonpermissive receptors. The flexibility in the RXR-LBD allows it to adopt various conformations that lead to its heterodimerization with a variety of NHRs such as PPARs, LXRs, FXR, PXR, RAR, and CAR. RXRs regulate the expression of genes that play a critical role in mediating several developmental, metabolic, and biochemical processes. The LXR/RXR heterodimer is classified as a permissive heterodimer. Therefore, the complex can be activated by ligands of either RXR or LXR and are synergistically activated in the presence of both ligands.

RXRα and RXRβ CRT assays were developed using D22 coactivator peptide and SR11237, a potent RXR agonist as positive control. NLAIs were inactive in this assay (Figure S1). It was crucial to test 68, the expected carboxylic acid metabolite of 39 in all assays. Several groups have exploited the importance of this motif through SAR studies and molecular docking, demonstrating that the acid is key to interaction with Arg 316 and Ala327 in the RXR LBD.38,39 We observed no activity for 68 in any CRT assay.

FXR is a bile acid sensor that is critical for maintaining bile acid homeostasis. Disrupted conversion of cholesterol to bile acids in the liver is associated with the excessive accumulation of cholesterol, which can lead to cardiovascular and neurodegenerative disease. This is a tightly regulated process with FXR as the key player inducing transcriptional feedback repression and feedforward regulatory loops. FXR has been shown to repress the expression of SREBP1c.40 FXR and LXR pathways are highly interconnected: LXR promotes fatty acid and triglyceride synthesis and storage, whereas FXR activation results in a decrease in hepatic as well as plasma triglyceride levels,41 providing a potential mechanism for triglyceride attenuation by NLAIs.

The FXR CRT assay was developed using SRC2 as coactivator and obeticholic acid (OCA), a potent and selective FXR agonist, as positive control. The highly studied LXR agonist, T0, has been reported as a dual agonist with weak binding to FXR (EC50 > 5 μM).35 A cell-based FXR reporter assay gave potency of 0.5 μM with efficacy 35% of that of control compound TTNPB.3639 and acid 68 were tested alongside T0, OCA, and synthetic FXR agonist GW4064.42 Our CRT results show that neither 39, 68, nor T0 are ligands for FXR (Figure S2). Based on the lack of binding shown by the NLAIs, the absence of lipogenesis observed in our previous HFD mouse study19 is not attributed to the suppression of SREBP1c via FXR activation.

PPARs perform overlapping and distinct functions, with PPARγ most closely associated with glucose metabolism, and both PPARα and PPARδ associated with fatty acid metabolism. All PPAR isoforms have been reported to modulate ABCA1 expression levels,43 although this effect may be via LXR-mediated transcription.44 PPAR agonists have been developed that increase ABCA1-mediated cholesterol efflux and provide clinical efficacy in T2D and cardiovascular disease. Gemfibrozil and other PPARα-selective agonists activate ABCA1, improve TG levels in preclinical and clinical studies, and have demonstrated efficacy in mouse models of AD models.45 PPARγ agonists have shown positive results in preclinical studies; although, not in AD clinical trials. Recent examples show positive effects on ABCA1 expression.46 A PPARδ agonist that increases ABCA1 has been described,47 and a PPARδ/PPARγ agonist with selectivity for PPARδ is in clinical trials for AD.48 A CRT assay was established for PPARδ using C33 peptide coactivator and GW501516 as positive control: 6, 39, and 68 were inactive in this assay (Figure S3).

Comparisons with Benchmark LXR Ligands

39 compares favorably to benchmark compounds described in the literature as ABCA1 inducing (Table 332,49−53). Initial attempts to develop LXR agonists that induce ABCA1 as therapeutic agents led to compounds such as T0 and GW3965. These molecules were potent agonists at both LXRα and LXRβ. In several studies, these agents beneficially altered AD phenotypes while also producing detrimental effects, including hepatomegaly, significant and rapid increases in plasma TGs, and severe liver steatosis.28,54−56 Efforts to avoid TG-related adverse effects led to several LXRβ-selective agonists, including two compounds from Bristol-Myers Squib and Wyeth that proceeded to clinical trials.32,49,50,52 However, selectivity for LXRβ vs LXRα in biochemical assays employing only LXR-LBDs did not correlate with selectivity in more complex biological systems (Table 3).31 As a class, these compounds offered poor selectivity for ABCA1 vs SREBP1c in cell-based gene expression assays. The Vitae candidate advanced to Phase 2a clinical trials as a topical treatment for atopic dermatitis, likely to avoid TG-related effects associated with oral dosing (NCT02655679).

We and others have been engaged in the development of a third generation of ABCA1-inducing compounds, seeking to avoid SREBP1c induction in vitro and/or TG-related adverse effects in vivo. The Wellington group identified compounds, including AZ-1, that increased expression of ABCA1 selectively in vitro by utilizing a counterscreen that removed LXR agonists.53 However, this approach negates the beneficial effects of direct LXR agonism including anti-inflammatory actions.57

The hypothesis that direct engagement of LXR does not automatically lead to lipogenesis and TG elevation is supported by studies that have identified distinct modes of transcriptional activation for these genes. Specifically, ABCA1 is controlled via a transrepressive function, with LXR residing on the DNA sequence and inhibiting transcription until an agonist is present, as evidenced by increased expression in LXR-KO vs LXR wild-type cells.58 Conversely, SREBP1c is controlled via a traditional transactivation function, in which gene expression occurs at a basal rate with LXR binding that increases with agonist binding.59 Moreover, different combinations of nuclear coactivator and corepressor proteins interact with the ABCA1 and SREBF1 promoter sequences.60 The report of compound IMB-808 and its ability to activate ABCA1 expression without inducing lipogenic genes via differential coregulator recruitment is compatible with our hypothesis.51 Suppression of SREBP1 maturation, as introduced above, provides another mechanism for control of cholesterol and TG production in response to LXR agonism. Finally, lipogenic gene activation by LXR via crosstalk among NHRs, including LXR, RXR, and PPAR isoforms, provides another potential mechanism to achieve selective ABCA1 induction.61

In a Eurofins cell-based NHR screen, early lead 6 exhibited weak PPAR and weaker RXR antagonism, in addition to LXR agonist activity.19 Although we speculated that this might contribute to the favorable lipidomic profile observed in HFD-treated mice, we observed extremely weak PPARγ binding in a TR-FRET assay. Based on our panel of CRT assays we can conclude that 39 and its metabolite 68 do not exhibit significant NHR binding at concentrations up to 5 μM and therefore estimated EC50 > 25 μM. A limitation of biochemical assays, such as CRT, is that a response is not observed for weak ligands. Several compounds studied in Phase 1 and Phase 2 of our optimization efforts showed single digit micromolar activation of ABCA1 but did not give measurable CRT response for LXR (Tables 1, 2). However, the structural similarity with 6 and 39 is likely to reflect activity via weak LXR agonism even for these analogues.

In Vitro Characterization of Lead Compound 39

In the assay used for HTS, which led to identification of hit 1, luciferase activity linked to an ABCA1 promoter (ABCA1-luc) was measured in CCF-STTG1 astrocytoma cells. In this assay, 39 demonstrated improved potency and efficacy relative to hit 1 and early lead 6 (Figure 1B). To improve biological fidelity compared to the immortalized astrocytoma cell line, 39 was tested in primary mixed glial cultures isolated from wild-type mice. By qPCR, we determined that 39 significantly increased expression of both ABCA1 and its related cholesterol transporter ABCG1 (p < 0.05 for multiple concentrations), matching or exceeding the efficacy of the control LXR agonist T0 (Figure 3A,B). Testing for induction of SREBF1 mRNA was performed in the HepG2 hepatocarcinoma cell line to model effects in the liver. 39 did not increase SREBF1 expression at concentrations ≤1 μM, and even at 10 μM did not match the induction of this lipogenic gene by T0 (Figure 3C).

Compound 39 was evaluated for ABCA1 activation by measuring cholesterol efflux to acceptor apolipoprotein downstream of ABCA1 expression. 39 significantly and dose-dependently increased efflux of a fluorescent-tagged cholesterol from J774 macrophages to APOA1 acceptor protein (Figure 3D). Co-treatment with ABCA1 inhibitor probucol, tested at the highest concentration of 39 (10 μM) returned efflux to baseline, indicating that the increase in cholesterol efflux induced by 39 is specific to enhanced ABCA1 expression, not upregulation of other cholesterol efflux mechanisms. Assay of the pan-LXR agonist, T0, increased cholesterol efflux to a lesser extent, again dependent on ABCA1.

These results were supported by Western blots in the same cell lines: 39 significantly and dose-dependently increased ABCA1 protein expression in primary mouse glial cells (Figure 3E), as well as in the J774 mouse macrophage cell line (Figure 3F), but only induced a small increase in SREBP1 protein at the highest concentration tested in HepG2 cells (Figure 3G).

Preclinical Assessment of 39 in EFAD Mice

Preclinical assessment of the lead compound was then performed in the EFAD mouse model. The E3/4FAD mouse was utilized because the APOE3/4 heterozygous genotype is the most common APOE genotype among patients with AD. Mice four months of age were provided an oral formulation of hydrogel as drinking water replacement containing 100 mg/kg/day of 39 for four months.

Target engagement of 39 was evaluated by measuring ABCA1 and APOE levels in the membrane associated fraction (TBSX). APOE levels in the TBSX fraction reflect levels of lipid-associated APOE, while levels in the TBS fraction reflect APOE that is unbound to lipids. 39 treatment increased ABCA1 levels in brain of E3/4FAD mice by 30% (treatment effect: F(1, 36) = 9.166, p = 0.0045) (Figure 4A). Total or FA-extracted APOE levels were not affected by 39 treatment (Figure 4B). However, levels of APOE measured in the TBS and TBSX fraction were affected differentially, with TBS-associated APOE levels decreased and TBSX-associated APOE levels increased (treatment effect: TBS: F(1, 35) = 5.138, p = 0.0297; TBSX: F(1, 34) = 5.625, p = 0.0235) (Figure 4C). This differential impact on TBS (i.e., free) and TBSX (i.e., lipid-associated) APOE was consistent with our hypothesis that upregulation of ABCA1 protein would enhance cholesterol movement from cellular membranes to APOE-containing lipoproteins.

Lead compound 39 was synthesized as a novel analog of the early lead compound 6, which we recently described.18,19 The synthesis consisted of four common organic chemistry reactions, using inexpensive, commercially available starting materials (Scheme 1). The overall yield was >40% across all four steps, enabling rapid production of several grams of highly pure final product for use in animal studies. N-methylation of c gives the intermediate d, which further gives the key carboxylic acid intermediate (e) by base hydrolysis.

Further, HATU condensation reaction of e and substituted aryl amines to afford the 39 and a series of analogs with high yields. Conclusions

ABCA1 induction elicits pleiotropic physiologic effects on lipid transport, endothelial integrity, insulin sensitivity, inflammatory signaling, amyloid production, and clearance. Owing to the broad range of beneficial effects, a drug successfully enhancing ABCA1 activity could be effective in conditions ranging from diabetes, cardiovascular disease, kidney failure, retinal degeneration, and neurodegenerative diseases, including ADRD.13,63 Recent studies continue to support the broad therapeutic potential of activating the LXR-ABCA1 axis to decrease lipid deposition in atherosclerosis64 and to protect against neovascularization in age-related macular degeneration.65

Recent dramatic evidence for the benefits of an LXR agonist acting via ABCA1 upregulation was reported in a tauopathy model of pathologic glial cell activation in AD, studying the effects of GW3965.15 Importantly, the observed reversal of tau-related pathology induced by GW3965 was replicated by genetic upregulation of ABCA1.

Also of interest is the very low dose of GW3965 administered (10 mg/kg/day in feed), which was selected not based on PK measurements, but the previous use of this drug administration protocol. The dose of 39 used in the present study is also low and provided ad libitum. Brain bioavailability of GW3965 was inferred by measuring acute ABCA1 upregulation in brain tissues.15 Administration of the equipotent LXRβ agonists (39, GW3965: Figure 2), in hydrogel, caused comparable elevation of ABCA1 expression in the brain tissues of C57BL/6 mice (Figure S4). From PK measurements with i.p. administration, 39 is present at higher concentrations in brain than plasma (Table S5); whereas administration of its metabolite 68 (Table S6) gives high plasma concentration and brain/plasma <1%.

The recent findings in a mice model of tauopathy,15 when combined with the effects of ABCA1 on amyloid-β pathology, presented herein, indicates that activating the LXR-ABCA1 axis: (i) is a unifying approach to mitigate both pathologic hallmarks of AD and slow disease progression; and (ii) is effective in the presence of APOE4, the major genetic risk factor for AD. Thus, NLAIs, acting as LXR agonists, are expected to have greater clinical efficacy at treating AD than amyloid- or tau-specific therapies that can only target a single aspect of the disease.

To date, drug discovery efforts related to ABCA1 induction have faced challenges in identifying compounds that can elicit therapeutic effects without unwanted side effects. Our efforts to discover, develop, and characterize NLAIs represent promising progress toward overcoming those challenges. With this study, we not only developed a potent and selective inducer of ABCA1 in vitro, but also demonstrated target engagement, efficacy, and tolerability in mice. Our in vivo data differentiate 39 from other promising recent selective ABCA1 inducers, as publications on AZ-1/253 and IMB-80851 thus far has been limited to in vitro systems.

Hurdles remain to enable clinical translation. First, we have pursued further medicinal chemistry optimization of our compound series, with a focus on metabolic stability. Various substitutions and modifications, such as ketones, amides, and sulfones were explored as ester replacements without success. In progressing from 1 to 39, the addition of the chlorine atom to the thiophene ring reduces the propensity to undergo cytochrome p450-mediated oxidation. However, additional sites for metabolic oxidation and dealkylation exist on the ethoxy/methoxy-substituted phenyl ring. Lead compound 39 has a calculated total polar surface area (tPSA) meeting the threshold for non-CNS drugs but is above the stricter 90 Å2 cutoff suggested for CNS penetration. Although oral administration of 39 increased ABCA1 protein expression in the cortex of EFAD mice, the scope for further lead optimization is readily apparent.

A final challenge is to address safety issues raised by LXR agonists that progressed to clinical trials. LXR-623 was limited to a single-dose Phase 1 trial due to adverse CNS effects, which, based on results from BMS-852927 (XL-041), appeared to be unique to that compound and not to LXR agonists nor ABCA1 induction in general.16 In a multiple-ascending dose trial, BMS-852927 was dose-limited by reductions in circulating neutrophil levels and increases in LDL cholesterol.17 The effect on LDL is rationalized as a consequence of differential regulation of the IDOL (inducible degrader of LDL) protein between mice and humans but has proven difficult to adequately address, as even nonhuman primates underpredicted LDL increases.17 The observed neutropenia was, in fact, predictable from murine models. Accordingly, we have already demonstrated that the early lead, 6, did not cause neutropenia in mice at therapeutic doses.19 Nevertheless, we have achieved important progress in this study with the further synthetic optimization and characterization in vitro and in vivo of the NLAI 39. It is clear that further exploration of PK/PD and TK/TD will be important in addressing the NLAI therapeutic index moving forward.

This is the first observation of the effect of an LXR agonist that attenuates Aβ pathology in a mouse FAD-Tg model, against a background of human APOE4 expression. Taken together with very recent positive observations on an LXR agonist in a mouse FAD-Tg model expressing aberrant tau pathology and human APOE4, these results provide strong support for the concept of NLAI therapy in ADRD.15

Experimental Section

In Vivo Studies

Animals

All experiments followed the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee protocols of the University of Illinois at Chicago. As previously described, the EFAD carriers are 5xFAD±/APOE+/+ and the noncarrier are 5xFAD–/–/APOE+/+.

Study, Design, Treatment, and Sacrifice

Male and female E3/4FAD mice were randomized into 2 groups (n = 12): vehicle (VC) and 39 treatment group (Tx). 39 was administered via hydrogel for 4 months from 4 to 8M. 39 was prepared as a 10 mM stock solution in DMSO, the appropriate volume of which was added to melted hydrogel to provide 5 mL hydrogel per day containing 100 mg/kg 39, using 23 g as mouse dosing weight. VC treated mice were treated with same formulation of DMSO in hydrogel without 39. For sacrifice, EFAD mice were anesthetized with ketamine and xylazine, and transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS with protease inhibitors. Both hemispheres were removed and dissected at the midline. Right hemispheres were dissected to cortex (CX) and hippocampus (HP), flash-frozen in liquid N2, and stored at −80 °C. Left hemispheres were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, and then stored in 0.05% sodium azide in PBS at 4 °C, as previously described.

Thio-S Staining

Brain hemis were transferred to a 30% sucrose in PBS solution for 24 h. The hemis were sectioned in the sagittal plane at 30 μm thickness, and stored in 30% glycerol, 30% ethylene glycol, and 40% PBS (cryoprotectant solution). The sections were washed in TBS, mounted on glass microscope slides, and dried for 1h. Sections were rehydrated in PBS, stained with Thioflavin-S in the dark, washed in 70% ethanol, and washed twice in PBS. A coverslip was applied, and slides were covered and dried overnight at room temperature. The slides were covered with coverslips after adding mounting media without DAPI and imaged at 10X with a Zeiss Fluorescent Microscope. The images were analyzed for area covered by amyloid in CX using ImageJ.

Biochemical Analysis

Protein extractions were conducted using a three-step-sequential protein fractionation method, resulting in soluble (Tris-buffered saline: TBS), nonionic detergent-soluble (TBS + 1%Triton X100: TBSX) and insoluble (neutralized formic acid: FA), as previously described.66 Total protein in the TBS and insoluble extracts was quantified using the Ready to Use Bradford Reagent (Bio-Rad) and total protein in the TBSX extracts where quantified with the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce).

Immunoblot

15 μg of protein from TBS-X fractions was separated on 4–12% Bis-Tris NuPAGE precast gels (Invitrogen) and transferred onto 0.2 μm PVDF membranes (Invitrogen). Membranes were incubated in 5% nonfat dry milk in TBS + Tween-20 (0.0625%) for 1 h and incubated overnight with primary antibodies against drebrin (Abcam ab60933), α-tubulin (Abcam ab4074), and ABCA1 (Novus NB400-105), at 4 °C. After 3 washes with TBS + Tween-20, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 45 min, washed again with TBS + Tween-20, developed with Pierce chemiluminescence reagents, and visualized with an Odyssey FC Imaging System.

ELISA

APOE (in-house) levels were measured in TBS, TBSX and FA brain extracts. In-house APOE ELISA utilizes α-APOE (1:2000, Millipore) as capture antibody and α-APOE biotin-gt (1:5000, Meridian) as the detection antibody, as previously described. A recombinant human APOE3 standard (695H-creative biomart) was prepared and diluted to produce standard curves. All samples were diluted to fit within the sensitivity of the ELISAs.

Cell Culture Studies

CCF-STTG1, HepG2, and J774.2 cell lines were purchased from ATCC and cultured per supplier’s instructions. All cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified environment containing 5% CO2. CCF-STTG1 cultures were grown in Dulbecco’s modified essential medium (DMEM) and F12 1:1 nutrient mix (Gibco); HepG2 in Eagle’s modified essential medium (Gibco); and J774.2 in DMEM high glucose (Gibco), all supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 1% antibacterial-antimycotic (ABAM, Gibco).

Primary glial cultures were isolated from P0–P2 C57Bl/6 mice per published procedures. Experiments were approved under ACC protocol #18-034. Briefly, brains were dissected in cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to yield cortical regions, with meninges removed and discarded. Resulting tissue was incubated in 0.05% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco) for 5 min at 37 °C, then triturated with a 10 mL pipet and an autoclaved glass pipet. Homogenate was centrifuged for 2 min at 2000 rpm, trypsin was replaced with fresh complete media (DMEM as described above for J774.2), and tissue was resuspended before passage through 100 μM and 40 μM filters. Resulting cell suspension was plated at a density of three hemispheres per 75 cm2 culture flask. Cells were passaged after reaching confluence 10–14 days postdissection, then used in assays upon reaching confluence again.

Luciferase Reporter Assays

CCF-STTG1 cells were infected with pGreenFire1-LXRE-in-ABCA1 Lentivector (SBI SystemBioscience), which contains a luciferase gene controlled by the ABCA1 minimal promoter region. This region has four LXR response element DR4 sequence binding sites. Cells that internalized the vector were selected with puromycin (1.5 μg/mL) and expanded into several clonal lines that were screened for Z′ factor value then frozen in liquid N2 until use. Cells were plated in 96-well plates in media containing charcoal-stripped FBS for 24 h, with treatment added from 10× PBS dilution for an additional 24 h. Cells were washed once with PBS, then PBS was removed and 20 μL of Passive Lysis Buffer (Promega) was added. Plates were lysed via one freeze–thaw cycle followed by shaking at room temperature, then luminescence activity was measured on a Synergy Neo2 plate reader (Biotek) using Luciferase Assay System (Promega). All data is shown as fold-change relative to DMSO.

qPCR

HepG2 cells were plated in 12-well format in media containing charcoal-stripped FBS for 1 d prior to treatment; J774.2 cells were plated in 12-well format in complete media for 4 h, then switched to serum-free media overnight before treatment; primary mixed glial cultures were plated in 6-well format in complete media for 3–4 d until 80% confluent, then switched to serum-free media overnight before treatment. All treatments were added as 20X solutions in PBS for 18–24 h, then cells were washed 1× with PBS, then PBS was removed and 350 μL of RLT lysis buffer (Qiagen) was added. Plates were then frozen at −80 °C until RNA extraction, which was performed using Qiagen RNEasy plus columns per manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined via A260/A280 method using a Synergy Neo2 plate reader. Reverse transcription to cDNA was performed with oligodT12–18, dNTP mix, and SuperScript III (200 U/μL, Invitrogen) per manufacturer instructions with 2 μg mRNA per sample. qPCR was performed using TaqMan gene expression master mix (Invitrogen) with 2 μL cDNA and desired primer (Abca1: Mm00442646_m1, Abcg1: Mm00437390_m1, Srebf1: Mm00550338_m1, ActB: Mm01205647_g1, Hprt: Mm03024075_m1) in a 20 μL reaction volume per well. Gene expression changes were quantified using ΔΔCt method on a StepOne qPCR instrument.

Cholesterol Efflux

J774.2 cells were plated in complete media for 4 h, then switched to serum-free media supplemented with 0.5 μM BODIPY-cholesterol (Cayman) for 18 h. Fresh serum-free media was replaced, to which test compounds were added as 10× stock in PBS for 24 h. If probucol was used, it was prepared as a 200 μM solution in serum-free media containing 4% bovine serum albumin (BSA), then added at 20 μM to the cells during the final 2 h of test compound treatment. On the final day, fresh serum-free media was replaced once more, supplemented with 20 μg/mL recombinant apolipoprotein A1 (APOA1, Sigma) as a cholesterol acceptor. After 6 h, media was transferred to a separate plate, and cells were lysed with RIPA buffer. Fluorescence (Ex480/Em530) was measured in both plates on a Synergy Neo2 plate reader. Data was reported as % efflux = (media FL)/(media FL + lysate FL) normalized to background efflux signal from vehicle-treated cells without APOA1 acceptor.

Coactivator Recruitment TR-FRET/HTRF (CRT)

LXRα/β Details are provided for LXR CRT with details for other CRT assays provided in the Supporting Information. To determine compound EC50 values, 10 mM compound stocks were prepared in DMSO. An initial 8-concentration, 3-fold dilution series in DMSO was performed in 96-well v-bottom, polypropylene plates at 100× the final desired concentration.
