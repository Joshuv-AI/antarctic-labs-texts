Development and evaluation of an artificial intelligence-based workflow for the prioritization of patient portal messages
Authors: Jie Yang, Jonathan So, Hao Zhang, Simon Jones, Denise M Connolly, Claudia Golding, Esmelin Griffes, Adam C Szerencsy, Tzer (Jason) Wu, Yindalon Aphinyanaphongs, Vincent J Major
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAccelerating demand for patient messaging has impacted the practice of many providers. Messages are not recommended for urgent medical issues, but some do require rapid attention. This presents an opportunity for artificial intelligence (AI) methods to prioritize review of messages. Our study aimed to highlight some patient portal messages for prioritized review using a custom AI system integrated into the electronic health record (EHR).Materials and MethodsWe developed a Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers (BERT)-based large language model using 40 132 patient-sent messages to identify patterns involving high acuity topics that warrant an immediate callback. The model was then implemented into 2 shared pools of patient messages managed by dozens of registered nurses. A primary outcome, such as the time before messages were read, was evaluated with a difference-in-difference methodology.ResultsModel validation on an expert-reviewed dataset (n = 7260) yielded very promising performance (C-statistic = 97%, average-precision = 72%). A binarized output (precision = 67%, sensitivity = 63%) was integrated into the EHR for 2 years. In a pre-post analysis (n = 396 466), an improvement exceeding the trend was observed in the time high-scoring messages sit unread (21 minutes, 63 vs 42 for messages sent outside business hours).DiscussionOur work shows great promise in improving care when AI is aligned with human workflow. Future work involves audience expansion, aiding users with suggested actions, and drafting responses.ConclusionMany patients utilize patient portal messages, and while most messages are routine, a small fraction describe alarming symptoms. Our AI-based workflow shortens the turnaround time to get a trained clinician to review these messages to provide safer, higher-quality care.

Introduction

The use of secure messaging platforms for asynchronous communication between patients and healthcare providers has steadily increased in recent years. Legislative and technology factors have proliferated the use of electronic health records (EHRs), yielding both institutional and patient adoption of secure messaging.1–3 Recently, the COVID-19 pandemic has transformed how patients expect to interact with their providers, accelerating both virtual care and messaging. In a study of 245 US health systems using Epic EHR, patient In Basket messages increased by 157% compared to pre-pandemic levels.4 Another study found a doubling in the number of patient messages received per day, jumping at the onset of the pandemic and sustaining.1 With this increasing demand from patients sending messages 24/7, it is crucial to prioritize messages with higher acuity to ensure urgent issues are addressed promptly.

NYU Langone Health enables patients to use their branded version of Epic’s MyChart app to send messages for various purposes: seeking medical advice, asking follow-up questions, and requesting refills. Different types of messages are routed to different recipients or pools where any member of a group of staff can respond. NYU Langone Health has a team of registered nurse care coordinators who triage and respond to these messages on behalf of physicians in our ambulatory practice network. This team typically works weekdays during normal business hours such that on workday mornings, especially Mondays, there are many new messages from the previous night or weekend waiting to be reviewed. To help in this otherwise manual process of triaging messages, we developed and implemented a tool to prioritize messages with the output of an artificial intelligence (AI) model designed to identify language that warrants immediate action, such as calling the patient. By highlighting these likely urgent messages, they can be read first, allowing staff to address these most pressing concerns as quickly as possible.

Methods

Study design and ethics review

This was an observational study of a quality improvement initiative. At the outset of this work, we anticipated participants (registered nurses) would be engaged in the design and would be solicited for feedback, which would inform rapid iterative improvements. With this intent, the work met the definition of quality improvement outlined by NYU Grossman School of Medicine’s institutional review board and therefore was determined to not be human subjects research. Accordingly, neither ethics review nor consent was required. As the user group received training and encouragement on the change they would see in their workflow, we opted to go live for all users at once and planned to evaluate the impact with a pre-post design.

Study setting and context

The ambulatory care network of NYU Langone Health includes hundreds of practices that are, increasingly, supported remotely by a large team of staff based in Florida. To ensure that messages are reviewed by the right person, they are routed into different pools based on their type, the provider, and their department. Clinical messages are grouped for review by a team of 100+ registered nurses referred to as the Clinical Access Center (CAC), whereas scheduling questions are routed to a different pool managed by different staff. This study involves 2 pools of clinical messages within the CAC that are not only some of the largest, and therefore most staffed, pools but also represent the largest groups of contributing departments (combined 14 at the time of system launch, 50+ at the time of analysis). The majority of these contributing departments are Internal Medicine locations in the New York City metropolitan area, but 20 other specialties are represented.

Clinical workflow and proposed integration

The pre-intervention state of the CAC was a team of registered nurses worked together to triage and action messages by reviewing the patient’s chart, for example, reviewing medications and recent visits. To ensure timely review of all messages, triaging was performed by reading the oldest unread message and labeling it into secondary, specialized pools for subsequent actions. As this process involves 2 steps, the team stopped triaging messages around 2 pm each business day to finish up the actions required for previously read messages.

The goal of this AI-enabled system was to automatically prioritize messages expected to warrant an urgent response, including those referencing acute problems such as chest pain, difficulty breathing, confusion, altered mental status, or syncopal episodes. We planned our model to integrate into the nurses’ triage workflow as a new display column named “1st Msg Acuity” which displayed High for high-acuity messages and otherwise blank. Users were trained to sort their list of messages by the new column and prioritize their work on those predicted High. A prototype of this display is shown in Figure 1 where the column is prominently displayed and was used to sort High acuity messages to the top of the display. The ordering of columns would remain configurable by each unique user and some variation in preferred appearance and workflow was expected. This workflow was enabled by deploying our model using Epic’s Nebula cloud platform (Epic Systems, Verona, WI, United States) to generate High labels within minutes of message creation. Definition of high acuity messages

Nurses determine the acuity of each message by reading the content and, often, reviewing that patient’s information in the chart. Depending on the urgency, the nurse may choose to respond to the message directly, contact the patient via phone, or, in some cases, forward the message with comments to the provider for their review. We developed this AI system in partnership with the CAC team, and our strategy was to develop a triaging tool that could detect if any action was required that was more urgent than replying or forwarding the message. We define high acuity messages as those where the reviewing nurse recommended (or should have) one of the following actions:

Recommending that the patient should go to the emergency room,

Recommending that the patient should go to the emergency room,

Recommending that the patient should go to an urgent care center,

These labels were chosen because they corresponded with a set of acute follow-ups that the CAC leadership had already trained their staff to document and thus, reporting indicators for these actions and recommendations were already in place. Since these decisions are made by humans, albeit registered nurses trained to identify patient-reported symptoms that warrant such urgent actions, these labels are inherently subjective. In addition, actions were not always completed or appropriately documented (eg, it might take a little time before a new staff member is up to speed). As a second review, a physician (who served as medical-IT liaison to the CAC team and who had been involved in developing internal training) served as adjudicator, correcting the labels of the test set (the training set was too large) to represent a gold-standard for model evaluation.

Development cohort

For model development, we included messages that were routed to 2 clinical In Basket pools and restricted to 1 message type, “patient medical advice request” (PMAR), which includes a diverse mix of clinical subjects and are triaged by nurses. The dataset comprised of 40 132 In Basket messages sent from patients (to providers supported by our centralized team of nurses) between December 9, 2020 and February 9, 2021. Of these messages, 10.4% (4181) were labeled as High acuity.

Model development

The development dataset (n = 40 132) was randomly split into 3 sets: 60% for training, 20% for validation, and 20% for testing. The validation set was used to monitor the learning loss during training and to apply early-stopping techniques to prevent overfitting. All messages were preprocessed to remove special characters and adjust casing. A baseline model was developed using a TF-IDF (term frequency-inverse document frequency) vectorizer (employing n-grams of size 1-3) combined with a logistic regression layer.

We utilized the BERT (Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers) model5 for our classification task by fine-tuning it on our dataset. BERT is a pre-trained deep learning model for natural language understanding that was developed by researchers from Google AI Language. It is designed to capture complex relationships between words in a sentence by processing text bi-directionally, allowing for a better understanding of context and semantics and yielding significant improvements in various tasks. We implemented BERT using the HuggingFace Transformers library5 in Python. We leveraged the “BERT-base-uncased” model and tuned 2 parameters: learning rate and the number of hidden states.

Evaluation: model, integration, and impact

This work progressed as part of the AMIA AI Showcase 2023 and to align with the showcase’s stages, evaluation will be structured into 3 phases: (1) technical model performance, (2) usability and workflow, and (3) impact evaluation.

Technical model performance evaluation

Model performance was evaluated with (1) discrimination using the receiver operating characteristic and the area under the curve (AUROC, equal to the C-statistic); (2) the precision-recall curve and the area under the curve (average-precision or AUPRC); and (3) the maximum F-score. These 2 graphs and 3 numbers are the standard machine-learning measures for a binary classification task.

As the CAC team desired a simple appearance of High or blank (Figure 1), a model thresholding decision was needed to define which messages would be identified as High acuity. Selection of an operational threshold focused on precision-recall curves where the team expressed their intent for near-perfect sensitivity (95%). Another more intuitive metric was used to communicate to stakeholders: the number of messages a nurse needs to read in order to discover 1 true positive, referred to as the number needed to read (NNR = 100%/precision). While nurses were not expected to monitor NNR in their work, it was a helpful quantification to communicate to the team that they should expect to review High messages as not truly urgent (also known as the false positives), but if they continued their review, they would discover the next true High (an average rate=NNR). Based on the model’s performance, several potential strategies were presented to these stakeholders in terms of precision, recall, and the NNR (number of High messages needed to review to correctly identify a high acuity message). One approach maximized precision, another maximized recall, while others sought a compromise.

Usability and workflow evaluation

Throughout the model development process, our clinical stakeholders were engaged for their feedback on model performance and potential clinical workflows. Once we integrated the system as a binarized column into the EHR workflow (Figure 1 and Appendix S1), we solicited feedback via biweekly meetings that spanned from late 2021 into early 2023. Initially, these calls focused on educating users about the machine-learning process, the model, and its limitations. Later, the calls included more direct feedback on users’ experience with the columns in their workflow, their perspectives on the model’s utility, and metrics on its overall impact.

Impact evaluation

Definition of pre- and post-implementation

The model output was piloted starting in November 2021 but that period focused on assessing safety and accuracy with a small set of users and led to a threshold revision released in February 2022. Several substantial changes took place in the summer of 2022, including workflow adjustments and continued user education including specific training on the AI and the new encouraged workflows. The AI-based workflow was not fully adopted until September 2022. For metric evaluation, we define September 2021 to May 2022 as the pre-implementation period and September 2022 to May 2023 as the post-implementation period; the same months help to mitigate confounding caused by seasonality.

Concomitant changes within the live setting

The goal of our initiative was to provide an AI-enabled means to prioritize the triage of messages. Our pre-go-live clinical validations proved that it was accurate and had efficacy. To measure effectiveness, it needed to be released live to be used by a diverse user population with realistic constraints within a real-world environment. However, NYU Langone Health has experienced a steadily increasing number of messages and has continued to onboard more providers and departments to be supported by our centralized team. Evaluating the adoption and impact of 1 initiative in this setting is extraordinarily complex: providers and departments are on-boarded inconsistently; nurses are hired, trained, and may depart; and internal recommendations for triage, coordination, and communication are refined. This fluid setting complicated evaluation with a pre/post analysis as it could appear that our AI initiative takes credit for concomitant changes.

Quantitative measures of impact

The operational value of the model was evaluated by examining how the model’s recommendation impacted the care coordination nurse team’s workflow, focusing on how quickly High scoring messages are read. Patients want a fast response but not all messages require the same action; some require no response, some only a brief response, and others need complex clinical review or escalation. Instead, we focused on read time, a measure of how quickly a message was triaged, as it is simpler, more consistent, and aligned with our AI-based prioritization workflow. Our hypotheses were: (1) High messages should have shorter read times than non-Highs in the post-intervention period and (2) read time of High acuity messages should decrease in the post-intervention period compared to the pre-period.

Read time was calculated as the time difference between message receipt (ie, creation time) and when the message was first read, both based on EHR events, but importantly, while only counting time within business hours (8 am to 4 pm, Monday to Friday and omitting public holidays). If a message was sent during off-hours, read time was not counted until 8 am the next business day. Median read time was calculated per month and then stratified by High versus low acuity and by messages created during business hours and non-business hours.

To assess the improvement in the speed at which messages are reviewed, we employed a difference-in-difference (DiD) approach,6 utilizing quantile regression analysis.7 DiD is a type of nonexperimental design which calculates the impact of the intervention referenced to the trend within the pre-period that is recommended when a randomized experiment is not practical.8 We applied DiD method separately to messages that are sent by a patient within or outside business hours under the expectation that the distributions would be different and the AI-based prioritization would impact the distributions disparately.

To evaluate adoption—how the team have changed their workflow to accommodate the tool—we analyzed how (within the post-period) nurses triage messages on a Monday morning in comparison to the pre-period policy to triage the oldest unread messages first. Mondays, intuitively, have the highest volume as messages accumulate over the weekend awaiting triage when staff resume work. The team hopes to triage all weekend messages on Monday and if they adopted the High acuity display, those messages should be read earlier than others and many should be read in the first hour of the day. To evaluate how the team prioritizes triage of High messages, we defined a measure called “coverage” as the fraction of High scored messages sitting over a weekend that are read within the first hour of business on a non-holiday Monday (before 9 am). Coverage can serve as a proxy for the team’s effectiveness in prioritizing messages early in the day. Our choice of 1 hour is arbitrary but is long enough that it is reasonably possible to achieve coverage of 100% (as <10% are classified as High). We report this measure within the post-period only and directly compare it to a simulated workflow setting where nurses would read the oldest messages first (“first in, first out,” referred to as FIFO). Due to logistics (not all members of the nurse team actively triage; some have other specialties/assignments), coverage may not reach 100% in 1 hour each week, but we expected that the use of the High display column would lift coverage higher than the FIFO baseline.

Results

Technical model performance evaluation

When applied to the test set, n = 7260 with adjudicated labels, the TF-IDF baseline model’s area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) is 0.95, the area under the precision-recall is 0.54, and the maximum F-score is 0.56. The BERT model’s ROC is 0.97, precision-recall is 0.72, and maximum F-score is 0.68. Both ROC and precision-recall curves indicate that the BERT model is globally superior to the baseline model (Figure 2).

Test set performance measured by receiver operating characteristic (left) and precision-recall (right) curves.

When presented with the ROC and precision-recall curves with several pre-selected thresholds, the clinical stakeholder team of CAC nurse managers and the overseeing medical director selected a high sensitivity of 95% (at PPV = 28% and NNR = 3.6).

Usability and workflow evaluation

After a brief pilot at 95% sensitivity, the team ultimately decided on a higher threshold so that Highs would be ⅔ correctly urgent (ie, PPV = 67%, NNR = 1.5) with a compromise on missing some true urgent cases (sensitivity reduced from 95% to 63%). The risk of harm from model errors is mitigated in our system as all messages are actioned; we only change the review order.

User feedback has been consistently positive during the ∼18 months of regular meetings, but feedback was never quantified directly from users and instead relayed by their managers. Feedback suggested that users reported the new column was easy to use, easy to recognize, and had a minimal learning curve due to having no required configuration. Several team members have become local experts in the model, assisting others with workflow questions and understanding the model.

The nurse team has reported that the model is precise enough to have the confidence to use the Highs to prioritize their work and has seen no cases of egregious error in the model missing critical messages, even after the threshold increase at the end of the pilot where a reasonable concern related to the reduction of sensitivity could enable cases to be “missed” (by the model, they would be caught by the nurses later as every message is reviewed). The model has been live for over a year and has predicted, on average, 4000 messages a week. The team reports high utilization of the model outputs by the nurse care coordinators although we have no quantitative data as we chose not to require every nurse to opt-in or to document their opinion on every message due to documentation burden concerns.

Impact evaluation

Over the 2-year span comprising the pre- and post-intervention periods, a total of 396 466 messages were sent by patients and scored by the model. Of these, 63% (249 235) were created during business hours and the remaining 37% were created outside business hours. In the pre-intervention period, monthly message creation ranged from 16 000 to 20 000 and this accelerated to between 24 000 and 29 000 by the post-intervention period (Figure 3). The increase was not limited to messages alone. We also observed drastic changes in the population supported by the CAC, with both the number of providers and patients rising during the study period (Figure 3).

Adoption measure of coverage

From September 2022 to May 2023, a total of 34 814 messages were sent after Friday at 10 am and not read until the next Monday (excluding public holidays). Variation is observed across weekends with 450-1100 (median 923) unread messages and 15-80 (median 68) High acuity messages. Perfect adoption of the AI-enabled workflow would see the nurses reading all 100% of High messages in the first business hour after a weekend (as the # of Highs < # of messages read in the first hour). We established a “first in, first out” (FIFO) comparison as a baseline, which reflects the previous workflow prior to the model’s implementation, where staff typically read messages in the order they were received.

Figure 6 demonstrated a statistically significant increase within the post-period of the fraction of High acuity messages that were triaged in the first hour of business data compared to the FIFO method (using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, P = .005). The FIFO baseline also improves between pre- and post-periods, likely influenced by the same initiatives that decreased read time. This finding suggests that the model and workflow led to a more efficient prioritization of high acuity messages but it remained lower than the desired 100% coverage due to factors such as intra-team coordination where a small group of nurses focus on triaging High messages (the “triage pod” noted in Figure 4 compared to other specialist pods who focus on messages sent to certain departments).

Discussion

We trained a large language model to score patient messages by acuity. By implementing this model in Epic, we enabled a new workflow that prioritizes messages identified by the model as more likely to be High acuity, rather than processing messages in a “first in, first out” (FIFO) order. It allows the nurses who triage and action patients’ messages to focus their effort on those with the high acuity flag when they start work to quickly address urgent matters as soon as possible. Overall, the model’s implementation was found to improve the nurse team’s workflow, enabling them to prioritize high-acuity messages and respond more quickly to urgent patient needs.

We found that the time to read the messages dropped significantly even considering all the other factors that took effect around the same time using the DiD approach. Specifically, the intervention cut a median of 9 minutes of read time for messages with high acuity flags sent during business hours, and 21 minutes during non-business hours (Table 1). From another perspective, the post-intervention read time for High acuity messages sent during non-business hours was 8 minutes compared with 24 minutes for those without a high-acuity flag. The team of nurse care coordinators respond a lot faster after the intervention and even faster when the message is flagged by the model as High acuity.

As shown in the analysis of our process measure, coverage (Figure 6), the team has been moderately utilizing the new workflow on Monday mornings to discover more of the predicted High acuity messages than the former, FIFO-style method. The model is able to help the team process urgent messages among a large number of messages queued up from the weekend, but the pattern of decreasing read time post-intervention persists throughout the week (Figure 5).

Limitations and next steps

The pre/post analysis has limitations as there were concomitant changes to the system, staff turnover and training, new providers added to the existing pools, incremental increase in demand, etc (Figure 3). The growth of providers outpaced the number of messages, suggesting either patient behavior changed or a dilution effect as newly added providers accounted for fewer messages than expected. These trends confound our use of a pre-vs-post evaluation design but would hinder any evaluation that did not impose more strict stability in the population.

Randomization was not deemed a feasible solution for ethical and practical reasons (it would also be complicated by the fluid and expanding study setting). Individual nurses utilize the tool slightly differently, depending on characteristics such as their background and training, which could confound user-level randomization in our relatively small sample. Our study was focused on quality improvement, with the workflow undergoing several changes post-launch.

In the future, we plan to expand the use of this tool into other audiences, providing a unique opportunity to be able to measure the impact on read time in more isolated settings, thereby improving reliability compared to the pre/post analyses. Smaller, local clinics with more homogenous patient populations, less staff turnover, and comparison over a shorter period of time will all help mitigate these temporal confounders. We are considering more sophisticated study designs, such as a stepped-wedge or crossover design, now that we have some evidence of effectiveness. We are also planning to expand into additional pools with independent sets of staff.

Our EHR integration focused on 1 High displaying column that expanded to a second column listing the 0-100 score used by some users as a means of fine-grained sorting. We hope to work toward a solution where users can see High or blank while the column can also intelligently sort by the background score (eg, a 37 vs a 52 vs a 78), which is not possible today.

Quantitatively, we aim to expand our analysis to include the time it takes to address the patient’s issue, not just to read it. However, due to a number of reasons, it is not feasible to report on action time. For example, there is no way in Epic to report when a message is truly resolved or to assess an acceptable solution. Epic labels messages “done” as soon as a nurse completes their part of the work, for example, either forwarding to the provider or another team (scheduling, pharmacy, etc) or replying to the patient with a message asking for a clarification. In either case, the message is marked “done” but the patient has not received their desired solution yet and needs to reply again.

Conclusion

An AI system can accurately and reliably identify patient messages that are acute and require urgent action. User feedback reports high satisfaction, indicating that the system is easy to use within clinical workflows and is easy to understand and learn for new users. Quantitatively, we report a decrease in the turnaround time of both low and high-acuity messages, which warrants further analyses, for example, by studying new users beyond this group.
