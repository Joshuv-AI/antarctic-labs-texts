Probing 5‐Nitroimidazole‐Triazole Hybrids as Potential Anti‐Kinetoplastid Agents: Synthesis and In Vitro Efficacy
Authors: Liandi Ferreira, Gideon D Badenhorst, Janine Aucamp, Keisuke Suganuma, David D N’Da
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeglected tropical diseases caused by kinetoplastid parasites, including leishmaniasis, human African trypanosomiasis and Chagas disease, remain severely underserved by current chemotherapies, which are limited by toxicity, resistance and poor efficacy. Nitroheterocycles continue to be clinically relevant due to their redox activation within parasites, and the 1,2,3‐triazole linker offers a modular approach by enabling systematic variation of substituents, thereby allowing controlled modulation of physicochemical and biological properties. Accordingly, this study investigated a new library of 5‐nitroimidazole‐1,2,3‐triazole hybrids and evaluated substituent‐dependent effects on their antiparasitic activity. A series of 5‐nitroimidazole‐1,2,3‐triazole hybrids was synthesised via copper(I)‐catalysed azide–alkyne cycloaddition and evaluated in vitro for their antileishmanial and antitrypanosomal activity, alongside cytotoxicity profiling. Most compounds exhibited low toxicity against mammalian cell lines. No antileishmanial hit was uncovered. In the trypanosome panel, 3j demonstrated selective micromolar inhibition against Trypanosoma congolense (IC50 = 6.38 ± 1.03 µM, SI = 48); hence, it was identified as a trypanocidal hit.Keywords: hybrids, imidazole, leishmaniasiss, triazole, trypanosomiasis

Introduction

Kinetoplastid diseases are parasitic infections of profound public health significance, contributing disproportionately to global morbidity and mortality [1]. Among these diseases, leishmaniasis, human African trypanosomiasis (HAT or sleeping sickness) and Chagas disease persist as neglected tropical diseases (NTDs), primarily affecting communities with limited access to effective treatment and reliable diagnostics [2, 3]. Despite sustained research efforts, therapeutic progress has been slow, and current treatment options are often inadequate due to toxicity, limited efficacy, emerging resistance and complex treatment regimens [4]. The deficit is largely due to a combination of biological complexity, limited commercial incentives, and an overreliance on outdated chemotherapeutic agents [5]. Consequently, there is a continuing need for new chemical therapeutics that offer improved selectivity, favourable pharmacokinetics and the ability to overcome resistance [6] to kerb these diseases.

Furthermore, animal African trypanosomiasis (AAT), notably that caused by Trypanosoma (T.) congolense, is an economically significant veterinary disease responsible for major livestock losses across sub‐Saharan Africa [7]. Veterinary trypanocidal drugs are outdated, and resistance to diminazene, isometamidium, and homidium is well documented [8]. Therefore, the development of compounds effective against T. congolense is important not only for animal health but also to support agricultural productivity and regional economic stability [7].

Across the kinetoplastid family, nitroheterocyclic compounds have long been valued for their antiparasitic activity, which arises from their ability to undergo reductive activation within parasite cells [9]. This process is catalysed by parasite‐specific type I nitroreductases, which convert nitro groups into reactive intermediates capable of promoting parasite death [10, 11]. Drugs such as antitrypanosomal fexinidazole, benznidazole and nifurtimox, as well as antibiotic nitrofurantoin, exemplify the clinical relevance of these scaffolds [12]. Despite their therapeutic utility, nitroheterocycles are associated with several challenges, including variable metabolic stability, susceptibility to off‐target reactivity, and suboptimal physicochemical properties [13]. As a result, rational structural modification is required to optimise the balance between bioactivity, pharmacokinetics and toxicity.

One promising strategy involves hybridising nitroimidazole pharmacophores with other chemically and pharmacologically advantageous moieties [14]. The 1,2,3‐triazole ring has gained prominence in medicinal chemistry due to its exceptional stability, resistance to metabolic degradation, favourable hydrogen bonding characteristics and ease of synthesis via copper(I)‐catalysed azide–alkyne cycloaddition (CuAAC) reactions [15, 16]. Incorporating a benzyl substituent at the N‐1 triazole position further introduces opportunities to explore substituent‐driven modulation of steric bulk, electronic effects and membrane permeability – all parameters known to strongly influence antiparasitic activity [17, 18].

Previously, we hybridised 1,2,3‐triazole with a benzyl moiety which led to disubstituted benzyltriazole analogues that were endowed with enhanced antiprotozoan potential [19, 20]. Subsequently, the swapping of the alkyl moiety with nitrofurantoin, bearing hydantoin and nitrofuran pharmacophores, revealed several promising antiparasitic hits [21]. Nitroimidazole and nitrofuran scaffolds are generally considered bioisosteres as they are often used interchangeably to achieve similar therapeutic effects, particularly in antimicrobial and antiparasitic applications. Both functional groups rely on the presence of a nitro group attached to a heteroaromatic ring to exert their biological activity, which is activated by microbial nitroreductases [22]. Thus, bioisosterism was applied in the current work by substituting nitrofuran with its nitroimidazole bioisostere, while omitting the polar hydrophilic hydantoin scaffold to improve lipophilicity (Figure 1), which is among the critical physicochemical features influencing the bioavailability of a drug.

In this context, 5‐nitroimidazole‐1,2,3‐triazole hybrids bearing benzyl or phenylethyl substituents were synthesised, and their anti‐kinetoplastid activities were examined in vitro. We herein report the synthesis and biological activities of these analogues.

Results and Discussion

Chemistry

A series of 5‐nitroimidazole‐1,2,3‐triazole derivatives (3a‐l) was synthesised via a three‐step process as depicted in Scheme 1 using literature‐reported procedures [21, 23]. First, 5‐nitro‐1H‐imidazole was reacted with propargyl bromide in the presence of potassium hydroxide (KOH) to afford the propargylated intermediate(1) in 40% yield (Lit [24] 42%). In parallel, commercial benzyl/phenylethyl bromides containing electron‐donating (EDG) or electron‐withdrawing groups (EWG) were converted to the corresponding azides (2a‐l) through nucleophilic substitution with sodium azide (NaN3). These intermediates, obtained in 70%–100%, have previously been reported [21, 25, 26], hence were not characterised in this study.

Multi‐step synthesis of 5‐nitroimidazole‐1,2,3‐triazole hybrids (3a‐l). Reagents and conditions: (i) propargyl bromide (1.5 equiv.), KOH (1.5 equiv.), DMF (100 mL), 24 h, rt; (ii) NaN3 (1.1 equiv.), DMSO (2 mL), 12 h, rt; (iii) β‐cyclodextrin (0.02 equiv.), sodium L‐ascorbate (0.3 equiv.), CuSO4·5H2O (0.1 equiv.), DMSO (4:1, v/v). 24–72 h, rt.

Several Click‐Chemistry procedures leading to the formation of 1,2,3‐triazole derivatives, including green synthesis [27, 28], have also been reported. However, in this research, we opted for the conventional most common reaction, which is CuAAC, which is highly selective and efficient. A CuAAC reaction between alkyne 1 and benzyl/phenylethyl azides 2a‐l was performed under click conditions using β‐cyclodextrin (green and sustainable rate catalyst), copper sulphate pentahydrate (CuSO4·5H2O; precursor of reactive species) and sodium L‐ascorbate (reducing agent) in a DMSO:H2O mixture at room temperature. This provided the target hybrids (Scheme 1) in poor to moderate yields (25%–65%) after recrystallisation from ethanol. The poor yields may be attributed to several reasons, including substituent electronic effects, steric hindrance, substrate solubility, and workup‐related factors such as crystallisation efficacy.

Electronic effects imposed by the different benzyl/phenylethyl substituents vary significantly when placed on the alkyne versus the azide component [29]. Alkynes substituted with EWGs have lower pKa values for the terminal C—H bond [30], which translates into an increased acidity of the terminal proton. This results in accelerated initial formation of the copper acetylide intermediate, driving the reaction to high yields even under mild conditions or low catalyst concentrations. On the contrary, EDGs decrease the acidity of the terminal hydrogen and make the alkyne less prone to initial copper coordination, resulting in low yields [30]. EWGs on the azide, on the one hand, reduce the electron density on the azide nitrogen atoms. This slows down the initial coordination step with the copper catalyst, resulting in low yield. EDGs conversely, increase electron density on the azido nitrogens, generally favouring the formation of the copper‐acetylide‐azide intermediate, which ultimately results in high yields [31].

In this study, as a result of the powerful electron‐withdrawing effect of the nitro group, the 2‐nitroimidazole‐1‐methylene is an EWG attached to an alkyne with a terminal Csp—H bond. The benzyl moiety is an EDG whose strength varies with the substituent on its phenyl ring. Thus, 4‐MePh (2b) and 4‐iPrPh (2c) are EDGs attached to the azide, while the remaining azide intermediates (2d‐j) and nitrophenylethyl (2k‐l) bear EWGs. Thus, the combined electronic effects of the substituents of alkyne and azide intermediates suggest high yields for 3b and 3c (EWG on alkyne and EDG on azide), while low yields are expected for hybrids 3d‐l (EWG on alkyne and EWG on azide). The experimental yields did not corroborate the former but supported the latter expectation.

Furthermore, steric hindrance near the azide or alkyne centres requires higher activation energy to overcome spatial crowding. This results in longer reaction times (24 h or more) to achieve full conversion, compared to unhindered substrates that react within minutes [32]. Since the hybrids were prepared in a 24–72 h period, bulkiness of substituents could not account for the poor yields.

Solubility of substrates influences the yield of the CuAAC reaction. Since the reaction occurs via a bimolecular encounter, both the azide and the alkyne must be dissolved in the same phase as the Cu(I) catalyst to undergo the cycloaddition. Accordingly, poor solubility of reagents results in poor yield [33]. In this context, all intermediates were fully soluble in the reaction medium (aqueous dimethylsulphoxide), indicating that substrate solubility did not contribute to the observed poor yields. Thus, the poor yields of 3b and 3b could be attributed to workup efficiency, a factor that also heavily dictates CuAAC yield and purity of product [34]. Recrystallisation employed to isolate the hybrids is inherently known for delivering lower yields compared to other separation techniques [35].

The structures of all compounds were confirmed using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), high‐resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) and infrared spectroscopy (IR), and their purity was determined with high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). In the 1H NMR spectra, the diagnostic triazole H‐5 resonance was observed consistently as a singlet in the δ 7.8–8.4 ppm region across the series, confirming formation of the 1,4‐disubstituted triazole and the expected regiochemistry of the CuAAC reaction. The protons of methylene bridging the triazole and phenyl groups gave a singlet at δ 4.6–5.8 ppm in the spectra of all compounds. The methylene connecting the nitroimidazole moiety to the triazole (at C‐6″) was observed as a singlet at ca. δ 5.4 ppm. Loss of the terminal alkyne H‐1 and the lack of azide‐derived signals further corroborated full conversion of the precursors. In 13C spectra, the benzylic carbons appear at ca. δ 47–54 ppm and triazole/quaternary carbons were found in the δ 120–145 ppm window. Nitroimidazole‐associated carbons (C=N/C–NO2 environments) resonated in the δ 145–155 ppm range as expected.

In compounds 3k and 3l, the phenyl and triazole rings are bridged through a two‐carbon unit (‐C‐1′‐C‐2′‐)‐saturated ethylene (‐CH2CH2‐) chain. Resonance of the methylene protons H‐1′ and H‐2′ of the bridge was assigned to the triplets at 4.57 and 3.08 ppm, respectively, with the coupling thereof resulting in J = 7.2 Hz. In the 13C spectra, the peaks of C‐1′ and C‐2′ are seen at ca. 55 ppm and in the 41–35 ppm range, respectively.

Proton‐fluorine and carbon–fluorine couplings were observed on the NMR spectra of the fluorinated derivatives 3e and 3h. For compound 3h, the 1H NMR spectrum displayed the expected fluorine‐coupling patterns, with H‐6′ and H‐7′ showing small long‐range H–F couplings (3 J H‐F and 4 J H‐F = 1.9 Hz). In the 13C NMR spectrum, the ipso carbon (C‐3′) exhibited a diagnostic large 1 J C–F coupling (252 Hz), together with smaller 2–4 J C–F couplings (2 J C‐F = 24.6 Hz, C‐4′; 3 J C‐F = 4.2 Hz, C‐7′; 4 J C‐F = 3.6 Hz, C‐6′), providing definitive confirmation of the C—F bond. In contrast, compound 3e showed an aromatic 1H NMR pattern (3 J H‐F = 4.6 Hz) consistent with a para‐fluorobenzyl substituent. However, the characteristic large 13C–19F one‐bond coupling for the ipso carbon was not resolved, likely due to low quaternary‐carbon intensity.

IR spectra provided complementary evidence: strong asymmetric and symmetric nitro stretches at ca. 1525–1545 and 1325–1355 cm−1, respectively, confirming retention of the 5‐nitro functionality in all derivatives. The absence of alkyne C≡C or azide (N3) vibrations in the 2100–2250 cm−1 region corroborated complete cycloaddition. Aromatic C–H stretches near 3100 cm−1 and imidazole C=N stretches around 1600–1620 cm−1 were also consistently observed.

HRMS data confirmed the molecular formulas for most compounds: observed molecular ions [M+H]+ matched calculated masses within acceptable error (<5 ppm).

Analytical purity as determined by HPLC was found to be generally ≥95% for most compounds, except 3b, which was isolated with 90% purity.

All derivatives exhibited high predicted gastrointestinal (GI) absorption, and none violated more than one of Lipinski's parameters, confirming their drug‐likeness according to standard criteria. Notably, compounds 3i and 3j showed a single Lipinski violation, yet both retained favourable absorption and permeability scores, indicating that the deviation is unlikely to compromise oral exposure. The topological polar surface area (TPSA) of most compounds remained below the 140 Å2 threshold associated with good passive diffusion, except for 3i and 3j, which approached this limit, consistent with their more polar substituents.

All hybrids were classified as drug‐like and lead‐like, presenting with predictions similar to those of nitroheterocyclic reference drugs benznidazole, fexinidazole and nifurtimox. The negative estimated aqueous solubility (ESOL) and Ali scores suggested low intrinsic solubility in water but acceptable levels for drug‐like compounds containing aromatic and heterocyclic moieties. No synthesised hydrid was predicted as BBB permeant. However, both nifurtimox and fexinidazole presented with similar predictions despite being effective during the meningoencephalitic stage of HAT [42]. Hence, the efficacy of the series as HAT treatment is still plausible.

Pharmacology

The synthesised nitroimidazole‐triazole derivatives were evaluated across a panel of mammalian cell lines to assess general cytotoxicity, and clinically relevant kinetoplastids to establish antiparasitic potential, and derive preliminary structure–activity relationships (SAR). The parasite strains used in this study were: Leishmania (L.) donovani MHOM/IN/95/9515 (strain 9515), an antimony‐resistant visceral leishmaniasis strain; L. major MHOM/SN/74/Seidman (strain NIH S), a commonly used cutaneous leishmaniasis laboratory strain; T. congolense strain IL3000, T. brucei brucei strain GUTat3.1 and T. equiperdium strain IVM‐t1, three principal species responsible for AAT; human‐infective subspecies T. b. gambiense strain IL1922 and T. b. rhodesiense strain IL1501; and T. cruzi strain Tulahuen C4 (+lacZ). The reference drugs used for comparison were amphotericin B (AMB) for Leishmania, suramin (Sur) and diminazene aceturate (DA) for Trypanosoma, and benznidazole (BZN) for T. cruzi.

Most compounds displayed low cytotoxicity, with half‐maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) values greater than 100 µM in Vero, human monocytic leukaemia (THP‐1) and Madin‐Darby bovine kidney (MDBK) cells (Table 2). Compounds 3c and 3i exhibited moderate toxicity towards THP‐1 (IC50 47.54 ± 7.32 µM) and Vero (IC50 35 ± 4.64 µM) cells, respectively, whereas their activity against MDBK cells remained low (IC50 > 100 µM).

Against Leishmania promastigotes, several analogues displayed measurable inhibition, with 3i and 3c representing the most potent compounds with IC50 values of 16 and 23 µM, respectively (Table 2). However, since both analogues were moderately cytotoxic, the promastigote inhibition observed was likely non‐intrinsic. Promastigote activity was otherwise weak and non‐selective between L. donovani and L. major, suggesting that the substituent variations in the series did not significantly influence activity at this extracellular stage.

The intracellular Leishmania assays revealed clear differences between the two strains (Tables 2 and 3). All compounds were inactive against L. donovani amastigotes (IC50 > 100 µM). In L. major amastigotes, several analogues showed improved potency, with 3b, 3f, and 3k producing IC50 values of 28–31 µM and maintaining cidal activity over 72 h. This difference in susceptibility between species suggests that the nitroimidazole‐triazole entity may either be species‐dependent or susceptible to resistance mechanisms present in the antimony‐resistant L. donovani strain but absent in the L. major strain. As mentioned, nitroheterocycles produce reactive species during nitroreduction that contribute to parasite death via oxidative stress. Antimony‐resistant strains of L. donovani have increased protection against oxidative stress, via increased thiol levels, faster regeneration and increased membrane fluidity eflux [43, 44]. Furthermore, these mechanisms are stage‐specific [43], which may correlate with the higher activity against log‐phase promastigotes in both strains versus the reduced activity against the intracellular amastigotes of the L. donovani strain.

For SAR analysis, the compounds were grouped based on chain length between the triazole and phenyl rings: methylene‐linked 3a‐3j (sub‐series 1) and ethylene‐containing 3k‐3l (sub‐series 2). On account of the electronic effect of R substituents on the phenyl ring, Me (3b) and iPr (3c) are EDGs, with the order of increasing strength being p‐Me (3b) < p‐iPr (3c). The order of increasing electronic effect among EWGs in sub‐series 1 was p‐Cl (3d) < p‐F (3e) < m‐Br (3f) < m‐Cl (3g) < o‐F m‐Br (3h) < p‐NO2 (3i) < m‐NO2 (3j), while H (3a) is neutral. In sub‐series 2, 4‐OMe (3k) is a strong EDG by resonance, while being weakly electron‐withdrawing by induction with a Hammett constant (σρ) of‐0.27. For 3‐OMe (3l), the resonance donation due to the lone pair of the oxygen cannot reach the meta‐carbons (in contrast to the ortho and para positions) of the phenyl ring. The powerful inductive withdrawing effect exerted by the strongly electronegative oxygen thus dominates (σm = +0.12). Accordingly, 3‐OMe is an EWG by induction, though it is weaker than halogens (3‐Br σm = +0.39; 3‐Cl σm = +0.37; 3‐F σm = +0.34) and nitro (3‐NO2 σm = +0.71) groups.

SAR analysis indicated that steric bulk and lipophilicity of substituents were required to induce activity against intracellular L. major strain NIH S. The most active amastigote inhibitors, 3b, 3f and 3k, share moderately bulky and lipophilic phenyl groups, suggesting that increased hydrophobicity favours macrophage entry and intracellular accumulation. EDGs (Me, ipr) and EWG (Br) were all represented among the active analogues, indicating that steric and hydrophobic factors outweigh electronic effects in governing L. major activity. Promastigote activity did not correlate with substituent class, supporting the conclusion that cellular permeability, rather than intrinsic antiparasitic potency, drove amastigote inhibition. None of the compounds met antileishmanial hit thresholds (IC50 < 10 µM; SI > 10) [46], indicating that substantial structural design is needed for potency optimisation in future.

The antitrypanosomal evaluation revealed limited activity across T. brucei subspecies and T. equiperdum, with most analogues exhibiting IC50 values >10 µM (Table 4). Notably, 3i and 3j demonstrated selective micromolar activity against T. congolense (IC50 = 9.7 ± 2.6 µM and 6.4 ± 1.0 µM, respectively), with favourable selectivity indices (SI = 11–48). Both compounds feature strongly EWG, i.e., a nitro substituent on the phenyl ring (p‐NO2 in 3i; m‐NO2 in 3j), suggesting that electron‐deficient aromatic systems may enhance susceptibility to T. congolense, possibly through species‐specific reductive activation pathways. The absence of activity against T. brucei subspecies highlights the metabolic differences between AAT‐ and HAT‐causing trypanosomes, further supporting the nitroimidazole scaffold as a promising species‐selective synthon for AAT‐focused drug development.

Overall, the nitroimidazole‐triazole series showed limited potency against human‐infective kinetoplastids but produced a notable and selective response in T. congolense. The SAR indicates that lipophilicity and steric bulk enhance intracellular Leishmania activity, while strongly electron‐withdrawing substituents favour activity against T. congolense. These findings establish a clear foundation for rational optimisation of the scaffold, particularly for AAT, where selective activity represents an attractive and underexplored direction for drug development.

Conclusion

This study reported the synthesis, characterisation, and biological evaluation of a novel library of twelve 5‐nitroimidazole‐1,2,3‐triazole hybrids (3a‐l) designed to explore how benzyl and phenylethyl substituent variation influences activity across several clinically and economically relevant kinetoplastid species. Although the series showed limited activity against L. donovani, T. brucei subspecies, and T. cruzi, the broader dataset revealed several meaningful and mechanistically informative trends that support further development of 5‐nitroimidazole‐1,2,3‐triazole hybridisation.

In the antileishmanial assays, activity against intracellular L. major correlated strongly with steric and lipophilic features on the phenyl ring, suggesting that membrane permeability and intracellular accumulation are key determinants of efficacy in this species. Compounds bearing bulkier or more hydrophobic substituents – particularly 3b, 3f, and 3k – exhibited improved inhibitory profiles and sustained cidal activity, indicating that the triazole‐benzyl/phenylethyl region can be strategically modified to enhance uptake and retention within host macrophages. These findings reveal a distinct SAR trend that can guide further optimisation efforts aimed at improving potency against cutaneous leishmaniasis.

The most notable species‐selective activity was observed in the T. congolense assays. Compounds 3i and 3j, both featuring strongly electron‐withdrawing nitro groups on the phenyl ring, exhibited micromolar inhibition specific to this species and absent in T. brucei subspecies. This pattern suggests that T. congolense may possess distinct redox or metabolic activation pathways that preferentially interact with these nitro‐substituted derivatives. Such species‐selective behaviour is particularly relevant to AAT, where therapeutic options are outdated, and resistance is widespread. Consequently, the nitroimidazole‐triazole anchoring therefore represents a promising starting point for the development of new AAT therapeutics. Collectively, the chemistry and biological data indicate that this combination, which is synthetically accessible and structurally versatile, is capable of generating selective antiparasitic profiles shaped by predictable electronic and steric effects. The SAR insight gained here provides a foundation for targeted optimisation, including adjustments to lipophilicity, redox activity and substituent electronics. Although further refinement is required to improve potency and achieve broader activity, the results support continued exploration of the 5‐nitroimidazole‐triazole hybrids as a rational and adaptable framework for drug discovery against neglected kinetoplastid diseases.

Experimental Section

Materials and Methods

All chemicals, including nitroimidazole, benzyl and benzyl‐substituted bromides, propargyl bromide, sodium azide and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (Johannesburg, South Africa). N,N‐dimethylformamide (DMF) was obtained from Merck (Johannesburg, South Africa). All other solvents listed in the study, as well as magnesium sulphate (MgSO4), were purchased from Associated Chemical Enterprises, ACE (South Africa). All chemicals and reagents were of analytical grade, and no further purification was needed.

General Procedures

The 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance III 600 spectrometer at a frequency of 600 and 151 MHz, respectively, in deuterated dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO‐d6). The chemical shifts were reported in parts per million (ppm) with the residual protons of the solvent as reference. The abbreviations of the splitting patterns were as follows: singlet (s), doublet (d), doublet of doublets (dd), doublet of doublets of doublets (ddd), doublet of triplets (dt), triplet (t), triplet of doublets (td), triplet of triplets (tt), quartette of doublets (qd), and multiplet (m).

HRMS was recorded on a Bruker MicroTOF Q II mass spectrometer. It was equipped with an atmospheric pressure chemical ionisation (APCI) source set at 200°C or 180°C and analysed using Bruker Compass Data Analysis 4.0 software. A full scan that ranged from 50–1500 m/z was performed at a capillary voltage of 4500 V, an end plate offset voltage of −500 V, with the nebuliser set at 1.6 and 0.4 Bar, respectively, and a collision cell RF voltage of 100 Vpp. Infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded on a Bruker Alpha‐P FTIR instrument. A BÜCHI melting point B‐545 instrument was used to determine melting points (mp), which were uncorrected.

Thin‐layer chromatography (TLC) was performed on silica gel plates (60F254) that were acquired from Merck (South Africa). HPLC analyses were conducted using a Shimadzu Nexera‐i LC‐2040C equipped with a temperature‐controlled autosampler, quaternary pump, column oven and diode array detector. HPLC‐grade acetonitrile (Merck) and Milli‐Q water (Millipore) were used for chromatography. A Venusil XBP column (4.60 × 150 mm, 5 µm) with an initial mobile phase (70% Milli‐Q water: 30% acetonitrile) was employed at a flow rate of 1 ml/min. The concentration of acetonitrile in the mobile phase was linearly increased over a period of 5 min to a final concentration of 85%. The time allowed for equilibration between runs was 5 min and the duration of each HPLC run was 15 min. The concentration of the test compounds injected varied (20 µl of 1 mM to 20 µl of 0.25 mM). The eluent was monitored at wavelengths of 210, 254, and 300 nm and at the λ max of each compound.

Syntheses

Synthesis of 1‐Propargyl‐5‐Nitroimidazole

Key intermediate 1 was synthesised according to a literature procedure [23] briefly described as follows. Potassium hydroxide (26.5 mmol, 1.49 g, 1.5 equiv.) was suspended in dimethylformamide (DMF) (10 mL), and 5‐nitroimidazole (17.7 mmol, 2.0 g, 1 equiv.), dissolved in DMF (90 mL), was added upon stirring. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 30 min followed by the addition of propargyl bromide (26.5 mmol, 2.27 mL, 1.5 equiv.). The reaction was left to stir for 12 h and monitored by TLC until completion. DMF was then removed in vacuo, and the residue was extracted with ethyl acetate (EtOAc) and water. The organic phase was dried over anhydrous magnesium sulphate, filtered and concentrated in vacuo. The crude product was used directly in the subsequent step without further purification.

Synthesis of benzyl azides 2a‐l

An extensively reported procedure [21, 25, 26] was adopted to synthesise these intermediates. Briefly, the appropriate benzyl/phenylethyl bromide (0.2 g, 1 equiv.) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (2 mL), and sodium azide (NaN3) (1.1 equiv.) was added. The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 12 h and monitored by TLC. Upon completion, the reaction was quenched with distilled water (5 mL), and the azide was extracted with EtOAc (3 × 5 mL). The combined organic layers were then washed with saturated brine (3 × 5 mL), dried over anhydrous magnesium sulphate, filtered, and concentrated in vacuo to yield the benzyl azide intermediate as oil‐like liquids. These compounds were used directly in the subsequent click reaction without further purification.

Synthesis of 5‐nitroimidazole‐1,2,3‐triazole hybrids (3a‐l)

The titled hybrids were synthesised according to Zuma et al. [21]. 1‐Propargyl‐5‐nitroimidazole 1 (1.32 mmol, 0.2 g, 1 equiv.) and appropriate azide 2 (1.45 mmol, 1.1 equiv.) were dissolved in a 10 mL DMSO:H2O mixture (4:1, v/v) upon stirring in a two‐necked flask. This was followed by the sequential addition of β‐cyclodextrin (0.03 mmol, 0.02 equiv.), sodium L‐ascorbate (0.4 mmol, 0.3 equiv.), and copper sulphate pentahydrate (0.13 mmol, 0.1 equiv.). The reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for 24–72 h and monitored by TLC until completion. Afterwards, the reaction was quenched with distilled water. The resulting solid mass was filtered off, thoroughly washed three times with both distilled water and ethanol, and recrystallised from ethanol to afford the target compounds.

In Vitro Biological Evaluation

General Cytotoxicity

Human Cytotoxicity Assay

The cytotoxic effects of the synthesised compounds were assessed using the resazurin reduction assay, which relies on the metabolic conversion of non‐fluorescent blue resazurin to pink, fluorescent resorufin in viable cells [47]. This method provides a reliable, non‐destructive means to evaluate cell viability and proliferation following compound exposure.

Vero cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium with L‐glutamine supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin (Sigma–Aldrich). Cultures were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For the assay, 96‐well plates were seeded with 200 µL of cell suspension (20 000 cells/mL) and incubated for 24 h.

After cell attachment, the following treatments were applied: (1) emetine dihydrochloride (Sigma–Aldrich; maximum concentration 100 µM) as positive control; (2) solvent diluted to the same concentration used for compound stock preparations as negative control; and (3) the test compounds serially diluted in growth medium (maximum concentration 100 µM). Medium without cells served as the blank. Plates were incubated for 72 h before 50 µL of 0.01% resazurin solution in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) was added to each well, followed by a 2 h incubation.

Absorbance was measured at 570 and 600 nm using a ThermoFischer Scientific Multiscan GO microplate reader, and the data were analysed in Skanlt 4.0 Research Edition software. Cell viability was calculated as:

Cell viability (%) = (δ Abs sample ‐ δ Abs blank)/(δ Abs neg control ‐ δ Abs blank) x 100

IC50 values were determined by nonlinear regression analysis in GraphPad Prism 5, and final IC50 values represent the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of biological replicates.

Animal Cytotoxicity Assay

Cytotoxicity of active samples was evaluated in MDBK cells using the Cell Counting Kit‐8 (CCK‐8) (Dojindo, Japan). MDBK cells were cultured in minimum essential medium (MEM) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin.

Cells were exposed to 1, 10 and 100 µg/mL compound concentrations for 72 h at 37°C and 5% CO2. After incubation, 10 µL CCK‐ 8 reagent was added, and absorbance at 450 nm was recorded after 4 h. Half‐maximal cytotoxic concentration (CC50) was calculated from nonlinear regression in GraphPad Prism 5 and reported as mean ± SD.

Leishmania Host Cell Toxicity

The cytotoxicity of compounds toward THP‐1 macrophages was assessed in parallel using a resazurin assay under conditions mimicking the intramacrophage infection model. THP‐1 cells were differentiated with phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA) as described below, then treated with serial compound dilutions (maximum 100 µM) for 72 h. Cell viability was determined using the same resazurin protocol and data analysis as for the antipromastigote assay.

Antileishmanial Assays

Antipromastigote Assay

Antipromastigote activity was determined against L. donovani strain 9515 (MHOM/IN/95/9515) and L. major strain NIH S (MHOM/SN/74/Seidman) using a modified resazurin‐based method [48, 49, 50].

Promastigotes were cultured in M199 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 25 mM HEPES, 4.2 mM sodium bicarbonate, 0.68 mM L‐glutamine, 0.0005% haemin, 0.0001% biotin, 0.1 mM adenine, and 50 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Sigma–Aldrich), adjusted to pH 7.3–7.4. Parasites were maintained at 26°C and harvested during the logarithmic growth phase.

Assays were performed in 96‐well plates by incubating 1.25 × 106 cells/mL promastigotes with seven twofold serial dilutions of each compound (maximum 100 µM) for 48 h. Amphotericin B (AMB) (10 µM) was used as a reference drug, and medium without parasites served as blank. After incubation, 50 µL of 0.01% resazurin was added, and the plates were incubated for 24 h at 26°C in the dark.

Absorbance at 570 and 600 nm was measured as described above, and parasite viability and IC50 were calculated using GraphPad Prism 5, expressed as mean ± SD for three biological replicates.

Intramacrophage Assay

To assess antileishmanial activity against intracellular amastigotes, previously reported methods [48, 51, 52] were adapted. Human THP‐1 cells were cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium as described for the Vero cells. Promastigotes of L. donovani and L. major were cultured as previously described.

THP‐1 cells were seeded at 5 × 105 cells/mL in 96‐well plates and differentiated into macrophages with 25 ng/mL PMA for 48 h. Non‐adherent cells were removed by washing with PBS, and macrophages were infected with stationary‐phase promastigotes at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 30:1 for L. donovani and 10:1 for L. major. After 24 h of infection, extracellular parasites were washed off, and macrophages were treated with compound dilutions (maximum 100 µM). AMB (10 µM) served as positive control.

Following 72 h of incubation, the wells were gently washed, lysed with 0.05% sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS), and incubated in promastigote medium to allow recovery of viable parasites. Parasite viability was quantified using the resazurin assay after 24 and 72 h incubation at 26°C, as previously described.

Antitrypanosomal Assays

Bloodstream Trypanosomes

Compounds were screened against bloodstream forms of T. b. brucei GUTat 3.1, T.b. gambiense IL1922, T.b. rhodesiense IL1501, T. equiperdum IVM‐t1, and T. congolense IL3000 using the CellTiter‐Glo luminescence assay [53].

Parasites were maintained in HMI‐9 medium (IMDM supplemented with 20% FBS, 60 mM HEPES, 0.1 mM bathocuproine, 1 mM pyruvate, 1 mM L‐cysteine, 0.0001% β‐mercaptoethanol, HT supplement, and ITS‐X) at 37°C for T.brucei spp. and T. equiperdum, and at 32°C for T. congolense.

Samples were first tested at 0.25 and 25 µg/mL; those showing >50% inhibition at 25 µg/mL proceeded to IC50 determination using twofold dilutions. After 72 h of incubation, 25 µL of CellTiter‐Glo reagent (Promega) was added, the plates were shaken for 2 min, incubated for 10 min, and luminescence was recorded at 450 nm. IC50 values were obtained by nonlinear regression using GraphPad Prism 5 and expressed as mean ± SD.

Trypanosoma Cruzi Antitrypomastigotes

Activity against bloodstream trypomastigotes of T. cruzi Tulahuen Clone C4 (+lacZ) was evaluated using a modified resazurin method [54].

Vero cells were infected at an MOI of 10:1 for 24 h, and trypomastigotes were harvested after 5–6 days. Parasite suspensions (1 × 107 cells/mL; ≥80% trypomastigotes) were incubated with compound dilutions (maximum 100 µM) for 48 h at 37°C. BZN (10 µM) served as reference.

After incubation, 50 µL of 0.01% resazurin was added, the plates were incubated for 24 h, and absorbance was measured at 570 and 600 nM. IC50 values were determined as described previously.

Trypanosoma Cruzi Intracellular Amastigotes

The β‐galactosidase assay [55] was adapted to evaluate intracellular T. cruzi amastigote inhibition.

Infected Vero cells (MOI 1:1, 4 × 105 cells/mL each) were incubated with compound dilutions (maximum 10 µM) for 72 h, followed by the addition of PBS containing 0.3% Triton X‐100 and 400 µM ONPG. Plates were incubated for 24 h in the dark and absorbance was measured at 420 nm. Growth inhibition (%) was calculated relative to untreated controls. Compounds showing >60% inhibition qualified for IC50 determination.
