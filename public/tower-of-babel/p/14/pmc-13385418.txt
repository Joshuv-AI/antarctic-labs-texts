Measuring Digital Transformation in Chinese Hospitals: Development and Validation of a Digital Maturity Evaluation Framework
Authors: Xinyi Liu, Xiao Han, Jianjun Chen, Guanghua Zhou, Guohong Li, Xianqun Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDigital transformation is a key priority for modernizing China’s public hospitals. However, a standardized and context-specific framework to evaluate their digital maturity remains absent. This study aims to develop and validate a comprehensive, multidimensional evaluation framework tailored to Chinese tertiary public hospitals to support systematic assessment and inform policy decisions. Based on systematic literature review and policy analysis, we constructed a framework comprising Digital Readiness, Technology Application, and Data Management Capability, with 11 subdimensions and 65 indicators. Indicator weights were derived using a two-round Delphi consultation, analytic hierarchy process, and criteria importance through intercriteria dependence method. The framework was applied to 1,361 tertiary public hospitals across 28 mainland provincial-level divisions in China. Digital maturity scores were analyzed using global sensitivity analysis (GSA), k-means clustering, and logistic regression with Firth’s penalized likelihood. Digital Readiness received the largest combined weight (34.9%), followed closely by Technology Application (34.7%) and Data Management Capability (30.4%), suggesting that hospital digital maturity reflects a balanced combination of organizational readiness, technology-enabled service application, and data governance. GSA further revealed discrepancies between combined weights and empirical sensitivity rankings, indicating that these approaches captured different aspects of indicator importance. Clustering and regression analyses showed that higher-maturity hospitals had higher values across many indicators, particularly in clinical digital applications and data quality management, whereas data sharing and exchange remained relatively weak across maturity groups. Robustness checks using alternative weighting and clustering methods generally supported the stability of the main findings, while also indicating residual sensitivity to methodological choices. The proposed framework provides a structure- and process-oriented diagnostic tool for assessing digital maturity in Chinese tertiary public hospitals. It can support policy monitoring, institutional benchmarking, and targeted improvement of hospital digital transformation. Future research should update the framework with more recent data and validate maturity scores against healthcare quality, safety, efficiency, patient experience, and equity outcomes. Introduction

In recent decades, rapid advancements in digital technologies have significantly reshaped healthcare delivery systems worldwide. Technologies such as electronic health records (EHRs), telemedicine, artificial intelligence (AI), big data analytics, and mobile health applications have been increasingly integrated into healthcare practices, improving the quality and efficiency of care [1], enhancing patient safety through reduced medical errors [2], and expanding the accessibility of services via telehealth initiatives [3]. Countries including the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, and Australia have actively pursued healthcare digital transformation strategies, making digital transformation a strategic priority for health system modernization [1]. In China, digital transformation in public hospitals is increasingly important due to pressures from an aging population, the rising burden of chronic diseases, and resource constraints within traditional healthcare delivery models [4, 5]. Healthy China 2030, China’s long-term health blueprint, explicitly calls for leveraging technology and innovation to modernize the health sector [6]. Similarly, the nationwide Digital China initiative has catalyzed investment in health IT as part of a broader digital economy strategy [7].

Despite strong policy support, Chinese public hospitals continue to face significant challenges on the path toward digital transformation. The large scale of China’s health system, serving a vast and geographically dispersed population, leads to uneven progress across regions [8]. Many hospitals, especially in less-developed areas, still lack the digital infrastructure, skilled IT workforce, and strategic planning needed for successful transformation. Moreover, hospital information systems often remain fragmented. There are reportedly thousands of different medical IT systems with limited interoperability across the country, resulting in “information silos” that hinder data exchange between hospitals and regions [5]. These issues pose hurdles to achieving the full benefits of digital health.

To address these challenges, the concept of “digital maturity” has emerged as an effective theoretical framework. Digital maturity refers to an organization’s capability to fully leverage digital technologies, encompassing not only IT systems but also governance structures, supportive processes, and human factors critical for successful digital transformation [9]. In healthcare, digitally mature hospitals integrate clinical and administrative processes extensively to improve efficiency, care quality, and patient outcomes [1]. Such integration also creates the data infrastructure required for advanced analytics and evidence-based management. Recent European evidence further reinforces this link, showing that machine learning-based frameworks can jointly assess hospital efficiency and patient-perceived quality, while explainable multi-objective optimization approaches extend the notion of efficiency to include allocative performance and energy sustainability [10, 11].

Several international frameworks have been developed to assess hospital digital maturity. The HIMSS Electronic Medical Record Adoption Model (EMRAM), one of the earliest and most recognized models, defines maturity along an eight-stage pathway (Stage 0–7), progressing from minimal digital adoption to fully integrated EHR environments [12, 13]. The UK’s NHS Digital Maturity Assessment (DMA) expands beyond EHR adoption, evaluating hospitals across infrastructure, interoperability, leadership, and innovation readiness [14]. Its multi-dimensional approach underscores the importance of organizational readiness and workforce capabilities alongside technological advancement [15]. While common indicators such as interoperability, IT infrastructure, and data security receive global recognition, individual nations, including Australia, Germany, and Norway, have developed maturity assessments tailored to their specific healthcare contexts. These models provide important foundations for evaluating healthcare digital transformation, but they were largely developed in health systems with institutional arrangements, regulatory environments, and hospital organization patterns that differ from those in China. Given the unique context of China’s healthcare system, there is a clear rationale for developing a China-specific hospital digital maturity assessment. Existing international models provide valuable references, but they may not capture critical local factors in the Chinese context. The hospital hierarchy and referral system, national e-governance standards, and the scale of public hospitals differ substantially from those in Western settings [16]. Recognizing this, Chinese health authorities have already begun to establish domestic benchmarks: in 2018 China’s NHC introduced a nine-level evaluation scale (Level 0–8) for EHR adoption in hospitals, with explicit criteria for each level and targets for hospitals to reach certain grades by 2022 [17]. In light of the policy mandates and the varied maturity levels observed across regions [17], a comprehensive Chinese digital maturity indicator framework is needed to systematically assess progress, identify gaps, and guide improvements in hospital digitalization.

Therefore, this study aims to develop and validate a digital maturity evaluation framework specifically tailored to China’s public hospitals. The proposed system provides an objective and multidimensional measure of hospital digital maturity to support evidence-based strategies and inform policymaking for advancing China’s healthcare digital transformation. The framework may also provide methodological insights for other countries with large populations, hierarchical health systems, and marked regional disparities, although its application in different institutional contexts would require local adaptation and empirical validation. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 describes the data source, indicator development process, Delphi consultation, weighting methods, and empirical validation strategy. Section 3 presents the finalized framework, indicator weights, sensitivity analysis, and clustering results of hospital digital maturity. Section 4 discusses the findings in relation to existing digital maturity frameworks, highlights policy implications, and addresses methodological limitations. Section 5 concludes the study and outlines future directions.

Methods

Data

We used data from the 2020 Digitalization Survey conducted by the National Health Commission (NHC) in mainland China. The survey was administered in 2019, covering 58.7% of tertiary hospitals in China. The analytic sample included 1,361 tertiary public hospitals from 28 mainland provinces. Qinghai, Guangxi, and Sichuan did not participate in this round of the survey and the reasons were unavailable. Because participation in the NHC survey was voluntary and information on non-responding hospitals was not available in the dataset, a formal non-response analysis could not be conducted. Therefore, the sample should be interpreted as broadly covering tertiary public hospitals across diverse geographic and socioeconomic settings, rather than as fully representative of all tertiary hospitals in China.

Construction of Evaluation Framework and Weighting

Establishment of the Evaluation System

We conducted a systematic review of relevant domestic and international literature to identify the evaluation dimensions. Searches were performed in both English and Chinese databases, including PubMed, Medline, Web of Science, CNKI, and Wanfang database, following the methodology outlined by Siddaway et al. [18].

The search strategy combined two sets of keywords: (1) “digital maturity” and “digital capacity”, and (2) “health” and “hospital”. Relevant studies published between January 1, 1990, and September 1, 2024, were retrieved. Exclusion criteria included: (1) studies focusing on non-hospital healthcare institutions; (2) models unrelated to digital transformation; (3) publications in languages other than Chinese or English; and (4) studies without full-text access.

Based on literature analysis and national policy documents regarding the orientation of tertiary public hospitals and digital transformation, we developed an evaluation framework comprising three primary dimensions (digital readiness, digital technology application, and data management capability) along with 11 secondary subdimensions and 65 tertiary indicators.

Expert Consultation

We conducted two rounds of Delphi expert consultations to refine and validate the indicator system. The Delphi method is a widely adopted approach for achieving expert consensus through structured, iterative surveys with anonymous feedback [19]. The Delphi panel included experts from hospital information management departments, government agencies, and academic institutions. Hospital information management professionals constituted the largest group because the framework focused on hospital-level digital maturity and required practical knowledge of digital infrastructure, interoperability, data governance, standardization, and technology application. Experts from government and academic institutions contributed policy and research perspectives, and the panel covered eastern, central, and western regions of China.

In our study, experts’ demographic characteristics and professional experience were collected, and each indicator was rated for importance, availability, and sensitivity using a five-point Likert scale. Expert authority was assessed based on judgment basis (Ca) and familiarity (Cs), with the authority coefficient (Cr) defined as the average of Ca and Cs (definition provided in Table S1). Empirical Sensitivity and Indicator Importance

Weighting, global sensitivity analysis (GSA), and clustering were conducted in Python 3.12.13, and logistic regressions were implemented with R 4.4.2.

Indicator Sensitivity Analysis

After combining subjective and objective weighting to compute digital maturity scores, we applied the Random Balance Designs Fourier Amplitude Sensitivity Test (RBD-FAST), a method of GSA, to quantify the first-order sensitivity of each indicator [23]. Indicators were ranked according to their sensitivity contributions and combination weights respectively, and discrepancies between the two rankings were analyzed.

Robustness Check

We performed following analysis to display the robustness of our findings: (1) Objective weights were compared with those using entropy, CV, and CRITIC methods; (2) We used other clustering methods, such as Jenks natural breaks, quantile cuts, and finite mixture modelling, and the number of clusters ranged from three to seven. The grading and regression results were compared to ensure consistent findings.

The Delphi component of this study was reported with reference to the CREDES guidance, and the observational empirical analysis was reported with reference to the STROBE statement.

Results

Delphi Expert Consultation

Two Delphi rounds were conducted to validate the indicators. The initial round had a response rate of 80% (32 of 40 experts; Table S2), comprising predominantly experienced professionals from hospital information management departments (81.25%), senior titles (62.50%), and extensive experience in digital healthcare (59.38% with over 20 years). The panel also covered eastern, central, and western China; experts from eastern China accounted for 53.13% in Round 1 and 53.57% in Round 2, while the remaining experts came from central and western regions (Table S2). The expert authority coefficient was notably high (Cr = 0.853, Table S3). For importance ratings, consensus was significant for subdimensions (Kendall’s W = 0.104, P < 0.001) but not for the three primary dimensions (Tables S4 - S5).

In the subsequent round (Table S4, Table S6), consensus was again significant for subdimensions (Kendall’s W = 0.161, P < 0.001) but not for the three primary dimensions, with 28 experts participating (87.5% response rate, Table S2). The non-significant dimensional-level Kendall’s W should be interpreted with caution. Because the three primary dimensions were conceptually prespecified and expert ratings were concentrated at high values, Kendall’s W alone may not fully capture practical agreement; however, this statistical non-consensus does indicate that the dimension-level weights should not be interpreted as a strict expert-consensus ranking. In this study, the coefficient of variation below 0.25 (Tables S5-S6) was used as the operational agreement criterion for retaining dimensions and indicators. The finalized framework therefore reflects acceptable convergence at the operational level, while the dimension weights are best understood as balanced high-level scaling factors for three core domains rather than as precise estimates of their relative importance. More detailed consensus and empirical differentiation were assessed at the subdimension and indicator levels, and the final maturity score further incorporated data-driven CRITID weights and robustness analyses.

Based on literature review, policy documents, and expert consultations, we established a structured indicator framework encompassing three primary dimensions: Digital Readiness, Digital Technology Application, and Data Management Capability. These dimensions further included 11 subdimensions and 65 specific indicators.

The descriptive statistics of all indicators are provided in Table S7. Because the NHC maintained strict quality control and double-checked all outliers with hospitals, we decided winsorisation was not needed after examining data distribution.

Weight Allocation

The weights for each indicator were calculated by the AHP (subjective) and CRITID (objective data-driven). The dimension of Digital Readiness was assigned a weight of 34.9% (Fig. 1 and Table S8). Subdimensions (Fig. 1 and Table S9) included Digital Development Strategy (8.4%), Investment (6.2%), Talents (12.5%), and Infrastructure (7.7%). The highest weighted indicator (Table 1) was “Dedicated staff for information standards” (8.2%), emphasizing the crucial role of specialized human resources and standardization capability in hospital digital maturity. Other infrastructure-related indicators generally showed lower weights, notably “Current storage capacity excluding backup” (0.5%), indicating less perceived and data-driven importance.

Robustness Check

Selection of Weighting Methods

Table S12 compares all indicator-level relative weights calculated with CRITIC, CRITID, CV, and the entropy method. Weights larger than 50% are bolded. As expected, weights from CRITIC and CRITID were similar, but they could be drastically different from those calculated with only the indicator variation measures (CV or entropy). The weight ranges from CRITIC and CRITID were [3.5%, 65.8%], which were similar to those from CV ([1.1%, 62.6%]) but less extreme than those from the entropy method ([0.2%, 80.0%]). The maximum CRITID weight was assigned to “Dedicated staff for information standards” within the Talents subdimension, reflecting the distinct role of specialized standardization personnel in hospital digital maturity. Nevertheless, presence of extremely large relative weight was a common drawback of all weighting approaches used and should be interpreted as a methodological limitation.

For reference, descriptive statistics of all indicators including the variances are provided in Table S7, and between-indicator Pearson correlation matrices in each subdimension were shown in Figures S2 – S12. Only four off-diagonal correlations were above 0.70 and the largest was 0.77, indicating possible but limited conceptual overlap that could be partially reduced by CRITID.

Selection of Clustering Methods

K-means was selected because of its familiarity for healthcare stakeholders and its ability to group hospitals with similar digital maturity scores into one cluster, and the number of k-means clusters was set at five for management purposes. Figure S13 and Table S11 demonstrate necessary model selection criteria of k-means, which present inconsistent suggestions regarding the optimal number of clusters. Because the silhouette score and Davies-Bouldin index supported the k = 5, we decided it was appropriate to use five clusters in the main analyses. In this section, we will demonstrate how different numbers of clusters and clustering methods may affect the findings.

Table S10 shows the number of hospitals within each cluster using different clustering methods and numbers of clusters. Jenks natural breaks and k-means produced similar results. Similar to Table 5 and Figure S1, we performed univariate logistic regressions using different thresholds of the cluster ID to convert the clusters into binary outcome variables (Figures S14 - S33).

We summarized the regression findings by calculating the proportion of Q-significant coefficients for each indicator, given the clustering method and the number of clusters (Table S13). All indicators were expected to significantly predict clusters of a higher digital maturity score (OR > 1). Nearly all indicators met this expectation for at least 50% of the thresholds, except: (1) Only “Number of systems graded level 3 or higher of the national Multi-Level Protection Scheme of information security” failed to predict so for more than 50% of all thresholds when the number of clusters was three, whichever the clustering method; (2) Only “Cybersecurity governance” could significantly predict clusters of a lower digital maturity score when the number of clusters was five or more (OR < = 1), meaning that, some but not all, hospitals with higher digital maturity scores might perform worse in this indicator.

The two indicators were also mostly affected by quasi-separation: “Cybersecurity governance” experienced unusually large upper CI boundaries ( > = 100) 73 times, and 28 times for “Number of systems graded level 3 or higher of the national Multi-Level Protection Scheme of information security”. Such indicators may represent foundational conditions that are almost universally present among high-performing hospitals, but we also acknowledge the possibility of model misspecification or selection bias in the surveyed sample. Therefore, these estimates should be interpreted cautiously and descriptively rather than as causal evidence. We have provided the descriptive statistics of all similar cases in Table S14 for reference.

Discussion

Our study developed and validated a Hospital Digital Maturity Evaluation Framework tailored to the Chinese context, yielding several key insights. The framework encompasses three primary dimensions (Digital Readiness, Digital Technology Application, and Data Management Capability), further divided into 11 subdimensions and 65 specific indicators. The combined weighting results showed a relatively balanced contribution of the three dimensions, with Digital Readiness receiving the largest weight within this framework, followed closely by Digital Technology Application and Data Management Capability.

Our evaluation framework aligns with global efforts to assess digital health maturity while addressing China’s unique healthcare context. Beyond foundational models like HIMSS EMRAM and the NHS DMA, many countries have developed tailored tools. Canada’s Pan-Canadian Digital Health Scorecard emphasizes interoperability and standardization across provinces [24], while Australia integrates maturity assessments into its national strategy, focusing on domains such as clinical systems, governance, and digital skills [1]. International organizations have further broadened the toolkit for benchmarking digital health. The WHO and global partners developed the Global Digital Health Index (GDHI), which provides countries with a standardized maturity model and a five-level scoring system to generate a digital health “scorecard” [25]. This enables nations to assess their eHealth environment, identify gaps, and compare against global averages [26]. Across these diverse international frameworks, there is a clear consensus on key dimensions. Common priority areas include robust ICT infrastructure, interoperability of systems, data governance and security, strong leadership and change management, and a digitally literate workforce [1, 9, 15]. Our framework follows these global principles while incorporating China-specific elements such as hospital tiering and national eHealth standards, contributing a validated, locally relevant model to the international discourse on digital maturity.

An important finding of our study is that hospital digital maturity should not be interpreted as a function of technology deployment alone, but as a multidimensional capability involving organizational readiness, governance, standardization capacity, workforce support, and technology-enabled service integration. This interpretation is consistent with prior work criticizing technology-centric maturity measures and emphasizing socio-technical approaches to digital transformation [1, 27, 28]. At the same time, the sensitivity analysis indicates that combined weights and RBD-FAST sensitivity rankings capture different aspects of indicator importance. Weights reflect expert-informed priorities and data-driven information content, whereas RBD-FAST evaluates how variation in each indicator contributes to variation in the final maturity score [23]. Therefore, discrepancies between the two rankings may reflect indicator redundancy, limited empirical variation, or conceptually important factors whose influence is mediated through other organizational mechanisms. In this context, leadership should be interpreted as part of a broader organizational readiness construct rather than as an isolated empirical driver of digital maturity.

The clustering and regression analyses further characterized how higher-maturity hospitals differed from lower-maturity hospitals. Indicators related to data quality management showed strong associations across maturity thresholds, suggesting that adherence to standardized data structures, coding systems, and information specifications is a core feature of digitally mature hospitals. This finding is consistent with the policy emphasis on interoperability, data standardization, and secure information governance in China’s smart hospital agenda [29]. In addition, clinical care applications were particularly useful in distinguishing the highest-maturity hospitals from others, indicating that advanced digital maturity is not merely reflected in the presence of infrastructure, but in the integration of digital tools into clinical workflows and service delivery [30]. By contrast, indicators related to data sharing and exchange showed weaker discriminatory power, which may reflect a common system-level bottleneck: even tertiary hospitals with relatively advanced internal systems may still face barriers in cross-institutional interoperability and regional information exchange. These findings suggest that the next stage of hospital digital transformation should move beyond internal system construction toward standardized, clinically embedded, and interoperable digital ecosystems [28].

From a methodological perspective, the choice of k-means clustering and logistic regression was guided by the exploratory and explanatory objectives of this study. K-means clustering was used to convert the continuous digital maturity score into five interpretable maturity tiers. In our analysis, the algorithm was implemented with k = 5, Euclidean distance, and k-means + + initialization. This approach is consistent with the classical use of k-means as a partitioning method that minimizes within-cluster variation and with later improvements in centroid initialization designed to enhance computational stability [31, 32]. Compared with hierarchical clustering, DBSCAN, and Gaussian mixture models, k-means offers a simple, transparent, and policy-friendly classification strategy for a one-dimensional maturity score. Hierarchical clustering is useful for exploring nested structures but is less direct for producing a fixed set of maturity tiers; DBSCAN is more appropriate for detecting irregularly shaped density-based clusters and outliers; and Gaussian mixture models require stronger distributional assumptions about the underlying data-generating process [33, 34]. Therefore, k-means was considered suitable for generating maturity categories that could be easily interpreted by hospital managers and policymakers. Logistic regression was then used to examine associations between individual indicators and maturity-tier membership. The models used a logit link function with Firth’s penalized likelihood, adjusted for hospital level, hospital type, affiliation, and province, and applied Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction for multiple testing. Compared with machine learning classifiers such as random forests, support vector machines, or neural networks, logistic regression provides more transparent effect estimates through odds ratios and is better aligned with the explanatory purpose of identifying indicators that characterize differences between maturity tiers [35]. Accordingly, these results should be interpreted as exploratory associations rather than causal effects or predictive validation.

Our findings have clear policy implications for China’s ongoing digital health agenda. Over the past decade, China has positioned digital transformation as a core component of health system reform. Our results validate these directions, particularly the emphasis on strategic planning, organizational readiness, and data governance. Hospitals meeting key requirements in NHC policy, especially long-term digital plans and dedicated IT standardization staff, consistently scored higher in maturity, suggesting that top-level design has had a measurable impact. Beyond confirming these policy priorities, the proposed framework provides a practical diagnostic tool for monitoring hospital digital transformation, identifying regional and institutional gaps, and informing differentiated resource allocation. For hospital managers, the framework can support internal benchmarking across organizational readiness, technology application, and data management capability, helping institutions identify specific weaknesses and formulate targeted improvement strategies. The study also highlights that many tertiary hospitals have met the basic targets of the national EHR grading policy, confirming progress in foundational digital infrastructure. However, only hospitals that went beyond EHR installation, featured by integrating advanced technologies like AI, big data, and IoT, achieved the highest maturity. This supports China’s recent shift of focus toward smart hospital development [36], emphasizing digital integration over infrastructure alone. Furthermore, the importance of data governance and standardization in our maturity model echoes national priorities around data security and interoperability. High-performing hospitals consistently demonstrated stronger adherence to standards and lower incidence of system failures. Thus, our framework offers a diagnostic tool for identifying specific digital gaps, helping inform targeted policy interventions and resource allocation to support lagging institutions. By aligning with national priorities and providing a measurable maturity baseline, this study contributes evidence to guide the next phase of China’s digital health transformation.

Although this framework was developed for Chinese tertiary public hospitals, it may provide methodological insights for health systems facing similar challenges, such as large public hospital networks, hierarchical delivery structures, and regional disparities in digital capacity. Its broader relevance lies less in the direct transfer of specific indicators or weights, and more in the approach of combining policy-informed indicator development, expert consultation, data-driven weighting, sensitivity analysis, and empirical stratification. Application in other countries or healthcare settings would require local adaptation and validation using context-specific data.

This study has several limitations. First, the analysis is based on cross-sectional data from the 2020 NHC Digitalization Survey, which was conducted in 2019–2020. The data provide a valuable baseline before the rapid post-COVID expansion of telehealth, online care models, and emerging AI applications. Therefore, the absolute maturity scores and the prevalence of specific technologies are likely to have changed by 2026, although the framework’s core domains, organizational readiness, technology application, and data management capability, remain relevant. Future assessments should update the framework using more recent data and incorporate new indicators reflecting generative AI, advanced analytics, and evolving data governance requirements. Second, although the sample included 1,361 hospitals across 28 mainland provincial-level divisions, Qinghai, Guangxi, and Sichuan did not participate in this round of the survey, and potential differences between responding and non-responding hospitals could not be assessed. The exclusion of Sichuan is particularly relevant because it is a large provincial hospital region with substantial tertiary hospital capacity; its absence may therefore compromise the geographic representativeness of western China. Our findings and framework may therefore not be directly generalizable to all tertiary hospitals, secondary or primary care facilities, or private hospitals. Future work should test and adjust the indicator system for these settings to ensure broader applicability. Third, although we employed a rigorous Delphi process and objective weighting methods, there is inherent subjectivity in indicator selection and weighting. Some criteria are difficult to measure quantitatively and were proxied by available indicators. In addition, the combined weighting strategy and maturity clustering may be sensitive to methodological choices. Future studies could further examine the robustness of maturity scores under alternative weighting and clustering approaches. Moreover, because CRITID relies on distance correlation, the stability of CRITID-based weights may vary with sample size, and further external evaluation is needed to assess its performance in smaller or more heterogeneous MCDM applications. Fourth, the discrepancies between combined weights and RBD-FAST sensitivity rankings indicate that some indicators may be conceptually important but empirically less influential in explaining score variation, whereas others may show high sensitivity because their variation overlaps with broader changes in the maturity score. Future studies could further examine indicator redundancy and the stability of the weighting structure using alternative validation approaches. Fifth, a portion of our data relies on self-reported survey responses by hospitals, which might introduce reporting bias or inconsistencies in interpretation of questions. We attempted to mitigate this through standardized definitions and rigorous data cleaning, but some degree of measurement error may remain. Sixth, the expert panel was composed largely of hospital-affiliated information management professionals. This composition was appropriate for assessing hospital digital maturity from an operational and technical perspective, but it may under-represent the views of government regulators, academic researchers, frontline clinicians, patients, and women. Future studies should further validate and refine the framework using a more diverse stakeholder panel. Finally, our clustering and regression analyses identify associations rather than causation. The present framework should therefore be interpreted as a structure- and process-oriented diagnostic tool for hospital digital transformation, rather than as a direct measure of healthcare quality, safety, efficiency, or patient experience. Future research should externally validate the maturity score against these outcome measures to examine whether higher digital maturity translates into measurable improvements in hospital performance and patient-centered care.

Conclusion

This study developed and empirically applied a digital maturity evaluation framework tailored to Chinese tertiary public hospitals. The framework integrates digital readiness, technology application, and data management capability, and combines expert-informed indicator development with data-driven weighting using national survey data. Our findings indicate that hospital digital maturity depends not only on infrastructure and technology adoption, but also on strategic planning, organizational readiness, workforce capacity, and data governance. The framework can support policy monitoring, institutional benchmarking, and targeted improvement of hospital digital transformation. Although outcome validation was beyond the scope of the current study, future work should investigate whether higher digital maturity scores are associated with measurable improvements in healthcare quality, patient safety, operational efficiency, patient experience, and health equity.
