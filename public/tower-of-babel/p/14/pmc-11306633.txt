Identification of genetic subtypes in follicular lymphoma
Authors: Victoria Shelton, Rajesh Detroja, Ting Liu, Keren Isaev, Anjali Silva, Verena Passerini, Mehran Bakhtiari, Lourdes Calvente, Michael Hong, Michael Y He, Saloni Modi, Samantha A Hershenfeld, Maja Ludvigsen, Charlotte Madsen, Stephen Hamilton-Dutoit, Francesco Annibale d’Amore, Marianne Brodtkorb, Nathalie A Johnson, Tara Baetz, David LeBrun, Josh W D Tobin, Maher K Gandhi, Andrew J Mungall, Wei Xu, Susana Ben-Neriah, Christian Steidl, Jan Delabie, Rosemarie Tremblay-LeMay, Opeyemi Jegede, Oliver Weigert, Brad Kahl, Andrew M Evens, Robert Kridel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollicular lymphoma (FL) exhibits considerable variability in biological features and clinical trajectories across patients. To dissect the diversity of FL, we utilized a Bernoulli mixture model to identify genetic subtypes in 713 pre-treatment tumor tissue samples. Our analysis revealed the existence of five subtypes with unique genetic profiles that correlated with clinicopathological characteristics. The clusters were enriched in specific mutations as follows: CS (CREBBP and STAT6), TT (TNFAIP3 and TP53), GM (GNA13 and MEF2B), Q (quiescent, for low mutation burden), and AR (mutations of mTOR pathway-related genes). The subtype Q was enriched for patients with stage I disease and associated with a lower proliferative history than the other subtypes. The AR subtype was unique in its enrichment for IgM-expressing FL cases and was associated with advanced-stage and more than 4 nodal sites. The existence of subtypes was validated in an independent cohort of 418 samples from the GALLIUM trial. Notably, patients assigned to the TT subtype consistently experienced inferior progression-free survival when treated with immunochemotherapy. Our findings offer insight into core pathways distinctly linked with each FL cluster and are expected to be informative in the era of targeted therapies. Subject terms: Cancer genomics, B-cell lymphoma, Translational research, B-cell lymphoma

Introduction

Follicular lymphoma (FL) is the most common indolent non-Hodgkin lymphoma [1]. It is generally characterized by a slowly progressive course with a continual remitting and relapsing disease cycle, typically persisting as a largely incurable malignancy. Patient outcomes are strikingly heterogeneous with, on the one extreme, a subset of patients that never require treatment. In contrast, others are at an increased risk of death from early progression and/or transformation to aggressive lymphoma. Approximately 15–20% of patients experience early disease progression after immunochemotherapy, which translates into inferior overall survival [2–5]. The advent of accurate molecular profiling has enabled the development of biomarkers incorporating information on gene mutation status [6, 7], gene expression changes [8–10], and the tumor microenvironment [11–14]. Nonetheless, treatment decisions in FL are not presently based on molecular characteristics and remain largely agnostic concerning the underlying tumor biology, a major impediment to precision medicine approaches and improving patient outcomes [15].

Over the last two decades, large-scale, unbiased molecular profiling has allowed the subtyping of many cancers into clinically relevant subgroups [16, 17]. The molecular classification of cancers has now become an essential step toward the clinical practice of precision medicine. For example, consensus clustering of genetic alterations such as gene mutations, chromosomal rearrangements, and somatic copy number alterations has uncovered distinct subsets of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) [18–21]. In contrast, no definitive molecular subtyping has been adopted in FL. While early reports indicated FL to be a biologically homogeneous disease [22], recent studies reveal remarkable inter-patient tumor molecular heterogeneity at multiple levels, spanning the spectrum of genetic, epigenetic, and immune microenvironment changes [23]. However, a definitive classification system has not yet been established. In this study, we dissect the heterogeneity of FL by utilizing genomic, transcriptomic, and methylomic approaches to identify fundamental properties that underlie biological and clinical distinctions in these tumors.

Methods

Patient cohorts for targeted DNA sequencing

We adopted an inclusive approach, aiming to enroll primary FL patient samples from both population-based cohorts and clinical trial series, and patients with diverse clinical presentations. To identify genetic clusters, we accrued a cohort of 713 cases (after quality control) from the following sources (“Cluster Development Cohort”, Fig. 1A and Suppl. Table 1):

A retrospective, multicenter cohort of patients with limited-stage disease, who were treated with radiation only, or with advanced-stage disease, treated with immunochemotherapy, accrued from Toronto, Montreal, Kingston, Aarhus, Oslo, and Brisbane (referred to as the “multicenter cohort”, n = 203);

A retrospective, multicenter cohort of patients with limited-stage disease, who were treated with radiation only, or with advanced-stage disease, treated with immunochemotherapy, accrued from Toronto, Montreal, Kingston, Aarhus, Oslo, and Brisbane (referred to as the “multicenter cohort”, n = 203);

A retrospective cohort of patients from existing data from a published study in which treatments were heterogeneous (referred to as the “PLOSMED cohort”, n = 242) [24];

A retrospective cohort of patients from existing data from a published study in which treatments were heterogeneous (referred to as the “PLOSMED cohort”, n = 242) [24];

The E2408 trial (NCT01216683), a randomized phase II trial in which high-risk untreated patients received induction therapy with bendamustine and rituximab, with or without bortezomib, followed by maintenance with rituximab, with or without lenalidomide (n = 83) [25];

The E4402 trial (NCT00075946), a randomized phase III trial in which patients with low-tumor burden were treated with single-agent rituximab, followed by rituximab maintenance or re-treatment, as needed (n = 185) [26].

Subsequently, a separate cohort of 418 cases from the GALLIUM trial [27] was made available to validate clustering findings and outcome correlations. Additionally, 116 PLOSMED cohort [24] cases with both FL and subsequent transformed FL samples were used to predict genetic cluster assignments in transformed FL samples for comparison with their FL cluster assignments.

All samples underwent expert hematopathology review at the respective institutions, except for the clinical trial series that underwent centralized pathology review. FL grades 1, 2, and 3A were included, whereas FL grade 3B was excluded.

Sample accrual and targeted DNA sequencing

The consort flow diagram in Suppl. Fig. 1 shows the disposition of samples. Formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue blocks from 731 primary FLs were accrued from University Health Network (UHN), collaborating institutions and ECOG-ACRIN. Twenty-one extracted DNA samples from FFPE were accrued from Brisbane. Of these, 589 samples were submitted for targeted, hybridization-based DNA sequencing. Additionally, we accessed 242 libraries from a published study (European Genome-Phenome Archive Accession number: EGAS00001001709), 170 and 72 of which had been generated from frozen tissue and FFPE samples, respectively. Two different gene panels were used (Suppl. Table 2), covering the entire coding sequence of 86 and 71 genes, respectively. Their intersection of 57 genes was used for all analyses described in this manuscript and covers frequently mutated genes in FL, as shown in our previous work [6, 24].

Detection of genetic variants

Single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions and deletions (indels) were called using Mutect2 (GATK v4.1.8.1) [28, 29]. Picard tools were utilized to assess sequencing coverage; samples with a mean on-target coverage of <50× were removed, leaving 713 samples for further analysis. Genetic variants were normalized, duplicate calls were removed, and the Genome Aggregation Database (GnomAD) was used to remove putative germline polymorphisms [30]. GATK FilterMutectCalls was used to filter raw mutation calls. Variants were annotated using Annovar (20180416) [31]. Complete details of variant calling, annotation, and filtering are provided in the Suppl. Methods.

Clustering and related analyses

Fifty-seven genetic features (Suppl. Table 3) were encoded as binary variables to identify clusters of patients with similar genetic attributes. Mutation co-occurrence or mutual exclusivity patterns were identified using the discoversomaticInteractions function from maftools [32]. Subtypes were identified by modeling the data as a finite mixture of Bernoulli distributions using the method described by Lacy et al. [21]. The number of identified subtypes was selected via the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) likelihood penalization method. Additional analyses were implemented to evaluate modeling outcomes. Firstly, bootstrap resampling was employed to determine the number of clusters; secondly, cluster stability was investigated by consensus clustering over several iterations; lastly, the most confident clustering solution was selected by ranking a set of clustering criteria (Suppl. Methods). The genetic subtypes of matched transformed FL samples (n = 116) were predicted using the developed finite mixture clustering model.

Clinical outcomes and statistical analyses

Kaplan–Meier survival and Cox proportional hazards model analyses were conducted. Log-rank tests were conducted to determine the significance of survival differences. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS). In the Cluster Development Cohort, PFS could be examined in 519 out of 713 patients from the multicenter, E2408, and PLOSMED cohorts. Additionally, we examined PFS in the 247 patients treated with immunochemotherapy and 117 patients treated with radiation for limited-stage presentation. Within the Cluster Development Cohort, we also explored the duration of response to rituximab in 134 patients from the E4402 trial. Overall survival was examined in 705 patients from the Cluster Development Cohort with available outcome data.

The time to transformation (TTT) of FL samples with matched transformed FL samples was explored between groups with identical subtype assignments and samples with differing subtype assignments before and after transformation. Kaplan-Meier analysis was employed to analyze TTT cumulative event differences between the two groups. The significance of cumulative event differences was calculated via a log-rank test.

Results

Somatic gene mutations in a combined cohort of pre-treatment FL samples

We accrued pre-treatment FL specimens from a multicenter retrospective cohort and two prospective frontline randomized clinical trials (E4402 and E2408) that were profiled with targeted DNA sequencing (Fig. 1A). Additionally, we included a previously reported dataset [24]. After uniform processing and quality control, targeted DNA sequencing data of 57 genes (Suppl. Table 3) were available for 713 pre-treatment FL samples (Suppl. Fig. 1 and Suppl. Table 1). The mean on-target coverage was 413× (Suppl. Table 4). Mutations are fully listed in Suppl. Table 5. There was no correlation between the coverage and the number of mutations detected per sample (Suppl. Fig. 2). The mean numbers of mutations and mutated genes were 5.7 (range 0–20) and 4.8 (range 0–14), respectively (including missense and nonsense SNVs, splice site mutations, and indels, but excluding synonymous variants). In 24 patients (3.4%), no coding mutations were detected, although 17 out of these 24 samples harbored non-coding mutations. KMT2D (63.4%) and CREBBP (57.5%) represented the most frequently mutated genes, followed by TNFRSF14 (34.4%), BCL2 (32.7%), EZH2 (20.6%), FOXO1 (14.8%) and STAT6 (13.1%) (Fig. 1B). The mutation frequency profile was similar among the sampled cohorts (Suppl. Fig. 3). We compared the frequency of gene mutations in the advanced (Ann Arbor III-IV) versus the limited-stage cases (Ann Arbor I-II) and found that KMT2D, BCL2, EP300 and HVCN1 mutations were significantly less common in the latter (Suppl. Fig. 4). Overall, these data aligned with the literature, indicating that our combined series accurately represents the broader FL population and was highly suitable for downstream analysis.

Finite mixture modeling reveals five distinct FL genetic subtypes

We further investigated the interactions between somatic mutations and identified both expected and unexpected patterns (Fig. 1C). The most significantly co-occurring mutations were GNA13/MEF2B (false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted p = 6.20 × 10−5) and HVCN1/MEF2B (FDR-adjusted p = 8.74 × 10−3), while significantly mutually exclusive mutations affected CARD11/EZH2 (FDR-adjusted p = 8.74 × 10−3) and EP300/CREBBP (FDR-adjusted p = 8.74 × 10−3). The existence of these patterns prompted us to pursue unsupervised clustering using finite mixture modeling. Applying the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and over 1,000 bootstrap resampling runs, the solutions with 5 or 6 clusters were most commonly identified (Suppl. Fig. 5A). We prioritized the 5-cluster solution as it was more parsimonious. For reference, stability metrics and the 2-, 3-, 4- and 6-cluster solutions are provided in Suppl. Fig. 5B and Suppl. Figs. 6–9, respectively. We chose the optimal 5-cluster solution through 100 mixture model iterations and selected the solution with the highest ranked score (Suppl. Table 6). The five genetic clusters identified in this optimal solution are henceforth referred to as subtypes (CS, TT, GM, Q, and AR) (Fig. 2A, Suppl. Table 7). Samples from all cohorts were represented in each subtype, with mild imbalances expected as the patient populations differed between the cohorts (e.g., limited-stage patients enriched in the multicenter cohort and in Q, see also next section) (Suppl. Fig. 10).

The CS subtype (n = 257) showed enrichment in gene mutations involving CREBBP, STAT6, and TNFRSF14. The TT subtype (n = 101) was characterized by mutations involving TNFAIP3, encoding a negative NF-κB regulator, with 28/34 (82%) predicted to disrupt protein sequence. Mutations of TP53 and MYD88 were also significantly enriched in the TT subtype. The GM subtype (n = 137) was characterized by mutations involving GNA13 and MEF2B. Samples from the Q subtype (n = 124) showed weak enrichment for HLA-DMB and NOTCH1 mutations. The AR subtype (n = 94) had variants in genes related to the mTOR signaling pathway (ATP6AP1, RRAGC, ATP6V1B2) and CTSS. Mutations of EZH2 were enriched in CS and GM (28 and 34%, respectively, vs. 15% in TT, 5% in Q and 7% in AR). Q samples had fewer mutations and mutated genes per sample, compared to other clusters, while GM samples had the highest mutation burden (Fig. 2B, C). We examined whether mutations occurred in somatic hypermutation (SHM) motifs (RGYW or WRCY) and found that GM had the highest percentage (12.5% of all mutations in this subtype) and number of mutations potentially attributable to SHM (Fig. 2D). This finding was consistent with the presence of SHM target genes being enriched in this subtype (BCL7A, BCL2, HIST1H1C, DTX1). Bootstrap consensus clustering indicated that the AR cluster had the highest stability, followed by Q, GM, CS, and TT (Fig. 2E). In a sensitivity analysis, we repeated clustering using a dataset comprised of only those cases with available t(14;18) annotation (n = 324). Enrichment for specific gene mutations with clusters was comparatively weak, although a cluster with mTOR pathway and CREBBP mutations was noted, as well as a cluster enriched for GNA13, MEF2B, and targets of SHM (Suppl. Fig. 11).

We repeated finite mixture modeling with the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) informing the model selection process. This approach yielded two genetically distinct clusters, one of which exhibited an enrichment of GNA13, MEF2B and HVCN1 mutations (C1), while the other was characterized by CREBBP, ATP6AP1, RRAGC, ATP6V1B2 and STAT6 mutations (C2) (Suppl. Fig. 12A). The majority of samples assigned to C1 (133/175, 76%) were assigned to the AIC GM subtype, while samples from C2 were almost exclusively attributed to the remaining AIC subtypes (534/538 99%; Suppl. Fig. 12B). Due to the only partial resolution of putative molecular dependencies by the BIC-informed clustering solution, we chose to focus our analysis on the five subtypes identified by AIC.

Clinico-pathological characterization of genetic subtypes

The genetically distinct subtypes prompted us to evaluate the differences in clinicopathological attributes among the clusters (Table 1). We found that FL grade 3A was most common in TT (21/86, 24%) (Fig. 3A), and infrequent in CS and Q (16/224, 7% and 10/114, 9%, respectively; chi-squared p < 0.001). Examining specific gene mutations, we found that mutations of B2M, TP53, HIST1H1B, KLHL6, and MYD88 were enriched in FL grade 3A, whereas CREBBP mutations were enriched in FL grade 1-2 (Suppl. Fig. 13). The presence of the t(14;18) translocation varied across the subtypes, with the lowest percentage in TT and Q (32/45, 71% and 30/46, 65%, respectively) and the highest in GM and AR (60/67, 90% and 37/44, 84%; chi-squared p = 0.016) (Fig. 3B). The genetic clusters also exhibited distinct clinical presentations, with Q strongly enriched in limited-stage and, specifically, Ann Arbor stage I cases (stage I: 43/123, 35%, Fig. 3C). In keeping with this observation, the percentage of cases with more than 4 nodal sites was lowest in Q (33/114, 29%). Advanced-stage disease was most common in the AR cluster (stage III-IV: 85/94, 90%; overall chi-squared p < 0.001), and this cluster also had the highest percentage of cases with more than 4 nodal sites (63/85, 74%; chi-squared p < 0.001), illustrating that lymphoma dissemination was a common characteristic in AR cases.

Outcome associations of genetic subtypes

The genetic subtypes showed divergent PFS in the overall cohort (log-rank p = 0.041; outcome data available for 519 patients, Fig. 3D). A modest, but statistically significant outcome association between genetic clusters and PFS was observed in patients treated with immunochemotherapy (log-rank p = 0.045; outcome data available for 247 patients, Fig. 3E). Cases from the TT cluster had the lowest 5-year PFS (45.2% vs. 66.0% for all other clusters, log-rank p = 0.008). Notably, progression of disease within 24 months (POD24) following immunochemotherapy was more frequent in patients from TT (15/37, 41%) compared to other subtypes (44/197, 22%) (chi-squared p = 0.033, Suppl. Fig. 14A, B). POD24 was associated with inferior OS in all genetic subtypes (Suppl. Fig. 14C). In a Cox multivariable regression model that adjusted for the FLIPI, the hazard ratio for progression or death was 1.8 (95% confidence interval 1.1–2.7, p = 0.014) when contrasting cases from TT against all other patients (Fig. 3F). When examining this outcome association within the FLIPI strata, TT was associated with inferior PFS in the FLIPI high-risk, but not low-intermediate-risk patients (Suppl. Fig. 15). There were no significant associations between the genetic subtypes and PFS in patients with limited-stage disease treated with involved-field radiation (outcome data available for 117 patients, Suppl. Fig. 16A), overall survival (data available for 705 patients, Suppl. Fig. 16B) or with the duration of response to rituximab (data available for 66 and 68 patients in the retreatment and maintenance rituximab arms of the E4402 trial [26], Suppl. Fig. 16C, D). Collectively, our findings highlight specific clinicopathological attributes associated with the genetic subtypes.

Evolutionary dynamics of the genetic subtypes over the course of transformation

The inclusion of 116 patients with matched FL-transformed FL sequencing data [24] provided insights into the stability of subtype assignments during transformation (Suppl. Tables 8, 9). Considering the established clinical and biological distinctions between transformed FL and preceding FL, cluster stability was not necessarily anticipated. Patients were split into two groups according to the calculated median TTT (2.97 years). Greater subtype stability was seen in patients with earlier transformation, especially in patients assigned to the TT or GM subtypes at diagnosis (Fig. 4A). Patients with later transformation exhibited a more marked divergence in cluster assignments between diagnosis and time of transformation, particularly for those initially assigned to Q and AR subtypes (Fig. 4B). Overall, subtype reassignment occurred in 61/116 (52.6%) cases and was associated with a longer TTT (p = 0.0014) (Fig. 4C), suggesting that clonal evolution over time leads to the emergence of clones with distinct genetic attributes at the time of transformation.

Prediction of the genetic subtypes in an external dataset

To verify the robustness of our clustering solution using an independent dataset, we applied the FlexMix model to data from the GALLIUM trial (NCT01332968) [27]. Mutation data were available for 418 patients (204 treated with rituximab-chemotherapy; 214 treated with obinutuzumab-chemotherapy, Suppl. Table 10). The resulting cluster map showed similarities to our previous findings, with CREBBP and STAT6 mutations enriched in CS, GNA13, and MEF2B mutations in GM and ATP6AP1 and RRAGC mutations in AR (Fig. 5A). TNFAIP3 and TP53 mutations remained associated with TT but their enrichment was less strong than in our original dataset. Notably, Q, which we discovered to be more prevalent in limited-stage FL, was relatively uncommon in the GALLIUM dataset. This observation is likely attributed to the fact that the GALLIUM trial only included previously untreated patients with symptomatic advanced-stage disease or bulky disease in need of systemic therapy. Next, we explored outcome correlations of genetic subtypes in GALLIUM. In line with our previous findings, TT was associated with unfavorable PFS (log-rank p = 0.027, Fig. 5B). In a multivariable Cox regression model that considered clinical risk factors, the chemotherapy backbone, and the type of anti-CD20 antibody, the hazard ratio for PFS was determined to be 1.6 for patients assigned to TT (95% confidence interval 1.05–2.4, p = 0.028, Fig. 5C). This finding confirmed the negative prognostic value of the TT subtype that we observed in our original dataset.

Genome-wide DNA methylation profiles in the genetic clusters

Since genome-wide methylation changes are commonly observed in cancer [33, 34], we utilized Infinium MethylationEPIC profiles to investigate cluster-specific methylation patterns. After quality control, we obtained data from 343 pre-treatment samples, including 328 FL, 5 reactive lymph node (RLN), and 10 DLBCL cases. Principal component analysis revealed that the RLN and DLBCL cases were separated by the first principal component, while the FL cases were dispersed across this component (Fig. 6A). Consistent with global hypomethylation representing a hallmark of cancer [33, 34], we found that DLBCL samples were hypomethylated compared to the RLN samples. While considering all CpGs, FL samples also demonstrated hypomethylation, although the Q subtype exhibited a global methylation level comparable to RLN and a lesser degree of hypomethylation when compared with other subtypes (Suppl. Fig. 17A, Fig. 6B). However, when specifically analyzing probes from CpG islands, a signal of hypermethylation was noticeable for all genetic subtypes, but was less pronounced for Q (Suppl. Fig. 17B, Fig. 6C). Applying epiCMIT (epigenetically-determined Cumulative MIToses) [35], a previously reported mitotic clock based on DNA methylation, FL and DLBCL were found to have higher scores, indicating a more protracted proliferation history compared to RLN cases (Fig. 6D). The epiCMIT varied among the FL clusters, with Q displaying a lower proliferative history than other subtypes (Fig. 6E). In accordance with limited-stage cases being enriched in Q (Fig. 3C), we found that epiCMIT scores increased with higher Ann Arbor stages (Fig. 6F). These findings collectively demonstrate that the subtypes exhibit distinct DNA methylation profiles that extend the inter-patient heterogeneity beyond differences in individual gene mutations.

Immunoglobulin isotype and somatic hypermutation analysis

The surface expression of either IgM (“non-switched FL”) or IgG/IgA (“switched FL”) is associated with distinct phenotypes, the burden of SHM and the reactivity of FL cells to the tumor microenvironment [36–38]. Aiming to identify dominant immunoglobulin isotype expression, we utilized RNA-seq data (n = 282 FL samples after quality control) to identify B-cell receptor sequences within the bulk transcriptome. Using TRUST4 [39], we identified a median of 916 IGH sequences per sample (interquartile range 543–1554) and a median of 332 dominant, clonal IGH sequences per sample (interquartile range 172–686). Constant chain calls were identified in 96.5% of all samples (272/282). Annotation of genetic subtypes with dominant IGH isotypes (n = 233 cases with both data points available) revealed differences between subtypes, with AR containing mostly IGHM-expressing cases (30/34, 88.2%), while the CS, TT, GM, and Q subtypes exhibited a higher prevalence of class-switched immunoglobulins (IGHG and IGHA) (Fig. 7A). To validate the prevalent expression of IGHM in AR, we conducted immunohistochemistry staining on tissue samples (Fig. 7B, C). IGHM expression was detected in 33 instances overall (out of 89 cases, 37%) and was more prevalent within the AR subtype (16 out of 20 cases, 80%, fisher p < 0.001). The variable gene identity to germline was slightly higher in the IGHM versus the IGHG-expressing cases (Fig. 7D), suggesting a lower SHM burden. However, on average, the variable gene identity remained low in nearly all IGHM-expressing cases, suggesting that FLs are universally germinal center-experienced. Comparing the variable gene identity between the five FL genetic subtypes, we found that samples from GM had the lowest similarity to germline (p = 0.0045, Fig. 7E), consistent with our previous observation of its high burden of SHM in genes from the targeted sequencing panel (Fig. 2D). Taken together, the analysis of immunoglobulin isotype expression revealed that the AR subtype is unique in the almost exclusive expression of IgM, perhaps suggesting a different ontology.

Discussion

This study revealed the presence of five genetic subtypes in FL, which exhibited differences across the analyzed levels. The CS subtype had frequent mutations in the epigenetic modifier CREBBP and STAT6 mutations. The TT subtype was characterized by mutations of NF-κB pathway members and TP53. It was associated with the shortest time to progression, in keeping with previous literature showing adverse outcome for TP53-mutated FL [6, 40, 41]. The most frequently mutated genes in subtype GM were GNA13 and MEF2B, and this subtype had overall the highest mutation burden, with evidence of higher loads of SHM within the targeted genes from our panel, and also the IGH variable regions. The Q subtype had the lowest mutation burden, was enriched for limited-stage cases, and had the lowest proliferative history. The AR subtype, characterized by mutations in mTOR pathway-related genes, was mainly composed of IGHM-expressing cases and was associated with advanced-stage. Interestingly, we observed frequent subtype switching with transformation, particularly in the case of late transformation, underscoring the clinical need and translational utility to obtain repeat histological samples at clinical inflection points. Subtype switching may reflect the outgrowth of distinct clones that may have pre-existed or acquired mutations over time.

Unlike DLBCL, which has well-established molecular subtypes supported by multiple studies [18, 19, 21], the classification of FL remains relatively less defined. Several studies have contributed to our understanding of the heterogeneity among FL patients. Koues et al. [42] reported that distinct subtypes can be recognized in FL based on the epigenome, with subtype 1 resembling normal centrocytes and subtype 2 resembling in vitro-activated peripheral blood B-cells. Crouch et al. [43] applied a targeted sequencing panel to 548 FL samples from the Haematological Malignancy Research Network in the UK. They found three clusters: FL_aSHM, defined by high levels of SHM; FL_STAT6, defined by frequent STAT and CREBBP mutations; and the remaining cases, referred to as FL_Com, often harboring KMT2D mutations. The FL_aSHM likely overlaps with our GM subtype, and the FL_STAT6 resembles the CS subtype. Their gene panel did not include mTOR pathway-related genes; hence, the authors could not identify the AR subtype. Dreval et al. [44] identified a constrained subgroup of FL that has a low rate of somatic hypermutation and is enriched for mTOR pathway-related gene mutations; hence, it may overlap with the AR subtype we identified. Conversely, the GM subtype resembles the DLBCL-like FL group due to more mutations falling into SHM motifs. Additional studies based on single-cell analysis have revealed insights into how specific mature B-cell transcription profiles relate to inter- and intra-patient heterogeneity. Wang et al. [36] demonstrated the existence of at least two phenotypically distinct subtypes in FL, one composed predominantly of cells resembling normal GC-like cells, and one resembling memory B-cells. Moreover, although the immune composition has long been recognized to modulate patient outcomes [11, 12], variations of the immune contexture have recently been proposed to categorize FL into several tumor microenvironmental subtypes [14]. Lastly, identifying genetic clusters may represent an orthogonal approach to understanding the heterogeneity of FL, in addition to prognostic indices based on gene mutations (e.g., m7-FLIPI) or gene expression signatures (e.g., 23 gene expression score) [6, 8].

We performed clustering analysis on a large cohort of FL samples without any predetermined assumptions about the expected clusters. Nonetheless, it is interesting to compare our clusters with those described in DLBCL, bearing in mind that the clinical and molecular phenotypes of FL and DLBCL differ. The EZB/C3/BCL2 subtype observed in DLBCL [18, 19, 21] shows similarities to our CS and GM subtypes. However, we identified contrasts between these clusters, primarily due to the separation of GNA13/MEF2B mutations from STAT6/CREBBP mutations. It is less straightforward to align our remaining FL clusters with those seen in DLBCL, although frequent TNFAIP3 mutations in the TT subtype might suggest some similarity with the DLBCL BN2 cluster described by Schmitz et al. [19] In contrast, mutations in genes involved in mTOR signaling appear pathognomonic for FL [45], and, to the best of our knowledge, a subtype defined by such mutations has not been reported in DLBCL.

Our study reveals the presence of five genetic subtypes within FL, showcasing differences in gene mutations, DNA methylation profiles, and patient outcomes. Our work has several limitations, such as the focused design of the sequencing panel and the exclusive reliance on gene mutations for clustering. Additionally, annotation with t(14;18) translocation status was available for only a subset of the cases included in our study, and we could not incorporate this information into the cluster identification steps. Moreover, our cluster development cohort was heterogeneous and outcome associations should continue to be explored in future studies to assess the prognostic impact of genetic subtypes in FL, especially as the therapeutic landscape is rapidly expanding. Yet, our findings contribute to a deeper comprehension of the molecular and clinical heterogeneity of FL and can potentially guide the development of subtype-specific treatment strategies, ultimately improving patient outcomes. As an illustration, one can conjecture that mTOR inhibitors may demonstrate efficacy in the AR subtype, while epigenetic modulation could be well-suited for the CS and GM subtypes. Conversely, patients categorized into the TT subtype might derive benefit from chemo-free therapeutic strategies. Future studies are needed to test the benefit of targeted and other novel therapies in specific genetic backgrounds of FL.
