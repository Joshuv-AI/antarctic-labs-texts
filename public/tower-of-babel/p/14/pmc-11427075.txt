CMFHMDA: a prediction framework for human disease-microbe associations based on cross-domain matrix factorization
Authors: Jing Chen, Ran Tao, Yi Qiu, Qun Yuan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPredicting associations between microbes and diseases opens up new avenues for developing diagnostic, preventive, and therapeutic strategies. Given that laboratory-based biological tests to verify these associations are often time-consuming and expensive, there is a critical need for innovative computational frameworks to predict new microbe–disease associations. In this work, we introduce a novel prediction algorithm called Predicting Human Disease-Microbe Associations using Cross-Domain Matrix Factorization (CMFHMDA). Initially, we calculate the composite similarity of diseases and the Gaussian interaction profile similarity of microbes. We then apply the Weighted K Nearest Known Neighbors (WKNKN) algorithm to refine the microbe–disease association matrix. Our CMFHMDA model is subsequently developed by integrating the network data of both microbes and diseases to predict potential associations. The key innovations of this method include using the WKNKN algorithm to preprocess missing values in the association matrix and incorporating cross-domain information from microbes and diseases into the CMFHMDA model. To validate CMFHMDA, we employed three different cross-validation techniques to evaluate the model’s accuracy. The results indicate that the CMFHMDA model achieved Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve scores of 0.9172, 0.8551, and 0.9351\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\pm $\end{document}0.0052 in global Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV), local LOOCV, and five-fold CV, respectively. Furthermore, many predicted associations have been confirmed by published experimental studies, establishing CMFHMDA as an effective tool for predicting potential disease-associated microbes.

Introduction

The channels through which the human body communicates with the outside world and the surface of the body are colonized by a large number of microbial communities. The human microbe refers to all microbes that coexist with humans and cause disease, including prokaryotic microbes, viruses, eukaryotic microbes, and the sum of their genetic information. Microbes are critical to human health and influence physiological and pathological processes. These microbes, found in substantial numbers in areas like the gut [1], skin [2], and lungs [3], outnumber human cells [4], underscoring their significant role. Beyond their mere existence, they actively participate in essential biological functions, including nutrient synthesis and immune system modulation [5].

Recent advances in microbe research have elucidated the profound impact of gut microbiota imbalances on various diseases, revealing a complex interplay between our microbial inhabitants and overall health. Microbes influence host health through mechanisms like nutrient metabolism, production of bioactive compounds, and modulation of the host’s immune response [6–11]. The imbalance of intestinal microbiota is associated with tumors, metabolic system diseases, etc. The intestinal microbiota can decompose food components indigestible by the human body, generating metabolites, which are crucial for maintaining intestinal health. Dysbiosis may lead to indigestion, inflammatory bowel disease, autoimmune diseases, and other diseases [12–14]. Beyond gastrointestinal disorders, alterations have been linked to systemic conditions such as obesity and metabolic syndrome, highlighting a broader influence on metabolic processes [15]. In addition, the microbial community on the skin is crucial for maintaining the skin barrier function and defending against foreign pathogen invasion. Skin flora imbalance is associated with skin diseases such as eczema and psoriasis. Intriguingly, emerging evidence also suggests a significant connection between gut microbiota and mental health, including conditions like depression and anxiety, underscoring the gut-brain axis’s role in neuropsychiatric disorders [16–19]. The microbe influences the host’s immune response, thereby being linked to autoimmune diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA) [20]. These associations emphasize that the gut microbiota is crucial for both health and disease, necessitating a deeper exploration of its mechanisms and therapeutic potential. Despite some documented interactions in databases like Human Microbe-Disease Association Database (HMDAD) [21], the complete extent of these complex relationships, driven by multifaceted microbial mechanisms, remains largely uncharted, emphasizing the need for advanced computational models to further elucidate them.

The traditional method of revealing the association between microbes and diseases based on biological experiments is inefficient. Several specialized databases focusing on specific microbial genome information have been developed [22, 23].The established computational models primarily categorized into the following approaches: path-based methods, bipartite local models, ensemble learning, and so on [14, 24–27]. The path-based method utilizes path information between three networks: microbe–disease association matrix, microbe similarity matrix, and disease similarity matrix. Such methods commonly measure the weight of potential pathways as fractions of undiscovered associations by taking into account indirect paths within the network. Chen et al. developed the KATZ measurement model (KATZHMDA) for predicting potential associations between microbes and diseases using the KATZ centrality metric [28]. The KATHMDA model integrates known associations and employs Gaussian interaction profile kernel similarity to predict new microbe–disease associations on a large scale.

ABHMDA is a typical ensemble learning-based method that employs adaptive reinforcement for ensemble learning [29]. In the study of Peng et al., they adopted a strategy of training multiple classifiers (basic classifiers) on a consistent training set and aggregating these classifiers trained in different proportions. Build a better-performing ensemble classifier to evaluate and rank samples. Considering the lack of detailed feature information and attribute tags, ABHMDA adopts a method to fuse the microbial similarity of the Gaussian interaction profile core with the symptom-based disease similarity to construct a feature set of training samples. ABHMDA behaves well in the case of data scarcity.

The bipartite local model calculates prediction scores by evaluating the correlation between diseases and microbes. The final score of this type of model is obtained by fusing the prediction scores from these two aspects. Wang et al. developed an innovative computing method named LRLSHMDA [30, 31]. LRLSHMDA designs two objective functions, one for microbes and the other for diseases, and minimizes these functions using Laplacian regularization. Given the scarcity of negative samples (i.e. microbes not associated with a specific disease), LRLSHMDA adopts a semi-supervised learning method. The model effectively utilizes the known structural information, such as nodes and edges, within the associated network [21].

The above works have made some progress in predicting microbe–disease association. In path-based methods, sparse data will lead to few paths linked to isolated nodes, so these nodes cannot be accurately identified. The path-based method is a more intuitive method. Random walk methods pay more attention to the probable destinations a walker might reach, while path-based methods concentrate on the specifics of all possible paths [32–39]. However, paths between homogeneous networks using computational correlation-based similarity measures are still not reliable enough. When using path-based methods, more attention should be given to new sources of similarity and measures to establish more effective isomorphic paths. In contrast, bipartite local models cover a wide range of approaches based on different ideas. LRLSHMDA uses similarity information between diseases and microbes to provide satisfactory results. However, the aforementioned models also have certain limitations. The KATZHMDA model calculates the likelihood of associations between diseases and microbes through a scoring function. Most scoring function-based models are not well-suited for predicting associations involving new diseases. Additionally, such models often rely on assumptions about probability distributions, and when these assumptions do not align with the actual data, the prediction accuracy can be compromised. In the ABHMDA model, after classification by a weak classifier, the sample weights are updated, which increases the model’s sensitivity to data noise. Similar to some scoring function-based models, the LRLSHMDA model relies solely on Gaussian interaction distribution kernel similarity for predictions, limiting its applicability to predicting microbes potentially associated with new diseases. Moreover, the issue of determining optimal parameter values in these machine learning-based models remains unresolved. [27].

In various correlation prediction problems, perform matrix decomposition on the correlation matrix, and the two low-dimensional matrices obtained represent the characteristics of the two parties of the correlation respectively. While predicting microbe–disease associations, one of them represents the characteristics of the disease, the other represents the characteristics of microbes. Multiplying the two low-dimensional matrices yields the final predicted correlation. [40–47]. Based on the linear combination of potential features, matrix factorization methods can often reveal hidden features behind the relationships between objects. For example, a typical application of matrix factorization based on neural network (MFNN) kernel model is to predict the interaction between unknown circular RNAs and RNA binding proteins [48]. Furthermore, similarity-constrained matrix factorization was utilized to reveal drugs that may be related to diseases [49, 50].

Inspired by the meaningful work of Wang et al. [51–57], we propose an innovative prediction algorithm named Cross-Domain Matrix Factorization for Predicting Human Microbe-Disease Associations (CMFHMDA). Specifically,CMFHMDA first integrates human microbe and disease information to construct a human microbe–disease heterogeneous graph. Based on this, we construct the Gaussian interaction spectrum kernel similarity of microbes and diseases to indicates the similarities between different microbes and diseases, this may lead to better performance of CMFHMDA in predicting microbes that may be associated with new diseases [58]. Since the current human microbe–disease association information is incomplete, we adopted a preprocessing method named Weighted K Nearest Known Neighbors (WKNKN) [59, 60], converting 0 in the human microbe–disease association matrix replaced with a value range from 0 to 1. We introduced the processed microbe–disease association matrix, integrated disease similarity matrix, and microbe similarity matrix into CMFHMDA. Global Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV) and local LOOCV are employed to assess the prediction ability of CMFHMDA. The results show that the AUC (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve) value of global LOOCV and local LOOCV reach 0.9172 and 0.8551 respectively. Besides, we performed case studies on three diseases using CMFHMDA. Among the top 10 microbes predicted to be most likely to be associated with RA, Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and Ulcerative colitis (UC), all prediction results were validated by relevant literature or HMDAD, respectively.

Results

Performance evaluation

To assess the predictive ability of CMFHMDA, we conducted global, local LOOCV, and five-fold CV on our model using the HMDAD database. In the global LOOCV, each of the 450 confirmed association instances (positive instances) was sequentially held out as a validation instance, utilizing the remaining 449 for model development. Samples with unconfirmed associations were treated as potential candidates (unknown instances). In global LOOCV, the validation instance was ranked against all potential candidates based on the model’s scoring, whereas in local LOOCV, the validation instance was ranked against candidates sharing the same disease, creating a more focused comparison. We gauged the models’ predictive accuracy using AUC metric from the LOOCV. This allowed us to plot a ROC curve, which is a plot of points for various thresholds, from which the AUC is derived. In the five-fold CV, the confirmed microbe–disease associations were randomly divided into five equal subsets. Each subset was used in turn as a test set, while the remaining four subsets formed the training set, mirroring the global validation approach by considering all unknown associations as potential candidates. The CMFHMDA algorithm assigned scores to each test and candidate instance, enabling us to rank the test instances by sorting their scores in descending order. The AUC was then calculated to assess the performance of CMFHMDA. This five-fold CV process was repeated 100 times, and the average AUC was used to gauge the predictive performance of the CMFHMDA. An AUC value > 0.5 but <1 indicates the model’s predictive strength, with higher values denoting superior performance.

Case study

To verify the ability of the CMFHMDA model, we conducted the case studies. Initially, all samples associated with a specific disease were categorized, and literature searches were conducted to confirm the prediction results.

In the first case study, we focused on RA and IBD, evaluating the alignment of the predicted microbes associated with each condition as predicted by the CMFHMDA model with existing scholarly sources. Studies have detected bacterial DNA and peptidoglycans in the joints of RA patients, suggesting a possible connection between the persistent presence of bacteria or their antigens and the disease’s pathogenesis, as referenced in PubMed [63, 64]. The CMFHMDA model’s predictions for RA-related microbes were corroborated by empirical research documented in PubMed (Table 4). The etiology of RA has been linked to upper urinary tract infections caused by Proteus mirabilis, it is recommended that RA patients receive anti-Proteus antibiotics and biologics promptly to prevent irreversible joint damage [65]. Marazzato et al. observed a higher prevalence of Blautia from the Lachnospiraceae family in RA patients compared to healthy individuals [66]. Vitor et al. identified Mycobacterium leprae as a primary actinomycete pathogen, with delayed leprosy diagnosis potentially leading to irreversible joint damage [67]. Simona et al., in their latest analysis, concluded that specific probiotic supplements exert anti-inflammatory effects, aiding RA patients in improving daily functioning and reducing symptoms [68]. Nguyen et al. discovered that the absence of the T-cell death-related gene 8 (TDAG8) in a murine model of RA significantly mitigated disease severity and pain [69].

Discussion

The connection between microbes and the well-being of humans holds immense importance in the domain of health science studies. Grasping the nuanced interactions between these tiny organisms and our physiology is vital for the creation of innovative treatment plans and the strengthening of community health initiatives. In our study, we have introduced a groundbreaking computational technique, referred to as CMFHMDA, which is aimed at forecasting possible links between microbes and diseases. This methodology is bifurcated into a pair of stages. Initially, we determined the composite similarity of diseases and the similarity of the Gaussian interaction profiles of microbes. The WKNKN algorithm is then applied to refine the matrix that correlates microbes with diseases. Subsequently, our CMFHMDA model is formulated by integrating the cross-domain information of microbe and disease to forecast potential associations between them. Furthermore, we have employed three separate CV techniques to assess the precision of the CMFHMDA model. The outcomes demonstrate that the CMFHMDA model has achieved AUC scores of 0.9172, 0.8551, and 0.9351\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\pm $\end{document}0.0052 in global LOOCV, local LOOCV, and five-fold CV, respectively. To sum up, the CMFHMDA model has demonstrated a higher level of accuracy in comparison to previous computational models that have been considered cutting-edge in this field. Additionally, we conducted two types of case studies: the first to predict a comprehensive range of potential microbe–disease connections, and the second focused on a specific condition known as UC, from which known associations were excluded to test the predictive capabilities of CMFHMDA. Overall, the findings from both case studies indicate that CMFHMDA has achieved a dependable level of predictive performance. CMFHMDA can identify the latent parallels between diseases and microbes by employing the Gaussian interaction profile kernel technique to extract their similarities. This approach not only proves effective for predicting microbe–disease associations but can also be applied to other biological relationships, such as predicting disease–drug associations.

The high-fidelity forecasting capacity of the CMFHMDA is credited to several key factors. To begin with, the data sets that our model employed were of a high degree of dependability. Subsequently, we identified the latent parallels between diseases and microbes by employing the Gaussian interaction profile kernel technique to extract their similarities. Moreover, the WKNKN pre-treatment process was adeptly applied to manage the extensive gaps in the association data by amalgamating the relational context of neighboring microbes and diseases, a step that was pivotal for the predictive modeling phase. In addition, this approach fuses the knowledge of human microbe–disease interactions to build a heterogeneous graph that encompasses the human microbe–disease network.

However, certain constraints persist that may impact the efficacy of the CMFHMDA. For instance, the absence of efficient strategy to determine the parameters for CMFHMDA presents a challenge. Additionally, the model’s efficacy is compromised by the limited collection of confirmed microbe–disease interactions, resulting from an inadequate number of training instances. Given the severity and high mortality rate of cancer, establishing a database of cancer-microbe associations is essential. Specific microbes may influence tumor growth by modulating the host immune system. Predicting and validating the associations between microbes and cancer can provide a scientific basis for cancer prevention. Should a more extensive array of microbe–disease relationships be empirically validated in the future, it is anticipated that the prognostic accuracy of CMFHMDA will be enhanced. While the CMFHMDA model is capable of identifying linear relationships, it falls short in precisely representing the complex, nonlinear interactions. Finally, when determining the composite similarity of diseases and the similarity of the Gaussian interaction profiles of microbes, the selection of features and the allocation of weights may effect the final prediction model. The selection of features needs to be based on a deep understanding of biology, otherwise key information may be overlooked. In addition, trained models may have insufficient generalization ability on new data, especially when new variants of diseases or microbes appear. Using more complex models to integrate information from multiple sources and collaborating with experimental biologists for biological validation to confirm the effectiveness of prediction results may help ensure the high accuracy and robustness of CMFHMDA.
