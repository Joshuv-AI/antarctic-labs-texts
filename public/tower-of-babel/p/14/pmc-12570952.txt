Mediating role of inspiratory muscle strength in the relationship between abdominal obesity and lung function in young individuals: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Cui Wang, Zeng Wen, Yetian Liang, Minghao Pan, Shaomei Shang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the mediating role of inspiratory muscle strength between abdominal obesity and lung function in young people.DesignCross-sectional study.SettingThis survey was conducted in Peking University Health Science Center, Beijing, China.Participants404 subjects aged 18 and older.Main outcome measuresForced expiratory volume in one second percent (FEV1%) predicted ≥80% was defined as the normal lung function.ResultsA total of 404 subjects were included, with maximal inspiratory pressure of 65.27±29.55 cmH2O. Among them, 61 subjects (15.10%) had central obesity, 106 subjects (26.24%) had a FEV1% predicted <80%. Abdominal obesity was significantly associated with lung function (OR=5.356, 95% CI 2.391 to 11.999, p<0.001) and inspiratory muscle strength (OR=3.537, 95% CI 1.441 to 8.679, p=0.006). Even after adjusting for inspiratory muscle strength, the relationship between abdominal obesity and lung function remained significant (OR=4.723, 95% CI 2.076 to 10.749, p<0.001), and there was a significant association between inspiratory muscle strength and lung function (OR=0.322, 95% CI 0.176 to 0.591, p<0.001). The z-test confirmed that inspiratory muscle strength mediated the relationship between abdominal obesity and lung function (|z|=2.14, p<0.05).ConclusionThis study highlighted the mediating role of inspiratory muscle strength between abdominal obesity and lung function.

Introduction

Obesity is a growing global health epidemic,1 with well-established links to metabolic and cardiovascular diseases. Accumulating evidence further indicates its adverse effects on respiratory health, particularly lung function.2 3 Abdominal obesity, characterised by visceral fat accumulation, may impair respiratory mechanics by mechanically restricting lung expansion. This is clinically significant, as even mild lung function impairment can increase the risk of respiratory diseases, reduce quality of life and elevate mortality—for instance, severe impairment is associated with a 2.54-fold higher mortality risk.4

Abdominal obesity has been linked to reduced lung function in multiple studies.2 3 For instance, Pan et al reported that central obesity was associated with decreased lung function and a higher risk of restrictive respiratory defects in older Chinese adults,2 while Gol and Rafraf observed a similar negative association in healthy adults.3 Despite these consistent findings, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Inspiratory muscle strength, which is critical for maintaining lung function through its action on chest expansion and diaphragmatic contraction,5 represents a potential mediator. This strength, primarily dependent on the diaphragm, is known to decline with age.2 3 Although extensively studied in lung diseases like chronic obstructive pulmonary disease,6 its role in healthy adults associated with obesity is less clear. While thoracic and abdominal fat deposition could theoretically impede diaphragmatic movement and reduce muscle efficiency,7 empirical evidence remains conflicting.8 Therefore, it is still unclear whether inspiratory muscle strength mediates the relationship between abdominal obesity and lung function, warranting further investigation.

Aims

This study aims to assess the association between abdominal obesity and lung function, and to explore the potential mediating role of inspiratory muscle strength in this relationship.

Methods

Study design and participants

This investigation employed a cross-sectional design to examine young adults from 20 schools within a university health science centre between July and October 2022. Eligible participants were aged 18–30 years and voluntarily completed both a health questionnaire and physiological assessments. Exclusion criteria focused on underlying health conditions that might confound respiratory measurements, including chronic cardiorespiratory diseases, acute respiratory infections within the past 6 months, and regular engagement in structured physical or respiratory training programmes. This study was reported according to the STROBE Checklist (https://www.strobe-statement.org/checklists/).

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University Health Science Center (IRB00001052-22073) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Dependent variable

Lung function was assessed by the percent predicted forced expiratory volume in one second percent (FEV1% predicted). This metric provides a comprehensive evaluation of airway obstruction by integrating both flow and volume, and it is a standard index for grading severity9 10 and predicting mortality.11 Its relevance is further underscored by established negative associations with abdominal obesity.12 13 Measurements were obtained using a portable spirometer (Suzhou, China) that was calibrated daily according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following the American Thoracic Society (ATS) and the European Respiratory Society (ERS) 2019 statement,14 patient maintain an upright sitting position with the head slightly elevated, securely place the mouthpiece in the mouth and wear a nose clip to prevent nasal breathing. The manoeuvre consists of inspiring rapidly until the lungs are completely full, exhaling with maximal effort until fully empty. The highest value from manoeuvres meeting the ATS/ERS acceptability and reproducibility criteria was recorded. According to the ATS/ERS guidelines, certain activities should be avoided prior to lung function testing, including vigorous exercise, smoking, vaping or water pipe use within 1 hour of the test.14 Spirometric results were interpreted using the Global Lung Function Initiative 2012 reference equations. The predicted normal values and the lower limit of normal (LLN) for FEV1% were calculated for each participant based on their age, height, sex and ethnicity. Participants were then classified into a normal lung function group (FEV1%≥LLN) and an abnormal lung function group (FEV1%<LLN).

Independent variable

Abdominal obesity was assessed via waist circumference (WC, cm) using tape. We used WC ≥85 cm in males and ≥90 cm in females as the abdominal obesity criteria.15 WC (cm) was categorised into two groups: ≥90/85 cm and <90/85 cm in men/women. In the current study, body mass index (BMI) was not used to define abdominal obesity, because BMI relied on weight and height measurements16 17; consequently, it may not be an ideal indicator of abdominal obesity, particularly in males with higher muscle mass.18 Additionally, BMI failed to accurately assess the relationship between abdominal obesity and associated diseases, as it does not account for variations in body fat distribution.19 20

Mediating variable

Inspiratory muscle strength was assessed by measuring maximal inspiratory pressure (MIP) in accordance with the ATS/ERS technical standards.14 Measurements were obtained using the PowerBreathe K-5 device (Technologies Ltd, Birmingham, UK), a handheld, non-invasive mouth-pressure manometer equipped with a rubber-flanged mouthpiece and connected to Breathe-Link Live Feedback software (V.2.3) for real-time monitoring and data acquisition.2123 A minimum of six acceptable manoeuvres (ie, without air leaks), three with variability <10% (repeatability criteria), were performed. The highest value of three reproducible manoeuvres was selected. The device was calibrated before each testing session according to manufacturer specifications. Certain activities should be avoided prior to testing, including vigorous exercise, smoking, vaping or water pipe use within 1 hour of the test.14 According to Lista-Paz et al., the cut-off for respiratory muscle weakness was defined as a T-score of ≥2.5 SD below the average peak pressure achieved in a healthy young population. Based on this criterion, participants were categorised into two groups: normal inspiratory muscle strength group (T-score <2.5 SD, indicating respiratory muscle weakness) and abnormal inspiratory muscle strength group (T-score ≥2.5 SD).24

Covariates

Survey items

The self-administered questionnaire collected data on sex, age, residence (urban/rural), height, smoking status (never, former, current), drinking status (never, former, current) and physical activity levels.

Physical activity levels were evaluated using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire Short Version.25 This instrument captures the frequency, duration and intensity of activities across life domains—including transportation, occupation, household tasks and leisure—over a typical week. Activity intensities were classified as light (3.3 metabolic equivalents (METs)), moderate (4.0 METs) or vigorous (8.0 METs). Total weekly energy expenditure, expressed in MET-minutes, was calculated by summing the products of duration and MET values for each activity.25

Anthropometric measurements

Anthropometric measurements, including body weight and trunk muscle mass, were obtained using a multifrequency tetrapolar bioelectrical impedance analysis instrument (Inbody-770, Biospace Co., Korea). After a 5 min standing period as per protocol, participants were instructed to stand barefoot on the scale electrodes and grip the hand electrodes. With participant details (height, gender, age) inputted, the measurement was conducted while the subject remained motionless.

Sample size

This study used logistic regression as the primary statistical analysis method. The sample size was determined based on the standard requirement for logistic regression models, which recommends 5–10 events (occurrences of the outcome variable) per predictor variable. With 11 independent variables included in our model (age, sex, residential area, smoking history, alcohol consumption, inspiratory muscle strength, physical activity level, height, weight, abdominal circumference and trunk muscle mass), we needed between 55–110 cases with FEV1%<LLN). After accounting for a potential 15% attrition rate, the required sample size ranged from 63 to 127 positive cases. Our final study population of 404 participants included 106 cases with FEV1%<LLN, which adequately met these sample size requirements while providing sufficient statistical power for the analysis.26

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0. Continuous variables were described as mean±SD, and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). Group comparisons between normal and abnormal lung function participants were performed using the χ2 test (or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate) for categorical variables, and the t-test or non-parametric tests for continuous variables, as applicable. A logistic regression approach was employed to analyse the relationships among abdominal obesity, inspiratory muscle strength and lung function. The mediation effect of inspiratory muscle strength was tested using the method proposed by Iacobucci,27 which is based on a z-test and is applicable to models with various variable types. This approach involved three models: Model 1 examined the total effect of abdominal obesity on lung function (coefficient c); Model 2 evaluated the effect of abdominal obesity on inspiratory muscle strength (coefficient a) and Model 3 assessed the direct effect of abdominal obesity on lung function after adjusting for inspiratory muscle strength (coefficient c′), along with the effect of inspiratory muscle strength on lung function (coefficient b). The significance of the mediation effect was tested using the z-test, which is applicable to various variable types in mediation models. According to a previous study, the z-value of the mediating effect test statistic was calculated. If |z| was greater than α=0.05 (1.96), the mediating effect of inspiratory muscle strength was significant.

Results

After excluding three participants with missing FEV1 measurements, 404 subjects were included in the final analysis. Discussion

This study explored the relationship among abdominal obesity, inspiratory muscle strength and lung function in young adults. Notably, we found that abdominal obesity was associated with a 4.7-fold increased odds of impaired lung function. Furthermore, we observed inspiratory muscle strength (quantified by MIP) as a significant mediator. These findings are particularly relevant for young Asian populations where such data were previously lacking.

Obesity has emerged as one of the most pressing public health challenges, with well-documented impacts on lung function.28 29 Our results confirm that abdominal obesity is significantly associated with reduced lung function, consistent with previous studies by Wan et al29 and Loukzadeh et al.30 Traditionally, this association has been attributed to mechanical constraints: adipose accumulation in the thoracic and abdominal regions restricts diaphragmatic descent and chest wall expansion, thereby reducing expiratory reserve volume and functional residual capacity.7 31 Additionally, obesity is linked to elevated levels of proinflammatory cytokines, which may contribute to small airway injury and impair both immune and mechanical functions of the lung.7 31 32

While mechanical mechanisms—such as limited diaphragm excursion and reduced chest wall compliance—remain relevant,7 31 our findings suggest a more complex pathophysiology. It is noteworthy that MIP was measured at residual volume, a lung volume that favours optimal diaphragmatic contractility. Despite this, MIP still partially mediated the association between abdominal obesity and decreased FEV1%, implying that factors beyond mechanical impairment—such as diaphragmatic dysfunction—may be involved. Animal models support this notion, showing that high-fat diets lead to diaphragmatic weakness, reflected in reduced normalised power output and decreased normalised isometric force.33 Furthermore, abdominal fat may physically impede diaphragmatic movement and weaken inspiratory muscles, thereby compromising inspiratory pressure generation and ultimately diminishing ventilation capacity.34 Importantly, inspiratory muscle performance is determined not only by diaphragmatic function but also by the coordinated action of accessory respiratory muscles, such as the external intercostals and sternocleidomastoid. Future investigations should systematically examine the specific contributions of these accessory muscle groups in the context of obesity-related respiratory dysfunction. Although our cross-sectional study identifies MIP as a partial mediator between abdominal obesity and lung function, these results should be interpreted cautiously regarding causal inference. Future studies should confirm these findings in healthy young adults.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a cross-sectional study. Therefore, it was not possible to establish causal relationships. Moreover, we selected FEV1 as the primary outcome due to its relevance in obesity-related ventilatory dysfunction; however, future studies should include additional measures for a more comprehensive assessment. Furthermore, the subjective questionnaire was used to assess physical activity and it would be preferable to use accelerometers or similar objective methods. Finally, while abdominal obesity was examined, neck adiposity may also affect lung function35 36 and should be considered.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that inspiratory muscle strength serves as a partial mediator in the relationship between abdominal obesity and lung function among young individuals. For individuals with abdominal obesity, a comprehensive strategy that combines inspiratory muscle training with weight-management approaches to reduce fat and increase muscle mass is likely to be most effective for improving respiratory health.
