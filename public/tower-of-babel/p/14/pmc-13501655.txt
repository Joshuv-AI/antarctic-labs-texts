Sotagliflozin, suPAR, and Cardiovascular Outcomes in Patients With Diabetes and Heart Failure: A SOLOIST-WHF Ancillary Study
Authors: ANIS ISMAIL, GRACE ERNE, ALEXI VASBINDER, CHRISTINA G HUTTEN, BAHJAT Z GHAZZAL, IAN PIZZO, KRISTEN MACHADO DIAZ, TONIMARIE CLAIRE CATALAN, THERESA FARHAT, BRENNA KNOTT, PENNELOPE KUNKLE, MICHAEL SZAREK, MICHAEL J DAVIES, PHILIPPE GABRIEL STEG, BERTRAM PITT, DEEPAK L BHATT, SALIM S HAYEK
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:Sotagliflozin, a dual sodium-glucose cotransporter 1 and 2 (SGLT1/2) inhibitor, improves kidney and heart failure (HF) outcomes through incompletely understood mechanisms. Soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (suPAR), an immune-derived glycoprotein, is implicated in kidney and cardiovascular disease pathogenesis. We investigated whether sotagliflozin reduces suPAR levels; whether baseline suPAR predicts cardiovascular outcomes; and whether baseline suPAR modifies sotagliflozin’s treatment effect.Research Design and Methods:We measured suPAR levels at baseline and at 1-year follow-up in a subset of patients from the SOLOIST-WHF (Sotagliflozin on Cardiovascular Events in Patients with Type 2 Diabetes Post Worsening HF) trial, which evaluated sotagliflozin’s effect on the composite outcome of cardiovascular death, HF hospitalization, and urgent HF visits in patients with type 2 diabetes and worsening HF. Cox proportional hazards modeling assessed associations between baseline suPAR and outcomes and tested for treatment-by-suPAR interaction. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) compared changes in suPAR between treatment groups.Results:In the main SOLOIST-WHF trial, sotagliflozin reduced the primary composite outcome (HR 0.67, 95% CI 0.52–0.85). In this ancillary analysis (n = 815 with available samples), the median baseline suPAR level was 4.7 ng/mL (IQR 3.7–6.1). SuPAR levels decreased similarly in both treatment groups at 1 year: sotagliflozin (n = 97, −6.2%, 95% CI [−12.0, −0.04]) vs placebo (n = 101, −7.9%, 95% CI [−13.5–2.0]; P = 0.69). Baseline suPAR was strongly associated with the primary outcome in a graded, dose-response manner, independent of treatment, systolic function, kidney function, and NT-proBNP levels: adjusted hazard ratio 2.21 (95% CI 1.36–3.60) for the fourth quartile (>6.06 ng/mL) vs the first quartile (≤3.67 ng/mL). The treatment effect of sotagliflozin was consistent across suPAR quartiles (P interaction = 0.90).Conclusions:The cardiovascular benefits of sotagliflozin are unlikely to be related to suPAR reduction, because sotagliflozin did not significantly alter suPAR levels, and treatment efficacy was consistent across suPAR strata. However, suPAR remains a strong, independent predictor of HF outcomes. Further studies are needed to determine whether suPAR-targeted therapies can improve HF outcomes.Keywords: HF, SGLTi, diabetes, outcomes, inflammation

Introduction

Sodium-glucose cotransporter (SGLT) inhibitors, initially developed as glucose-lowering therapies to treat hyperglycemia, have revolutionized the management and prevention of kidney disease and heart failure (HF).1–8 SGLT inhibitors, such as the SGLT-1 and SGLT-2 inhibitor sotagliflozin, have been shown to reduce the risk of HF and to slow the progression of kidney disease in patients with and without type 2 diabetes.1 Additionally, sotagliflozin, perhaps due to its SGLT-1 inhibition, also lowers the risk of myocardial infarction and stroke.9 The protective mechanism of sotagliflozin on the cardio-renal axis appears to involve more than the SGLT inhibitors’ regulation of glucose absorption in the kidneys and intestines.10 Other purported mechanisms include the modulation of inflammation.11–15

Soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (suPAR) is a signaling glycoprotein produced primarily by immature myeloid cells, the levels of which are thought to reflect the aggregate activity of the innate immune system.16–18 SuPAR has gained notoriety for its role as a mediator of kidney disease and was recently found to have a pathogenic role in the development of atherosclerosis.16,18–22 SuPAR levels have been associated with incident HF in the general population, are notably higher in patients with HF, and are strongly predictive of outcomes in that population of patients, independent of traditional risk factors or natriuretic peptide levels.23–25

Whether SGLT inhibitors improve outcomes through modulation of the suPAR pathway of immune activity in patients with HF remains unknown. We leveraged the Sotagliflozin on Cardiovascular Events in Patients with type 2 diabetes Post Worsening HF (SOLOIST-WHF) trial to: (1) characterize the association between suPAR and outcomes in patients with type 2 diabetes and HF; (2) examine whether sotagliflozin reduces suPAR levels; and (3) determine whether the association between SGLT inhibitors and cardiovascular outcomes is dependent on suPAR levels.

Methods

Study Design and Population

We included 815 patients of the 1222 total patients in the SOLOIST-WHF study, a double-blinded, randomized, placebo-controlled trial that enrolled patients with type 2 diabetes and recent hospitalization for worsening HF (WHF), regardless of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF).1 Participants were randomized to receive either sotagliflozin (a dual inhibitor of SGLT1 and SGLT2, at 200 mg daily (with uptitration to 400 mg) or placebo, initiated before or within 3 days of hospital discharge for WHF. The trial design and the inclusion and exclusion criteria have been published previously.1 The main SOLOIST-WHF study conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki, and this ancillary study was approved by the institutional review board at the University of Michigan (Ann Arbor, MI). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants at the time of enrollment.

Follow-up and Outcomes

Patients were monitored over a median follow-up time of 9 months for incident adverse cardiovascular outcomes, including occurrence of cardiovascular death, hospitalization due to HF, and urgent HF visits. The primary outcome was the composite outcome of total occurrences of cardiovascular death, hospitalization due to HF, and urgent visits due to HF. Cardiovascular death and HF-related outcomes (hospitalization due to HF and urgent HF visits) were also examined individually.

Measurement of suPAR

Of the 1222 randomized patients, plasma samples were available for 815 patients at baseline and for 198 patients at 1 year. Patients had to provide separate written informed consent to collect biosamples. Plasma suPAR levels were measured by using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) with a double monoclonal antibody sandwich (suPARnostic assay, ViroGates, Birkerød, Denmark). The assay has a lower detection limit of 100 pg/mL and an intra- and inter-assay variation of 2.75 and 9.17%, respectively.

Statistical Analysis

We report demographic and clinical characteristics stratified by quartiles of suPAR levels, with continuous variables expressed as means (± standard deviations) and medians (25th–75th interquartile range) for normally and non-normally distributed data, respectively; categorical variables are expressed as numbers and percentages. We used analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) to compare continuous variables among suPAR quartiles and χ2 tests for categorical variables. We then used multivariable linear regression to determine whether baseline characteristics were independently associated with suPAR levels, including age, sex, race, body-mass index (BMI), left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), NT-proBNP levels, and use of angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, loop diuretics, metformin, sulfonylureas, GLP-1 receptor agonists, insulin, and sotagliflozin as covariates. Linearity assumptions for continuous predictors were assessed by using restricted cubic splines, and residual diagnostics (normality, homoscedasticity) confirmed model adequacy. We used ANCOVA to compare the percent change in suPAR levels from baseline to 1 year between treatment groups, adjusting for baseline suPAR levels in the subset of patients with suPAR measured at both time points (n = 198).

Survival Analysis.

We plotted Kaplan-Meier curves and used Cox proportional hazard modeling to characterize the association between baseline suPAR levels and outcomes, as well as the interaction between suPAR levels and treatment arm (sotagliflozin vs placebo). We assessed suPAR levels both as quartiles, with the first quartile of suPAR serving as the reference group, and as a continuous variable (log base 2). SuPAR was categorized into quartiles to examine potential nonlinear associations with outcomes and to facilitate clinical interpretation by categorizing patients into clinically meaningful risk groups, consistent with prior suPAR studies in cardiovascular populations. Log base 2 transformation was used so that hazard ratios reflected the risk associated with a doubling (100% increase) of suPAR levels, facilitating clinical interpretation. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed by using Schoenfeld residuals (global test and covariate-specific tests) and visual inspection of log-log survival plots. We adjusted for covariates sequentially in 5 models. Model 0 was unadjusted. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, race (white vs others) and BMI. Model 2 included the aforementioned variables in addition to histories of hypertension, ischemic HF, LVEF, HbA1C levels, eGFR, metformin and insulin use. Model 3 incorporated covariates from Model 2 in addition to N-Terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) levels. Last, Model 4 was further adjusted for sotagliflozin treatment. Sensitivity analyses were performed to explore whether the association between suPAR and outcomes differed according to specific subgroups based on age (<65 vs ≥65 years), sex, race (white vs others), BMI (<30 and ≥30 kg/m2), eGFR (<60 vs ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2), ischemic vs nonischemic HF, LVEF (<50 vs ≥50%), NT-proBNP (<1806 vs ≥1806 pg/mL), baseline insulin use and treatment with sotagliflozin. Variables were selected a priori based on their known association with suPAR and outcomes. A Forest plot was created to illustrate the hazard ratios of log base 2 suPAR for each subgroup. Last, we examined the incremental value of adding suPAR to risk-prediction models that included age, gender, race, BMI, history of smoking, history of ischemic HF, LVEF, HbA1C levels, eGFR, NT-proBNP levels, metformin, insulin, and sotagliflozin for predicting the composite outcome. C-statistics were calculated based on Cox proportional hazards modeling to evaluate the predictive abilities of models with and without suPAR by using the survC1 R package. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2024). A 2-sided P value <0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics of 815 patients with HF and type 2 diabetes included in this study are shown in Table 1, stratified by suPAR quartiles: first quartile (≤3.67 ng/mL); second quartile (>3.67 and ≤4.74 ng/mL); third quartile (>4.74 and ≤6.06 ng/mL); and fourth quartile (>6.06 ng/mL). Characteristics of this cohort generally conformed to those of the overall SOLOIST-WHF cohort: the mean age was 69 years (standard deviation [SD] 9), 65.2% were male, 93.7% were white, and the median BMI was 30.6 (interquartile [IQR] 26.9–34.3) (Table 1). The median suPAR level was 4.7 ng/mL (IQR 3.7–6.1). SuPAR levels were independently associated with several demographic and clinical variables (Supplemental Table 1). Higher suPAR levels were associated with older age (β = 0.36 per 10 years, 95% CI [0.25, 0.47]), insulin use (β = 0.43 ng/mL, 95%CI [0.23, 0.64]), and higher NT-proBNP levels (β = 0.10 per 500 pg/mL, 95% CI [0.05, 0.10]).

Sotagliflozin and Change in suPAR

In a subset of 198 patients with available 1-year follow-up measurements, baseline characteristics did not differ from those of patients without 1-year suPAR measurements (Supplemental Table 2). At baseline, median suPAR levels were similar between treatment groups: 4.7 ng/mL (IQR 3.7–6.1) in the sotagliflozin group and 4.6 ng/mL (IQR 3.6–6.0) in the placebo group. At 1 year, median suPAR levels were 4.4 ng/mL (IQR 3.5–5.8) in the sotagliflozin group and 4.3 ng/mL (IQR 3.4–5.7) in the placebo group (Supplemental Fig. 2). SuPAR decreased from baseline in both treatment groups, but there was no significant difference between the sotagliflozin group (n = 97, −6.2% (95% CI [−12.0, −0.04])) and the placebo arm (n = 101, −7.9% (95% CI [−13.5, −2.0]) (ANCOVA; P = 0.69).

Incidence of the Primary Outcome Across Baseline suPAR Quartiles

During a median follow-up of 9 months, there were 384 events (composite of CV death, hospitalization for HF, and urgent HF visit) among the 815 patients in the suPAR analysis cohort. The cumulative event rate per 100 patients for the primary outcome increased in a stepwise fashion across suPAR quartiles (Fig. 1). In unadjusted analysis, suPAR levels (continuous, per doubling) were associated with a hazard ratio of 1.72 (95%CI [1.32–2.23]) for the composite outcome. In a multivariable analysis adjusting for age, sex, race, BMI, history of hypertension, ischemic HF, LVEF, HbA1C levels, eGFR, NT-proBNP levels, metformin, insulin, and sotagliflozin use, suPAR remained associated with the primary outcome (adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) 1.55, 95% CI [1.16–2.1]). When examined as a categorical variable, patients in the third (4.74–6.06 ng/mL) and fourth (>6.06 ng/mL) suPAR quartiles had a 1.96-fold (95%CI [1.27–3.04]) and a 2.21-fold (1.36–3.60) increased risk of the composite outcomes, compared with those in the first suPAR quartile (<3.67 ng/mL) after adjusting for the aforementioned variables, respectively. When outcomes were examined individually, suPAR levels (per doubling) were associated with a 1.60-fold (95%CI [1.07–2.52] and 1.70-fold (95%CI [1.07–2.52]) increase in the risk of CV death and HF-related outcomes in unadjusted analysis, respectively. When examined as categorical variables and after adjusting for age, sex, race, BMI, history of hypertension, ischemic HF, LVEF, HbA1C levels, eGFRs, NT-proBNP levels, and metformin, insulin, and sotagliflozin use, patients in the fourth (>6.06 ng/mL) suPAR quartile had a 2.50-fold (95%CI [1.02–6.12]) and a 2.01-fold (95%CI [1.23–3.56]) increase in the risk of CV death and HF-related outcomes, respectively, compared with those in the first suPAR quartile (<3.67 ng/mL) (Fig. 2).

Interaction Between Sotagliflozin and suPAR

The efficacy of sotagliflozin in reducing the composite outcome did not differ according to baseline suPAR levels. The HR for sotagliflozin treatment (vs placebo) was 0.49 (95% CI [0.24–1.01]) in Q1 (≤3.67 ng/mL), 0.54 (95% CI [0.28–1.05]) in Q2, 0.61 (95% CI [0.33–1.12]) in Q3, and 0.58 (95% CI [0.31–1.09]) in Q4 (>6.06 ng/mL) (P interaction = 0.90 (Supplemental Table 3). The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied for suPAR, and all covariates in the fully adjusted models (global test P >0.05; all individual covariate tests, P >0.05).

Risk Discrimination

Last, we assessed the incremental value of adding suPAR to a risk-prediction model that included age, gender, race, BMI, history of smoking, history of ischemic HF, LVEF, HbA1C, eGFR, NT-proBNP, metformin, insulin, and sotagliflozin. Incorporating suPAR into a model adjusting for age, sex, race, and BMI improved the C-statistic from 0.600 to 0.640. This improvement was attenuated to a modest ΔC-statistic of 0.011 when suPAR was added to a fully adjusted model (Table 2).

Discussion

In this post hoc analysis of patients with type 2 diabetes and worsening HF from the SOLOIST-WHF trial, we made several important observations regarding the relationship among suPAR, sotagliflozin, and cardiovascular outcomes. First, we found that treatment with sotagliflozin did not significantly alter suPAR levels compared to placebo over 1 year of follow-up, suggesting that the beneficial cardiovascular effects of SGLT inhibition are unlikely to be related to modulation of the suPAR pathway. Second, we demonstrated that baseline suPAR levels were strongly and independently associated with HF outcomes in a stepwise fashion, with patients in the highest suPAR quartile experiencing a more than 2-fold increased risk compared to those in the lowest suPAR quartile, even after adjustment for traditional risk factors and NT-proBNP levels. Third, the beneficial effects of sotagliflozin were consistent across the spectrum of suPAR levels, indicating that elevated suPAR does not identify patients who are more or less likely to benefit from SGLT inhibition. Finally, the strong independent association between suPAR and outcomes, combined with its limited correlation with established biomarkers, suggests that this inflammatory pathway represents an unexplored mechanism that may be worthy of therapeutic investigation.

SGLT inhibitors have revolutionized the treatment of HF, but with consistent benefits observed across the spectrum of ejection fraction, their precise mechanism of action remains incompletely understood. Our findings suggest that modulation of the suPAR pathway, an important mediator of inflammation and kidney and cardiovascular disease, is unlikely to explain these benefits. This observation aligns with previous studies showing that SGLT inhibitors may act primarily through other mechanisms, including optimization of cardiac energetics, reduction in cardiac preload and afterload, and attenuation of oxidative stress.10 The differential effects on various inflammatory pathways suggest that SGLT inhibitors may have selective effects on specific inflammatory cascades, rather than broad anti-inflammatory properties.26,27

Our findings expand on previous observations regarding the prognostic value of suPAR in a high-risk population with type 2 diabetes and worsening HF. The strong, stepwise association between suPAR levels and adverse outcomes persisted after adjustment for traditional risk factors, kidney function, and NT-proBNP levels. These results align with previous studies demonstrating higher suPAR levels in patients with HF compared to those without HF and a strong association between suPAR and HF outcomes in a broader patient population.23,24,28–30 Notably, in a study of 1116 high-risk patients with HF and coronary heart disease, elevated suPAR levels were strongly associated with major adverse cardiovascular events, with suPAR outperforming NT-proBNP levels in predicting poor outcomes.23 The modest correlation between suPAR and NT-proBNP levels suggests that these biomarkers reflect distinct and complementary pathophysiological processes.23 The observed stronger association in patients with ischemic HF compared to those with nonischemic etiology may reflect suPAR’s established role in atherosclerosis, suggesting potential mechanistic differences in how this pathway contributes to differing HF phenotypes.16

Our finding that patients taking metformin had significantly lower suPAR levels is possibly attributable to the drug’s well-established anti-inflammatory effects.31 Metformin is known to inhibit the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, a primary driver of inflammation-related gene expression. NF-κB directly promotes the transcription of the urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR), the precursor to suPAR, so metformin’s suppressive action on NF-κB likely downregulates uPAR expression, leading to reduced release of its soluble form into circulation.

The consistency and magnitude of suPAR’s association with outcomes across multiple subgroups, coupled with previous evidence of its complementary role with natriuretic peptides, suggest that suPAR levels reflect a distinct biological process that current therapies do not fully address.23 Unlike other inflammatory markers, such as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein or IL-6, suPAR levels are remarkably stable, showing no circadian variation and remaining consistent, even during acute stress32,33 and underscoring its reliability; they also provide the rationale to evaluate, specifically, their relationship with SGLT inhibition. The consistent cardiovascular benefit of sotagliflozin across all suPAR levels has clinical relevance: SGLT inhibition should be prescribed to eligible patients, regardless of their inflammatory status as measured by suPAR. Additionally, the lack of effect of sotagliflozin on suPAR levels, despite clear clinical benefits, suggests that combining SGLT inhibition with future anti-inflammatory therapies targeting the suPAR pathway might provide complementary benefits. This concept of complementary mechanism-based therapies is particularly relevant, because ongoing phase-2 clinical trials of anti-suPAR therapies (NCT06466135) may provide new therapeutic options for patients with HF. Finally, the stronger association between suPAR and outcomes in patients with ischemic HF suggests that this subgroup might be of particular interest for future trials of anti-suPAR therapies.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, it includes a large number of patients from a randomized, double-blinded clinical trial of patients with HF and type 2 diabetes. Second, the substantial number of events provides robust statistical power for examining associations with HF outcomes. Third, repeated measurements of suPAR at a prespecified time point allow for a reliable assessment of treatment effects. Fourth, the comprehensive collection of demographic and clinical characteristics enabled adjustment for potential confounders, including renal function, HF status, and medications.

Several limitations of our study warrant consideration. First, follow-up suPAR measurements were available in only a subset of patients (n = 198) at 1 year, which may have limited our ability to detect small changes in suPAR levels with sotagliflozin treatment, and small changes in suPAR cannot be definitively ruled out, given the relatively short follow-up period, which may not capture the long-term dynamics of the changes in suPAR levels. However, the similar magnitude and direction of change in both treatment arms suggest that our finding of no differential effect is robust. Second, our cohort consisted predominantly of white patients (93.7%) and was limited to patients with type 2 diabetes and recent worsening of HF, potentially limiting its generalizability to other populations, and the study might not reflect the diverse populations most affected by HF and diabetes. Third, this is a single-biomarker analysis and might not reflect what is happening with other biomarkers, such as IL-6, TNF-α and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein. Fourth, while we adjusted for multiple potential confounders, residual confounding variables cannot be excluded.

Our findings set the stage for several important future research directions. First, larger studies with serial measurements of suPAR and its specific fragments could provide deeper insights into the temporal dynamics of this inflammatory pathway in HF progression and its relationship with various therapeutic interventions and would validate our findings. Second, should results from phase 2 trials of anti-suPAR therapy prove promising, trials specifically targeting cardiovascular phenotypes may be of great interest. Third, mechanistic studies to better understand how the suPAR pathway interacts with other pathophysiological processes in HF, including metabolic dysfunction and atherosclerosis, could help to identify additional therapeutic targets and guide patient selection for future trials. Finally, investigation of other inflammatory pathways modified by SGLT inhibition could provide additional insights into the mechanisms underlying their beneficial effects in HF.

Conclusions

In summary, suPAR levels are a strong predictor of outcomes in patients with HF and type 2 diabetes and are not affected by sotagliflozin treatment. The cardiovascular benefits of sotagliflozin are unlikely to be related to the suPAR pathway of inflammation, because suPAR levels were not affected by treatment. Further studies are needed to determine whether modulation of suPAR can improve outcomes in HF.

Article Highlights

Why did we undertake this study?

Soluble urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (suPAR) has emerged as a novel immune-derived pathogenic factor in cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome, prompting the need for therapies that can modulate this pathogenic pathway.

What is (are) the specific question(s) we wanted to answer?

Using the randomized SOLOIST-WHF (Sotagliflozin on Cardiovascular Events in Patients with type 2 diabetes Post Worsening HF) trial, we asked whether the SGLT1/2 inhibitor sotagliflozin lowers circulating suPAR and whether baseline suPAR modifies or predicts cardiovascular benefit in patients with diabetes hospitalized for worsening heart failure.

What did we find?

Baseline suPAR showed a graded, independent association with the composite of cardiovascular death or heart-failure events (highest vs lowest quartile (HR 2.21, 95% CI 1.36–3.60). SuPAR levels decreased similarly in both the sotagliflozin and placebo groups at 1 year, with no significant difference between groups (P = 0.69). Sotagliflozin was effective across all suPAR quartiles (P interaction = 0.90).

What are the implications of our findings?

SuPAR, an immune-derived biomarker for cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome, is strongly predictive of heart failure outcomes, and was not affected by sotagliflozin, suggesting that the inflammatory pathway reflected by suPAR represents a potential area of residual risk warranting further investigation as a therapeutic target. Targeted anti-suPAR therapies may be warranted to further improve outcomes in this population of patients.
