The effect of education based on planned behavior theory on women’s knowledge and attitudes about human papillomavirus
Authors: Leila Rafeie, Fatemeh Vizeshfar, Narjes Nick
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman papillomavirus is the most common sexually transmitted infection in the world. Improving knowledge and attitude is the key to controlling and preventing, but women’s knowledge about this virus is not enough. This study aimed to determine the effect of educational intervention based on planned behavior theory on knowledge and attitude toward HPV and its vaccination in women of reproductive age. The study was a single-blind, randomized clinical trial study with a control group was done in 2022, which was conducted on 85 women referred to selected comprehensive health centers in Shiraz, Iran. In this study, the sampling was performed is a multi-stage random way. Eighty-three women who met the inclusion criteria were randomly divided into two intervention and control groups. The data collection tool was the knowledge and attitude questionnaire about HPV and its vaccine, which was confirmed to be valid and reliable. Data analysis was performed with descriptive and analytic statistics at a significance level of P < 0.05 with SPSS (22) software. The results showed that the educational intervention has caused a significant increase in the components of knowledge, attitude, social norms, perceived behavior control, and willingness to receive the HPV vaccine in the intervention group. So that in both stages after the intervention, there was a significant difference between the intervention group and the control group in the studied components (P < 0.05). The educational intervention based on the planned behavior theory significantly affected the knowledge, attitude, social norms, and intention for HPV vaccination in women of reproductive age. Therefore, educational intervention recommended as awareness-raising programs and strategies for women.Trial registration: (RCT code: IRCT20220131053891N1). Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is the most common sexually transmitted infection in the world, which is associated with genital warts, malignancies, and some cancers in men and women. The number of people with HPV is increasing all over the world, especially in developing countries 1. Among the major epidemiological risk factors of the HPV virus, intercourse at a young age, history of STI disease, multiple sexual partners, history of smoking, and chronic immunosuppression can be mentioned 2. Considering that HPV infection occurs mainly during unprotected sex, about 80% of sexually active people will be infected with the HPV virus. 3. One of the main complications of HPV infection is the occurrence of various types of cancer and warts 4,5.

Cervical cancer is the most common HPV-associated cancer, and some cancers of the vulva, vagina, penis, anus, and oropharynx are also caused by HPV 4,6. Every year, about 30,000 cases of HPV-related cancer occur in the United States, and about two-thirds of them are in women 7. Cervical cancer is the second most common cancer in women in the world, and the first in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia 2,8, more than 95% of which are related to the HPV virus 2. Cervical cancer, which is the cause of death of one woman every 2 min in the world 9. Approximately 85% of cervical cancer deaths in low- and middle-income countries 10. In Iran, cervical cancer is the third cause of death 11. The age-standardized prevalence and death rates of cervical cancer in Iran in 2018 were estimated to be 2.14 and 0.93 per 100,000 people, respectively 12. These warts cause a lot of physical and mental stress to the patients, which widely reduces the life satisfaction of the sufferers 13. In addition to physical consequences and problems, this disease has many psychological and social consequences for patients. Infection with this virus can lead to marital problems, lack of satisfaction in married life, the tendency to risky behaviors, personal and psychological problems, depression, and other psychiatric disorders 14. Also, infection with this virus may hurt reproduction and reproductive health 15.

However, despite the high rate of HPV infection and its connection with female genital cancers, studies show that there is little knowledge and awareness among sufferers as well as ordinary people 16,17. A systematic review showed that only 15–31% of respondents had heard about HPV and less than 11% knew it as a risk factor for cervical cancer 18. Increasing women's awareness is the first step to enhancing their participation in the early screening program for cervical cancer. Therefore, the World Health Organization emphasizes the education of the target community and considers it effective in preventing the occurrence of HPV and early detection of cancer 8. However, in low- and middle-income countries, very limited access to vaccination, insufficient screening and education, and the high rate of sexually transmitted diseases are the main challenges to improve the current situation 10.

Since HPV and unprotected sex may face society and the health system with the challenge of increasing the incidence of cancers, warts, and other complications 19, education and raising people's awareness about the HPV virus and its vaccine are very important. Education about sexually transmitted diseases can be effective in preventing sexually transmitted infections 20, because there is a big difference in the level of knowledge and awareness about HPV infection and its vaccination, especially among teenagers 21. Therefore, sexual health education plays an important role in increasing sexual health and well-being and ensuring that people have the necessary knowledge and skills to develop and maintain healthy relationships 22. Moreover, education can increase the demand for vaccination and other preventive health services by increasing awareness, increasing access to services, increasing people's efficiency, and empowering them in future choices 2. An important driving factor in promoting HPV vaccination is parents' awareness and attitude. According to the studies, knowledge, attitude, advice and financial support of parents are very important factors for the acceptance of HPV vaccination by adolescents. Evidence also shows that informed mothers have a great potential to persuade their children to take active measures for the primary prevention of cervical cancer 21.

One of the key points in teaching health issues is attention and appropriate use of behavioral science theories 23. One of the theory used to predict individual behavior and education is the theory of planned behavior. 24. This theory predicts the individual's intention to perform the behavior and partially explains the relationship between intention and health behavior and use in many community health education 2,18,25. Therefore, this clinical trial was conducted to determine the effect of an educational intervention for the prevention and treatment of HPV based on planned behavior theory on the knowledge and attitude of women of reproductive age.

Materials and methods

Study type

The study was conducted as a single-blind randomized clinical trial with intervention and control group. The CONSORT reporting guideline was used in this study26. Trial registration: (RCT code: IRCT20220131053891N1). First Registration date: 28/04/2022.

Participants

The research population included women of reproductive age who were referred to comprehensive health centers in Shiraz in 2022. Inclusion criteria were as follows: women aged 15–49 living in Shiraz, not employed in the treatment team, literate, have a phone, have no history of neurological and mental illness, no history of hysterectomy, and no history of uterine cancer. The exclusion criteria were participation in similar educational classes, unwillingness to continue participating in the study, change of residence, failure to participate in more than one training class session, and failure to complete the questionnaire completely. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants before participating in the study. All participants were assured of the confidentiality and privacy of the collected data. They were also given the right to withdraw from the study at any stage.

Sample size and sampling method

According to a study by Torabizadeh et al.27, the mean difference is 3.21 and the standard deviation difference is 6.95, at a significance level of 0.05 and the power of the test is 80%, using the following formula, the minimum sample size is 40 people for each group and the total sample is 80 people. In this formula, alpha is the first type of error (%5), beta is the second type of error (%20), sigma is the standard deviation and delta is the mean.

Due to the possibility of a 5% dropout of samples during the study, the final sample size was considered to be 85 people, due to the withdrawal of one person from the control group, 84 women participated in the “immediately after the intervention” stage. Also, one person in the intervention group did not cooperate in completing the questionnaire in the last stage (3 month after intervention) of the study, and finally, the data analysis was done with 83 participants (intervention group: 41 women and control group: 42 women).Fig. 1 show CONCORT diagram.

CONSORT flow diagram.

The sampling method was multi-stage. First, among the comprehensive health centers of Shiraz, 3 centers were randomly selected by lottery method. Using household information in the integrated health system, women with entry criteria were determined. Then, according to the number of women who meet the entry criteria in each center, the sample size of each center was determined by the quota method. Finally, the block randomization method was used to divide the samples into two intervention and control groups. For this purpose, randomization was done in blocks of 6 using the List Randomization website26.

Instrument

In this study, the collecting information tool is the knowledge and attitude questionnaire about HPV and its vaccine, the validity and reliability of which were checked by the researchers. This questionnaire was available in English by Ajzen29. After translating and re-translating the questionnaire, quantitative and qualitative methods were used to determine the validity (face, content, and structure) as described below. First, the face validity of the tool was checked using the item impact score by 10 faculty members of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences and 10 women of reproductive age eligible to participate in the study. At this stage, the importance of each item was determined using a 5-point Likert scale from “completely important (5 points)” to “not important at all (1 point)”. Items with an impact size of less than 1.5 were modified or removed if they existed. All the items that had an impact size higher than 1.5 were evaluated as appropriate. Thus, the face validity of the data collection tool was confirmed.

Quantitative methods of content validity rate (CVR) and content validity index (CVI) were used to determine the content validity of the questionnaire28. To determine CVR, 10 faculty members of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, who specialized in the field of instrumentation and women’s health, graded the items in the form of a 3-point spectrum of “necessary”, “useful but not necessary” and “not necessary”. The content validity rate varies between 1 and − 1, with a higher value indicating greater agreement among experts regarding the necessity of the item in the tool. The minimum acceptable CVR value for each question was 0.62, and for all items, a score higher than 0.8 was obtained. To determine the CVI of the items, the content validity index of Waltz and Bausell, which measures the "relevance" of the questionnaire items based on a four-point Likert scale (1 = the statement is not relevant, 2 = the statement needs to be revised to some extent, 3 = the statement is relevant but needs a little revision, 4 = very relevant) was used. The minimum acceptable CVI value was 0.79, which was obtained above 0.9 for all items. In this way, the content validity of the data collection tool was confirmed.

The internal consistency method was used to determine the reliability of the tool. Thus, the questionnaire was given to 40 qualified women and its Cronbach's alpha coefficient was determined to be 0.962. These people were not part of the study samples. Thus, the questionnaire was designed in six parts: demographics, knowledge/awareness, attitude, social norms, perceived behavior, and intention to receive the vaccine. The demographic part includes 14 questions about age, marital status, education level, occupation, number of children, health insurance, having sex, number of sexual partners, prevention methods, history of sexually transmitted diseases, and Pap smear test. The knowledge section contains 9 true or false questions about the human papillomavirus and its vaccine. In the attitude section, 9 items regarding the attitude towards human papilloma and 6 items related to the attitude towards the human papilloma vaccine were investigated with the semantic differential method. 5 items to measure the norms of the samples, 4 items to control the perceived behavior, and 4 items regarding the willingness to take the vaccine were evaluated with a 5-point Likert scale.

Data gathering

After obtaining the necessary permits and identifying the samples, first, the objectives of the study were explained to the samples, and they completed the pre-test questionnaire. Then three training sessions of 30–45 min were held for the intervention group. The teaching method was face-to-face in 4 small groups. There were 6 to 12 people in each group. Due to the spread of covid-19, the training was provided in person, following health protocols. At the time of the study, the control group did not receive any training (except for the center's routine training provided by the personnel working there).

The educational content of the first session included an explanation of the objectives of the research, completion of the pre-test, an explanation of the importance of preventing HPV infection and the ways of its transmission, and giving an educational booklet containing important information related to HPV. The second session was to answer the participant’s questions about the content presented in the first session, the complications of HPV infection, and its treatment methods. In the third session, participants' questions were answered about the content presented in the previous sessions, the benefits of HPV vaccination, completing the post-test, and encouraging them to read the training booklet. Meetings were held weekly with the opinion of the participants. Both groups completed the post-test questionnaire immediately and three months after the intervention. At the end of the study, to comply with ethical principles, a booklet containing educational materials was provided to the members of the control group who were willing.

Analysis

Descriptive statistics indicators such as frequency, percentage, mean and standard deviation, and analytical tests were used for data analysis using SPSS (22) software. First, the normality of the data was checked using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Due to the non-normality of the data, non-parametric tests Mann Whitney, Kruskal Wallis, Chi-square, Friedman, and Spearman correlation were used. A significance level of P < 0.05 was considered.

All Intervention performed in the current study were approved by the Ethics committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (approval number: IR.SUMS.NUMIMG.REC.1400.080)".The study will be carried out in accordance with the basic principles of the Helsinki Declaration (2013), Investigator commitment will be requested of all women’s that participate in the study. Discussion

According to the findings, the level of knowledge and attitude of the participants regarding HPV and its vaccine was low before the intervention, which is in line with previous studies in Iran27,30,31. Also, the results were consistent with He and He’s study in western China, which showed that only 28.85% of participating women were aware of HPV17. In Johnson et al. study, 15.4% and 32% of women were aware of HPV and HPV vaccines, respectively32. While other studies indicate a low level of awareness and a negative attitude toward HPV and its vaccine16,33,34, some studies have also reported a high level of awareness and a positive attitude35–37. The difference in people's awareness and attitude towards HPV and its vaccine can be caused by the difference in religious cultural context, educational system, public health policies, and health literacy level of people in different societies. In this regard, the findings of the present study showed that the educational intervention increased the average score of knowledge and attitude toward HPV and its vaccine in intervention groups.

The study of Thanasas et al. on Greek adolescents showed that health education intervention significantly increased awareness and knowledge about HPV. So the lack of awareness increased from 44.4% in the first stage to 1.6% in the second stage and to 1.8% in the third stage38. A study on high school youth in South Africa showed that before the educational intervention, the knowledge level of 0.83% of people was “good”, which reached 73.38% after the intervention. Thus, after the educational intervention, people’s knowledge and awareness about HPV increased significantly39. A study on Saudi pregnant women also showed that the educational intervention increased 2–3 times in awareness and positive attitudes toward HPV vaccination40. Li et al. showed that educational intervention has a significant effect on increasing the level of knowledge and attitude of Chinese rural women about HPV36. In Grigore et al.’s study, the most important barriers to HPV vaccination were the fear of complications, the perception of being dangerous, and financial issues. Along with having more transparent relationships, they suggested holding training courses to improve the level of awareness and attitude41. Of course, it should be noted that the slight increase in the knowledge and attitude of the control group compared to before the intervention could probably be due to the effect of completing the questionnaire, sensitizing and encouraging them to study.

According to the findings of the study, although before the intervention there was a significant difference between the social norms and perceived behavior control of the two control and intervention groups, this difference became more evident after the intervention. Furthermore, no significant change was found in the socials and perceived behavioral control of the control group regarding HPV during the study, but it was observed in the intervention group. In their study, Johnson and Ogletree showed that social norms and perceived behavioral control are important predictors of people's intention to inject the HPV vaccine42. Yarmohammadi et al. found that the educational intervention significantly increases the impact of social norms and perceived behavior control on the behavioral intention and health behaviors of women with low HPV risk. They found that social norms s through behavioral intentions are an important factor in increasing health behavior43. The study of Torabizadeh et al. on the knowledge and attitude of nurses and nursing students towards HPV showed that the educational intervention had a positive and significant effect on the perceived behavior of the participants27.

According to the obtained results, the level of willingness of women to receive the HPV vaccine before the intervention was low. In the study of Ozyer et al., only 11% of the participants were willing to get an HPV vaccination, and the main reason for not wanting to get vaccinated was a lack of information and lack of awareness44. During the study, no significant change was found in the willingness of the control group to receive the HPV vaccine, but after the educational intervention, significant changes were observed in the willingness of the intervention group to receive the HPV vaccine. A study on Greek adolescents showed that the acceptance rate of the HPV vaccine in the first phase was 71%. After the intervention of health education, the willingness to HPV vaccine increased to 89.1% in the second stage and to 83.5% in the third stage. This increase in desire for the HPV vaccine was statistically significant41, which is in line with the present study. A study on Chinese rural women showed that after the educational intervention, women's intention to HPV vaccination for themselves and their daughters increased significantly45, which is in line with the present study. Therefore, it can be said that in this study, health education was able to encourage women to inject the vaccine by highlighting the risk factors, consequences of the disease, prevention methods, and benefits of vaccination.

In general, the findings have shown a low level of awareness, a negative attitude, and a low level of willingness of women to receive the HPV vaccine before the intervention. One of the main and serious obstacles to the low awareness and attitude of people about issues and sexual diseases such as HPV in Muslim countries, including Iran, is cultural and religious issues46 that prevent the education of such issues to people, especially girls, and women. Because many socio-cultural and family restrictions in the field of sexual and gender issues have created a culture of being conservative and taboo in talking about sexual issues and sexually transmitted diseases and ignoring them44,45. For this reason, researchers have considered it necessary for policymakers to pay attention to the design and implementation of intervention programs with the cooperation of various sectors, including education, higher education, and the health system, to break taboos and promote sexual health in Islamic societies47.

Furthermore, the findings of the study showed the effective and positive impact of training based on the planned behavior theory on awareness, attitude, social norms, perceived behavior control, and women's willingness to receive the HPV vaccine. Although in the control group, familiarization and sensitization may have increased people’s awareness and attitude to some extent, it did not affect their behavioral intention or their social norms and, as a result, their willingness to receive the vaccine. But in the intervention group, it was confirmed that the social norms and behavioral intentions of people to receive the vaccine have increased significantly. Therefore, based on the findings of the present study and relevant evidence, it can be concluded.

That changing the social norms and behavioral intentions of people requires structured and deep training41.

Considering that HPV is the cause of more than 95% of cervical cancer cases3 and the prevalence of HPV in women with invasive cervical cancer in Iran is estimated at 81%47, education about prevention and timely treatment of HPV infection and its vaccine can reduce a significant part of the consequences of this disease. For this reason, it is suggested that educational programs and strategies, preventive and early diagnosis and treatment of HPV should be included in the agenda of health system managers. Because vaccination and screening programs are suitable strategies for the prevention of HPV, precancerous lesions, and cancer, it requires education and awareness of society about HPV48–50. In addition, a cost–benefit analysis of the HPV vaccine in Iran showed that vaccination is a useful strategy51. A study in Nigeria found national HPV vaccination and cervical cancer screening to be cost-effective52. Therefore, improving the level of awareness and attitude through education and adding HPV vaccines to the national vaccination program, and implementing comprehensive screening programs in Iran can help reduce the burden of the disease and its economic burden.

Conclusion

The educational intervention based on the planned behavior theory significantly affected the knowledge, attitude, social norms, and desire for the HPV vaccine in women of reproductive age. Therefore, education about the prevention and timely treatment of HPV infection and its vaccine can reduce a significant part of the consequences of this disease. For this reason, it is suggested that educational programs and strategies, preventive and early diagnosis and treatment of HPV should be included in the agenda of health system managers.

Limitation

There were some limitations in this study, such as the outbreak of the coronavirus, which caused the intervention procedures to be prolonged. Another limitation of this study was the impossibility of long-term follow-up of the study participants to investigate the long-term effect of the educational intervention. The study was prolonged due to the need for the agreement of the samples to participate in the training classes and the gap between the pre-test and post-tests.
