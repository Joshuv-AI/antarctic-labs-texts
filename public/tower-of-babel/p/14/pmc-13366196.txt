Age, Multidomain Lifestyle Intervention, and White Matter Integrity
Authors: Pauline Maillard, Prashanthi Vemuri, Danielle J. Harvey, Theresa M. Harrison, Ashritha L. Reddy, Hwamee Oh, Stephen Salloway, Simin Mahinrad, Thomas M. Holland, Rachel Whitmer, Mark A. Espeland, Laura D. Baker, Susan M. Landau, Charles DeCarli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This prespecified secondary analysis of a randomized clinical trial assesses whether a multidomain lifestyle intervention, vs a self-guided program, alters trajectories of cerebrovascular magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) markers, whether baseline diffusion MRI estimates subsequent vascular injury, and whether any effects differ by age among adults 60 to 79 years of age who are at increased risk for cognitive decline.

Introduction

Nonpharmacologic multidomain lifestyle interventions that target physical activity, diet, cognitive engagement, and vascular risk factors are promising strategies to prevent or delay cognitive decline in older adults at risk for dementia.1,2,3 Large randomized trials suggest that structured multidomain lifestyle interventions may influence cognitive trajectories, with mixed results.4,5,6 Much less is known about the effects of these interventions on cerebrovascular brain injury and white matter microstructure, key substrates linking vascular risk to late-life cognitive impairment.7,8

Cerebrovascular pathologies, including white matter hyperintensity (WMH) volume, microstructural white matter damage, and cerebral microbleeds (CMBs), are highly prevalent in late life and contribute substantially to cognitive impairment and dementia.9,10,11 Diffusion magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)–derived free water (FW) is an emerging biomarker of early white matter injury,12 often preceding overt WMH.13 Other diffusion-based markers, including fractional anisotropy (FA), peak width of skeletonized mean diffusivity (PSMD), and the analysis along the perivascular space (ALPS) index, provide complementary measures of microstructural integrity, small-vessel disease burden, and glymphatic function.14,15,16,17 Despite their growing use as cerebrovascular imaging biomarkers,18,19 it remains unclear whether and how these measures respond to intensive lifestyle interventions in older adults at elevated risk for cognitive decline.

To date, previous multidomain lifestyle trials have focused primarily on cognitive outcomes, with limited imaging data to interpret the biological relevance of the findings. In assessments of data from an exploratory 2-year multidomain intervention, the Finnish Geriatric Intervention Study to prevent Cognitive Impairment and Disability randomized clinical trial, the authors found evidence of FA reduction in the multidomain intervention group (vs an education control), which is contrary to expectations but, as authors suggested, may be explained by the small sample size7 and a highly variable metric such as FA.20 In the Prevention of Dementia by Intensive Vascular Care trial,21 a cluster-randomized clinical trial, the authors found slower WMH progression in the multidomain intervention but no difference in lacunar infarct incidence.8 In the Action for Health in Diabetes clinical trial, a 10-year lifestyle intervention study, the authors reported that WMH volume was 28% lower among participants in the lifestyle intervention compared with those receiving diabetes support and education.22

The US Study to Protect Brain Health Through Lifestyle Intervention to Reduce Risk (US POINTER) study was a large, multicenter randomized clinical trial that compared a structured multidomain lifestyle intervention vs a self-guided intervention in older adults at increased risk for cognitive decline.23 The trial demonstrated greater benefits of the structured intervention on global cognition over time compared with the self-guided intervention.24 The POINTER Imaging ancillary study enrolled a subset of US POINTER parent trial participants.25 Primary imaging outcomes focused on global markers of white matter injury and small vessel disease and were reported separately.26 The present analysis addressing the secondary and exploratory MRI outcomes of the trial was aimed at characterizing microstructural vulnerability and identifying individuals at higher risk for lesion progression.

In the present study, we examined whether the structured arm compared with the self-guided arm modified the longitudinal trajectories of FW, WMH, PSMD, FA, and the ALPS index. Given the age range (60-79 years) of the trial and prior evidence that cerebrovascular injury accelerates disproportionately during the eighth decade of life—coinciding with increasing vascular stiffness, impaired repair capacity, and heightened vulnerability of white matter to injury27,28—analyses were conducted by prespecified age groups23 (<70 vs ≥70 years). We further evaluated whether baseline diffusion MRI measures estimated incident CMBs and WMH progression, independent of intervention group assignment. Because FW has been reported as an early marker of CVD-related WM injury,12 we hypothesized that FW would be particularly sensitive to interventions and would identify individuals at higher risk for progression of macrovascular and microvascular brain injury.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

The POINTER Imaging ancillary study was embedded within the US POINTER randomized clinical trial, a 2-year, single-blind, multicenter randomized clinical trial of 2 lifestyle interventions in community-dwelling older adults at risk for cognitive decline.23 The single institutional review board at Wake Forest University School of Medicine approved the US POINTER parent trial and the POINTER Imaging ancillary study with concurrence by local institutional review board sites. All participants provided written informed consent for both the parent trial and the imaging ancillary study. This ancillary study of a randomized clinical trial follows the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guideline.

The POINTER Imaging study protocol and statistical analysis plan are available in Supplement 1. Briefly, participants were aged 60 to 79 years, were not regularly physically active, and had a suboptimal diet and at least 2 additional risk factors (eg, family history of memory impairment, cardiometabolic risk factors, older age, or male sex).23 Participants were selected to be cognitively unimpaired but at risk for decline and dementia due to the aforementioned well-established risk factors. Individuals were excluded with clinically significant cognitive impairment as per self-report, the modified Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status29 (score <32 on a scale of 0 to 50, with higher values indicating better cognitive function), or a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score higher than 0.5 of possible scores ranging from 0 to 3, with higher values indicating greater severity and progression of cognitive decline. If the CDR–Sum of Boxes was higher than 1, continued eligibility required lead neuropsychologist approval indicating no cognitive impairment.23 Participants were recruited from 5 US sites and randomized 1:1 to a structured multidomain intervention or a self-guided program. Starting August 1, 2020, and continuing until March 1, 2023, all newly enrolled US POINTER trial participants who met criteria (ie, no contraindications for neuroimaging) were given the opportunity to enroll in POINTER Imaging (see the eFigure in Supplement 2) and underwent MRI scanning sessions at baseline, 12 months, and 24 months. The imaging study ended May 14, 2025. For the present analysis, we included participants who had MRI imaging data, including diffusion-weighted imaging MRI data (n = 960). One participant with major structural lesions was excluded, resulting in 959 participants. For CMB analyses, we further required the availability of baseline and follow-up susceptibility-weighted imaging (n = 956).

Interventions

The structured multidomain intervention comprised supervised aerobic, resistance, and flexibility exercises, an individualized nutrition plan based on the Mediterranean-DASH Intervention for Neurodegenerative Delay diet,30 cognitive training, social engagement, and behavioral coaching in 38 participant team meetings across 2 years.23 The self-guided intervention group received educational materials covering the same lifestyle domains in 6 facilitated team meetings across 2 years but no goal-directed coaching. Both arms received results from annual clinic visits that included cardiovascular disease risk factor assessments. Usual medical care was provided by participants’ health care practitioners. Randomization was stratified by site. Outcome assessors and MRI readers were masked to intervention assignment.

Vascular Risk Factors

Vascular risk factors were assessed at baseline during the prerandomization screening visit. Information on hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and smoking history was obtained from a combination of participant-reported medical history, review of medical records, and medication use brought to the clinic. Hypertension was defined as a history of systolic blood pressure of 130 mm Hg or higher or the use of antihypertensive medication. Diabetes was defined as a self-reported history of type 2 diabetes (participants requiring insulin treatment in the prior 2 months were excluded per trial criteria). Smoking status was defined as ever vs never smoking.

MRI Acquisition and Processing

MRI was acquired at participating sites using harmonized protocols on Siemens 3T scanners (Tim Trio, Magnetom, Verio, Skyra, and Prisma Fit). Sequences included 3-dimensional T1-weighted imaging, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and a 3-echo train T2* gradient recalled echo sequence. Acquisition parameters for these sequences have been described in detail elsewhere.25 Participants were scanned at baseline and then annually for up to 2 follow-up visits.

Indicators of white matter integrity included WMH, FW, FA, PSMD and the ALPS index. These markers have been widely used in population-based studies of cerebral small-vessel disease and have undergone methodological validation and harmonization efforts within the National Institutes of Health MarkVCID consortium.31,32,33 Briefly, WMH was quantified using a previously described bayesian segmentation approach based on T1-weighted and FLAIR images.34,35,36,37 Values are in cubic centimeters, with higher values indicating greater white matter injury burden. FW (range, 0-1) and FA (range, 0-1) were estimated using a 2-compartment diffusion model separating isotropic FW from tissue-restricted diffusion.38,39,40 Elevated FW is often interpreted as a marker of neuroinflammation or tissue degeneration, and lower FA suggests microstructural damage or demyelination.41,42 PSMD (in squared millimeters per second) was derived from the distribution of mean diffusivity values along the white matter skeleton, with higher values indicating greater microstructural disruption.43,44 The ALPS index (range, 0 to 1) was computed as a diffusion-based index reflecting perivascular fluid transport,45,46 with lower values suggesting impaired interstitial fluid clearance. Additional methodologic details are provided in the eMethods in Supplement 2.

Total cranial volume was estimated using a convolutional neural network–based segmentation approach.47 WMH volumes and PSMD were log-transformed to normalize population variance. Interpreting log-transformed variables converts additive changes into multiplicative (percentage) changes. WMH volumes were also residualized against total cranial volume to account for head size variability. From gradient recalled echo images, CMBs were visually identified by trained analysts and confirmed by blinded neuroradiologists. Incident CMBs were defined as the presence of at least 1 new microbleed at any follow-up exam but absent at baseline. At baseline, 128 participants (13.4%) had at least 1 CMB, and 70 participants (7.3%) developed incident CMBs during the 2-year trial period. Scanner-adjusted MRI biomarker values were derived using linear mixed-effects models that accounted for scanner and diffusion protocol differences while preserving within-participant longitudinal change. All continuous MRI measures were z scored for comparability.

Age Groups and Time Variable

Participants were classified using the study prespecified age groups (Supplement 1) based on baseline age (<70 vs ≥70 years). This prespecified cutoff is supported by prior epidemiological and neuroimaging studies showing that cerebrovascular burden and white matter injury accelerate in the eighth decade of life, coinciding with increased vascular stiffness, multimorbidity, and reduced repair capacity.27,28 Thus, this threshold provides a clinically meaningful distinction between stages of late-life vascular brain aging. Time was measured as the continuous time difference between baseline and follow-up dates; for mixed-effects models, we used a centered time variable to improve interpretability of interactions.

Statistical Analysis

Effect of Intervention Arm on Longitudinal Trajectories of MRI Markers

For each continuous MRI biomarker (FW, WMH, PSMD, FA, and the ALPS index), we fit linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts and, when supported by the data, random slopes for time at the participant level. Models included intervention arm, age group, and time as well as their interactions as independent variables, and sex as a covariate. Because vascular risk factors have been shown to impact vascular health,18 history of hypertension (yes vs no), diabetes (yes vs no), and smoking (ever vs never) were also included as covariates. Incident CMBs, defined as the occurrence of at least 1 new microbleed at any follow-up visit relative to baseline (no vs yes), was modeled using a logistic regression including intervention arm and age group as well as their interaction as independent variables, and sex, hypertension status, diabetes, and smoking history as covariates. We also assessed arm-specific estimated trajectories by age group and visualized them with 95% CIs.

Sensitivity Analyses

We repeated the aforementioned models with the following changes for sensitivity analyses: (1) replacing the dichotomous age group (<70 vs ≥70 years) with continuous baseline age; (2) restricting analyses to participants who completed both planned follow-up MRI visits to assess the impact of attrition and variable follow-up; (3) using uncorrected MRI measures with scanner and DWI parameters included as a covariate instead of using scanner-adjusted metrics, to evaluate robustness of findings to the harmonization approach; and (4) restricting analyses to participants for whom scanner and DWI parameters were unchanged over the course of the trial.

Imaging Estimators of WMH Trajectory and Incident CMBs

Although not the primary focus of this work, we also investigated whether baseline diffusion markers estimated WMH progression for the entire imaging cohort as further evidence of their relevance as a marker of small vessel disease. Toward this goal, we used separate mixed-effects models with each diffusion marker (FW, FA, PSMD, or the ALPS index), time, and the interaction between diffusion marker and time as the independent variables, adjusting for age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, and intervention arm. Using logistic regression, we also investigated whether diffusion markers estimated the occurrence of CMBs, using each diffusion marker as the independent variable and controlling for age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, and intervention. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs were estimated.

A 2-tailed level of α = .05 was established to determine statistical significance. Statistical analyses were completed using R, version 4.3.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing) for the evaluable population.

Results

Participant Characteristics

For 959 participants, the mean (SD) age was 68 (5) years, 594 (61.9%) were female, and 365 (38.1%) were male. (Table 1). There were 503 participants in the structured intervention arm, 456 in the self-guided intervention arm, 533 in the group younger than 70 years, and 426 in the group 70 years or older. Baseline vascular risk factors and MRI marker measures were comparable between interventions. The distributions of intervention arm (286 [53.7%] vs 217 [50.9%]; P = .40) and female sex (341 [64.0%] vs 253 [59.4%]; P = .15) were not significantly different across age groups. As compared with the younger age group, the percentage of participants with hypertension (61.5% [n = 328] vs 68.3% [n = 291]; P = .03), diabetes (13.3% [n = 71] vs 18.1% [n = 77]; P = .04), and smoking history (39.6% [n = 211] vs 50.7% [n = 216]; P < .001) was significantly higher in the older age group. All MRI measures but CMB (mean [SD], 0.33 [3.49] vs 0.51 [3.50] counts; P = .42) were worse for the older age group compared with the younger group (eg, FW mean [SD], 0.17 [0.03] vs 0.20 [0.03] units; P < .001). Continuous measures of follow-up duration at 12 months (mean [SD] for intervention arm, 1.07 [0.22] vs 1.07 [0.21] years, P = .99; and for age group, 1.08 [0.22] vs 1.06 [0.21] years, P = .37) and 24 months (mean [SD] for intervention arm, 2.09 [0.26] vs 2.09 [0.25] years, P = .99; and for age group, 2.08 [0.26] vs 2.10 [0.25] years, P = .24) were comparable between arms and age groups, and loss to follow-up was modest and balanced.

Longitudinal Trajectories of FW and Other Biomarkers

Across all participants, all markers but the ALPS index showed significant time effects that were consistent with progressive small-vessel disease and microstructural aging (Table 2). FW increased significantly over time (β = 0.820; SE = 0.009; P < .001) and showed a significant interaction between time, arm, and age group (β = 0.050; SE = 0.018; P = .006) indicating that FW trajectories differed by both intervention arm and age stratum. Post hoc analyses derived from the mixed-effects model revealed that in participants younger than 70 years, the structured intervention arm was associated with attenuated increases in FW over time compared with the self-guided intervention arm (β = −0.031; SE = 0.012; P = .009), whereas in participants aged 70 years or older, FW increased over time in both arms with no clear between-arm difference (β = 0.019; SE = 0.014; P = .18). Among participants in the structured arm, the rate of the FW increase was steeper in those 70 years or older compared with the younger group (β = 0.048; SE = 0.013; P < .001).

Sensitivity Analyses

In separate sensitivity analyses either (1) modeling age as a continuous modifier, (2) using unharmonized MRI markers with scanner and DWI parameters entered as covariates, (3) restricting to participants with complete MRI follow-up, or (4) restricting to participants with no scanner and DWI parameter changes over time, the main findings—that is (1) a significant time × arm × age interaction on FW and (2) no main or interaction effect of intervention arm on FA, PSMD, the ALPS index, WMH trajectory, or CMB incidence—remained unchanged (eTables 2-5 in Supplement 2). The only exception was that the time × arm × age interaction on FW was attenuated when age was modeled continuously (β = 0.019; SE = 0.010; P = .06) (eTable 2 in Supplement 2). This pattern emphasized that the FW intervention effect was age dependent and appeared strongest when contrasting younger participants (60-69 years) with older participants (70-79 years) participants.

Baseline Diffusion Markers and Incident CMBs

Among participants free of CMBs at baseline with follow-up susceptibility imaging, higher baseline FW was associated with incident CMBs (OR = 1.63 [95% CI, 1.24-2.14]; P < .001) (Figure 2; eTable 6 in Supplement 2). Higher PSMD was also associated with incident CMBs (OR = 1.40 [95% CI, 1.08-1.81]; P = .01). Neither the ALPS index (OR = 0.97 [95% CI, 0.74-1.27]; P = .82) nor FA (OR = 0.81 [95% CI, 0.64-1.03]; P = .09) estimated the incidence of new CMB during the 2-year trial period.

Baseline Diffusion Markers as Estimators of WMH Progression

Higher baseline FW (β = 0.014; SE = 0.006; P = .02) and lower baseline FA (β = −0.018; SE = 0.006; P = .004) were both independently associated with faster WMH accumulation during follow-up (Figure 3 and eTable 6 in Supplement 2), suggesting that FW and FA capture early microstructural vulnerability that precedes overt WMH growth. Neither the baseline ALPS index (β = −0.010; SE = 0.006; P = .09) nor baseline PSMD (β = 0.010; SE = 0.006; P = .10) were significantly associated with the slope of WMH.

Discussion

In this large ancillary imaging study of the US POINTER randomized clinical trial of multidomain lifestyle interventions, we found that FW was the cerebrovascular MRI marker most sensitive to the effects of a structured intervention in younger older adults (60 to <70 years) and was the best estimator of downstream cerebrovascular injury. Two main findings emerged.

FW trajectories showed a significant time × arm × age interaction, indicating that the structured intervention altered FW trajectories relative to the self-guided intervention selectively in younger participants (60 to <70 years). In this subgroup, the aging-expected FW increase over time was slower (1) in the structured arm relative to the self-guided arm and (2) within the structured arm, in the younger group compared with the older group (≥70 years). Older participants exhibited FW increases, regardless of intervention arm and at a rate comparable to the self-guided younger group. We hypothesize that this attenuation reflects a more advanced stage of cerebrovascular injury, characterized by greater vascular risk burden and accumulated tissue damage, which may be less amenable to short-term modification, consistent with prior work showing increasing cerebrovascular burden and reduced plasticity with advancing age.48 Importantly, the attenuation of the interaction, when age was modeled continuously, suggests a gradual effect, with the contrast between participants younger than 70 years vs 70 years or older enhancing interpretability rather than indicating a strict threshold.

The selective responsiveness of FW to the intervention likely reflects differences in the temporal sensitivity of cerebrovascular imaging markers. FW is thought to capture increases in extracellular fluid related to multiple processes, including blood-brain barrier dysfunction, impaired interstitial fluid clearance, and disruption of perivascular transport pathways,49,50,51 and to reflect early microstructural alterations that may precede overt structural damage.12 This notion is consistent with our second finding showing that high FW levels identified individuals at greatest risk for WMH progression and incident CMBs, without regard to intervention group assignment. In contrast, the absence of significant intervention effects on WMH, PSMD, the ALPS index, and FA should be interpreted with caution. Although the sample size was substantial, these markers reflect more established tissue injury that evolves over longer time scales.52 As such, the null findings do not exclude potential longer-term benefits, particularly for WMH burden, which is known to accumulate gradually,53 warranting further follow-up of this cohort to test this hypothesis. Finally, although FW has been linked to interstitial fluid dynamics and may be influenced by glymphatic function, it is not specific to this pathway and reflects a combination of processes, as described earlier in this paragraph. The absence of an intervention effect on the ALPS index, which is intended to more specifically reflect perivascular fluid transport, therefore does not necessarily contradict the observed FW findings but suggests that the intervention may have influenced broader extracellular fluid dynamics rather than a single mechanistic pathway.

The parent trial reported no differences in the structured intervention cognitive benefit between younger and older participants, which is compatible with the notion that cognition, as a distal outcome, may require longer follow-up for age-related differences to manifest compared with cerebrovascular imaging markers.53,54 These imaging findings are complementary to the primary cognitive outcomes and provide mechanistic insight into the biological pathways through which the intervention may exert its effects. Follow-up data from the ongoing longitudinal POINTER Imaging cohort will be important to evaluate longer-term trajectories of MRI and cognitive outcomes.

The clinical implication of the observed FW effect lies less in the absolute size of the standardized coefficient than in what it represents biologically: attenuation of an early microstructural marker linked, in this study, to subsequent WMH progression and incident CMBs. While recognizing this caveat, the effects are substantial when comparing the coefficients between the structured and self-guided arms for the under 70 age group (intervention effect, β = −0.031) compared with the overall rate of FW increase over time (β = 0.082). Additionally, FW can be derived from standard diffusion MRI acquisitions that are widely available in clinical practice, without the need for additional scan time, and therefore have clinical translational potential. Current use, however, relies on specialized postprocessing technics that are not yet integrated into routine clinical workflows. As these approaches mature,19 FW may serve as an early marker of cerebrovascular injury and a sensitive outcome measure for monitoring intervention effects, particularly in prevention trials.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this study include its embedding in a large, rigorously conducted randomized clinical trial; having harmonized, multisite MRI acquisition; simultaneously assessing several complementary cerebrovascular biomarkers; and longitudinal modeling of both trajectories and incident events. Sensitivity analyses using alternative age parameterization, scanner adjustments, and sample restrictions yielded similar results, supporting the robustness of our main findings.

The study limitations include the ancillary nature of the imaging cohort, comprising a subset of trial participants, with a slightly lower proportion of female participants and modestly higher prevalence of vascular risk factors compared with the parent cohort (eTable 1 in Supplement 2); the 2-year follow-up duration, which may be relatively short for some macrovascular outcomes; and limited power to detect small intervention arm effects on WMH or CMBs, especially within age strata. In addition, the higher burden of vascular risk factors in older individuals may contribute to reduced responsiveness to intervention. However, because randomization was preserved within age groups and models adjusted for these factors, the observed age-dependent differences are unlikely to be fully explained by baseline vascular burden alone. Finally, because the self-guided arm included educational components and limited engagement, it may also have conferred some benefit, such that the observed between-group differences likely reflected the incremental effect of a higher-intensity intervention.

Conclusions

In this secondary analysis of older adults at risk for cognitive decline enrolled in a multidomain lifestyle randomized clinical trial, FW emerged as the cerebrovascular marker most responsive to a structured lifestyle intervention and a sensitive indicator of subsequent small vessel disease–related brain injury. The structured intervention was associated with modified FW trajectories in younger participants, whereas higher baseline FW, FA, and PSMD identified individuals at increased risk for WMH progression and incident CMBs. In contrast, markers of more established cerebrovascular pathology (ie, WMH, CMBs) showed limited short-term change. Together, these findings support FW as an early, intervention-responsive marker of cerebrovascular brain health and suggest that intensive lifestyle interventions implemented earlier for older adults may yield greater benefits for preserving brain microvascular integrity.
