Associations between essential trace elements and Alzheimer's disease and related dementias
Authors: Xin Wang, Roger L. Albin, Bruno Giordani, Bhramar Mukherjee, Henry L. Paulson, Kelly M. Bakulski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONDysregulation of essential trace elements has been implicated in Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathogenesis, yet comprehensive epidemiologic evidence remains limited. We investigated associations of seven plasma essential trace elements, including manganese, iron, cobalt, copper, zinc, selenium, and molybdenum, with AD and all‐cause dementia risk.METHODSWe analyzed 1,737 participants from the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center. Cross‐sectional analyses assessed prevalent disease; longitudinal analyses evaluated incident cases among 1,101 initially dementia‐free participants (median follow‐up: 2.05 years). Multivariable logistic regression, Cox proportional hazards models, and quantile‐based g‐computation evaluated individual and mixture effects.RESULTSA simultaneous one‐quartile increase in the seven‐element mixture was associated with lower prevalent AD (odds ratio [OR] = 0.69; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.52–0.91). Longitudinally, higher plasma iron was associated with lower incident AD (hazard ratio [HR] = 0.41; 95% CI: 0.19–0.92, Q4 vs. Q1), while selenium predicted higher incident AD risk (HR = 2.42; 95% CI: 1.11–5.27, Q4 vs. Q1).DISCUSSIONLower plasma iron and higher plasma selenium were each associated with greater dementia risk, identifying iron and selenium as potentially modifiable factors for dementia prevention, especially in selenium‐replete populations.

BACKGROUND

Dementia, including Alzheimer's disease (AD), represents one of the most pressing global health challenges. In the United States, 7.2 million adults aged 65 years and older were living with AD in 2025, and total payments for dementia care are projected to exceed $380 billion annually. 1 The etiologies of AD and related dementias are complex and multifactorial. Despite considerable advances in understanding the pathophysiology of AD, significant knowledge gaps persist regarding modifiable factors that may influence disease risk. The 2024 Lancet Standing Commission on dementia identified 14 modifiable risk factors that collectively account for approximately 45% of dementia cases worldwide, implying that a proportion are unexplained by currently recognized factors. 2 Given that disease‐modifying therapies remain unavailable for the vast majority of dementia cases, identifying additional modifiable exposures that may contribute to AD and related dementias (ADRD) risk remains a critical public health priority.

The role of essential trace elements in AD has garnered increasing attention. 3 , 4 Elements such as manganese (Mn), iron (Fe), cobalt (Co), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), selenium (Se), and molybdenum (Mo) serve as cofactors for enzymatic reactions, antioxidant defense systems, and neurotransmitter metabolism. 5 Their relationship with neurological health is biphasic; however, each is required at physiological concentrations but can be neurotoxic at supranutritional levels or in particular chemical species. It is therefore the maintenance of homeostasis is critical for the brain, and dysregulation of these elements has been implicated in AD pathogenesis through oxidative stress, amyloid‐β (Aβ) aggregation, tau hyperphosphorylation, ferroptosis, synaptic dysfunction, and neuroinflammation. For example, Fe is essential for myelination, mitochondrial energy metabolism, and neurotransmitter synthesis; 6 Cu binds Aβ to catalyze reactive oxygen species generation; 7 Zn modulates Aβ misfolding and synaptic integrity; 8 and Se exhibits a U‐shaped dose‐response relationship, whereby selenoproteins confer neuroprotection at physiological concentrations, but supranutritional levels may be neurotoxic. 9 Despite this biological plausibility, epidemiologic evidence remains limited. Most studies have employed case‐control or cross‐sectional designs susceptible to reverse causation, focused on single elements or small subsets, and used small sample sizes. 10 , 11 Prospective studies measuring trace elements before disease onset are rare, and no study has examined associations of all seven essential trace elements with clinically diagnosed dementia, both individually and as a mixture.

To address these gaps, we investigated the prevalent and prospective associations of plasma concentrations of seven essential trace elements (Mn, Fe, Co, Cu, Zn, Se, and Mo) with the risks of AD and all‐cause dementia, both individually and as a mixture, in a large sample of participants from the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC).

Relevant citations are appropriately included.

2. Interpretation: In 1,737 well‐characterized National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center (NACC) participants, higher plasma iron was consistently associated with lower risk of prevalent and incident AD and all‐cause dementia. Plasma selenium showed divergent associations: inversely associated with prevalent AD, likely reflecting reverse causation, but positively associated with incident AD. Given the short follow‐up, the longitudinal selenium association should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in studies with longer follow‐up. The seven‐element mixture was collectively associated with lower odds of prevalent AD.

3. Future directions: Larger prospective cohorts with longer follow‐up, repeated trace element measurements, selenium speciation, integration with fluid and imaging AD biomarkers, and replication in diverse and population‐representative samples are needed to confirm causality and inform targeted nutritional strategies for dementia prevention.

METHODS

Study population

This study used data from the NACC Uniform Data Set (UDS), version 3. 12 The NACC, funded by the National Institute on Aging, maintains a longitudinal database of standardized clinical assessments collected from participants enrolled at National Institutes of Health (NIA) ‐funded Alzheimer's Disease Research Centers (ADRCs) across the United States. 13 The UDS includes demographic information, medical history, neuropsychological testing, clinical diagnoses, and functional assessments obtained through standardized protocols at approximately annual visits. Participants are enrolled at individual ADRCs through a convenience‐based process rather than population‐based sampling, with recruitment occurring via clinician referral, self‐ or family‐referral, active community outreach, and research volunteers; most centers also enroll cognitively normal volunteers, although enrollment protocols vary across centers. 13 Consequently, the dementia‐free participants in this study comprise both individuals evaluated for cognitive concerns who were not found to have dementia and cognitively normal volunteers enrolled without suspicion of cognitive decline.

Dementia outcome assessment

The primary outcomes were all‐cause dementia and AD dementia. In the NACC UDS, clinical diagnoses are assigned at each visit by a consensus panel or a single clinician, depending on the practice of each ADRC, using standard consensus diagnostic criteria. 13 Each participant receives a cognitive status diagnosis of cognitively unimpaired, impaired‐not‐mild cognitive impairment (MCI), MCI, or dementia, based on a comprehensive evaluation including medical history, neurological examination, neuropsychological testing, and functional assessment. All‐cause dementia is diagnosed according to the NIA‐AA 2011 core clinical criteria when cognitive or behavioral symptoms interfere with the ability to function at work or in usual activities, represent a decline from previous levels, and are not explained by delirium or a major psychiatric disorder. 14 Clinicians also assign one or more presumptive etiologic diagnoses, indicating whether each suspected etiology is a primary, contributing, or non‐contributing cause. AD dementia was defined as dementia with Alzheimer disease indicated as a primary or contributing etiologic diagnosis. Throughout this manuscript, “AD” refers to AD dementia as defined here, clinically diagnosed dementia with AD indicated as a primary or contributing etiologic diagnosis, and not to Alzheimer's disease as the underlying neuropathology.

Plasma essential trace elements measurement

From all the NACC UDS cohort with banked plasma available at the National Centralized Repository for Alzheimer's Disease and Related Dementias (NCRAD), we randomly selected a sample of 1,999 individuals for trace element quantification. 15 Random selection was used to obtain a representative sample of the available repository population and to minimize selection bias. For participants with samples collected at multiple visits, only the earliest available sample was used. Plasma concentrations of seven essential trace elements (Mn, Fe, Co, Cu, Zn, Se, and Mo) were quantified using triple quadrupole inductively‐coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS) at the Trace Element Analysis Core, Dartmouth College, following United States Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA) SW‐846 Methodology 6020B with rigorous quality control procedures including continuous calibration verification, duplicate and spiked sample analyses, intra‐ and inter‐batch assessments, and comparison against certified reference materials (Seronorm Serum Levels 1 and 2; Billingstad, Norway). 16 Values below the limit of detection (LOD) were imputed as LOD/√2 (Table S1).

Covariates

Demographic and clinical covariates were obtained from the NACC UDS at the visit corresponding to plasma collection. Age, sex, self‐reported race, and years of education were collected on Form A1. Educational attainment was categorized as high school or less (≤ 12 years), some college (13–15 years), or college or above (≥ 16 years). Smoking status were assessed on Form B2 and classified as never, former, or current smoker. Alcohol abuse history was documented by the clinician on Form A5, based on the structured medical‐history interview with the participant and a co‐participant (informant) rather than by a self‐administered questionnaire, and classified as absent, inactive, or /active; it reflects a history of clinically significant impairment in work, driving, legal, or social functioning. The number of apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 alleles (0, 1, or 2) was obtained from the NACC Genetics file.

Statistical analysis

After excluding participants with missing information on core covariates, the final analytic sample comprised 1,737 participants (Figure 1). We performed both cross‐sectional and longitudinal analyses because they address distinct and complementary research questions. The cross‐sectional analysis, which included all 1,737 participants at the visit when plasma was collected (baseline), examines associations between trace element concentrations and prevalent dementia. These associations may reflect biological effects of trace elements on disease risk but may also capture disease‐related changes in trace element metabolism, as dementia itself can alter nutritional status and systemic inflammation. In contrast, the longitudinal analysis evaluates whether pre‐disease trace element concentrations predict the subsequent development of dementia, which more directly informs the potential for prevention and intervention. For this analysis, we restricted the sample to 1,101 participants who were dementia‐free at baseline and had at least one subsequent follow‐up visit. Together, the two designs allow us to distinguish associations that may arise from the disease process itself from those that precede and potentially contribute to disease onset.

Flow chart depicting included and excluded participants.

In the cross‐sectional analysis, multivariable logistic regression was used to estimate the associations of individual essential trace element concentrations with prevalent AD and all‐cause dementia at baseline. All models were adjusted for age, sex, race, education, smoking status, alcohol abuse history, and number of APOE ε4 alleles. Plasma trace element concentrations were log2‐transformed to approximate normality and improve model fit, enabling interpretation of odds ratios (ORs) per doubling of each element's concentration. To evaluate potential nonlinear associations, we additionally categorized element concentrations into quartiles and estimated ORs for the second, third, and fourth quartiles relative to the lowest quartile as the reference group. Tests for linear trend across quartiles were performed by modeling quartile rank as a continuous variable.

In the longitudinal analysis, Cox proportional hazards regression was used to evaluate associations between trace element concentrations and incident AD and all‐cause dementia among participants dementia‐free at baseline. The same covariate adjustment was applied. Person‐time was calculated from plasma collection to first dementia diagnosis, with dementia‐free participants right‐censored at their last available visit.

To assess the joint effect of the trace element mixture, we applied quantile‐based g‐computation in both analyses. 17 Because trace elements are correlated co‐exposures, simultaneously modeling all of them in a single regression would introduce multicollinearity and yield unstable, difficult‐to‐interpret individual estimates; quantile‐based g‐computation is instead designed for correlated exposure mixtures, accounting for co‐exposure confounding and collinearity while estimating an interpretable joint effect. All seven elements were included simultaneously with concentrations categorized into quartiles. The mixture effect was estimated as the OR (cross‐sectional) or hazard ratio (HR; longitudinal) associated with a simultaneous one‐quartile increase in all element concentrations, adjusting for the same covariates. Quantile‐based g‐computation was implemented using the qgcomp package in R. All analyses were performed using R version 4.5.2.

RESULTS

Descriptive statistics

The study population included 1,737 participants with a mean baseline age of 72.2 years; 348 participants (20.0%) had all‐cause dementia and 300 (17.3%) had AD (Table 1). Compared with participants without dementia, those with all‐cause dementia were older (mean age, 74.3 vs 71.7 years), more likely to be male (52.3% vs 37.4%), be White (94.0% vs 78.1%), have a higher prevalence of APOE ε4 allele carriage (52.5% vs 38.0% with ≥1 allele), and less likely to have attained a college education (61.5% vs 72.7%). Participants with dementia also had lower mean plasma Cu concentrations. Similar patterns were observed for AD specifically, with AD participants being older, more frequently male, a greater burden of APOE ε4 alleles, and having lower plasma Cu concentrations. Within the longitudinal sample, participants who subsequently developed incident dementia or AD showed a similar profile to those who remained dementia‐free, being older, more frequently APOE ε4 carriers, and having higher baseline plasma Se and lower plasma Cu (Table S1) Details of distributions of plasma trace element concentrations were summarized in Table S2. Detection rates were 80% for Mn and exceeded 99.9% for all other elements.

Cross‐sectional analysis at baseline

In multivariable logistic regression analyses, higher plasma Fe was associated with lower odds of prevalent AD in both the continuous and quartile‐based models (Table 2). Each doubling of plasma Fe concentration was associated with 26% lower odds of AD (OR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.57–0.96). The highest quartile of plasma Fe had 39% lower odds of AD than the lowest quartile (95% CI: 0.41–0.88). Plasma Se and Mn also showed significant inverse trends across quartiles. No significant associations were observed for Cu, Zn, Mo, or Co with prevalent AD. In the mixture analysis using quantile‐based g‐computation, a simultaneous one‐quartile increase in all seven essential trace elements was associated with 31% lower odds of prevalent AD (OR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.52–0.91).

For prevalent all‐cause dementia (Table S3), plasma Fe was similarly inversely associated with disease in both modeling approaches. Higher plasma Co was also inversely associated with all‐cause dementia when modeled continuously (OR per doubling = 0.79; 95% CI, 0.66–0.94). No other individual elements were significantly associated with prevalent all‐cause dementia. In the mixture analysis, the joint effect of all seven elements was consistent with that observed for AD: a simultaneous one‐quartile increase in all element concentrations was associated with 29% lower odds of all‐cause dementia (OR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.54–0.92).

Longitudinal analysis during follow‐up

Among 1,101 participants who were dementia‐free at baseline, 75 developed all‐cause dementia during follow‐up, of whom 66 were diagnosed with AD. The median follow‐up time was 2.05 years for both outcomes.

In Cox proportional hazards models for incident AD (Table 3), higher plasma Se was associated with a substantially elevated risk. Each doubling of Se concentration was associated with a nearly 5‐fold increase in the hazard of AD (HR = 4.71; 95% CI, 1.59–13.98), and a significant positive trend was observed across Se quartiles, with participants in the highest quartile having more than twice the risk of those in the lowest (HR = 2.42; 95% CI, 1.11–5.27). Higher plasma Fe showed a significant inverse trend across quartiles, with participants in the highest quartile having a 59% lower hazard of incident AD (HR = 0.41; 95% CI, 0.19–0.92. No other individual elements were significantly associated with incident AD. The quantile‐based g‐computation analysis yielded a nonsignificant joint effect of the seven‐element mixture on incident AD (HR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.55–1.53).

For incident all‐cause dementia (Table S4), plasma Fe was inversely associated with risk in both modeling approaches. Each doubling of Fe was associated with a 50% lower hazard (HR = 0.50; 95% CI, 0.31–0.80), and a significant decreasing trend was observed across quartiles. Higher plasma Cu was also inversely associated with incident dementia when modeled continuously (HR per doubling = 0.43; 95% CI, 0.21–0.88). As with AD, higher plasma Se was positively associated with incident all‐cause dementia, with a significant trend across quartiles and the highest quartile conferring approximately twice the risk (HR = 2.06; 95% CI, 1.00–4.27). The mixture analysis showed a nonsignificant inverse association (HR = 0.74; 95% CI, 0.46–1.20).

Post hoc longitudinal mixture analysis excluding Se

Given the strong positive associations of plasma Se with incident dementia observed in the longitudinal analysis, which is contrary to the direction of the other elements, we conducted a post hoc analysis excluding Se from the mixture model. When all six remaining essential trace elements increased by one quartile simultaneously, the joint effect on incident all‐cause dementia was significant (HR = 0.64; 95% CI, 0.40–1.00), suggesting a protective mixture effect that had been attenuated by the opposing direction of the Se association. The corresponding HR for incident AD was 0.79 (95% CI, 0.48–1.30), directionally consistent but not statistically significant.

Post hoc restricted cubic spline analyses of Fe and Se

To characterize the shape of the dose‐response relationships for the two elements with the most consistent associations, Fe and Se, we conducted post hoc restricted cubic spline analyses. Plasma concentrations were modeled on the log2 scale and referenced to the median concentration, and covariate‐adjusted ORs (cross‐sectional) and HRs (longitudinal) were estimated across the ranges of exposure, with adjustment for the same covariates as the primary models. Analyses were implemented with the rms package in R (Figures S1–S4).

For prevalent AD, the adjusted odds decreased approximately monotonically with increasing plasma Fe (Figure S1). For plasma Se, the adjusted odds declined with increasing Se up to approximately the median and remained near the null at higher concentrations (Figure S2).

In the longitudinal analysis, the adjusted hazard of incident AD decreased approximately monotonically with increasing plasma Fe, paralleling the cross‐sectional pattern (Figure S3). In contrast, the adjusted hazard of incident AD increased approximately monotonically with increasing plasma Se, without an apparent plateau (Figure S4).

DISCUSSION

In this study of 1,737 NACC participants, we examined the associations of seven plasma essential trace elements with AD and all‐cause dementia using cross‐sectional and longitudinal designs. Two principal findings emerged. First, higher plasma Fe was consistently associated with lower risk of both prevalent and incident AD and all‐cause dementia across analyses, representing the most robust individual‐element finding. Second, plasma Se exhibited a complex pattern: higher Se concentrations were associated with lower odds of prevalent AD at baseline, yet were strongly associated with higher incidence of AD and all‐cause dementia during follow‐up. These findings extend the current evidence by simultaneously evaluating multiple essential trace elements in a well‐characterized cohort for dementia research and suggest that maintaining optimal Fe and Se homeostasis may represent modifiable targets for AD and dementia prevention.

One major finding of this study is that higher plasma Fe concentration was associated with lower prevalence and incidence of both AD and all‐cause dementia. This finding is consistent with prior epidemiologic evidence linking low Fe status and anemia to increased dementia risk. In the Health Aging and Body Composition Study, baseline anemia was associated with a 64% increased risk of incident dementia over 11 years of follow‐up among 2,552 older adults. 18 In the Rotterdam Study of 12,305 participants with 12‐year follow‐up, anemia was associated with a 34% increase in all‐cause dementia risk and a 41% increase in AD risk. 19 A national health screening study in Korea involving 37,900 adults demonstrated a dose‐response relationship, with dementia prevalence rising progressively from mild to moderate to severe anemia. 20 Cross‐sectional data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey similarly demonstrated an inverse, linear dose‐response association between serum Fe and cognitive impairment among adults aged 60 years and older. 21

Several biological mechanisms support the plausibility of a protective role for adequate Fe status against AD. Fe is an essential cofactor for tyrosine hydroxylase and tryptophan hydroxylase, the rate‐limiting enzymes in dopamine and serotonin synthesis; Fe deficiency disrupts monoaminergic signaling throughout the striatum and hippocampus, impairing attention, memory consolidation, and executive function. 22 , 23 Oligodendrocytes are the most Fe‐rich cells in the brain and require Fe for myelination; 24 white matter degeneration, an early feature of AD, may be exacerbated by Fe insufficiency. 25 At the mitochondrial level, Fe‐sulfur clusters and heme groups are indispensable across all four complexes of the electron transport chain, and Fe deficiency in hippocampal neurons reduces the respiratory control ratio and impairs dendritic mitochondrial motility. 26 A more disease‐specific mechanism involves amyloid precursor protein (APP), which contains an Fe‐responsive element and possesses ferroxidase activity that facilitates neuronal Fe export via ferroportin stabilization. 27 Under conditions of adequate Fe, APP processing favors the nonamyloidogenic α‐secretase pathway, reducing amyloid‐β production, whereas amyloidogenic processing destabilizes ferroportin, trapping Fe intracellularly and promoting ferroptosis, an Fe‐dependent form of regulated cell death implicated in hippocampal neurodegeneration. 28

In this study, we observed that higher plasma Se was associated with lower prevalent AD at baseline, yet among participants free of AD at baseline, higher Se predicted greater incidence of AD during follow‐up. The inverse cross‐sectional association may be driven by reverse causation. Patients with established dementia frequently experience weight loss and malnutrition due to appetite dysregulation and olfactory dysfunction, leading to generalized depletion of micronutrients including Se. 29 AD is also characterized by chronic systemic inflammation. 30 Selenoprotein P (SELENOP), the primary hepatic transporter delivering Se to the brain, is a negative acute‐phase reactant. 31 Systemic inflammation significantly downregulates hepatic SELENOP synthesis, independent of dietary Se intake. 32 Consequently, lower Se concentrations in prevalent AD cases likely represent a biomarker of disease‐induced inflammation and frailty rather than a predisposing etiologic factor. This interpretation is consistent with evidence that cross‐sectional and case‐control designs may not reliably capture the etiologic role of Se in AD, owing to reverse causation affecting most Se species. 33

Conversely, the longitudinal association, in which higher baseline Se predicted incident AD in an initially disease‐free cohort, may reflect the neurotoxic potential of Se at supranutritional concentrations. Se is essential for brain redox homeostasis through its incorporation into selenoproteins, including the glutathione peroxidase family, thioredoxin reductases, and SELENOP. 9 , 34 . At physiological concentrations, these Se‐dependent enzymes exert potent antioxidant effects. Critically, however, plasma selenoprotein concentrations plateau at Se concentrations of approximately 70 to 90 µg/L; beyond this threshold, further increases reflect non‐specific incorporation of selenomethionine into albumin and other proteins in place of methionine. 35 Given that participants in our study had a median plasma Se concentration of 130.8 µg/L, well above this saturation point, higher Se concentrations likely represent increases in non‐functional selenomethionine‐substituted proteins rather than enhanced selenoprotein activity. The biological effects of Se thus follow a U‐shaped dose–response curve, and participants with the highest plasma Se likely reside on the harmful right limb. High concentrations of certain Se compounds, particularly selenite, can induce oxidative DNA damage and cytotoxicity.3 1 Recent cerebrospinal fluid speciation analyses demonstrate that higher concentrations of inorganic selenate, but not organic selenoproteins, predict conversion from mild cognitive impairment to AD and accelerate hippocampal atrophy. 36 Finally, higher plasma Se has been linked to insulin resistance, 31 which drives amyloid accumulation and tau hyperphosphorylation via dysregulation of the PI3K/Akt/GSK‐3β signaling axis. 37 , 38 This prospective association nonetheless warrants cautious interpretation given the short median follow‐up (2.1 years), as we cannot fully exclude that preclinical disease present at baseline influenced selenium concentrations. Notably, because the reverse causation operating in established disease lowers selenium, it would be expected to bias the prospective association toward the null rather than generate the positive association observed; confirmation in cohorts with longer follow‐up is nonetheless needed.

Beyond Fe and Se, we observed that higher plasma Co was associated with lower odds of prevalent all‐cause dementia at baseline, and higher plasma Cu was associated with reduced incident all‐cause dementia during follow‐up. The inverse Co association may be mediated through its role as the central atom of vitamin B12, which is essential for methionine synthesis, homocysteine metabolism, and myelin maintenance. 39 Vitamin B12 deficiency leads to elevated homocysteine, consistently associated with increased dementia risk through oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and direct neurotoxicity. 40 The neuroprotective effect of Cu is supported by its role as a cofactor for cytochrome c oxidase and as a regulator of neurotransmitter synthesis via dopamine β‐hydroxylase. 41 Cu also fuels Cu/Zn–superoxide dismutase (SOD1), a critical defense against oxidative stress. 42

These findings carry several clinical and public health implications. The consistent protective association of plasma Fe with dementia risk suggests that maintaining adequate Fe status in older adults may be relevant to cognitive health, particularly given the high prevalence of Fe deficiency and anemia in aging populations. 43 For Se, our cohort was Se‐replete and typical of the United States (median plasma Se 130.8 µg/L; NHANES adult mean ≈137 µg/L), 44 with concentrations well above the European weighted mean (≈85 µg/L), 45 above the ∼70‐90 µg/L range at which selenoprotein synthesis plateaus, and above the level corresponding to the recommended daily allowance (RDA; 55 µg/day); 46 few participants approached intakes near the tolerable upper intake level (400 µg/day) 46 or concentrations associated with overt selenosis. These observations are hypothesis‐generating: they raise the possibility that, in already Se‐replete populations, Se intake beyond requirements offers little additional benefit, but our data cannot establish causality and do not, on their own, justify changes to the RDA. They do, however, reinforce caution about indiscriminate Se supplementation for dementia prevention pending confirmation in larger studies with longer follow‐up. The protective mixture effect observed in both cross‐sectional and post hoc longitudinal analyses underscores the value of evaluating trace elements collectively rather than in isolation. Because we did not estimate dietary intakes from plasma concentrations—plasma Fe is not a perfect intake marker, and the plasma Se–intake relationship is nonlinear and form‐dependent—these comparisons should be interpreted qualitatively. Collectively, these findings support integrating essential trace element assessment into research on modifiable dementia risk factors and may inform future dietary guidance and targeted nutritional interventions. This study has several strengths. To our knowledge, it is the first to examine associations of all seven essential trace elements with clinically diagnosed AD and all‐cause dementia, both individually and as a mixture. A key strength is the use of data from the NACC UDS, which provides rigorously standardized clinical assessments conducted by multidisciplinary teams at NIA‐funded ADRCs. Dementia diagnoses are based on comprehensive evaluations integrating medical history, neurological examination, neuropsychological testing, and functional assessment, assigned through consensus conference or expert clinician review, representing a substantially higher diagnostic standard than administrative codes or screening instruments used in many epidemiologic studies. Additionally, quantile‐based g‐computation provided an estimate of the joint mixture effect accounting for correlations among elements.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the median follow‐up of 2.1 years was relatively short. Beyond limiting the number of incident events and statistical power, the short follow‐up raises the possibility of reverse causation in the longitudinal analyses: baseline trace element concentrations could be influenced by undiagnosed preclinical disease rather than preceding it, and a 2‐year window may not capture the etiologically relevant exposure period. This is particularly relevant for selenium; although the malnutrition‐ and inflammation‐related processes that lower selenium in established disease would be expected to bias the longitudinal association toward the null or an inverse direction, which is opposite to our positive finding, we cannot exclude other preclinical influences, and the longitudinal selenium association should be interpreted cautiously and confirmed in cohorts with longer follow‐up. Because incident non‐Alzheimer's dementia in this AD‐focused cohort spanned several etiologically distinct subtypes, we did not examine subtype‐specific (e.g., vascular, Lewy body, frontotemporal) or early‐onset dementia associations, which warrant dedicated studies with larger subtype‐specific samples and longer follow‐up. Second, trace element concentrations were measured at a single time point and may not capture long‐term exposure. In addition, we measured total plasma concentrations by ICP‐MS rather than individual species; because the biological activity and toxicity of trace elements are sometimes species‐specific (for example, functional selenoproteins versus inorganic selenite or selenate, or the redox‐active non–transferrin‐bound fraction of Fe), total concentrations may not fully capture species‐specific effects, and speciation‐resolved measurements in future studies would refine mechanistic interpretation. Third, although we adjusted for multiple confounders, residual confounding by unmeasured factors such as dietary intake, supplement use, or other environmental exposures cannot be excluded. Finally, the NACC cohort is a convenience sample of clinically referred and volunteer participants enrolled at ADRCs rather than a population‐based sample, limiting generalizability.

In summary, higher plasma Fe was consistently associated with lower risk of both prevalent and incident AD and all‐cause dementia, while higher plasma Se was associated with elevated risk of incident AD and all‐cause dementia. The essential element mixture was collectively associated with lower odds of prevalent AD and all‐cause dementia, and this protective joint effect was attenuated longitudinally by the opposing Se association. These findings suggest that plasma essential trace elements, particularly Fe and Se, represent modifiable factors relevant to dementia risk and warrant further investigation in larger prospective studies with longer follow‐up and repeated measurements to inform targeted nutritional strategies for dementia prevention.
