Potential differences between real-life and randomized controlled trial populations in chronic myeloid leukemia
Authors: Tomáš Horňák, Daniela Žáčková, Jiří Mayer
Published: 2025

To the editor,

Randomized clinical trials (RCTs) are the cornerstone of evidence-based medicine. Their findings shape everyday clinical practice by guiding the development and use of new therapeutic agents, improving patient outcomes, and forming the basis of evidence-based clinical guidelines. To ensure that these findings are robust and reliable, strict adherence to study protocols is essential. During the screening process, patients must meet all inclusion criteria and none of the exclusion criteria. This methodology helps to protect vulnerable populations—such as those with significant comorbidities or on concomitant medication—and ensures data consistency and validity [1]. However, such stringent selection criteria may exclude many patients typically seen in daily practice, resulting in underrepresentation of real-world populations, and risk introducing inadequate generalizability of the trial results [2].

In oncology, differences between patients enrolled in RCTs and those treated in real-world clinical settings have been well-documented and are a frequent topic of discussion [3]. These discrepancies challenge the external validity and generalizability of clinical trial results. A widely held belief is that patients included in clinical trials tend to experience better outcomes. However, while this topic has been extensively investigated, the conclusions are often contradictory [4]. Such inconsistencies can be attributed to variations in diagnosis, cancer stage, geographic and institutional differences, as well as trial design [5]. Within the field of hematologic oncology, disparities between clinical trial cohorts and real-world patient populations have been analyzed for diseases such as acute myeloid leukemia, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, mantle cell lymphoma, multiple myeloma and others [6–8]. However, less is known about how these differences apply to patients with chronic myeloid leukemia (CML). For patients with CML, no published data currently exactly examine the potential differences between RCT populations and real-world patients. Two reports showed, however, that these populations do not necessarily match, also in CML [9, 10]. Hoffmann et al. [9] found that a comparison of EUTOS baseline characteristics with pivotal treatment studies data confirmed that patients enrolled in prospective treatment studies were somehow different from, and not fully representative of, patients in routine care, and that some of them were systematically underrepresented. The difference was particularly relevant for the main company-sponsored phase III-trials that evaluated tyrosine kinase inhibitors’ therapeutic effects to obtain the marketing authorization from major drug authorities like the FDA and EMA.

In recent years, a substantial number of newly diagnosed CML patients in our department have been excluded from RCTs. Given the lack of data on this issue, we conducted an analysis comparing patients eligible for RCTs to the real-world population of CML patients treated at our institution.

The Department of Internal Medicine, Hematology and Oncology at University Hospital Brno in Czech Republic is a centre of specialized hematological care certified by the Czech Ministry of Health and has a catchment area of approximately 1,5 million people. For patients diagnosed with CML within the said catchment area, the CML treatment is reimbursed by health insurance only if treated at the certified centre. At the time of diagnosis at our centre, virtually every patient provides written consent for their data to be stored in a patient database and used anonymously for scientific purposes. For this report, we analyzed data collected between 2014 and 2024. During this time period, four clinical trials for patients with newly diagnosed CML were active: Treatment Free Remission after Nilotinib Plus Peg-Interferon Alpha Induction and Peg-Interferon Alpha Maintenance Therapy for Newly Diagnosed Chronic Myeloid Leukemia Patients (TIGER trial, https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01657604); A Multicenter Phase 3 Randomized, Open-Label Study of Bosutinib versus Imatinib in Adult Patients with Newly Diagnosed Chronic Phase Chronic Myelogenous Leukemia (BFORE trial, https://www.clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02130557); A Phase III Study of Asciminib vs. Investigator-Selected Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitor in Patients with Newly Diagnosed Chronic Myeloid Leukemia in Chronic Phase (CML-CP) (ASC4FIRST trial, https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04971226); A Phase IIIb, Open-Label, Randomized Study of Tolerability and Efficacy of Asciminib Versus Nilotinib in Patients with Newly Diagnosed Philadelphia Chromosome-Positive Chronic Myelogenous Leukemia in Chronic Phase (ASC4START trial, https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05456191). At diagnosis, all patients were evaluated for eligibility for the active trials. Eligible patients were invited to participate.

Between 2014 and 2024, 244 patients were newly diagnosed with CML. In 130 cases, treatment was initiated when no active trial was open for recruitment. Of the remaining 114 patients, 49 (43%) were successfully enrolled in a clinical trial, 10 (9%) declined participation, and 55 (48%) did not meet eligibility criteria. There is no doubt that RCTs are the driving force of medical progress. No other scientific method is as broadly accepted by the medical community and regulatory authorities. Their benefits are undeniable. However, RCTs also have limitations. In daily practice, we frequently encounter patients who would not be eligible for clinical trials. So RCTs results must be applied with caution, considering inclusion and exclusion criteria for a particular trial, side effects of the intended treatment, and many patient-related aspects. In addition, some other trial data may not reflect real-life circumstances, including the spectrum of comorbidities and various clinical or laboratory findings.

One solution to this dilemma lies in the generation and analysis of real-world data through observational studies. While these studies lack the internal control and randomization of RCTs [11], they offer complementary insights into how treatments perform in typical clinical environments. These studies also help assess the external validity of RCT findings and examine whether results are reproducible in everyday practice, outside the controlled setting of a trial.

Producing high-quality real-world data requires considerable effort. Academic studies often lack the financial resources and personnel available to industry-sponsored clinical trials. Proper database design, secure data storage, ongoing data maintenance, and cybersecurity measures are all essential. Digitalizing health care data and the availability of clinical, laboratory, and patient-reported information derived from routine clinical care represent opportunities to accelerate the generation of evidence. These opportunities encourage data evaluation from sources outside traditional clinical trials. Real-world data have potential to help develop better ways to prevent and cure disease, enhance quality of care, and empower patients to improve their health [12, 13].

Our analysis demonstrates that study cohorts of newly diagnosed CML patients do not fully represent the broader CML population. Therefore, observational real-world studies should be conducted to validate the findings of RCTs across diverse clinical contexts.
