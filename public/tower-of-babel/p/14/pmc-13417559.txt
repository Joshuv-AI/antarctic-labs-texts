An 83-Year-Old Woman With Sudden-Onset Large Volume Hemoptysis During Right Heart Catheterization
Authors: Sammy Tran, Maya Khodor, Adam Hurwitz, Everett Rogers, Ryan Charpin, Eddib Ahmed, Salah Al-andary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Case PresentationAn 83-year-old woman with a history of severe mitral and tricuspid valve regurgitation requiring surgical repair, sick sinus syndrome status after permanent pacemaker placement, and paroxysmal atrial fibrillation presented to the hospital for outpatient right heart catheterization as part of an evaluation for pulmonary hypertension and severe tricuspid regurgitation. During the procedure, the patient began to expectorate a large volume of blood. A rapid response was called due to concerns for respiratory compromise, and the patient was emergently intubated for airway protection. She was subsequently transported to the appropriate unit for further diagnostic studies.

Physical Examination Findings

The patient arrived in the ICU intubated, ventilated, and sedated. She was hemodynamically stable with a BP of 149/72 mm Hg, heart rate of 69 beats/min, respiratory rate of 20 breaths/min, temperature of 97.6 °F, and oxygen saturation of 99% on mechanical ventilation. Her exterior oral mucosa contained dried blood. Her pupils were equal, round, and reactive to light. On auscultation, she was found to have regular rate and rhythm without an appreciable murmur. Coarse breath sounds were appreciated bilaterally with equal rise while mechanically ventilated. Her abdomen was soft and nondistended. No edema was noted in her extremities. The remainder of her physical examination (musculoskeletal, genitourinary, and integumentary) was either unremarkable or limited due to sedation.

Diagnostic Studies

After arriving in the ICU, diagnostic bronchoscopy revealed diffuse tracheobronchial tree hemorrhage, with likely right middle lobe origin. Hemostasis was achieved with epinephrine. After repeat bronchoscopy, hematologic parameters remained stable with hemoglobin of 12.0 to 12.7 g/dL (normal: 11.2-15.7 g/dL), platelets of 151 to 210 ×103/μL (normal: 150-400 ×103/μL), and PT/INR of 12.2 s/1.1 (normal: 10.0-12.8 s/0.8-1.1, respectively). The remainder of her hematologic and metabolic profiles were within normal limits on ICU admission. However, an initial anterior-posterior portable chest radiograph revealed a large right-sided middle lobe opacification (Fig 1). A stat CT angiogram of the chest was performed, which revealed a large right middle lobe anomaly measuring 6.8 × 6.0 × 11.4 cm, with direct vascular communication with a subsegmental branch of the right middle lobe pulmonary artery system and significant adjacent compressive atelectasis (Fig 2).Figure 1Anteroposterior chest radiograph with dense right middle lobe opacification (blue arrow). What is the diagnosis?

Diagnosis: Pulmonary artery pseudoaneurysm

Discussion

Pulmonary artery pseudoaneurysm (PAPA) is a dilatation and outpouching of the tunica intima and media of the pulmonary artery without complete rupture of the tunica adventitia. This results in weakening of the arterial wall and the potential for complications such as extravascular compromise and rupture. This phenomenon can occur through several mechanisms, most commonly from infection (eg, TB, mucormycosis) or iatrogenic causes (eg, tissue manipulation, trauma). Depending on the size and stability of the pseudoaneurysm, presentation can vary from an incidental finding to acute hemorrhage. Inadvertent PAPA occurs in < 0.5% of right heart catheterizations (RHCs); however, the incidence of PAPA that occurs as a sequela of RHC is variable throughout the literature.

The morbidity and mortality rate of PAPA vary significantly depending on etiology. For example, a systematic review of PAPA after RHC identified 41 patients from 27 separate studies and found an admission to discharge mortality rate of 15%. Hemoptysis, the most common presenting sign of PAPA after RHC, was attributed as the cause 22% of the time in this cohort, likely due to rupture of the pseudoaneurysm. A fatal complication of iatrogenic PAPA due to RHC is pulmonary artery rupture, which has an estimated incidence of 0.031% and 0.05% and mortality rates of 70% and 50%, respectively, in 2 studies with large sample sizes. Depending on the size, presentation, and risk of rupture, management of PAPA can include pulmonary wedge resection, endovascular stent placement, coil embolization, or close observation. Coil embolization shows significant promise, with postintervention survival rates between 90% and 100% among different reviews. Pulmonary artery rupture often requires more extensive repair, including balloon occlusion and stent implantation, and is associated with a higher mortality rate.

Data from PAPA of infectious etiology varies, and accurate mortality rates are not well established. One case series examined 5 patients with COVID-19-associated pulmonary mucormycosis (CAPM) with PAPA (2 cases in which CAPM and PAPA were diagnosed concomitantly; 3 cases had COVID-19 preceding pulmonary mucormycosis). All patients were treated with some form of amphotericin B (either amphotericin B deoxycholate or liposomal amphotericin B). One patient, who had both coil embolization and lobectomy, had an overall positive clinical outcome, cessation of hemoptysis, subsequent discharge home after 45 days, and significant improvement on 2-month outpatient follow-up. One patient, who had only coil embolization, died 7 days after discharge. Two patients who did not undergo embolization died. One patient, who had a pneumonectomy performed, died in the postoperative period. One additional case of CAPM and PAPA outside of the 5-patient case series, a postrenal transplant recipient, had lobectomy performed. This patient showed clinical improvement after lobectomy and was successfully discharged home.

Overall, the utilization of plug embolization has comparable efficacy with coil embolization in survival rate with the added benefit of shorter procedure time, reduced radiation exposure, and durability of occlusion in larger vessels. Larger studies are required to assess the net benefit of using plug embolization over coil embolization as definitive treatment for PAPA.

Clinical Course

After discovery of the PAPA on chest CT angiography (CTA), the patient was taken emergently to the interventional radiology suite for percutaneous vascular plug embolization to the pseudoaneurysm neck. Immediately after this procedure, a repeat chest CTA redemonstrated the pseudoaneurysm measuring 7.9 × 18 cm with continuing vascular supply (Fig 3A). Furthermore, new subsegmental pulmonary emboli (PE) were noted within the right lower lobe (Fig 3B). Of note, the migration of plug adhesives is a known complication of plug embolization and may have attributed to the thrombotic event. Anticoagulation was deferred due to concern for pseudoaneurysm rupture. The patient subsequently passed a spontaneous breathing trial and was extubated to nasal cannula. She had sputum cultures collected, which revealed pan-sensitive Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which was treated with cefepime. Repeat chest CTA 5 days later showed improvement with the pseudoaneurysm measuring 7.9 × 10.2 cm (Fig 4), without active extravasation, and stable PE. Her hemoptysis improved and she was transferred out of the ICU. Surveillance chest CTA 1 week later demonstrated stable pseudoaneurysm (Fig 5) and new distal left lower lung PE. Ultrasound of the lower extremities showed a nonocclusive deep venous thrombus of the right common femoral vein. Clinical Pearls

1. PAPA is a rare phenomenon that occurs most commonly due to iatrogenic causes (eg, tissue manipulation, trauma) or infection (eg, TB, mucormycosis). 2. Pulmonary artery rupture is a rare complication of PAPA caused by RHC with a high mortality rate when not identified promptly. 3. Depending on the size and risk of rupture, pseudoaneurysms can be managed with surgical lobectomy, endovascular stenting, embolization, or close observation. 4. Larger studies are required to assess the net benefit of using plug embolization over coil embolization as definitive treatment for PAPA.
