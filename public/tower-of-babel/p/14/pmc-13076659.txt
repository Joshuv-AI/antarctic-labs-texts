The architecture and energy transfer pathways of PSI–LHCI–LHCII in the phototrophic flagellate Euglena gracilis
Authors: Guoqiang Huang, Shishang Dong, Lin Li, Lixia Zhu, Jiayi Bai, Xing Han, Jiasheng Jiang, Jinxin Ju, Sen-Fang Sui, Xiaochun Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Photosystem I (PSI) harvests light through light-harvesting complexes (LHCs) to drive electron transfer. Euglena gracilis, a secondary endosymbiotic green alga, yet the architecture of its PSI–LHCs supercomplex has remained unresolved. Here, we report a 2.06 Å cryo-electron microscopy structure of the E. gracilis PSI–LHCI–LHCII supercomplex, revealing a minimal PSI core associated with ten antennas arranged in two layers on the PsaM-facing side. Each inner and outer LHC forms a face-to-face pair, while four LHCI heterodimers, stabilized by conserved Loop_23 motifs, assemble into two tetramers bridged by a central LHCI–LHCII pair. Notably, a ~ 40-residue insertion in PsaD was observed, anchoring LHC-3 and stabilizing LHC belts. Moreover, Euglena-specific chlorophylls and a distinctive arrangement of red chlorophylls establish a distinct pigment network, channeling excitation-energy into the core. These findings uncover a lineage-specific strategy for light-harvesting, highlighting how secondary plastids remodel PSI–LHCs architecture to support photosynthesis.

Introduction

Photosystem I (PSI) is a large, multisubunit pigment-protein complex composed of a central PSI core surrounded by peripheral light-harvesting complex I (LHCI). It utilizes light energy to drive electron transfer from plastocyanin or cytochrome c6 to ferredoxin, ultimately leading to the production of NADPH, which is essential for carbon fixation1. The PSI core is highly conserved across cyanobacteria, algae, and plants, yet the peripheral light-harvesting antenna systems exhibit remarkable diversity reflecting evolutionary adaptation to variable light environments2,3. In typical land plants and green algae, a mono- or double-layered crescent-shaped LHCI, composed of four or eight Lhca proteins, is bound in a conserved manner to the PsaF/J side of the PSI core4–12.

The phototrophic flagellate Euglena gracilis (E. gracilis) represents a unique evolutionary branch that acquired its plastid via secondary endosymbiosis with a green alga13–16. Distinct from primary green-lineage organisms, including land plants and green algae, E. gracilis exhibits a number of unusual structural, genomic, and pigmentary features in its photosynthetic machinery. First, its PSI core contains only eight subunits (PsaA–PsaF, PsaJ, and PsaM) and lacks several conserved accessory proteins that are typically present in the green-lineage organisms. These include PsaK and PsaG, which facilitate LHCI binding, as well as PsaH, PsaL, and PsaO, responsible for the reversible association of light-harvesting complex II (LHCII) from photosystem II (PSII) during state transitions11,17–19. Second, E. gracilis LHC proteins are encoded as polyproteins that undergo post-translational cleavage into functional subunits prior to assembly into PSI or PSII. Phylogenetic analyses classify these LHCs into five Lhca and eight LhcbM groups20, and spectroscopic studies have further suggested that certain LHCs can dynamically associate with either PSI or PSII in a stage-dependent manner21,22. Third, 77 K fluorescence spectra revealed a red-shifted emission peak at 731 nm in E. gracilis23, closely resembling that of land plants and more red-shifted than the 714-nm peak typical of primary green algae such as Chlamydomonas reinhardtii (C. reinhardtii)11,24. In addition, E. gracilis lacks the typical xanthophyll-cycle carotenoids of the green-lineage organisms but instead possesses diadinoxanthin and diatoxanthin, which are common in red-lineage organisms25–27. These distinctive features make E. gracilis an intriguing model for exploring PSI–LHCI organization and adaptation in a secondary green plastid context. However, despite its evolutionary and physiological significance, the PSI–LHCI supercomplex of E. gracilis has not been structurally resolved, and its antenna arrangement, subunit composition, and energy transfer mechanisms remain largely unknown.

Here, we present the 2.06 Å cryo-EM structure of the E. gracilis PSI–LHCs (termed EgPSI–LHCI–LHCII) supercomplex, which reveals a minimal PSI core (PsaA–E, PsaM) surrounded by ten peripheral antenna subunits—nine Lhcas and one Lhcb—arranged exclusively on the PsaM-facing side. This architecture is distinct among known PSI–LHCI supercomplexes and uncovers how antenna organization, pigment distribution, and energy-transfer pathways have been evolutionarily remodeled to sustain photosynthetic function in this branch.

Results

A minimal PSI core surrounded by ten antennas on the PsaM-facing side

The EgPSI–LHCI–LHCII supercomplex was purified from E. gracilis thylakoid membranes. To obtain a structurally complete and homogeneous complex, multiple detergent conditions and gradient fractions were systematically evaluated by biochemical analyses, confirming the presence of the PSI core together with multiple associated antenna proteins (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b; see “Methods”). The room-temperature absorption spectrum displayed typical chlorophyll (Chl) a absorption peaks at 436.5 and 674.5 nm (Supplementary Fig. 1c). The 77-K fluorescence emission spectrum exhibited a major peak at 733 nm, the longest emission wavelength observed among green-lineage algae and closely matches that of angiosperms, most likely attributed to LHCI (Supplementary Fig. 1d)23,28,29. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) pigment analysis identified Chl a, Chl b, diadinoxanthin (Ddx), and β-carotene (BCR) (Fig. 1e–i), with absorption spectra characteristic of each pigment. Cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) resolved the EgPSI–LHCI–LHCII supercomplex at an overall resolution of 2.06 Å (Supplementary Fig.2 and Supplementary Table 1). The structure reveals a minimal PSI core composed of subunits PsaA–E and PsaM, semi-encircled by ten peripheral LHC proteins, all positioned exclusively on the PsaM-facing side (the side opposite to PsaF/J) of the PSI core (Fig. 1a). In the supercomplex, 187 Chl a, 20 Chl b, 35 carotenoids, 2 phylloquinones, 3 Fe4S4 clusters, 16 lipids and numerous water molecules were identified (Supplementary Table 2), with representative densities shown in Supplementary Fig. 3.

In E. gracilis, most LHC proteins are encoded as large polyproteins, each comprising up to five tandem Lhca or Lhcb subunits, with a short decapeptide linker separating each subunit20. Phylogenetic analysis classified all subunits derived from Lhca1-Lhca8 polyproteins (category 1 LHCs with diverse subunit compositions20) into five distinct isoforms (Supplementary Fig. 4a). These isoforms are numbered sequentially from 1 to 5 according to their position from the N- to C-terminus within the full-length LHC polyproteins (for example, Lhca5_1 denotes isoform 1 of the Lhca5 polyprotein) (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Based on the high-resolution structure and with the assistance of ModelAngelo, a deep-learning–based software for atomic model building into cryo-EM density maps, we identified the ten antenna proteins as nine LHCI subunits and one LHCII subunit (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 5). Subunit assignments were unambiguous for most LHCs, except for LHC-2 and LHC-10, where near-identical sequences could not be fully distinguished (Supplementary Fig. 5i,j); for consistency, both were designated as Lhca8_5. Structurally, the antennas are organized into two concentric belts. The inner belt—comprising LHC-1, −3, −5, −7, and −9 (derived from Lhca5_1, Lhca6_4, Lhca7_2, Lhca5_1, and Lhca6_4, respectively)—is tightly associated and directly anchored to the PSI core. The outer belt, including LHC-2, −4, −6, −8, and −10 (from Lhca8_5, Lhca5_3, Lhcbm10_3, Lhca5_3, and Lhca8_5, respectively), extends outward from the inner belt (Fig.1a).

Comparative structural analysis reveals two striking features of the EgPSI–LHCI–LHCII supercomplex. First, although the PSI core architecture formed by PsaA–E is relatively conserved, E. gracilis lacks several small subunits (PsaG, PsaK, PsaH, PsaI, PsaL, and PsaO) that are present not only in primarily endosymbiotic green algae such as Chlamydomonas reinhardtii (Fig. 1c) but also in land plants, suggesting a remodeling of the antenna-binding landscape. Second, unlike the PSI–LHCI complex green algae and land plants, in which LHCI subunits are predominantly located on the PsaF/J side of the PSI core, all ten LHC antennas in our structure are instead positioned exclusively on the opposite, PsaM-facing side (Fig. 1c).

Recently, a cryo-EM structure of the EgPSI–LHC supercomplex revealed that antenna proteins can associate with both the PsaF/J- and PsaM-facing sides of the PSI core31. Comparison of our structure with this recently reported structure reveals both shared and distinct organizational features (Supplementary Fig. 6a). On the PsaF/J-facing side, densities corresponding to the PsaF and PsaJ subunits, as well as the associated LHCI belt observed in the reported structure, are not resolved in our reconstruction. In contrast, on the PsaM-facing side, our structure reveals a more complete antenna system, including additional LHCII (LHC-6) and LHCI (LHC-10) subunits (Supplementary Fig. 6b), which are not fully resolved in that structure. Despite these differences in peripheral antenna composition, the shared PSI core and conserved LHCIs display highly similar conformations in the two structures, with a root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) of 0.334 Å, whereas local shifts are observed in the LHC6–10 region (Supplementary Fig. 6b).

Collectively, the observed differences in peripheral subunit and antenna occupancy are likely attributable, at least in part, to differences in detergent conditions and the labile nature of these components during purification, rather than to major alterations in the underlying PSI core architecture.

A Euglena-specific insertion of PsaD mediates the PSI–LHC-3 interaction

The EgPsaD-specific insertion extends into the spatial region normally occupied by PsaH, PsaL, and PsaO subunits in primarily endosymbiotic green algae and land plants (Fig. 2b), resulting in pronounced steric clashes, particularly with PsaL (Fig. 2b, c). As PsaL is highly conserved across most representatives of cyanobacterial, green- and red-lineages but notably absent in E. gracilis (Fig. 2d), these findings suggest a co-evolutionary scenario in which the acquisition of the PsaD insertion was coupled with the loss of PsaH, PsaL and PsaO.

Within the EgPSI–LHCI–LHCII structure, this PsaD insertion creates a distinct interface with the LHC-3 subunit (Fig. 2b, e). Specifically, Y342 of LHC-3 forms a hydrogen bond with E118 of PsaD, anchoring the antenna to the PSI core. In addition, R113 of PsaD engages in a hydrogen-bond network with Chls a1011 and a1012 of LHC-3, further stabilizing the interaction (Fig. 2e). Thus, the PsaD insertion not only compensates structurally for the missing PsaH/L/O subunits but also functionally recruits and stabilizes LHC-3, potentially optimizing excitation-energy transfer (EET) into the PSI core.

Hierarchical organization of LHC heterodimers and tetramers in the EgPSI–LHCI–LHCII supercomplex

Structural comparison of the ten LHC subunits revealed a conserved three-helix transmembrane core (denoted H1–H3 from N- to C-terminus), with the greatest structural variability confined two surface-exposed loops: stromal Loop_23 (between H2 and H3) and lumenal Loop_12 (between H1 and H2) (Supplementary Fig.8a, b). These ten LHCs organized into five heterodimers, each comprising one inner-belt and one outer-belt subunit to form a stacked two-layer antenna system (Fig.3a). The four LHCI–LHCI dimers adopt nearly identical architectures (Fig.3b). In each, the two monomers align in parallel in a face-to-face orientation, with their H2 helices closely apposed to form a tight interface, a mode that contrasts with the canonical face-to-back LHCI dimeric arrangement reported in other PSI–LHCI supercomplexes5–7. This interface is primarily stabilized by the conserved stromal Loop_23. Within Loop_23, several short conserved motifs mediate heterodimer formation, including WD-(6AAs)-R or WN-(6AAs)-R in inner antennas and WD-(5AAs)-R in outer antennas (where ‘nAAs’ denotes a stretch of n intervening residues; Fig. 3c, d). These Loop_23 motifs provide the key residues for dimerization: each monomer contributes a tryptophan (W) and an asparagine/aspartate (N/D) that pair with the partner’s arginine (R), establishing cross-monomer hydrogen bonds, cation–π interactions, and salt bridges (Fig.3c). By contrast, dimer3 represents an LHCI–LHCII pairs and exhibits a shifted helical arrangement and an altered Loop_23 orientation compared to LHCI–LHCI dimers (Fig.3e). Consistent with this, both Lhca7_2 and Lhcbm10_4 in dimer 3 lack these conserved Loop_23 motifs, and their interface is instead stabilized predominantly by hydrophobic contacts between H2 helices (Supplementary Fig.8c), resulting in a more flexible assembly.

To rationalize these differences, we analyzed all 37 LHC subunits derived from the 11 Lhca polyproteins (Supplementary Fig. 9). Isoforms 1 and 4 predominantly contain WD-(6AAs)-R and WN-(6AAs)-R motifs observed in our structure, as well as the similar WN-(6AAs)-Q motif. Isoforms 3 and 5 typically carry WD-(5AAs)-R motif, or similar motifs including WQ-(5AAs)-R and WF-(5AAs)-R. This motif distribution correlates with spatial positioning observed in our structure: isoforms 1 and 4 are located in the inner antenna belt, whereas isoforms 3 and 5 reside in the outer belt where they mediate LHCI–LHCI dimerization. By contrast, isoform 2 generally lacks such motifs and instead participates in the LHCI–LHCII pairing. Differences in motif composition, and hence in interaction strength, may explain why only a subset of subunits is resolved in our structure. Collectively, these observations establish a sequence-based framework for assigning distinct structural roles to Lhca isoforms in organizing the E. gracilis PSI antenna.

Building on these dimeric modules, adjacent LHCI–LHCI dimers organize into tetramers. In tetramer 1 (LHC-1/2/3/4), LHC-3 acts as a central hub, bridging the two dimers through hydrogen bonds with LHC-1 (N225–P120, T227–D113) and LHC-2 (D250–S63) (Fig. 3f), thereby creating a triangular interaction network. LHC-4, lacking direct contacts with LHC-1 or LHC-2, occupies a peripheral position but interacts with LHC-3 and LHC-5 via additional hydrogen bonds (Supplementary Fig. 10), establishing a stable crosstalk network that links LHC-1 to LHC-5. Tetramer2 (LHC-7/8/9/10) adopts a similar arrangement, with LHC-9 as the hub. Structural superposition of the two tetramers reveals slight positional shifts of monomers, particularly in LHC-4 and LHC-10 (Fig.3g). Tetramer 1 and tetramer 2 are bridged by LHCI–LHCII pairs positioned between them.

Together, these findings define two distinct organization modes in the EgPSI–LHCI–LHCII supercomplex: stable LHCI–LHCI dimers mediated by conserved Loop_23 motifs that further into tetramers, and a more labile LHCI–LHCII dimer stabilized by hydrophobic contacts.

Euglena-specific chlorophylls in LHCI and their structural roles

The EgPSI–LHCI–LHCII supercomplex contains 142 pigments distributed across ten LHC subunits, including 101 Chlsa, 20 Chlsb, and 21 Ddx (Fig.4a and Supplementary Table 1). For cross-species comparability, we adopted a unified Chl nomenclature based on canonical PSI–LHCI and LHCII structures from pea and spinach4,32. These pigments are principally arranged in two layers along the membrane (Fig. 4b). The stromal layer harbors a conserved set of eight Chls (a1002, a1003, a1009–a1012, b1001, b1008) that are present in every LHC subunit, similar to other green-lineage PSI–LHCI complexes4. First, all LHCs—except for LHC-6, an LHCII antenna—lack Chls a1006 and a1007, which are otherwise relatively conserved in LHCI of land plants and green algae. Second, two Chls (a1020 and a1021) are unique to E. gracilis and absent from known green- and red-lineage PSI–LHCI structures (Fig. 4c). These pigments are restricted to inner-belt LHCI subunits, with a1021 shifted toward the middle of the membrane (Fig. 4b). In LHC-1, Chl a1021 lies at the trimeric interface formed by LHC-1/-2/-3 of tetramer 1, in closest proximity to a1003/a1009 of LHC-2 and a1020 of LHC-3(Fig. 4d). Thus, the Euglena-specific a1021 and a1020 forms a pigment cluster that links LHC-2, LHC-1, and LHC-3. A comparable cluster is also present in tetramer 2. This arrangement establishes direct pigment–pigment contacts across subunits and provides a structural basis for enhanced antenna connectivity.

In summary, the Euglena-specific a1020–a1021 cluster creates inter-subunit bridges that reinforce the coupling between inner and outer LHCs, thereby stabilizing energy-transfer routes within each tetramer.

In tetramer 1 (around PsaA), excitation energy harvested by outer-belt LHC-2 and LHC-4 is funneled into their respective lowest-energy a1003–a1009 pairs. From there, outer-to-inner EET proceeds in two possible routes (Fig. 5b). One is a stromal–stromal route, in which excitation passes from a1003–a1009 pairs of LHC-2 and LHC-4 to those in inner-belt LHC-1 and LHC-3 ( ~ 15 Å). The other is a stromal–lumenal–stromal pathway mediated by the a1021–a1020 clusters: excitation proceeds from the a1003–a1009 dimer of LHC-2, through a1021 of LHC-1 ( ~ 12 Å) and a1020 of LHC-3, and finally into the a1003–a1009 pairs of LHC-3. A parallel pathway runs from LHC-4 through LHC-3 and LHC-5 in the same manner. Within the inner-belt LHCI, in addition to these a1021/a1020-mediated pathways, b1001–b1008 couplings, including b1001 of LHC-3 and b1008 of LHC-1 (10.9 Å), and b1001 of LHC-5 and b1008 of LHC-3 (11.0 Å), establish lateral connectivity among LHC-1, -3, and -5. Together, these couplings create a dense pigment network that enables efficient redistribution of excitation within the inner-belt LHCI. The final steps from inner LHCI into the PSI core occur at the closest interfaces between the inner LHCs and PsaA. Specifically, a1012 of LHC-3 couples with a901 of PsaA (15.6 Å), a1011 of LHC-3 with a843 of PsaA (15.2 Å), and a1012 of LHC-1 with a822 of PsaA (15.9 Å).

Tetramer 2 (around PsaB) exhibits a parallel architecture (Fig. 5c). Excitations harvested by outer-belt LHC-8 and LHC-10 are funneled into inner-belt LHC-7 and LHC-9 through both stromal–stromal and stromal–lumenal–stromal routes, followed by redistribution within inner-belt LHCs before entering the PSI core in PsaB side. Terminal coupling occurs via a1011 of LHC-7 to a821 of PsaB (15.0 Å), and a1012 of LHC-7 to a813 of PsaB (14.3 Å).

Besides, the outer LHCII subunit LHC-6 (Lhcbm10_3) provides an additional but relatively weak input, contacting LHC-7 at 18.9 Å. Excitation entering through LHC-6 must be relayed via LHC-7 and transferred via the inter-subunit b1001–b1008 bridge to LHC-9 before reaching the PSI core (Fig. 5c). Notably, LHC-6 is only partially occupied (classes 02 and 04, ~54% of particles; Fig. 5d), which may reflect a potential regulatory mechanism, reminiscent of the reversible PSI–LHCII association in state transitions17. Like LHCII, LHC-10 also exhibits a labile association with the PSI supercomplex. Its partial absence (Supplementary Fig. 2c) may reflect an alternative mode of antenna reorganization that modulates excitation flow within PSI independently of PSII. However, as the present structures were determined from a detergent-purified complex, we cannot exclude preparation-dependent heterogeneity, including effects arising from culture conditions or detergent treatment. Functional validation in vivo will therefore be required to establish the physiological relevance of this putative antenna rearrangement.

Taken together, the excitation landscape of EgPSI indicates that LHC-3 and LHC-9 act as the primary gateways for energy delivery into the PSI core, as they integrate multiple inputs from outer antennas while maintaining close spatial proximity to the reaction center. In contrast, LHC-1 contributes secondarily, whereas LHC-5 and LHC-7, despite receiving excitation, are positioned too far (> 20 Å) from the core to serve as high-efficient entry points (Fig. 5a). This arrangement illustrates a strategy of robust energy funneling, with potential regulatory flexibility provided by the labile and partially occupied LHCII and LHC-10.

Face-to-face red chlorophyll shape excitation flow in E. gracilis

The 77 K fluorescence-emission measurements of EgPSI–LHCI–LHCII reveal a prominent 733-nm peak (Supplementary Fig. 1d), which is markedly red-shifted compared with the bulk Chls that typically peak at 685 nm. This red-shifted characteristic of PSI–LHCI is shared by both land plants and green algae, although the exact emission peak wavelength varies among species24,33. In plant PSI–LHCI, red Chls (Chl a603–a609 pairs) are located at the antenna–core interface, and such an arrangement requires uphill excitation transfer from the inner antennas to the PSI core. In green algae PSI–LHCI, which contains two layers of LHC, red Chls locate at both the outer–inner antenna interface and the antenna–core interface (Supplementary Fig. 11a), there by requiring uphill excitation transfer from both the outer to inner antennas and from the inner antennas to the PSI core.

In E. gracilis, however, the five LHC heterodimers adopt a distinctive face-to-face arrangement (Fig. 3b, e), which restricts all red Chls (a1003–a1009 pairs) to the outer–inner antenna interfaces (Supplementary Fig. 11a). This configuration positions the a1011–a1012 pairs as the main entry sites to the PSI core, thereby reduce the need for uphill transfer at the antenna–core interface. At the same time, this arrangement imposes a trade-off: an uphill step may still occur within the inner antenna itself, as excitations must migrate from the a1003–a1009 pairs to the a1011–a1012 pairs. A further potential advantage arises from the close spacing between outer- and inner-belt red Chls (~14–15 Å between a1009 sites), which could allow direct transfer between these red Chls, bypassing the a1021–a1020 clusters and thereby eliminating energy-uphill from outer to inner antennas.

In land plants, the most red-shifted absorption is primarily attributed to Lhca3 and Lhca4, where an asparagine (Asn) residue serves as the ligand for Chl a603. Mutation of this Asn to histidine (His) abolishes the most red-shifted absorption, suggesting that Asn is a prerequisite for this property34–39. In our structure, all LHC Chl a1003 molecules are coordinated by His, except in LHC-3 and LHC-9, where they are ligated by Asn (Supplementary Fig. 11b). These observations suggests that LHC-3 and LHC-9 may contribute to the formation of the most strongly red-shifted Chl sites in E. gracilis, although other LHCs may also exhibit red-shifted forms. In addition, the red Chls likely act as an energy funnel, channeling excitation in LHC-3 and LHC-9, and thereby positioning LHC-3/-9 as the primary entry points for EET from the antennas into the PSI core, consistent with the EET pathways proposed above.

Collectively, these pigment networks highlight three distinctive features of EET in E. gracilis. First, the Euglena-specific a1021–a1020 clusters create inter-subunit bridges that reinforce connectivity within each tetramer and mediate a lumenal–stromal–lumenal transfer pathway. Second, multi-tiered transfer routes converge excitation predominantly into LHC-3 and LHC-9, while the labile binding of the LHCII (LHC-6) and LHCI (LHC-10) provides auxiliary input, together illustrating a strategy that combines robust energy funneling with regulatory flexibility. Third, the distinctive face-to-face arrangement of a1003–a1009 red Chls at the outer–inner interfaces minimizes uphill transfer both between outer and inner antennas and at the antenna–core interface. In addition, the exclusive incorporation of Ddx, instead of lutein or violaxanthin that predominate in green-lineage LHCs, highlights a distinct pigment-binding specificity in E. gracilis. This substitution may contribute to photoprotective regulation while reflecting its secondary plastid ancestry.

Methods

Purification of the EgPSI–LHCI–LHCII

E. gracilis cells (FACHB-848, Freshwater Algae Culture Collection at the Institute of Hydrobiology) were cultivated in 1 L of HUT medium at 24 °C for one week under continuous illumination at 30 μmol·m-2·s-1, using a modified method provided by FACHB. All subsequent procedures were conducted under green light conditions. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, yielding a pellet of 3 g (wet weight), which was then resuspended in 10 mL of lysis buffer [30 mM Tricine-Tris pH 8.0, 15.0 mM NaCl, 10% (w/v) sucrose]. Cells were lysed using a low-temperature ultra-high-pressure cell disruptor (JNBIO) at 1500 bar, followed by centrifugation at 17,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris and unbroken cells.

The supernatant was ultracentrifuged at 237,100 × g for 90 min to obtain a membrane pellet, which was resuspended in incubation buffer [30 mM Tricine-Tris pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 10% (w/v) sucrose] and incubated on ice for 30 min. Following a second ultracentrifugation under identical conditions (237,100 × g, 90 min, 4 °C), the pellet was resuspended in storage buffer (20 mM Tricine-Tris pH 8.0) to yield the thylakoid membrane solution.

To identify detergent conditions that preserve the integrity of the EgPSI–LHCI–LHCII supercomplex, multiple detergents and concentrations were systematically tested during thylakoid membrane solubilization (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Solubilization with 1% (w/v) n-octyl-β-D-glucoside (β-OG) resulted in poorly resolved pigment–protein bands after sucrose density gradient centrifugation, whereas n-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside (β-DDM) over a concentration range of 0.5–1% (w/v) led predominantly to dissociation of antenna proteins and photosystem cores. In contrast, solubilization with 2% (w/v) n-dodecyl-α-D-maltoside (α-DDM) enabled enrichment of a PSI–LHCI–LHCII–containing fraction, although partial loss of peripheral antenna subunits was still observed.

According, the thylakoid membranes were solubilized with 2% (w/v) α-DDM in an ice bath for 30 min. The insoluble fraction was removed by centrifugation at 17,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant containing the EgPSI–LHCI–LHCII supercomplex was then subjected to further purification through ultracentrifugation on a linear sucrose density gradient (300–900 mM) at 243,500 × g for 16 h at 4 °C, which resolved four distinct bands (Bands 1–4) in the gradient (Supplementary Fig. 1a).

Biochemical characterization of individual gradient fractions by SDS–PAGE and room-temperature absorption spectroscopy revealed that Band 1 mainly contained LHCII monomers and free pigments, Band 2 predominantly LHCII trimers, Band3 primarily PSII complexes with minor PSI contamination, whereas Band 4 was enriched in the EgPSI–LHCI–LHCII supercomplex (Supplementary Fig. 1b). The absorption spectrum of Band 4 measured at room temperature exhibited an absorption peak in the QY band region at 674.5 nm (Supplementary Fig. 1c), and 77 K fluorescence emission spectra were measured under 440 nm excitation (Supplementary Fig. 1d). The Band 4 sample was finally precipitated with 50% (w/v) Polyethylene Glycol 6000, dissolved in storage buffer, adjusted to a concentration of 2.5 mg/mL, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C for subsequent experiments.

Pigment analysis of EgPSI–LHCI–LHCII

All kinds of pigments were extracted from the purified EgPSI–LHCI–LHCII samples in 80% (v/v) ice-cold acetone and analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Thermo Scientific). The extracted samples were analyzed on an analytical C18 column (3.0 mm by 25 cm) (Thermo Scientific). The solutions used for elution consisted of solution A [0.1 M sodium acetate, 80% (v/v) methanol], solution B [90% (v/v) acetonitrile] and solution C [100% (v/v) ethyl acetate], and the elution was performed as follows: at the time of injection, 100% A; during the first 4 min, solution A decreases linearly from 100% to 0 while solution B increases linearly from 0 to 100%; from 4 to 18 min, solution B decreases linearly from 100% to 20% while solution C increases linearly from 0 to 80%; between 18 and 21 min, solution B rises linearly from 20% back to 100%, whereas solution C falls linearly from 80% to 0; from 21 to 24 min, solution B decreases linearly from 100% to 0 while solution A increases linearly from 0 to 100%; finally, constant 100% A for 6 min. The flow rate was 1.0 ml/min, and the column temperature was 40 °C. The eluent was monitored at 440 nm. All elution profiles and absorption spectra features are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1e-i.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data collection

A 4-μl aliquot of protein sample (~3 mg chlorophyll ml-1) was applied to glow-discharged holey carbon grids (Quantifoil Au R1.2/1.3, 300 mesh) and vitrified by rapid plunging into liquid ethane cooled by liquid nitrogen using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Plunge-freezing parameters were: blotting time, 7 s; blotting force, −1; waiting time, 10 s; chamber humidity, 100%; and chamber temperature, 8 °C.

Cryo-EM sample screening was carried out on a Tecnai Arctica or Talos Arctica 200 kV transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) equipped with a K2 direct electron detector (Gatan). High-resolution datasets were acquired on a Titan Krios electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific) operated at 300 kV, equipped with a K3 Summit direct electron detector (Gatan) and a GIF Quantum energy filter (Gatan) with a 20 eV slit width. Data collection was performed in super-resolution mode at a nominal magnification of 105,000 × (calibrated pixel size, 0.4175 Å) using AutoEMation40, with a defocus range of −1.5 to −1.0 μm. In total, 8,566 movies were recorded, each comprising 32 frames with a total exposure time of 1.28 s, corresponding to an accumulated electron dose of 50 e⁻ Å⁻².

Cryo-EM image processing

Data processing was performed in cryoSPARC41 (versions 4.5.0–4.6.0) with the latest software updates (Supplementary Fig. 2). Movie stacks were pre-processed using patch motion correction and patch contrast transfer function (CTF) estimation. Micrographs with a CTF fit resolution better than 5 Å were retained, resulting in 6,705 images for downstream analysis. An initial set of 1,187,379 particles was selected using blob-based auto-picking, followed by three iterative rounds of 2D classification to remove contaminants and poorly aligned particles, yielding a final dataset of 304,705 high-quality particles (Supplementary Fig. 2b).

These particles were subjected to ab initio reconstruction and heterogeneous refinement to yield a well-defined initial model of the supercomplex. Non-uniform refinement42 with defocus refinement and global CTF refinement after reference-based motion correction produced an overall reconstruction at 2.06 Å resolution from 104,987 particles. To analyze structural variability in the peripheral antenna, we performed 3D variability analysis (3DVA)43 and displayed in cluster mode, which resolved four distinct conformational clusters primarily differing in the organization of LHC subunits 6–10.

Particles from clusters 01, 03, and 04 (totaling 84,159) were selected for focused analysis. A soft mask was applied to subtract the PSI core and LHC 1–5, followed by a second round of 3DVA focused on LHC 6–10. This approach resolved four additional sub-clusters, from which particles corresponding to the most-complete densities of LHC 6–9 (clusters 02 and 04) and of LHC-10 (clusters 02, 03 and 04) were subjected to local refinement. The resulting focused maps were refined to resolutions of 2.60 Å for LHC 6–9 and 2.57 Å for LHC-10. These focused maps were then combined with the original overall map to generate a composite map for structural interpretation. Gold-standard FSC curves confirmed the final resolution, and angular distribution showed uniform particle orientation. Local resolution estimation indicated that PSI core region were resolved close to even better than 2 Å, while LHC antenna ranged from ~2.5-4 Å.

Model building and LHC subunits identification

The PSI core subunits (PsaA–PsaE and PsaM) of the EgPSI–LHCI–LHCII supercomplex were initially built automatically in ModelAngelo v1.0.1244 using their known protein sequences. Among these, the amino acid sequences of the PsaA–PsaC and PsaM subunits were obtained from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/)45, whereas those of the PsaD and PsaE subunits were derived from transcriptome data generated in this study (Supplementary Fig. 12). Pigment identities were inferred from the C. reinhardtii PSI–LHCI structure and HPLC pigment analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1e-i). Unlike most green-lineage complexes, E. gracilis expresses its LHC genes as polyproteins containing up to five tandem LHC units, complicating the assignment of individual LHC subunits. To address this, we used the non‑sequence mode of ModelAngelo to build de novo models of the LHC antennas based on the overall or focused cryo-EM maps (Supplementary Fig. 4a-f). These de novo models were used as queries for BLAST searches against the E. gracilis proteome. For each LHC or its segments, the top five BLAST hits were manually inspected, and only sequences whose side‑chain identities matched the cryo‑EM density were retained. This workflow enabled the reliable identification of nearly all ten LHC subunits.

For LHC-4 and LHC-8, the candidates were isoforms 3 or 5 of Lhca5, which differ only by a Val/Leu substitution. Local density at position 438 supported the presence of valine, allowing assignment of LHC-4 and LHC-10 as Lhca5_3 (Supplementary Fig. 4g,h). By contrast, LHC-2 and LHC-10 could only be assigned to isoform 3 or 5 of Lhca6 or Lhca8. These isoforms differ by a Ser/Cys substitution at the N-terminus and a Thr/Ala substitution at the C-terminus. The cryo-EM density supported alanine at the C-terminus (resolved in LHC-2 but not LHC-10), but could not discriminate between serine and cysteine at the N-terminus. Thus, although these antennas can be attributed to the Lhca6/Lhca8 polyproteins, definitive assignment to a specific isoform remains ambiguous (Supplementary Fig. 4i,j). Pigment positions in these subunits were assigned by comparison with the C. reinhardtii LHCI or LHCII model and verified against the density map. Especially, chlorophyll a and b were assigned based on a combination of local map features, pigment stoichiometry, and site conservation. In regions with higher local resolution (~2.5 Å), density consistent with the C7-formyl group enabled site-specific differentiation (Supplementary Fig. 3), whereas in lower-resolution regions (worse than 3 Å), assignments were guided by structurally homologous LHC sites. The overall assignments are consistent with pigment-binding patterns conserved in C. reinhardtii. The complete EgPSI–LHCI–LHCII model was refined through iterative manual adjustments in Coot (v0.8.9.2)46 and real‑space refinement in Phenix (v1.21.2‑5419)47. Interactions between subunits were analyzed using the PDBe PISA server (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/msd-srv/prot_int/cgi-bin/piserver) and through a manual check. Data collection, processing and refinement statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Phylogenetic analysis

Amino acid sequences of LHCs were retrieved from the UniProt database45 and aligned in MEGA v12 using the ClustalW algorithm48. The phylogenetic tree (Supplementary Fig. 3a) was inferred with IQ-TREE v3.0.1 (http://www.iqtree.org)49, with the best-fit substitution model automatically selected by ModelFinder50. Tree visualization was performed in iTOL v6 (https://itol.embl.de/)51, and branch support was assessed using the ultrafast bootstrap approximation with 1000 replicates.
