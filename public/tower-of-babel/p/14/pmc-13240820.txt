Study on the regulatory effect of ovalbumin-konjac glucomannan complexes-stabilized HIPPEs on the stability of loaded astaxanthin
Authors: Lijuan Zhang, Ziyue Wang, Jiaxin Li, Xingtao Zhao, Kun Ma, Shuang Zhao, Tingting Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Food-grade high internal phase Pickering emulsions (HIPPEs) prepared from natural protein-based particles have attracted much attention in the food manufacturing industry. This study developed ovalbumin-konjac glucomannan (OVA-KGM) complexes to stabilize astaxanthin (AST)-loaded HIPPEs. The 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex could stabilize 85% of the internal phase of HIPPEs. The complex formed a three-dimensional network structure around the oil droplets, thereby endowing HIPPEs with higher viscoelasticity, recovery, and thixotropy. The residual AST content in HIPPEs was 51.4% higher than that of free AST after storage at room temperature under visible light for 30 days. Importantly, the in vitro digestion results showed that the release rate of FFA in HIPPEs was as high as 96.7%, and the bioaccessibility of AST was improved to 46.9%. This work demonstrates that OVA-KGM complexes effectively solve the stability limitation of protein-based HIPPEs, providing an efficient, food-grade delivery system for hydrophobic bioactive substances like AST.

Introduction

Astaxanthin (AST) is a fat-soluble carotenoid that is widely present in algae, yeast, aquatic organisms, etc. (Shen et al., 2023). The structure of AST is rich in unsaturated double bonds and hydroxyl groups, and has many physiological functions such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-aging, anti-tumor activities, and lowering blood cholesterol (Zhou et al., 2024). However, due to its water insolubility and environmental sensitivity, AST is easily degraded during processing and storage, and has low bioavailability, which greatly limits the application of AST in functional food and medicine (Chen et al., 2018). To enhance the bioavailability of AST, researchers have encapsulated AST through various carrier systems such as microcapsules, liposomes, nano-dispersions, solid lipid nanoparticles and nanostructured lipid carriers (Sun et al., 2021). However, these carrier systems still face significant limitations, such as complex preparation processes, potential residual organic solvents, insufficient long-term stability, and often unsatisfactory bioavailability (Chen, Su, et al., 2022). Among available strategies, emulsion-based delivery systems stand out due to their relative simplicity of preparation, high encapsulation efficiency, and good biological safety (Sun, Li, et al., 2025). Critically, emulsions are highly effective in improving AST bioavailability (Ge et al., 2024). For instance, AST-loaded emulsions stabilized by fish gelatin effectively inhibited oxidation and light-induced degradation (Ho et al., 2024), while β-sitosterol oleogel-based emulsions enhanced AST bioavailability, correlating positively with fatty acid release (Wang, Qin, et al., 2024). These findings suggest that dispersing AST within the oil phase of an emulsion is a viable strategy to enhance its delivery and absorption.

Conventional emulsions stabilized by surfactants have been widely studied (Guo & Zhang, 2019). However, with consumers' pursuit of “clean-label” and natural ingredients, Pickering emulsion has attracted the attention of researchers (Jiang et al., 2020). Pickering emulsion is stabilized by solid particles that irreversibly adsorb at the interface, thereby eliminating the need for synthetic surfactants (Li, Yu, et al., 2024). These particle-stabilized emulsions offer superior stability against coalescence and Ostwald ripening, along with better environmental tolerance and edibility (Zhang, Huan, et al., 2023). High internal phase Pickering emulsions (HIPPEs) are prepared when the dispersed phase volume fraction is >74% (Sun et al., 2023). For oil-in-water HIPPEs, they were conferred excellent potential for applications in encapsulation and delivery of hydrophobic bioactive ingredients due to its lower water content, higher encapsulation rate, and easier to transport and store.

Protein-based particles have attracted much attention for preparing HIPPEs due to their excellent emulsifying properties, biocompatibility, and ability to form interfacial layers (Liu, Cheng, et al., 2023). For example, sonicated silkworm pupa protein has been used to formulate HIPPEs with potential for intestinal-targeted delivery (Jiang et al., 2023). However, single protein-stabilized HIPPEs, including those formed by ovalbumin (OVA), suffer from two fundamental structural defects: (I) a weak and brittle interfacial film due to insufficient intermolecular interactions at the oil-water interface, and (II) a limited or absent three-dimensional network in the continuous phase. These deficiencies make them highly susceptible to coalescence, Ostwald ripening, and phase separation under gravitational stress, thermal treatment, centrifugation, or long-term storage, which severely restricts their practical application in the food industry (Ribeiro et al., 2021). A promising strategy to overcome this weakness is to modify proteins with polysaccharides, forming complex particles that synergistically enhance HIPPEs' stability (Ma et al., 2024). Polysaccharides can enhance the viscosity of the continuous phase, and more importantly, they can interact with proteins to form an elastic interfacial layer and a cohesive three-dimensional network within the emulsion, thereby improving the structural stability of emulsions (Wang, Liu, et al., 2023). Such protein-polysaccharide complex-stabilized HIPPEs can effectively inhibit Ostwald ripening through a combined steric and physical barrier effect, enabling more efficient delivery of bioactive substances (Song et al., 2022). For instance, modifying whey protein with flaxseed gum effectively improved the encapsulation efficiency, stability, and bioaccessibility of AST (Zhang, Chen, et al., 2021). Furthermore, compared to systems like liposomes or nanoemulsions, which may suffer from burst release or physical instability in the gastrointestinal tract, protein-polysaccharide HIPPEs offer the advantage of a more controlled release profile synchronized with lipid digestion, potentially leading to enhanced and sustained bioavailability (Abdullah et al., 2020).

While several protein-polysaccharide HIPPEs systems have been explored for hydrophobic active substance delivery, these systems still have distinct limitations. For example, whey protein isolate-chitooligosaccharide HIPPEs rely on Maillard conjugation, which requires prolonged heating and may generate undesirable off-flavors (Yu et al., 2021). Casein-chitosan HIPPEs depend on electrostatic interactions that are highly sensitive to pH and ionic strength (Liu et al., 2022). Additionally, gliadin-based HIPPEs often require organic solvents for fabrication (Ma et al., 2020). In contrast, konjac glucomannan (KGM), a natural polysaccharide with exceptional thickening and gelling properties, is an excellent candidate for modifying proteins (Fei et al., 2023). Studies have shown that adding KGM to myofibrillar protein promotes protein unfolding, improving wettability and leading to HIPPEs with better storage stability and rheological properties (Wang, Liu, et al., 2023). Importantly, the OVA-KGM complex is formed through non-covalent, hydrogen-bond-dominated interactions under mild, aqueous conditions without organic solvents or prolonged heating, making it highly suitable for clean-label food applications. Therefore, modifying OVA with KGM presents a convenient and effective strategy to enhance the structural stability of HIPPEs.

The novelty of this work is threefold. Firstly, we systematically characterize the non-covalent interaction mechanism and binding kinetics between OVA and KGM using multi-spectroscopic techniques and molecular docking. Secondly, we demonstrate how the OVA-KGM complex simultaneously addresses the two defects of single-protein HIPPEs by forming a high-strength interfacial layer and a self-recovering three-dimensional network, thereby achieving superior stability. Thirdly, it provides the evidence that this interface-engineered system not only protects AST from environmental degradation but also significantly enhances its bioaccessibility by promoting controlled lipolysis during digestion, outperforming several previously reported systems. Therefore, in this study, we developed OVA-KGM complexes as novel particulate stabilizers for AST-loaded HIPPEs (Scheme 1). We aimed to (I) characterize the interaction mechanism between OVA and KGM, (II) investigate the ability of the complexes to stabilize HIPPEs, analyze their rheological properties and stability against environmental stresses, and (III) evaluate the protective effect of the OVA-KGM complex-stabilized HIPPEs on AST under UV, thermal, and storage conditions, as well as its performance during in vitro digestion. This work provides a new strategy for creating robust, food-grade HIPPEs and offers insights into the efficient delivery of hydrophobic bioactive substances.Scheme 1Schematic illustration of the formation and in vitro simulated digestion of HIPPEs stabilized by OVA-KGM complexes.Scheme 1

Materials and methods

Materials

Ovalbumin (Biotechnology grade) and konjac glucomannan (purity ≥95%) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. Corn oil was procured from Shandong Luhua Group Co., Ltd. Astaxanthin (purity >90%) and simulated saliva (pH 6.8) were purchased by Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. Bile salt (Cholic acid content ≥65%), trypsin (250 U/mg, CAS 9002-07-7), pancreatin (4000 U/g, CAS 8049-47-6), and pepsin (3000 U/g, CAS 9001-75-6), etc. were sourced from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co. Ltd. Other reagents were analytical grade.

Preparation and characterization of OVA-KGM complexes

Preparation of OVA-KGM complex

OVA was dispersed in deionized water at pH 7, stirred magnetically at room temperature for 4 h, yielding a 2.0–6.0% (w/v) solution. Prepared a KGM solution with a concentration of 0.2%–0.8% (w/v), adjusted pH to 7, and hydrated overnight to allow sufficient expansion and dissolution of KGM. 10 mL of OVA solutions (2.0%, 3.0%, 4.0%, 5.0% and 6.0% (w/v)) were mixed with 10 mL of 0.4% KGM solution and homogenized the ULTRA TURRAX®T18 high speed homogenizer (IKA, Staufen, Germany) at 8000 r/min for 1 min respectively, to obtain 20 mL of 1.0% OVA-0.2% KGM, 1.5% OVA-0.2% KGM, 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM, 2.5% OVA-0.2% KGM and 3.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complexes, respectively. 10 mL of 4.0% OVA solutions were mixed with 10 mL of KGM solutions (0.2%, 0.4%, 0.6% and 0.8% (w/v)) and homogenized at 8000 r/min for 1 min respectively, to obtain 20 mL of 2.0% OVA-0.1% KGM, 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM, 2.0% OVA-0.3% KGM, 2.0% OVA-0.4% KGM complexes, respectively. 2.0% OVA after all the OVA-KGM complex preparation processes was used as the control group.

Characterization of OVA-KGM complexes

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of the OVA-KGM complex were recorded on an IR Prestige-21 spectrometer (Shimadzu, Japan) from 4000 to 500 cm−1. Prior to analysis, the sample was mixed with KBr (1:100, w/w) and compressed into a tablet. The fluorescence spectra of OVA-KGM complex (0.5 mg/mL) were measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (LS55, PerkinElmer, United States) at an excitation wavelength of 280 nm. The ultraviolet spectra were obtained by scanning the sample (0.25 mg/mL) in the range of 220–500 nm with a UV–visible spectrophotometer (UV–vis) (UV-2450, Shimadzu, Japan). The secondary structure of the OVA-KGM complex (0.25 mg/mL) was analyzed using a circular dichroism spectrometer (CD) (Chirascan V100, Applied Photophysics, United Kingdom). Conformational changes during binding of OVA to KGM were monitored by using a circular dichroism spectrometer equipped with a stop-flow instrument (SF-CD) (Chirascan V100, Applied Photophysics, United Kingdom). The two syringes of the stop-flow instrument were filled with OVA (2 mg/mL) and KGM (0.2 mg/mL), respectively, and the solutions were rapidly and simultaneously injected into the mixing chamber. Then, the mixed solution was flowed into the cell of the polarized spectrometer, where the ellipticity of the sample solution was continuously measured at a wavelength of 222 nm. The dead time of the SF-CD system was approximately 1.2 ms. The 3D structure of KGM (PubChem CID: 58–86-6) was obtained from the PubChem database (Li et al., 2026). The crystal structures of OVA (PDB ID: 1OVA) were obtained from the RSCB Protein Database (Xie, Cao, et al., 2025). The molecular docking was performed using Autodock4 with a semi-flexible docking method, and the docking results were visualized using the PyMOL software. The three-phase contact angle (θ) of the OVA-KGM complex was observed by a drop shape analyzer (DSA100, KRÜSS, Germany). The freeze-dried OVA-KGM complex was pressed into 2 mm tablets, which were soaked in corn oil. The tablet was placed on a glass slide and 5 μL of water was added with a syringe. The topography of the droplet was obtained by a high-speed camera. To measure the dynamic interfacial tension, 30 μL of the OVA-KGM complex was dropped into the corn oil for 180 min, and the adsorption behavior of the OVA-KGM complex at the oil-water interface was measured by a droplet shape analyzer. The collected data were analyzed using the Young-Laplace equation method. The particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) of the OVA-KGM complex (0.5 mg/mL) were measured using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) analyzer (SZ-100, Horiba, Japan) at 25 °C. The zeta potential of the OVA-KGM complex was measured by a Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument (Malvern, UK). The emulsifying activity index (EAI) and emulsifying stability index (ESI) of the complex were measured by a UV–visible spectrophotometer (UV-1600, MAPADA, China) and then calculated.

Preparation and characterization of HIPPEs

Preparation of HIPPEs

To prepare different proportions of OVA-KGM complexes-stabilized HIPPEs, the complexes (for Section 2.2.1) were used as the aqueous phase, while corn oil was the internal phase (φ = 80%). The aqueous phase was mixed with corn oil, and the mixture was homogenized at 8000 rpm for 1 min using the ULTRA TURRAX® T18 high-speed homogenizer to prepare HIPPEs. To obtain HIPPEs with different internal phase volume fractions (φ = 74%, 77%, 80%, 85%, 88%), 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex was mixed with corn oil and homogenized at 8000 rpm for 1 min. The φ represents the percentage of the inner phase (corn oil) in the total volume of HIPPEs (the sum of the aqueous phase and the oil phase).

Morphological characterization

Upright and inverted appearance images of the emulsion were recorded by a camera. 10 μL of HIPPEs were placed on a glass slide, and the microstructure of the HIPPEs was observed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (IX83, Olympus, Japan) after covering the glass slide. The microstructure of HIPPEs was characterized by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, FV3000, Olympus, Japan). The corn oil and OVA were stained with Nile red (1 mg/mL) and Nile blue (1 mg/mL), respectively. A 10 μL of the stained HIPPEs was deposited onto glass slides and covered with coverslips. CLSM imaging was performed using two laser excitation sources at 488 nm (for Nile red) and 633 nm (for Nile blue), with two receiver channels. The particle size distribution of HIPPEs was statistically analyzed by Image J software, and the particle size of 150 droplets in the micrograph was recorded. The structural morphology of HIPPEs was further analyzed using cryo-scanning electron microscopy (cryo-SEM) (SU8010, Hitachi, Japan). The HIPPEs were placed onto a hollow cylindrical sample holder, immediately pre-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then transferred into a low-temperature preparation and transfer system for sublimation and gold sputtering. Finally, the samples were moved into a − 145 °C SEM testing chamber for observation (Wang, Cheng, et al., 2024).

Rheological behavior of HIPPEs

Rheological properties of HIPPEs using a rheometer (Discovery core, Beijing Teaijia Instrument Technology Co., Ltd., China) were measured by a 40 mm parallel plate. Strain sweep tests were performed at a fixed strain range of 0.8%–1000% and a fixed angular frequency of 10 rad/s to determine the linear viscoelastic region (LVR). The frequency sweep test was carried out under the condition of a 0.1–10 Hz frequency range and in the linear viscoelastic range at a fixed strain of 0.5%. The viscosity curve was obtained by determining the shear rate in the range of 0.1–100 s−1. The viscosity recovery was performed by shear rates of 0.1 s−1, 100 s−1, and 0.1 s−1 for 60 s, 20 s, and 100 s, respectively. The thixotropic recovery was determined at a strain of 0.1% or 100% for 200 s and a fixed frequency of 1 Hz, respectively. The temperature scanning test was carried out at 20–80 °C, the angular frequency was 10 rad/s, the fixed strain was 0.5%, and the temperature rise and fall were 5 °C/min.

Stability analysis

The HIPPEs were stored at room temperature under visible light for 30 days to determine their storage stability. To investigate the effect of pH on the stability of HIPPEs, the pH of the 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex aqueous phase was adjusted to 3, 5, 7, 9, and 11. For salt ion stability, NaCl was added to achieve final salt ions concentrations of 0, 50, 100, 200 and 400 mmol/L. Thermal stability was measured by heating at 90 °C for 1 h and cooling to room temperature. The centrifugal stability of HIPPEs was evaluated via centrifugation of the samples at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The appearance of HIPPEs after storage, pH, salt ion, centrifugation, or heat treatment was recorded and their microstructure and particle size changes were analyzed. The HIPPEs were diluted 2500-fold with DI water. And the zeta potentials HIPPEs were measured three times using a Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument (Malvern, UK) at 25 °C. Interfacial protein adsorption percentage was analyzed on the basis of the previous experimental method (Liu et al., 2024). The oil loss rate was analyzed by centrifuging fresh HIPPEs at 10000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, weighing and calculating.

Determination of lipid oxidation in HIPPEs

Primary oxidation products and secondary oxidation products of HIPPEs were determined according to previous experimental methods (Zhang et al., 2022). To accelerate oil oxidation, freshly prepared HIPPEs were incubated for 15 days at 50 °C.

The supernatant was obtained by treating 0.4 mL of HIPPEs with 3.0 mL of isooctane/2-propanol at a volume ratio of 3:1, vortexing for 3 min, and then centrifuging at 8000 rpm for 5 min. To 0.4 mL of the resulting supernatant, 5.6 mL of 1-butanol/methanol (1:2, v/v) was added, followed by sequential addition of 30 μL ammonium thiocyanate and Fe2+ solutions. After dark reaction of the above samples for 20 min, the absorbance at 510 nm was recorded. The lipid hydroperoxide (LH) concentration was determined based on the H2O2 standard curve.

Secondary lipid oxidation products: 0.4 mL of HIPPEs were mixed with 1.6 mL of deionized water and 8 mL of thiobarbituric acid (TBA). The sample was heated at 100 °C for 10 min and then cooled to room temperature. After centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 5 min, the absorbance of the sample at 532 nm was measured. The malondialdehyde (MDA) content was calculated according to the standard (1, 1, 3, 3-tetraethoxypropane).

Preparation and stability analysis of AST-loaded HIPPEs

Dissolved the AST with a concentration of 0.1 mg/mL in corn oil, and homogenized it with the OVA-KGM complex solution at 8000 rpm for 1 min to obtain HIPPEs loaded with AST. The AST-loaded HIPPEs were diluted 25-fold using a dichloromethane/methanol mixture (2: 1, v/v) and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min, and the absorbance in the organic phase was measured by a UV–visible spectrophotometer at 480 nm. The AST standard curve was used to calculate the content of AST in HIPPEs. The encapsulation efficiency and loading capacity of AST in HIPPEs were calculated by the following formula:Encapsulation efficiency%=MH/MI×100Loading capacitymg/mL=MH/VTwhere MH represents the mass of AST loading in HIPPEs, MI represents the total mass of the AST added to HIPPEs and VT represents the total volume of the AST loading HIPPEs.

The UV stability of AST was measured by irradiating the sample under UVB for 6 h. Storage stability was determined by storing the samples for 30 days at room temperature and under visible light conditions. Finally, the change in AST content of HIPPEs loaded with AST was determined after incubation in a water bath at 50 °C for 30 days. The residual AST was calculated by the following formula:ResidualAST%=Ct/C0×100where Ct represents the AST content after treatment and C0 represents the initial AST content.

Analysis of digestion characteristics of AST-loaded HIPPEs

Construction of in vitro digestion model of AST-loaded HIPPEs

The stability and bioaccessibility of AST in HIPPEs were evaluated using simulated in vitro digestion experiments. The configuration of simulated saliva, simulated gastric juice, and simulated intestinal juice was slightly modified according to the previous method (Miao et al., 2021). All digestion processes were performed at 37 °C, and both samples and digestion juice were pre-incubated at 37 °C for 10 min before use. Initially, the sample was mixed with an equal volume (7.5 mL) of simulated saliva and digested at 100 rpm for 10 min at pH 6.8. The simulated gastric juice was prepared by dissolving NaCl (2 g/L) and pepsin (3.2 g/L) in deionized water. Next, the same volume (15 mL) of simulated gastric juice as the orally digested sample was added, the pH was adjusted to 2, and incubated at 100 rpm for 120 min. The simulated intestinal fluid (7.5 mL) contained 1.5 mL of CaCl₂ (36.7 mg/mL) and NaCl (218.7 mg/mL) solutions, 2.5 mL of PBS solution containing pancreatin (60 mg/mL) and lipase (60 mg/mL) and 3.5 mL of bile salt solution (187.5 mg/mL). Finally, the simulated intestinal fluid (7.5 mL) was added to the gastric digestion mixture, the pH was adjusted to 7 and digested at 100 rpm for 120 min. Visual appearance of the digestive process in the oral, gastric, and small intestinal phases of the samples were observed using a camera. The microstructure variations of the HIPPEs stained with Nile Red during gastrointestinal digestion were analyzed by CLSM.

Determination the release rate of FFA

For the determination of the free fatty acid (FFA) release rates during intestinal digestion, the HIPPEs need to be diluted to a level of 4% corn oil before the experiment begins, according to the previous methods (Ma et al., 2020). The digestion was titrated using 0.02 mol/L NaOH so that its pH was maintained at 7.0, recording the volume of NaOH consumed over 2 h (Huang et al., 2024). The FFA release rate was calculated by the following formula:FFA%=VNaOH×CNaOH×Moil/2×moil×100where VNaOH, CNaOH, Moil and moil represent the volume of NaOH consumed, the concentration of NaOH solution (0.02 M), the average molecular weight of corn oil, and the mass of lipids initially present in intestinal digestion, respectively.

Bioaccessibility of AST

Bioaccessibility of AST was determined using undiluted HIPPEs. After the in vitro simulated digestion was completed, the digested juice was centrifuged at 10000 rpm for 60 min to collect the middle layer astaxanthin micellar phase. The content of AST is determined according to the method in Section 2.4. The transformation, bioaccessibility index, and bioaccessibility of AST were calculated as follows:Transformation%=C1/C0×100Bioaccessibility index%=C2/C1×100Bioaccessibility%=C2/C0×100where C0, C1 and C2 represent initial AST content, AST content in digest juice, and AST content in micellar phase, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were replicated in triplicate and data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. All figures were drawn using Origin 2021. One-way ANOVA was performed using SPSS 27 software and statistical differences were compared, p < 0.05 was considered significant difference.

Results and discussion

Characterization of OVA-KGM complex

In this study, OVA-KGM complexes were constructed based on non-covalent interactions, and the preparation process is shown in Fig. 1A. FTIR spectroscopy was able to identify the interactions between proteins and other molecules, as shown in Fig. 1B, and the absorption peak near 3400 cm−1 reflected the stretching vibration of intermolecular hydrogen bonds (O—H) (Li, Zhang, et al., 2024). The introduction of 0.1%, 0.2%, 0.3% and 0.4% KGM shifted the absorption peaks of OVA from 3384.14 cm−1 to 3391.35 cm−1, 3392.17 cm−1, 3391.82 cm−1 and 3390.54 cm−1, respectively. The results showed that compared with OVA, the absorption peaks of the amide A band in the OVA-KGM complex were all blue-shifted, indicating the presence of hydrogen bonding interactions between OVA and KGM in the complexes (Huang et al., 2025). Furthermore, compared with OVA, the absorption peaks of OVA-KGM complex in the amide I band (C—O stretching vibration) and amide II band (C—N stretching vibration and N—H bending vibration) also shifted. Fig. S1A shows the effect of different concentrations of OVA on the structure of the OVA-KGM complex. The microenvironment around hydrophobic amino acid residues can cause changes in the intrinsic fluorescence spectra, which is often used to analyze the changes in protein tertiary structure. The fluorescence spectra of the OVA-KGM complex are shown in Fig. 1C, and the maximum emission wavelength of OVA is about 340 nm at the excitation wavelength of 280 nm. In the non-covalent binding of OVA and KGM, the intrinsic fluorescence of OVA was partially quenched. With the increasing proportion of KGM, the fluorescence intensity of the OVA-KGM complex initially decreased and then increased, with the 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex showing the lowest fluorescence intensity. This indicated that the interaction between OVA and KGM shifts the aromatic amino acids to the hydrophilic region, resulting in a shielding effect. Aromatic amino acids in hydrophobic environments exhibited higher quantum yields, and the fluorescence intensity was significantly reduced when they moved from the core of the protein to a polar environment (Liu, Shan, et al., 2023). Fig. S1B illustrates the effect of different OVA concentrations on the fluorescence intensity of the complex, further confirming that the 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex exhibits the lowest peak. This phenomenon might be attributed to the formation of non-covalent interactions between OVA and KGM, which led to the quenching of fluorescence of tryptophan residues in OVA. The binding of KGM to OVA likely altered the microenvironment surrounding the tryptophan chromophores (Wang, Ma, et al., 2023).

The interactions inside the OVA-KGM complex were further analyzed by UV spectroscopy. The results are shown in Fig. 1D, all samples showed typical absorption peaks around 280 nm, which is due to the absorption of UV light by the side chain groups of tryptophan and tyrosine residues in the OVA structure (Wang et al., 2020). With the increase of KGM, the intensity of the UV absorption peak first increased and then decreased, and the UV absorption peak of 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM reached the maximum value. Furthermore, Fig. S1C shows that with the increase of OVA concentration in the complex, the UV absorption peak of the OVA-KGM complex shows a trend of rising first and then decreasing. The fluorescence intensity of complex formation with KGM was stronger when the OVA concentration was 2.0%, mainly because KGM promoted the gradual unfolding of OVA structure, resulting in full exposure of hydrophobic groups (Li, Zhang, et al., 2024).

The influence of KGM addition on the structure of OVA was analyzed by circular dichroism spectroscopy (CD). As shown in Fig. 1E and Table S1, OVA contained 43.6% α-helix, 16.1% β-sheet, 13.2% β-turn, and 27.0% Random coil. Compared with OVA, the content of α-helix in 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM decreased to 32.8%, β-sheet increased to 20.4%, β-turn and Random coil increased to 16.3% and 30.5%, respectively, which might be due to the thermodynamic incompatibility between protein and polysaccharide, which led to the gradual unfolding of the structure of OVA, exposing more hydrophobic groups and promoting the hydrophobic interaction between OVA molecules (Ma et al., 2025). The decrease in the content of α-helix made the OVA-KGM complex more easily surrounded by solvent molecules, which might enhance the encapsulation effect of the OVA-KGM complex on hydrophobic active substances (Yan et al., 2024). The increase in β-sheet content indicated that KGM could promote the formation of more extended, flexible structures of OVA, which was conducive to the more effective diffusion and rearrangement of the OVA-KGM complex at the oil-water interface to form a denser interface film (Jian Cedric Sow & Du, 2024). Moreover, the introduction of KGM promoted the unfolding of the hydrophobic region of OVA, which would facilitate the anchoring of the hydrophobic groups of OVA at the oil interface, thereby enhancing the emulsifying properties of the complex.

To investigate the binding kinetics of the interaction between OVA and KGM, conformational changes during the binding of OVA to KGM were monitored by SF-CD. The time-dependent conformational changes during the binding of OVA and KGM revealed that at the beginning of the binding interaction, the CD signal showed a linear change, and then approached the limit value at approximately 5.7 s (Fig. 1F). In the initial stage, the interaction between KGM and OVA mainly led to the partial unfolding of the secondary structure of OVA. Subsequently, the conformational rearrangement of the OVA -KGM complex occurred and tended to stabilize. The rapid conformational changes supported the hypothesis that the OVA-KGM complex could rapidly adapt and reorganize at the oil-water interface during the emulsion formation process.

To further determine the binding sites and main interaction forces between OVA and KGM, molecular docking was performed on them, as shown in Fig. 1G. The molecular docking analysis predicted a strong binding affinity between OVA and KGM with a binding energy of −5.0 kcal/mol. KGM combined with amino acid residues Thr104, Lys105, Asn107, Tyr110, Ser111, and Phe112 of OVA to form hydrogen bonds. The hydrophobic interaction was formed between the amino acid residue Pro106 of OVA and the C1 and C2 atoms of KGM. Importantly, multiple contact points between KGM and OVA contribute to enhancing the stability of the complex at the interface, facilitating the extension of KGM into the aqueous phase for directional adsorption, and promoting the formation of a dense interfacial network through cross-linking between adjacent complexes (Li, Wang, et al., 2024). The interfacial wettability of the OVA-KGM complex was evaluated by the θ. Studies have shown that when θ approaches 90°, the particles exhibit a strong ability for interfacial adsorption, which is beneficial for their attachment to the oil-water interface, thereby contributing to the stability of the emulsion structure (Chen, Yi, et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 1H, the θ values of 2.0% OVA, 2.0% OVA-0.1% KGM, 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM, 2.0% OVA-0.3% KGM and 2.0% OVA-0.4% KGM were 68.1°, 76.4°, 82.0°, 77.6° and 75.8°, respectively. Compared with OVA, the OVA-KGM complex significantly improved the wettability of OVA (p < 0.05). Notably, the θ of 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex approached 90°, indicating that the complex possesses balanced amphiphilicity and strong interfacial adsorption affinity. The dynamic interfacial tension between the two phases was measured to investigate the adsorption behavior of OVA-KGM complexes at the interface (Fig. 1I). In general, the adsorption kinetics of the complexes mainly consists of three steps, (I) diffusion of the complexes onto the oil interface, (II) penetration of the complexes at the oil interface, (III) rearrangement of the complexes at the oil-water interface and formation of the interface film (Chen et al., 2025). Compared with OVA, the complex was more effective in reducing the oil-water interfacial tension, thereby promoting the interfacial adsorption kinetics, and demonstrating the high potential for stabilizing emulsions. Importantly, the 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex reached a steady state within 180 min and exhibited the lowest interfacial tension, which indicated that the complex diffused to the oil-water interface and rapidly adsorbed to form a stable interfacial layer. This might be due to the fact that KGM promoted the unfolding of the hydrophobic region of OVA, and the hydrophobic groups of OVA were anchored at the oil interface, while the hydrophilic part of KGM was oriented towards the aqueous phase, thus forming a stable protective layer. However, when the KGM was higher than 0.3%, the complex exhibited a relatively high interfacial tension, which indicated that an excessively high KGM concentration would reduce the adsorption efficiency of the complex at the oil-water interface.

The particle size could reflect the migration rate of the complex at the oil-water interface to some extent. On the oil-water interface, smaller particles quickly aggregated to form a dense protective layer, which could effectively prevent the coalescence of oil droplets and reduce the interfacial tension, while the adsorption rate of larger particles was slower (Zhao et al., 2023). Compared with OVA, the particle size of OVA-KGM complexes decreased, and the average particle size of 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complexes was 208.8 nm (Fig. 1J). This reduction might be attributed to the strong shear forces during high-speed homogenization, which facilitated to the dissociation of protein aggregates and promoted the formation of more uniform OVA-KGM complexes. In addition, as the proportion of OVA increased (Fig. S2A), the particle size of the OVA-KGM complex first increased and then decreased, among which the particle size of 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM was the smallest, possibly due to the excess protein combined with the complex to form larger-sized particles, resulting in the particle size rise. Moreover, the PDI change trend of the complexes was similar to the particle size results, and 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM had good uniformity and stability. The repulsive force between particles could effectively prevent particle aggregation, enabling the OVA-KGM complex to form a more stable dispersion system in aqueous solution. Excessive polysaccharide concentration would cause particles to form aggregates, because when polysaccharide molecules per unit volume were dense, it would cause the penetration imbalance of adjacent colloidal particles, resulting in the particles squeezing each other to form flocs (Zhu et al., 2019). As shown in Fig. S2B, at pH 7, the zeta potential of OVA was −24 mV. Compared with OVA, the absolute value of the zeta potential of the OVA-KGM complex decreased slightly when the KGM concentration was from 0.1% to 0.2%. However, when the KGM concentration exceeded 0.2%, the absolute value of the complex zeta potential decreased significantly (p < 0.05), mainly because the excess neutral KGM chains shielded the surface charge of the protein (Yu et al., 2024). This charge shielding effect weakens the electrostatic repulsion between the adsorbed complexes at the oil-water interface, resulting in a decrease in the resistance of the interface layer to the droplet approach, thus promoting the flocculation and agglomeration of the droplets.

To investigate the emulsifying properties of the OVA-KGM complex, specifically its capacity for interfacial adsorption and subsequent stabilization of emulsion droplets, the emulsifying activity index (EAI) and emulsifying stability index (ESI) of the complex were analyzed. The EAI and ESI of the OVA-KGM complex are shown in Figs. 1K and S2C–D. Compared with OVA, the protein-polysaccharide interaction increased the EAI and ESI of the complex, and the EAI and ESI of 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM reached 17.6 m2/g and 73.1 min, respectively. Mainly, after the combination of OVA and KGM, the hydrophobic groups hidden in the protein molecules were exposed and adhered to oil droplet surfaces, while the hydrophilic groups of polysaccharides combined with water, effectively preventing the oil-water separation caused by the aggregation of oil droplets, thereby synergistically stabilizing the emulsion (Deng et al., 2023). Overall, the driving force between OVA and KGM was dominated by hydrogen bonding, supplemented by hydrophobic interaction. This driving force promoted the unfolding of hydrophobic groups of OVA, which facilitated the complex to adsorb at the oil-water interface more rapidly and stably.

To further investigate the structural characteristics and stabilization mechanism of HIPPEs, their microstructures were observed using Cryo-SEM (Fig. 2C). The HIPPEs stabilized by OVA exhibited a relatively loose structure with incomplete particle coverage at the oil-water interface, which might lead to direct contact and aggregation of adjacent droplets. In contrast, HIPPEs stabilized by the OVA-KGM complex showed a more compact structure in which the droplets were tightly crosslinked by the complex. It was noteworthy that the HIPPEs stabilized by the 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex displayed a dense interface shell, and a solid 3D network structure was formed between the droplets, which was beneficial to enhance the stability of HIPPEs. However, when the KGM concentration was higher than 0.2%, the excess KGM competed with the OVA-KGM complex for interface adsorption, resulting in larger droplet sizes and reduced uniformity. Previous studies have also shown that high concentrations of KGM result in an increase in the concentration gradient from the continuous phase to the surface of the droplet, reducing the stability of the interface film and causing the droplets to aggregate (Xie, Zhang, et al., 2025).

Fig. 2D shows a schematic diagram of the mechanism of HIPPEs formed from the OVA-KGM complex. The concentration of KGM could regulate the adsorption behavior of the complex on the surface of oil droplets, thus affecting the stability of the emulsion. The droplets of HIPPEs stabilized only by 2.0% OVA and 2.0% OVA-0.1% KGM complexes were loosely arranged and poorly homogeneous. The droplet size of HIPPEs stabilized by 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex was small and uniform, which was due to the interfacial shell formed by OVA and KGM on the surface of oil droplets via intermolecular hydrogenic bonding, and the gel network structure formed by hydrogen bonds between KGM molecular chains filled between droplets, thus preventing droplets from coalescing. However, when the KGM concentration increased to 0.3%–0.4%, KGM competitively adsorbed with the complex at the oil-water interface, reducing the effective emulsification area of the complex, and emulsion droplets aggregated.

Rheological properties of HIPPEs

The rheological properties are important indicators for evaluating the texture, stability and application value of HIPPEs. Shear strain scanning was used to evaluate the effect of different KGM concentrations on the permanent deformation resistance of HIPPEs (Xu et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 3A, the OVA-KGM complex-stabilized HIPPEs have a relatively broader linear viscoelastic region (LVR) compared to the OVA-stabilized HIPPEs. The loss modulus (G″) was consistently nearly an order of magnitude lower than storage modulus (G′) across the LVR for all HIPPEs, indicating that the gel structure of HIPPEs is dominated by elastic behavior. Compared with OVA-stabilized HIPPEs, the linear viscoelastic region range of complex-stabilized HIPPEs was increased, indicating that their network structure is stronger. As the shear strain increased, the structure of HIPPEs was damaged, and G′ subsequently decreased. When the strain reached about 50%, G′ and G″ of HIPPEs intersected, which was called the yield point here, marking the change of HIPPEs from elastic behavior dominance to viscous behavior dominance, which is mainly due to the structure being damaged by shear forces and rearranged. The higher the yield point in the strain range, the stronger the resistance of HIPPEs to external stresses. The yield point of HIPPEs stabilized by OVA was approximately at 50% strain. The yield point of HIPPEs stabilized by the complex appeared at a higher shear strain. Importantly, the yield point of HIPPEs stabilized by 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM appeared at around 90% strain, indicating the stronger structural stability and destructive resistance of this HIPPEs (Zhan et al., 2023). Based on the microstructure of HIPPEs (Fig. 2A and C), which was mainly due to the formation of a dense interfacial shell at the oil-water interface by this complex, and the formation of a 3D network between droplets, which enhanced the structural stability of HIPPEs.Fig. 3Shear strain scans (A), frequency scans (B), and shear rate scans (C) of HIPPEs. The frequency sweep results of HIPPEs are shown in Fig. 3B. The G′ of HIPPEs was always greater than the G″ throughout the entire scanning frequency range, indicating that HIPPEs have solid-like properties. The G′ and G″ of HIPPEs gradually increased with increasing frequency, which also proved that the structure of HIPPEs was dominated by elastic gel. The G′ and G″ of HIPPEs stabilized by OVA-KGM complex were significantly higher than those of HIPPEs stabilized by OVA, indicating that the addition of KGM effectively improved the gel network structure of HIPPEs. As shown in Cryo-SEM (Fig. 2C), the network structure was formed between the droplets of HIPPEs stabilized by OVA-KGM, which also confirmed this result. Moreover, with the increase of KGM concentration, the G′ and G″ HIPPEs of stabilized by the OVA-KGM complex also gradually increased. Nevertheless, when the concentration of KGM was raised to 0.3%, there was no significant increase in G′ and G″ values, indicating that the additional KGM did not significantly affect the gel strength of HIPPEs.

The viscosity of HIPPEs stabilized by the OVA-KGM complex as a function of shear rate is shown in Fig. 3C. As the shear rate increased, the viscosity of all HIPPEs decreased, exhibiting typical shear-thinning behavior. The viscosity of the OVA-KGM complex-stabilized HIPPEs was improved compared to the OVA-stabilized HIPPEs. Moreover, the HIPPEs' viscosity demonstrated a positive correlation with KGM concentration, which might be that KGM thickened the aqueous phase of the emulsion (Zhan et al., 2023).

To assess the structural recovery capability of HIPPEs, the viscosity of HIPPEs as a function of shear rate was measured. As shown in Fig. 3D, all samples have similar trends. When the initial shear rate was 0.1 s−1, HIPPEs had a relatively high viscosity, and this viscosity remained stable over time. When the shear rate increased to 100 s−1, the viscosity of HIPPEs significantly decreased, which was attributed to the shear thinning behavior caused by the disruption of the gel structure. When the shear rate decreased to 0.1 s−1, the viscosity of HIPPEs quickly recovered to close to the initial state, indicating that the gel network structure of HIPPEs recovered. To further analyze the structural recovery capability of HIPPEs quantitatively, the G′ at the end of interval 1 was taken as 100% and compared with the peak G′ of interval 3 (Zhang et al., 2022). Studies have shown that the recovery rate of samples is over 70%, and it is considered that HIPPEs have good thixotropic recovery (Yuan et al., 2024). The results indicated that the recovery rate of HIPPEs stabilized by the OVA-KGM complex was greater than 90%, and the recovery rate of HIPPEs stabilized by 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex reached 95%. This high recovery rate from the fact that the introduction of KGM could promote the formation of more extended, flexible structures of OVA, which was conducive to the more effective diffusion and rearrangement of the complex at the oil-water interface, thus re-forming a stable HIPPEs structure. This is also consistent with the results of CD (Fig. 1E) and dynamic interfacial tension (Fig. 1I). The above results demonstrated that the HIPPEs stabilized by the OVA-KGM complex had the ability to reconstruct the network structure and maintain the semi-solid state (Yuan et al., 2024). Moreover, the thixotropic recovery of HIPPEs under different strain conditions was analyzed, and the results are shown in Fig. 3E. At the initial stage when the strain was 0.1%, HIPPEs maintained a relatively high G′. When the strain reached 100%, the G′ of HIPPEs dropped rapidly. When the strain returned to the initial stage, the G′ of HIPPEs rapidly recovered, and the elastic solid properties of HIPPEs could be quickly restored. This result was consistent with the results obtained in the shear rate test. And the thixotropic recovery rate of all complex-stabilized HIPPEs was higher than 100%, which might be advantageous in food processing applications with higher requirements for deformation tolerance.

Heat treatment process is usually used in food processing, so the stability of HIPPEs under heating-cooling cycle is evaluated by dynamic temperature scanning test. As shown in Fig. 3F, when the temperature was heated from 20 °C to 80 °C, the G′ and G″ of HIPPEs did not change significantly, indicating high resistance to thermal breakdown under the tested conditions. In Fig. 3G, G′ and G″ of HIPPEs show an upward trend with the decrease of temperature, presenting typical elastic gel characteristics. In the temperature-changing test, both G′ and G″ values of HIPPEs were higher than the initial values, which might be due to the evaporation of part of the moisture in HIPPEs under the heating-cooling cycle and the more compact gel network structure of the emulsion. The above results indicated that, compared with HIPPEs stabilized by OVA, the addition of KGM effectively improved the rheological properties of OVA-KGM complex-stabilized HIPPEs. The rheological results showed that the HIPPEs stabilized by OVA-KGM complex had higher viscoelasticity, thixotropic recovery, thermal stability, and resistance to external damage, which enabled them to be incorporated into food matrices as fat substitutes such as mayonnaise, cookies and cakes, etc.

Stability analysis of HIPPEs

The stability of HIPPEs under different environmental stresses is a key indicator for their practical application. Fig. 4 shows the visual appearance, microstructure, and particle size distribution of the HIPPEs stabilized by the OVA-KGM complex after storage for 30 days. The fresh HIPPEs samples all presented a uniform gel-like morphology, and they could still maintain their self-supporting ability after being inverted. The corresponding microscopic structure and particle size distribution statistical analysis indicated that the size of the droplets in the emulsion decreased first and then increased with increasing KGM concentration. The HIPPEs stabilized by the 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex exhibited a smaller particle size of 15.4 μm. This phenomenon was consistent with the previous research, which showed that an appropriate amount of KGM helped to improve the adsorption efficiency of OVA-KGM complexes at the oil-water interface, thus promoting the formation of smaller and more uniform droplets (Wang, Liu, et al., 2023). However, when the KGM concentration was increased to 0.4%, the droplet size of HIPPEs increased to 27.6 μm, which might be due to the excessive KGM binding to water, forming a continuous hydrogel that penetrated into the network structure of OVA, resulting in the destruction of the network structure of OVA (Jiang et al., 2023). After being stored for 10 days, significant oil precipitation was observed in the HIPPEs stabilized by OVA, and phase separation occurred 20 days later. In contrast, the samples with KGM addition maintained a stable gel-like appearance over 20 days. Nevertheless, the average droplet size of all samples increased with longer storage time, indicating that Ostwald ripening or slight coalescence between droplets occurred. After 30 days of storage, the particle size of all HIPPEs increased by 16.5 μm (2.0% OVA), 17.4 μm (2.0% OVA-0.1% KGM), 7.2 μm (2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM), 10.8 μm (2.0% OVA-0.3% KGM), and 17.6 μm (2.0% OVA-0.4% KGM), respectively. Compared with the HIPPEs stabilized by OVA, the HIPPEs stabilized by the complex still maintained macroscopic uniformity and inverted self-supporting ability. The protein adsorption content on the interface is one of the key parameters for evaluating the stability of Pickering emulsions. As shown in Fig. S5A, the interfacial protein adsorption content of HIPPEs stabilized by OVA was only 53.6%, and that of HIPPEs stabilized by OVA-KGM complex was significantly increased (p < 0.05). However, when the KGM concentration was further increased to 0.3% and 0.4%, the protein adsorption content of HIPPEs was significantly decreased (p < 0.05). This might be due to the excessive KGM chains diffusing near the oil-water interface and competing with OVA for interface sites, reducing the migration rate of OVA and easily replacing the originally saturated OVA-KGM complexes on the interface. Previous studies have also found that excessive KGM resulted in a decrease in the protein adsorption of β-conglycinin (7S), which is not conducive to the formation of a denser interface film, thus leading to droplet aggregation (Guo et al., 2025). The physical stability of the emulsions was further evaluated by determining the oil loss rate of HIPPEs after centrifugation treatment. As shown in Fig. S5B, the complex reduced the oil loss rate of HIPPEs compared to OVA. The oil loss rate of HIPPEs stabilized by 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM was only 1.2%, further verifying that these HIPPEs have a strong anti-phase separation ability, which is also consistent with the structural stability of HIPPEs.

The protective effect of HIPPEs on AST

The visual appearance of AST-loaded HIPPEs stabilized by OVA and OVA-KGM complexes is shown in Fig. S6, and all samples exhibit self-support after inversion. It was found (Fig. 6C) that the encapsulation efficiency (EE) of AST in OVA-stabilized HIPPEs was 73.2%, and the introduction of KGM could significantly improve the EE of AST in HIPPEs (p < 0.05). Among them, the EE of AST in HIPPEs stabilized by 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex was as high as 94.2%, which was mainly attributed to the structural stability of HIPPEs. The loading capacity of AST in HIPPEs was also evaluated (Fig. S7). The 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex-stabilized HIPPEs exhibited the highest loading capacity (0.075 mg/mL), which was significantly higher (p < 0.05) than that of OVA-stabilized HIPPEs (0.059 mg/mL). As an active substance that is extremely sensitive to heat, light and oxygen, the stability of AST is highly dependent on the protective effect of the carrier system (Zhang, Zhao, et al., 2021). In order to systematically evaluate the protective effect of HIPPEs stabilized by OVA-KGM complex on AST, the stability of AST-loaded HIPPEs was analyzed under UV radiation, room temperature visible light and heat treatment environments. After 6 h of UV irradiation (Fig. 6D), the residual rate of free AST was only 28.6%, because AST was directly exposed to the UV environment, and corn oil could not prevent the damage of AST by the external environment. HIPPEs stabilized by OVA increased the residual rate of AST to 52.3%, and the protective effect of HIPPEs stabilized by OVA-KGM complex on AST was more significant (p < 0.05). After 30 days of storage at room temperature under visible light conditions (Fig. 6E), the residual rate of AST decreased in all samples. The residual rates of AST were 31.3% (Oil), 47.4% (2.0% OVA), 75.4% (2.0% OVA-0.1% KGM), 82.7% (2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM), 78.5% (2.0% OVA-0.3% KGM) and 70.6% (2.0% OVA-0.4% KGM), respectively. The results indicated that HIPPEs stabilized by 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex showed a more efficient protective effect on AST. This was mainly due to the dense interface shell formed by the complex at the oil-water interface and the bridging network structure formed by intermolecular hydrogen bonds between the droplets, which physically prevented contact between AST and the external environment. After storage at 50 °C for 30 days, the effect of HIPPEs on the residual rate of AST was evaluated (Fig. 6F). The results showed that the trend of AST residual rates was consistent with the above results for UV and storage stability. It was found that HIPPEs stabilized by OVA-KGM complex can effectively alleviate the degradation of AST by ultraviolet radiation, visible light and thermal stress. Among them, HIPPEs stabilized by 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex showed the potential of efficient delivery carriers as hydrophobic bioactive substances.

To evaluate the effects of HIPPEs on the bioaccessibility of hydrophobic bioactive substances, HIPPEs were studied in simulating oral, stomach and intestinal digestion. The effects of OVA-KGM complex on lipid digestion behavior and AST absorption potential in HIPPEs were systematically analyzed by recording macroscopic morphological, microstructure changes, free fatty acid (FFA) release rate and AST bioaccessibility of samples during digestion. As shown in Fig. 7A, there were almost no significant changes in all samples during the oral stage. After the gastrointestinal digestion phase, free corn oil obviously accumulates oil droplets at the top of the digestive juice. HIPPEs stabilized by 2.0% OVA, 2.0% OVA-0.1% KGM, and 2.0% OVA-0.4% KGM failed to resist the digestive environment, with a few aggregated oil droplets at the top of the digestive juice. While the HIPPEs stabilized by 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM and 2.0% OVA-0.3% KGM always maintained a uniformly dispersed state, indicating that their micellization process was more adequate. Fig. 7B is a schematic diagram of HIPPEs during the digestion process in vitro, which visually reflects that HIPPEs maintain a complete structure in the oral and gastric stages, and begin to release AST and form a micellar phase after entering the intestinal, indicating that HIPPEs can effectively improve the stability and controlled release performance of AST in the gastrointestinal environment. The structural evolution of HIPPEs during in vitro digestion was further visualized by CLSM (Fig. 7C). After the gastric digestion phase, the corn oil showed an aggregated state and the HIPPEs maintained relative droplet integrity. Where HIPPEs stabilized by 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM and 2.0% OVA-0.3% KGM maintained small and evenly distributed droplets, indicating good resistance to acidic gastric environments and effective protection of AST from premature release in the stomach. During the intestinal stage, the droplet size of all HIPPEs decreases, which can sufficiently interact with lipases to accelerate the rate of oil droplet digestion (Wang, Ji, & Xia, 2024). The hydrolysis of fatty acids mainly occurs in the small intestine stage, and the release of FFA can reflect the degree of lipid digestion of HIPPEs. The release curve of FFA during the small intestine stage is shown in Fig. 7D. Within the initial 30 min of digestion, the lipids of all samples were rapidly digested. The lipolysis rate gradually decreased in the subsequent 30 min, and the FFA release curve tended to be horizontal and reached the maximum value in the final 60 min. After digestion for 120 min, the order of FFA release rates for all samples was 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM (96.7%) > 2.0% OVA-0.3% KGM (91.0%) > 2.0% OVA-0.1% KGM (82.5%) > 2.0% OVA-0.4% KGM (75.1%) > 2.0% OVA (63.7%) > Oil (52.3%), which mainly indicates that the surface area of the droplet interface layer and structural properties of the droplet are the main factors influencing the rate and degree of lipid digestion (Zhang et al., 2022). Microstructural analysis showed that the droplet size of HIPPEs stabilized by 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex was smaller, and the smaller droplets had relatively large specific surface area, which might promote the contact of oil droplets and between lipase and improve the efficiency of lipid hydrolysis, thus accelerating the release of FFA (Sun et al., 2024). Moreover, the 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex was believed to have formed an interface that could resist aggregation and still be dynamically contacted by digested components, which also promoted lipolysis efficiency. It was also found in previous studies that the addition of appropriate proportions of KGM helped the protein to form smaller-sized emulsion droplets, which promoted lipid hydrolysis and sustained release of FFA (Zhang, Wang, et al., 2023). However, HIPPEs stabilized by 2.0% OVA, 2.0% OVA-0.1% KGM, and 2.0% OVA-0.4% KGM complexes caused droplets to aggregate during gastric digestion due to structural instability, the emulsion was destroyed, and thus digestion was slowed down. The transformation, bioaccessibility index, and bioaccessibility of AST in HIPPEs are shown in Fig. 7E. The transformation (49.2%), bioaccessibility index (52.6%) and bioaccessibility (25.8%) of AST in unencapsulated corn oil were low, which was mainly due to the aggregation of corn oil, which reduced the contact sites with lipase and hindered the conversion of AST from oil phase to micellar phase. Compared with free AST, the encapsulation of HIPPEs effectively enhanced the bioaccessibility of AST, and the promotion effect of HIPPEs stabilized by the complex was more significant (p < 0.05). Importantly, HIPPEs stabilized by the 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex made the bioaccessibility of AST up to 46.9%, which could be explained by two reasons. Firstly, the HIPPEs formed by this complex guaranteed the integrity of droplets in the gastric phase due to the stable interfacial cross-linking structure, thus slowing down the degradation of AST. Secondly, the complex-stabilized HIPPEs had a higher level of FFA release rate, which facilitated the formation of AST micellation during digestion, thereby improving the bioaccessibility of AST (Sun, Du, et al., 2025). Therefore, the protective effect of 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex-stabilized HIPPEs in a simulated gastrointestinal environment reduces the loss of AST to improve its bioaccessibility. The OVA-KGM complex could promote lipid digestion and AST controlled release by optimizing the interface structure, especially HIPPEs stabilized by 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex, which showed promising application prospects as an efficient carrier for AST delivery.

Conclusion

In summary, this study successfully developed a food-grade HIPPEs stabilized by a novel OVA-KGM complex for the effective delivery of AST. The OVA-KGM complex is formed by hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interaction. Among them, the 2.0% OVA-0.2% KGM complex showed lower interfacial tension and higher emulsifying activity. The HIPPEs stabilized by the complex form a stable interface layer on the oil droplet surface and form a dense three-dimensional network structure, which endows HIPPEs with significantly enhanced environmental stability and rheological properties. Crucially, this robust microstructure provides effective protection for encapsulated AST, preventing UV light, heat, and oxidative degradation. Additionally, during in vitro simulation, this system promoted effective lipid digestion, resulting in a high FFA release rate (96.7%) and a significantly enhanced bioaccessibility of AST (46.9%) (p < 0.05). This work provides an effective strategy to overcome the stability limitations of HIPPEs based on a single protein and offers new insights into the delivery of lipophilic bioactive compounds. However, there are still challenges in scaling up this technology, including the consistent production of the OVA-KGM complex, the adaptation of high shear homogenization processes for industrial equipment, and the verification of stability in specific foods under commercial storage conditions. Future research should focus on the performance of this system in specific food matrices, explore its in vivo absorption kinetics, and fully realize the potential of this promising delivery platform.
