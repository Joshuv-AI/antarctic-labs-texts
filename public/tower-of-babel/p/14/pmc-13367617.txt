A case report: A man creates holes in his own heart
Authors: Yibing Wu, Xuan Zhang, Xinlong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report a rare case of a 59-year-old male with a history of depression stabbed himself from xiphoid to heart. Following initial cardiothoracic surgery for hemothorax and cardiac repair, the patient was discharged and transferred to a psychiatric facility for depression treatment. Four weeks later, he returned with complaints of chest pain, cough, and expectoration. Further examination found abnormal blood pathways in heart and second surgery confirmed these were attribute to the original penetrating injury. This case highlights the delayed complications of penetrating cardiac trauma and underscores the importance of multidisciplinary management in such complex scenarios.

Introduction

Penetrating cardiac injuries are infrequent but lethal. In Germany, the penetrating injuries occurrence rate is 4% and stab wounds account for 1.8%.[1] So it requires medical workers make the correct diagnosis and initiate surgery timely.[2] In this case, we present the progress of diagnosis and treatment of penetrating cardiac injury, especially how the ultrasound help. We hope it could make practitioners more cautious on such cases.

Case Represent

A 59-year-old male with a documented history of depression presented to the emergency department on 7th December 2023 with active bleeding and chest pain following a self-inflicted stab wound to the chest [Figure 1]. Emergent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) was performed, including hemothorax evacuation, pericardiotomy with exploration, pulmonary laceration repair, and resection of a premediastinal pericardial hematoma. After operation, the patient was stabilized and transferred to a psychiatric hospital on 19th Dec for specialized care. Four weeks later, on 14th January, 2024, the patient returned to the emergence department with chest pain, cough, and expectoration. In the transthoracic echocardiographic examination, his heart was found two abnormal blood pathways [Figure 3]. And in the later transesophageal echocardiographic examination [Figure 4] and aortic contrast-enhanced computed tomographic angiography (CTA) [Figure 5], the two pathways were both confirmed. He was transferred to another hospital for cardiothoracic surgery, where surgeons confirmed the channels were caused by the knife stab [Figure 6].

Discussion

Based on the patient’s upper abdominal scar, it is hypothesized that during the suicide attempt, the sharp blade penetrated upward from the sub-xiphoid into the cardiac chambers. The trajectory passed through the right ventricle (RV) outflow tract into the RV, also perforated the non-coronary sinus of the aorta, and ultimately entered the left atrium (LA). Given the thin and narrow nature of the blade, the RV wall injury resulted in only minimal pericardial effusion. While the RV wall lesion healed gradually, persistent abnormal intracardiac bloodstream formed two rare pathological blood pathways.

Remarkably, the unique trajectory of the penetrating injury avoided catastrophic cardiac rupture and fortunately bypassed the right coronary artery and critical valvular structures, thereby preventing immediate life-threatening consequences. Now the patient could care for himself without sequela.

In this work, we present a rapid and correct process of initial assessment, surgical management, ultrasound examination, and cardiac repair. To be specifically, echocardiography played a crucial role in the diagnosis. Based on its safety, speed, accuracy and convenience, echocardiography should be seen as the first examination.[3] While it also requires the sonologist with experience and carefulness.

The clinical severity of penetrating cardiac injuries depends on three key factors: anatomical location of injury, defect size, and degree of structural damage to cardiac components.[4] Epidemiological data shows RV injuries are most common (48.6%), followed by left ventricle (LV) injuries (47.49%).[5] Notably, LV ruptures carry higher risks of lethal pericardial hemorrhage and functional impairment compared to RV injuries, with substantially poorer clinical outcomes.[6]
