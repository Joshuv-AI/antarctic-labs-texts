Oryza sativa heavy metal ATPase (OsHMA) transporter-mediated cadmium uptake in rice: functional insights for low-Cd breeding
Authors: Sidra Charagh, Jingxin Wang, Suozhen Hui, Liang Zhou, Lingwei Yang, Yujuan Chen, Yuanyaun Zhang, Yu Wu, Chunyu Luo, Ruijie Cao, Gaoneng Shao, Zhonghua Sheng, Fengli Zhao, Yusong Lyu, Shaoqing Tang, Shikai Hu, Peisong Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCadmium (Cd) is a hazardous and non-essential heavy metal that enters the environment through anthropogenic activities and natural emissions, polluting crops and threatening human health. We hypothesized that OsHMA transporters play distinct and functionally specialized roles in Cd uptake and transport in rice, and their expression could reduce Cd accumulation in shoots, while enhance plant tolerance to Cd-induced toxicity. This study identified and functionally characterized OsHMA family members involved in this process. Heterologous expression in the Cd-sensitive yeast strain ∆ycf1 showed that OsHMA1/2/3/4/5/7/8/9 possess Cd-specific functional activity. Under Cd treatment, rice overexpressing OsHMA1/3/4/5/7/8/9 exhibited improved growth and reduced Cd accumulation in shoots, while CRISPR-Cas9 knockouts of OsHMA1/3/4/7/9 accumulated more Cd in shoots and showed greater sensitivity. Notably, KO-OsHMA5 and KO-OsHMA8 mutants exhibited higher Cd content in roots and lower in shoots and improved growth performance than wild type. Subcellular localization revealed a diverse distribution of OsHMA proteins; OsHMA1, OsHMA7 and OsHMA8 co-localized with chloroplasts; OsHMA5 and OsHMA9 were localized to the plasma membrane; OsHMA2, OsHMA4, and OsHMA6 were localized both to the plasma membrane and endoplasmic reticulum; and OsHMA3 had fluorescence signals in the vacuolar (tonoplast) membrane. These findings confirm that OsHMA genes are functionally specialized in Cd uptake, supporting our hypothesis, and demonstrate that targeted manipulation of these genes through CRISPR/Cas9 tool may provide further insight into their roles in regulating Cd accumulation in rice.Keywords: Oryza sativa heavy metal ATPase, Cadmium, In-silico characterization, Yeast expression system, CRISPR-Cas9

Introduction

Rice (Oryza sativa L.) is the dietary staple of more than half the world's population. It has an effective ability for cadmium (Cd) uptake from the contaminated soils, representing the primary route of Cd exposure in human via contaminated food consumption (Clemens & Ma, 2016; Ilyas et al., 2022). Unlike other cereal crops, rice is grown under flooded conditions that favors the solubilization of soil Cd into pore water, thus increasing its bioavailability to roots and also its subsequent transmission into vegetative and edible tissues (Arao et al., 2009). Cd accumulation in polished rice presents a direct food-safety concern in human. On the one hand, Cd accumulation triggers heavy metal (HM) associated stress in rice and drastically influences the growth, resulting in compromised rice quality and quantity (Chen et al., 2022; Gu et al., 2025). On the other hand, chronic dietary exposure of Cd is strongly related to the carcinogenesis, skeletal damage, and renal tubular dysfunction in human, threatening public health-safety (Charagh et al., 2024; Clemens et al., 2013; Järup & Åkesson, 2009). Therefore, understanding the molecular mechanistic insights governing Cd translocation from soil to root towards shoots through the plant vascular system is of fundamental significance to develop low-Cd accumulated rice varieties through targeted genetic intervention.

Over the last decade, significant advancements have been achieved in understanding the molecular mechanisms underpinning plants' uptake and transport of Cd (Charagh et al., 2025a; Noor et al., 2024). Numerous transporter proteins have been characterized in plants, where they facilitate Cd uptake, transportation, and sequestration (Charagh et al., 2025b; Noor et al., 2022). Uptake from the rhizosphere into root cells is primarily mediated by OsNramp5, a plasma membrane transporter constitutively expressed at the distal side of both root exodermis and endodermis (Sasaki et al., 2012; Zhang et al., 2024). Once inside root cells, Cd is partitioned between two competing pathways: vacuolar sequestration, mediated by OsHMA3, a tonoplast-localized P1B-type heavy metal ATPase that limits root to above-ground tissues translocation (Ueno et al., 2010) and xylem loading, facilitated by OsHMA2, a plasma membrane-localized homologue expressed in the root that drives long-distance zinc and Cd movement towards the shoot (Yamaji et al., 2013). At the nodal vasculature, intervascular Cd transfer and phloem reloading are further modulated by OsHMA2 and OsLCT1, ultimately directing Cd flux towards developing grain (Takahashi, Ishimaru, et al., 2012; Uraguchi et al., 2011). These observations collectively establish that OsHMA transporters family play a crucial role in regulating Cd uptake and its translocation from roots to shoots in rice (Satoh-Nagasawa et al., 2012).

P1B-ATPases, also known as Heavy Metal ATPases (HMAs), are part of the E1-E2 ATPases superfamily of P-type ATPases (Charagh et al., 2025b); facilitate the movement of heavy metal into vacuoles where they can be sequestered and detoxified (Argüello et al., 2011). Nine HMA transporters are encoded in the rice genome (Takahashi, Bashir, et al., 2012). Among them, OsHMA3 transports Cd as well as serves as an agent for Cd adsorption in root vacuoles, while OsHMA1 in rice is thought to take part in the shipping of zinc (Miyadate et al., 2011; Ueno et al., 2010). OsHMA2 transferring zinc and Cd in vascular tissue (Yamaji et al., 2013). OsHMA5 have a function in Cu transfer to the xylem (Deng et al., 2013). The vascular tissues of the xylem and phloem are where OsHMA9 is primarily expressed. OsHMA9 can remove metal ions from cells when significant Pb, Zn, Cu and Zn levels build up among vascular tissues. (Lee et al., 2007). Although some OsHMA members, such as OsHMA2, OsHMA3, and OsHMA9 have been associated with metal transport and Cd homeostasis in rice, the Cd-specific physiological functions of OsHMA1, OsHMA4, OsHMA5, OsHMA7, and OsHMA8 remain unclear. Their potential roles in Cd uptake and root-to-shoot translocation under Cd stress conditions yet need to be functionally characterized. Thus, determining whether OsHMA (HMA1/4/5/7/8) participate in Cd uptake remains an intriguing open question that our current research aims to resolve.

This knowledge gap highlights the need for further functional characterization of OsHMA family members, which this study addresses by investigating their Cd-specific functional activity. We hypothesized that OsHMA family members perform functionally distinct but complementary roles in Cd uptake and transport across rice roots and shoots. To test this, we performed yeast functional assays, expression profiling, subcellular localization, and transgenic plant analysis to resolve the functional characterization of each family member within a unified framework. Thus, this study provides insights into developing rice with reduced Cd levels.

Materials and methods

Database search, retrieval of sequences, multiple sequence alignment, and phylogenetic analysis of OsHMA family genes

The genomic sequences for Oryza sativa were retrieved from “The Rice Annotation Project (RAP)” site (https://rapdb.dna.affrc.go.jp/ (accessed on 19 January 2023). Additional information was gained from the EnsemblPlants (http://plants.ensembl.org/index.html (accessed on 31 January 2023) database regarding the assembly of CDS, genome, and amino acids. HMA protein sequences of Arabidopsis were obtained from TAIR (https://www.arabidopsis.org/). The amino acid sequences of AtHMA1-AtHMA8 were used as queries for local BLASTp (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi (accessed on 2 February 2023) with an E value <1e−10, 50% identity, and coverage ≥60%, against rice protein database to identify putative HMA members. The retrieved sequences were further validated for the presence of conserved domains of HMA proteins (i.e., E1-E2 ATPase: PF00122; Hydrolase: PF00702; HMA: PF00403) using the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (CDD) and redundant sequences were discarded. The MUSCLE algorithm with default parameters was used to perform multiple sequence alignment of the amino acid sequences of HMAs from rice and Arabidopsis. MEGA 11 (https://www.megasoftware.net/) software was used to conduct a phylogenetic analysis by the neighbor-joining (NJ) method with 1000 bootstrap replicates, to allow comparison with published HMA phylogenies that commonly employ NJ method (Huang et al., 2026; Ma et al., 2021; Wenli et al., 2020; Zahra et al., 2023).

Gene structure analysis, cis-regulatory elements, conserved domains, and motif recognition

The identified genes' coding and genomic sequences were obtained through RAP and EnsemblPlants databases to examine the intron/exon arrangement of OsHMAs. TBTools and Gene Structure Display Server 2.0 (http://gsds.gao-lab.org/ (accessed on 31 January 2023) were employed to further utilize the sequences to illustrate the gene structure. The conserved motifs were located using the MEME server. MEME version 5.5.7 (http://meme-suite.org/tools/meme (accessed on 6 February 2023) was utilized to investigate the conserved motifs for every OsHMA protein with default parameters, maximum number of motifs was set to 10, and motif widths ranging from 6 to 50 amino acids. The 2 kb upstream sequences from the transcription start sites were retrieved for promoter analysis, because most stress-responsive and hormone-responsive cis-regulatory elements are enriched within this proximal promoter region. The PlantCARE website (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/ (accessed on 24 January 2023) was employed to predict the cis-regulatory elements in these sequences, and TBTools were utilized to depict the picture. The protein sequences of OsHMAs were then searched for those that included the domains for HMA via NCBI Conserved Domain Database Search (NCBI-CDS) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi (accessed on 6 February 2023).

Chromosomal distribution and synteny analysis

The OsHMAs genes were mapped on various chromosomes based on the chromosomal location obtained from GFF file of the Ensembl Plants (http://plants.ensembl.org/Oryza_sativa/Info/Index (accessed on 31 January 2023). Then Tbtools@v1.082 software was used to visualize the results. The tandem and segmental duplication or whole-genome duplication (WGD) provides new insights into genes development and genome progression. The duplication events of OsHMAs genes and the syntenic relationships of HMA genes between rice, maize, and wheat were analyzed using the Multiple Collinearity Scan toolkit (MCScanX) (Wang et al., 2012), and HMA relationships between the target species were visualized by using Circos (Krzywinski et al., 2009).

Determination of physio-chemical properties and transmembrane helices of OsHMA proteins

To determine the physio-chemical characteristics (Supplementary Table 2) of OsHMA proteins, like GRAVY, number of amino acids, molecular weight, instability indices, aliphatic indices, as well as pI of OsHMA proteins, the ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/ (accessed on 28 January 2023) in the ExPASy (https://www.expasy.org/; https://web.expasy.org/compute_pi/ (accessed on 28 January 2023) proteomics server was utilized. However, information on the length of CDS, DNA, amino acids, molecular weight, locus name, and CDS coordinates (5′-3′) was extracted from the China Rice Data Center website (https://www.ricedata.cn). Phyre2 was used to estimate the transmembrane helices in OsHMAs (http://www.sbg.bio.ic.ac.uk/∼phyre2/html/page.cgi?id=index (accessed on 1 February 2023).

Rice materials and growth conditions

This study employed the Oryza sativa ssp. japonica var. Zhonghua 11 (ZH11) (GCA_014526345.1) as a wild type (WT). The ZH11 background was used to generate the mutants as well as overexpression lines of OsHMA family genes. The WT, KO-OsHMA, and OE-OsHMA lines were grown in the paddy field of China National Rice Research Institute in Hangzhou (Zhejiang Province, China, 30 °C 150′N). For hydroponic culture, WT, KO-OsHMA, and OE-OsHMA seeds were soaked in water for 2–3 days and kept in a growth incubator with a day/night temperature of 30/25 °C and light intensity of around 400 μmol m−2 s−1 (sodium vapour lamps), to break the possible dormancy. Following germination, the seedlings were placed in Yoshida rice nutrient solution (Coolaber, NSP1040; pH 5.6 to 5.8), which was replaced every three days.

Generation of knockout mutants and overexpression plants for functional characterization

Target site selection, construction of BGK03::OsHMA CRISPR/Cas9 vector, and screening of knockout mutants for mutation identification

To generate CRISPR-edited transgene-free plant, used the CRISPR-GE web application (http://skl.scau.edu.cn), two guide RNAs (gRNA), including the PAM sequence, were created from the coding region of OsHMAs and cross-checked using CRISPRdirect (http://crispr.dbcls.jp/), to reduce off-target sites (Naito et al., 2015) (Fig. 5; Table 1). To create vectors, the targets with the greatest scores were utilized. After finalizing the two target sites (TS1 and TS2; Table 1) their primers were designed as per the protocol provided by manufacturers (Biogle, China). The synthetic oligo was combined with the company-provided annealing buffer after being diluted by adding ddH2O at a rate of 10 μL, following the reaction specified in the Supplementary Table 4. Following oligo preparation, a second reaction was made to ligate the oligos (Supplementary Table 5) to the BGK03 vector that had already been cut. The ligation mixture was transformed into DH5α competent E. coli and then were sent for sequencing, and confirmed BGK03::OsHMA (TS1 and TS2 combined) inserts were retained and forwarded to BioRun Company for knockout mutant plant generation. For mutant generation, approximately 18 independent lines per OsHMA gene were obtained and identified the mutation sites later on. The genomic DNA was PCR amplified with Cas9-specific primers (Supplementary Table 3). PCR samples were then sent to the company for sequencing. KO-OsHMA-specific primers were used in PCR to amplify the DNA fragments containing the target sites, followed by Sanger sequencing. The sequencing results were analyzed using SnapGene to identify mutations at the target sites. Based on sequence confirmation, two independent homozygous mutant lines per gene were selected for downstream analyses.

Construction, identification and screening of OE-OsHMA plants

For overexpression of OsHMA genes, the binary vector pCUbi1390 controlled by the Ubi promoter was first propagated in E. coli through primary and secondary culture. Plasmid DNA was then isolated using standard plasmid extraction procedures, followed by restriction digestion and purification to prepare the linearized pCUbi1390 vector for cloning. The full-length CDS with termination codon of the candidate genes OsHMA1 to OsHMA9 were PCR amplified (the primers used are listed in Supplementary Table 3) and purified using a TIANGEN gel/PCR purification kit to obtain high-quality inserts (OsHMA) for functional complementation studies. The purified OsHMA were then ligated into the prepared pCUbi1390 overexpression vector. The recombinant plasmids were transformed into E. coli, and positive colonies were selected on LB medium containing the K+ antibiotic. Putative positive clones were cultured, and successful ligation was confirmed followed by Sanger sequencing. Sequence verification was performed using SnapGene. After confirmation, the recombinant constructs (pCUbi1390::OsHMA) were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens and used for plant transformation. Transgenic plants were selected using kanamycin resistance screening (Hgy-Forward: CGCTTCTGCGGGCGATTTG; Hgy-Reverse: CGGATTTCGGCTCCAACAA) and positive lines were further validated by PCR. Plants were advanced through T1 and T2 generations to obtain stable expression lines, and two independent homozygous overexpression lines per gene were selected for further analyses.

All phenotypic and molecular analyses were performed using at least two independent KO and OE lines per gene to ensure reproducibility and to minimize line-specific effects.

Heterologous expression of OsHMA family genes in yeast mutants

The PCR-amplified sequences of OsHMA family genes (HMA1-HMA9) were ligated into the HindIII-Xba1 sites of the pYES2 yeast expression vector, regulated by the GAL1 (galactokinase gene) promoter to construct the recombinant pYES2::OsHMAs plasmid. After sequence confirmation, the empty pYES2 vector and pYES2::OsHMA recombinant plasmids were transformed into the yeast wild-type strain BY4741 and yeast mutant strain Δycf1 using the PEG-LiAC technique. The Cd-sensitive mutant Δycf1, which is defective in vacuolar Cd sequestration due to loss of YCF1 (Yeast Cadmium Factor 1), provides a sensitive system to evaluate the role of OsHMA genes in Cd uptake. A synthetic medium (SD/-U selection media) containing amino acids without uracil was used to cultivate the yeast cells that had been transformed with the indicated constructs. After being spread out on SD/-U selection media, the transformed yeast cells [BY4741-pYES2, Δycf1-pYES2, and OsHMA-Δycf1-pYES2 (HMA1-HMA9 genes)] were cultured for two to three days at 30 °C. Then, positive colonies were cultivated in liquid media containing SD/-U and grown overnight at 30 °C with 200 rpm shaking. After being centrifuged for five minutes at 5000 rpm to enrich the yeast cells, they were twice rinsed with sterile water. The cells were resuspended and adjusted to OD600 = 0.5 and serially diluted (100 to 10−3) using sterile distilled water. Seven microliters (7 μL) of each serially diluted yeast culture were spotted onto SG/-U solid medium supplemented with varying concentrations of CdCl2 (from a 20 mM stock). The final CdCl2 concentrations used were 44 μM and 48 μM for OsHMA2; 48 μM and 56 μM for OsHMA9, OsHMA3, OsHMA4, OsHMA6, and OsHMA1; 64 μM and 72 μM for OsHMA5; and 48 μM and 56 μM for OsHMA7, while OsHMA8 was tested at 64 μM and 72 μM. Afterward, the SG/Ura (K+) plates were photographed after incubating for three to five days at 30 °C to raise yeast colonies.

Yeast growth curve and Cd content determination in yeast by ICP – MS

For yeast growth curves, a single colony of yeast cells transformed with different vectors [BY4741-pYES2, Δycf1-pYES2, and OsHMA-Δycf1-pYES2 (HMA1-HMA9 genes) were produced overnight, and SD-Ura broth was used to bring the OD600 nm to 1.0. Next, 1.2 mL of SG-Ura broth and the required CdCl2 (20 μM CdCl2 for OsHMA1, OsHMA2, OsHMA6; 30 μM CdCl2 for OsHMA3, OsHMA4, OsHMA5, OsHMA7, OsHMA9; and 40 μM CdCl2 for OsHMA8) were added into a centrifuge tube having 20 μL of yeast cell suspension (OD600nm = 1). After that, continued shaking at 30 °C with 200 rpm for at least 12 h and begin monitoring the OD600nm. The yeast solution's optical density was recorded seven times at the OD600 value after every four-hour interval using a microplate reader.

Yeast strains that had been transformed with various plasmids were precultured overnight to OD600 = 1.0. Next, 1.2 mL of SG-Ura broth and the required CdCl2 (as described previously for growth curve) were added into a centrifuge tube having 20 μL of yeast cell suspension (OD600nm = 1). After that, continued shaking at 30 °C for at least five days or until a yeast pellet appeared. Retained the yeast pellet and discarded the supernatant after centrifuging for five minutes at 8000 rpm. After ddH2O was added and centrifuged for 5 min at 8000 rpm; the yeast pellet was kept, and discarded the supernatant. The yeast pellet was finally left behind after being rinsed with ddH2O three times. After being dried for 72 h at 70 °C, the samples were digested in a digester (DigiBlock ED16, LabTech) using concentrated HNO3 (ultrapure). The dried yeast pellets (three biological replicates) were collectively weighed; then transferred into digestion tubes, followed by the addition of 8 mL of concentrated nitric acid (HNO3). Subsequently, all samples were digested at 120 °C for 1 h. After initial digestion, 3 mL of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was added dropwise, and the samples were further heated for an additional 1 h until 1 mL solution was retained. The digested samples were then cooled and diluted to a final volume with 10 mL of double-distilled water (ddH2O). The Cd concentration of yeast cells was ascertained by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS 7800, Agilent Technologies (accessed on 10 June 2023)), with three independent replicates.

Analysis of the relative expression pattern of OsHMA family genes by qRT-PCR

To ascertain the pattern of OsHMA expression throughout the rice growing period, ZH11 was cultivated in a paddy field from mid-June to late September. Total RNA was extracted from root, shoot, leaf sheath, panicle, basal, node-I, node-II, node-III, node-IV, internode-I, internode-II, internode-III, internode-IV, 6 days seeds, and 24 days seeds, using TRIzol reagent or RNA extraction kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). The RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) (Supplementary Table 6). Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was performed to evaluate gene expression patterns using SYBR Green I mix (Takara) in a Light cycler 480 System (Roche). Rice UBIQUITIN gene was used as the internal reference gene based on prior validation in rice under heavy metal stress. Almas and Kamrodi (2018) assessed eight housekeeping genes in rice exposed to Cd, Zn, Cu, and Pb and identified UBQ10 and UBC as the most stably expressed reference genes across all conditions. After preparing qRT-PCR reaction mixture (see supplementary Table 8 for qRT-PCR reaction mix). The qRT-PCR program and system were as follows: Step 1: 95 °C for 3 min; Step 2: 95 °C for 10 s, 58 °C for 15 s, 72 °C for 15 s, Step 2 for 45 cycles; Step 3: 72 °C for 2 min. Supplementary table 3 lists the gene-specific primers used for qPCR. According to Livak and Schmittgen (2001), the 2-ΔΔCt method was used to determine the OsHMAs' relative expression levels after normalizing the expression data. The expression data was visualized by creating heatmaps with the TBtools software.

Hydroponic experiments and phenotype analysis via Cd tolerance assays

In order to evaluate the WTs, KO-OsHMA, and OE-OsHMA sensitivity for Cd stress, rice seeds were surface-sterilized and soaked in distilled water under dark conditions for 2–3 days to break dormancy and promote uniform germination. After slight germination, the seeds were transferred to plastic seedling trays containing individual holes for seed placement. The seedlings were cultivated under normal growth chamber conditions for 14 days using Yoshida rice nutrient solution (Coolaber, NSP1040; see Supplementary Table 9 for nutrient solution composition). Growth conditions were maintained with a day/night temperature of 30/25 °C and light intensity of around 400 μmol m−2 s−1 (sodium vapour lamps). After 14 days of growth, Cd treatment was initiated by supplementing the nutrient solution with the required concentration of CdCl2 (2 μM CdCl2 for KO-OsHMA and 2.5 μM for OE-OsHMAs in 800 mL nutrition solution). As a control (Mock), the nutrient solution devoid of CdCl2 was used. The seedlings were continuously maintained under the same environmental conditions throughout the Cd treatment period. To ensure a stable nutrient supply and uniform Cd exposure, the nutrient solution containing Cd was replaced with freshly prepared solution at 3-day intervals. For morpho-physiological analyses, plant samples were taken after a 14-day treatment period. Plant roots were removed from shoots and then rinsed twice with a 0.5 mM CaCl2 solution and three times with ddH2O. The roots and shoots samples were dried for three days at 80 °C.

Metal content measurement by ICP-MS

Root and shoot samples were harvested, thoroughly washed with deionized water, oven-dried and finely ground into powder. Cd content was determined using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS 7800, Agilent Technologies). Approximately, 0.01–0.02 g of oven-dried root and shoot samples of KO-OsHMA and OE-OsHMA were taken with 8 mL of concentrated nitric acid (HNO3, 80%) and kept overnight (≥12 h) for pre-digestion. Subsequently, the samples were digested at 120 °C in a digester (DigiBlock ED16, LabTech) until the volume was reduced to approximately 5 mL. Thereafter, 3 mL hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was added, and the digestion was continued at 120 °C until 1 mL solution left. The digests were cooled, diluted to 10 mL with deionized water, and analyzed for Cd content using ICP-MS (ICP-MS 7800, Agilent Technologies, (accessed on 11 December 2024). ICP-MS analysis was performed following established protocols with strict quality control. Digestion blanks (reagents only, no plant material) were included in each batch to determine background levels. The mean blank value was subtracted from all sample measurements. Certified reference material (rice flour GBW10045, National Institute of Metrology, China) was used for quality assurance, with recovery rates of 95–105%.

Subcellular localization analysis

The subcellular localization of OsHMA family genes was examined by PCR amplifying its full-length coding sequence (without termination codon), ligating it to the pYBA1132-GFP vector's EcoRI-HindIII site to form the pYBA1132::OsHMAs-GFP fusion construct, and then transforming it into rice protoplasts per the protocols (Zhang et al., 2011b). The rice protoplasts were made from hydroponically grown, 2-week-old seedlings and the recombinant pYBA1132::OsHMAs-GFP construct, together with the marker vector was co-transformed into rice protoplasts using the polyethylene glycol (PEG)-mediated transformation method (Zhang et al., 2011a). Following transformation, the protoplasts were incubated in darkness for 18 to 36 h at 28 °C to allow transient expression of the fusion proteins. FM™4-64FX staining was additionally performed to visualize vacuolar/plasma membrane (Miyadate et al., 2011; Wenli et al., 2020), HDEL-mCherry for endoplasmic reticulum (ER) (Wang et al., 2024), while chlorophyll autofluorescence was used for chloroplast visualization (Wang et al., 2025). The empty GFP vector was used as a control, and the GFP control protein was observed to be uniformly distributed throughout the cytoplasm and nucleus (Nelson et al., 2007; Wang et al., 2024). A confocal laser scanning microscope (Karl Zeiss, Jena, Germany (accessed on 26 April 2025) was used to view the GFP signal.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted using at least three independent biological replicates. Data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD), and error bars in figures represent SD values. For experiments involving multiple genotypes, treatments, or their interactions, two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed as appropriate. Multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey's post-hoc test in GraphPad Prism Version 8.0.2 (263). The adjusted p-values obtained from Prism were used to generate compact letter display (CLD) using the multcompView package in R (version 4.6.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Different letters above bars indicate statistically significant differences between groups (Tukey's post-hoc test, p < 0.05).

Results

Genome-wide profiling, phylogenetics, multiple sequence alignment, cis-elements, and synteny analysis of the OsHMA gene family

We performed a phylogenic analysis of the OsHMA and AtHMA families to look into the evolutionary history of rice's HMA gene family. After eliminating repetitive and redundant sequences derived from HMMER and BLASTp results, found that rice genome contains nine HMA genes. The full-length protein sequences of nine OsHMAs and seven AtHMAs were used to construct a phylogenetic tree in order to examine the OsHMA family's phylogenetic relationship. According to phylogenetic analysis, all OsHMAs were well-clustered with ortholog of Arabidopsis and named according to their predicted phylogenetic relationships (Fig. 1A). Although based on multiple sequence alignment, OsHMAs also had HMA paralogs, consensus transport motifs, and transmembrane domains (TMDs). The alignment of all the OsHMA sequences with AtHMA4 showed that OsHMAs contained all domains (Fig. 1B). Seven other OsHMAs contained 8 TMDs, while OsHMA5 and OsHMA7 contained 7 TMDs (Fig. S3).

To look into the cis-elements in the OsHMAs promoter sequences, around 2000 bp upstream sections of the genomic sequences were obtained (Fig. 1C). After being functionally identified, the cis-regulatory elements were divided into four groups: light-responsive, hormone-responsive, growth process, and stress-responsive. Stress-responsive (ARE, as-1, MBS, Myc, STRE, WRE3, WUN-motif) found to be the most frequent (85), then hormone-responsive (TGACG-motif, CGTCA-motif, AAGAA-motif, ABRE, ERE, TCA-element, and P-box; 81), light responsive (GT1-motif, TCT-motif, GATA-motif, A-box, and G-box; 53), as well as growth process (CCGTCC-motif, MYB, O2-site; 51) (Fig. 1C). In the stress-responsive subcategory, the Myc (21) was the most prevalent, followed by ARE (19) and STRE (15). The hormone response contained ABRE (32), CGTCA motif (13), TGACG motif (13), and ERE (8) elements; however, the light response comprises G box (27), GATA motif (8), TCT motif (7), GT1 motif (6), and A box (5). The most frequent subcategory among the growth process-related elements was MYB (39), followed by O2-site (7) and CCGTCC-motif (5) (Fig. 1C). In order to gain a deeper comprehension of the HMA gene family's evolutionary history and orthologous relationships, the synteny analysis between two representative plant species (two monocots, Zea mays (maize) and Triticum aestivum (wheat) was carried out. Six and twelve orthologous pairs were found between maize and wheat, respectively (Fig. 1D).

Insights into gene structure, chromosomal mapping, motif analysis, and most conserved domains of OsHMA

We looked at the gene structure, conservative motifs, and domains of OsHMA family proteins to investigate their conservation and diversity across long-term evolution. According to analysis of gene structures, the exon numbers varied between 1 and 17; in contrast, the intron numbers varied from 1 to 16. OsHMA2, OsHMA6, and OsHMA9 contained 9 exons and 8 introns, OsHMA3, OsHMA4, and OsHMA5 have 7, 8, 6 exons and 6, 7, 5 introns, respectively, while OsHMA1, OsHMA7, and OsHMA8 had 13, 17, 16 exons and 12, 16, 15 introns, respectively (Fig. S2-A). Chromosomal mapping showed that OsHMA1, OsHMA2, and OsHMA9 genes were located on Chr-06. OsHMA4 as well as OsHMA6 positioned on Chr-02, whereas, OsHMA3, OsHMA5, OsHMA7, and OsHMA8 were shown on Chr-07, Chr-04, Chr-08, and Chr-03 respectively (Fig. S2-B). The domain analysis showed that the E1-E2_ATPase and hydrolase superfamily is present in almost all of the OsHMA genes, whereas the HMA superfamily is only present in all genes except OsHMA3, and the HAD_like superfamily is present in OsHMA6 and OsHMA9, but the DNA_ligase_OB superfamily is only present in OsHMA2 (Fig. S1). According to a motif distribution analysis, OsHMAs exhibited ten conserved motifs. We employed the online MEME tool to evaluate ten conserved motifs of proteins belonging to the OsHMA family. In this study, all OsHMA proteins contain all the motifs except OsHMA2 and OsHMA3 proteins, which lack motifs 3, 4, and 8 (Fig. 2).

Then, transformed into the wild-type strain BY4741 and the Cd-sensitive mutant Δycf1, which is defective in vacuolar Cd sequestration (Li et al., 1997). Transformed yeast cells were spotted on SG-U agar plates supplemented with 0 and increasing concentrations of CdCl2. Growth patterns were compared among BY4741 (positive control), Δycf1-pYES2 (negative control), and Δycf1 strains expressing OsHMA1–9 (Fig. 3B).

The development of the yeast cells containing the positive control, negative control, and OsHMA1–9 genes did not differ significantly in the absence of Cd. However, when exposed to different levels of CdCl2, the Δycf1 yeast cells' growth expressing the functional OsHMA1/2/3/4/5/7/8/9 were significantly suppressed by Cd relative to BY4741-pYES2 and Δycf1-pYES2. Furthermore, we used the transformed yeast cells to test a time-dependent shift in cellular yeast growth response to Cd stress. When we analyzed the relative growth in SG-U broth supplemented with Cd, the growth of Δycf1 cells expressing OsHMA1/2/3/4/5/7/8/9 was lower than BY4741-pYES2 and Δycf1-pYES2 (Fig. 3C). Expression pattern and subcellular localization of the OsHMA gene family

To examine the OsHMA family genes' relative expression trends during various rice growth and development phases, ZH11 was grown from mid-June to late-September in a paddy field at the China National Rice Research Institute (CNRRI-CAAS), Hangzhou, China. Samples from various tissues were collected during different stages of rice growth, including seedling, tillering, booting, flowering, as well as grain-filling stages. The wild-type ZH11's total RNA was extracted and converted into cDNA from various tissues during numerous phases of development. qRT-PCR analysis showed that OsHMA family genes were constitutively expressed in rice tissues at different growth stages, among which OsHMA1, OsHMA2, and OsHMA4 were most highly expressed in seeds 6 days after filling, OsHMA3 and OsHMA5 was most highly expressed in panicles and internode IV respectively, OsHMA6 was most highly expressed in leaf sheaths and the first node, OsHMA7 was most highly expressed in leaves, OsHMA8 was most highly expressed in stems, and OsHMA9 was most highly expressed in roots and stem bases (Fig. 4A; Fig. S4; Supplementary Table 1).

Genome editing of OsHMA family genes in the ZH11 variety by the CRISPR/Cas9 system

To investigate the biological role of OsHMA family genes, homozygous knockout (loss-of-function) mutants were created using the CRISPR/Cas9 system which could help to develop low-Cd-accumulating rice varieties. In this regard, sgRNA and the rice U6 promoter are carried by a CRISPR/Cas9 vector that targets the exon of OsHMA family genes (HMA1-HMA9), constructed (Fig. 5).

The CRISPR/Cas9 construct of OsHMA family genes was inserted into ZH11 via Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. In T0 generation, transgene-free, homozygous novel alleles of the OsHMA family genes were determined by PCR-based screening as well as Sanger sequencing. The CRISPR/Cas9 system produced numerous knockout mutant plants; however, two homozygous lines for each gene were selected for further studies. For example, KO-OsHMA1–1 and KO-OsHMA1–2 contained A-insert and 4 bp-deletions, respectively, KO-OsHMA3–1 and KO-OsHMA3–2 both contained A-insert, KO-OsHMA4–1 and KO-OsHMA4–2 contained A-insert and 2 bp-deletions respectively, KO-OsHMA5–1 and KO-OsHMA5–2 contained A-insert with 2 bp-deletions and A-insert respectively, KO-OsHMA7–1 and KO-OsHMA7–2 contained A-insert and 6 bp-deletions, KO-OsHMA8–1 and KO-OsHMA8–2 contained T-insert and 2 bp-deletions respectively, KO-OsHMA9–1 and KO-OsHMA9–2 both contained 2 bp-deletions. However, the OsHMA2 knockout plants could not survive, and we could not acquire OsHMA2 knockout plants. Following their propagation to the T1 generation, the mutant lines' seeds were collected for subsequent studies (Fig. 5B).

Using CRISPR/Cas9 technology, OsHMA's knockout homozygous mutant lines were created to study the biological role of OsHMA family genes in transporting Cd metal ions. Following 14-day incubation with 2 μM CdCl2, the growth phenotypes regarding length (cm) as well as fresh weight (g) of roots and shoots of control plus Cd-treated WT and mutants were compared (Fig. 6AC). Knockout of OsHMAs displays increased Cd concentration in shoots

We further examined the Cd content in roots and shoots of OsHMA family gene mutants by ICP-MS. Both mutant lines of OsHMA1, OsHMA3, OsHMA4, OsHMA5, OsHMA7, OsHMA8, and OsHMA9 showed no significant difference in levels of root Cd contents without Cd (mock); likewise, OsHMA1, OsHMA3, OsHMA4, OsHMA5, OsHMA7, OsHMA8, and OsHMA9 showed no significant difference in levels of shoots Cd concentration than WT in the absence of Cd stress (Fig. 6B).

When exposed to 2 μM of CdCl2, the Cd content in the roots and aboveground parts of the mutant seedlings was detected by ICP-MS. The results showed that under Cd stress, the Cd content in the roots of the KO-OsHMA1, KO-OsHMA3, KO-OsHMA4, KO-OsHMA7, and KO-OsHMA9 mutants was significantly lower than that of the WT, while the Cd content in the shoots was significantly higher than WT (Fig. 6B). In contrast, the Cd accumulation in the roots of the mutants KO-OsHMA5 and KO-OsHMA8 was higher compared to WT, while the Cd accumulation in the shoots was lower than WT.

Discussion

Cd is extremely hazardous HM and could be detrimental to human health when it accumulates in the body (Hu et al., 2022). The consumption of Cd-contaminated food leads to bioaccumulation-particularly in the lungs, liver, and kidneys, thereby heightening overall cancer risk in humans (Suhani et al., 2021). The well-being of people particularly those who rely on rice as their staple food, depends on producing safe rice (Hu et al., 2024). Therefore, finding the transporters in rice roots that facilitate Cd uptake is crucial to lowering Cd content because the transporters mostly facilitate this process in absorbing vital nutrients (Liu et al., 2025). Prior research findings showed that several transporters transport HMs in rice. For instance, in rice, three NRAMP genes were identified as having a functional relationship with Cd transport (Li, He, et al., 2022; Li, Mao, et al., 2022). Transmembrane proteins known as P1B-type heavy-metal ATPases (HMAs) transport metals and maintain metal homeostasis. To date, rice OsHMA2 (Satoh-Nagasawa et al., 2012; Takahashi, Ishimaru, et al., 2012), OsHMA3 (Miyadate et al., 2011; Yan et al., 2024), and OsHMA9 (Lee et al., 2007) are the only three HMA members reported in the OsHMA family genes mediating Cd transport. The current study involved the functional validation of OsHMA1/4/5/7/8 family members to determine their Cd-specific functional activity. According to phylogenetic analysis and sequence alignment, the rice HMA family possessed more homology to A. thaliana proteins (Wenli et al., 2020). Except for the Hydrolase domains as well as E1-E2 ATPase, some OsHMA proteins had other domains, like HMA superfamily, HAD_like superfamily, and DNA_ligase_OB superfamily, which revealed the wider functional diversity of the OsHMA gene family, making it worthwhile to explore. Moreover, OsHMAs contain 7–8 TMDs, introns 1–16, and exons 1–17. Based on cis-regulatory regions analysis in OsHMA family members' 5′ upstream sequences (putative promoter regions), every gene has multiple cis-elements that are susceptible to abiotic stress, implying that abiotic stressors may cause these genes to become active.

Subcellular localization emerged as the strongest mechanistic predictor of functional phenotype. Our localization analysis revealed that OsHMA1, OsHMA7, and OsHMA8 are localized to the chloroplast, suggesting their involvement in metal homeostasis and the protection of photosynthetic processes. Consistent with this observation, Ren et al. (2026) identified OsHMA7 as a chloroplast envelope-localized Cu transporter in rice, and its disruption reduces plastocyanin accumulation and impairs photosynthetic electron transport efficiency (Ren et al., 2026). Whereas OsHMA1 is proposed to function in Zn efflux from chloroplasts analogous to AtHMA1 in Arabidopsis (Takahashi, Bashir, et al., 2012). OsHMA5, OsHMA6, and OsHMA9 localized to the plasma membrane, directly explaining their roles in cellular metal efflux and xylem loading. Deng et al. (2013) reported that OsHMA5 is a pericycle plasma membrane Cu/Cd xylem loader whose knockout retains metals in roots. OsHMA6 is a plasma membrane Cu efflux protein (Wenli et al., 2020). OsHMA2 and OsHMA4 primarily localized to the plasma membrane (Huang et al., 2016; Takahashi, Ishimaru, et al., 2012; Yamaji et al., 2013). However, in the present study, fluorescence signals of OsHMA2, OsHMA4, and OsHMA6 were also detected in the ER, suggesting dual subcellular targeting of these transporters. Likewise, OsZIP1, a rice Zn/Cu/Cd transporter, similarly localizes to both the ER and plasma membrane and functions as a metal efflux detoxification transporter in rice roots (Liu et al., 2019). OsHMA3 localized to the vacuolar membrane (tonoplast), fully consistent with its primary role as the major vacuolar Cd sequestration checkpoint in rice roots, where tonoplast-directed Cd efflux into vacuoles directly prevents xylem loading and shoot translocation (Miyadate et al., 2011; Ueno et al., 2010). Collectively, the distribution of OsHMA members across chloroplast, plasma membrane, tonoplast, and ER compartments reflects their evolutionary specialization (Miyadate et al., 2011; Ren et al., 2026; Wenli et al., 2020; Yan et al., 2024), as multi-checkpoint gatekeepers of Cd homeostasis across distinct cellular locations in rice.

Yeast is widely used as heterologous expression system to determine the cellular function of plant transporter genes because of its shared eukaryotic organelles with plants, including the vacuole and cell wall (Dong et al., 2026). In our study, the functionality of OsHMA family genes for Cd was verified by heterologous expression in the ∆ycf1 mutant strain susceptible towards high Cd (Gravot et al., 2004; Miyadate et al., 2011; Morel et al., 2009). As demonstrated in Fig. 3B, BY4741-pYES2, Δycf1-pYES2, and ∆ycf1 expressing OsHMA6 did not differ significantly in their growth phenotype. Wenli et al. (2020) also reported that OsHMA6 has no Cd transport activity, but is a copper efflux protein located in the plasma membrane. Whereas, the growth of ∆ycf1 expressing OsHMA1/2/3/4/5/7/8/9 genes were less than yeast cells that were transformed using the BY4741-pYES2 and Δycf1-pYES2. The growth inhibition observed in ∆ycf1 yeast upon expression of functional OsHMA1/2/3/4/5/7/8/9 family genes, indicate that OsHMA1/2/3/4/5/7/8/9 may act as a transporter that facilitates the import of Cd ions into the yeast cells. Since the ∆ycf1 lacks the ycf1 gene, which is crucial for HMs detoxification (Gravot et al., 2004; Morel et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2025), introducing OsHMA family genes could enhance Cd uptake without the corresponding ability to sequester and detoxify the metal. For instance, the heterologous expression of TpNRAMP5 in yeast significantly increased Cd sensitivity and Cd concentration, particularly in the Δycf1 mutant strain than empty vector control (Peng et al., 2018). Liao et al. (2023) found that expression of ZmHMA3 in Δycf1 mutant markedly increased sensitivity to Cd stress, suggesting that ZmHMA3 is involved in Cd transport and may facilitate Cd influx and accumulation in yeast cells. Likewise, the overexpression of full-length CaHMA1 in the Δycf1 yeast strain markedly increased Cd sensitivity (Xu et al., 2023). Similarly, heterologous expression of SpHMA2, SpHMA3, and SpNramp6 in the Cd-sensitive yeast mutant Δycf1 resulted in enhanced Cd sensitivity and reduced yeast growth under Cd stress, indicating increased Cd influx and intracellular accumulation (Yang et al., 2023). As a result, the increased intracellular accumulation of Cd may lead to heightened oxidative stress and toxicity, ultimately inhibiting yeast growth. The altered Cd sensitivity phenotype observed in ∆ycf1 yeast expressing OsHMA1/2/3/4/5/7/8/9 members suggests their possible involvement in Cd-specific functional activity.

To shed light on the role of the OsHMA gene family in Cd accumulation and detoxification in rice, we created the overexpression lines as well as homozygous knockout mutants by CRISPR/Cas9. As expected, we found that homozygous knockout (loss-of-function) mutants of OsHMA1, OsHMA3, OsHMA4, OsHMA7, and OsHMA9 were more sensitive to excess Cd, displayed a significant decrease in above-ground parts as well as fresh weights of roots and shoots, and showed significantly lower Cd contents in roots and higher levels in shoots (Fig. 6). Knockout of OsHMA3, localized to the tonoplast, directly abolishes vacuolar Cd sequestration in root cells, increasing cytosolic Cd availability and xylem loading, resulting in elevated shoot Cd accumulation (Miyadate et al., 2011). Loss of plasma membrane-localized OsHMA4 and OsHMA9 eliminates cellular Cd efflux activity, causing cytosolic Cd overflow that saturates vacuolar sequestration capacity and forces excess Cd into the xylem translocation stream (Huang et al., 2016; Lee et al., 2007). Knockout of chloroplast-localized OsHMA1 and OsHMA7 indirectly elevates shoot Cd by impairing chloroplast metal homeostasis and antioxidant defense capacity, compromising the root cell's overall ability to retain Cd under stress (Ren et al., 2026). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the increased shoot Cd phenotype observed across all knockout lines is mechanistically encoded in the distinct subcellular localizations of individual OsHMA members, operating through direct vacuolar sequestration, plasma membrane efflux, and indirect organellar protection pathways respectively. Previously, it has been reported that significant yield losses caused by mutations in Cd transporters, such as OsZIP5, OsZIP9, OsHMA2, and OsNramp5 (Satoh-Nagasawa et al., 2012; Tan et al., 2020; Tang et al., 2017). When OsHIPP16 was knocked out, rice's growth response was less, and its Cd content was higher (Cao et al., 2022). OsGST's mutant loss function showed an increased buildup of Cd and a Cd-sensitive phenotype (Guo et al., 2024). The OsABCC9 gene, positioned at parenchymal cells' vacuolar membrane inside the stele, is expressed in rice's reaction to Cd stress. OsABCC9 silencing increased Cd levels also hypersensitivity (Yang et al., 2021).

Interestingly, knockout of OsHMA5 and OsHMA8 mutants exhibited substantially improved growth and accumulated less Cd in shoots, while, more in roots in contrast to the WT; these findings indicate that the OsHMA5 as well as OsHMA8 knockout decreased Cd uptake and reduced the impact of Cd on rice growth and improved rice's resistance to Cd (Fig. 6). This is mechanistically explained by the established plasma membrane, pericycle-localized xylem-loading function of OsHMA5, its loss abolishes root-to-xylem metal flux, retaining Cd in roots and reducing shoot translocation, consistent with the confirmed role of OsHMA5 at the pericycle plasma membrane in xylem loading reported by Deng et al. (2013). OsHMA5 knockout resulted in reduced Cu concentration in shoots and increased Cu in roots at the vegetative stage (Deng et al., 2013). Lei et al. (2020) demonstrated that the insertion of a Sukkula-like transposable element into the wild-type HvHMA3 allele generated a superior HvHMA3 variant, which significantly reduced Cd accumulation in barley grains. Moreover, knockout of OsCSG1 significantly exhibited increased Cd in roots but not in shoots under Cd stress. OsCSG1 knockout enhanced rice's ability to withstand Cd by controlling the subcellular distribution of Cd (Liang et al., 2025). Under Cd-contaminated hydroponic conditions, OsLCD knockout mutants significantly accumulated lower Cd in shoots than WT plants, because of reduced Cd translocation from roots-to-shoots. Moreover, the mutants exhibited lower oxidative damage and improved growth performance under Cd stress, indicating enhanced Cd tolerance relative to WT (Chen, Ye, et al., 2023). Furthermore, silencing OsLCT1 facilitates Cd transportation among vascular tissues, reducing the grain's Cd levels (Uraguchi et al., 2014). Collectively, these findings support our results that knockout of OsHMA5/8 restricts Cd translocation from roots to shoots, thereby reducing Cd toxicity in aerial tissues.

Furthermore, our results demonstrated that overexpression of OsHMA1/3/4/5/7/8/9 conferred Cd tolerance to transgenic plants. Cd contents were measured via ICP-MS in shoots as well as roots to verify the Cd-specific functional role. ICP-MS analysis showed that root Cd contents were significantly higher in OE-OsHMA1/3/4/5/7/8/9, while OE lines' shoots had significantly lower Cd concentrations than WT, showing that Cd translocation from roots towards shoots was decreased by overexpression of OsHMA family genes (Fig. 7). Thus, overexpression of OsHMA1/3/4/5/7/8/9 enhances Cd tolerance by promoting Cd sequestration according to their respective subcellular localizations, thereby reducing the translocation of toxic Cd to the shoots. Guo et al. (2024) reported that transgenic lines that overexpressed OsGST showed improved Cd tolerance and decreased Cd accumulation, similar to other metal transporters. Wang et al. (2025) found that in OE-OsNTP1 lines' Cd content was substantially lower compared to WT, as determined by ICP-MS analysis. Without negatively affecting yield, ZmHMA3a overexpressing lines increased Cd sequestration and decreased Cd concentrations in gains when grown in Cd-polluted soil (Chen, Chao, et al., 2023). OsHIPP16 (OE) overexpression enhanced rice development by increasing the height of plants, yield, and chlorophyll levels while reducing oxidative damage and Cd buildup (Cao et al., 2022). Likewise, OE-OsHIPP9 might enhance Cd buildup in nodes as well as transport into enlarged leaves along with leaf sheaths, consequently reducing the Cd level transmitted to the panicle, and particularly the rice grain (Xiong et al., 2023). Significantly reducing shoot Cd accumulation with OsZIP2 overexpression suggests that OsZIP2 overexpression inhibited the transfer of Cd from roots into shoots (Li et al., 2024). OsLCT2 overexpression limits the quantities of Cd entered the xylem and prevents translocation of Cd from rice roots to shoots, hence reducing Cd accumulation in rice shoots (Tang et al., 2021). Although this study comprehensively characterized the Cd-specific roles of OsHMA1/3/4/5/7/8/9 members, future studies should investigate downstream regulatory pathways, grain-specific Cd flux, and field-level performance of OsHMA family members to exploit its potential in breeding low-cadmium rice cultivars.

Conclusion

This study provides the first systematic and functional characterization of the OsHMA gene family under Cd stress in rice, revealing that OsHMA members perform functionally distinct and complementary roles in Cd detoxification rather than a uniform protective function. Integrating subcellular localization, expression profiling, yeast functional assays, and transgenic plant validation, we demonstrate that OsHMA family members operate as specialized gatekeepers at multiple cellular checkpoints along the root-to-shoot Cd translocation pathway. Tonoplast-localized members (OsHMA3) sequester Cd in root vacuoles, preventing xylem loading and shoot translocation, while plasma membrane-localized members (OsHMA2, OsHMA4, OsHMA5, OsHMA6, OsHMA9) regulate xylem-loading flux at the pericycle, and chloroplast-targeted members (OsHMA1, OsHMA7, OsHMA8) protect photosynthetic integrity under Cd stress. Overexpression of all OsHMA1/3/4/5/7/8/9 consistently improved Cd tolerance and reduced shoot Cd accumulation, confirming their collective protective roles through mechanistically distinct pathways. These findings advance our understanding of how the OsHMA family members govern Cd partitioning between roots and shoots in rice, providing a comprehensive candidate gene resource for future functional studies aimed at reducing Cd accumulation in rice tissues under controlled conditions.
