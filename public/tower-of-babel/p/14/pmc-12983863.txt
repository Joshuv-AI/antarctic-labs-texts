Stimulating amyloid-β clearance in cerebral amyloid angiopathy with low-sodium oxybate and/or non-invasive vagus nerve stimulation (Clear-Brain): study protocol for a randomised pre-post trial
Authors: Sanne E Schriemer, Lydiane Hirschler, Ellis S van Etten, Erik W van Zwet, Gert Jan Lammers, Eric J Liebler, Marianne A A van Walderveen, Annelies M Slats, Adriaan C G M van Es, Marcel M Verbeek, Matthias J P van Osch, Marieke J H Wermer, Rolf Fronczek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is caused by the accumulation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) in the cerebrovasculature. The glymphatic system is thought to be involved in the clearance of cerebral waste products, including Aβ. Stimulation of the glymphatic system through enhancing deep sleep with low-sodium oxybate (LXB) or inhibition of cortical spreading depolarisations via non-invasive vagus nerve stimulation (nVNS) could potentially increase clearance of Aβ and hence improve disease course.Methods and analysisWe will perform a pre-post trial to assess whether treatment with LXB, nVNS or a combination of both interventions can enhance the clearance of Aβ in patients with CAA. A total of 60 participants, 30 with sporadic CAA and 30 with Dutch-type CAA, will be randomly assigned to receive either LXB, nVNS or both interventions, resulting in three groups (20 in each group: LXB, nVNS and both). The study spans 6 months, comprising a 3-month observational phase and a 3-month intervention phase. The primary outcome measure will be the morning levels of Aβ40 and Aβ42 in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) before and after the intervention. We will assess possible disease progression with (non-)haemorrhagic imaging markers on 7-Tesla MRI at baseline, before and after intervention, as a secondary outcome. Additionally, the activity of the glymphatic system by means of fluid dynamics will be assessed with CSF-Selective T2-weighted Readout with Acceleration and Mobility encoding (CSF-STREAM) on 7-Tesla MRI.Ethics and disseminationThe study was reviewed and approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee Leiden The Hague Delft (P23.100) on 8 April 2024. The first participant was enrolled on 27 March 2025. Study results will be published in peer-reviewed journals and presented at scientific conferences. Additionally, study updates and results will be shared with participants via our newsletter twice a year.Trial registrationEU CT number 2023–5 06 128-10-00, Universal Trial Number U1111-1295-1113, ClinicalTrials.gov NCT06421532.

Introduction

Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is one of the major causes of intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH), cognitive dysfunction and dementia in the elderly. Approximately 60% of all lobar ICHs are attributed to CAA.1 In CAA, accumulation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) occurs within the leptomeningeal arteries, cortical arterioles and capillaries of the brain, thereby compromising the structural integrity of these vessels. The sporadic variant (sCAA) accounts for most CAA cases worldwide. Dutch-type hereditary CAA (D-CAA) is a rare hereditary form of CAA in which Aβ deposits are formed due to a mutation at codon 693 of the amyloid precursor protein (APP) gene on chromosome 21.2 Patients with D-CAA have a severely increased risk of ICH at a relatively young age. Since the subsequent pathological changes are similar, D-CAA can be seen as a model for CAA, although with an accelerated clinical disease course.3 No curative or disease-slowing treatments exist for CAA.

Unlike other organs, the brain lacks a traditional lymphatic network for the clearance of interstitial fluid and waste products. The glymphatic system was proposed as a macroscopic waste clearance system responsible for the removal of waste products, including Aβ. One of the current leading hypotheses is that CAA represents a brain clearance disorder, characterised by Aβ accumulation in the vessel wall. The glymphatic pathway clears via perivascular spaces (PVS) into the subarachnoid space, where dural lymphatic vessels, routes along cranial nerves and venous uptake via arachnoid granulations support further egress out of the cranium.4 Details on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and interstitial fluid flow are still debated, with the original models of intramural periarterial drainage and glymphatics being updated continuously.58

Several studies have demonstrated that the glymphatic system is mostly active during sleep, particularly during deep, slow-wave sleep.9 A study investigating the effect of gamma-hydroxybutyric acid (GHB) versus placebo in healthy male volunteers showed that GHB prolongs slow-wave sleep (stage N3).10 GHB is a naturally occurring neurotransmitter and the active moiety of both sodium oxybate (SXB; Xyrem) and low-sodium oxybate (LXB; Xywav). LXB is safely and effectively used in treatment for the primary sleep disorder, narcolepsy, in both adults and children,11 as well as idiopathic hypersomnia in adults.12 Consequently, LXB stands as a plausible candidate for deepening sleep and, by extension, stimulating the glymphatic system.

Furthermore, cortical spreading depolarisations (CSDs) are thought to disrupt the glymphatic system. CSDs are pathological waves of neuronal and glial depolarisations throughout the cortex. CSDs are thought to be involved in the pathogenesis of migraine aura, transient focal neurologic episodes (TFNEs) and the development of microinfarcts in CAA and in secondary damage after stroke.1316 Research in mice has shown that CSDs induce rapid closure of PVS around the surface, penetrating cortical arteries and veins. After a CSD-related closure, PVS remains partially constricted for ≥30 min.17 During PVS closure, the inflow of intraparenchymal-injected dye from the cortex into the PVS is reduced.17 Research in rats has shown that invasive as well as non-invasive vagus nerve stimulation (nVNS) is very effective in inhibiting CSDs.18 19 Moreover, VNS appears to be a safe treatment for several conditions in humans.2024 However, the specific impact of nVNS on the glymphatic system in humans has not yet been explored. In patients with CAA, we anticipate an effect, given the high incidence of CSD-related phenomena such as migraine with aura, TFNEs and cortical microinfarcts.2527

We hypothesise that treatment of patients with D-CAA and sCAA with LXB, nVNS or both interventions will enhance the clearance of Aβ by stimulating the glymphatic system. This hypothesis will be tested in a pre-post clinical trial, with morning levels of Aβ40 and Aβ42 in CSF serving as the primary outcome measures to assess improved clearance. Additionally, a novel, non-invasive and replicable method for visualising CSF mobility in large CSF spaces, such as ventricles, as well as in smaller spaces like PVS, will enable us to explore the functioning of the glymphatic system.

Methods and analysis

Study design

Our study is a pre-post trial with additional randomisation, divided into three groups, each comprising 20 participants diagnosed with CAA (figure 1). The study is designed to measure the difference between preintervention and postintervention. Randomisation is added to the study design for the secondary outcome measures to assess differences between the groups. The trial is a single-centre study conducted at the Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC), Leiden, the Netherlands. Participants will be allocated to receive either LXB, nVNS or both interventions simultaneously. The study unfolds over a 6-month period, with the intervention phase starting at 3 months and continuing until the 6th month.

Study logo.

At baseline assessment (t=0), participants will undergo a battery of evaluations, including a 7-Tesla MRI (7-T MRI) scan and the administration of the following five questionnaires: Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale, Insomnia Severity Index, Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MOCA), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index and Short Form Health Survey (SF-36). Subsequently, at 3 (t=3) and 6 months (t=6), the following procedures will be performed: lumbar puncture for the collection of CSF, 7-T MRI, blood sample collection and repeated administration of the five questionnaires from baseline (figure 2).

Participants will be screened for sleep apnoea using respiratory polygraphy between the baseline visit and the start of the intervention period. The respiratory polygraphy will be analysed by a pulmonologist, resulting in an apnea-hypopnea index (AHI). Participants with an AHI score of ≥15, central AI score ≥5, mean peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) ≤90% or >25% of total sleep time with SpO2 <90% will not be eligible for LXB and will instead be assigned to the nVNS intervention group. At 1, 3 and 5 weeks after the start of the intervention, participants using LXB will be screened for the development of sleep apnoea using the STOP-Bang questionnaire (Snoring, Tiredness, Observed apnoea, high blood Pressure, Body mass index, Age, Neck circumference, and Gender) administered via telephone.28 Participants showing an increased risk for OSA based on their STOP-Bang score (‘yes’ to two or more questions of the first four STOP questions) will be reviewed by a neurologist-somnologist. Based on the initial respiratory polygraphy results, STOP-Bang scores and anamnesis, it may be necessary to repeat the polygraphy. Side effects and compliance will be assessed with a questionnaire at 6 months.

As a control group for secondary outcomes, we will use data from the placebo group of the 'minocycline for sporadic and hereditary cerebral amyloid angiopathy' (BATMAN) study (P19.110) included in the Neurological Diseases Biobank of the LUMC. The BATMAN study is a randomised controlled trial conducted at the LUMC, following a study protocol similar to that of the Clear-Brain study. In the BATMAN study, minocycline versus placebo is investigated as an intervention over 3 months in participants with sCAA and D-CAA, with a lumbar puncture performed before and after the intervention.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We will include participants with D-CAA aged 30 years or older, with a proven APP mutation or a history of one or more lobar ICHs and a positive family history for D-CAA in at least one first-degree relative, and the presence of at least one (non-)haemorrhagic marker on MRI. Haemorrhagic markers are cortical superficial siderosis (cSS) or cortical microbleeds (CMBs), and non-haemorrhagic markers are severe PVS in the centrum semiovale (CSO-PVS) or white matter hyperintensities (WMHs) in a multispot pattern. The Boston criteria 2.0 and the Standards for Reporting Vascular Changes on Neuroimaging criteria are used for the definition of CSO-PVS and WMHs.2931 CSO-PVS will be assessed on T2-weighted images and characterised as more than 20 visible PVS in the CSO of one hemisphere. WMHs will be evaluated on T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery images and defined as more than 10 small hyperintense lesions within the subcortical white matter of both hemispheres. Presymptomatic participants must be aware of their D-CAA diagnosis. Participants with sCAA can be included if they fulfil the Boston criteria 2.0 for a diagnosis of probable sCAA.29 Suppose the criteria for probable sCAA are not met due to the presence of deep haemorrhagic lesions. In that case, participants can be included only if cSS and predominantly lobar CMBs are present or if no cSS is present but a ratio of 10 times more lobar CMBs than deep microbleeds is observed. Moreover, eligibility requires that participants have had no more than two symptomatic ICHs, with the most recent event occurring at least 1 year before enrolment. Participants must not have experienced more than one episode of CAA-related inflammation (CAA-ri), confirmed by MRI in combination with compatible clinical symptoms. In the event of a single prior CAA-ri episode, participants are only eligible if they have remained clinically stable for at least 6 months following the completion of corticosteroid treatment. The inclusion criteria are designed to investigate stages three and four of the CAA pathology frameworks.32 The exclusion criteria and specific contraindications to the study procedures, including LXB, nVNS, 7 T MRI and lumbar puncture, are listed in table 1.

Randomisation

Computer-based block randomisation stratified by disease will be performed by the LUMC pharmacy, resulting in six subgroups (10 in each subgroup: subgroup 1 D-CAA LXB, subgroup 2 D-CAA nVNS, subgroup 3 D-CAA LXB+nVNS, subgroup 4 sCAA LXB, subgroup 5 sCAA nVNS and subgroup 6 sCAA LXB+nVNS). We will exclude participants with a prior diagnosis of sleep apnoea. However, participants who test positive for sleep apnoea at baseline screening will not be randomised but will instead be assigned to the nVNS intervention group. This strategy aims to avoid the exclusion of a disproportionate number of participants after the first study visit. Furthermore, participants will be withdrawn from the study if the first lumbar puncture is unsuccessful and CSF cannot be obtained, as this is required for the primary outcome. After withdrawal, individual participants will be replaced with new participants. If the second lumbar puncture could not be obtained, participants will be excluded from the primary outcome, but not from the secondary outcomes.

Intervention

We will use XYWAV as LXB for deepening sleep and the gammaCore Sapphire device for nVNS. XYWAV is an oral solution containing oxybate with a lower amount of sodium and is a central nervous system depressant. The chemical name of oxybate is GHB. LXB in fluid form will be taken orally at bedtime with a starting dose of 2 g. Because of its short half-life of 30–60 min, participants must take LXB immediately before sleep. Participants will only take one dose per night. The dosage will subsequently be titrated with a weekly increase of 0.5 g until a target dose of 5 g is reached after 6 weeks. Hence, each participant will maintain a stable dose period of at least 6 weeks. If side effects occur, a slower titration and a lower dose than 5 g will be accepted, with a minimum dose of 2.25 g. If participants consequently wake up during the night due to the short half-life of LXB, a second dose after 2.5–4 hours can be added to the dosage regimen. If a second dose is necessary, a maximum of 9 g per night is allowed and should preferably be divided equally between the two doses. However, an unequal split will be permitted if necessary. Furthermore, certain antiepileptics (valproic acid, phenytoin, ethosuximide and topiramate) may interact with LXB. Therefore, a 20% dose reduction of LXB should be considered if these antiepileptics are to be continued during the intervention period. In the case of benzodiazepine use before inclusion, a two-night washout is required before the start of the intervention (t=3). During participation, illicit use of GHB is prohibited, and a 1-week washout is required before inclusion (t=0).

For participants assigned to nVNS, the gammaCore Sapphire handheld device will be used twice per day with two 2-min stimulations: once within an hour after waking and once within an hour before sleeping. Participants will be trained by qualified researchers to use the stimulator independently. Researchers themselves will receive training from electroCore, the manufacturer of the gammaCore Sapphire. On the final study day (t=6), nVNS should be applied within an hour before the 7 T MRI session. Participants can choose their preferred stimulation side (right or left), unless contraindicated by specific conditions (table 1). The stimulator employs a constant voltage signal comprising 5 kHz sine waves repeated at a frequency of 25 Hz. Stimulation intensity is adjustable within a scale of 0, indicating no stimulation, to 40, representing maximum stimulation, for a predetermined duration of 2 min. The stimulation should be perceptible but not painful, and the level of the amplitude is hence based on individual preference between 15 and 25. The stimulation protocol was developed in collaboration with electroCore.

Participants will be instructed to limit their caffeine intake to a maximum of five cups of coffee or other caffeinated beverages per day.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome measures are the morning Aβ 40 and 42 levels in CSF before and after treatment. CSF will be obtained through a lumbar puncture at t=3 and t=6 months.

Sample size calculation

The sample size of this proof-of-concept study is based on our primary objective, the morning levels of Aβ40 and Aβ42 in CSF. A formal power calculation is difficult due to the lack of information on longitudinal Aβ levels in patients with CAA and the effect of glymphatic system stimulation on these levels. An ongoing natural history study may provide insight into the longitudinal changes in Aβ levels in patients with CAA and could serve as a reference, but the results of this study are not yet available. A previous study in healthy controls showed significantly decreased morning CSF levels of Aβ40 and Aβ42 after sleep deprivation, with a mean reduction in Aβ42 level of 76pg/mL (477±148 pg/mL vs 401±165 pg/mL).40 Assuming at least a similar effect size after 3 months of sleep-deepening treatment using a paired t-test per patient (power of 0.9, alpha=0.05), a minimum of 51 participants would be required to yield a power of 90% for detecting a 70 pg/mL difference in CSF Aβ levels. To ensure sufficient power and account for loss to follow-up and potential difficulties in obtaining a second lumbar puncture, we selected a total sample size of 60 participants. Two considerations support the expectation of a larger effect. First, another study demonstrated a greater difference following sleep deprivation.41 Second, the change in Aβ levels may be more pronounced in patients with CAA than in healthy controls or other patient groups.42 43 Therefore, we would expect a difference exceeding 70 pg/mL. The study will have sufficient power to compare groups of 20 participants if the treatment results in a difference of ≥115 pg/mL in CSF Aβ levels.

Patient and public involvement

The study design and participant information material have been discussed with the patient representatives, namely the Dutch CAA Foundation and the HCHWA-D Foundation. Additionally, patient representatives are involved in the trial steering committee.

Recruitment

The LUMC has a history of performing scientific studies in CAA, and there is a database of people interested in participating in research. Participants of former CAA studies will be contacted by email or telephone and informed about this study. Furthermore, the study will be advertised on social media and through the Dutch Brain Foundation’s and CAA patient foundations’ websites.

Data collection and management

All data will be collected pseudo-anonymised with an assigned identification code for each participant. The key for participant identification will be stored, separately from the data, on a server with accessibility limited to the principal investigator, selected researchers and a monitor. A standardised data collection procedure is developed to ensure data quality. Data will be documented with Castor Electronic Data Capture (Castor EDC) using specific case report forms. Encryption and specific roles will be used in Castor EDC to ensure confidentiality. Manual data entry will be entered and checked by two researchers. If additional informed consent is obtained, CSF and blood samples will also be collected for the CAA Biobank of the LUMC for ancillary use in future studies.

Data analysis

We will use descriptive statistics for baseline characteristics, the tolerability of LXB and nVNS, the number of participants developing sleep apnoea, Aβ levels in blood plasma, other biomarkers in CSF (eg, Aβ38, Aβ43, t-tau and p-tau181), frequency of the different (non-)haemorrhagic markers on 7-T MRI and total CAA-CSVD score. The evolution of MRI markers will be assessed by comparing the scans during the observational period (t=0 and t=3) with those during the intervention period (t=3 and t=6) using a paired-sample t-test. Differences in the evolution of MRI markers between the three intervention groups will be analysed with an analysis of variance (ANOVA) test, and groups will be tested pairwise with adjustment for multiple testing in case of significant differences. Furthermore, a regression model of the MRI markers will be created using the Aβ levels in CSF corrected for age, sex and CAA type (D-CAA or sCAA).

We will assess the difference between Aβ levels at t=3 and t=6 with a paired-sample t-test. A correction for multiple testing will be applied due to the analysis of two Aβ parameters (40 and 42) by adjusting the significance threshold with a Bonferroni correction, dividing the alpha-level by the number of comparisons (0.05/2=0.025). If clear outliers are present, a Mann-Whitney test will be used instead. We will also compare the difference in Aβ levels of the three intervention groups with the placebo control group of the BATMAN study using a posthoc ANOVA test, and groups will be tested pairwise with adjustment for multiple testing in case of significant differences. Only randomised participants will be used for comparison of Aβ level between groups. If participants from the placebo control group of the BATMAN study also participate in the Clear-Brain study, this will be considered repeated measures in our statistical analysis. Missing values of the primary outcome are not expected due to the per-protocol analysis, as patients are excluded from the primary outcome in case a lumbar puncture could not be obtained.

Questionnaires resulting in metric variables will be analysed with paired-sample t-tests and ordinal variables with non-parametric tests. Because CAA is a neurodegenerative disease, no improvement of the MOCA score comparing before and after treatment is expected. We define a difference in MOCA score of two or more to be clinically relevant. The eight variables of the SF-36 can be computed into a Mental Component Score (MCS) and Physical Component Score (PCS). After comparing the total SF-36 score, further exploration into improvement will be done, including examination of the MCS, PCS and the eight individual variables. A difference of five points for the MCS and PCS will be considered clinically relevant.44 45

The differences in CSF mobility, fractional anisotropy and the principal orientation of CSF mobility between the observational period (t=0 and t=3) and the intervention period (t=3 and t=6) will be evaluated using a paired-sample t-test. The CSF-STREAM metrics will also be compared between the three intervention groups using an ANOVA test with posthoc pairwise comparisons. Multiple imputation will be used for handling missing data of secondary outcome measures.

Ethics and dissemination

The Clear-Brain study is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06421532), in the Clinical Trial Information System (2023–5 06 128-10-00), and in the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (U1111-1295-1113). The study was reviewed and approved by the Medical Research Ethics Committee (MREC) Leiden The Hague Delft (P23.100) on 8 April 2024. The first participant was enrolled on 27 March 2025. Written informed consent to participate will be obtained from all participants. All participants are covered by a clinical trial insurance provided by LUMC, in accordance with Dutch regulations on medical research involving human subjects.

Twice per year, the study will be monitored by an assigned independent monitor of the LUMC. The trial steering committee will evaluate the study once a year. The study will be reviewed by the principal investigator (RF) and researchers monthly. No Data Safety Monitoring Board was appointed, and no audit will be conducted as approved by the MREC. Participants will be instructed to report side effects and complaints to the researchers. During the final study visit (t=6), side effects will be assessed with a questionnaire. All potential (serious) adverse events will be documented in detail, and study-related serious adverse events will be reported to the sponsor and the MREC immediately. Additionally, an annual safety report will be submitted through the Clinical Trials Information System (CTIS).

The full protocol will become available via the CTIS after completion of the trial. An English translation of the participant information material and informed consent form is provided as online supplemental material 1. Study results will be published in peer-reviewed journals and presented at scientific conferences. Additionally, study updates and results will be shared with participants via our newsletter twice a year. The principal investigator (RF) and the first author (SES) will have access to the final study dataset. The aggregated dataset analysed during the current study and the statistical code will become available via the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Discussion

The Clear-Brain study is designed as a pre-post trial with randomisation, aimed at investigating the potential benefit of the enhancement of the glymphatic system in patients with CAA. The primary goal is to stimulate the clearance of Aβ through the glymphatic pathway by deepening sleep through the use of LXB and by inhibiting CSDs with nVNS, potentially delaying disease progression.

LXB is a well-established treatment for improving nocturnal sleep and the daytime symptoms of narcolepsy and idiopathic hypersomnia, approved by the US Food and Drug Administration, but not yet available in Europe.11 12 Our choice for LXB over SXB was based on a 92% reduction in sodium intake associated with LXB. Hypertension is an important risk factor for ICH.46 47 Since sodium intake is associated with increased blood pressure and cardiovascular risk,48 49 an increase in sodium intake is undesirable for patients with CAA. The dosage of LXB is derived from (i) the treatment schedule for narcolepsy patients with SXB and (ii) a recent study that demonstrated the safe and effective treatment of idiopathic hypersomnia with LXB, both once nightly with a maximum of 6 g per night, as well as twice nightly with a maximum of 9 g per night.50 Addressing practical concerns, participants will receive comprehensive information about the steps involved in the use of LXB, along with a practical guide for use at home. During the titration phase, researchers will maintain close contact with the participants about dose adjustments by phone interviews.

Invasive VNS is an established treatment for patients with refractory epilepsy and treatment-resistant depression.20 21 51 A study conducted in nine tertiary care centres, including the LUMC, showed that nVNS was superior to sham therapy for the treatment of episodic cluster headache attacks.22 The NOVIS study conducted at the LUMC investigated the effect of nVNS on reducing secondary damage after acute ischaemic stroke. nVNS was found to be safe and feasible in stroke patients, but results have not yet been published.52 A practical concern for nVNS could be related to the incorrect use by individual participants. To ensure optimal application of the device, researchers will be in contact with the participant in the first 2 weeks of the treatment phase.

This study has some limitations. First, the 3-month timeframe for comparing disease progression might be too short for detecting a substantial effect on microvascular damage. However, small vessel diseases appear to be more rapidly progressive in the short term than previously thought.53 Moreover, the ultra-high-field MRI used in this study enables the visualisation of microvascular changes in much more detail compared with more conventional MRI techniques and allows for the detection of even minor differences.54 Second, the optimal time point for measuring CSF mobility with CSF-STREAM is yet unknown. However, it is imaginable that this could be at night-time during slow-wave sleep. We have chosen to perform the MRI scan in the early morning, as the first assessment of the day, to avoid the practical implications and burden of sleeping in the 7-T MRI. Additionally, both the 7-T MRI and lumbar punctures will be performed in the morning at each time point, ensuring that measurements are taken at the same time of the day to account for potential circadian influences. Third, a definite diagnosis of sCAA can only be established by histopathological examination of brain tissue, and therefore, only patients with a diagnosis of probable CAA based on clinical and imaging criteria can be included. The absence of a definitive pathological diagnosis could potentially lead to the inclusion of participants with other cerebral pathologies. However, the same diagnostic criteria are used in clinical practice, and our findings will therefore reflect real-world diagnoses. Additionally, a validation study has shown that the Boston criteria 2.0 have high sensitivity and specificity when compared with histopathological assessment of CAA.29 Besides, this only applies to participants with sCAA, since participants with D-CAA have a genetically confirmed diagnosis. Fourth, we use a historical control group from the BATMAN study. This decision was based on feasibility and the burden on the control group, which includes, among others, undergoing lumbar punctures with the concomitant risk of complications. Also, a control group could lead to recruitment issues due to the inevitable rise in sample size, which is difficult considering the limited population of patients with D-CAA. Moreover, due to the unmistakable effects of LXB as well as nVNS, adequate blinding with a control group is difficult to realise. However, the placebo group in the BATMAN study is a suitable substitute for a real control group because the inclusion criteria of the BATMAN study are very similar to those of this study, and participants were recruited from the same Dutch patient population. Moreover, to ensure consistency between the BATMAN and the Clear-Brain study, Aβ levels in CSF will be analysed in a single batch, mitigating potential batch differences. Fifth, we will not measure the sleep architecture of participants for validation of the effect of LXB during the study because (i) the effect of LXB to increase the amount of slow-wave sleep has already been proved in a previous study,10 and (ii) we consider repeated nightly polysomnography too much of a burden for the participants.

Strengths of this study include the possibility of inclusion of D-CAA participants with a definite diagnosis of CAA who have only non-haemorrhagic MRI markers and thus are in stage three of the CAA pathological framework, whereas other studies focusing on sCAA patients often only included participants in stage four of this framework.32 Since it would be reasonable to suggest that treatment initiation in earlier stages of the disease could have a more profound impact on disease progression, this would increase the chance of observing a relevant treatment effect. Another strength is the observational period 3 months before the start of the intervention, which provides a baseline for secondary outcomes. Additionally, our study allows for the evaluation of both CSF markers as well as radiological markers and clinical outcomes. Furthermore, we will use ultra-high-field MRI, resulting in high-quality imaging and providing us with the unique opportunity to explore the glymphatic system using CSF-STREAM, which cannot be performed on 3-T MRI. Moreover, the use of a combination intervention group using both LXB and nVNS allows us to explore an additive effect. Since the glymphatic system’s functioning is still highly debated, our results may also provide important insights into the physiology of the glymphatic pathway and the potential for stimulating this waste clearance mechanism. If our intervention is proven to stimulate glymphatic clearance, it could be a breakthrough in the treatment of CAA. In addition, it may be a potential target for treatments of other neurodegenerative disorders that are caused by protein aggregation, such as Parkinson’s and Alzheimer’s disease.

Study status

The first participant was enrolled on 27 March 2025. As of the time of writing, eight participants have been included. Recruitment is anticipated to continue for approximately 2 years. This manuscript is based on protocol V.4.0, dated 31 October 2025.
