The Wiskott-Aldrich Syndrome protein (WASp) contribution to microglial phagocytic function and neurodevelopmental support
Authors: Serena Seminara, Aurora Bianchi, Davide Comolli, Marco Spreafico, Wenjie Liao, Hemanta Sarmah, Grazia Iannello, Barbara Corneo, Gianluigi Forloni, Elisa R. Zanier, Massimiliano De Paola, Alessandro Fantin, Stefano Fumagalli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Myeloid progenitor cells colonize the brain during embryogenesis and differentiate in microglia. Microglia shape neuronal wiring during development and maintain brain homeostasis in adulthood, both actions requiring intact cytoskeletal functionality. The Wiskott-Aldrich syndrome protein (WASp) mediates cytoskeletal dynamics of peripheral myeloid cells, suggesting a similar role in microglia. To investigate WASp’s role in microglia, we impaired WASp function in human induced pluripotent stem cells-derived microglia (iMicro) and zebrafish embryos.WASp colocalized with the actin cytoskeleton at membrane ruffles in phagocytic iMicro and appeared required for their phagocytic function. When co-cultured with neuronal cells, the support of iMicro with defective WASp function to neuronal wiring was impaired. Similarly, zebrafish embryos exposed to WASp inhibition showed brain accumulation of uncleared apoptotic bodies, reduced brain colonization of myeloid cells, and impaired sensorimotor response to mechanical stimuli.These findings identify WASp as a regulator of microglial phagocytosis and cytoskeletal dynamics, with implication in neuronal wiring during neurodevelopment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12974-026-03948-3.

Background

Resident macrophages of the central nervous system (CNS) can be divided into two principal populations: microglia, which are located within the parenchyma, and CNS-associated macrophages (CAMs), found in extra-parenchymal regions, specifically at the interface of brain limitans (i.e. the blood-brain barrier, the choroid plexus and the meninges). Both CAMs and microglia derive from progenitors in the embryonic yolk sac (YS) [1, 2] and settle in the central nervous system during embryogenesis [3], in specific niches providing differentiating cues [4]. Microglia share key immune functions, such as phagocytosis and phenotypic traits with peripheral macrophages and dendritic cells [5–7]. Microglia contribute to brain homeostasis and readily respond to threatening conditions: they constantly survey the brain [8, 9], presenting sensor systems that are linked to signaling mechanisms that allow these cells to respond to and adapt to changes in the environment [10]. Besides microglia’s functions in the adult brain, emerging evidence has helped identify a role in brain development during early post-natal phases. Microglia sculpt neuronal connections by pruning synapses through phagocytosis, modifying their cytoskeleton to allow engulfment of complement-tagged synapses and complete their elimination [11]. Moreover, microglia promote oligodendrocyte precursor cell (OPC) differentiation and myelin regeneration by releasing extracellular vesicles (EVs) that carry pro-myelinating lipid messengers, such as the endocannabinoids anandamide (AEA) and 2-arachidonoylglycerol (2-AG) [12].

Microglia, through their constant surveillance of the brain parenchyma, their role in development, and their responsiveness to environmental cues, serve as critical intermediaries linking immune activity to neurodevelopmental disorders [13]. Immune defects, especially if already present during early brain development, can adversely affect neuronal development and function, clinically manifesting later in life. In particular, immune dysfunction that alters microglial activity may disrupt neural circuitry and compromise cognitive processes [14]. Moreover, increasing evidence indicates that elements of neuroinflammation are present in a significant proportion of inborn errors of immunity [15].

The Wiskott-Aldrich Syndrome (WAS) is a rare, recessive, X-linked primary immunodeficiency with an incidence of one case per 100,000 live male births. Patients are typically diagnosed within the first five years of life, although recent studies have reported adult-onset cases in women with a median diagnosis age of 47 years [16, 17]. A triad of clinical manifestations characterizes the syndrome: eczema, thrombocytopenia (low platelet count), and immunodeficiency, which leads to recurrent infections [18, 19].

WAS is caused by mutations in the WAS gene, located on the X chromosome (Xp11.22-p11.23), which encodes the Wiskott-Aldrich Syndrome protein (WASp). WASp is a 502-amino-acid cytoplasmic protein expressed exclusively in hematopoietic cells and plays a crucial role in transducing signals from the cell surface to actin polymerization through the actin-related protein (Arp)2/3 complex [20]. Through this pathway, WASp regulates various cellular functions, including phagocytosis, cytoskeletal remodeling, and cell motility. Studies have shown that peripheral immune cells from WAS patients exhibit defective migration, while macrophages display impaired phagocytosis due to failure in forming phagocytic cups, along with defective adhesion and chemotaxis caused by the absence of podosomes [21, 22].

WAS manifests a large range of clinical symptoms and the clinical spectrum can vary from mild, such as isolated thrombocytopenia, to severe complications that can be life-threatening [23, 24]. Genotype-phenotype correlations have been established in patients carrying hemizygous variants in WAS expressing either no WASp, reduced amounts or functionally-defective protein [25–27]. Vallée et al. [25] identified a group of WAS genetic variants, called class I, as predictive biomarkers for a less severe disease phenotype. Class I missense mutations are mostly localized in exons 1 and 2 and result in a reduction of WASp expression. On the contrary, class II mutations are linked to severe, life-threatening disease phenotypes.

Neurological symptoms may arise, like acute brain bleeding (causing 16% of total mortality in WAS patients) and inflammation [25], or decreased psychosocial health, emotional and cognitive functioning [28]. These effects may involve microglia, which is the only cell type in the brain that expresses a high level of WASp. Of note, microglia responses were hampered in Was−/− mice subjected to neuroinflammation models [29].

However, the mechanisms underlying WAS-related brain pathology remain poorly understood. To address this gap, we investigated the role of WASp in microglial cytoskeletal organization and phagocytic function and examined the behavioral consequences of WASp loss linked to microglial dysfunction.

In the current study, we demonstrated that WASp is a critical regulator of microglial phagocytosis, cytoskeletal integrity and cell movements of human microglia derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs). To do this, we analyzed the ability of microglia to phagocytize fluorescent bioparticles in the presence or not of WASp pharmacological inhibitors or following genetic inactivation of the WAS gene. Furthermore, we used in vivo inhibition of WASp in zebrafish embryos to analyze their sensorimotor functions and correct brain/body development.

Methods

In vitro

Human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs)

We used two human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) lines in this study. One was purchased from Gibco™ (Life Technologies, CA, US, Lot V2.0) under the approval of the Johns Hopkins University Institutional Stem Cell Research Oversight Committee for their collection and use; the other one (NYSCF, ID 7889SA) was obtained from the repository of New York Stem Cell Foundation (Inc. d/b/a NYSCF Research Institute, 619 West 54th Street New York, NY 10019), with all ethical approvals for the collection of these samples from human donors being under the authority of the foundation. After being thawed, the two hiPSCs lines were cultured under feeder-free conditions in a serum-free culture medium (mTeSR™ Plus, STEMCELL Technologies, Cat. No. 100–0276) supplemented with ROCK inhibitor (StemMACSY27632, Milteny Biotec s.r.l, Cat. No120-193-922), at a concentration of 1 µM, in Matrigel® (Corning 356231, SIAL s.r.l) coated 6-well plates. Cells were passaged every 3–5 days when they reached 70%–80% confluence with the use of ReLeSR™ (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat. No. 05872). All cells were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Differentiation of microglia

STEMCELL Technologies STEMdiff™ kits and manufacturer’s protocol were used to differentiate microglia from both the Gibco™ hiPSCs line and the NYSCF hiPSCs line. On day − 1, hiPSCs at a confluence of 70%-80% were detached using ReLeSR™. Multiple plating densities were tested: 100, 140 and/or 190 aggregates/well were plated onto Matrigel®-coated 6-well plate, containing 2 ml of mTeSR™ Plus with ROCK inhibitor. On day 0, mTeSR™ Plus medium was replaced with 2 ml of Medium A (45 ml HPC basal media + 225 µl supplement A, STEMdiff™ Hematopoietic Kit-1 Medium A, STEMCELL Technologies, Cat. No. 05310) to start hematopoietic progenitor (HPC) differentiation. On day 2, half medium was replaced with fresh medium A and on day 3, medium A was completely replaced with 2 ml of medium B (75 ml STEMdiff HPC basal media + 375 µl supplement B, STEMdiff™ Hematopoietic Kit-1 Medium B, STEMCELL Technologies, Cat. No. 05310). Medium B was changed every other day until day 11. On day 12, non-adherent cells released by HPC were collected, and 1 × 105 − 2 × 105 cells/well were seeded into a Matrigel®-coated 6-well plate with 2 ml of STEMdiff™ Microglia Differentiation Medium (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat. No. 100 − 0019). Cells were fed every other day by adding 1 ml of STEMdiff™ Microglia Differentiation Medium. On day 24, cells were collected in tubesand centrifuged at 300 x g for 5 minutes. Supernatant was removed and pellet resuspended in 1 ml of fresh medium. Cells were plated from every tubes respectively to their original well of the 6-well plate containing 1 ml fresh STEMdiff™ Microglia Differentiation Medium. 1 ml of medium was added every other day until day 36. On that day, cells were collected from each well into tubes and centrifuged at 300 x g for 5 minutes. Supernatant was removed until 1 ml remained up to the cells, which were plated from every tube respectively to a well of a new Matrigel®-coated 6-well plate with 1 ml fresh STEMdiff™ Microglia Maturation Medium (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat. No. 100 − 0020). 1 ml of medium was added every other day. Microglia are terminally differentiated after 4–10 days in STEMdiff™ Microglia Maturation Medium. After 10 days in maturation medium, there may be an increase in cell death. The fully differentiated cells are referred to as hiPSC-derived microglia (iMicro). Cells were maintained at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Differentiation of astrocytes and neurons

Neural progenitor cells (NPCs) were obtained from Gibco™ hiPSC. Cells were detached using 1 ml/well of Versene (Life Technologies Europe, Cat. No. 15040066) for 2 minutes at room temperature (RT) and seeded onto Matrigel®-coated 6-well plate in STEMdiff™ SMADi Neural Induction Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat. No. 08582) plus 5 µM ROCK Inhibitor. Cells were passaged every 6–9 days and after 21 days the medium was changed to STEMdiff™ Neural Progenitor medium (NPM) (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat. No. 05883). The first step in obtaining astrocyte-neuron co-cultures was the differentiation of astrocytes from NPCs, Subsequently, new NPCs were plated over the formed astrocytic layer and differentiated into neurons. To achieve this, NPCs at confluence were detached with StemPro™ Accutase™ Cell Dissociation Reagent (Life Technologies, Cat. No. A1110501), counted and seeded at a density of 15,000–20,000 cells/cm2 onto Matrigel®-coated 6-well plate with 1 ml of NPM with 1 µM ROCK Inhibitor. The day after 1 ml of Astrocytes Medium (ScienCell Research Laboratories, Cat. No. 1801-sc) was added to each well and, from day 3 and until day 25, half media changes were carried out every 2–3 days to allow proliferation and differentiation of cells into astrocytes. At day 25 of differentiation, astrocytes were detached with StemPro™ Accutase™ Cell Dissociation Reagent and seeded at a density of 15,000–20,000 cells/cm2 onto Matrigel®-coated 24-well plate with coverslip glasses. Astrocytes were sustained by changing half media for 5–7 days. After these days, astrocytes formed a layer; on top of which NPCs were seeded for neuronal differentiation. To achieve this, NPCs were detached and seeded at a density of 10,000 cells/cm2 (ratio 1:1 with astrocytes). Neuronal differentiation was achieved after 30 days, by changing half medium twice a week, with BrainPhys™ Neuronal Medium N2-A & SM1 Kit (STEMCELL Technologies, Cat. No. 05793) supplemented with 200 nM of Ascorbic Acid (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. A4544), 1 mM of Dibutyryl-cAMP (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. D0627), 20 ng/mL of Human Recombinant BDNF (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat. No. 130-138-877) and 20 ng/mL of Human Recombinant GDNF (Miltenyi Biotec, Cat. No. 130-129-542).

Generation of astrocyte-neuron-microglia co-cultures

To generate astrocyte-neuron-microglia co-cultures, microglia at the end of differentiation (day 36) were collected in tubes, centrifuged and counted. Then, cells were plated, at a density of 10,000 cells/well (ratio 1:2 microglia-neurons), over differentiating astrocyte-neuron co-cultures that reached the 22th-25th days of neuronal differentiation in 300 µL of mixed microglia/neuron differentiation medium at ratio 1:1. Microglia cells were let interact with astrocyte-neuron co-cultures for at least 2 days before media change. At 3 days from seeding, media was changed with fresh BrainPhys™ Neuronal Medium N2-A & SM1 medium and the 30 days of neuronal differentiation were reached. Experiments were performed after 5–8 days from microglia seeding, avoiding its expected cell death.

Cell activation and inhibition of WASp

iMicro was collected and seeded at a density of 90,000 cells/well into 8-well Matrigel®-coated IBIDI (µ-Slide 8 Well, IBIDI, Cat.No: 80826) for time-lapse analysis. Cells were stimulated with two different inflammatory stimuli: 1 µg/ml (stock 1 mg/ml) of lypopolysaccharide (LPS) derived from E. Coli (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat.No: L2630) or 10 ng/ml of TNF-α (Biolegend, Cat.No 570104) plus IL-1β (Peprotech, Cat-No: 200-01B) for 18–24 h (h). Then, iMicro were treated with two WASp functional inhibitors: Wiskostatin (MERCK, Cat.No: E2270), at a final concentration of 5 µM (stock 5 mg/ml), and CK-666 (Merck Life Science S.r.l, Cat.No: 442633-00-3) at a concentration of 100 µM (stock 10 mM). Both the inhibitors were diluted in DMSO and used for one hour. Co-cultures were treated with Wiskostatin (5 µM), CK-666 (100 µM) or vehicle (1% DMSO) 1 h per day for 5 consecutive days.

Phagocytosis assay by confocal time-lapse microscopy

Confocal microscopy was done on a Nikon A1 confocal scan with a 40 × 0.75 numerical aperture (NA) objective lens, managed by NIS-elements software. Before starting the phagocytosis assay, cells were activated and treated with WASp inhibitors as described previously. Active phagocytosis was revealed by time-lapse confocal imaging of pHrodo™ E. Coli-conjugated nanoparticles (P35361, ThermoFisher). iMicro was fluorescently labelled by the vital cell tracer CFSE (Vybrant™ CFDA SE Cell Tracer Kit, Invitrogen, Cat.No: V12883) diluted 1:1000 in PBS for 30 min and then, cells were imaged baseline for 15 min, with a time interval of every 5 min, with excitations λ 488 nm (for cell tracer) and λ 540 nm (for pHrodo’s channel background noise). After media change with 100 µl of FluoroBrite medium (Life Technologies Italia FluoroBrite™ DMEMGreen, Cat.No: A1896701), pHrodo nanoparticles were added to the medium and iMicro was imaged for 3 h, time interval 5 min.

Cell fixation

iMicro and co-cultures were fixed with 4% PFA at RT for 20 min and 30 min respectively. After 2 PBS washes cells were stored in PBS at 4 °C until using.

qPCR SYBR green

Total RNA was extracted from HPC and iMicro collected during the differentiation protocol. RNA was extracted using the PureLink RNA miniKit (Life Technologies) according to manufacturer’s instructions. Samples of total RNA (130 ng) were treated with DNAse (Applied Biosystems) and reverse-transcribed with random hexamer primers using Multi-Scribe Reverse Transcriptase (Life Technologies). Real-time RT-PCR was done using Power SYBR Green according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Applied Biosystems). Actin and GADPH were used as housekeeping genes. Relative gene expression was determined by the ΔΔCt method. Data are expressed as the log2 fold-change difference over the HPC.

Primer design

To characterize iMicro several transcriptomic markers were selected for characterization, some were selected for expected characteristics and others to distinguish from important brain resident cells of similar lineage. We analyzed gene expression of CD68, HEXB, P2RY12, SALL1, WAS, and WASL. Primers were designed using a variety of freely available online tools. We obtained the mRNA sequence of the gene of interest via the GenBank ® nucleotide search engine available through the NCBI website. The primer design tool employed was Primer3, and the mRNA sequence was copied and put into the primer design tool. We identified exon-to-exon boundaries to have the final amplicons including this region thus improving primers’ selectivity for mRNA over total RNA. The following values were used to ensure a quality primer was designed, primer size min: 18 max: 27, melting temperature min: 59 max: 61, amplicon size: 80–110. Primers could then be generated and copied into an existing document to be tested for quality. To achieve this the NCBI Primer-Blast tool was used. Product size was set to be a maximum size of 500 and a minimum size of 70, the database used was Refseq RNA (refseq_rna) and species selected as required, in this case Homo Sapiens. Results generated then informed us if primers were cross reactive for other genes and other important values such as self-complementarity.

Generation of WAS KO hiPSC line with CRISPR-Cas9

The knock-out (KO) hiPSC line was obtained as a byproduct of the generation of mutated WAS gene. Briefly, we adapted the protocol described by Patel et al. [30] to deliver CRISPR–Cas9 and introduce the c.256 C > T (p.Arg86Cys) mutation – often observed in WAS subjects [25] - into the WAS gene in FA0000010 hiPSCs derived from a healthy male donor [31] by Sendai virus–mediated reprogramming. Three candidate sgRNAs were designed based on predicted high on-target activity and low off-target risk. Target regions were validated by PCR and Sanger sequencing, identifying FP1-RP1 as the optimal primer set. sgRNA efficiency was assessed by electroporating Cas9/sgRNA ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes and analyzing editing outcomes using ICE analysis, an online tool from Synthego; sgRNA2 showed the highest indel and knockout scores and was selected.

A ssODN donor template containing the R86C mutation and a silent PAM-disrupting substitution was used for knock-in. hiPSCs were electroporated (Lonza 4D-Nucleofector) with Cas9 (IDT), sgRNA2 (Synthego), and ssODN (IDT). ICE analysis of pooled cells showed 84% indels and 77% knock-in efficiency. Edited cells were clonally expanded and genotyped; clones with frameshift mutations resulting in WAS KO hiPSCs were selected. Lines were confirmed mycoplasma-free and karyotypically normal. Stemness was assessed by immunofluorescence staining for the markers SOX2 (rabbit, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:100), OCT4 (mouse, Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:50) and NANOG (rabbit, Cell Signaling Technology; 1:400). Confocal images were analyzed using Imaris software. Nuclei were segmented by generating isosurfaces with a maximum diameter of 8 μm and a surface grain of 2 μm. Cell segmentation was refined using a region-growing algorithm with an estimated diameter of 9 μm. For each cell, the mean fluorescence intensity of each marker within the nucleus was quantified. Finally, the multi-lineage differentiation potential of the edited hiPSCs was evaluated by immunostaining for germ layer–specific markers, including OTX2 (goat, R&D Systems; 1:20) as an ectoderm marker, Brachyury (goat, R&D Systems; 1:20) as mesodermal markers, and SOX17 (R&D Systems; 1:10) as an endodermal marker.

Digital image analysis

The distribution of F-actin and WASp positive pixels was analyzed by GLCM, as previously published [32]. Briefly, images were converted to 8-bit for the gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) analysis. After background normalization, a region of interest (ROI) including the cell was drawn and GLCM done with the ImageJ plugin ‘GLCM analysis’ (using step size = 1 pixel, step direction = 0 degrees).

To analyze cell motility, we used the function “Tracking” of Imaris. After selecting an average cell diameter of 1.5 microns, we calculated the path travelled by the cells during the first hour of the phagocytosis assay. The motility of the cells was expressed as total covered distance (displacement, µm) and average speed (nm·sec− 1).

Fluorescence signals of astrocytes (GFAP), synapses (synaptophysin) and microglia (Iba1) in the co-cultures were quantified using the Imaris software. We built the isosurfaces on each signal, setting 1 μm (for GFAP and Iba1) or 0.6 μm (synaptophyisin) as the max diameter of the object fitting the structure of interest. This allowed us to calculate the number of synapses and the volume occupied by GFAP or Iba1 + structures. To capture representative images, we took snapshots of the individual channels and their respective isosurfaces.

The Sholl analysis was done using the dedicated ImageJ plugin. Briefly, large view microphotographs sized 2370 × 1800 × 10 μm were obtained by widefield fluorescence microscopy, using a CellR by Olympus, detecting the signal for nuclei (DAPI) and neurons (MAP2). After image segmentation using the Otsu automatic thresholding, 25 MAP2 + cells were randomly selected over the entire large image and their soma centroid identified. The Sholl analysis was done for each selected neuron in the range of 20–200 μm from the centroid, with a 3 μm step between the concentric fields.

The microphotographs of phalloidin and WASp stained iMicro were obtained using an A1 Nikon confocal microscope, managed by NIS Elements software, with a 100x objective lens, NA 1.49, refractive index in oil 1.515, voxel size 124 × 124 × 360 nm, image size 1024 × 1024 × 29 pixels. The microphotographs of PSD95 and synaptophysin stained synapses were obtained using a FV5000 confocal microscope by Evident, managed by Cell Sense software, with a 60x objective lens, NA 1.42, refractive index in oil 1.518, voxel size 51.79 × 51.79 × 400 nm, image size 2048 × 2048 × 7 pixels, 2x zoomed. We used deconwolf [33], using 20 iterations per channel to deconvolve the confocal microphotographs. The deconvolved images were then processed with ImageJ to obtain merged microphotographs and to plot the signal histograms.

In vivo

Zebrafish lines

Zebrafish (Danio rerio) were maintained according to international (EU Directive 2010/63/EU) and national guidelines (Italian decree No 26 of the 4th of March 2014) at University of Milan, Department of Biosciences. Tg(-3.1:neurog1:GFP) [34] and Tg(mpeg1:Gal4;UAS:lyn-tagRFP-T) [35, 36] zebrafish strains were used. Embryos were staged as described in Kimmel et al. [37] and raised in fish water (0.01% Instant Ocean, 0.01% sodium bicarbonate, 0.019% calcium sulphate, 0.1% Methylene Blue) at 28 °C in Petri dishes, according to established techniques. Embryonic stages are expressed in hours post fertilization (hpf) and days post fertilization (dpf). When needed, 0.003% 1-phenyl-2-thiourea (PTU, Sigma-Aldrich) was added to fish water to prevent pigmentation. For sample storage before imaging, embryos were fixed in 4% PFA in PBS overnight at 4 °C and then they were dehydrated by methanol scale.

Pharmacological treatment of zebrafish embryos

Zebrafish embryos at 2 dpf were manually dechorionated and treated with 50 µM CK-666 for 1 overnight, 20 embryos/well in 6-well plates. CK-666 was dissolved in DMSO and added to fish water. DMSO was used as a negative control. After overnight treatment, water containing drug was replaced with fresh water.

Heart rate evaluation

The heartbeat frequency was measured in zebrafish embryos at 2 and 3 dpf by optical visualization. Heartbeats were manually counted, and the number of beats/15s was multiplied by 4 to obtain the beats per minute (bpm). To minimize the ambient temperature effect on the zebrafish embryo heart rate, 5 embryos per time were taken from the 28° C incubator to be assessed for their heartbeat.

Touch-evoked response assay

Touch-evoked response assay was performed at 3 dpf. Embryos were placed individually in 35 mm diameter Petri dishes and the tail of each embryo was touched with a micro-loader tip to elicit a response. The touch was repeated until the embryo reacted or until a maximum of 10 touches. A reaction after 1 or 2 touches was considered an efficient response, while a reaction after 3 or 4 touches indicated mild response impairment. A reaction after more than 4 touches or no reaction was considered severe response impairment.

TUNEL staining

TUNEL staining was performed on 5 randomly selected 3 dpf Tg(-3.1neurog1:GFP;mpeg1:Gal4;UAS:lyn-tagRFP-T) zebrafish embryos per experimental group. Fixed embryos were rehydrated and then TUNEL staining was carried out using the Click-iT™ Plus TUNEL Assay Alexa Fluor 647 (ThermoFisher Scientific), according to manufacturer instructions. Zebrafish embryos were then mounted in 1% low-melting agarose in PBS. Images sized 320 × 320 × 80 μm, 0.31 μm/pixel, step size 0.89 μm, were acquired using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Evident FV5000), using a 20x objective lens at 2x zooming and with excitation wavelength of 488 nm for GFP, 561 nm for RFP and 640 nm for TUNEL. TUNEL-positive apoptotic bodies were quantified using the spot detection function in Imaris software (Bitplane), with an expected object diameter of 10 μm. Spots located inside of GFP-derived isosurfaces (generated with a fixed smoothing of 2 μm and an object size of 20 μm) were included for analysis. Additionally, RFP isosurfaces were generated using a smoothing factor of 1 μm and an object size of 5 μm. Engulfment of TUNEL-positive material by RFP+ microglia/macrophages was assessed by calculating the ratio of TUNEL integrated density within RFP isosurfaces relative to that within GFP isosurfaces.

Quantification of microglia

Digital fluorescence images of embryos at 3 and 5 dpf were captured with an Olympus SZX16 stereomicroscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an ORCA-spark digital camera (Hamamatsu Photonics) with HCImage Imaging software (Hamamatsu Photonics), using an excitation wavelength of 540 nm. Embryos were anesthetized with 0.016% tricaine (Ethyl 3-aminobenzoate methanesulfonate salt, Sigma-Aldrich) before proceeding with experimental protocols. The number of RFP+ cells was manually counted in the region corresponding to the zebrafish optic tectum. The latter region was visually identified and traced based on the atlas of the zebrafish midbrain (https://zebrafishucl.org/midbrain).

Time lapse imaging of zebrafish

Tg(-3.1neurog1:GFP;mpeg1:Gal4;UAS:lyn-tagRFP-T) zebrafish at 2 dpf were mounted in 1% low-melting agarose in 0.016% tricaine in fish water and incubated at 28 °C o/n during time lapse confocal imaging for evaluating RFP+ cell motility. Microphotographs sized 400 × 442 × 166 μm (pixel size was 0.65 μm) including the head of the zebrafish were acquired for 16 h, with 15 min time frame intervals by using an ECLIPSE Ti2-E with Yokogawa W1-SoRa spinning disk confocal microscope (Nikon). Time lapse images were processed by Imaris (Bitplane) to detect RFP+ cells by the spot function, setting estimated diameter 20 μm, max distance 100 μm, max gaps 4 and min track duration 2000 s. Three independent experiments were performed with a total of three zebrafish embryos derived from different clutches per experimental group. Statistical analysis

The data are expressed as mean ± SD from at least two independent experiments. For statistical analysis, we used the GraphPad Prism software (San Diego, CA, version 11). We performed unpaired t-test or Mann-Whitney test and one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s or One-way Welch-corrected ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s T3 test, or two-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's test, to compare the differences between two or more groups. The Welch correction was applied after verifying through the Bartlett test that the variances were not homogeneous. The details for the specific test used are indicated in each figure legend.

hiPSC-derived microglia (iMicro) expressed specific microglia markers and the WAS gene

iMicro were generated from two hiPSC lines and were analyzed for several key genes expressed by microglia to verify their differentiation at transcriptomic level. We evaluated the expression of microglia-specific genes using RT-qPCR, comparing iMicro cells with their hematopoietic progenitors. In particular, we analyzed HEXB [38], SALL1 [39], CD68 [40] and P2RY12 [41], as shown in Fig. 1. We observed that the P2RY12 gene was expressed at significantly higher levels in iMicro compared to progenitors. HEXB was instead detected in both iMicro and progenitors at similar levels; however, HEXB transcript levels were significantly higher in iMicro when compared to macrophage-like cells differentiated from the same hiPSCs (Supplementary Fig. S1), supporting the acquisition of a microglia-specific phenotype. Unexpectedly, SALL1 was downregulated in iMicro, suggesting either that our differentiation protocol failed to maintain this microglial marker — as previously reported for certain methodologies [42] — or that the iMicro have adopted a more activated state. In line with the latter hypothesis, transcripts coding for CD68, a lysosomal-associated membrane protein that indicates phagocytic function, were significantly enriched in iMicro compared to their progenitors.

iMicro showed active phagocytosis that was reduced by treatment with WASp inhibitors

To determine the ability of iMicro cells to do phagocytosis, we performed a time-lapse confocal microscopy phagocytic assay on live iMicro exposed to fluorescent pHrodo™ BioParticles™ (500 nm in diameter). These nanoparticles emit fluorescence in a pH-dependent manner; specifically, the acidification of the nanoparticles, upon their engulfment in cell lysosomes, induces dye signal emission. The measurement of the variation in fluorescence intensity of pHrodo™ in cells thus represents an index of their phagocytic function. To drive iMicro cells towards a pro-inflammatory state and activate their phagocytic function, we evaluated two methods of activation: 18 h of treatment with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 1 µg/ml) vs. a two-factor cocktail with TNF-α and IL-1β (10 ng/ml each), starting the day before the assay. We analyzed the change in fluorescence intensity over time relative to the baseline, defined as the confocal acquisition obtained before exposing the cells to pHrodo™. The traces indicate the value of the fluorescence change (ΔF/F0), measured every 5 min over the total 200 min of the assay, with the confidence interval (standard deviation) for at least 3 visual fields per well. iMicro activated with TNF-α and IL-1β showed a higher phagocytosis compared to the inactive controls, whereas the group activated with LPS did not, with phagocytosis levels comparable to the non-activated controls (Fig. 2A). After validating the two-factor cocktail as the best inflammatory hint to activate iMicro cells, we investigated the effect of WASp pharmacological inhibition on their phagocytic function. Thus, we introduced two experimental groups of cells activated by the two factors and treated for 1 h with either Wiskostatin 5 µM or with CK-666 100 µM (Fig. 2B). The two compounds were selected because they inhibit WASp signaling via distinct mechanisms. Wiskostatin targets the regulatory GTPase-binding domain of WASp, stabilizing it in an autoinhibited conformation [43]. In contrast, CK-666 acts downstream by preventing formation of the short-pitch Arp2–Arp3 dimer, a WASp-promoted intermediate that offers a template for nucleation of a new actin filament [44]. The use of two inhibitors acting at different levels of the WASp signaling cascade would help strengthening the robustness of our findings. Additionally, the experiments were performed on iMicro derived from Gibco™ hiPSCs and replicated also on iMicro derived from NYSCF hiPSCs (Fig. 2C for pooled analysis, and Supplementary Fig. S2 for each cell line). In both cases, we confirmed that iMicro activated with TNF-α and IL-1β had a higher phagocytosis score than the non-activated control cells, whereas treatment with inhibitors resulted in a significant reduction in phagocytosis, with CK-666 reducing phagocytosis to levels even lower than untreated and non activated control cells. We calculated the Area Under the Curve (AUC) for each condition (Fig. 2D), showing that Wiskostatin (84.87 ± 21.65 of AUC ± SD) and, to a bigger extent, CK-666 (25.56 ± 6.63) suppressed iMicro phagocytic function. To rule out potential bias in pHrodo engulfment measurements arising from inhibitor-induced differences in cell size or shape, we assessed iMicro morphology at baseline (prior to pHrodo exposure). Comparable size (area) and shape (circularity) were observed across all experimental groups (Supplementary Fig. S3).

When activated, iMicro showed increasing pHrodo fluorescence within the first ~ 75 min, indicating engulfment and fusion with lysosomes, whereas fluorescence quenching at longer time points possibly indicates probe removal from the lysosomes by either recycling or exocytosis. We tracked the iMicro cells during the first hour of exposure to pHrodo™ BioParticles™ to calculate their motility under the different experimental conditions. In particular, we analyzed the displacement, i.e. the distance between the starting and ending point of the cell’s path expressed in µm, and the mean speed expressed as nm per second (Fig. 2E). We observed that microglia activated by TNF-α and IL-1β increased their average speed (37.9 ± 58.7 of nm· sec− 1 ±SD) compared to non activated cells (11.3 ± 26). Wiskostatin caused iMicro stalling, i.e. their displacement and average speed had similar values as non activated cells, while CK-666 did not affect iMicro motility upon activation, with the cells having an average speed of 57.7 ± 86.7 nm · sec− 1 (Fig. 2F). These observations suggest that the WASp inhibitors affected iMicro phagocytosis to different extents.

WASp inhibitors altered cells’ cytoskeleton architecture and activation

In order to investigate on iMicro cytoskeletal organization, we fluorescently labelled the cells fixed at the end of the phagocytic assay (i.e. 3 h after pHrodo™) for WASp and phalloidin (Fig. 3A), a toxin isolated from the fungus Amanita phalloides, capable of binding F-actin and allowing cytoskeletal filament visualization. In particular, we observed that control activated iMicro (CTRL) showed a highly ordered organization of actin filaments (in purple), mainly localized along the cell border (cortical actin fibers), possibly contributing to membrane ruffles and filopodia sprouting, as previously reported from microglia [45](white arrows in Fig. 3A). Microglia treated with the inhibitors Wiskostatin and CK-666 had F-actin filaments that lost their peripheral localization and did not show emerging filopodia/membrane ruffles. WASp partially colocalized with the phalloidin signal along the cell border but was not detected in filopodia/membrane ruffles regions (Fig. 3A).

We further analyzed the cytoskeletal organization in all experimental groups (i.e. with/without Wiskostatin or CK-666) by quantifying phalloidin and WASp fluorescent pixel distribution in the microphotographs (Fig. 3B). Non activated iMicro presented a homogeneous distribution of phalloidin and WASp in the cytoplasm; when iMicro were activated with the two-factor cocktail, WASp clustered at the border of the cells, suggesting a possible contribution to the formation of membrane ruffles/filopodia in active phagocytic cells. Activated iMicro treated with Wiskostatin or CK-666 showed a rearrangement of phalloidin and WASp signals, losing WASp enrichment at cell periphery (Fig. 3B). We then used a Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) analysis [32] (Fig. 3C) to measure the distribution of both phalloidin and WASp positive pixels. The GLCM is a way to perform texture analysis, a measure that quantifies spatial patterns and intensity variations in images, yielding readouts like entropy (measures randomness) and angular second moment (uniformity). In this context, elevated entropy values reflect the localization of the fluorescent signal at membrane ruffles. The clustering of positive pixels within these structures, surrounded by background pixels, increases spatial disorder (i.e., entropy), as positive pixels are more likely to be adjacent to negative neighbours. For both the phallodin and WASp markers, we observed a significantly higher level of pixel entropy in the activated iMicro (phalloidin entropy 2.13 ± 0.90; WASp: 2.0 ± 0.48) compared to non activated iMicro (0.74 ± 0.66; 0.65 ± 0.65) and to activated iMicro treated with CK-666 (0.77 ± 0.31; 0.57 ± 0.28), whereas the Wiskostatin treatment induced a trend for reduced entropy, albeit not significant.

We also measured the Angular Second Moment (ASM), a texture feature that measures the pixel homogeneity of an image, hence with opposite values than entropy. iMicro activated with the two-factor cocktail showed significantly lower ASM values compared to non activated cells for both WASp and phalloidin measurements, which were reverted completely by CK-666 treatment and only partially, not significantly, by Wiskostatin (Fig. 3C).

Co-cultures of microglia, neurons and astrocytes, derived from hiPSCs, showed a reduction in the number of synaptic markers and neuronal dendrites after treatment with CK-666

Microglia are involved in the remodelling of neuronal networks during development through multiple mechanisms, including synaptic pruning, clearance of neuronal debris, and modulation of neuronal processes, as well as through interactions with other cell types such as astrocytes [46]. These functions rely on intact cytoskeletal dynamics and phagocytosis. We therefore investigated the functionality of microglia within co-cultures of neurons and astrocytes derived from hiPSCs. These co-cultures could develop a full neuronal network, showing the presence of the pre and post synaptic markers synaptophysin and PSD95, respectively (Fig. 4A). After image deconvolution to enhance resolution, the two markers appeared closely juxtaposed (Fig. 4B), consistent with their expected localization within a synaptosome.

We then derived WAS KO iMicro and subjected them to activation using the two-factor protocol (18-hour exposure to 10 ng/mL TNF-α and IL-1β). Activated and non-activated iMicro were subsequently exposed to E. coli-derived nanoparticles to induce phagocytosis. After 3 h, cell morphology was analyzed by confocal microscopy. Wild-type (WT) iMicro treated with TNF-α and IL-1β displayed a reactive phenotype compared to non-activated controls (Fig. 6D), characterized by increased cell size (4.9-fold increase in area, 3.1-fold in perimeter, and 2.8-fold in caliper; Fig. 6D shows an example of a WT treated iMicro with exceptional increase in cell area) and greater morphological complexity (0.54-fold decrease in circularity, Fig. 6E). In addition, activated WT iMicro displayed vacuolized cytoplasm, suggesting phagocytosis (Fig. 6D). In contrast, WAS KO iMicro did not exhibit these activation-associated morphological changes upon cytokine treatment, remaining small and rounded, similar to non-activated cells (Fig. 6D, E).

Zebrafish embryos exposed to CK-666 showed increased accumulation of apoptotic bodies in the brain compared to vehicle controls and delayed responses in the touch-evoked behavioural test

We next moved to an in vivo zebrafish embryo model to investigate the effects of WASp inhibition on neurodevelopmental functions of brain myeloid cells, including microglia, which largely rely on their phagocytic function. Supporting the use of zebrafish as a relevant model to investigate WASp involvement in microglial function, our analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from the Daniocell zebrafish database [48] revealed enrichment of was gene expression (both wasa and wasb paralogs) within the mpeg1+ population, corresponding to myeloid cells (Supplementary Fig. S4). In contrast, wasl gene paralogs, which encode neural-WASp (N-WASp), are not expressed in these cells. The availability of the Tg(-3.1neurog1:GFP;mpeg1:Gal4;UAS: lyn-tagRFP-T) transgenic zebrafish line expressing a green fluorescent protein (GFP) under the -3.1 neurog1 promoter together with a red fluorescent protein (RFP) reporter under the mpeg1 promoter enabled us to simultaneously visualize neurons and myeloid cells, and characterize their behaviour, including brain homing, phagocytic activity, and migratory dynamics following modulation of WASp activity by CK-666. To do so, at 2 dpf, embryos were randomized into two groups and treated with CK-666 inhibitor (50 µM) or vehicle (0.5% DMSO) overnight. At 3 dpf we performed touch-evoked response test [49–51] (Fig. 7A). Before starting the behavioural test, we measured the heart rate of the embryos, to include in our next experiments only those having a rate ≥ 120 bpm, index of healthy zebrafish [52] (Fig. 7B). These inclusion criteria were based on the observation that the target of the CK-666 inhibitor, the Arp2/3 protein complex, was also expressed in cardiac pericytes of the zebrafish embryos (See Supplementary Fig. S5).

All animals in the DMSO (vehicle treatment) group were included in the study, while 15 animals (14.7%) in the CK-666-treated group were excluded (Fig. 7B). As shown in Fig. 7C, touch-evoked response assay revealed that 67% of DMSO-treated embryos presented a prompt response to stimulus (reaction after 1–2 touches), 17% a mild impaired response (reaction after 3–4 touches) and 16% were not responsive (reaction after > 4 touches). As for the CK-666-treated embryos, 11% presented a mild impaired response, 78% were not responsive and only 11% responded normally to the stimulus, hence indicating a strong sensorimotor impairment in animals with inhibited WASp.

The development of numerous organs, including the brain, relies on programmed cell death as a key regulatory process. The neurotrophic theory proposes that neurons compete for a restricted supply of survival factors, and as a consequence, a significant proportion undergo cell death [53]. A key mechanism of neurodevelopment is the clearance of those apoptotic bodies in the brain through microglia-mediated phagocytosis [53]. In order to understand if WASp inhibition affected this mechanism, we performed TUNEL staining [54, 55] on 3 dpf Tg(-3.1neurog1:GFP;mpeg1:Gal4;UAS:lyn-tagRFP-T) zebrafish embryos to visualize apoptotic bodies following CK-666 treatment. Compared to controls, we observed a significant increase of apoptotic bodies (Fig. 7D) in the brains of embryos treated with CK-666. We quantified this by measuring cells positive for TUNEL within the GFP+ volume (Fig. 7E). A substantially higher number was observed in CK-666–treated zebrafish (151.4 ± 72.35, mean ± SD) compared to DMSO-treated controls (10.0 ± 5.96). In addition, RFP+ cells appeared to engulf apoptotic bodies in DMSO, but not in CK-666-treated zebrafish. In line with this observation, the fraction of TUNEL signal intensity within RFP+ cells relative to the total signal in the GFP+ volume was higher in DMSO-treated animals (0.051 ± 0.028, integrated density fraction ± SD) than in CK-666–treated fish (0.012 ± 0.004, Fig. 7F).

Zebrafish embryos treated with CK-666 showed reduced brain colonization by myeloid cells and an alteration of their motility compared to controls

Developmental apoptosis is a cue driving brain positioning of microglia, mainly observed in the zebrafish optic tectum within the first 6 dpf [53]. We therefore exploited the Tg(mpeg1:Gal4;UAS:lyn-tagRFP-T) zebrafish line to assess the number of myeloid cells in the optic tectum of CK-666-treated zebrafish embryos at 3 and 5 dpf (Fig. 8A-D). At 3 dpf, the number of RFP+ cells was smaller in the optic tectum of CK-666-treated (11.04 ± 4.14 of cell number ± SD) compared to DMSO-treated (14.23 ± 4.14) zebrafish embryos (Fig. 8A, B). A similar effect was observed at 5 dpf, when the number of RFP+ cells in the optic tectum was still reduced in CK-666-treated zebrafish (6.00 ± 2.89, mean ± SD) compared to DMSO-treated controls (8.64 ± 3.23; Fig. 8C, D). To investigate myeloid cell homing to the zebrafish optic tectum, we performed time-lapse confocal imaging using Tg(-3.1neurog1:GFP;mpeg1:Gal4;UAS:lyn-tagRFP-T) zebrafish embryos. Control embryos treated with DMSO exhibited intense cellular trafficking in this region. In contrast, zebrafish treated with CK-666 displayed altered migratory behaviour of RFP+ cells, with fewer cells entering or moving within the optic tectum (Fig. 8E). Quantitative analysis of myeloid cell motility over 16 h revealed that CK-666-treated zebrafish showed reduced displacement (80.98 ± 55.86 μm, mean ± SD; Fig. 8F) and lower migration speed (0.020 ± 0.012 μm·sec⁻¹; Fig. 8G) compared to DMSO-treated controls (102.7 ± 68.70 μm and 0.028 ± 0.001 μm·sec⁻¹, respectively). Overall, our data suggest that WASp functions in myeloid cells, including microglia, by promoting their trafficking and accumulation in the brain to help clearing apoptotic neurons and therefore physiological neurodevelopment.

Discussion

The present study identifies a role for the Wiskott-Aldrich Syndrome protein (WASp) as a regulator of microglial phagocytosis and cytoskeletal integrity. Building on WASp’s known role in peripheral myeloid cell maturation and cytoskeletal organization, our study provides new insights into its function in brain-resident myeloid cells. Moreover, our original observation that the inhibition of WASp affected the behavior and the clearance of brain apoptotic bodies of zebrafish embryos underlies its implication in microglial neurodevelopmental functions.

In peripheral myeloid cells, WASp transduces signals received from the cell surface to actin polymerization via the Arp2/3 complex, regulating essential cellular functions such as phagocytosis and cytoskeletal remodelling [20]. In order to understand whether this was the case for brain-resident myeloid cells, we exposed hiPSC-derived microglia (iMicro) to inhibitors preventing the activation of the Arp2/3 complex, i.e. Wiskostatin, or stabilizing the complex in its inactive form, i.e. CK-666. Both inhibitors reduced the engulfment of fluorescent E. Coli–conjugated nanoparticles by iMicro, supporting a role for WASp in microglial phagocytosis. This process relies on cytoskeletal rearrangement mediated by Arp2/3 complex activation, as well as on scavenger and opsonin-related receptors, including CD36, complement receptors (CR3), and Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4).

Immunofluorescence revealed that WASp co-localized with F-actin, particularly in the cell periphery of actively phagocytic iMicro, further indicating that the protein is directly involved in the structural changes of microglial cytoskeleton. Indeed, microglia redistribute F-actin filaments by localizing them to membrane ruffles, which underpin phagocytosis, reflecting a change in microglial behavior from extensive surveillance to localized, small-scale movements, implicated in active immune functions, like phagocytosis [45].

In order to quantify the different localization of F-actin filaments and of WASp across the experimental groups, including those with WASp inhibitors, we applied a texture analysis by GLCM, allowing the measure of pixel intensity distribution [32]. GLCM analysis of F-actin signal showed that activated iMicro had high fluorescent pixel entropy and low angular second moment (ASM), two parameters reflecting the spatial organization of the signal within selective structures. In fact, positive F-actin pixels were mainly present in the cell peripheral structures, e.g. the membrane ruffles, thus creating a strong contrast between neighbor pixels (positive signal vs. background). On the contrary, the treatment with inhibitors caused the loss of the peripheral localization of F-actin, which showed a more even distribution in the cytoplasm. This homogeneous distribution of F-actin in WASp-inhibited iMicro was reflected by a significant reduction in entropy and increase in ASM, accounting for a cytoskeletal architecture not supporting phagocytic functions. Applying the same analysis to the WASp localization signals yielded results similar to those obtained for F-actin, further supporting the close spatial association of WASp with the cytoskeleton.

Since phagocytosis requires cell motility, we next assessed whether WASp inhibition could affect this property of iMicro. After activation with an inflammatory hint, iMicro moved quicker and for longer distances than non activated cells. In active iMicro, Wiskostatin reduced both speed and displacement, while CK-666 did not change cell motility. This observation suggests that the two inhibitors dampened active iMicro phagocytosis through different mechanisms. We may hypothesize that Wiskostatin affected cell response to chemotactic stimuli, while CK-666 impaired the internalization process of the target nanoparticles.

Once we generated WAS KO iMicro, we observed that these cells did not acquire a reactive phenotype in response to cytokine stimulation. In particular, they did not develop the typical features associated with activation, such as increased cell size, ramified processes, or cytoplasmic vacuolization following induction of phagocytosis. These findings are consistent with a role for WASp in microglial responses to activation cues and suggest that it may contribute to both phagocytosis and associated morphological changes. Phagocytosis is the main process through which microglia contribute to brain development. Indeed, starting from fetal stages, microglia sculpt the forming neural network by synaptic pruning, a process that requires an active phagocytic function [11]. Defective microglia, and consequently impaired synaptic pruning, alter brain development, resulting in long-term behavioral consequences. This is supported by the observation that mice conditionally depleted early postnatally, but not at later stages, of SHIP1, a newly identified protein essential for proper microglia-mediated synapse remodeling, had synapse loss and developed cognitive deficits in adulthood [56].

We explored whether WASp inhibition could affect synapse formation in vitro by co-culturing iMicro with hiPSC-derived neurons and astrocytes. We observed that co-cultures treated with CK-666 exhibited a reduced number of synapses compared with controls, consistent with impaired neuronal wiring support by microglia following WASp inhibition. In line with this observation, neurons in co-cultures exposed to CK-666 displayed reduced dendrites, both in terms of their number and length. None of these effects were seen when Wiskostatin was used. The absence of a detectable effect of Wiskostatin may reflect ineffective inhibition achieved with the applied treatment regimen (1 h daily for 5 days).

Based on our in vitro findings, which demonstrate a role for WASp in the engulfment machinery of iMicro and in their ability to support synapse formation when co-cultured with neurons, we extended our investigation to an in vivo zebrafish embryo model. This allowed us to assess whether WASp is essential for neurodevelopment. Similar to mammals, zebrafish show enriched was gene expression in the immune cell subset of mpeg1+ microglia and macrophages, supporting their use as a model to study WASp function in these cells. However, zebrafish possess two paralogs, wasa and wasb, both expressed in mpeg1+ myeloid cells. Notably, only wasa—and not wasb—is enriched in spic+ metaphocytes (non-hematopoietic macrophage-like cells in the skin that are mpeg1+ but CSF1R-independent). This complexity limits straightforward genetic targeting for stable knockout models. Therefore, we employed the WASp inhibitor CK-666, which showed the strongest effect on synaptic remodeling in vitro, to investigate WASp function in zebrafish embryos.

We observed that zebrafish embryos exposed to CK-666 had impaired sensorimotor functions at 3 dpf, as indicated by their poor performance in the touch response test. The latter was accompanied, at the same time point, by the accumulation of apoptotic bodies in the brain. Developmental apoptosis is a physiological process in many organs, including the brain. As previously reported, the clearance of apoptotic bodies in the developing brain relies on phagocytic microglia, and represents a key process in the homing of tissue-resident macrophages [53]. In zebrafish embryos with inhibited WASp, apoptotic bodies accumulated in the brain. The reduced fraction of TUNEL signal detected within mpeg1+ cells is consistent with defective engulfment or clearance by myeloid cells, including microglia. However, the marked higher levels of total TUNEL signal following CK-666 treatment may also indicate increased apoptosis, rather than reduced clearance alone. Thus, increased apoptosis and impaired clearance might both contribute to the elevated TUNEL signal observed in embryos treated with the WASp inhibitor. Of note, following WASp inhibition, mpeg1+ cells also exhibited reduced homing in the optic tectum, the brain area with the highest microglial colonization rate during neurodevelopment [53]. Supporting the latter observation, CK-666 treatment altered mpeg1+ cell motility, resulting in reduced displacement and mean speed, along with decreased transit within the optic tectum. The observation that CK-666 affected myeloid cell motility in zebrafish, but did not impair iMicro motility, suggests model-specific differences. This discrepancy may be due to the distinct targets available in each system (i.e., E. Coli bioparticles for iMicro assays versus apoptotic bodies for zebrafish myeloid cells). The in vivo zebrafish model may therefore better reflect the physiological processes involved in neurodevelopment, providing an intact brain microenvironment shaping microglial identity, responsiveness and dynamics [57]. Our data thus suggest the implication of WASp in physiological neurodevelopment. At 5 dpf, zebrafish embryos treated with CK-666 showed altered body morphology, suggesting their impaired overall development (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Mutations in the WAS gene cause the Wiskott-Aldrich Syndrome (WAS), a X-linked primary immunodeficiency [16]. This syndrome’s phenotypical consequences are mostly reported for peripheral myeloid cells, which have impaired phagocytosis - due to failure in forming phagocytic cups - and defective adhesion and chemotaxis - caused by the absence of podosomes [21, 22]. Less is known on WAS consequences on brain-resident myeloid cells. A study of Bosticardo et al. [29] explored the role of WASp in a murine model of experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), which mimics multiple sclerosis. Namely, the authors reported that WASp-deficient mice had delayed development of EAE-induced autoimmunity in the CNS compared to WT littermates, which depended, among other mechanisms, to reduced microglia activation.

Our data expand the understanding of WASp’s role in microglial function by showing that this protein mediates phagocytosis — a key process for maintaining brain homeostasis and shaping neuronal wiring during neurodevelopment. Disruption of this pathway may help explain the neurological manifestations observed in WAS subjects [25, 28], the 20% of which have been reported to be immune-mediated [58]. For example, defective WASp could impair the ability of microglia to limit injury progression after cerebral hemorrhage or compromise cognitive functions if neuronal network support is insufficient.

In order to address WASp’s role, we used two pharmacological inhibitors of WASp, so to interfere with the protein functions, but not with its synthesis. This approach accounts for the many mutations identified in the WAS gene which determine a truncated or partially functional protein in WAS subjects classified as mild phenotypes (score 1–2, labeled as X-linked thrombocytopenia, XLT) [25, 59]. These patients typically have a longer life expectancy than those with complete WASp deletion (severe disease score 3–5) and may develop neurological symptoms that become apparent later in life. In addition, we tested iMicro derived from a hiPSC line which was depleted of WASp, observing their impaired ability to acquire a reactive morphology with vacuolized cytoplasm, typical of engulfing cells. We acknowledge that, presently, our experimental approach does not directly translate into diagnostic/therapeutic applications for the syndrome. Nevertheless, given the involvement of microglia in virtually all brain pathologies, our findings highlight a previously unrecognized pathway of brain-resident myeloid cell activation, which warrants further investigation across a range of neurological diseases.
