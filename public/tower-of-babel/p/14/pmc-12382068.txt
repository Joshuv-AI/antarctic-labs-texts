Weight-adjusted waist index and mortality in diabetic retinopathy: a NHANES 1999–2018 cohort study
Authors: Liang Pan, Xuedong Xu, Min Zhang, Chiping Sun, Wuping Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThe weight-adjusted waist index (WWI), an innovative indicator of central obesity, is still inadequately researched in relation to diabetic complications. This study sought to elucidate the relationship between WWI and all-cause as well as cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality in US patients with diabetic retinopathy (DR).MethodsWe performed a retrospective cohort research with 1247 people in the United States diagnosed with diabetic retinopathy (DR). Qualified technicians collected anthropometric data using defined techniques. Mortality data was collected from participant recruitment until December 31, 2019. Cox proportional hazards models limited cubic spline curves, and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis were employed to investigate the research aims.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 6.75 years, there were 446 documented deaths from all causes and 163 fatalities attributed to cardiovascular events. Multivariable-adjusted Cox regression indicated a positive association between WWI and all-cause mortality (HR = 1.35, 95%CI:1.16–1.56, P < 0.001) as well as CVD mortality (HR = 1.36, 95%CI:1.07–1.73, P = 0.011). Subgroup analyses corroborated these findings, demonstrating similar relationships across various age, sex, race, and comorbidity strata.RCS analysis revealed a linear positive connection between WWI and both all-cause and cardiovascular disease mortality (P for non-linearity > 0.05).ConclusionsHigher WWI was significantly associated with increased risk of all-cause and CVD mortality in patients with DR, suggesting a positive correlation between WWI and mortality risk in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01923-1.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR), the predominant microvascular complication of diabetes mellitus, represents a major global health challenge with systemic implications beyond ocular complications [1, 2]. Recent data identifies DR as a key indicator of widespread vascular dysfunction, with longitudinal cohort studies revealing a 58% increase in all-cause mortality among individuals with retinal hemorrhages compared to those without DR [3]. Recent advancements in multimodal imaging have revealed significant pathophysiological connections between retinal capillary dropout patterns and subclinical cardiovascular remodeling [4].

Although conventional obesity metrics such as body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference (WC) are extensively utilized to evaluate metabolic risk, their inadequacies in differentiating fat mass from lean mass and accounting for height-related biases cast doubt on their predictive validity for mortality in chronic diseases [5, 6]. The weight-adjusted waist index (WWI) [7], an innovative anthropometric measure that combines waist circumference and body weight, overcomes these limitations by disentangling the nonlinear correlation between weight and height, potentially providing a more accurate representation of visceral fat and metabolic stress [8].

Recent epidemiological studies indicate that WWI had a distinct correlation with hypertension, insulin resistance, and atherosclerosis, surpassing BMI in forecasting subclinical organ damage [9]. Prior research has shown that elevated levels of WWI markedly heightened the risk of cardiovascular disease mortality in diabetic individuals, potentially mediated by chronic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction [10, 11]. Nevertheless, current research primarily emphasizes general diabetes cohorts, overlooking the distinct pathophysiological interactions among diabetic retinopathy severity, systemic microvascular injury, and obesity-related metabolic disruptions.

This study seeks to examine the correlation between WWI and mortality in persons with diabetic retinopathy, addressing the significant deficiency in risk classification for this high-risk cohort. If validated, these findings may improve early identification of patients at increased mortality risk, facilitating tailored lifestyle or pharmaceutical strategies to alleviate negative effects.

Methods

Study design and participants

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) is a comprehensive research program aimed at evaluating the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the United States. The data generated by this effort is available at the following link: https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/Default.aspx. The methodologies utilized in NHANES have obtained ethical endorsement from the Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics, and all participants provided signed informed consent before their involvement.

Data concerning patients diagnosed with DR were extracted from the NHANES database over ten cycles from 1999 to 2018. The diagnosis of DR was established based on confirmations from healthcare professionals. In particular, individuals who responded affirmatively to the question, “Has a doctor ever informed you that diabetes has impacted your eyes or that you had retinopathy?” were categorized as having DR. The detailed participant selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. From the initial NHANES population of 101,316 individuals (1999–2018), we identified 9,571 patients with diabetes. Among these, 1,500 patients had self-reported diabetic retinopathy. After excluding participants with missing data for weight (n = 136), waist circumference (n = 114), and mortality (n = 3), our final analytical sample comprised 1,247 participants with diabetic retinopathy. This retrospective cohort study utilized baseline data from the NHANES 1999–2018 period, with comprehensive assessment of variables at enrollment, followed by longitudinal tracking of mortality outcomes through December 31, 2019.

Assessment of WWI

WWI was defined as waist circumference (cm) divided by the square root of weight (kg). This computation utilizes established anthropometric techniques. The subjects’ body weights were measured with a digital scale while attired in lightweight clothing, and waist circumference was accurately recorded at the iliac crest using a steel measuring tape. All measurements were conducted by trained individuals in accordance with the protocols specified by NHANES to ensure measurement consistency [12, 13]. The WWI formula (WWI = waist circumference / √weight) has been confirmed in epidemiological research, confirming its reliability as a tool for evaluating central obesity, independent of total body mass [14].

Outcomes

The principal outcome was all-cause death, while CVD mortality was designated as a secondary outcome. Based on the 10th revision of (ICD-10) codes, deaths due to heart disease (ICD 100-I09, I11, I13, and I20-I51) or cerebrovascular disease (I60-I69) were defined as CVD mortality [15]. Mortality statistics were obtained by linking the NHANES dataset with death certificate information from the National Death Index (NDI), a nationally maintained mortality registry managed by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) [16].

Covariate

Data were collected using a comprehensive approach incorporating three modalities: questionnaire surveys, physical examinations, and laboratory investigations. Standardized questionnaires were utilized to obtain participants’ demographic characteristics, including gender, age, race, smoking history [17], alcohol use [18], poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), and educational level. Laboratory analyses of blood samples included measurements of serum glucose (Glu), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine (Cr), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels. Clinical data collection encompassed disease duration in diabetic patients, along with medical histories of hypertension [19], hyperlipidemia [20], and chronic kidney disease (CKD) [21].

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were presented as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables, and as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Group comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA, the Kruskal-Wallis test, and the chi-square test, contingent upon the characteristics of the variables involved. Survival probabilities for all-cause and cardiovascular mortality were calculated using Kaplan-Meier curves and analyzed by log-rank testing. To assess the impact of WWI on mortality risk, Cox proportional hazards regression models were developed to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), treating WWI as both a continuous variable and as categorized into tertiles. The choice of tertiles was based on several methodological considerations: (1) ensuring adequate sample size per group (n ≈ 416 per tertile) to maintain statistical power while avoiding over-stratification that could lead to unstable estimates, (2) providing sufficient number of mortality events in each group to generate stable and reliable hazard ratio estimates, (3) maintaining consistency with the majority of existing WWI mortality studies in diabetic and cardiovascular populations [22], (4) achieving optimal balance between statistical precision and clinical interpretability for practical risk stratification purposes, and (5) avoiding potential spurious associations that might arise from excessive stratification given our total sample size of 1,247 participants. Three hierarchical models were utilized: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, and race; and Model 3 additionally controlled for PIR, smoking history, educational attainment, alcohol consumption, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney disease, and diabetes duration. Trend analyses evaluated dose-response connections across tertiles. Due to the extended enrollment period (1999–2018) and fixed mortality endpoint (December 31, 2019), participants enrolled in later cycles inherently had shorter follow-up times. To address this, we employed left truncation in Cox models, where follow-up time started at the date of NHANES examination. Furthermore, restricted cubic spline modeling was utilized to examine the nature and linearity of the relationship between WWI and mortality risks. Subgroup analyses using interaction terms were performed to identify possible effect modification by demographic and clinical variables. Sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of our findings by adjusting for DR severity. The complete study population (n = 1,247) from NHANES 1999–2018 cycles was utilized for this analysis. Since DR severity data were not consistently available across all study cycles, we employed multivariate multiple imputation with 5 replications and a chained equation approach to handle missing DR severity data and other covariates across the entire study population [23, 24]. DR severity was classified as mild non-proliferative retinopathy (NPR), moderate/severe NPR, and proliferative retinopathy based on available grading information. This approach ensures the utilization of the complete dataset while properly accounting for uncertainty in imputed values. Cox proportional hazards models were then re-run with additional adjustment for DR severity. The threshold for statistical significance was established at a two-sided p-value of less than 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.4.3 (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) and Free Statistics software version 2.1.1.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 1,247 participants were included in this study, with a mean age of 62.07 years (± 12.45), of which 52.61% were male. There were 446 recorded deaths from all causes and 163 due to cardiovascular events. The all-cause mortality rate among the cohort was 35.77%, while the CVD mortality rate was 13.1%. Table 1 delineates the baseline attributes categorized by WWI tertiles. The study demonstrated that both all-cause and cardiovascular death rates significantly escalated with higher WWI tertiles (P < 0.001 and P = 0.037, respectively). Notable disparities across WWI tertiles were identified in age, PIR, gender, race, diabetes duration, and alcohol consumption. Metabolic evaluations indicated increased TG and BUN, and reduced HDL-C in the high WWI cohort. Moreover, those with increased WWI exhibited a poorer educational attainment and a higher incidence of chronic diseases, such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and CKD. No statistically significant variations were observed among WWI tertiles regarding Glu, TC, Cr, HbA1c, and smoking history.

Association of WWI with the risk of mortality in participants with DR

Table 2 demonstrates the association between WWI and the risk of all-cause and CVD mortality among patients with DR. In the crude model without adjustment for covariates (Model 1), WWI as a continuous variable was significantly associated with the risk of all-cause mortality (HR 1.55, 95% CI 1.36–1.76, P < 0.001); after adjusting for sex, age, and race (Model 2) the association remained significant (HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.23 1.64, P < 0.001), and after further adjustment for the confounders of PIR, smoking history, education level, alcohol use, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, CKD, and duration of diabetes (Model 3), WWI remained independently and significantly associated with the risk of all-cause mortality (HR 1.35, 95% CI 1.16–1.56, P < 0.001). Analyzed in WWI triads, the highest group (T3) demonstrated a significant increased risk of all-cause mortality in all three models (HR 1.52, 95% CI 1.18–1.95, P = 0.001 in Model 3) and a significant trend relationship (P for trend = 0.001). In terms of the risk of CVD mortality, the continuous variable of WWI also showed a significant positive association with risk (Model 3: HR = 1.36, 95% CI: 1.07–1.73, P = 0.011). There was a marginally significant difference in CVD mortality between the highest and lowest WWI groups. (HR = 1.46, 95% CI: 0.96–2.20, P = 0.074).

The Kaplan-Meier survival analyses demonstrated clear and statistically significant disparities among WWI tertiles regarding all-cause and cardiovascular death over the follow-up period. The survival curves exhibit a gradient effect, indicating that heightened WWI is consistently correlated with a greater chance of mortality.

Sensitivity analysis

Among participants with available objective DR severity data (n = 683), 555 (81.3%) had mild NPR, 100 (14.6%) had moderate/severe NPR, and 28 (4.1%) had proliferative retinopathy. To utilize the complete study population and address missing DR severity data, we employed multiple imputations for all 1,247 participants. The sensitivity analysis using the complete dataset with multiple imputations confirmed the robustness of our findings. WWI remained significantly associated with both all-cause mortality (HR = 1.37, 95%CI: 1.17–1.59, P < 0.001) and CVD mortality (HR = 1.38, 95%CI: 1.08–1.76, P = 0.011) even after adjusting for DR severity (Supplementary Table S1). These results demonstrate that the positive association between WWI and mortality in DR patients persists after proper adjustment for disease severity using rigorous statistical methodology.

Subgroup and interaction analyses

The subgroup analyses revealed that the adjusted hazard ratios for both all-cause mortality and cardiovascular mortality were uniformly heightened across all assessed subpopulations. No statistically significant interactions were observed across strata defined by age, gender, race, duration of diabetes, hypertension, CKD, or hyperlipidemia (all p for interaction > 0.05), indicating that these factors did not modify the association between the exposure and mortality risk (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study involving 1,247 patients with diabetic retinopathy revealed robust associations between WWI and all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, with sensitivity analysis confirming the independence of these associations after adjusting for DR severity, suggesting WWI’s potential value as a prognostic indicator in DR patients. The primary analysis of the full cohort demonstrated significant associations, while the enhanced sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation validated the robustness and independence of WWI’s prognostic value. Following thorough adjustment for demographic, socioeconomic, and clinical variables, each unit increase in WWI was associated with a 35% and 36% elevated hazard for all-cause and cardiovascular disease death, respectively. Individuals in the highest WWI tertile demonstrated a 52% elevated risk of all-cause death compared to those in the lowest tertile. Subgroup analysis demonstrated that the correlation between WWI and mortality remained continuously evident across many strata, including gender, age, racial demographics, and patients with pre-existing comorbidities such as hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and chronic kidney disease. Restricted cubic spline analysis validated a linear correlation between WWI and mortality outcomes, highlighting its potential as a continuous risk stratifier in this high-risk demographic.

The advantage of WWI compared to traditional anthropometric measures such as BMI or WC is its mathematical separation of weight and height, which reduces confounding by lean mass and improves specificity for visceral fat [25, 26]. Our data concerning the link between WWI mortality and DR patients demonstrate both agreement and discrepancy with the current literature. The dose-dependent link aligns with findings documented in prior work concerning hypertension [27], type 2 diabetes [28], and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease cohorts [29], hence strengthening the association between WWI and mortality across metabolic illnesses. Notably, the linear risk trajectory without threshold effects in DR contrasts with the nonlinear CVD mortality pattern reported in prediabetic or diabetic populations [30, 31], likely due to the unique pathophysiology of DR, where microvascular damage and systemic metabolic dysregulation heighten risks even at lower WWI levels. The stability of associations across demographic and comorbidity subgroups parallels findings in hypertension and osteoarthritis cohorts [32]. However, the magnitude of mortality risk surpasses estimates in general hypertension yet is diminished compared to elderly Chinese cohorts [33], possibly indicating population-level disparities in adiposity distribution, disease severity, and socioeconomic factors [34]. These findings highlight the accuracy of WWI in identifying persons at elevated risk for cardiovascular problems, even when conventional metrics inadequately assess such risks [35]. Significantly, in contrast to other studies concentrating on non-DR populations [36], these data highlight the substantial correlation between WWI and mortality in patients with diabetes retinopathy, which is a subgroup showing increased microvascular damage and increased inflammation susceptibility caused by obesity [37]. Our findings indicated more pronounced effects than those observed in larger diabetic populations, implying that WWI more effectively delineates adiposity-related hazards in the presence of pre-existing microvascular damage. Numerous pathologic and physiological mechanisms clarify the association between WWI and mortality risk in patients diagnosed with DR. A notable mechanism is the persistent inflammation induced by visceral fat, marked by increased concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1). This inflammatory condition can exacerbate systemic endothelial dysfunction, thereby collaborating with retinal microvascular injury linked to diabetic retinopathy and heightening the chance of lethal cardiovascular incidents [38]. Furthermore, hypoxia in adipose tissue can provoke oxidative stress through NADPH oxidase activity, which not only intensifies apoptosis in retinal Müller cells but also disrupts cardiac mitochondrial bioenergetics, so establishing a dual-organ susceptibility to metabolic disturbances [39]. The lipotoxic effects related to WWI, indicated by elevated ceramide-1-phosphate levels, have been connected with retinal neovascular bleeding via sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor 2 (S1PR2) signaling. This further amplifies pro-thrombotic platelet activation and signifies an elevated risk of both hemorrhagic stroke and pulmonary embolism [40]. The relationship between WWI and gut microbial dysbiosis is significant, as it is known to elevate circulating endotoxin levels that undermine the integrity of the retinal pigment epithelium barrier and the hepatic detoxifying capacity [41]. The interrelated mechanisms underscore the complex role of WWI as a biomarker, involving the evaluation of visceral adiposity and its substantial downstream vascular pathophysiological consequences and implications for mortality risk management in patients with DR.

Our findings are consistent with emerging evidence that WWI captures not only visceral adiposity but also sarcopenic obesity—a phenotype characterized by low muscle mass coexisting with central fat deposition [42]. High WWI may indicate weight loss from muscle breakdown (e.g., due to chronic inflammation or insulin resistance) while central fat persists. In DR, this is particularly harmful as sarcopenia exacerbates microvascular damage by impairing glucose disposal and increasing endothelial dysfunction. Recent studies show WWI is a stronger predictor of sarcopenia than BMI [43], suggesting that the mortality risk may partly stem from the frailty and metabolic fragility of individuals with high WWI. Although an ‘obesity paradox’ (lower mortality in overweight diabetic patients) exists in general diabetes cohorts [44], this protective effect was absent in DR patients with high WWI. This discrepancy likely stems from WWI’s design, which specifically identifies individuals with excess visceral adiposity relative to total body massWithin the DR population, where microvascular injury exacerbates systemic inflammation [45], the sarcopenic obesity indicated by high WWI may accelerate cardiometabolic decline. Conversely, low WWI may indicate the presence of protective subcutaneous fat or maintained muscle mass. Consequently, WWI offers enhanced risk classification by encompassing body composition dynamics overlooked by traditional measures. Our analysis conclusively shows that WWI remains consistently associated with increased mortality risk across different DR patient populations. Within this high-risk cohort, each unit rise in WWI was associated with a 35% increase in all-cause mortality, underscoring its additional use in identifying diabetic retinopathy patients at heightened risk. This aligns with prior work showing DR as a mortality marker [46] but extends it by revealing modifiable risk via body composition optimization.

The results of our study suggest that WWI may serve as a practical, low-cost anthropometric marker to identify DR patients at heightened risk of mortality. This is particularly valuable in resource-constrained settings where comprehensive ophthalmologic assessment may not be readily available. Our sensitivity analysis, employing multiple imputation and adjustment for DR severity, confirmed the independence and robustness of the WWI-mortality association, reinforcing WWI as a valuable clinical tool for risk stratification. Based on our findings, we recommend more frequent cardiovascular risk assessment, intensified glycemic control, and consideration of multidisciplinary care involving endocrinologists and cardiologists for DR patients with elevated WWI, while also emphasizing the importance of comprehensive retinal evaluation when feasible. The dose-response relationship observed in our analysis suggests that WWI-targeted lifestyle interventions may contribute to mortality risk reduction as part of comprehensive DR management.

The strengths of this study encompass a prolonged longitudinal follow-up (median 6.75 years) and the adjustment for socioeconomic variables frequently neglected in obesity studies. The uniform outcomes from multiple sensitivity analyses, encompassing nonlinear assessments and subgroup stratification, bolster the reliability of our principal findings. However, several limitations warrant consideration. First, as an observational study, our findings demonstrate an association between WWI and mortality but cannot establish causality. Second, owing to the constraints associated with the NHANES data, while we adjusted for known confounders, unmeasured factors (e.g., chronic inflammation, subclinical frailty, physical activity, detailed dietary patterns or comprehensive medication profiles) may contribute to both elevated WWI and increased mortality risk. Third, DR diagnosis in the primary analysis relied on self-reported physician assessments rather than standardized retinal imaging, potentially introducing misclassification bias. Additionally, the primary analysis could not account for DR severity, which represents an important limitation as different DR stages are associated with varying degrees of systemic microvascular damage and baseline mortality risk. To partially address this limitation, we conducted sensitivity analysis with adjustment for DR severity using multiple imputation methods, which showed consistent results supporting our main findings. However, the limitation of self-reported DR diagnosis in the primary analysis remains, and future studies using standardized retinal imaging throughout would strengthen these findings. Fourth, WWI and covariates were assessed only at baseline, preventing the capture of longitudinal changes. Single-time measurements may not reflect dynamic shifts in body composition, and unintentional weight loss could artificially elevate WWI, especially in frail individuals. Additionally, the absence of weighted calculations restricts the applicability of the generalizability of the study’s conclusions beyond the sample data, thereby necessitating additional investigation in diverse demographic groups. While WWI showed a consistent dose-response relationship with all-cause mortality, the association with CVD mortality in the highest WWI tertile was marginally significant. We retained CVD mortality as a secondary outcome due to its clinical relevance, but caution against overinterpreting marginally significant associations. This may reflect limited statistical power due to the relatively small number of CVD deaths. These constraints highlight the importance of careful analysis of study findings and the need for improved research methodologies to overcome these limitations. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal measurements of WWI, standardized and adjudicated DR severity scales, and granular data on lifestyle factors, medication adherence, and healthcare access. Mechanistic investigations into WWI-related inflammation and metabolic disruption in DR are also warranted. Ultimately, prospective trials assessing the impact of interventions targeting WWI on clinical outcomes would clarify its role in mortality risk reduction.

Conclusions

This study identified a linear dose–response relationship between WWI and all-cause and CVD mortality in the patients with DR. A higher WWI was associated with an increased all-cause and CVD mortality. These findings suggest that incorporating WWI into clinical assessments could lead to more targeted interventions, potentially improving patient prognosis and quality of life among those suffering from DR.
