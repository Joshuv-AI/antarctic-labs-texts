RNA in situ hybridization for ISG15 and IFI6 highlights IFN-I activity in discoid lupus erythematosus
Authors: Jeff R. Gehlhausen, Jeffrey M. Cohen, Emily Baker, Sophia Luyten, Wilson Gipson, Christine J. Ko, Jennifer M. McNiff, William Damsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IFN-I signaling is a hallmark of discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE), but routine tissue-based assays to detect this pathway are limited. Re-analysis of public transcriptomic data identified ISG15 and IFI6 as candidate IFN markers, ranking among the top differentially expressed genes in DLE datasets. We performed RNA in situ hybridization for both markers on archival specimens from DLE (n =11), other inflammatory dermatoses (n = 11; atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, lichen planus), and controls (n = 8). A total of 81 slides were scored (0–4+) in epidermal and dermal compartments. RNA in situ hybridization showed strong staining in basal keratinocytes and dermis of DLE, with deep dermal positivity in 89% of specimens with sufficient depth for analysis. Deep staining was absent in controls and other dermatoses. DLE demonstrated significantly higher staining intensity than inflammatory controls (P < .001). RNA in situ hybridization for ISG15 and IFI6 identifies IFN activation in DLE, distinguishing it from other dermatoses and providing objective molecular evidence applicable to routine specimens.

Introduction

IFN-I signaling drives the pathophysiology of discoid lupus erythematosus (DLE), manifesting as a robust transcriptional signature in affected tissues.(Achtman and Werth, 2015; Wenzel, 2019) Despite this shared molecular mechanism, reliable tissue-based assays to detect IFN activation remain limited or indirectly rely on CD123 staining of plasmacytoid dendritic cells.(Walsh et al, 2015) Current diagnostic approaches rely primarily on histopathologic pattern recognition, which may be subtle or ambiguous in early lesions or superficial biopsies. Molecular confirmation of IFN-I pathway activation could improve diagnostic accuracy while potentially identifying patients likely to respond to targeted therapies.

The therapeutic relevance of IFN-I detection has increased with ongoing trials of IFN-I receptor blockade (anifrolumab) and JAK1/TYK2 inhibition (Deucravacitinib, Brepocitinib) in DLE; prior smaller studies have suggested high potential efficacy.(Paik et al, 2022; Shaw et al, 2023) Despite these therapeutic advances, identifying and quantifying IFN pathway activation in tissue remains essential for confirming diagnosis and understanding disease activity. However, detecting IFN-Is directly in tissue remains challenging due to the lack of validated antibodies and established relevant targets. IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs) provide robust surrogate markers for pathway activation, with potential utility for both diagnosis and therapeutic selection.

CD123 immunohistochemistry identifies plasmacytoid dendritic cells, which have been implicated as major IFN-I producers in DLE lesions.(Walsh et al, 2015) However, recent evidence suggests that plasmacytoid Dendritic Cells in lupus skin often exhibit an exhausted phenotype and that other cell types (eg, keratinocytes, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and myeloid cells) make substantial contributions to IFN-I production. (Psarras et al, 2020; Vazquez et al, 2024) Furthermore, this approach requires adequate dermal tissue and does not directly assess IFN-I response across cell types. RNA in situ hybridization (RISH) for ISGs could provide a broader assessment of IFN-I activity across multiple cell populations, applicable to routine diagnostic specimens, including superficial biopsies.(Chen et al, 2023; Wang et al, 2021)

We identified ISG15 and IFI6 through transcriptomic analysis as candidate markers for IFN activation in skin. We validated these markers across DLE and inflammatory controls to establish their diagnostic utility and characterize disease-specific staining patterns. Our findings demonstrate that RISH for these ISGs provides objective molecular evidence of IFN activation, with potential applications in both diagnosis and treatment selection.

Results

Identification of IFN-stimulated gene targets

Analysis of previously published gene expression data (GSE72535) comparing DLE and normal skin revealed multiple significantly upregulated transcripts, including those associated with increased IFN-I signaling. Among these, ISG15 and IFI6 ranked among the top 6 upregulated genes and were abundantly expressed in DLE (Figure 1a). Differential RISH staining patterns in DLE versus normal skin

For validation, we first planned to employ immunohistochemistry, though on review, there were no clear, well-established antibodies in use for ISG15 and IFI6 staining in formalin-fixed tissues. Thus, we decided to use RISH for validation. Both ISG15 (Figure 1c and e) and IFI6 (Figure 1d) RISH staining revealed robust positivity in DLE samples (Figure 1c–e), with minimal to no staining in normal control skin (Figure 1b). In DLE, staining was most prominent in regions typically involved in pathology, including the basal keratinocytes and follicular epithelium at sites of interface dermatitis. Although staining was most intense in the superficial dermis, deeper dermal staining was also observed. The staining pattern reflected pervasive IFN-I activity in the DLE microenvironment, with positivity observed in multiple cell types, including lymphocytes, histiocytes, keratinocytes, endothelium, adnexa, and fibroblasts. In contrast, control tissues only very rarely showed isolated positive cells.

Comparison with other inflammatory dermatoses

The staining patterns in DLE were distinct from those observed in other common inflammatory dermatoses (Figure 3). In atopic dermatitis, staining was typically scant, with some punctate signal seen focally in keratinocytes but negligible staining in the dermis. In psoriasis, scattered foci of weak to moderate staining were observed in the epidermis, especially in the granular layer of the epidermis. Otherwise, dermal inflammatory cells were the most observed positive cells with overall weak staining. Lichen planus featured prominent staining along the basal keratinocytes coincidental with interface dermatitis, along with granular layer staining seen in regions of hypergranulosis; otherwise, the dermal inflammatory infiltrate demonstrated relatively weak staining. Differential RISH staining intensity in DLE versus other dermatoses

Semi-quantitative analysis of staining intensity in both epidermal and dermal compartments revealed significant differences between diagnostic categories (Figure 4). DLE consistently showed the highest mean intensity scores for both ISG15 and IFI6 in both epidermal and dermal compartments. For ISG15, DLE samples exhibited mean epidermal staining of 3.2 ± 0.3 and dermal staining of 3.1 ± 0.3, significantly higher than all other conditions (P < .05). Distinctive staining patterns distinguish DLE from other inflammatory dermatoses

Analysis of staining patterns revealed characteristic signatures for each disease category (Figure 5). In DLE, ISG15 showed a strong preference for basal (91%) and follicular (45%) patterns in the epidermis, with prominent deep dermal (64%) and superficial dermal (91%) staining. In contrast, lichen planus exhibited a distinct staining pattern, with both ISG15 and IFI6 showing strong basal layer staining (100% for both markers) but minimal follicular involvement and negligible deep dermal staining. The granular layer pattern was prominent in lichen planus for ISG15 (75%), correlating with the hypergranulosis typically seen in this condition.

Psoriasis demonstrated a distinct pattern with granular expression (100% for ISG15) in the epidermis, correlating with hypogranulosis, one of its signature histopathologic findings. Atopic dermatitis showed predominantly patchy epidermal staining (100% for ISG15, 67% for IFI6) with minimal basal involvement. Normal skin samples showed absent or "none" for all staining patterns, with occasional focal positivity (17%).

Critically, deep dermal staining was exclusively observed in DLE (ISG15: 64%; IFI6: 73%), suggesting that this pattern may serve as a specific diagnostic indicator. In nearly all DLE cases that lacked deep dermal staining, depth could not be assessed due to the superficial nature of the shave biopsy rather than the true lack of staining. This was also true of the follicular staining, in that not every DLE sample had follicles present.

Principal component analysis demonstrates segregation between disease categories

Principal component analysis using R 4.4.2 (see methods) based on both staining intensity and pattern features demonstrated clear separation of diagnostic categories (Figure 6). When analyzing the combined ISG15 and IFI6 data (Figure 6, bottom panels), DLE samples (orange ellipse) formed a distinct cluster separated from other inflammatory dermatoses. Normal skin, atopic dermatitis, and psoriasis typically clustered together with some overlap, while lichen planus (purple ellipse) occupied an intermediate position between these conditions and DLE. Analysis of the statistical factors driving this diagnostic separation revealed that the primary axis of differentiation was driven by overall staining intensity for both markers in both tissue compartments, with the strongest contributions from dermal staining intensity (ISG15 dermal: 0.396, IFI6 dermal: 0.390) and epidermal intensity (IFI6 epidermal: 0.379, ISG15 epidermal: 0.377), combined with the presence of basal staining patterns (0.380). This axis effectively separates samples with robust expression of both markers throughout the tissue (characteristic of DLE) from those with minimal or absent staining. The secondary axis was predominantly driven by patchy staining patterns, including patchy superficial (0.518), patchy basal (0.501), and patchy granular (0.381) distributions. These are patterns that were not seen in DLE, save for one case with patchy superficial dermal staining. This analysis demonstrates that the most diagnostically important features for these RISH markers are the strong expression of both markers extending through all tissue layers in DLE, particularly when combined with basal epidermal involvement.

Discussion

ISG15 and IFI6 are RISH markers that have a distinctive staining pattern in DLE that is absent in normal skin and prototypical inflammatory disorders. These markers not only demonstrate robust staining intensity in DLE tissues but also exhibit distinctive staining patterns that effectively distinguish DLE from normal skin and three other common skin disorders that are sometimes included in the clinical differential diagnosis for DLE. The staining patterns observed align with the known pathophysiology of DLE, with prominent staining in regions of interface dermatitis and widespread positivity reflecting the pervasive IFN-I signature characteristic of lupus.

Our semi-quantitative analysis revealed significantly higher staining intensity for both ISG15 and IFI6 in DLE compared to other conditions. While lichen planus features interface dermatitis potentially mimicking DLE histopathologically, it typically shows only granular and basal keratinocyte positivity on RISH staining, lacking the intense and widespread dermal RISH staining characteristic of DLE. This finding is consistent with the stronger and more pervasive IFN-I signature in lupus compared to other inflammatory conditions.

Statistical analysis guides practicing pathologists on which features are most valuable diagnostically. The data demonstrate that the strongest discriminating factor is robust staining intensity for both ISG15 and IFI6 throughout the tissue, particularly in the dermis, combined with characteristic basal keratinocyte involvement. This pattern combination—strong dermal expression plus basal epidermal staining—provides a reliable diagnostic signature for DLE.

CD123 immunohistochemistry can identify dermal plasmacytoid dendritic cells, which may contribute to IFN-I production, supporting the diagnosis of DLE.(Walsh et al, 2015) One clear advantage of our RISH-based approach is the characteristic staining in both the epidermis and dermis. Furthermore, the RISH approach directly visualizes the IFN response across multiple cell types, providing a more comprehensive assessment of the IFN-I signature that defines DLE.

These findings have several potential clinical applications. First, these markers effectively identify IFN-I activation in DLE, providing objective confirmation of pathway activation in tissue. Second, their pattern analysis serves as a diagnostic adjunct for challenging cases, helping distinguish IFN-I-driven conditions from histologic mimics such as lichen planus, which showed only basal staining without significant dermal involvement. Third, dermatoses that are identified to have comparable IFN signatures to DLE suggest that they could perhaps be candidates for IFN-targeted therapies regardless of specific clinical diagnosis. Fourth, as treatments like anifrolumab and TYK2 inhibitors become increasingly available for IFN-driven dermatoses, these markers provide a direct method to confirm target pathway activation and potentially monitor treatment response.

Our study has several limitations. The sample size, while adequate for initial characterization, warrants expansion in larger cohorts. In particular, the comparator groups of individual inflammatory dermatoses were small (3–4 samples per condition), and further validation studies with larger groups of inflammatory dermatoses are necessary to confirm the diagnostic performance of these markers. Our study examined only DLE; the applicability of these findings to other cutaneous lupus erythematosus subtypes, such as subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus and acute cutaneous lupus erythematosus, which may lack deep dermal or follicular involvement, remains to be determined. We acknowledge that mRNA expression does not always correlate directly with protein levels; however, ISG expression serves as a well-established surrogate for IFN-I pathway activation. Semi-quantitative scoring was used for practical implementation in routine diagnostic settings; future studies could incorporate automated image analysis for more precise quantification. Additionally, we focused on prototypical inflammatory dermatoses for comparison; future studies should evaluate these markers in other conditions, including lichenoid drug eruption, which represents an important clinical and histologic mimicker, as well as other connective tissue diseases, including dermatomyositis, systemic sclerosis, mixed connective tissue disease, and overlap syndromes. Prospective studies correlating baseline RISH intensity with treatment response to IFN-targeted therapies would be particularly valuable for establishing these markers as predictive biomarkers.

In conclusion, RISH staining for ISG15 and IFI6 effectively identifies IFN-I activation in DLE, distinguishing this condition from other inflammatory dermatoses. These markers represent practical tools for routine diagnostic use, requiring only standard formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue and applicable to the superficial biopsies commonly obtained in clinical practice. Future studies should focus on validating their utility as companion diagnostics for emerging IFN-I-targeted therapies.

Methods

Transcriptomic analysis

We re-analyzed previously published gene expression data comparing DLE tissue with normal skin publicly available on Gene Expression Omnibus (GSE72535) (Chong et al., 2015).

Data were analyzed using R 4.4.2 and the DESeq2 package for computational analysis of transcriptomic data.(Love et al, 2014) Re-analysis of DLE single-cell RNA sequencing data (GSE294296) was performed using Seurat v4.3.(Butler et al, 2018; Gehlhausen et al, 2025) Following quality control and normalization, cells were classified into major lineages based on canonical markers. Differential expression analysis comparing DLE to healthy controls identified significantly upregulated genes (adjusted P < .05, log2FC > 2) within each cell type, with ISG15 and IFI6 ranking among the top differentially expressed genes across all populations examined.

Tissue samples

For our validation study, we examined formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue sections from 6 healthy control samples, 11 DLE, and 11 samples of other inflammatory dermatoses (3 samples of atopic dermatitis, 4 psoriasis, and 4 lichen planus). These conditions were selected as they represent inflammatory dermatoses that may enter the clinical differential diagnosis with DLE. All samples were obtained from the Yale Dermatopathology archives following institutional review board approval (Yale Human Investigation Committee, HIC 2000022585).

RNA in situ hybridization (RISH)

Formalin fixed paraffin embedded sections were stained using the Biocare ONCORE Pro X automated staining system with Molecular Instruments HCR RISH protocol according to manufacturer instructions. After deparaffinization, target retrieval was performed, followed by probe hybridization for ISG15 and IFI6 (Molecular Instruments). For detection, Warp Red chromogen staining was utilized with hematoxylin counterstaining. Quality controls included PPIB (positive control) and dapB (negative control) on parallel sections.

Statistical Analysis

Staining intensity was evaluated in both epidermal and dermal compartments using a semi-quantitative scoring system (0–4+) by two independent dermatopathologists who were blinded to the diagnosis. The scoring rubric was defined as follows: 0 = none, 1+ = focal/light/patchy staining, 2+ = moderate surface area of visible staining, 3+ = broader regions of visible staining, 4+ = intense, distinct staining over broad areas. For epidermal assessment, staining was further characterized by pattern location (basal, follicular, granular, or pan to describe the vertically oriented location in the epidermis). For dermal assessment, staining patterns were classified as perivascular, periadnexal, superficial, deep, or none. For some cases of epidermis and all cases of the dermis, an additional modifier of breadth, including patchy, focal, or diffuse, was added. Staining intensity scores (combined epidermal and dermal) were compared between DLE and each diagnosis using Mann-Whitney U tests with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction for multiple comparisons.

Pattern analysis

To analyze the distribution of staining patterns, we recorded the frequency of each pattern type across diagnostic categories. Pattern frequencies were normalized to the total number of samples within each diagnostic category and expressed as percentages. This allowed us to identify characteristic staining signatures for each condition. Principal component analysis, a statistical method that identifies patterns in complex datasets and displays similarities and differences among samples as visual clusters, was performed using R to visualize the separation between diagnostic categories based on both staining intensity values and pattern data. This approach enabled multidimensional assessment of staining profiles to determine if disease groups could be distinguished by their combined intensity and pattern characteristics.
