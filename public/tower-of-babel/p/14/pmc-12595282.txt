Interactive effects of Bisphenol F exposure and TLR4 rs4986790 on unexplained miscarriage
Authors: Manli Wang, Wenxin Huang, Weina Chen, Xiaoping Yue, Yi Sun, Yanbing Lin, Shuaishuai Xing, Depeng Zhao, Yanxin Wang, Geng Guo, Qigang Fan, Zhihong Zhang, Wenli Cheng, Xinying Huang, Linlin Wu, Huidong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundUnexplained miscarriage (UM) occurs frequently and significantly limits global human reproduction. However, till now, whether environment–gene interactions might interactively regulate UM has not been well characterised.MethodsWe combined epidemiological study using a UM case–control group and in vitro functional assays using mouse model and human trophoblast HTR-8/SVneo cells. The interactions between environmental exposure to Bisphenol F (BPF) and genetic variation in TLR4 rs4986790 (A896G) were explored. Trophoblast cell migration and invasion (key functions for healthy pregnancy), the interactions between TLR4 variants and β2GPI (β-2 glycoprotein I, an inflammatory mediator), and the downstream signalling molecules MMP9 and RAC1 were investigated.FindingsBPF exposure and the TLR4 rs4986790 genotype interactively influence the occurrence of UM and impaired trophoblast cell migration/invasion. Mechanistically, TLR4-A binds more strongly to β2GPI than TLR4-G. BPF exposure further enhances β2GPI-TLR4-A interactions but has a lesser effect on β2GPI-TLR4-G. As a result, BPF more severely suppresses MMP9 and RAC1 protein levels—critical for cell migration/invasion—in TLR4-A cells.InterpretationCollectively, this study discovers that BPF exposure and TLR4 rs4986790 interactively govern trophoblast cell migration/invasion and UM from epidemiological, functional, and mechanistic perspectives, providing a comprehensive methodology for further investigation of environment–gene interactions in other diseases and a scientific foundation for precise treatment against various human diseases.FundingThis work was supported by the 10.13039/501100001809Natural Science Foundation of China (NSFC No. 82373602), Shenzhen Medical Research Fund (No. B2303002), 10.13039/501100021171Guangdong Basic and Applied Basic Research Foundation (2023B1515120054 and 2023A1515110497), Shenzhen Science and Technology Programme (No. JCYJ20220530144403008, JCYJ20220818103607015, JCYJ20230807111401002, JCYJ20240813180400002, and JCYJ20241202152800001), 10.13039/501100003787Hebei Natural Science Foundation (B2024201087), Futian Healthcare Research Project (No. FTWS011 and FTWS2022002).

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Unexplained miscarriage (UM) highly occurs but its causes are largely unknown. TLR4 rs4986790 (A896G) is linked to immune regulation and pregnancy disorders, but its association with UM is contradictory. Bisphenol F (BPF) is a high-prevalence bisphenol analogue with endocrine-disrupting activity and has been widely detected in humans, but its role in UM has not been well characterised. Moreover, the epidemiological and mechanistic evidence for interactions between BPF exposure and TLR4 rs4986790 in UM are completely unknown.

Added value of this study

We conduct a UM case–control study, BPF-exposed mouse model, and cell-based assays and find that BPF exposure and TLR4-A transcriptype interactively increase UM risk. Higher urinary BPF levels and TLR4-A are independently and also multiplicatively associated with UM. Functionally, BPF more severely suppresses migration/invasion of TLR4-A trophoblast cells than TLR4-G cells by more down-regulating MMP9 and RAC1 levels. Mechanistically, TLR4-A shows stronger interactions with β2GPI than TLR4-G, and BPF enhances their interactions by promoting FOXP3-mediated TLR4 transcription and by increasing β2GPI membrane abundance.

Implications of all the available evidence

This study identifies environment–gene interactions in UM, showing that women carrying TLR4-A are more vulnerable to BPF exposure. The findings provide an approach to reduce exposure in susceptible individuals and a mechanistic basis to precise prevention of miscarriage. This study also offers a framework for dissecting environment–gene interactions in other human diseases.

Introduction

About 15%–25% pregnant women worldwide might experience spontaneous miscarriage, i.e. the loss of early embryos or foetus in a nature state.1 About 1%–5% pregnant women might suffer from recurrent miscarriage (RM, two or more times consecutive spontaneous miscarriage) globally.2,3 Miscarriage greatly limits human fertility and global reproduction. However, the causes of approximately 50% miscarriage are still clinically unexplained,4,5 which are generally termed as unexplained miscarriage (UM).

In general, human genotypes might affect the occurrence and development of various diseases.6, 7, 8, 9 Till now, it has been identified that dozens of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) are associated with the risk of miscarriage.10, 11, 12 Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) is a pattern recognition receptor that plays an important role to maintain innate host immunity, induce trophoblast inflammation, and impair cell migration.13,14 Emerging epidemiological analysis has indicated that TLR4 rs4986790 (nucleotide A896G corresponding to TLR4-A vs TLR4-G or amino acid residue D299G) might be potentially related with miscarriage. It has been reported that TLR4-A is associated with antiphospholipid syndrome (APS, which is characterised as miscarriage and the occurrence of thrombotic events) (OR = 0.288, 95% CI, 0.098–0.844) based on an APS cohort containing 299 A/A women, 30 A/G women, and 1 G/G woman.15 Another study shows that TLR4-A is associated with miscarriage in Uygur women (OR = 8.272, 95% CI, 1.168–40.358, based on 294 A/A women, 15 A/G women, and 0 G/G woman) but is not associated with miscarriage in Han women (no exact OR values, based on 297 A/A women, 6 A/G women, and 0 G/G woman).16 Therefore, the exact association and biological mechanisms between TLR4-A or TLR4-G and miscarriage remains largely contradictory and unclear. It is of great significance to explore the association between TLR4 rs4986790 and miscarriage by epidemiological analysis and to investigate the regulatory mechanisms by experimental assays.

In addition to human genotypes, exposure to environmental toxicants also increases the risk to human health.17,18 In this group, we have identified a group of environmental toxicants could induce UM, such as polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon BaP/BPDE,19,20 nanoplastics,21,22 hypoxia,23 or disinfection by-products of tap water.24 In addition to these, Bisphenol A (BPA) has been widely used and detected out in various polycarbonate, epoxy resin, and common plastic bottles; and the average daily intake level is estimated as 2.53 μg/person/day for human.25,26 Due to BPA's potential reproductive toxicity, its applications in maternal and child products have been restricted in North America and the European Union. However, its various analogues or alternatives have been largely emerged and used.26 Among them, Bisphenol F (BPF) has a highest daily intake amount, which raises global concerns about its adverse effects on reproduction, especially miscarriage. Emerging studies have shown that BPF exhibits oestrogenic and/or anti-androgenic activities and is associated with lower birth weight, birth length, and ponderal index.27,28 However, till now, whether BPF exposure might induce unexplained miscarriage has not been well characterised.

The interactions between environmental exposure and genetic factors, either synergistic or antagonistic, are crucial for the occurrence and development of most human diseases.6,29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34 However, till now, whether and how gene (TLR4 rs4986790) and environment (BPF exposure) might interactively govern the occurrence of unexplained miscarriage have not been well characterised at both epidemiological and experimental levels. Therefore, in this study, we expect (1) to investigate the interactive effects of BPF exposure and TLR4 rs4986790 on the occurrence of unexplained miscarriage, (2) to investigate their effects on migration/invasion of human trophoblast cells, which play crucial roles in embryo implantation and healthy pregnancy35, 36, 37, 38, 39 and (3) to further discover their underlying mechanisms. This study gives the solid epidemiological and experimental evidence that environment and gene interactively govern trophoblast cell migration/invasion and the occurrence of unexplained miscarriage.

Methods

Study design and tissue collection

We newly recruited patients with unexplained miscarriage (UM group) and women who sought induced miscarriage to terminate their unwanted pregnancies, who were considered as healthy control (HC) group, in the age between 25 and 30 and treated at the Eighth Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University from April 2022 to January 2024, as the methods described previously.21,40 Any women with clinically known causes of miscarriage were excluded, such as cervical incompetence, chromosome abnormalities, endocrine or metabolic diseases, virus or bacterial infections, as described previously.21,40 We selected the UM group, who had one or more previous miscarriages without any prior successful pregnancies, and the HC group, who did not report any previous miscarriages. HC and UM women did not receive any treatment. After exclusion, 77 UM and 128 HC women were finally enrolled. The characteristic of these HC and UM women were listed in Table S1, including baseline characteristic (age, body mass index, education), clinical information (gestational weeks, RBC, WBC, Hb), and lifestyle (smoking, drinking). All the information was obtained from medical records. Villous tissue samples were collected, dissected from other abortive materials, washed sequentially, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for further use in Western blot analysis. Some tissue samples were also fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin for immunohistochemical analysis. A single-spot of midstream urine sample was collected from these women using glass containers on the same day before surgical miscarriage. BPF levels in urine samples were determined by UHPLC-MS/MS.

Reagent validation

All reagents used in this study, including antibodies and cell lines, were commercially sourced. Antibody information, including catalogue numbers, vendors, and Research Resource Identifier (RRID), were provided in the Western blotting section in the Methods. Human trophoblast HTR-8/SVneo cell line was commercially available and authenticated by Short Tandem Repeat (STR) profiling.

Ethics statement

All experiments with human samples have been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Eighth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No. 2022-017-01). Written informed consents were signed by all the participants before enrolment. All animal research protocols were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Eighth Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University (Approval No. 2023-021-01).

Detection of BPF concentrations in HC and UM urine samples

The levels of BPF in HC and UM urine samples and in mouse serum samples were detected as the methods described previously.41, 42, 43 The sample storage, processing, and analysis were performed using glass instruments or tubes to avoid potential BPF contamination. All the urine samples were stored at −20 °C and serum samples at −80 °C before analysis. Briefly, a 500-μL urine sample or 100-μL serum sample was incubated with 10 μL β-glucuronidase/sulfatase solution (>110,000 units), 50 μL isotope internal standards, and 200 μL 1 M ammonium acetate buffer (pH = 5.0) at 37 °C for 24 h. Then, the mixture was extracted thrice with 3 mL methyl-tert-butyl ether/ethyl acetate. The extracts were dried by nitrogen and re-dissolved in 150 μL water/acetonitrile. The bisphenols in the samples were separated by a UHPLC system Ultimate 3000 equipped with a 1.7 μm, 100 × 2.1 mm Acquity UPLC BEH C18 analytical column (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) with water/acetonitrile and were analysed by an AB Sciex 6500 triple quadrupole mass spectrometry (MS/MS, Toronto, Canada) in negative mode. The intra- and inter-batch precision were below 6.3% and 1.7%, respectively. The quality control samples used the pooled urine samples spiked with the target chemicals and a blank sample was analysed along with each batch. The limits of detection (LODs) for BPF in urine samples was 0.10 μg/L. BPF levels in reagent blanks were all below the LODs. BPF had been detected out in all urine and serum samples. About 78.8%–92.5% spiked BPF could be recovered, and the standard deviations were below 10%.

Cell culture

Human trophoblast HTR-8/SVneo cells (RRID: CVCL_7162) were immortalised by SVneo virus (CL0164, Hunan Fenghui Biotechnology Co., Ltd.). HTR-8/SVneo cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 media with 10% FBS (GIBCO) in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Recent mycoplasma testing assays showed the negative results. To explore the effects of real environmental exposure dose (REED) of bisphenols on miscarriage, we calculated the actual daily intake dose of total bisphenols in human body. One study showed that the total level of all bisphenol analogues was 144 ng/mL in serum of pregnant women in Beijing, China.44 This dose could be considered as a representative of human real intake dose under an inadvertent environment. According to the molecular weight of BPF is 200.23 g/mol, the REED of total bisphenols corresponded to 0.7 μM BPF for cells. It has been reported that BPF exposure (20–80 μM for TM3 Leydig cells and 25–100 μM for MCF-7 cells) significantly increased cell apoptosis and decreased cell viability in a dose-dependent and time-dependent manner.45, 46, 47 Meanwhile, 50 μM BPF exposure significantly decreased the endocytic capacity of immature monocyte-derived dendritic cells.48 Based on the doses used in literature and pre-experiments, in this study, we selected 0 (control), 0.7 (1-fold REED of total bisphenols), 25 (35-fold), 50 (70-fold), 75 (107-fold), or 100 (143-fold) μM BPF to construct BPF-exposed human trophoblast cell models. 293FT cells were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific and cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FBS.

High-throughput mRNA sequencing

HTR-8/SVneo cells were treated with 0 or 50 μM BPF for 24 h. Total RNAs were extracted from HTR-8/SVneo cells or UM and HC villous tissues using Trizol reagent kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer's protocols. RNA quality was assessed on NanoDrop ND-2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and by RNase-free agarose gel electrophoresis. Subsequently, mRNAs were enriched by Oligo (dT) beads, fragmented into short fragments, and reversely transcribed into cDNAs using NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (NEB#7530, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). The purified double-stranded cDNA fragments were end repaired, added with A bases, ligated to Illumina sequencing adaptors, and then purified with the AMPure XP Beads (1×). After amplification by polymerase chain reaction (PCR), the resulting cDNA library was sequenced using Illumina Novaseq6000 by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. (Guangzhou, China).

Analysis of transcriptype of TLR4 mRNA in human and mice

Total RNAs were extracted from HC or UM villous tissues or mouse placental tissues. The isolated mRNAs (1 μg) were converted into cDNAs using EasyScript® All-in-One First-Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix kit, which contained gDNA remover to remove the genomic DNA. The target sequence containing the TLR4 SNP site was amplified by PCR using Phanta Flash Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Then, the PCR products (corresponding to TLR4 mRNA) were sequenced using Sanger Sequencing. The primer (Sangon Biotech) sequences were shown in Table S2.

Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR)

Total RNAs were converted into cDNAs. Then, the cDNAs were amplified using a 20 μL SYBR Green Supermix (Takara, Kyoto, Japan). The qPCR programme was described as follows: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, cycling reaction at 95 °C for 10 s for 40 cycles, dissolution at 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 60 s, and then 95 °C for 15 s. The sequences of the specific primers were shown in Table S3. The levels of mRNAs were expressed as 2−ΔΔCt, where Ct was cycle threshold, ΔCt = testing gene (Ct)–average GAPDH (Ct), and ΔΔCt = sample group Δ(Ct)–average control group Δ(Ct). All experiments were replicated thrice.

Bioinformatic analysis

Bioinformatic analysis was performed using Omicsmart, a real-time interactive online platform for data analysis (http://www.omicsmart.com). The differentially expressed mRNAs with fold change ≥1.28 or ≥2 and the adjusted p-value ≤0.05 were used for subsequent bioinformatic analysis. Volcano plots were generated using Rpackage ggplot2. All the differentially expressed mRNAs were mapped to GO terms in the Gene Ontology database (http://www.geneontology.org/). The R packages UpSetR and GOplot were utilised to visualise gene enrichment results. GO terms with p-value <0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Lentiviral transduction

Recombinant lentiviral particles were produced by transient co-transfection of psPAX2, pMD2.G, and pCDH-TLR4-A or pCDH-TLR4-G plasmids into HEK293T cells. After transduction for 24 h, 48 h, or 72 h, viral supernatants were collected and filtered individually. Subsequently, HTR-8/SVneo cells were infected with viral supernatant containing 8 μg/mL polybrene and then selected in the presence of 0.5 μg/mL puromycin. The pCDH-TLR4-A or -G plasmid (Table S4) was used to generate HTR-8/SVneo-A or -G cells, respectively. In these HTR-8/SVneo-A and HTR-8/SVneo-G cells, the expression levels of TLR4 were examined by RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis.

Cell transfection

Plasmid for overexpression of FOXP3 mRNA (pcDNA3.1-FOXP3) was synthesised by Sangon Biotech (sequence in Table S4). The corresponding DNA sequence was obtained from NCBI database: Gene Bank 2013. Empty pcDNA3.1 was used as negative control (vector). Si-FOXP3 and si-NC (negative control) were designed and synthesised by Sangon Biotech (Table S5). HTR-8/SVneo cells (1 × 106 cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured to 80% confluence. Transfection was performed in Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 24 h according to the manufacturer's protocols. The transfection efficiencies were validated by RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis.

Western blotting

Total soluble proteins were extracted from cells or tissues with RIPA lysis buffer (P0013B, Beyotime Biotechnology, China) containing PMSF. Membrane proteins were extracted with ProteinExt™ Mammalian Membrane Protein Extraction Kit (Transgen Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's protocols. Their concentrations were quantified using Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (23227, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Proteins were separated on 10% sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (PVDF, Millipore, Billerica, USA). PVDF membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma Aldrich, USA) at room temperature for 1 h and then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibodies included anti-TLR4 (1:1000, #38519, CST, USA, RRID: AB_2924306, exclusively for Western blotting of TLR4 protein in women villous tissues and human trophoblast cells), anti-MMP9 (1:1000, R380831, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE, China, RRID: AB_3712851), anti-RAC1 (1:1000, 66122-1-Ig, Proteintech, USA, RRID:AB_2881521), anti-β2GPI (1:1000, #389197, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE, China, RRID: AB_3712853), anti-FOXP3 (1:1000, A4953, Abclonal, RRID:AB_2863396), anti-GAPDH (1:10000, ab8245, Abcam, UK, RRID:AB_2107448), anti-Tubulin (dilution 1:10000, 66240, Proteintech, USA, RRID:AB_2881629), and anti-NA/K-ATPase A1 (1:10000, ab76020, Abcam, UK, RRID:AB_1310695). Subsequently, the PVDF membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Secondary antibodies included horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (1:10000, ab6721, Abcam, UK, RRID:AB_955447) and anti-mouse IgG (1:10000, ab6728, Abcam, UK, RRID:AB_955440). Then, the protein bands were visualised by enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL; Tanon, Shanghai, China). All experiments were replicated thrice. The intensity of each band was quantified by Image J. The value of each band density in experimental and control groups was normalised to its corresponding loading control.

Wound-healing assays and transwell assays

HTR-8/SVneo cells were incubated in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS containing 0 (control), 50, or 100 μM BPF for 24 h. Then, the equivalent amounts of BPF-exposed and living HTR-8/SVneo cells and unexposed control cells were re-seeded in a clean medium in 6-well plate. Then, the plates were scratched by a 10-μL pipette tip and washed twice with PBS to remove the detached cells. After growth for 12 h or 24 h, the migration distance was compared relative to the initial reference line. For transwell migration assays, the equivalent amounts of BPF-exposed and living HTR-8/SVneo cells and unexposed control cells were seeded in 24-well transwell chambers (Corning) and incubated for 24 h. For transwell invasion assays, 100 μL aliquot of Matrigel (BD Biosciences) diluted in RPMI 1640 media (dilution ratio 1:30) were coated on 24-well transwell chambers and were solidified at 37 °C for 1 h. Cells were plated on the top of Matrigel matrix and cultured for 24 h. The bottom chamber contained medium containing 10% FBS as chemoattractant for human trophoblast cells. After 24 h, the whole chambers were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and then stained with crystal violet for 15 min. The invaded cells on the bottom surface of membrane were visualised and photographed on Axio Observer 3 (Zeiss) and counted in five random fields.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

HTR-8/SVneo cell lysates (1 × 107 cells in 100 μL IP lysis buffer), human villous tissue lysates (60 mg in 500 μL IP lysis buffer), or mouse placental tissue lysates (60 mg in 500 μL IP lysis buffer) were incubated with anti-TLR4 (19811-1-AP, Proteintech, USA, exclusively for IP and IHC assays and Western blotting of murine Tlr4, RRID:AB_10638446) or anti-β2GPI (1:20, #389197, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE, China, RRID: AB_3712853) at 4 °C overnight on an inverse rotator, with equal weight of IgG antibody (ab172730, Abcam) as negative control. Afterwards, cell or tissue lysates were incubated with 40 μL magnetic beads A/G (B23201, Bimake, USA) at 4 °C for 4 h with gentle rotation. The amount of antibody on magnetic beads was identical but relatively less than the amount of its target protein in cell lysate in IP assays. Subsequently, the beads were washed for five times with IP lysis buffer, and then boiled at 100 °C in SDS loading buffer for 10 min. Then, the immunoprecipitated proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and detected by Western blotting using anti-TLR4 (1:1000, #38519, CST, USA, RRID: AB_2924306) and anti-β2GPI (1:1000, #389197, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE, China RRID: AB_3712853). All experiments were independently replicated thrice.

ChIP (chromatin immunoprecipitation) assay

HTR-8/SVneo cells (2 × 107) were cross-linked using 1% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature and then treated with 250 mM glycine for 5 min to quench this crosslink. Cells were lysed in cell lysis/wash buffer (150 mM NaCl, 5 mM EDTA [pH = 7.5 at 25 °C], 50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.5 at 25 °C], and 0.5% NP-40) containing protease inhibitor for 10 min on ice and then sonicated to generate DNA fragments with length in 200–500 bp in shearing buffer (1% SDS, 10 mM EDTA [pH = 8.0 at 25 °C], 50 mM Tris–HCl [pH = 8.0 at 25 °C]) containing protease inhibitor using a Diagenode BioruptorPlus sonicator (30 s on and 30 s off for 12 cycles). Then, DNA fragments were incubated with anti-FOXP3 (dilution 1:100, A4953, Abclonal, RRID:AB_2863396) or IgG as control (Invitrogen, 02–6102) overnight at 4 °C and then incubated with Dynabeads® Protein G beads at 4 °C for another 4 h on a rotating wheel. Subsequently, beads were washed for six times with cell lysis/wash buffer at 4 °C and then with cold TE buffer (Invitrogen, 12090015) twice. The crosslink was terminated by treating cells in elution buffer (100 mM NaHCO3 and 1% SDS) on a shaker at room temperature for 15 min. Then, the antibody-bound chromatin complexes were dissociated at 65 °C overnight to release free DNA fragments. Subsequently, each sample was treated with 50 ng/μL RNase A at 37 °C for 30 min and then with 10 mM Proteinase K at 45 °C for 1 h. The immunoprecipitated DNA was extracted with Min-Elute PCR purification kit (Qiagen, 28004) and was analysed by qPCR using the specific primers (Table S3).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Human villous tissue and murine placental tissue samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Sections (4 μm in thickness) were de-waxed, re-hydrated, and incubated in EDTA Antigen Retrieval Solution (Cat. C1034, Solarbio, China) for 10 min at 95 °C for antigen repair. Non-specific antigens were blocked with 5% BSA at room temperature for 1 h. Then, the sections were incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. Primary antibodies included anti-TLR4 (19811-1-AP, Proteintech, USA, exclusively for IP and IHC assays and Western blotting of murine Tlr4), anti-MMP9 (1:100, R380831, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE, China), and anti-RAC1 (1:50, ab155938, Abcam, UK). On next day, sections were washed and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. The sections were counter-stained with haematoxylin (Cat. No. H9627, Millipore, USA). After dehydration with gradient ethanol and wash with xylene, the sections were sealed with neutral gum and photographed on an Axio Imager A2 microscope (Zeiss, Germany). The relative protein staining intensity was quantified by ImageJ software and plotted.

Mouse models

Six-to-eight-week-old wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 mice (Charles River, Beijing, China) and Tlr4 knockout (KO) mice generated by CRISPR/Cas9 technique (Cyagen Biosciences, China)49,50 were raised in a filter-top cages with a 12 h light/dark cycle, autoclaved bedding, food, and water. Female mice were mated with male mice overnight. The appearance of vaginal plug was defined as day 1 (D1), which was further confirmed by monitoring their weight. These mice were randomly divided into several groups for subsequent experiments. The experiments were blinded to data analysts.

Model I. A BPF-exposed mouse model was contructed. To explore the effects of real environmental exposure dose (REED) of bisphenols on miscarriage, we calculated the actual daily intake dose of total bisphenols in human body. One study showed that the total level of all bisphenol analogues was 144 ng/mL in serum of pregnant women in Beijing, China.44 This dose could be considered as a representative of human real intake dose under an inadvertent environment. According to body surface area coefficient (mouse/human = 12), this REED of total bisphenols corresponded to 1.8 mg/kg/day BPF in mouse. It has been reported that male rats exposed to BPF (25 or 50 mg/kg/day) for continuous 28 days exhibits oxidative stress and adverse effects on testes and spermatogenesis.51 Exposure of rats to 50 mg/kg/day BPF for 28 days produces excessive DNA damage in vivo.52 Furthermore, exposure to100 mg/kg/day BPF for 22 days significantly reduces the serum testosterone levels and sperm count in cauda epididymis.53 The LD50 of BPA in mice is 841 mg/kg.54 Considering the doses used in literature, short lifespan of mice, and the feasibility of mouse model assays, in this study, we construct a BPF-exposed pregnant mouse model (n = 6 per group) by orally giving 0 (control, corn oil as solvent), 1.8 (1-fold REED of total bisphenols), 50 (medium-dose group, 28-fold REED, corresponding to 1/17 of BPA LD50), or 100 mg/kg/d BPF (high-dose group, 56-fold REED, corresponding to 1/9 of BPA LD50) from gestational day (D) 1 to D13.

Model II. A BPF-exposed mouse model with Tlr4 intervention was constructed. BPF-exposed pregnant mice were also treated with AS-Tlr4, with AS–NC as control. Pregnant mice were randomly divided into four groups (each n = 6): (1) Corn oil group, (2) 100 mg/kg/d BPF group, (3) 100 mg/kg/d BPF + AS–Nc, and (4) 100 mg/kg/d BPF + AS-Tlr4. These mice were daily given corn oil or 100 mg/kg/d BPF from D1 to D13 by oral gavage. Meanwhile, mice in groups 3–4 were also intraperitoneally injected with 1 mg/kg AS-Tlr4 (antisense oligonucleotides targeting murine Tlr4 mRNA) or AS–Nc (negative control) once per three days from D1 to D13.

Model III. A BPF-exposed mouse model with Tlr4 knockout (KO) was also constructed. Wild-type pregnant mice and pregnant mice with Tlr4 KO were daily given 100 mg/kg/d BPF from D1 to D13 by oral gavage.

All the mice were euthanised by injection with nembutal (100 mg/kg) for collection of uterus on D14, which corresponded to mid-gestation. The operation and humane endpoints complied with The ARRIVE guidelines 2.0. Mouse blood samples were collected from the angular vein on D14 using glass instruments to avoid potential BPF contamination. Serum samples were isolated within 30 min by centrifugation and were stored in aliquot at −80 °C until further use. The storage, processing, and analysis were performed using glass instruments or tubes to avoid potential BPF contamination. The embryo resorption was characterised as smaller or darker appearance compared to healthy viable and pink healthy embryos.55 Miscarriage rate in each mouse was calculated by the number of embryo resorption divided by the number of total embryos (absorbed embryo + healthy embryo). A random placenta from healthy pregnant mouse (without BPF exposure) was used as the control group. The placenta of a random absorbed embryo from BPF-exposed mouse was used as the exposed miscarriage group. These placental tissues were collected and used for WB, IHC, and HE experiments. The research protocols were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Eighth Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University.

Molecular docking

DynaMut was used to analyse the thermodynamic stability of protein. The Asp at position 299 of TLR4_HUMAN (UniProt ID: O00206) was mutated to Gly to generate TLR4-G protein. The vibrational entropy energy (ΔΔSVib ENCoM) was calculated using the ENCoM (Empirical Normal-Coordinate Analysis Method).56 The APBS plugin in PyMOL software was used to generate protein electrostatic potential–surface maps. In the electrostatic potential maps, the blue areas represented positive charges and red areas represented negative charges. The total protein hydrophobicity was calculated and coloured based on the hydrophobicity of every amino acid residue, with red indicating strong hydrophobicity and white indicating weak hydrophobicity. The protein structure of TLR4-A (UniProt ID: O00206) was predicted by AlphaFold and the structure of TLR4-G was from protein PDB 4G8A. The Chain A (residues from 227 to 351) of TLR4 protein, which encompassed two LRR (leucine rich repeats) domains surrounding the SNP site, was truncated as the segment of TLR4 and the Chain A (residues from 140 to 202) from PDB 6V06, which included the Sushi 3 domain, was truncated as the segment of β2GPI for prediction of their protein interactions. HDOCK was used for protein–protein docking with appropriate docking parameters. The docking results were assessed by the docking score and confidence score. A comprehensive description and systematic analysis of the binding interface of protein–protein complex were conducted using PyMOL to supplement interaction details, resulting in a 3D interaction diagram. BPF 3D structure was made using ChemDraw (20.0). Protein 3D structures and the amino acid residues involved in the interactions among proteins and BPF were analysed by PyMOL v2.5.4 and PLIP tool. The binding free energy (ΔG, in kcal/mol) is calculated using gmx_MMPBSA v1.6.1 with the Single Trajectory (ST) method.

Molecular dynamics

The binding stability of complex was analysed by MD simulation using GROMACS v2020.6. The Amber03 force field was utilised to generate parameters and topology files for proteins. Periodic boundary condition (PBC) was set, and the simulation box dimensions were optimised. The protein was placed at the centre of a cubic box with a minimum distance of 1.0 nm to the box edges, and the box was filled with water molecules (with TIP 3-point as template). To maintain system neutrality, solvent water molecules were partially replaced with Na+ or Cl− ion at a concentration of 0.15 M. The entire system's energy was minimised using the steepest descent minimisation method to ensure the reasonable initial geometric configuration and solvent molecule orientation. To initiate actual dynamic simulations, pre-equilibration of solvent and ions around protein was performed to achieve a stable thermodynamic ensemble under desired conditions. The pre-equilibration comprised two stages: to stabilise the system's temperature using an NVT system at 300 K for 100 ps and to stabilise the system's pressure using an NPT system at 1 bar for 100 ps. A leapfrog algorithm was employed for dynamic integration equilibration, and all MD simulations were conducted under isothermal-isobaric conditions at 300 K and 1 bar for 100 ns. Subsequently, trajectory correction was performed with the protein as the centre to conduct Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) analysis, which reflected the structural differences between the simulated and the initial structure as the reference. A smaller RMSD indicated greater similarity between the simulated and reference structures. Before RMSD calculation, structures were superimposed onto the reference structure using the least squares method to eliminate overall system motion. The Gibbs free-energy changes in these complexes were computed using the Molecular Mechanics Poisson-Boltzmann Surface Area (MMPBSA) method implemented in the g_mmpbsa tool.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were replicated thrice independently with similar results. All schematic diagrams and graphical abstracts were generated using Biorender. The measured or calculated data (including control group) were presented as mean ± SD (standard deviation). SPSS26.0 was used to analyse the data, and GraphPad Prism8.0 was used for figure generation. In cellular assays, the number n = 3 indicated that all experiments were replicated thrice independently. In animal model, the number n = 6 indicated 6 mice in each group. In villous tissue assays, n meant the number of HC or UM samples. Levene's test was used to evaluate the homogeneity of variance of the data. Student's t-test was used to compare differences between two groups. ANOVA was used to compare differences among multiple groups. The Pearson chi-square test was employed for comparing categorical variables in various clinical parameters. The linear correlation analysis of the relative expression levels was performed using Pearson analysis. The odds ratios (ORs) of each variable and their 95% confidence interval (95% CI) were analysed by univariate logistic regression analysis before adjusting potential confounders and by multivariate logistic regression analysis after adjusting potential confounders. The interactive effects were evaluated by derived measures of biological interactions, including analysis of relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI >0), attributable proportion due to interaction (AP > 0), and synergy index (S ≠ 1).57 These women were divided into four sub-groups based on their transcriptypes and BPF exposure levels (Table 3): group 1, women carrying A/G + G transcriptype and low BPF exposure (<0.93 μg/L, the median of total BPF levels in all women urine samples), which was also considered as reference group); group 2, women carrying A transcriptype and low BPF exposure; group 3, women carrying A/G + G transcriptype and high BPF exposure; group 4, women carrying A transcriptype and high BPF exposure. The difference was considered to be statistically significant when p value < 0.05.

TLR4-A transcriptype was associated with women unexplained miscarriage

To screen which genes might involve in BPF-induced miscarriage, UM vs HC villous tissues, as well as 50 vs 0 μM BPF-exposed trophoblast HTR-8/SVneo cells, were used for high-throughput transcriptome sequencing. Human trophoblast cells play crucial roles in maintenance of female healthy pregnancy and HTR-8/SVneo cells have been widely used as cell model in various miscarriage studies.19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24 In sequencing data of UM vs HC groups, 7158 genes were down-regulated and 1621 genes were up-regulated with fold change >1.28 and q-value <0.05 (Fig. S2A). Section II. Cellular functions: BPF exposure and TLR4 rs4986790 interactively regulated human trophoblast cell migration/invasion

Subsequently, we explored the mechanisms how TLR4-A suppressed migration/invasion relative to TLR4-G. GO analysis of the DEGs between HTR-8/SVneo-A and HTR-8/SVneo-G cells showed that plasma membrane and receptor binding were significantly regulated (Fig. S5H–I). It has been reported that TLR4 interact with β2GPI in cell membrane to affect its downstream cell migration in vascular smooth muscle cells.59,60 This inspired us to explore whether TLR4-A or TLR4-G might interact with β2GPI differently and thus affect migration/invasion differentially. Firstly, we investigated the potential structural differences of TLR4-A and TLR4-G based on molecular dynamic modelling. We found that the alteration of nucleotide A at 896 to G (corresponding to the amino acid residue D at 299 to G) disrupted α-helix of TLR4, increased molecular flexibility, and reduced protein stability (Fig. S6A). In details, in TLR4-A protein, D299 formed H-bonds with I301 and D302 with H-bond distance of 2.9 Å and 3.2 Å, respectively, giving an α-helix structure in residues from 300 to 302 (Fig. S6A). However, the absence of H-bonds in TLR4-G protein resulted in the formation of a loop structure in 300–302 region. Moreover, this alteration also produced different polarity, hydrophobicity, and hydrophilicity around this SNP site (Fig. S6B–C). Furthermore, molecular docking also showed that the binding energy of β2GPI with TLR4-A (−31.32 kcal/mol) was obviously lower than that with TLR4-G (−26.13 kcal/mol) (Fig. S6D–E). In details, there were three pairs of hydrophobic interactions and nine pairs of hydrogen bonds between β2GPI and TLR4-A and two pairs of hydrophobic interactions, ten pairs of hydrogen bonds, and two pairs of salt bridges between β2GPI and TLR4-G (Fig. S6D–E). The docking score between β2GPI and TLR4-A or TLR4-G was −176.6 or −163.47, respectively (Fig. S6D–E), indicating that β2GPI and TLR4-A had higher binding stability relative to that with TLR4-G. The confidence score was reduced from 0.6299 for β2GPI-TLR4-A to 0.5669 for β2GPI-TLR4-G complex (Fig. S6D–E), showing that β2GPI and TLR4-A had higher binding stability than that with TLR4-G. Experimentally, IP assays using an identical but limited amount of TLR4 antibody showed that TLR4-A exhibited higher β2GPI enrichment than TLR4-G (Fig. 4H); in contrast, IP assays using an identical but limited amount of β2GPI antibody also showed that β2GPI pulled down more TLR4-A than TLR4-G protein (Fig. 4I), indicating that β2GPI was more prone to interaction with TLR4-A rather than with TLR4-G. Furthermore, the enhanced protein interactions reduced the protein levels of MMP9 and RAC1 in HTR-8/SVneo-A vs HTR-8/SVneo-G cells (Fig. 4D). Collectively, these results indicated that, relative to TLR4-G, TLR4-A was more prone to interaction with β2GPI protein and thus suppressed its downstream migration/invasion in human trophoblast cells.

Section III. Underlying mechanisms: BPF exposure and TLR4 rs4986790 interactively regulated TLR4 and β2GPI protein interactions

The effects of the interactions of BPF-TLR4 SNP on trophoblast cell migration/invasion

Subsequently, we explored their potential regulatory mechanisms. Firstly, we analysed whether BPF might directly interact with TLR4. Molecular docking showed that BPF could interact with TLR4-A or TLR4-G, giving total binding energy of −5.2 or −5.8 kcal/mol, respectively (Fig. 6A–B), showing a slightly stronger binding affinity between BPF and TLR4-G compared to TLR4-A. Secondly, we further explored whether BPF exposure might affect the protein interactions between TLR4 and β2GPI in trophoblast cells. Analysed by molecular docking, RMSD (Root-mean-square analysis) curves showed that the equilibrium time of β2GPI-TLR4-A complex was 2–5 ns, much shorter than 22–25 ns for β2GPI-TLR4-G complex (Fig. 6C), showing that β2GPI-TLR4-A complex was more stable than β2GPI-TLR4-G complex. After binding with BPF, the amplitude of RMSD was a little reduced for β2GPI-TLR4-A complex but was obviously increased for β2GPI-TLR4-G complex, indicating that BPF increased the stability of β2GPI-TLR4-A complex but reduced the stability of β2GPI-TLR4-G complex. Discussion

BPF exposure induces miscarriage by up-regulating TLR4

Bisphenols have endocrine disruptor properties and have shown reproductive toxicity.43,61 Among various bisphenols, BPF has the highest detectable content.26 Emerging studies have shown the adverse effects of BPF exposure on male reproduction or female uterine development using animal models.21,62,63 However, whether BPF exposure might induce women miscarriage has not been well characterised. In this study, we discover that BPF exposure is closely associated with unexplained miscarriage by analysing UM and HC women urinary BPF levels. BPF-exposed mouse model also confirms that BPF exposure induces mouse miscarriage. Subsequently, cellular assays discover the underlying mechanisms that BPF exposure suppresses trophoblast cell migration/invasion by down-regulating the protein levels of MMP9 and RAC1, key proteins for cell migration/invasion.38,39 Therefore, we discover that BPF exposure suppresses migration/invasion to induce unexplained miscarriage.

TLR4 has been widely known to regulate innate and acquired immune responses.64,65 Notably, TLR4 also interacts with β2GPI, leading to the activation of P38/MAPK signalling pathway66; and this pathway is negatively associated with trophoblast cell migration/invasion.67 However, whether TLR4 might regulate miscarriage, especially with BPF exposure, remains completely unexplored. In this study, as expected, we find that TLR4 is highly expressed in UM vs HC villous tissues and in placental tissues of BPF-exposed mouse miscarriage model, strongly supporting that TLR4 contributes to miscarriage. In mouse model, knockdown of Tlr4 could efficiently reduce mouse miscarriage rates. Meanwhile, in a separate cohort study, TLR4 expression levels are also higher in decidual tissues at the maternal–foetal interface of patients with unexplained miscarriage relative to those in healthy control group,68 supporting our findings. Similarly, a recent study has also shown that baicalein prevents miscarriage by enhancing Treg function and promoting decidualization via TLR4/NF-κB pathway inhibition, highlighting TLR4 as a potential therapeutic target for miscarriage.69 Another study shows that TLR4 deficiency impedes Toxoplasma gondii excreted-secreted antigens (ESA)-induced miscarriage in a mouse model,70 highlighting the broader role of TLR4 in immune–environmental interactions at the maternal–foetal interface. Collectively, all these results confirmed that TLR4 could induce miscarriage.

In this study, we focus on the effects of TLR4 on trophoblast cell migration/invasion. In mechanism, TLR4 interacts with β2GPI to down-regulate the protein levels of MMP9 and RAC1, two key proteins for migration/invasion, and thus suppresses trophoblast cell migration/invasion. In addition to the effects of SNPs on protein–protein interactions as identified in this study, SNPs might also affect cellular functions through other approaches, such as promoter variants,71,72 CpG methylation modifications,73 or non-coding RNAs like lncRNAs,33 which should also be further investigated. Moreover, it is worth noting that TLR4 might induce miscarriage possibly by increasing inflammation, since TLR4 could regulate innate and acquired immune responses.65 Recently, we have found that environmental BPDE exposure increases the levels of inflammation (i.e. the levels of IL-1B) in human trophoblast cells, which acts as upstream modulator to suppress trophoblast cell migration/invasion to induce miscarriage.74 Therefore, inflammation might be another important downstream pathway of TLR4. In addition to TLR4's functions in trophoblast cells, TLR4 may also regulate earlier reproductive events such as fertilisation and embryo implantation possibly through inflammatory signalling and immune cell recruitment,75 which should also be studied.

Both epidemiological analysis and experimental assays confirm that BPF exposure and TLR4 rs4986790 interactively govern unexplained miscarriage

Increasing cross-sectional and epidemiological studies have shown that gene–environment interactions are associated with various human diseases, such as Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, cancers et al.30,31,57,76,77 For example, human with G2385R, R1628P, and S1647T variants who were non-caffeine drinkers had significantly higher risk of Parkinson's disease compared to wild-type carriers who were caffeine drinkers, suggesting that caffeine intake and gene variation jointly regulate Parkinson's disease.57 Individuals with the GA + AA genotypes of CYP17A1 rs743572 and high BPA exposure have a 2.49-fold higher risk of breast cancer compared to those with the GG genotype and low BPA exposure,31 highlighting the role of environmental toxicants in modifying genetic susceptibility. Moreover, smokers with rs75932628-CT/TT genotype have higher late-onset Alzheimer's disease risk compared to non-smokers with rs75932628-CC genotype (OR = 2.73, 95% CI, 1.72–3.79).78 These results are consistent with our partial findings. However, experimental evidence and mechanism exploration remain largely rare. As for unexplained miscarriage, due to its complexity and multifactorial nature, no cohorts are currently available to explore the association between gene–environment interactions and its occurrence frequency. In this study, we find the association and causation that the interactions between environment BPF exposure and TLR4 rs4986790 interactively govern unexplained miscarriage by comprehensive epidemiological analysis, mouse model, and cellular mechanism studies.

In SNP-related databases, TLR4 rs4986790 has been implicated in multiple autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, such as ankylosing spondylitis, psoriasis, ulcerative colitis et al.79, 80, 81 In this study, we identify that TLR4-A is more prone to miscarriage compared with TLR4-G. This might be agreed with high miscarriage rates (15%–25%) in pregnant women.3,5 This is also consistent with another finding that women carrying TLR4-A allele have a higher risk of APS (characterised by miscarriage and thrombotic events) than those carrying TLR4-G in an APS cohort.15 However, another study reports the contradictory results that Uygur women carrying TLR4-G have higher miscarriage rates than those carrying TLR4-A but Han women with TLR4-A or TLR4-G do not show significant difference in miscarriage rates.16 The differences in these findings may be originated from several factors. Firstly, different genetic backgrounds between populations, such as differences in allele frequency, and different environmental exposure, might jointly affect the roles of TLR4 SNP in miscarriage. Secondly, this study was conducted in Xinjiang, China; and the regional differences and unique women characteristics might also affect the roles of TLR4 SNP in miscarriage. Thirdly, in addition to rs4986790, other SNPs in TLR4 or other miscarriage-related genes containing SNPs might also be associated with miscarriage risk in an environment-SNP interactive manner. Therefore, it is valuable to further explore multiple SNP loci or use polygenic risk scores to understand the interactions between genetic SNPs and environmental exposures in miscarriage or other human diseases. Therefore, these studies further indicate that gene–environment interaction investigation should consider the genetic diversity, environmental exposure, multiple SNP loci, and regional characteristics.

Both BPF exposure and TLR4 rs4986790 interactively govern miscarriage. Women carrying TLR4-A are more sensitive to BPF exposure and thus have higher miscarriage risk than those carrying TLR4-G. Experimentally, migration/invasion of trophoblast cells are obviously suppressed in HTR-8/SVneo-A cells relative to HTR-8/SVneo-G cells. Moreover, BPF exposure more severely suppresses migration/invasion of HTR-8/SVneo-A cells relative to that of HTR-8/SVneo-G cells. Collectively, BPF exposure and TLR4-A/G interactively govern trophoblast cell migration/invasion and unexplained miscarriage.

The proposed mechanisms

In human trophoblast cells, β2GPI stronger interacts with TLR4-A but rarely with TLR4-G (Fig. 9F). Mutation of amino acid residue D to G at 299 position of TLR4 might disrupt α-helical and β-strand structures in the extracellular domain of TLR4,82 which might impair its interaction with β2GPI. Dynamic simulation and IP assays all confirm that the interactions of β2GPI with TLR4-A are much stronger than that with TLR4-G. The stronger interactions of β2GPI with TLR4-A more severely suppresses the expression levels of its downstream migration/invasion proteins, such as MMP9 and RAC1, and therefore suppresses migration/invasion in HTR-8/SVneo-A cells, which might increase the miscarriage risk in women carrying TLR4-A transcriptype relative to women carrying TLR4-G. With BPF exposure, FOXP3 acts as a transcription factor of TLR4, and BPF exposure promotes FOXP3-mediated TLR4 transcription and thus up-regulates its expression levels in human trophoblast cells. Meanwhile, BPF exposure also up-regulates the protein levels of β2GPI in cell membrane. Therefore, BPF exposure enhances the protein interactions between β2GPI and TLR4-A by up-regulating their protein levels in women carrying TLR4-A but less affect the interactions between β2GPI and TLR4-G in women carrying TLR4-G. This leads to a severe suppression of its downstream migration/invasion, ultimately increasing the miscarriage risk for women carrying TLR4-A transcriptype but less for those carrying TLR4-G transcriptype after BPF exposure. Therefore, the differential binding of β2GPI with TLR4-A or TLR4-G could well explain the distinct risk of miscarriage and migration/invasion for women carrying TLR4-A or TLR4-G transcriptype. Collectively, we experimentally demonstrate that BPF exposure and TLR4-A/G interactively govern the migration/invasion of trophoblast cells and the occurrence of unexplained miscarriage, possibly explaining the heterogeneity of the GWAS studies of a specific disease in different cohorts.

Limitations and prospects

Although the statistical results are consistent with the mouse model assays and cellular assays, the case–control study has several limitations. First, although we consider some confounders in this study, other confounders might be ignored. Second, although we classify some confounders based on literature, the possibility of misclassifications might not be completely avoided. Third, some information is collected from medical records by self-report, some factors (e.g., recall bias) might affect the data reliability. Fourth, although we find that BPF levels are higher in UM vs HC urine samples, we use single-spot of urine samples, which might have potential misclassification bias if the BPF levels vary greatly over time due to changed external exposures, daily activities, or physiological status.

In this study, we use a short-term and high-dose BPF-exposed mouse miscarriage model to predict the adverse effects of long-term and low-dose BPF exposure on women miscarriage. Due to the ethical limitations, till now, this kind of animal model is still the best choice for prediction of human diseases, not only in this study, but also in other studies, such as BaP exposure for miscarriage,19,20,83 nanoplastics for miscarriage,21,22 disinfection by-products of tap water for miscarriage,24 caffeine-induced cardiovascular disease,84 PM2.5-induced lung cancer,85 and polystyrene nanoplastics-induced Parkinson's disease.86 However, it is still challenge for direct translation of these findings to real-world human long-term and low-dose exposure. It requires ongoing research and a multidisciplinary approach to better understand and bridge the gap between experimental animal data and human health outcomes.

Furthermore, the women number in this case–control group should be increased for better statistical analysis. The differences in the protein interactions of β2GPI with TLR4-A or TLR4-G should be further investigated. In general, the correlations between a gene and a disease are very complicated, which are largely decided by both gene–gene interactions and gene–environment interactions. In addition to TLR4, other genes also regulate human healthy pregnancy. It is great significance to further explore the roles of other genes in unexplained miscarriage. Moreover, the mechanism how BPF increases β2GPI membrane abundance should be explored, including using knockout mouse models. Additionally, it is crucial to study whether and how the foetus sex might influence placental functions and whether there is sex-specific miscarriage risk with BPF exposure. Given the structural similarities and co-existence of various bisphenol analogues, the real co-exposure to BPF and other bisphenol analogues might produce additive or synergistic effects on human healthy, which should also be investigated. The investigation on gene–environment interactions is still very preliminary and should be further investigated using various animal models with gene knock-out or knock-in. For pregnant women with different SNPs, more precision prevention strategy is required for treatment against unexplained miscarriage. Preconception exposure for miscarriage prediction is highly meaningful.87 The potential toxicity of BPF exposure before pregnancy on miscarriage should be further investigated.

In summary, we provide the experimental evidence that environment BPF exposure and TLR4 SNP interactively govern trophoblast cell migration/invasion and unexplained miscarriage. Functionally, migration/invasion of TLR4-A trophoblast cells are suppressed more severely with BPF exposure relative to TLR4-G cells. Mechanistically, BPF exposure enhances β2GPI interactions with TLR4-A but less affects those with TLR4-G, thereby suppresses migration/invasion protein levels in TLR4-A cells but less affects them in TLR4-G cells. This study provides new insights for further studying environment–gene interactions and various human diseases and also provides new insights in personalised and precise treatment against diseases.
