The story of a Taiwanese female student studying dentistry in Japan during the Japanese colonial period: An oral history by Dr. Yueh-E Wu
Authors: Feng-Chou Cheng, Mei-Huei Li, Chien-Chi Chu, Chun-Pin Chiang
Published: 2026

Modern medicine was introduced to Taiwan during the Japanese colonial period (1895–1945). Under the framework of what has been termed “hygiene colonization”, a systematic medical infrastructure was established, and the Taiwan Government Medical School was set up to train local physicians. While women were initially confined to the roles such as midwives and nurses, it is noteworthy that, despite the colonial education system not explicitly facilitating women’s entry into clinical professions, the strong emphasis on physiological hygiene in school curricula inadvertently fostered their professional aspirations. By the 1920s and 1930s, amid increasingly liberal social attitudes and the growing trend of overseas education, female students, exemplified by Dr. A-Hsin Tsai, began pursuing medical studies in Japan. In doing so, they challenged the prevailing gender dichotomy of “male physician, female nurse” and emerged as the first officially registered Taiwanese female physicians.1,2

An early Shōwa-era photograph, discovered in an antique market in Taiwan, depicts four young women standing before the exterior wall of the Toyo Girls’ Dental College Affiliated Hospital. Behind them, a hospital sign clearly outlines the departmental divisions and service hours per day (Fig. 1), suggesting that the photograph was likely brought back by a Taiwanese female student who had studied dentistry in Japan. Previous research has identified Dr. Hsiu-Yu Kuo as the first officially registered Taiwanese female dentist, who also graduated from the Toyo Girls’ Dental College.3 However, limited scholarship has examined the motivations behind Taiwanese female students’ decisions to pursue dental education in Japan or the nature of their academic experiences. Motivated by this visual artifact, the present study draws upon the oral history of Dr. Yueh-E Wu, a Taiwanese female dentist and graduate of the same institution. Originally recorded in Taiwanese,4 this oral narrative is analyzed to reconstruct her educational journey. From Tamsui to Tokyo, Dr. Wu’s story offers a compelling account of a Taiwanese woman’s pursuit of professional identity through dental education.

In 1940, a young woman who had just graduated from the Tamsui Girls’ High School had never envisioned leaving her hometown. However, upon encountering a recruitment poster from Tokyo displayed on the school bulletin board, she, for the first time, began to envision a future as a clinician. With the support of her family, she applied to a medical school in Tokyo. Despite meeting the academic qualifications, her application was ultimately rejected due to institutional constraints and quota allocations. Rather than discouraging her, this setback became a turning point. Encouraged by her teachers and accompanied by friends, she made the bold decision to travel to Tokyo independently in search of opportunities. On the day of her departure from Keelung Harbor, she bid farewell to her family with tears welling in her eyes, yet remained silent. During the voyage, she endured severe seasickness while also witnessing the breathtaking scenery of the Seto Inland Sea, her emotions oscillating between anxiety and awe. Upon arriving in Japan, the unfamiliar yet vibrant urban landscape, the allure of modern entertainment, and the dynamic cityscape offered her a profound realization of the vastness of the world.

When determining her future path, she recalled encountering a female dentist during her childhood, whose professionalism and elegance left a lasting impression. Inspired by this memory, she decided to pursue dentistry and was subsequently admitted to the Toyo Girls’ Dental College. Upon enrollment, she encountered a markedly different academic environment. Students from across Japan and overseas gathered in large lecture halls, with over two hundred new students, including many from Taiwan, China, and Korea. The institution was exceptionally well-equipped, ranging from anatomy laboratories to clinical training facilities with hundreds of dental units, reflecting the rigor and sophistication of modern medical education. Dental training demanded not only theoretical knowledge but also intensive hands-on practice. She was required to fabricate dentures and metal prostheses manually, as well as acquire skills in metal casting and precision machining. These tasks proved highly challenging, often requiring her to practice late into the night. Nevertheless, through this demanding process, she gradually cultivated both technical expertise and professional perseverance.

Beyond her academic pursuits, she also forged meaningful interpersonal relationships in a foreign environment. Taiwanese students provided mutual support, while interactions with peers from Japan and other countries facilitated cross-cultural exchange. One classmate brought joy through piano performances. However, her premature death due to illness impressed upon Dr. Wu the fragility of life. Dormitory life itself constituted a form of personal training. From initially struggling to adapt to Japanese cuisine to eventually developing an appreciation for miso soup, and from fearing underground passageways lined with anatomical specimens to confronting them with composure, these incremental changes collectively contributed to her personal growth. In the vast and unfamiliar metropolis of Tokyo, she not only acquired professional dental knowledge but also learned to adapt, confront her fears, and persist in the face of uncertainty.

During the Japanese colonial period, Taiwanese female students who pursued medical or dental education in Japan typically enrolled in women’s medical or dental colleges in Tokyo.5 Although many of these students came from relatively affluent backgrounds, they were widely regarded as representatives of the emerging “new woman”, navigating both academic challenges and complex processes of cultural adaptation. In summary, the development of modern medical education during this period played a crucial role in cultivating Taiwanese medical professionals.1,2 While institutional constraints initially limited women’s access to medical qualifications, the expansion of educational opportunities and shifting societal attitudes enabled Taiwanese women to seek overseas pathways for professional advancement. In doing so, they not only expanded their own career horizons but also contributed to dismantling entrenched gender barriers within the medical field.
