Development and usability testing of a health recommender system for symptom management in women with breast cancer who were receiving or had recently received chemotherapy
Authors: Tingting Cai, Jialin Chen, Yiwen Duan, Anyue Dai, Ling Wang, Changrong Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveBoth patients with breast cancer and health care providers face ongoing challenges in managing physical and psychological symptoms. This study aimed to develop a health recommender system (HRS) for symptom management of women with breast cancer who were receiving or had recently received chemotherapy and evaluate its usability.MethodsConceptually informed by the Digital Intelligent Precise Nursing Framework, the Breast Health Recommender App (BHRA) was developed for breast cancer symptom management. This development involved qualitative interviews with patients with breast cancer and health care providers to establish a comprehensive knowledge base. Recommender rules were formulated based on insights from a multicenter, cross-sectional study using deep neural network (DNN) analyses. A multidisciplinary research team employed a user-centered design methodology to develop the app. A preliminary usability assessment was then conducted to assess the app's preliminary acceptability and perceived usefulness.ResultsThe BHRA comprises three user-facing modules—user profile management, symptom assessment, and individualized symptom management—and one back-end DNN classifier module. Its knowledge base is divided into a common module and a personalized recommendation module. Following iterative optimization and hyperparameter tuning, the final classifier employed four hidden layers with an initial learning rate of 0.10 and demonstrated robust multiclass classification performance, with excellent discrimination and overall predictive accuracy across the three symptom profile groups. Usability testing with 10 patients generated predominantly positive feedback, and participants suggested enhancements in the app's information presentation, instructions, structure, and interactive feedback capabilities.ConclusionsThe BHRA demonstrated preliminary promise as a supportive tool for individualized symptom self-management among women with breast cancer who were receiving or had recently received chemotherapy. Further controlled or longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate its clinical effectiveness and real-world implementation.Keywords: Chemotherapy, Breast cancer, Symptom, Health recommender system

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the most common cancers among women,1 and chemotherapy remains a mainstay treatment for many patients.2 Nevertheless, women with breast cancer undergoing chemotherapy often experience multiple physical and psychological symptoms, such as fatigue, sleep disturbance, hair loss, nausea, and vomiting,2,3 which may adversely affect treatment, recovery, and overall health outcomes.4,5 Recent evidence suggests that, among women with nonmetastatic breast cancer receiving chemotherapy, dose delays, dose reductions, and early treatment discontinuation occurred in 38%, 38%, and 23% of patients, respectively, with treatment-related toxicities being the predominant reasons for these modifications.5 These findings highlight the need for symptom-focused care and early intervention to support patients in completing planned chemotherapy. However, the severity and frequency of these symptoms vary according to patient demographics, chemotherapy protocols, and disease status, creating substantial challenges for symptom management.2,6 As hospital stays have shortened and more symptom management has shifted to the outpatient setting, patients increasingly need timely, tailored support to manage symptoms outside the hospital, while health care providers, especially outpatient nurses, often have limited capacity to meet these individualized needs.

Patients with breast cancer frequently turn to the Internet for information to alleviate their symptoms.7 Yet, the large volume of online health information and the mismatch between available digital resources and patients’ preferences may make it difficult for patients to identify accurate, relevant, and personally useful information.8 While there are numerous digital tools and mobile applications designed to support patients with breast cancer, existing interventions vary considerably in content and functions, and many still provide relatively generic rather than truly personalized support.9,10 In practice, patients with different characteristics, such as age, symptom burden, and treatment conditions, often have different information and support needs.11 Consequently, there is a pressing need for programs that facilitate symptom self-management by providing personalized information based on individual patient characteristics.

Mobile health technology presents an important opportunity to enhance patient engagement and self-management in cancer care.12 At the same time, the growing volume of online health information has increased the risk of information overload, making recommender systems a useful strategy for filtering large amounts of information and delivering content that is more relevant to users’ needs.13 In the health care context, HRSs can further offer targeted, intelligent, and personalized information retrieval, helping patients access higher-quality and more relevant information, thus potentially reducing misinformed decisions.14 Evidence indicates that HRSs empower patients, enabling them to actively participate in their health care decisions, adhere to medical advice, and engage more effectively in health-promoting behaviors.14

A systematic review showed that existing HRSs mainly focus on lifestyle, nutrition, general health care information, and specific health conditions.13 Deep learning–based models have the potential to capture complex, non-linear relationships in multidimensional health data.15 However, evidence on recommender systems specifically designed for cancer symptom management remains limited. Related reviews of breast cancer mHealth interventions suggest that mobile app-based tools may support chemotherapy-related symptom monitoring and self-management, but available interventions remain heterogeneous.9,10 Therefore, an HRS tailored to this population may provide a promising approach to delivering personalized symptom self-management support. Against this background, this study aimed to develop a mobile application specifically designed for patients with breast cancer who were receiving or had recently received chemotherapy and to evaluate its usability as a means of delivering individualized, expert-informed symptom management support.

Methods

Theoretical framework of BHRA development

The development and reporting of the BHRA were retrospectively organized and conceptually interpreted according to the Digital Intelligent Precise Nursing Framework,16 which consisted of three core components—multidimensional data, a solution bank, and recommendations linked by an inferential mechanism (Fig. 1). Multidimensional data encompassed information collected through various sensing and information modalities, including different data types and sources. The solution bank consisted of structured educational resources and digital supportive solutions. The inferential mechanism integrated machine-learning models and rule-based logic to match patient data with appropriate solutions and to generate tailored recommendations, which could include supportive measures, action plans, and individualized self-management suggestions. User feedback was incorporated into the system to iteratively optimize data features, solution content, and inference rules. The development of the BHRA was conceptually informed by this framework. Specifically, the framework provided a conceptual basis for identifying relevant patient data, constructing a symptom management knowledge base, and implementing an inferential mechanism to generate tailored recommendations.

BHRA development procedure

The BHRA development procedure consisted of six related components: patient needs identification, incorporation of prior symptom profiles, knowledge base construction, development of the DNN classifier and recommendation rules, BHRA interface and workflow development, and usability testing. The relationship between the previous multicenter study and the present BHRA study is summarized in Fig. 2. Specifically, the DNN classifier for symptom profile classification was developed between August 2020 and December 2021, followed by expert consultation from September to December 2022, interface and workflow development from June to December 2023, and usability testing from January to March 2024.

Research flow linking the previous study and the present BHRA study. BHRA, Breast Health Recommender App.

Step 1: Patient needs identification.

Employing a purposeful sampling approach, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 14 patients with breast cancer undergoing chemotherapy at a tertiary hospital in Shanghai. Open-ended questions focused on symptoms experienced during chemotherapy and preferred forms of symptom management during home stays. Key themes identified from the interviews included: (1) early symptoms during chemotherapy; (2) dietary issues after chemotherapy; (3) medication-related symptoms; and (4) challenges in managing symptoms outside the hospital setting. Patients expressed a preference for diverse information formats, such as videos, pictures, and real-life patient stories. These findings were subsequently incorporated into the development of the knowledge base.

Step 2: Incorporation of prior symptom profiles.

Symptom profiles identified in our previous multicenter study were incorporated into the current BHRA development. Briefly, the previous study was a cross-sectional, multicenter survey conducted across five tertiary hospitals in Mainland China among 1180 women with breast cancer who were receiving or had recently received chemotherapy.6 Latent class analysis identified three distinct symptom and functional profiles: a low symptom burden and low function group, a moderate symptom burden and function group, and a low symptom burden and high function group. Based on this classification, key predictors of symptom burden were identified, and patient profiles were constructed to support individualized recommendation strategies, as detailed in our prior report. In the present study, these prior symptom profiles were used as the basis for patient stratification and were incorporated as the output layer in the DNN structure of the BHRA. Their key symptoms, functions, and clinical characteristics are visually summarized in the word clouds shown in Fig. 3.

Symptom profiles of women with breast cancer receiving chemotherapy. Step 3: Knowledge base construction.

To construct the knowledge base, we conducted interviews with eight health care providers specializing in breast cancer symptom management from a tertiary hospital in Shanghai. The expert panel included one clinician, three nurses, and four nursing researchers. Building upon the initial knowledge base derived from patient interviews, the research team gathered information on local symptom management practices. The interview guide covered three topics: (1) symptom management measures that should be added to the knowledge base; (2) potential barriers to implementing these measures and possible solutions; and (3) recommender rules for matching patient profiles with appropriate knowledge-base content. Based on these discussions, the experts refined the knowledge base, addressed anticipated implementation challenges, and finalized the recommender rules until a consensus was achieved.

Step 4: Development and evaluation of the DNN classifier.

A DNN classifier was developed and trained using Python 3.7 and the Keras package. Input data, including patients’ sociodemographic information, disease characteristics, and symptom reports, were preprocessed before model input. Continuous variables were standardized using z-score standardization, and categorical variables were transformed using one-hot encoding. The output layer corresponded to the three symptom profiles identified in our prior work.6 The dataset was divided into training and testing subsets, with 90% used for model training and 10% for testing. The initial model was subsequently refined by optimizing the number of hidden layers, the number of neurons in each layer, the activation functions, and the learning rate. Sparse categorical cross-entropy was used as the loss function, and the Adam optimizer was applied for model training. Deep learning has been widely applied to health care data to model complex, non-linear relationships in prediction tasks.15,17 Therefore, a DNN classifier was used to assign patients to predefined symptom profiles. Model performance was evaluated using multiclass receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC).18 Macro-average and micro-average ROC curves were generated to assess overall classification performance.19

Step 5: BHRA architecture and workflow.

The BHRA was developed by integrating patient profiles, predictive factors, and a symptom management knowledge base. The system comprised three user-facing modules and one back-end DNN classifier module: (1) user profile management, which stored patients’ sociodemographic and disease-related information; (2) symptom assessment, which collected patient-reported symptom data; (3) individualized symptom management, which delivered tailored recommendations via a WeChat mini-program interface; and (4) a back-end DNN classifier, which assigned patients to predefined symptom profiles based on input features. The system operated as follows. After patient data were entered and symptom profiles were identified by the DNN classifier, a rule-based matching process, informed by association rules20 and expert consensus, was used to connect patient profiles and symptom domain scores with corresponding recommendation content from the knowledge base. The tailored recommendations were subsequently delivered to the user through the WeChat mini–program interface.

Usability testing of BHRA

Target users

Women with breast cancer were recruited from tertiary hospitals in Shanghai through purposeful sampling between February and March 2024. Eligibility criteria were generally consistent with those used in the multicenter survey: participants were aged 18 years or older, had a breast cancer diagnosis, were informed about their disease, were receiving or had recently received chemotherapy, owned and could use a smartphone, were capable of using WeChat, and were proficient in reading and speaking Chinese. Exclusion criteria included patients who were illiterate, terminally ill, suffering from disorders of consciousness, mental illnesses, or communication disorders.

Data collection

Throughout the development process, a multidisciplinary team undertook the usability testing of the BHRA. The target users participated in a summative usability evaluation, which involved interviews focused on their experiences using the BHRA and suggestions for its improvement. The main aspects explored in the interviews included the alignment between the BHRA's recommended content and the users' expectations, the BHRA's shortcomings as a personalized recommender system, suggestions for addressing these deficiencies, and identifying which functions within the system needed modifications.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed using SPSS 20.0 software to summarize participants’ sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. Interview recordings were transcribed verbatim and analyzed using thematic analysis. Two researchers independently coded the transcripts, compared codes, and resolved discrepancies through discussion to identify possible usability issues and gather suggestions for improvements.

Results

BHRA development results

Participant characteristics

The knowledge base content study included one clinician, three nurses, and four nursing researchers specializing in symptom management for breast cancer. All experts were female, with an average age of 37.3 years (SD = 6.9, range 33–52). Five held doctoral degrees, and three held master's degrees. The average working experience among them was 13.9 years (SD = 9.7).

Knowledge base results

Based on expert discussion, the knowledge base was organized into two modules: a common module and a personalized recommendation module (Fig. 4). The common module consisted of educational and FAQ-related content on breast cancer and chemotherapy, including understanding breast cancer, breast cancer health education, post-chemotherapy precautions, chemotherapy drug-related information, interpretation of examination results, management of digestive tract symptoms, dietary guidance, and notes on common examinations. This content was accessible directly after logging into the BHRA, without the need to complete any questionnaire.

Examples from the common knowledge base module of the BHRA. The screens shown illustrate examples of the educational resources available in the common knowledge base module. BHRA, Breast Health Recommender App.

The personalized recommendation module included three sections: (1) self-management information on symptoms for women with breast cancer who were receiving or had recently received chemotherapy; (2) knowledge about the chemotherapy process and drug-related care, including chemotherapy regimens, commonly used drugs, the use and maintenance of PICC and infusion ports, and preventive and management guidance for common chemotherapy-related adverse effects, such as gastrointestinal reactions, malnutrition, myelosuppression, hepatic and renal injury, cardiopulmonary toxicity, alopecia, lymphedema, and peripheral neuropathy; and (3) guidance for home-based rehabilitation, encompassing diet, upper limb exercises, physical activity, rest and sleep, and body image. After completing the symptom assessment questionnaire, patients could access personalized recommendation content in the symptom management module, whereas the common educational content remained continuously available. The BHRA also displayed trend charts showing score changes across different symptom-related dimensions.

Recommendation rule results

Participants

Out of 1320 participants, 140 were excluded due to incomplete information or presenting invalid response patterns, leaving 1180 patients for model development. The mean age of the patients was 48.9 years (SD = 10.1). Detailed characteristics of this cohort have been reported previously6 and are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

DNN classifier and recommendation results

The BHRA includes three user-facing modules—user profile management, symptom assessment, and individualized symptom management—and one back-end DNN classifier module. Following iterative model optimization, the final DNN classifier consisted of four hidden layers configured as [256, 256, 128, 128], with an initial learning rate of 0.10 selected through hyperparameter tuning. The model was trained using sparse categorical cross-entropy as the loss function and the Adam optimizer. Multiclass ROC analysis showed a macro-average AUC of 0.92 and a micro-average AUC of 0.94, and the AUC values for the three symptom profile groups exceeded 0.80 (Fig. 5). In addition, the model achieved an accuracy of 0.91, a recall of 0.89, and an F1 score of 0.90. Finally, the trained DNN classifier was integrated into the BHRA to support personalized recommendation delivery. Based on patients’ health-related data, the system identified symptom profiles and matched corresponding expert-informed recommendation content from the knowledge base. Participants varied in educational background and came from four province-level regions. The number of chemotherapy cycles completed ranged from 1 to 7. Discussion

Main findings

This study developed and preliminarily evaluated the BHRA, a personalized HRS designed to support symptom self-management among women with breast cancer who were receiving or had recently received chemotherapy. Conceptually informed by the Digital Intelligent Precise Nursing Framework, the BHRA integrated patient-reported symptom assessment, latent symptom profile classification, and individualized recommendation delivery through a WeChat mini-program. The DNN classifier showed good internal classification performance, and usability testing indicated that participants generally perceived the BHRA as acceptable and useful.

Previous reviews suggest that mobile app interventions for women with breast cancer undergoing chemotherapy should incorporate symptom monitoring, educational content, and self-management support tailored to patients’ needs.9,10 The development of the BHRA adhered to these criteria, particularly in terms of personalization. Incorporating evidence-based content is an important foundation for the development of HRSs, as it may help ensure the reliability and clinical relevance of recommended information and support patient trust, acceptability, and sustained engagement.21,22 Accordingly, the BHRA knowledge base was developed collaboratively with health care providers and patients and was organized into a common module and a personalized recommendation module to provide both basic educational content and symptom assessment–based individualized recommendations. Additionally, a systematic review by de Croon et al.13 indicates that users of HRSs often desire a degree of control over the health recommendations they receive, including understanding the rationale behind these recommendations and having access to alternative options. In line with this, the BHRA displays symptom categories, scores for each symptom domain, and explanations of symptom causes on the homepage of the knowledge base, helping patients understand the recommended content and its relevance to their symptom status. Findings from the initial needs assessment and usability testing also indicated that patients preferred more diverse information formats and functions, such as pictures, videos, favorites, and continuous updates. These findings will guide future iterations of the BHRA. Overall, these design features may improve the transparency and perceived relevance of the recommended content.

Incorporating recommender rules was a key step in enabling individualized symptom management recommendations, consistent with HRS research emphasizing the role of recommendation techniques in matching user characteristics with relevant health information.23 Existing electronic symptom monitoring systems often rely on predefined symptom thresholds or rule-based alert pathways,24 which may not fully capture the coexistence and variability of multiple symptoms among women receiving chemotherapy for breast cancer. In this study, the DNN classifier was used to assign patients to three latent symptom profiles with distinct symptom and functional characteristics identified in our previous multicenter study.6 The model showed good internal classification performance, suggesting that data-driven symptom profiling may provide a quantitative approach to stratifying patients according to multidimensional information, including sociodemographic characteristics, disease-related factors, and patient-reported symptoms. This approach may support the identification of patients with different symptom and functional profiles and facilitate the delivery of more tailored symptom management support when clinical resources are limited. At the same time, the use of DNN-based classification should be interpreted cautiously. Deep learning models are well suited to modeling complex, non-linear relationships in high-dimensional health data, but their “black-box” nature may limit clinical transparency and users’ trust.15 To address this issue, the recommended content is expert-informed rather than fully generated by the DNN. After symptom profile classification, the BHRA used a rule-based matching process to link symptom profiles and symptom domain scores to specific symptom management recommendations in the knowledge base. This process was informed by association rules20 and reviewed and confirmed through consensus among clinical experts. Although this approach does not fully eliminate the interpretability limitations of DNNs, it increases the transparency of how classified symptom profiles are translated into recommendation content and helps ensure that the recommendations remain clinically meaningful and expert-informed.

In line with recent evidence on the development and evaluation of HRSs, the initial development of the BHRA emphasized end-user needs and preferences.23,25 This approach aligns with HRSs in which end-user involvement is integral not only to design but also to the evaluation process.23,25 User feedback represents a fundamental aspect of personalization in HRSs and plays a critical role in app usability assessment.26,27 Although the sample size was small, usability testing commonly involves relatively small samples to identify major usability concerns. In our study, we involved 10 participants to identify usability concerns related to content, interface, readiness for use, and potential barriers. The findings indicated positive perceptions of acceptability and usability, along with the perceived potential to support symptom self-management. Most feedback highlighted the BHRA's success in tailoring information to individual needs and enhancing the user experience in information search and retrieval. However, participants suggested several enhancements, including integrating more engaging elements such as entertainment features, incorporating diverse media like pictures and short videos in the knowledge base, and enhancing interactivity through features for sharing experiences with friends and family and interacting with health care providers. A notable concern was the BHRA's usability among elderly patients, suggesting a need for cautious use in this demographic. To address this and further enhance the BHRA's effectiveness, future modifications will aim to make the app more accessible and user-friendly for elderly patients with breast cancer.

Implications for nursing practice and research

The BHRA may serve as a supportive tool for symptom self-management among women with breast cancer who were receiving or had recently received chemotherapy. By integrating patient-reported symptom assessment, symptom profile classification, and individualized recommendation delivery, the BHRA can help patients obtain tailored, expert-informed symptom management information outside hospital settings. In nursing practice, it may assist oncology nurses in extending symptom education and supportive care from hospital-based encounters to home-based recovery and facilitate more individualized follow-up for patients with different symptom profiles.

Future nursing research should further examine the clinical value of the BHRA beyond usability testing, particularly its effects on patient-reported symptom outcomes, self-management capacity, quality of life, and treatment-related adherence or continuity. As the BHRA moves toward real-world implementation, several challenges should be carefully considered. First, digital access, digital literacy, and sustained engagement may affect patients' ability to use the WeChat-based system over time, particularly among older adults or those less familiar with smartphone applications. Second, effective integration into oncology nursing workflows is essential. This includes clarifying how human–technology collaboration should be organized, how patient-reported symptom data and automated recommendations should inform follow-up and escalation decisions, and how the system can support timely care without unduly increasing nurses’ workload. Third, maintaining the accuracy, relevance, and timeliness of the knowledge base will require continuous expert review and iterative refinement based on patient feedback.

Limitations

The limitations of the study are as follows: (1) although a small sample size is acceptable for preliminary usability testing, the limited number of participants and the uneven distribution across chemotherapy cycles may have restricted the depth of exploration into participants' perceptions of the BHRA. In addition, usability was evaluated using qualitative interviews without a standardized usability scale such as the System Usability Scale, which may limit comparability with other usability studies. (2) the symptom profiles used to develop the recommendation rules were derived from a cross-sectional dataset, which may not fully capture dynamic symptom changes across chemotherapy cycles and disease progression. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal patient-reported outcome data to refine symptom profiles and update recommendation rules over time; (3) the DNN analyses did not include an independent validation set, which may have introduced optimistic bias into the performance estimates. Consequently, external validation is required to confirm the model's generalizability; (4) the current version of the BHRA was developed primarily for women with breast cancer who were receiving or had recently received chemotherapy. Exact chemotherapy regimens or drugs for usability-testing participants were not recorded, limiting assessment of symptom variation by specific agents. Future iterations of the BHRA could tailor recommendations to specific chemotherapy regimen combinations. Its applicability to patients receiving other systemic therapies, such as immunotherapy, targeted therapy, or endocrine therapy, requires further evaluation, and future studies should refine the program and assess real-world utility.

Conclusions

By centering on the needs and preferences of women with breast cancer, the BHRA was designed to address their symptom management needs during chemotherapy and support engagement in health management. Preliminary usability testing suggested that the BHRA was perceived as user-friendly, convenient, and useful, with participants reporting high acceptability and willingness to use the app for symptom management. These findings offer valuable design insights for HRSs in oncology nursing and suggest that personalized recommender systems may be a feasible approach to supporting symptom self-management. Further research is warranted to evaluate the app's efficacy in supporting symptom self-management among patients with breast cancer in real-world settings.
