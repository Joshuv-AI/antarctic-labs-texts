Adverse events associated with sequential immune checkpoint inhibitor and alectinib in patients with ALK-rearranged advanced non-small-cell lung cancer
Authors: J.M. Cheung, S. Waliany, B.Y. Yeap, J.L. Peterson, A. Liu, A. Do, J. Liang, J.J. Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are the standard first-line therapy for ALK-rearranged [ALK fusion-positive (ALK+)] advanced non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC). In real-world practice, patients may receive immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) before initiating ALK TKIs. We aimed to assess the frequency of treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) associated with this sequential approach.Patients and methodsWe retrospectively analyzed alectinib-associated TRAEs in patients with advanced ALK+ NSCLC who received alectinib as their first TKI, with or without prior ICI exposure.ResultsWe identified 166 patients, of whom 12 had prior ICI exposure. From alectinib initiation, the 12-month cumulative incidence in patients with versus without prior ICI was as follows: all-grade pneumonitis, 25.0% versus 4.6%; transaminase elevation, 25.0% versus 14.4%; and rash, 43.3% versus 5.9%. Competing-risks regression detected a higher risk of all-grade pneumonitis [hazard ratio (HR) 5.2], all-grade rash (HR 7.8), and grade ≥3 rash (HR 8.9) in patients with versus without prior ICI. Alectinib required discontinuation or hospitalization for TRAEs in 25.0% and 16.7% of patients with prior ICI, respectively, compared with 7.8% and 3.2% of patients without prior ICI.ConclusionsAlectinib after ICI was associated with higher risks of pneumonitis and rash, and numerically higher risk of grade ≥3 hepatotoxicity, leading to increased rates of alectinib interruption and steroid use. These findings underscore the importance of expedited genotyping to guide treatment selection and avoid unwarranted ICI exposure.

Introduction

Early tumor biomarker testing is essential for appropriate treatment selection in patients with advanced/metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC).1,2 In patients with ALK fusion-positive (ALK+) advanced NSCLC, next-generation ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) are established as first-line therapy,3, 4, 5 whereas immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) show limited efficacy.6,7 However, in real-world practice, patients may receive ICIs before ALK fusion detection, as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) testing results are often available earlier than next-generation sequencing (NGS).8

Prior studies reported toxicities with the first-generation TKI crizotinib when administered concurrently with, or sequentially after, ICI.9,10 As next-generation TKIs (e.g. alectinib) have supplanted crizotinib as first-line therapy for ALK+ NSCLC, further studies are needed to evaluate treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) with next-generation TKIs after prior ICI. This retrospective study compared TRAEs with alectinib in patients with and without prior ICI.

Patients and methods

The objective of this study was to estimate and compare the frequency and severity of TRAEs during alectinib therapy in patients with versus without prior ICI exposure. We carried out a retrospective cohort analysis of patients with metastatic ALK+ NSCLC treated at Mass General Brigham between January 2008 and December 2024. ALK fusion was identified by NGS, real-time PCR, immunohistochemistry (IHC), and/or FISH. Eligible patients received alectinib as their first TKI and were categorized according to prior ICI exposure.

TRAEs, specifically pneumonitis, aspartate aminotransferase elevation, alanine aminotransferase elevation, and cutaneous TRAEs, were assessed retrospectively, initially adjudicated by the treating provider, and subsequently retrieved and graded by the study investigators according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0. These TRAEs were selected as the primary AEs of interest for this study based on prior analyses identifying pneumonitis, transaminase elevations, and cutaneous toxicities as TRAEs frequently associated with sequential therapy involving ICI followed by targeted therapies.9,11

The 12-month AE cumulative incidence timepoint was defined a priori before data analysis. The study was conducted under an institutional review board-approved protocol (IRB 2009P000035). The data cut-off date was 1 December 2024. Statistical analyses are described in the Supplementary Materials, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105842.

Of the 12 ICI-exposed patients, 11 received ICI while awaiting molecular testing results; most (n = 8) received only one or two ICI doses (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105842). Regimens included platinum-based chemotherapy/pembrolizumab (n = 9) and pembrolizumab monotherapy (n = 3). The median time from diagnosis of advanced NSCLC to treatment initiation was 0.70 months (95% CI 0.23-1.08 months) for ICI-exposed patients and 0.95 months (95% CI 0.82-1.08 months) for non-ICI-exposed patients (log-rank: P = 0.023). In total, 118 patients had paired data on time from diagnosis to ALK and PD-L1 results (prior ICI: n = 11, no prior ICI: n = 107). Time to ALK result was calculated based on the first reported positive finding from any testing modality (NGS, RT–PCR, IHC, or FISH) to reflect real-world clinical practice. The median time to result in ICI-exposed patients was 26 days [interquartile range (IQR) 16-37 days] for ALK versus 14 days (IQR 8-20 days) for PD-L1 (P = 0.008). In non-ICI-exposed patients, the median time to result was 14 days (IQR 7-20 days) for ALK and 13 days (IQR 7-23 days) for PD-L1 (P = 0.84) (Supplementary Figure S1A, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105842). The difference in testing time was longer in ICI-exposed compared with non-ICI-exposed patients (P = 0.004). Although more non-ICI-exposed patients had unknown PD-L1 status (P = 0.038), among those with known status, PD-L1 levels were comparable between ICI- and non-ICI-treated patients (P = 0.32; Supplementary Figure S1B, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105842). The median time from last ICI to alectinib initiation was 3.2 weeks (95% CI 1.3-4.3 weeks).

Pneumonitis

Pneumonitis occurred in 3 (25.0%) ICI-exposed versus 9 (5.8%) non-ICI-exposed patients (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105842). Competing-risks regression demonstrated a higher risk of all-grade pneumonitis in ICI-exposed patients [hazard ratio (HR) 5.2, 95% CI 1.4-19.4, P = 0.014; Figure 1]. From alectinib initiation, the 12-month cumulative incidence of all-grade and grade ≥3 pneumonitis was 25.0% and 8.3%, respectively, among ICI-exposed patients, compared with 4.6% and 2.6% in non-ICI-exposed patients (Figure 2A). All patients experienced radiographic and symptomatic resolution, except for one non-ICI-exposed patient who developed fatal pneumonitis complications. Among the three ICI-exposed patients with pneumonitis, all required steroids; one required intensive care unit hospitalization, one discontinued alectinib, and two required alectinib dosing interruption (3.0 weeks and 2.3 months; Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105842). Among the nine non-ICI-exposed patients with pneumonitis, six required steroids, three required hospitalization, five discontinued alectinib, and two had dose interruptions (3.0 weeks and 14.1 months).

Transaminase elevation

Transaminase elevation occurred in 3 (25.0%) ICI-exposed versus 25 (16.2%) non-ICI-exposed patients (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105842). Competing-risks regression showed a similar risk of both all-grade and grade ≥3 elevations between ICI- and non-ICI-exposed patients (Figure 1). In multivariate analyses adjusting for liver metastases at the start of alectinib treatment, the risk of all-grade elevation remained similar (HR 2.5, 95% CI 0.7-8.9, P = 0.17), whereas the risk of grade ≥3 elevation was higher in ICI-exposed patients (HR 10.7, 95% CI 1.1-103.2, P = 0.04).

The 12-month cumulative incidence of all-grade and grade ≥3 transaminase elevations was 25.0% and 8.3%, respectively, in ICI-exposed patients and 14.4% and 2.0% in non-ICI-exposed patients (Figure 2B). The median time from alectinib initiation to transaminase elevation was 0.6 months (range 0.3-6.8 months) in patients with prior ICI and 1.2 months (range 0.1-22.4 months) in those without prior ICI (P = 0.85), with resolution in all cases except three non-ICI-exposed patients. Among the three ICI-exposed patients with transaminase elevation, one required steroids and a 10-week alectinib interruption before resuming at a reduced dose, but later discontinued therapy due to pneumonitis and transaminitis (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105842). Among the 25 non-ICI-exposed patients, 1 required hospitalization and alectinib discontinuation, 2 required dose interruptions before resuming reduced-dose alectinib, and 6 underwent dose reductions.

Cutaneous toxicity

Rash occurred in 5 (41.7%) ICI-exposed and 13 (8.4%) non-ICI-exposed patients (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105842). Competing-risks regression showed a higher risk of all-grade rash (HR 7.8, 95% CI 2.7-22.6, P = 0.0002) and grade ≥3 rash (HR 8.9, 95% CI 1.5-53.5, P = 0.017) in patients with versus without prior ICI (Figure 1). The 12-month cumulative incidence of all-grade and grade ≥3 rash was 43.3% and 16.7%, respectively, in ICI-exposed patients versus 5.9% and 2.0% in non-ICI-exposed patients (Figure 2C). The median time from alectinib initiation to rash was 0.6 months (range 0.3-10.0 months) in ICI-exposed patients and 3.9 months (range 0.03-76.5 months) in non-ICI-exposed patients (P = 0.32).

All patients experienced resolution of rash. Among the five ICI-exposed patients with rash, two required hospitalization, and three required oral steroids (two with initial intravenous steroids) along with alectinib interruptions lasting 2.3 weeks to 5.9 months; of these three, one resumed dose-reduced alectinib, one resumed standard-dose alectinib, and one underwent desensitization before resuming standard-dose alectinib (Supplementary Figure S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2025.105842). Among the 13 non-ICI-exposed patients with rash, one was hospitalized and discontinued alectinib due to drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms, and another discontinued alectinib due to dermatomyositis. Four patients required dosing interruption, three required dose reductions (two following dose interruption), and four required oral steroids (one with initial intravenous steroids).

Treatment discontinuation and interruption

Among ICI-exposed patients, 7 (58.3%) discontinued alectinib due to progression and 3 (25.0%) due to TRAEs. In the non-ICI-exposed group, 74 (48.1%) discontinued alectinib for progression, 12 (7.8%) for TRAEs, and 5 (3.2%) for other reasons. The median time to alectinib discontinuation was 11.9 months (95% CI 5.2-38.9 months) in ICI-exposed patients and 30.1 months (95% CI 23.0-41.4 months) in non-ICI-exposed patients (P = 0.09).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first retrospective study to evaluate TRAEs with alectinib after prior ICI. Although next-generation TKIs are established as front-line treatment for ALK+ metastatic NSCLC,3,4 delays or barriers to NGS may lead patients in the community to receive ICI before transitioning to an ALK TKI upon detection of ALK+ disease. Defining the toxicities of sequential ICI–alectinib therapy is therefore crucial.

Rates of all-grade pneumonitis, transaminase elevation, and rash during alectinib were 25.0%, 25.0%, and 41.7%, respectively, among ICI-exposed patients versus 5.8%, 16.2%, and 8.4% in non-ICI-exposed patients. Pre-alectinib ICI was associated with increased risks of all-grade pneumonitis (HR 5.2), all-grade rash (HR 7.8), and grade ≥3 rash (HR 8.9) during alectinib therapy. More ICI-exposed patients required alectinib interruption, discontinuation, or steroid courses, demonstrating clinically consequential toxicity risks with sequential ICI–alectinib. Notably, two-thirds of ICI-exposed patients had received only one or two cycles, illustrating that even short ICI courses may be sufficient to confer harm in this population.

Prior studies have evaluated the toxicity of concurrent ICI-targeted therapy,10 with limited studies addressing sequential ICI–ALK TKI. A phase Ib study of concurrent alectinib–atezolizumab reported elevated rates of rash, transaminase elevation, and pneumonitis,12 with similar risks observed across other concomitant ALK TKI–ICI regimens.10,13,14 The mechanisms by which anti-PD(L)1 ICIs increase TRAE risks during sequential or concurrent ALK TKI therapy remain unclear. For example, osimertinib has been associated with increased rates of pneumonitis and other TRAEs after ICI exposure, unlike earlier EGFR TKIs such as erlotinib.11,15 Similarly, certain KRAS G12C inhibitors, such as sotorasib, have shown increased hepatotoxicity in patients pretreated with an ICI.16 These observations suggest that toxicities augmented by prior ICI exposure may be TKI-dependent or TKI-specific rather than a class effect.17 A combination of factors, including off-target effects on T-cell activation, alterations in the immune microenvironment, and differences in TKI tissue distribution, may contribute. As ALK TKIs promote immunologic remodeling with increased T-cell tumor infiltration,12,18 activation of tumor-specific T cells followed by increased infiltration upon TKI initiation represents one potential mechanism for the enhanced toxicity observed in this study.

Our findings highlight the need for expedited upfront biomarker testing in patients with advanced NSCLC. In this study, among ICI-exposed patients, there was a notable difference in the time to PD-L1 versus ALK result (median 14 versus 26 days), with most (n = 8) discontinuing ICI upon detection of ALK fusion. This delay in ALK results may lead some providers to initiate ICI prematurely. Rates of TRAEs requiring steroids and alectinib interruption were substantially higher among ICI-exposed patients, with a trend toward higher alectinib discontinuation rates and shorter alectinib duration. Given the ineffectiveness of available anti-PD-(L)1 ICIs for ALK+ NSCLC and the robust efficacy of ALK inhibitors,6 augmented TRAEs with sequential ICI–alectinib underscore the importance of expedited genotyping to allow timely initiation of biomarker-matched therapy. Any therapy not recommended per guidelines that increases toxicity risk and triggers discontinuation of ALK inhibitors should be avoided.1,2 Our findings also have implications in early-stage NSCLC, where adjuvant alectinib is now the standard of care for resected ALK+ disease.19 With the increasing adoption of adjuvant and perioperative regimens incorporating immunotherapy, earlier ALK testing could guide treatment selection and avoid unnecessary ICI exposure. Overall, global efforts are imperative to close biomarker testing gaps, particularly in settings where rapid genotyping may not be routinely implemented before treatment selection.20 While expedited NGS is ideal to enable multiplex testing for a broader panel of targetable oncogenic drivers, in resource-constrained settings, parallel ALK IHC may facilitate faster treatment decisions, albeit with the major limitation of single-gene testing.

Study limitations include its retrospective design and small cohort size, which may introduce selection biases. There were a few baseline differences between ICI-exposed and non-ICI-exposed patients, although a greater proportion of ICI-exposed patients had hepatic metastases. Certain variables, such as the number of disease sites, were unavailable, and therefore could not be compared between patients with and without prior ICI exposure. All patients with prior ICI received pembrolizumab, potentially limiting the generalizability of these findings to other ICIs. Importantly, the small number of ICI-exposed patients (n = 12) introduces uncertainty in estimating the incidence of TRAEs in this subgroup, increases the risk of type II error, and limits statistical power, particularly for detecting differences in outcomes such as hepatotoxicity, where only numerical trends were observed. These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution, and further studies with larger sample sizes are warranted. Additionally, regional differences in biomarker testing, such as the routine use of rapid ALK IHC in some areas, may limit the applicability of our results; however, our findings remain relevant in settings where upfront biomarker testing can be delayed or incomplete.

Our study demonstrates that sequential ICI–alectinib is associated with significantly higher risks of pneumonitis and rash compared with alectinib without prior ICI, resulting in increased rates of hospitalizations and alectinib interruptions, numerically higher discontinuation rates, and shorter alectinib duration. These findings underscore the importance of expedited tumor genotyping to guide treatment selection, thereby minimizing unwarranted ICI use and potential harm.
