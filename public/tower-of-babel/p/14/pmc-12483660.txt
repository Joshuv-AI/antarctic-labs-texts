Trends and differences in cardiovascular disease and alzheimer's disease-related mortality among older adults in the United States, 1999 to 2023: A CDC WONDER database analysis
Authors: Jianren Wen, Jingxuan Hu, Guohui Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular disease (CVD) and Alzheimer's disease (AD) are leading causes of death in the United States and often co-occur, worsening outcomes. AD contributes to CVD mortality through shared risk factors, neurovascular dysfunction, and secondary complications such as immobility and dysautonomia. As global aging intensifies, mortality trends and disparities between these diseases remain poorly summarized. This study examines CVD- and AD-related mortality trends from 1999 to 2023 among U.S. adults aged 65 and older, by sex, race, and region.MethodsThe death certificates of individuals aged 65 years > from 1999 to 2023 were extracted using the CDC WONDER multi-cause database and listed I00–I99 (CVD) and G30 (AD) as the criteria. Age-adjusted mortality rate per 100,000 people (AAMR) was calculated and Joinpoint regression was used to estimate mean annual percentage change (AAPC) and annual percentage change (APC).ResultsA total of 1,283,881 deaths from CVD combined with AD were recorded from 1999 to 2023. The overall AAMR increased from 110.13 (95 % CI: 109.02 to 111.25) in 1999 to 116.80 (95 % CI: 115.87 to 117.74) in 2023 (APC: 0.14 (95 % CI: −1.00 to 1.29)). AAMR is consistently higher for women than for men (2023: 126.95 vs 100.64). By race, non-Hispanic whites had the highest number of deaths in 2023 (121.46 (95 %CI: 120.37 to 122.55)), followed by blacks (110.50 ((95 %CI: 107.35 to 113.65), Hispanic ((95 %CI: 112.97 (109.84 to 116.10)) and other races ((95 %CI:74.05 (71.20 to 76.91)). The regional distribution showed that the AAMR in the West was the highest in 2023 (158.43 (95 % CI: 156.13 to 160.74)), followed by the Midwest (118.32 (95 %CI: 116.28). to 120.36)), the South (113.36 (95 %CI: 111.85 to 114.87)) and the Northeast (73.15 (95 %CI: 71.47 to 74.84)). AAMR is highest in people over 85 years of age (17.6). Between 1999 and 2023, the AAMR in rural areas (36.90) was higher than in urban areas (28.75).ConclusionThe age-adjusted mortality rate of CVD combined with AD in the United States continued to increase from 1999 to 2023, with the highest burden among women, non-Hispanic whites, rural areas, and individuals over 85 years of age. The results suggest the need for targeted early interventions to mitigate disparities and reduce mortality from comorbidities.Keywords: CDC WONDER, Cardiovascular disease, Alzheimer's disease, Mortality, Comorbidity

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) and Alzheimer's disease (AD) ranked first and fifth in the causes of death in the U.S. population, respectively, and both showed significant age-dependent incidence curves. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD 2021) data, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) due to CVD and AD contribute 28.6 million and 13.4 million, respectively, in people over 65 years of age, and are on the rise [1]. At the same time, according to research, about 700,000 deaths due to CVD in 2022, accounting for 1/5 of the total deaths, may be related to these risk factors: hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, obesity and smoking [2]. In the United States, there are about 6.7 million people over the age of 65 who suffer from AD, which is expected to increase to 13.8 million by 2060, of which 13.9 % of men and 20.1 % of women have a lifetime risk of 60 years old [3,4].

Cardiovascular disease is a group of heart and vascular disorders, including coronary heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral artery disease, etc., mainly due to atherosclerosis leading to blood vessel damage, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia and aggravated inflammation. AD is a well-known heterogeneous group of pathologies, characterized by a similar pattern of progressive deterioration of cognitive dysfunction and behavioral disorders caused by neurodegeneration, due to the formation of age spots due to β-amyloid (Aβ) deposition, and the formation of nerve fiber tangles due to excessive phosphorylation of tau protein, leading to synaptic dysfunction and neuronal apoptosis, and finally the occurrence of the disease. They share common pathophysiological pathways such as atherosclerosis, hypertension, insulin resistance, lipid metabolism disorders, and chronic systemic inflammation, forming the so-called “cardio-cerebral axis” interaction network [5]. Although various therapies have been developed in recent decades, including coronary interventions, LV assist devices, SGLT2 inhibitors and anti-Aβ monoclonal antibodies, and remote cardiocerebral monitoring, these measures have not led to a general reduction in mortality at the population level [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]]. Studies have shown that multimodal interventions can improve survival in specific subpopulations, but the efficacy varies with socioeconomic background, comorbidity spectrum, and geographic accessibility. Therefore, the best plan of action is to take preventive measures aimed at reducing the impact of risk factors and promoting healthy living.

This study uses CDC WONDER data from multiple causes of death from 1999 to 2023 to systematically depict the evolution trend and differences of mortality caused by CVD-AD comorbidity in people aged 65 years and older in the United States, providing an empirical basis for identifying high-risk groups and formulating precise heart-brain co-treatment strategies.

Method

Data sources

This study used the CDC WONDER 1999–2023 Multiple Causes of Death Open Database as the sole data source to extract cases ≥ CVD (ICD-10: I00-I99) and AD (ICD-10: G30) recorded in the death certificates of 65-year-old U.S. residents. Data are stratified by age, gender, race, and U.S. Census region, state, urbanization, etc., to pinpoint population heterogeneity and temporal evolution of CVD-AD comorbidity deaths. The database, maintained by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in all 51 states, complies with the Code of Federal Regulations' exemptions for public de-identification data, so it does not require additional approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB).

Data processing

Our analysis leveraged national mortality data from the CDC WONDER database (1999–2023), covering all 51 U.S. states. The data included stratified demographic variables: gender (male/female), age group (65–74 years, 75–84 years, and ≥ 85 years), and race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, Hispanic/Latino, other races). he geographic classification follows the U.S. Census Bureau's criteria, dividing states into four regions: Northeast (Connecticut, Maine, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, Pennsylvania, Rhode Island, Vermont), Midwest (Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kansas, Michigan, Minnesota, Missouri, Nebraska, North Dakota, Ohio, South Dakota, Wisconsin), South (Alabama, Arkansas, Delaware, District of Columbia, Florida, Georgia, Kentucky, Louisiana, Maryland, Mississippi, North Carolina, Oklahoma, South Carolina, Tennessee, Texas, Virginia, West Virginia), and West (Alaska, Arizona, California, Colorado, Hawaii, Idaho, Montana, Nevada, New Mexico, Oregon, Utah, Washington, Wyoming) and based on population thresholds (cities≥50,000; Rural <50,000) undergoes additional urban-rural stratification [12].

Statistical analysis

Our study employed a nationally representative cohort of 10,000 participants to analyze mortality patterns for CVD and AD from 1999 to 2023. Crude mortality (CMR) is calculated by dividing the annual number of CVD/AD deaths by the corresponding US population estimate (expressed per 100 population). To account for age distribution differences, we applied direct normalization using the 2000 US standard population. Using Joinpoint regression (v5.0, National Cancer Institute), we identify temporal trend changes by calculating annual percentage change (APC) with 95 % confidence intervals. When the regression slope was significantly negative, a statistically significant reduction in mortality was identified (P < 0.05, two-tailed t-test), indicating a meaningful trend inflection point over a given period.

Results

Overall trends

Our study shows that the age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) for CVD and AD in people aged 65 years and older has increased from 110.13 (95 % CI: 109.02 to 111.25) in 1999 to 116.80 (95 % CI: 115.87 to 117.74) in 2023, with a mean annual percent change (AAPC) of 0.14 (95 % CI: −1.00 to 1.29). (Supplementary Table 1). It rose sharply from 1999 to 2005 (APC: 2.76 (95 % CI: 0.58 to 4.99)), followed by a significant decrease between 2005 and 2013 (APC: -3.12 (95 % CI: −4.65 to −1.56)), and continued to rise significantly from 2013 to 2021 (APC: 3.44 (95 % CI: 1.95 to 4.96), p < 0.05), There was another significant decline from 2021 to 2023 (APC: -7.14 (95 % CI: −16.37 to 3.11)) (Fig. 1).

Gender stratification

Stratified by gender, we observed an increase from 113.15 (95 % CI: 111.77 to 114.54) in 1999 to 126.95 (95 % CI: 125.70 to 128.21) in 2023, and 0.32 (95 % CI: −0.88 to 1.53) for AAPC. The AAMR for women decreased from 101.93 (95 % CI: 100.06 to 103.79) in 1999 to 100.64 (95 % CI: 99.26 to 102.01), and the AAPC was −0.01 (95 % CI: −0.56 to 0.55 (Supplementary Table 1)).

AAMR was consistently significantly higher in adult women than in adult men throughout the study period compared to adult men. In both men and women, AAMR showed a trend of first rising, then decreasing, and then rising, then decreasing. Between 1999 and 2005, AAMR increased significantly in both men and women and was higher than in women, with APC of 2.16 (95 % CI: 0.98 to 3.35) for men and 3.04 (95 % CI: 0.78 to 5.35) for women. Between 2005 and 2013, AAMR was significantly higher in men than in women, with APC of −2.83 (95 % CI: −3.50 to −2.14) for men and − 3.00 (95 % CI: −4.60 to −1.37) for women. In women, AAMR increased significantly between 2013 and 2021, with an APC of 3.67 (95 % CI: 2.10 to 5.27), and again between 2021 and 2023, AAMR decreased significantly with an APC of −7.15 (95 % CI: −16.85 to 3.69). In men, the AAMR rose sharply from 2014 to 2020, with an APC of 4.36 (95 % CI: 3.03 to 5.70), and a rebound decline from 2020 to 2023, with an APC of −4.17 (95 % CI: −6.71 to −1.55) (Fig. 1).

White deaths accounted for 1,077,943, Black deaths at 99,259, Hispanic deaths at 73,262, and other races at 31,509, with other races having the highest AAPC (1.61), followed by Hispanics (1.09), Blacks (0.20), and Whites (0.17) (Supplementary Table 1).

In addition, non-Hispanic whites are consistently at the highest risk tier and maintain the highest mortality levels for a long time, while non-Hispanic other races consistently maintain the lowest mortality rates. Discussion

In this study, we systematically reported the evolution trajectory of CVD and AD comorbidity mortality in the United States from 1999 to 2023 and population and geographic differences. First, the mortality rate of CVD and AD comorbidities is on the rise as a whole. Secondly, gender and age differences are consistent throughout: the mortality rate for women is always higher than that for men over 85 years old. Third, non-Hispanic whites have the highest AAMR, and fourth, geographic stratification shows that the western region and rural areas maintain the highest AAMR. These observations have important implications for public health policy.

Our study shows that between 1999 and 2023, the mortality rate of different subgroups of patients diagnosed with CVD and AD showed a reversal of “first up, then down, then up, then down”: a significant increase in 1999–2005, a continuous decline in all subgroups from 2005 to 2013, then another increase after 2013, and finally a rebound decline in 2021. This trend is very different from previous single-etiology studies, highlighting the compound burden of death driven by comorbidities [[13], [14], [15]]. Specifically, women, non-Hispanic whites, rural residents and older age groups in the West saw the steepest increases, suggesting that multiple gender-race-geography-age inequalities are amplifying risks. The resurgence of CVD-AD comorbidity mortality over the past two decades can be attributed to the resurgence of common risk factors such as obesity, hypertension, and diabetes, as well as the failure to form a population protective effect in vulnerable populations due to clinical breakthroughs in novel anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies, SGLT2 inhibitors, and PCSK9 inhibitors due to medical insurance accessibility, prescription differences, and insufficient grassroots follow-up [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22], [23]]. Although remote cardiocerebral monitoring and integrated cognitive-cardiovascular rehabilitation programs have been shown to reduce readmission rates, the penetration rate in rural and ethnic minority areas is still less than 20 %. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a comprehensive care model with “heart-brain co-treatment” as the core, combining remote management of risk factors, equitable access to innovative drugs, and social prescription intervention to truly reverse the upward trend of comorbid mortality of CVD and AD at the population level.

In addition, our study captured significant reductions in CVD-AD comorbidity mortality from 2005 to 2013 and from 2021 to 2023, and significant increases in age-adjusted mortality between 2013 and 2021, this is not the same trend as Muzamil Akhtar et al. on the continued decline in ischemic heart disease and Alzheimer's disease reported before 2020 [24]. On the contrary, the overall AD mortality curve in the United States has risen sharply in the past decade, while the decline in CVD deaths has slowed down, and the inverse changes of the two have amplified the burden of comorbidity [25,26]. The decline in 2012–2015 coincided with the launch of the Affordable Care Act of 2010, which promoted screening and intervention for hypertension, diabetes, and early cognitive impairment [27]. At the same time, the significant reduction in mortality in 2021–2023 may be due to the rapid establishment of a population immune barrier (by December 2021, the completion rate of basic immunization of the new crown vaccine in the United States ≥65-year-olds was as high as 92 %, the booster vaccination rate was 65 %, and the population-wide antibody positivity rate was 94.7 %). SGLT2 inhibitors and PCSK9 inhibitors entered the fast track of medical insurance, reducing the rehospitalization rate of heart failure by 14 %, and the spillover effect of non-pharmacological interventions (NPIs) lowered the mortality curve in the short term [[28], [29], [30]]. The significant increase in age-adjusted mortality between 2013 and 2021 may be related to obesity, sugary beverage consumption, a high-salt diet and increased sedentary time, and the continued increase in the prevalence of hypertension and diabetes, as well as inadequate Medicaid and the superposition of drugs, further amplifying the risk of death [31,32].

Age is a determinant of CVD-AD comorbidity death. Crude mortality increases exponentially after age 75, and this age-dependence is mainly due to the synergy of vascular-neural unit degeneration: increased arterial stiffness leads to decreased cerebral blood flow reserve (risk of AD ↑27 % for every 1 SD increase in PWV), and overactivation of the AGE-RAGE axis (serum AGEs ↑3–5 times) simultaneously promotes coronary artery calcification (28.7 Agatston units per year) and Aβ deposition (clearance ↓ 40–60 %) [[33], [34], [35], [36]]. Elderly patients are more likely to develop cardiogenic cerebral hypoperfusion (HR 2.34) and drug metabolism disorders (CYP450 activity ↓40 %), forming a vicious circle. Age-stratified management is recommended: intensive antihypertensive therapy at age 65–74 years (target <130/80), neurovascular protectors (e.g, nimodipine, SGLT2inhibitor) at age 75–84, and pharmacogenomics guidance (e.g., CYP2D6 testing) at age 85+ [[37], [38], [39]].

Non-Hispanic whites have consistently undertaken the highest burden of CVD-AD comorbidity deaths throughout the 1999–2023 period compared to other racial groups. According to the 2020 GBD data, the age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) for atrial fibrillation-dementia-related mortality (AAMR) in non-Hispanic whites aged ≥65 years was 47.0 per 100,000, which was significantly higher than that of non-Hispanic blacks (26.6/100,000), Hispanics (23.1/100,000), and Asian Pacific Americans (18.0/100,000) [40]. This difference stems from the large absolute population base of whites and the highest depth of aging (≥77 % of whites aged 85 years old in the United States), but also reflects that although whites enjoy higher prescription rates, the benefits of drugs are diluted due to the complexity of comorbidities and polypharmacy in old age, and the disconnection of primary follow-up has not reduced population-level mortality [6,41,42].

The AAMR among Hispanics has risen significantly since 2017 and is converging with trends in non-Hispanic white and black groups, signaling the weakening or disappearance of the “Hispanic health paradox.” This convergence is the result of a combination of factors, and the effect of healthy migration weakens with generational change, and future generations still face socioeconomic disadvantages such as low income and medical disabilities while adopting high-calorie diets and sedentary American lifestyles [[43], [44], [45]]. The COVID-19 pandemic has disproportionately impacted the Hispanic community, who are more likely to work in frontline jobs, live in crowded homes, and have low access to healthcare, which not only directly drives mortality but also exacerbates the burden of chronic diseases [46,47]. Long-term structural inequality leads to inadequate medical insurance coverage, lack of preventive care, and lagging chronic disease management, and its negative effects accumulate over time [[48], [49], [50]]. The shift in the disease spectrum to chronic diseases such as heart disease and cancer, coupled with the psychological stress caused by immigration status-related stress and discrimination, further increases the risk of all-cause mortality [[51], [52], [53]]. These trends suggest that macrosocial and environmental risks have overwhelmed traditional cultural conservation factors, and this trend calls for targeted interventions to address underlying health inequalities.

Age-adjusted mortality rates in older CVD-AD patients in non-metropolitan areas are consistently higher than in metropolitan areas. These differences stem from multiple socioeconomic disadvantages: the density of primary cardiology specialties is only 1/3 of that of urban areas, and the coverage rate of memory clinics is less than 25 %; Food deserts and lack of exercise facilities lead to high prevalence of obesity, hypertension, and diabetes [54].

The results of our study have direct implications for clinical practice and public health policy. Our findings highlight significant racial and geographic disparities in CVD-AD comorbidity mortality, particularly affecting Black communities and rural populations. To reduce these disparities, systematic interventions targeting social determinants of health are essential, including: improving education access in Black communities, implementing policies to alleviate poverty, and enhancing access to healthy food—specifically through programs that increase affordability and availability of diets rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and lean proteins, which have been shown to directly ameliorate key risk factors including hypertension, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and systemic inflammation—pathological processes that are fundamental to the development and progression of both CVD and AD, thereby ultimately reducing mortality. Secondly, evidence-based guidelines for combined cardio-cerebral risk assessment and hierarchical management should be established for adults aged ≥75 years, focusing on rigorous control of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia to lower the risk of dual cardiovascular and cognitive endpoints. Third, healthcare systems should prioritize strengthening integrated cardiovascular and cognitive care infrastructure in rural and underserved areas, and expand remote monitoring and tele-rehabilitation services for combined cardio-brain health management. Finally, in high-risk regions such as the West, community health campaigns and policy incentives should be deployed to raise awareness about the importance of blood pressure control, glycemic management, and early cognitive screening, enabling precision prevention and reducing mortality from comorbid CVD and AD.

There are also limitations of this study that are worth noting: First, the death certificate data cannot distinguish between specific subtypes of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and Alzheimer's disease (AD), such as ischemic heart disease vs. hemorrhagic stroke, or early-onset vs. late-onset AD, which limits our analysis of specific disease subpopulations. Second, the database does not contain key pathological diagnostic information (such as AD diagnosis confirmed by brain autopsy), which may lead to underestimation of AD mortality. Third, multiple updates to AD diagnostic criteria during the study period (such as the transition from DSM-IV to DSM-5) caused inconsistencies in diagnostic criteria and affected long-term trend comparison. In addition, important environmental factors (e.g., air pollution exposure) and lifestyle variables (e.g., dietary patterns, physical activity), which are known to be strongly associated with CVD and AD risk, are not taken into account. Another key limitation is the lack of data on drug use, particularly statins and newer AD treatments such as Aduhelm, which can significantly impact disease mortality. Finally, confounding factors such as changes in Medicare policies (such as the expansion of Medicare coverage) and increased awareness of the disease (such as the promotion of AD screening programs) may also lead to superficial changes in documented mortality rather than real epidemiological trends. Future research will require the integration of multi-source data, including clinical registration systems, environmental monitoring data, and drug prescription records, to provide a more comprehensive analytical perspective.

Conclusion

Mortality varied significantly across strata across our studies. CVD and AD patients continued to show an overall growth trend from 1999 to 2023, and this trend was evident among women, blacks, and individuals living in rural areas and 85 years old. This finding has exposed that health inequality in the United States is approaching a tipping point, and there is an urgent need for urgent and targeted public health initiatives to intervene immediately with gender-sensitive, racially equitable, urban-rural precision public health strategies to reverse the existential disadvantage of vulnerable populations in the shortest possible time and ensure equal access to health care for everyone.
