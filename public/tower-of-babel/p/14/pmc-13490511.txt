Non-Destructive Blade Sharpness Characterization Using a High-Shear Responsive Fluid and Analytical Drag Modeling
Authors: Haolin Zhao, Pingfa Feng, Nana Chai, Youhong Gong, Enlai Jiang, Qizhong Yue, Jie Xu, Feng Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFor various thin-edged blades and tools, the traditional sharpness measurement techniques (e.g., optical measurements and solid material cutting tests) suffer from drawbacks such as lengthy testing time, high cost, and material waste. To fill this gap, a sharpness evaluation method based on a high-shear-responsive fluid was proposed in this study for such scenarios. A tailored fluid formulation was developed to detect edge variation with micron-scale sensitivity. The drag-decomposition framework, by utilizing cross-model rheology with Hiemenz and Falkner-Skan solutions, was established, and the real-fluid behaviors for sharpness evaluation were predicted by simulation. Experiments were carried out using a specifically developed platform to model the relationship between edge radius, cutting speed, penetration depth and force responses. Furthermore, an analytical sharpness index by integrating wedge-flow theory with shear-rate-dependent viscosity was proposed, proving the positive contribution to sensitivity. These results highlight the need for nonlinear modeling and precise fluid selection in blade-sharpness measurement, and provide a basis for further investigation of fluid-mediated sharpness evaluation.Subject terms: Mechanical engineering, Characterization and analytical techniques

Introduction

Sharpness defines the performance of every blade, from household kitchen1–3 and craft knives4 to surgical scalpels5–8 (even forensic examination9) and industrial cutters10, where microscopic edge defects can increase cutting effort11,12 compromise precision and safety13, degrade finished quality14,15 and shorten tool life, even humanitarianism16,17.

Traditional sharpness evaluation falls into three main categories: non-contact optical profiling, standardized cutting tests, and manual assessment. Non-contact optical profiling18,19 to reconstruct topography, offering detailed edge maps but requiring precise alignment and risking stylus-induced damage or optical artifacts20. Standardized cutting tests draw blades through consumable substrates, paper tape sheet stacks21, polymer rods/lines22, polyurethane blocks23,24 or biological tissues25,26 realized through various devices27–29, to generate a “sharpness index”30–32 based on the length of material severed per unit force or the number of sheets cut before failure. Despite simple and low-cost, these cutting-based assays exhibit four key drawbacks: high sampling-point randomness, since each test probes only a small, arbitrarily chosen segment of the edge, making comprehensive, whole-edge analysis unreliable; single-use substrates, as each solid test material is destroyed in one trial, preventing repeat measurements at the exact location; inherent material loss, because the cutting action consumes the test medium, generating debris and altering cutting conditions over time; and transient, single-point variability, since force or penetration is recorded only during brief, localized events, leading to large fluctuations and poor reproducibility across tests. Previous studies on cutting mechanics have highlighted penetration theories33, the role of friction and adhesion34, scaling laws influenced by toughness and wear24, the link between cutting and tearing35, and material-dependent responses36.

This paper introduces a non-destructive, fluid-based sharpness assay that immerses a blade in a custom shear-thinning medium and uses steady-state drag-force measurements to determine the complete edge profile. By integrating an analytical rheo-hydrodynamic model with a precision cutting platform and a tailored starch-based fluid, the method provides continuous, high-resolution sharpness signal diagrams with reproducibility. Compared with conventional approaches, it reduces reliance on single-point and destructive testing and provides a basis for real-time, whole-edge diagnostics in blade evaluation.

Results

Methodology of fluid

The fluid‑based sharpness testing method offers a comprehensive, non‑destructive, and fully repeatable evaluation of the entire cutting edge, overcoming the limitations of traditional line‑cut tests, including single‑point, one‑time, and highly variable measurements. By capturing steady‑state drag signals rather than transient cutting‑force spikes, this approach substantially enhances data reliability and repeatability. Among candidate non‑Newtonian media, polymers37, emulsions38, mineral suspensions39, and biopolymer solutions40, starch sol-gel41–43 stand out for their delicate, homogenous texture, low cost, environmental friendliness, and stability44. As a classic shear‑responsive colloid, raw starch solution exhibits marked viscosity increases at low shear rates and rapid thinning at high shear rates45,46, making it exquisitely sensitive to changes in cutting‑edge radius. The colloidal network deforms locally around the blade tip, amplifying subtle geometric differences into measurable force variations and thus delivering high‑precision sharpness discrimination.

The flow behavior of non-Newtonian fluids on moving wedges or stretching plates was studied, and a unified calculation method was proposed. Under non-Newtonian fluid conditions, the shear rate at the edge becomes extremely large, so that the boundary layer there is considered extremely thin, while the shear rate along the flank face remains smaller and smoother, but differs from a laminar flat-plate flow47. The blunt, rounded cutting edge can be idealized as a truncated wedge48 with a cutting-edge radius r and truncation width b. To quantify its degree of “blunting”, the blunting-degree parameter δ is introduced as δ=r/b (Fig. 1). In this study, only the blade’s lateral cutting edge is engaged; the bottom tip is not involved and is not considered further.

When a blade translates through a shear-thickening fluid, the load-bearing regions can be idealized as the rounded cutting edge and the flank face. By analogy with the ITTC resistance model49 and the Holtrop-Mennen formulation50 for ship-hull drag, the total resistance experienced by the blade is dominated by two components: skin-friction drag and form drag, while other contributions may be neglected with slight influence. As the fluid flows over the rounded edge, viscous shear stresses give rise to skin-friction drag, which depends on the blade’s surface roughness, the fluid’s viscosity and density, and the local flow velocity and regime. Concurrently, the rounded edge's geometry obstructs the flow, generating pressure drag that is primarily a function of the cutting-edge radius. The total drag can be expressed as Ftotal=Ffriction+Fpressure.

In the non-Newtonian fluid, the rounded leading edge is projected both onto the direction normal to the incoming flow and onto the flank face. A simplified geometric model is given to depict the cutting edge for simplified calculation, yielding a trapezoidal profile composed of three straight segments: a flat, blunt plane at the fore (Part Ⅰ) and two side segments parallel to the flank face (Part Ⅱ) (Fig. 2).

The former plane adopts the Hiemenz solution51–53, which describes the two-dimensional flow in the vicinity of a stagnation point on a body because the cutting-edge radius induces a stagnation point that profoundly influences local flow behavior and thus the blade’s effective sharpness. The latter approach adopts the Falkner-Skan equation54–56, which characterizes two-dimensional, steady laminar boundary layer flows subject to a pressure gradient.

When the cutting edge is oriented normal to the depth direction, the blade’s skin-friction drag can be resolved into two primary components: the blunt-cutting edge and the wedge-shaped flank face. If the edge were not held perpendicular, additional skin-friction drag would also arise along the edge direction. Typically, the cutting depth is 101 to 102 times the thickness of the cutting edge. Two conditions are necessary to ensure that the induced pressure drag of the blunt edge dominates over the drag of the wedge. Firstly, the apparent viscosity of a non-Newtonian test fluid must be highly nonlinear with respect to shear rate, namely susceptible to changes in shear rate γ. Secondly, the non-Newtonian fluid should exhibit near-perfect plasticity, characterized by a negligible elastic regime and deformation dominated by plastic flow, to provide compliance during cutting, despite being very sticky. And it also possesses sufficiently low interparticle forces so that its zero-shear apparent viscosity remains minimal. Under these circumstances, viscous-shear drag reliably reflects edge sharpness.

In brief, the evaluation of straight blade sharpness in shear-thickening fluids should be conducted at an appropriate cutting speed where the shear-thickening response induced by shear rate is highly coupled to the blunt edge radius.

Numerical simulations

Two candidate starch-suspension formulations were evaluated: one comprising 43.5 wt% cassava starch + 13 wt% high‑amylopectin corn starch + 0.05 wt% xanthan gum, and the other containing 29 wt% cassava starch blended with 26 wt% high‑amylopectin corn starch (Fig. 3). Both mixtures exhibit typical shear thickening at low shear rates and shear thinning at high shear rates; since our sharpness measurements rely predominantly on the high shear‑rate regime, these rheological transitions ensure heightened sensitivity to variations in cutting‑edge radius. The rheological curve obtained by simulation diverges from the two experimentally measured curves in its overall form, with particularly pronounced errors in the high shear-rate regime. Further analysis reveals that this limitation arises primarily from the Cross-model's restricted parameter range in the simulation software, which hinders accurate fitting of the fluid’s actual rheological behavior at elevated shear rates, thereby diminishing the precision of the results. Discussions before this demonstrated that shear-thinning under high-shear conditions enhances the accuracy of sharpness determination; however, due to the aforementioned parameter constraints, the numerical model cannot fully reproduce the accurate shear-thinning response of the raw starch solution at high shear rates. Accordingly, when calibrating the model, one should minimize the viscosity-growth rate in the high shear-rate domain to approximate the fundamental rheological properties as closely as possible, thereby improving the accuracy of simulated sharpness evaluations. To clarify the viscosity conditions emphasized in this study, we provide the corresponding viscosity ranges at shear rates of 10 ~ 1000 s⁻¹. The simulation model yielded viscosities ranging from 12.03 to 906.69 Pa·s. For the experimental formulations, the 29 wt% cassava starch + 26 wt% high‑amylopectin corn starch exhibited viscosities of 1.77 ~ 1221.91 Pa·s, while the 43.5 wt% cassava starch + 13 wt% high‑amylopectin corn starch + 0.05 wt% xanthan gum showed viscosities of 2.48 ~ 875.03 Pa·s.

Compared to conventional solid-material cutting, cutting speed exerts a markedly greater influence on sharpness characterization when machining high shear-responsive fluids. A high-concentration starch suspension behaves as a complex non-Newtonian medium, exhibiting shear thickening at low shear rates and shear thinning at high shear rates. In the sharpness tests conducted at cutting speeds up to 0.5 m/s, thereby generating high shear conditions, shear stress increases with speed, and the gradient of the shear-rate vs. shear-stress curve directly governs the slope of the cutting-speed vs. cutting-force response. Although, as in solid cutting, an increase in contact area at a given pressure requires higher cutting forces, the flow behavior of the fluid near the cutting edge is uniquely tied to local shear rate: edge blunting thickens the boundary layer and reduces shear rate, whereas a sharper edge produces a thinner boundary layer and higher shear rate. Thus, the shear-rate vs. shear-stress relationship of the fluid directly shapes the cutting-speed vs. cutting-force curve. An ideal high-shear-responsive test fluid for sharpness measurement would therefore display a decreasing shear stress with increasing shear rate in the low shear‑rate regime, maximizing sensitivity to edge geometry.

The simulation captures the fundamental interaction between blade geometry and fluid flow (Fig. 3). The rheological relationship between shear rate and viscosity significantly affects the accuracy of quantitative measurements and the sensitivity of sharpness tests. In the simulations, the total cutting force is partitioned into two principal contributions: one acting on the cutting edge itself and the other on the tool body, including the flank face. Each contribution comprises both skin-friction drag and form drag.

Drag generated by the tool body was quantified (Fig. 4d). Both skin-friction and form drag of the tool body are governed primarily by cutting velocity and fluid properties. Because the blade body geometry, whose cross-sectional dimensions are orders of magnitude larger than the edge radius, remains invariant during tests, its drag contribution remains constant under fixed test conditions. Consequently, variations in the measured signal predominantly reflect changes in edge sharpness. For the cutting edge, form drag typically exceeds skin-friction drag by a factor of several; in contrast, (Fig. 4a, b), for the tool body, skin-friction drag outweighs form drag (Fig. 4 cd). Consequently, the total drag of the blade is decided jointly by skin-friction drag and form drag, which are directly linked to the blunt radius and the local pressure distribution. In contrast, its skin-friction drag is directly related to the local shear rate, which increases as the edge radius decreases. In the area of the cutting edge, form drag spans approximately 0.006 ~ 0.018 N, while skin-friction drag lies between about 0.0013 ~ 0.0043 N. As the blade becomes more blunted, the sensitivity of cutting force to cutting speed likewise increases. The variation in skin-friction drag of the cutting edge (≈2.5×) closely matches that of the blunt radius (≈2.6×). While form drag governs the drag of the cutting edge, skin-friction drag shows the greater sensitivity to variations of the blunt radius, which is absent in conventional solid-cutting situations. For the tool body, the skin-friction drag slightly exceeds form drag. Still, its rate of increase diminishes at higher cutting speed, indicating that the fluid-drag effect approaches saturation under high-velocity cutting conditions.

In the results using the simulated fluid, straight blades with their wedge faces present a more streamlined geometry: this reduces form drag along the flank relative to that on the traditional thin-blades but is accompanied by a rise in skin-friction drag (Fig. 5). Thus, the geometry of the blade sets the baseline cutting drag. Although the absolute change in cutting force due to variations in the blunt edge radius is comparable between the tool body and the blunt cutting edge, the straight-blade design yields a higher total cutting force, resulting in a lower relative rate of force variation with respect to the blunt radius. The model assumes shear stress increases with shear rate, in which the skin-friction drag of the blade varies only moderately with the blunt radius. In contrast, real fluids exhibit shear thinning at higher shear rates, which amplifies the effect of the blunt radius on skin-friction drag and indicates that actual fluids are more sensitive to sharpness than predicted by simulations. Real-fluid shear-thinning at high shear rates further heightens sharpness sensitivity by provoking pronounced local flow changes.

Experiments

An optimal shear‑thickening fluid was formulated by blending 43.5 wt% cassava starch with 13 wt% high‑amylopectin corn starch and adding 0.05 wt% xanthan gum, along with 0.5 wt% cesium chloride powder to mitigate sedimentation. To suppress oxidative and microbial degradation of the starch suspension, 1 wt% sodium isoascorbate and 1 wt% sodium lactate were incorporated. All dry powders were thoroughly premixed, then introduced in stages into deionized water under continuous stirring, with vigorous mixing applied just before the viscosity transition and immediately after the final additive was added. The mixture was allowed to rest for 30 minutes to ensure complete hydration and homogeneity. However, the cassava-corn starch matrix alone provides high mechanical strength but low cutting force; the xanthan gum restores and enhances the sensitivity of the cutting force to variations in the cutting‑edge radius.

At high shear rates, the tested non-Newtonian fluid exhibits pronounced shear thinning while retaining sufficient viscosity to generate measurable resistance, yielding markedly superior sharpness discrimination compared to the simulation material (Fig. 6). As cutting depth increases (Fig. 6a–d), the separation between the cutting‑force versus speed curves for different sharpness grades progressively widens. At a shallow depth of 5 mm, the curves remain closely clustered, indicating that edge sharpness has not been fully amplified in its effect on cutting force. At an intermediate depth of 16 mm, distinctiveness becomes apparent, especially at higher speeds, reflecting enhanced sensitivity. At depths of 23 mm and 33 mm, the curves are distinctly separated, allowing for clear differentiation of sharpness levels. By coupling cutting force with cutting depth, cutting speed, and blunt radius, we obtained a power‑law fit (Eq. 20, R2 = 0.9152) (Fig. 6e). The power‑law model closely tracks the experimental data, particularly at 5 mm and 23 mm, with minimal error in both peaks and troughs. This highlights that the nonlinear formulation more comprehensively captures the coupled effects of multiple variables and confirms that greater cutting depths enhance the stability and reliability of sharpness characterization.

In the present study, however, cutting speed varies by more than five times and cutting depth by more than six times, giving rise to obvious nonlinear behavior that invalidates a purely linear approach. By contrast, our power-law model represents the cutting force F as the product of key parameters raised to fitted exponents, thereby capturing the complex nonlinear coupling induced by fluid deformation mechanisms, flow-field variations, and blade geometry. This formulation yields markedly higher fit accuracy across all test regimes. In particular, the exponent for cutting depth is 1.244, indicating a strong depth dependence, suggesting that increasing the blade-length-to-thickness ratio above 40 would further enhance measurement precision. The blunt radius exponent of 0.968 reveals an almost linear sensitivity of cutting force to radius, whereas the velocity exponent of only 0.720 confirms that cutting speed exerts a comparatively weaker influence, an observation that is entirely consistent with our computational flow-field results.

Additional experiments were conducted using five commercial utility blades under identical blunting method, fluid condition, and experimental setup. At a cutting depth of 20 mm, with the edge perpendicular to the cutting direction, the overall cutting drag of utility knives is lower than that of straight blades at the same depth. This reduction is attributed to the thinner blade geometry, which decreases the back-of-blade drag. Despite this, the sensitivity to edge sharpness is preserved, and its relative contribution to the total resistance becomes more pronounced. The relationship between cutting velocity and drag exhibits a more linear trend, with a more stable increase in drag.

Verification of non-destructive advantages

Cutting forces measured during fluid cutting are much lower than those encountered in solid-material machining, and the formation of a boundary layer along the blade surface provides a lubricating effect that reduces tool wear to negligible levels. Consequently, near-steady-state force signals can be acquired without inflicting additional damage on the edge. Straight blades are evaluated before and after fluid cutting; there is no significant change in measured sharpness. For straight blade samples labeled Sharpness 01-05, the calibrated blunt‑radius changes after testing were only +10.90 %, +2.79 %, +1.49 %, −0.85 %, and +0.90 %, respectively (Fig. 7a). The pre‑and post‑test edge status, surface roughness, textural features, and pitting patterns, remain virtually identical, confirming that fluid cutting preserves the macroscopic sharpness of the entire edge (Fig. 7b, c). In contrast to prior studies on plate‑ or line‑type specimens, where wear concentrates at discrete contact points, whole‑edge testing in a fluid medium yields a comprehensive measure of blade sharpness while dramatically reducing material loss. By exploiting the boundary‑layer cushion and velocity gradient inherent in fluid flow, this approach minimizes direct compression and friction between the blade and the workpiece, effectively overcoming the intrinsic drawbacks of traditional solid-block cutting. Another blade underwent multiple tests cutting solid medium (polystyrene foam (EPS), density 25 kg/m³), resulting in some wear and introducing uncertainties in the characterization of sharpness (Fig. 7d, e).

Discussion

In this framework, the sharpness index is primarily governed by the edge radius. The wedge geometry and back-of-blade profile remain constant for a given blade and therefore contribute only to a fixed baseline drag. The fluid-solid interaction serves as a controlled measurement mechanism that converts edge geometry into a measurable viscous response. As a result, variations in the measured drag predominantly reflect changes in edge radius.

The non-Newtonian test medium used in this study is a starch-based sol that exhibits pronounced shear-thinning behavior at high shear rates. This rheological response provides a distinct advantage for tool sharpness characterization: the fluid acts as a sensitive medium in which even small variations in blade edge geometry are effectively translated into measurable resistance forces. In this way, the use of a high-shear-responsive starch sol enhances the sensitivity and reproducibility of sharpness evaluation compared with conventional solid-based tests.

Our investigation establishes a unified framework for assessing straight-blade sharpness in shear-thinning media, with results indicating that the surrounding non-Newtonian fluid response is sensitive to edge geometry. By formulating a fluid whose viscosity profile is carefully tailored, one that is low enough to allow unhindered tool immersion yet sharply responsive to moderate shear, small changes in edge radius translate into clearly distinguishable drag-force signatures. This dual requirement of ease of immersion and amplified shear response proved essential: without rapid shear thinning at high rates, boundary-layer thickening would obscure tip-driven effects, and without adequate stability against sedimentation, long-duration tests would yield inconsistent measurements. In this way, our optimized fluid bridges the gap between practical testability and theoretical sensitivity, enabling the resolution of submicron edge features across a wide matrix of cutting speeds and depths. It should be noted that the present method does not directly resolve Coulombian or adhesion-dominated frictional regimes, which are typically emphasized in solid–solid penetration mechanics. Instead, the cutting tests in this work are performed within a viscous non-Newtonian fluid environment, where the dominant resistance mechanism arises from skin-friction drag rather than classical frictional forces. Under such laminar conditions, small-scale roughness plays only a minor role in altering the resistance, and the measured sharpness index is therefore primarily governed by edge geometry and wear.

Complementary numerical simulations further elucidated the roles of form drag and viscous drag under shear-dependent flow, confirming that knife geometry, especially the blunt radius, imprints itself on cutting-force profiles in predictable ways. Crucially, we discovered that even minor discrepancies between the idealized rheology used in simulations and the fluid’s actual shear-thinning or shear-thickening behavior can meaningfully skew predicted force sensitivities. This finding underscores the importance of incorporating accurate, rate-dependent viscosity curves rather than relying on simplified approximations. In turn, our drag-decomposition approach, which combines the Cross rheological model with Hiemenz stagnation-point solutions and Falkner-Skan boundary-layer theory, provides a rigorous computational foundation for predicting how edge geometry governs the force response in real fluids.

Experimental validation on a purpose-built sharpness-testing platform supported the model predictions and captured the coupled effects of edge radius, cutting speed, and penetration depth. The observed force profiles closely match the simulation outputs when the appropriate fluid rheology is used, thereby validating our analytical sharpness index, a closed-form expression that integrates wedge-flow theory and shear-rate-dependent viscosity. This analytical model offers a direct linkage between blunt radius, wedge angle, and cutting velocity to measurable drag forces, offering a basis for interpreting sharpness-related responses in this study.

Together, these results highlight both the promise and the remaining challenges of fluid-mediated sharpness testing. On one hand, by moving beyond single-point, solid-substrate methods, our fluid-based approach delivers whole-edge diagnostics with reproducibility. On the other hand, this paradigm shift demands careful fluid selection: subtle rheological mismatches can compromise sensitivity, and long-term stability under cyclic shear must be ensured for industrial implementation. We acknowledge that cost considerations require careful reassessment, as the economic context of blade evaluation systems is inherently complex. Future work should therefore focus on expanding the library of candidate fluids, for example, by exploring viscoelastic or thixotropic formulations, and on integrating real-time rheology to adjust model parameters dynamically. Extension to other blade geometries should be evaluated separately in future studies.

Methods

Since the differentiation of the blunt radius of the tool edge is within 0.65 ~ 1.71 µm, the number of meshes on the entire tool wall reaches the million level. An analogical approach is used: the tool is set as a stationary wall. At the same time, the fluid flows through the wall at a velocity equivalent to the actual tool motion, allowing the flow field to be simulated under steady-state single-phase conditions. The blade sharpness tester consists of four integrated subsystems: a cutting-force sensing module, a servo-motor drive controller, an analyzer, and an external computer interfaced with data-processing software (Fig. 9a,b). The blade is mounted vertically beneath the ball-screw drive in a dedicated clamping fixture. A flanged holder sleeve threads onto the tool shank and locks in place with copper nuts, while two manual adjustment bolts advance or retract the blade in 1 mm increments (Fig. 9c, d). The cutter itself is pinched between two precision blocks tightened with six torqued bolts and elastic washers, applied in a cross-symmetrical sequence to ensure uniform clamping. A boat-shaped backing fixture, secured by additional set screws, provides lateral and axial support and allows adjustment of rake, clearance angles, and cutting depth (Fig. 9e, f). By employing eleven discrete line speeds, the experimental protocol enables a systematic assessment of the cutting performance of the blades and the dependence of their measured sharpness on cutting velocity.

The straight blades, fabricated from GU20 cemented carbide by Xiamen Jinlu Special Alloys Co., Ltd., were blunted for 0, 1, 3, 5 and 8 minutes on a dedicated wear rig, then examined by SEM with the cutting edge oriented upward for optimal imaging (Fig. 10). Across these wear intervals, abrasive, adhesive and oxidative mechanisms dominated: initial stages showed only fine wear scars and shallow pits, followed by increased surface roughness, material spallation and chipping as subsurface cracks propagated. In the final stage, extensive spallation, deep fissuring, and broadening of the wear band confirmed severe edge degradation under prolonged loading. For the utility knives (Fig. 9e, f), a commercial trapezoidal blade made of SK4 carbon tool steel was employed. The blade features a double-bevel edge design, which is low-cost, readily available, and structurally simple, while retaining the typical geometric features of thin blades. Wear is observed in section I of the cutting edge.
