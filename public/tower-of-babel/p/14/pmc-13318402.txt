Epidemiology, Genomic Diversity, and Evolutionary Dynamics of Human Metapneumovirus Over 7 Years of Integrated Community‐ and Hospital‐Based Sentinel Surveillance in Senegal, 2018–2024
Authors: Mamadou Malado Jallow, Mamadou Aliou Barry, Ndeye Awa Ndiaye, Marie Pedepa Mendy, Mareme Seye Thiam, Safiétou Sankhe, Samba Niang Sagne, Ndiende Koba Ndiaye, Seynabou Mbaye Ba Souna Diop, Ndaraw Diack, Deborah Goudiaby, Cheikh Loucoubar, Amadou Alpha Sall, Moussa Moïse Diagne, Ndongo Dia

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIn late 2024, a major human metapneumovirus (HMPV) outbreak in China caused widespread pneumonia and thousands of hospitalizations, drawing global attention to this virus. In Senegal, a concomitant rise in HMPV detections was observed in both community and hospital settings. This study aimed to describe the epidemiology, genomic diversity, and the evolutionary dynamics of HMPV strains circulating in Senegal over 7 years of integrated community‐ and hospital‐based surveillance (2018–2024).MethodsNasopharyngeal samples were tested using multiplex real‐time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction. A subset of HMPV isolates underwent sequencing, followed by phylogenetic and Bayesian evolutionary analyses.ResultsHMPV was detected in 3.3% (n = 19,204) of tested specimens. The highest positivity rate was observed among infants aged ≤ 11 months (31.2%), with decreasing rates in older age groups. Following COVID‐19 related disruptions, HMPV circulation re‐established a seasonal pattern after 2022, peaking during the rainy season. Genomic analyses revealed sustained cocirculation of sublineages A2b1, A2b2, B1, and B2, with periodic lineage replacement every 1–3 years. Genetic variability was predominantly observed in the G glycoprotein, with evidence of positive selection at codons 102, 153, 167, 169, 179, 181, and 202. Estimated evolutionary rates were 9.30 × 10−4 substitutions per site per year for HMPV‐A and 9.86 × 10−4 substitutions per site per year for HMPV‐B. Phylogeographic analyses indicate that HMPV epidemics in Senegal are driven by repeated introductions and ongoing exchange with other regions.ConclusionsOur findings indicate sustained circulation and dynamic turnover of pre‐existing sublineages rather than the emergence of a novel variant within the population, especially in infants aged ≤ 11 months.Keywords: epidemiology, genomic diversity, human metapneumovirus, phylodynamics, phylogeography, Senegal, surveillance

Introduction

Human metapneumovirus (HMPV) is a respiratory pathogen first identified in 2001 in the Netherlands [1]. Since then, it has been recognized as one of the leading viral causes of respiratory infections, particularly affecting children, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals [2, 3]. However, retrospective serological studies have shown that HMPV has been circulating in humans for more than half a century prior to its discovery, and almost all children show evidence of past HMPV infection by the age of five [4, 5]. In 2018, an estimated 11.1 million cases of acute lower respiratory infection (ALRI) globally were attributable to HMPV, resulting in approximately 502,000 hospitalizations and 113,000 deaths [6, 7]. At the clinical and epidemiological levels, HMPV is similar to respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), causing illnesses that range from mild respiratory infections to severe bronchiolitis and pneumonia, especially in young children [8, 9]. HMPV exhibits a seasonal pattern of circulation, with peaks typically occurring during late winter and early spring in temperate regions and during the rainy season in tropical countries, overlapping with those of other common respiratory viruses, especially RSV and influenza [10, 11, 12]. Despite the global impact of HMPV on public health, no approved antiviral drugs or licensed vaccine are currently available, although various vaccine candidates are under investigation [13, 14]. Therefore, early diagnosis and preventive measures, such as good hygiene, remain the most promising strategies for controlling seasonal epidemics of the virus.

HMPV is a nonsegmented, negative‐sense, single‐stranded RNA virus belonging to the genus Metapneumovirus in the Pneumoviridae family. Its genome, approximately 13 kb, contains eight genes encoding nine proteins, including the fusion protein and the attachment glycoprotein [15]. To date, two main genetic groups (A and B) have been identified, each further subdivided into six genotypes: A1, A2a, A2b, A2c, B1, and B2 [7, 16, 17]. Generally, several genotypes cocirculate, with the predominant subgroups often being replaced after one or two epidemic seasons [18, 19]. In Sub‐Saharan Africa, data describing the long‐term epidemiology and evolutionary dynamics of HMPV remain limited. In Senegal, continuous sentinel surveillance of influenza‐like illness (ILI) has been in place since 1996 and severe acute respiratory infection (SARI) since 2015. These complementary surveillance efforts enable real‐time monitoring of trends in viral respiratory infections in both community and hospital settings. During the 2024 surveillance season, a notable increase in HMPV infections was observed among both outpatients and in‐patients in Senegal. This unexpected increase in viral circulation raised questions regarding potential changes in viral epidemiology, lineage replacement, or the emergence of genetically distinct variants with potential public health implications. So the present study aimed to describe the epidemiology, genomic diversity, and evolutionary dynamics of HMPV over seven consecutive years of integrated community‐ and hospital‐based surveillance in Senegal (2018–2024).

Material and Methods

Study Design and Sample Collection

This study used respiratory specimens collected between January 2018 and December 2024 as part of the routine activities of the national sentinel surveillance system for influenza and other respiratory viruses in Senegal, coordinated by the National Influenza Center (NIC) at the Institute Pasteur de Dakar (IPD). Samples were obtained from the outpatient surveillance system for ILI and the hospital‐based surveillance system for SARI, implemented within the framework of the WHO Global Influenza Surveillance and Response System (GISRS). Inclusion criteria for surveillance included outpatients and inpatients of all ages attending designated sentinel sites and meeting the ILI or SARI case definitions. During the study period, the surveillance network comprised 32 community sentinel sites for ILI distributed across the 14 administrative regions of Senegal and 7 hospital sentinel sites for SARI located in Dakar. ILI was defined according to the WHO case definition as “an acute respiratory infection with measured temperature of ≥ 38°C and cough or sore throat in an outpatient of any age, with the onset of symptoms within the last 10 days before presentation.” For SARI, the case definition was similar to that of ILI but additionally required hospitalization. Standardized forms were used to record epidemiological and clinical data from enrolled patients. Nasopharyngeal and/or oropharyngeal swabs were collected in a universal viral transport medium (VTM) (Becton Dickinson and company) and promptly transported at a controlled temperature (4°C–8°C) to the laboratory for respiratory pathogen screening. Upon receipt in the laboratory, samples were tested using a multiplex real‐time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) assay, and left‐over clinical specimens were stored at −80°C for biobanking and additional analyses (e.g., NGS sequencing).

Nucleic Acid Extraction and Detection of Respiratory Viruses

Total nucleic acid was extracted from 200 μL of swab suspension in VTM, using the QIAamp Viral RNA Kit (QIAGEN, Valencia, CA, United States), following the manufacturer's protocol. For the detection of viral respiratory pathogens, the AllplexRV Essential Assay (Seegene, Seoul, Republic of Korea) was used and performed on a CFX96 platform (Bio‐Rad, California, United States) and then analyzed using Seegene's Viewer software, as previously described [20].

Whole‐Genome Sequencing of HMPV

For HMPV whole‐genome sequencing, positive samples from all epidemiological weeks included in this study with a relatively high viral load (Ct value < 30) were selected for NGS sequencing using an Illumina sequencing platform and the Twist Respiratory Virus Research Panel (103067; Twist Biosciences, San Francisco, CA), as previously described [20]. To generate the consensus genomes of HMPV in all samples, raw reads from Illumina sequencing in FASTQ format were processed and analyzed using the Chan‐Zuckerberg ID (CZ ID), a cloud based open‐source bioinformatics pipeline for metagenomics sequencing data (https://czid.org/, accessed on January 31, 2025).

Prediction of Potential N‐Glycosylation Sites

Posttranslational modifications such as N‐linked glycosylation sites were predicted using the NetNGlyc 1.01 webserver (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/NetNGlyc, accessed on February 3, 2025), with default settings for the F and G proteins to identify sequence motifs N–X–S/T, where X can be any amino acid except Asp or Pro. Only sites with an average potential score > 0.5 were considered as potential glycosylation sites.

Selection Pressure Analysis

Selection pressures acting on each gene were investigated by estimating the ratio (ω) of nonsynonymous (dN) to synonymous (dS) mutations (ω = dN/dS) using the HyPhy software package accessed via the Datamonkey web server (https://www.datamonkey.org/, accessed on February 3, 2025). Four different methods were used: fixed effects likelihood (FEL), single likelihood ancestor counting (SLAC), mixed effects model of evolution (MEME), and fast unconstrained Bayesian approximation (FUBAR). An ω < 1 indicates negative or purifying selection pressure; ω = 1 implies neutral evolution; and ω > 1 indicates positive selection. For the SLAC, FEL, and MEME methods, sites with p values < 0.1 were accepted as candidates for selection, whereas for FUBAR a posterior probability > 0.9 was considered significant. As results can differ depending on the chosen method, sites selected by at least three methods were considered robust.

Phylodynamic and Phylogeographic Analysis of HMPV

For phylodynamic and phylogeographic analyses, complete HMPV genomes generated in this study were classified into Groups A and B and combined with temporally and geographically diverse global reference sequences retrieved from GeneBank (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov, accessed on January 31, 2025). Each dataset was aligned separately using MAFFT v7.526 [21]. Maximum likelihood phylogenetic trees were reconstructed for each group using IQ‐TREE v3.0.1 [22], and temporal signal was assessed by root‐to‐tip in TempEst v.1.5.3 to identify outliers and evaluate their suitability for molecular clock analyses. Model selection for nucleotide substitution, molecular clock, and demographic priors was performed using stepping‐stone sampling and marginal likelihood comparison. Final Bayesian analyses were performed using BEAST v1.10.5 [23] under a General Time Reversible (GTR) substitution model with a relaxed exponential molecular clock (REC). Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains were run for 400 million iterations, with sampling every 400,000 iterations. Convergence was confirmed (ESS > 200) in Tracer v1.7.2 [24]. Maximum clade credibility (MCC) trees were summarized with TreeAnnotator v1.10.4 and visualized in FigTree v1.4.4. Stochastic mapping of introductions was performed from the MCC tree using phytools v2.0 (100 simulations, make.simmap), with state transitions interpreted as introduction events. Results were visualized as colored phylogenies, geographic flow maps, and summary plots in R (3.0.1 version).

Data Management and Statistical Analysis

Both ILI and SARI case‐based data were entered into an EpiInfo database, merged with laboratory results, and subsequently analyzed using R statistical software (R.3.0.1 version). Chi‐square (χ 2) and Fisher's exact tests were used to support the comparisons of the categorical data, where a p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Uncertainty was expressed as 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Results

Demographic and Clinical Characteristics of Enrolled Patients

Over the seven consecutive years of influenza and other respiratory viruses surveillance (January 2018 to December 2024), a total of 19,204 patients with acute respiratory infections (ARIs) were enrolled. Among them, 15,432 (80.4%) were outpatients with clinical evidence of ILI, whereas 3772 (19.6%) were inpatients with SARIs. The highest number of enrolled patients was recorded in 2024 (30.5%; 5862/19,204) and the lowest in 2020 (8.2%; 1580/19,204). The male‐to‐female sex ratio was 1 (9617/9538). The age of enrolled patients ranged from 1 month to 100 years, with a mean age of 14.7 years and a median age of 6 years. Regarding symptoms, although it was not always reported on the clinical form, all enrolled patients had fever, as it was the main inclusion criterion for the surveillance. The other symptoms commonly reported were cough (87.6%), rhinitis (34.6%), and headache (30.2%) (Table 1).

Detection of HMPV

Of the 19,204 samples analyzed during the study period, 631 were confirmed positive for HMPV, representing an overall positivity rate of 3.3%. The HMPV positivity rate was higher in in‐patients with SARI (5.03%, 190/3772) compared to outpatient with ILI (2.8%, 441/15,432). The age of patients infected with HMPV ranged from 1 month to 85 years, with mean age of 14.8 years and a median age of 6 years. No significant difference was observed in HMPV detection between male and female patients (p = 0.058). Regarding the age distribution, although HMPV infections were observed in all studied age groups, detection rates varied significantly (p < 0.005), decreasing with patient age. Infants aged ≤ 11 months were the most infected group, accounting for 31.2% (197/631) of detected cases. HMPV infections declined rapidly after the age of 2, with detection rates decreasing from 29.5% in children aged 1–2 years (186/631) to 3.3% in patients aged 11–15 years (21/631). The most common symptoms of HMPV infection are cough (87.6%) and fever (84.6%), followed by rhinitis (34.6%). The HMPV positivity rate was highest in 2024 with 37.9% (239/631) and lowest in 2019 with 0.9% (6/631) (Table 2).

Among HMPV‐positive patients, 69.4% (438/631) had single infections, whereas 30.6% (193/631) were coinfected with at least one other respiratory virus. The most common coinfecting viruses were rhinovirus, detected in 59 cases (9.3%), followed by seasonal influenza H3N2 in 38 cases (6%), adenovirus in 23 cases (3.6%), SARS_CoV‐2 in 19 cases (3%), and parainfluenza viruses (PIV) in 18 cases (2.8%). Coinfections of HMPV with respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) were identified in 19 patients (3%).

Temporal Distribution of HMPV Cases

The trends in the epidemiology of HMPV transmission before, during, and after the COVID‐19 pandemic were analyzed and are shown in Figure 1. Prior to 2020, the virus circulated at very low levels, with sporadic cases (1.1%; 20/1779 in 2018 and 0.3%; 6/1856 in 2019) in the population, without a clear seasonal distribution. During the COVID‐19 pandemic period (2020–2021), although we observed a slight increase in HMPV infections (1.96%; 31/1580 in 2020 and 1.92%; 49/2545 in 2021) compared to prepandemic levels, we noticed a temporal shift from the usual seasonal distribution, with a peak in September. HMPV typically exhibited higher activity during the rainy season in Senegal (May to September). From 2022, following the easing of the COVID‐19 restrictions, we observed an unprecedented increase in HMPV infections (7%: 179/2564 in 2022; 3.5%: 107/3018 in 2023; and 4.1%: 239/5862 in 2024) among outpatients, as well as a rise in pediatric hospitalizations. There was a systematic increase in HMPV circulation in the population from May through September, with a consistent peak in June each year.

Genome Sequencing and Temporal Distribution of HMPV Genotypes

For whole‐genome sequencing of HMPV, 204 positive samples (112 SARI cases and 92 ILI cases) were selected. In total, NGS sequencing yielded 137 HMPV genomic sequences, mostly from samples collected in 2024 (41.6%; 57/137) and 2023 (15.3%; 21/137). Sequences analysis using the online HMPV typing tool available in Nexclade (https://clades.nextstrain.org, accessed February 24, 2025) showed that 61.3% (84/137) of the strains belonged to Clade A and 38.7% (53/137) to Clade B. Within Clade A, the A2b1 and A2b2 sublineages represented 54.8% (46/84) and 45.2% (38/84) of the strains, respectively, whereas the B1 and B2 genotypes within Clade B circulated in comparable proportions with 49% (26/53) and 51% (27/53), respectively. Temporal analysis of the genotypes revealed cocirculation of several HMPV lineages during the study period, with changes in predominance depending on the year. In 2018, Genotype B1 was the predominant circulating HMPV lineage, and then, genotype A2b2 became dominant between 2019 and 2022. In 2023, Genotype B2 emerged as the dominant lineage, accompanied by the reappearance of Genotype A2b1 after a 4‐year absence. In 2024, HMPV infections were primarily associated with Genotype A2b1, whereas A2b2, B1, and B2 circulated at low frequencies. During the entire study period, no A1 or A2a genotypes were detected (Figure 2).

Analysis of Potential N‐Glycosylation Sites in the F and G Genes

After scanning the fusion (F) protein and the attachment (G) glycoprotein using the NetNGlyc 1.0 webserver, three conserved N‐linked glycosylation sites were predicted in the F protein at amino acid positions 57–60 (NLTC), 172–175 (NLTR), and 353–356 (NIST). In the G protein, different patterns of N‐glycosylation sites were predicted among the various HMPV sublineages. Four N‐glycosylation sites were identified in strains belonging to the A2b1 genotype at positions 52–55 (NYTI), 140–143 (NRTK), 155–158 (NTTS), and 145–148 (NSTV). In A2b2 strains, three sites were predicted at amino acid positions 30–33 (NASL), 52–55 (NYTI), and 88–91 (NPSS). In B1 strains, three conserved N‐glycosylation sites were predicted at positions 101–104 (NPTL), 165–168 (NQTT), and 177–180 (NTTN), with some strains presenting additional potential sites at positions 169–172 (NQTT), 167–170 (NTSQ), and 163–166 (NTSQ). Viruses of the B2 genotype presented the highest number of potential N‐glycosylation sites, with seven conserved sites identified at positions 28–31 (NATL), 30–33 (NATL), 58–61 (NMTK), 177–180 (NTTN), 178–181 (NTTS), 180–183 (NQTS), and 196–199 (NGTT).

Selection Pressure Analyses

In this study, we analyzed sites under positive and negative selection using five selection models: SLAC, FEL, IFEL, MEME, and FUBAR and the results are summarized in Table 3. The analyses revealed that the evolutionary dynamics of HMPV strains circulating in Senegal between 2018 and 2024 were dominated by strong purifying selection across most genes. The M, M2, and SH genes showed no evidence of pervasive positive selection, with numerous codons identified under significant negative selection. However, two positively selected codons at Positions 49 and 152 in the N gene and one positively selected codon at Position 112 in the P gene were detected only by MEME. In contrast, limited but notable adaptive evolution was observed in the F and L genes: The fusion protein F contained one positively selected codon at position 503 detected by FUBAR and one positively selected codon at position 143 detected by MEME, whereas the L polymerase exhibited several episodically selected sites identified by MEME (six sites) and one site at position 1821 by FUBAR. The strongest signals of positive selection were found in the attachment G glycoprotein, which displayed multiple positively selected codons across all models, including several sites detected by more than one algorithm (codon 169 detected by all four models and codons 167 and 179 detected by three models), providing robust evidence of diversifying selection.

Temporal Signal and Bayesian Evolutionary Dynamics of HMPV

To investigate the evolutionary dynamics of HMPV strains circulating in Senegal, publicly available HMPV reference genomes were combined with the consensus genomes generated in this study. Root‐to‐tip regression analysis revealed a clear temporal signal for both HMPV groups. For HMPV‐A, a strong linear relationship between genetic divergence and sampling time was observed (R 2 = 0.87), with an estimated slope rate of 9.84 × 10−4. HMPV‐B also exhibited a good temporal signal, although slightly weaker (R 2 = 0.76), with a slope corresponding to a rate of 8.52 × 10−4. Time‐scaled Bayesian phylogenetic reconstruction based on complete genomes revealed the persistent cocirculation of multiple genotypes in Senegal, including A2b1 and A2b2 within HMPV‐A, and B1 and B2 within HMPV‐B (Figure 3). These Senegalese strains circulating between 2018 and 2024 clustered with contemporaneous strains from diverse geographic regions, indicating repeated viral introduction events within Senegal followed by local transmission and regional dissemination. For HMPV‐A, the maximum clade credibility (MCC) tree dated the tMRCA to 1992.5 (95% HPD: 1985.6–1998.7), indicating that the contemporary diversity of HMPV‐A circulating globally and within Senegal likely emerged in the early 1990s. In contrast, HMPV‐B exhibited a substantially older tMRCA, with a mean estimate of 1972.4 (95% HPD: 1957.6–1984.6), suggesting earlier diversification of this genotype. Bayesian molecular clock analyses conducted under a relaxed uncorrelated lognormal clock model yielded comparable evolutionary rates for the two HMPV groups. The mean evolutionary rate for HMPV‐A was estimated at 9.30 × 10−4 substitutions per site per year (95% HPD: 8.12 × 10−4 to 1.05 × 10−3), whereas HMPV‐B exhibited a slightly higher mean rate of 9.86 × 10−4 substitutions per site per year (95% HPD: 8.73 × 10−4 to 1.10 × 10–3).

Phylogeographic Reconstruction and Viral Migration Patterns

After establishing the temporal structure and evolutionary history of our datasets, we next investigated the spatial diffusion dynamics of HMPV genotypes. Discrete phylogeographic reconstruction revealed that Senegal is embedded within a globally interconnected transmission network for both HMPV‐A and HMPV‐B. HMPV epidemics in Senegal were characterized by multiple independent introductions into the country over the study period, originating from diverse geographic regions. For HMPV‐A, viral introduction events were inferred from Europe (the Netherlands and Switzerland), North America (United States), South America (Peru), and Asia (China), with additional evidence of viral exports toward North America (United States), Europe (the Netherlands and Switzerland), and Asia (Japan). Similarly, HMPV‐B showed repeated introductions from Europe (Netherlands), North America (United States), Asia (China), and Oceania (Australia), alongside outbound dissemination toward Kenya, United States, China, and Australia. These bidirectional migration patterns suggest recurrent international seeding events into Senegal followed by local amplification and onward transmission, highlighting sustained global connectivity in HMPV spread during 2018–2024 (Figure 4).

Discussion

In this study, we present a comprehensive description of the epidemiological patterns, genomic diversity, and evolutionary dynamics of HMPV in Senegal over seven consecutive years of integrated ILI and SARI sentinel surveillance (2018–2024). The overall HMPV positivity rate was 3.3%, similar to that observed in our previous study conducted between 2012 and 2016, in which HMPV was detected in 3.75% [25], suggesting relatively stable circulation of HMPV among patients with ARI in Senegal in recent years. However, a similar study conducted in Côte d'Ivoire by Kadjo et al. [26] reported a higher detection (6.23%). This discrepancy is likely attributable to differences in study design and local climatic conditions. In contrast to studies from China, where the lowest positivity rate for HMPV was observed in infants < 1 year of age and infection rates increased with age [27, 28], our findings showed a different pattern. Infants aged ≤ 11 months were the most affected group, with infection rates decreasing with age and declining markedly after 2 years. This trend likely reflects early‐life immunological naïveté and the progressive acquisition of immunity following repeated infections. Additionally, the high proportion of children < 5 years in our study population (47.7%) may have contributed to the observed distribution. These findings highlight the importance of targeted prevention and healthcare strategies for pediatric populations. Although HMPV circulation fluctuated over the study period, we identified a clear seasonal pattern, with increased activity during the rainy season (May–September) in Senegal. In contrast, studies from other tropical regions have reported year‐round circulation with less pronounced seasonality. For instance, continuous HMPV activity has been documented in Côte d'Ivoire [26] and Vietnam [29], with infections occurring throughout the year and variable seasonal peaks. Global surveillance analyses further indicate that HMPV seasonality varies substantially across geographic regions, with tropical settings often exhibiting weaker or irregular patterns compared with temperate climates [30]. The COVID‐19 pandemic significantly disrupted the circulation of many respiratory viruses, including HMPV. As observed in several countries, including Israel, Australia, South Korea, and Spain [31, 32, 33, 34], HMPV activity in Senegal declined sharply during the pandemic period. Following the gradual relaxation of nonpharmaceutical interventions (NPIs), HMPV circulation returned to its prepandemic seasonal pattern from 2022 onward, with a notable increase in positivity rates among ILI and SARI cases. This resurgence could be explained by an accumulation of susceptible individuals due to the prolonged reduction in HMPV exposure during the pandemic, particularly among young children who had not acquired early natural immunity. Although HMPV infection induces humoral and cellular immune responses, acquired immunity appears to be incomplete and of limited duration, with reinfections frequently reported throughout life [35, 36]. Furthermore, decreased maternal exposure to HMPV during the pandemic may have contributed to a reduction in the passive transfer of protective antibodies to infants [7]. Young children, who were particularly represented among cases of ILI and SARI cases in this study, may therefore have been more vulnerable when viral circulation resumed. Similar observations have been reported by Reicherz et al. [37] for its closely related family member RSV. The genetic analysis of 137 genomes revealed that currently circulating HMPV strains in Senegal belong to four sublineages: A2b1, A2b2, B1, and B2. This finding is consistent with reports from several countries worldwide, including China [38], the United States [39], Mexico [40], and Côte d'Ivoire [26]. Although Arshad et al. [41] reported the circulation of the A2a genotype in Pakistan, no strains belonging to this sublineage were detected in our study, and no viruses from the A1 lineage were identified. These findings support the hypothesis that older lineages such as A1 and A2a are progressively being replaced by emerging variants, particularly those within the A2b lineage [28]. In line with previous studies [28, 34], the temporal distribution of HMPV clades in Senegal over the 7‐year surveillance period followed an alternating pattern B, A, A, B, B, B, A, suggesting cyclical shifts in the predominance of HMPV‐A and HMPV‐B approximately every 1–3 years. At the genotype level, B1 predominated in 2018, followed by A2b2 during the COVID‐19 period (2019–2021), which was subsequently replaced by B1 in 2022 and B2 in 2023. In contrast to China, where the 2024 winter epidemic was triggered by the clade B2 [27], HMPV infections in Senegal during the same period were predominantly linked to the A2b1 genotype, which had not circulated locally for more than 4 years. This re‐emergence may explain the observed increase in HMPV cases, possibly due to reduced population immunity to this lineage. Posttranslational modifications such as glycosylation at antigenic sites are well‐known mechanisms of viral immune evasion. In HMPV, N‐ and O‐linked glycans on surface glycoproteins may shield antigenic epitopes from host antibody recognition [42, 43, 44]. In this study, sequences analyses showed that although the F protein contained three conserved N‐glycosylation (Positions 57, 172, and 353), the G protein exhibited substantial variability in both the number and distribution of these sites across genotypes. Notably, B2 strains displayed the highest number of predicted N‐glycosylation sites, with seven conserved sites identified. This observation aligns with previous reports highlighting the high variability of the HMPV G protein [40, 45]. Selection pressure on HMPV indicated that most genes are subject to strong purifying selection. Although some models identified potential positively selected sites in the N, P, F, and L genes, these signals were weak and detected only by a single method. In contrast, the G gene showed consistent and robust signals of positive selection across all models, confirming it as the most variable region of the HMPV genome. Several codons (Positions 102, 153, 167, 169, 179, 181, and 202) were identified as being under positive selection, with codon 169 detected by all four models. Although the functional significance of this codon remains unknown, this observation suggests that it may be subject to evolutionary pressures related to viral adaptation or interactions with the host immune system, as has been reported for other variable regions of the G gene [46]. Conversely, internal proteins such as N, P, M, and M‐2 appear to be functionally constrained and evolve primarily under purifying selection to maintain essential roles in viral replication and assembly. Similar evolutionary patterns have been reported in previous studies [34].

Phylodynamic analyses revealed similar evolutionary dynamics between the HMPV‐A and HMPV‐B lineages, with evolutionary rates comparable to those reported in genomic studies conducted in the United States [47, 48]. Our results also suggest an earlier emergence of the HMPV‐B lineages compared to the HMPV‐A lineages, in agreement with observations reported by Nao et al. [49] in Japan. Phylogeographic reconstructions indicate that HMPV epidemics in Senegal likely resulted from multiple independent introductions from different regions of the world. Both HMPV‐A and HMPV‐B clades showed evidence of bidirectional viral flow, reflecting Senegal's integration into global transmission networks. Similar patterns have been described for other respiratory viruses, including Influenza and RSV, where human mobility and international exchanges play an important role in viral introduction and spread [26]. The potential contribution of Asia as a major source region is consistent with previous phylogeographic studies that have identified this region as an important reservoir and center of diversification for HMPV [9].

This study has several limitations. First, although the surveillance covered 39 sentinel sites, it may not fully represent the entire country and is subject to selection bias. Second, despite the large number of enrolled ARI cases (n = 19,204), the number of sequenced samples (137/631) remains limited, potentially underrepresenting viral diversity. Third, the uneven availability of global genomic data, particularly from Africa, may bias phylodynamic and phylogeographic inferences.

Conclusion

In summary, this 7‐year integrated community‐ and hospital‐based surveillance provides a comprehensive overview of the epidemiology, genomic diversity, and evolutionary dynamics of HMPV in Senegal. Overall, our study shows that HMPV contributes to a moderate but consistent proportion of respiratory infections (3.3%), with infants ≤ 11 months representing the most affected group. HMPV circulation exhibited a clear seasonal pattern aligned with the rainy season (May–September), although this pattern was temporarily disrupted during the COVID‐19 pandemic, followed by a pronounced resurgence after the relaxation of public health measures. Genomic analyses revealed the cocirculation of multiple genotypes (A2b1, A2b2, B1, and B2), with the dominant genotype being replaced every 1–3 years, highlighting the dynamic nature of HMPV evolution. Our findings also suggest that the re‐emergence of the A2b1 genotype after several years of absence was likely a key driver of the unusual increase in HMPV cases observed in 2024. Phylodynamic and phylogeographic analyses further highlighted similar evolutionary rates for both HMPV‐A and HMPV‐B and showed that epidemics in Senegal are driven by multiple independent introductions and sustained by bidirectional viral exchange with other regions, emphasizing the importance of global connectivity in local transmission dynamics. Although currently circulating HMPV strains do not exhibit distinctive genomic features warranting heightened concern, this study underscores the need for sustained, integrated genomic, and epidemiological surveillance to strengthen preparedness for future public health challenges.
