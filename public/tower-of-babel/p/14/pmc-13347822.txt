Greenhouse gas emissions from surgical care: a retrospective cohort study of scope 1 and scope 2 emissions across specialties and operative approaches
Authors: Yoshimasa Akashi, Kumiko Yamada, Koichi Ogawa, Yohei Owada, Tsuyoshi Enomoto, Kinji Furuya, Shinji Hashimoto, Kazuhiro Takahashi, Osamu Shimomura, Yoshihiro Miyazaki, Tatsuya Oda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo quantify Scope 1 and Scope 2 greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions associated with surgical care and identify key modifiable drivers at the institutional level, including operative duration and operating room (OR) type.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingA tertiary university hospital with 17 ORs in Japan.ParticipantsAll 6996 elective surgeries were performed under general anaesthesia between 1 January and 31 December 2022.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was per-case total Scope 1 and Scope 2 carbon dioxide equivalent emissions (CO₂e) emissions. Secondary analyses evaluated emission differences across specialties, surgical approaches (open, endoscopic, and robotic), and temporal electricity consumption patterns. Multivariable linear regression was performed to assess the associations between per-case scope 2 emissions, operative time, and OR type.ResultsTotal emissions were 1943.4 t CO₂e, of which Scope 1 accounted for 71.0%, predominantly driven by desflurane (69.2% of total emissions). Although scope 2 accounted for 29.0% of total institutional emissions, scope 1 emissions dominated the carbon footprint of most individual procedures because of volatile anaesthetic use. Weekday daytime electricity consumption accounted for only 47.7% of total weekly use, with more than half occurring during nighttime and weekend periods, largely due to continuous heating, ventilation and air conditioning (HVAC) operation. Regression analysis demonstrated that operative time had a limited independent effect on per-case scope 2 emissions, whereas OR type—including hybrid and bio-clean rooms—was strongly associated with higher emissions.ConclusionsAnaesthetic choice and infrastructure-related energy demand are the principal determinants of surgical GHG emissions. Within direct and energy-related emissions, reducing volatile anaesthetic use and optimising OR energy consumption—particularly during non-operative periods—represent the most effective strategies for emission reduction.

Introduction

Climate change poses an escalating and urgent threat to global health, with the healthcare sector responsible for an estimated 4–10% of worldwide (GHG) emissions..13 Within hospitals, (ORs) are among the most energy-intensive and resource-dependent environments, generating substantial waste4 and requiring continuous high power ventilation, lighting, and specialised equipment.5 As global attention increasingly turns toward sustainable healthcare systems, understanding and mitigating the environmental impact of surgical care has become a critical priority.6

The carbon footprint of a process represents the total direct and indirect GHG emissions produced, expressed in terms of carbon dioxide equivalents (CO₂e).7 Although CO₂ constitutes the majority of healthcare-related emissions, other gases—including nitrous oxide (N₂O) and volatile anaesthetic agents—have substantially higher global warming potentials and therefore contribute disproportionately to climate impact.8 The Greenhouse Gas Protocol,9 the most widely adopted international emissions accounting framework, categorises emissions into three scopes: scope 1 (direct on-site emissions), scope 2 (indirect emissions from purchased energy), and scope 3 (all other upstream and downstream emissions). Identifying the relative contributions of these areas to surgical care is essential for guiding effective decarbonisation strategies.

Despite the growing interest in environmentally sustainable surgery, three major evidence gaps persist. First, most existing studies use procedure-specific life cycle assessments (LCAs) to evaluate individual operations; however, heterogeneity in system boundaries and methodologies limits comparability across studies and does not provide a comprehensive picture of surgical services.10 Second, few studies have assessed GHG emissions across multiple surgical specialties within a consistent institutional environment, leaving uncertainty regarding how different clinical fields contribute to total surgical emissions.11 Third, limited evidence exists on how surgical approaches—open, endoscopic, and robotic—differ in their carbon footprints when performed under similar perioperative conditions.12

Although a comprehensive analysis of scope 1–3 emissions across surgical approaches is needed, annualised scope 3 data, particularly those related to instrument manufacturing, consumables, and robotic system supply chains, were not consistently available across all procedures at our institution. While the present study was originally designed to evaluate the full scope 1–3 footprint of surgery using 1 year of institutional data, only scopes 1 and 2 could be analysed comprehensively. To address this limitation, scope 3 emissions for four major cancer procedures (gastrectomy, low anterior resection, lobectomy, and total hysterectomy) were evaluated separately using a process-based LCA in our previously published work.13 This complementary analysis provides additional insights into the differences in scope 3 emissions among the open, endoscopic, and robotic approaches, whereas the current study focuses on scopes 1 and 2 across all surgical procedures performed at our institution.

To address the remaining evidence gaps, we conducted a retrospective cohort analysis of all general anaesthesia surgeries performed at a tertiary academic hospital over a 12-month period. Using standardised accounting methods aligned with the Greenhouse Gas Protocol, we quantified Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions across surgical specialties, procedures, and operative approaches. By identifying the major emission drivers and modifiable factors, this study provides practical evidence to inform anaesthesia choices, OR energy management, policy development, and decarbonisation strategies aimed at advancing sustainable surgical care.

We hypothesised that GHG emissions would differ across surgical specialties, procedures, and operative approaches, with higher emissions associated with surgeries requiring energy-intensive technologies, such as robotic systems or cardiopulmonary bypass devices.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

Setting

The study was performed at a tertiary academic university hospital in Japan with 17 ORs (ORs), including two bio-clean rooms and three hybrid suites (two angiography and one MRI-equipped) (online supplemental table S1). Approximately 7000 general anaesthesia cases are performed annually at our hospital.

Participants

All surgeries requiring general anaesthesia between 1 January and 31 December 2022 were included. Emergency procedures were excluded because perioperative workflows, staffing, and OR allocation differ substantially from those of elective surgeries and may confound comparisons across specialties. The procedures were performed by multiple board-certified surgeons and trainees under supervision at a tertiary academic centre.

Data collection and variables

Data extraction followed a pre-specified protocol to ensure reproducibility and consistency of the results.

Surgical procedure classification

Surgical procedures were categorised according to a hospital-standardised taxonomy. For comparative analyses across specialties, up to three of the most frequently performed procedures (>10 cases per year) were selected from 11 surgical fields: cardiovascular surgery (CV), endocrine surgery (EN), general surgery (GEN), gynaecology (GYN), hepatobiliary and pancreatic surgery (HBP), lower gastrointestinal surgery (LGI), neurosurgery (NEU), orthopaedics (ORT), respiratory surgery (RES), upper gastrointestinal surgery (UGI), and urology (URO).

To compare emissions across different surgical approaches, four cancer-related procedures commonly performed using open, endoscopic, and robotic techniques were included: distal gastrectomy, low anterior resection, lobectomy, and total hysterectomy.

Study size

All eligible cases performed over 12 months were included; therefore, no formal sample size calculation was required.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean±SD. For comparisons among the surgical approaches (open, endoscopic, and robotic), the homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test when the variances were equal, and the Games-Howell test when the variances were unequal.

To examine the factors associated with scope 2 emissions, a multivariable linear regression analysis was performed with scope 2 CO₂e per case as the dependent variable and operative time and OR type as independent variables. The OR type was included as a categorical variable using dummy coding. Model fit was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R²) and multicollinearity was evaluated using the variance inflation factor.

Linear regression analyses shown in online supplemental figure S1 were performed using the operative time as the independent variable and scope 2 emissions as the dependent variable.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version V.28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA), and a two-sided p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of this study.

Results

Overall surgical volume and emissions

During the study period, 6996 surgeries were performed under general anaesthesia. The combined carbon footprint of all procedures was 1943.4 t CO2e, with Scope 1 and Scope 2 contributing 71.0% and 29.0%, respectively (table 1). Desflurane was the dominant emission source, accounting for 1344.8 t CO2e (69.2%). Conversely, direct CO2 emissions were minimal, amounting to approximately 2.5 t CO2e (0.1%).

Electrical energy consumption (scope 2)

Although scope 2 emissions comprised approximately 30% of the total operating theatre emissions (table 1), their contribution at the individual procedure level was mostly less than <5% (online supplemental figure S2A). Therefore, temporal analyses by time of day and day of the week were performed to better delineate the scope 2 emission dynamics.

On weekdays, the HVAC electricity consumption increased during the daytime (average 76.6 kWh) and decreased at night (average 47.7 kWh) (figure 3A). On weekends, the HVAC electricity consumption remained relatively constant at approximately 45–55 kWh throughout the 24-hour period. Meanwhile, the daily energy consumption for plugs and lighting in each operating theatre increased to approximately 2–3 kWh during the day in all rooms except Rroom 1 and decreased to less than <1 kWh at night on weekdays (figure 3B). Room 1 exhibited significantly high power consumption, not only during the day (approximately 5 kWh) but also at night when it was not in use (approximately 4 kWh). This is because Rroom 1 is a hybrid OR that can use MRI during surgery and has the largest area (170 m²), including the MRI room and its control room (online supplemental table S1).

On weekdays, the average daytime electricity consumption during scheduled operations was 1337 kWh, whereas the night-time consumption was 916 kWh, corresponding to 68.5% of the daytime use (figure 3C). During weekends, when no scheduled surgeries were performed and only limited emergency cases occurred, the mean daily electricity consumption remained high at 1382 kWh, representing 63.1% of the weekday average (2187 kWh). Consequently, daytime electricity use during the 5 weekday (Monday to Friday) operating periods accounted for only 47.7% of the total weekly consumption, indicating that more than half of the OR electricity demand occurred outside the scheduled operating hours, which was largely driven by continuous HVAC operation (figure 3D).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive, institutional-level assessment of the GHG emissions associated with surgical care by quantifying Scope 1 and Scope 2 contributions across multiple specialties, procedures, and operative approaches. Two principal findings have emerged. First, scope 1 emissions, which are largely attributable to desflurane use, were the dominant contributors to both overall and procedure-specific carbon footprints. Second, although the total scope 2 emissions increased with operative duration, the differences between procedures and operative approaches were modest after accounting for time. Multivariate analysis indicated that OR infrastructure, particularly baseline energy demand associated with HVAC operation, exerted a greater influence on electricity-related emissions than procedure-specific technologies. Collectively, these findings suggest that the environmental footprint of surgical care is determined by both clinical practice and institutional energy systems, with infrastructure-related factors playing a larger role than is often recognised.

Consistent with previous studies,5 15 16 volatile anaesthetic agents—particularly desflurane— were the principal contributors to scope 1 emissions. Although desflurane remains clinically useful in selected contexts because of its rapid emergence profile17 modelling in the present study suggested that replacing desflurane with sevoflurane could reduce scope 1 emissions by approximately 90%. Although the environmental impact of desflurane remains a subject of debate,18 its contribution to perioperative GHG emissions in the present study was substantial, accounting for nearly 70% of total emissions. These findings reinforce anaesthetic choice as one of the most readily modifiable determinants of perioperative GHG emissions.

Our initial hypothesis was that procedures requiring energy-intensive equipment, such as robotic systems or cardiopulmonary bypass devices, would exhibit significantly higher scope 2 emissions than other procedures. However, scope 2 emissions were remarkably similar across specialties and procedures after accounting for the operative duration. Although neurosurgical procedures and total knee arthroplasty showed higher electricity-related emissions per hour, these differences were largely attributable to the use of hybrid or bio-clean ORs rather than the procedures. Similarly, while robotic procedures exhibited slightly higher scope 1 and 2 emissions than open or endoscopic approaches, the differences were primarily explained by longer operating durations and were modest after normalisation for time. Previous studies have reported conflicting findings regarding the environmental impact of robotic surgery, with some demonstrating a higher carbon footprint than conventional approaches12 19 and others reporting comparable or lower emissions because of differences in operative efficiency and postoperative recovery.20 Taken together, our findings suggest that within scope 1 and scope 2 emissions, procedural and approach-related differences are relatively small compared with the influence of OR infrastructure. However, previous studies have reported that scope 3 emissions associated with robotic surgery are comparable to or greater than scope 2 emissions,12 19 and our own analysis similarly demonstrated higher scope 3 emissions for robotic procedures than those for conventional approaches.13 Therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as indicating equivalent total carbon footprints for all surgical approaches.

The most notable finding of the present study was the substantial contribution of baseline OR energy demand to scope 2 emissions. Multivariable analysis demonstrated that OR type had a stronger association with electricity-related emissions than operative duration, indicating that infrastructure and environmental control requirements exert a greater influence on scope 2 emissions than procedural characteristics alone. This interpretation is supported by the observation that only 47.7% of the total weekly electricity consumption occurred during scheduled weekday operating hours, whereas more than half was consumed during nights and weekends. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that HVAC systems account for the majority of OR energy consumption.5 21 Importantly, a substantial proportion of this out-of-hours energy use is potentially modifiable.22 Strategies such as HVAC system upgrades and setback protocols for unused ORs have been shown to reduce energy consumption while maintaining appropriate environmental conditions and infection control standards.22 23 Together, these results highlight institutional energy management, particularly HVAC optimisation, as a key opportunity for reducing scope 2 GHG emissions from surgical care.

This study has several limitations. First, case-level CO₂ insufflation data were unavailable and annual procurement records were used for estimation; however, the overall contribution of CO2 to total emissions was negligible. Second, the carbon footprint of propofol was not included because standardised life-cycle inventory data remain limited, although previous studies suggest that its contribution is substantially smaller than that of volatile anaesthetic agents.24 Third, because room-level HVAC electricity data were unavailable, HVAC-related electricity consumption was allocated across ORs, which may have introduced some imprecision in procedure-level scope 2 estimates. Fourth, operative duration may have been influenced by surgeon-related factors, including experience, technical proficiency, and learning-curve effects, which were not evaluated in the present study. Fifth, comprehensive annual scope 3 emissions were not available, and equipment-level factors such as reusable, refurbished, or single-use devices were not evaluated. Finally, this was a single-centre study based on data collected in 2022, and emergency procedures were not included in the analysis. At our institution, emergency procedures accounted for 16.2% of all surgical cases in 2022. Differences in infrastructure, anaesthetic practices, energy systems, emergency case mix, and subsequent changes in perioperative workflows after the COVID-19 pandemic may limit the generalisability of the absolute emission estimates, although the overall patterns observed are likely applicable to other institutions.

In conclusion, GHG emissions associated with surgical care were predominantly driven by volatile anaesthetic use and infrastructure-related OR energy demand. While differences between procedures and operative approaches were relatively modest within scope 1 and scope 2 emissions, substantial opportunities for emission reduction exist through anaesthetic practice and optimisation of OR energy management, particularly HVAC operation during non-operative periods. Further studies incorporating comprehensive scope 3 emissions are needed to fully evaluate the environmental impact of different surgical approaches.
