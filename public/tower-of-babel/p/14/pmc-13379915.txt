Physiologic Unmasking of an Anomalous Right Coronary Artery in a Competitive Lacrosse Athlete

Anomalous origin of the right coronary artery (ARCA) is a rare congenital artery anomaly, where the right coronary artery (RCA) arises from the aorta at an abnormal location, most commonly the left coronary sinus instead of the right coronary sinus.1 Anomalous coronary artery anomalies occur in approximately 1% of the population and account for approximately 17% to 19% of sudden cardiac death (SCD) in athletes, making them the second most common cause of SCD in this group.1,2 Although ARCA is more prevalent than anomalous left coronary artery, left coronary anomalies are more frequently associated with SCD.2 Although up to 5 well-defined anatomic courses are described in the literature, an interarterial course carries the highest risk of ischemia and SCD.2 This risk is attributed to dynamic compression between the aorta and pulmonary artery, as well as anatomical features such as a slit-like orifice or acute angulation, leading to ostial narrowing and further luminal compression during exercise.3

Diagnosing physiologic significance in athletes is particularly challenging, as their enhanced cardiopulmonary fitness can mask subtle ischemic findings.4 Transthoracic echocardiography has limited accuracy for detecting coronary artery anomalies. Coronary computed tomography angiography is the test of choice when echocardiography raises concern or when symptoms create a high suspicion for an anomalous coronary artery.5 Traditional testing, including exercise stress testing and electrocardiogram (ECG), has poor sensitivity for identifying anomalous coronary arteries at risk for ischemia or SCD.6

Even in the presence of high aerobic capacity or unremarkable noninvasive testing, symptoms like medically refractory inordinate exertional dyspnea or reduced exercise tolerance should prompt a high index of suspicion for hemodynamically significant coronary anomalies. We present a diagnostically challenging case of an athlete with subtle exertional symptoms and ARCA with an interarterial and intramural course. Notably, this patient could not sustain aerobic activity despite otherwise excellent fitness. We emphasize the limitations of traditional ischemia testing and the importance of integrating advanced physiologic assessment and history into decision-making.

History of presentation

An 18-year-old male high school lacrosse player presented for evaluation due to 1 year of progressive exertional dyspnea, primarily during sprints. He denied chest pain, syncope, or palpitations, but endorsed chest tightness with deep inspiration during exertion. He thought his level of conditioning was incongruent with his level of training, and he reported that his performance lagged behind peers who played the same position. Notably, he was unable to sustain jump rope activity. His training included speed endurance, agility, and powerlifting, excluding long-distance running. He denied smoking, drug use, or supplement use. He had no known family history, as he was adopted. Importantly, he had undergone a bronchodilator trial with no improvement, which reduced the likelihood of exercise-induced bronchoconstriction and prompted further cardiac evaluation.

Past Medical History

He had no significant past medical history.

Investigations

His vitals and examination results were normal. Resting ECG showed a normal sinus rhythm. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated preserved biventricular function but suggested ARCA. Subsequent coronary computed tomography angiography confirmed ARCA with an interarterial and intramural course with mild ostial stenosis. The RCA originated from the sinotubular junction above the right-left commissure with a proximal angulation causing only mild narrowing (Figure 1). Resting cardiac magnetic resonance demonstrated no evidence of late gadolinium enhancement. The nuclear exercise stress test results were negative for evidence of ischemia by ECG or myocardial perfusion imaging, and the patient demonstrated excellent exercise capacity having achieved 20.3 metabolic equivalents at 96% of predicted maximum heart rate. To exhaustively assess functional capacity and investigate exertional symptoms, cardiopulmonary exercise testing (CPET) with sprint testing was performed and confirmed supranormal aerobic capacity (peak Vo2 51.4 mL/kg/min, 129% predicted), without ECG signs of ischemia or arrhythmia at similarly achieved maximum heart rate (97% of predicted maximum heart rate). However, there was a plateau in Vo2 and O2 pulse at submaximal heart rate (Figure 2A). Pulmonary function testing was unremarkable, and no evidence of bronchoconstriction was found on CPET.

Management

Despite excellent functional capacity and unremarkable stress testing, the premature plateau in Vo2 and O2 pulse raised concern for subtle ischemia when considered in the context of his exertional symptoms and high-risk coronary anatomy. After comprehensive shared decision-making involving the patient, his family, and a multidisciplinary team, including sports cardiology, congenital heart disease, and cardiac surgery, the patient opted for surgical management. The patient underwent mini-sternotomy with unroofing and neo-ostium creation. Intraoperatively, the anomalous right coronary artery was noted to have a tighter intramural course than anticipated based on preoperative assessment, with an ostium arising above the sinotubular junction. Transesophageal echocardiography would confirm proper RCA repositioning. Postoperatively, he remained stable without complications, started aspirin, low-dose beta-blockade, and colchicine, and was discharged on postoperative day 3.

Outcome and Follow-Up

At 8-week follow-up, the patient reported marked symptom improvement. He resumed daily exercise, including 30 minutes of jump rope and lower-body strength training, without dyspnea or chest pain. Repeat CPET showed peak Vo2 of 48.4 mL/kg/min (119% predicted) and no ischemia. Notably, the postoperative Vo2 and O2 pulse curves demonstrate normal augmentation without the premature plateau seen preoperatively, reflecting improved physiologic response to exercise (Figure 2B). His continued improvements suggested a successful surgical outcome and reduced risk of SCD. He had yet to resume full lacrosse participation but was on track for clearance, with expectations of gradual return to high-level athletics.

Discussion

This case illustrates the diagnostic complexity of coronary anomalies, particularly ARCA. Despite a negative exercise single-photon emission computed tomography, subtle gas-exchange derangements on CPET at a comparable maximal heart rate provided the key diagnostic clue. Although there is limited data directly comparing CPET and nuclear stress testing in ischemic evaluation, there is some evidence to suggest at least comparable sensitivity.7 Traditional tools often fail to detect risk in athletes, and the absence of ischemia on routine testing does not preclude clinical benefit from surgery.

This case was notable because the athlete presented with minimal, nonspecific symptoms despite significant pathology. He was high-performing, adopted with no known family history, and demonstrated excellent CPET performance without ischemic ECG changes, which could have masked the diagnosis. However, his inability to sustain jump rope activity suggested reduced exercise reserve. Resolution of limitations after surgery retrospectively confirmed the appropriateness of the intervention.

Although coronary anomalies are equally prevalent among athletes and nonathletes, they are more likely to become symptomatic or life-threatening in athletes due to the physiologic stress of intense exercise. The management of asymptomatic patients with ARCA presents a clinical dilemma, as the condition is generally benign but still carries a risk of SCD. The higher mortality associated with anomalous left coronary arteries supports surgical intervention upon diagnosis, whereas decisions for ARCA should be guided by individualized assessment of potential risk and benefits.8 Conservative management of ARCA has been associated with very low midterm mortality, whereas surgical intervention remains a safe and effective option for symptomatic patients to restore perfusion, relieve symptoms, and reduce the risk of SCD.5

In contrast to interarterial anomalous left coronary artery, in which surgical intervention is recommended before resumption of competitive sport, interarterial ARCA involves more complex risk stratification and management.9 According to the 2025 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Scientific Statement on clinical considerations for competitive sports participation in athletes, those with interarterial ARCA should be evaluated for symptoms and inducible ischemia with provocative stress testing, with surgical consideration if ischemia is evidenced. In this case, the athlete’s premature plateau in Vo2 and O2 pulse on CPET raised concern for ischemia.

The reliability of O2 pulse as a surrogate for stroke volume and subsequently a clinical indicator of myocardial disease is highly debated, especially in the athletic population. Additionally, the use of the O2 pulse plateau in diagnosing myocardial ischemia has demonstrated conflicting results in the general population and has not been validated in athletes. Interpretation becomes challenging in athletes, as elite athletes may demonstrate a physiologic plateau in both VO2 and O2 pulse at peak exercise as a normal finding, with up to 50% of professional soccer players exhibiting this pattern.10 Distinguishing physiologic from pathologic plateaus remains an area requiring further investigation. Regardless, our athlete’s resolution of symptoms supports the notion of myocardial ischemia as a function of his anomalous coronary.

Athletes who undergo surgical correction for ARCA should be restricted from competitive sports for 3 months, followed by re-evaluation with maximal exercise stress testing, and may return to training if no inducible ischemia or symptoms are present. For those athletes with ARCA who forgo surgery, the most recent American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines do not mandate restriction to Class IA activities; instead, they recommend a shared decision-making approach that incorporates symptom burden, ischemia testing, anatomic risk features, and athletes' preferences.9 Sports eligibility decisions are complex in asymptomatic ARCA cases with uncertain SCD risk and limited data. They are now guided by a shared decision-making model that balances scientific evidence with individual values and input from athletes, families, and other stakeholders.2 There is a growing need for standardized screening protocols and clearer surgical thresholds in this population.

Future directions include investigating how to best integrate anatomical imaging with CPET to refine surgical thresholds. Another consideration is in identifying the most effective surgical techniques for patients with commissural coronary origins, as these unique anatomical features may influence postoperative outcomes and return-to-play activities.5 These efforts are essential for advancing evidence-based management of ARCA to optimize care and ensure safe return-to-play for athletes.
