Diastematomyelia with tethered cord and concurrent distal cord intramedullary dermoid cyst: A case report
Authors: Hidayatullah Hamidi, Aarzu Amiri, Hasina Noori
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiastematomyelia is a rare congenital anomaly in which the spinal cord splits into 2 lateral halves. Spinal dermoid cysts are uncommon lesions. Hereby authors present a case of type 2 diastematomyelia in a 5-year-old male patient with concurrent distal cord intramedullary dermoid cyst, diagnosed by MRI and treated surgically.Keywords: Split cord malformations, Diastematomyelia, Tethered cord, Concurrent epidermoid cyst, Medical imaging modalities, Case report

Introduction

The term diastematomyelia was first used by Olivier in 1827 [1]. It is derived from Greek words diastema means slit or cleft, and myeloa means cord [2]. Diastematomyelia also known as split cord malformation (SCM), is a rare congenital anomaly in which the spinal cord splits into 2 lateral halves [1,2]. Dermoid/epidermoid cysts are rare lesions within the spinal canal and their association with diastematomyelia is unusual [3,4]. Hereby authors present a case of type 2 diastematomyelia in a 5-year-old male patient with concurrent distal cord intramedullary dermoid cyst.

Case presentation

A 5-year-old male child was complaining from chronic back pain, which has gotten worse for last 4 months. The debilitating chronic pain was interfering proper daily activities of the child, and made him considerable weak compare to other children of the same age. Plain radiograph of the lumbar spine reveals no abnormal finding. There was no family history of such unexplainable chronic back pain. The siblings of the child have no congenital anomaly or any chronic complaints, and were normal. The laboratory exams of the patient (vitamin D3, WBC, ESR, CRP) were within normal ranges.

A lumbar spine magnetic resonance (MR) imaging was prescribed. The images revealed a well-defined intramedullary expansile lesion of the distal cord at T11-L1 level. The lesion demonstrated homogenous high signal intensity on T2W1 and STIR sequence, low signal on T1WI, and no post contrast enhancement. The lesion showed diffusion restriction based on which the diagnosis of epidermoid cyst was first made (Figs. 1A-E).

A well-defined intramedullary expansile lesion in the distal cord at D11-L1 level demonstrating homogenous high signal intensity on T2WI (A), and STIR sequence (B), low signal on T1W images (C), and no post contrast enhancement (D). The lesion showed diffusion restriction (E and F). Low lying conus medullaris is noted terminating at L4 level (B, white arrow).

Additionally, the spinal cord was divided into 2 halves within signal thecal sac. There was a T2 low signal bar between the 2 halves (representing the spur). Low lying conus medullaris was present terminating at L4 level (Fig. 1B). No significant proximal syrinx formation was seen (Fig. 2).

Spinal cord is divided into 2 halves within signal thecal sac (A). There is a T2 low signal bar between the 2 halves (B, white arrow) representing the spur.

The overall findings were reported as diastematomyelia (type II) and tethered cord associated with intramedullary epidermoid cyst.

The patient was referred to spine surgeon. At inspection dermal sinus tract was seen at T11-12 level.

The patient underwent surgery in which de-compressive laminectomy was done at T10, T11 and T12 levels with midline duratomy and myelotomy. Decompressive laminectomy was also done at L1 level as with excision of bony spur associated with duraplasty. Untethering of filum terminal was performed. Inter-laminar space dissection was done at L5-S1 level with duratomy. Lastly excision of the dermoid sinus tract, as well as the lesion with its components was also successfully performed. The lesion reported by MRI was actually dermoid cyst with dermal sinus tract.

According to the attendance of the patient (father), the child was almost completely stable and has no complaints from lower back pain anymore (4 months after surgery).

Discussion

Diastematomyelia is a form of spinal dysraphism in which the spinal cord or filum terminale is longitudinally divided into 2 separate entities by a fibrocartilaginous or osseous septum [1,4].

Diastematomyelia is a rare disorder. It is becoming more frequently diagnosed, with development and accessibility of medical imaging modalities [3].

Dermoid cysts are also uncommon lesions within the spinal canal. Authors here present the case of a 5-year-old boy who has a combination of these individually considered rare anomalies. Differential diagnosis for dermoid cyst could be extensive including dermoid, epidermoid, meningioma, neurofibroma, glial neoplasm, hemangioblastoma, intramedullary metastasis, lipoma, infectious abscess [5].

Diastematomyelia states to the spinal cord division into 2 halves in sagittal plan which may be more or less wide [2]. The exact pathogenesis of SCM is unknown [6]. SCMs is divided into 2 types [1,3]. Type I or diplomyelia which accounts for 40% to 50% of cases, is also called true duplication of the spinal cord as it consists of 2 hemicords, each contained within its own dural tube and separated (symmetrically or asymmetrically) by a dura-sheathed rigid osseocartilaginous median septum/bony spur [1,4,6]. Type II which accounts for 50% to 60% of cases, consists of 2 hemicords housed in a single dural tube divided (symmetrically) into 2 halves by a nonrigid, fibrous median intradural septum/spur [1,2,6].

According to the position of spur, Mahapatra and Gupta have classified type 1 diastomatomyelia in to 4 sub-groups, which has surgical importance: in type 1A spur is placed medially, and cord is bifurcated equally below and above the septum, in type 1B spur is placed superior to the bifurcation of the cord, and the larger portion of bifurcation is below the spur, in type 1C spur is closer to inferior section, and the larger portion of bifurcation is placed above the spur, and in the last type 1D, no space is left above and below the spur [7].

Diastematomyelia usually involves T9 to S1 vertebral levels (50% being between L1 and L3 and 25% between T7 and T12) so lumbar spine is the most frequently involved site followed by the thoracic spine, while cervical involvement is very rare and mostly seen in adults [1,2,4].

As the diastematomyelia is a complex spinal cord malformation, it is almost always accompanied by other associated cord or column anomalies, including vertebral anomalies (i.e. spina bifida, scoliosis, lordosis, butterfly vertebrae, block vertebral bodies, laminar dysplasia and hemi vertebrae), tethered cord syndrome, Chiari malformations, epidermoid tumors, teratoma/dermoid, lipoma, low-lying conus, disc herniation, pilonidal sinus cyst, venous angioma, arachnoid cyst, coccygeal cyst, spondylolisthesis, meningocele, syringomyelia and syringohydromyelia [1,2,4,6]. About 40%-67% of patients reveal presence of cutaneous stigmata like hypertrichosis (hairy patch), dimple or dermal sinus in lumber region on physical exam. These marks may or may not be associated with diastematomyelia [2,3]. The cutaneous lesions are most often the first indicators of congenital spinal lesions [7]. The majority of lesions found in association with diastematomyelia are located within proximity to the SCM [3]. Diastematomyelia can also be associated with visceral lesions like ectopic or horseshoe kidney, utero-ovarian, and anorectal malformation [7].

Clinical signs and symptoms may occur due to following factors: vascular factors (ischemia due to compression of spinal artery), anatomical factors (hypoplasia of one of the hemicords), mechanical factors: stretching and compression of spinal cord by the septum [1,2].

Adults with diastematomyelia usually present with neurological defects such as lumbago, lumbosciatica or perianal dysesthesia and some may have asymmetry in the buttocks, leg muscles or gluteal folds asymmetrical weakness and lower limb atrophy, gait difficulties, foot ulcer, neurogenic claudication [1,2].

In presence of cutaneous manifestations in adults who present with radiculopathy, myelopathy, tethered cord syndrome, or cauda equina syndrome; diastematomyelia, must be kept in differential diagnosis [3].

In majority of cases diastematomyelia is diagnosed in children but very rarely in adult population [1]. The exact cause for late onset of symptoms in adults is not well known, however, the repetitive stretching and mechanical injury on the cord as well as the degree of traction on the conus medullaris may be the involved factors that determine the onset time of symptoms [[1], [2], [3]]. Conspicuously, a long and relatively asymptomatic period of life is not prohibited by presence of other concurrent neurological lesions with Diastematomyelia; however, according to the literature Diastematomyelia diagnosed in adults is not usually associated with remarkable dermatological, and skeletal findings [3,7].

MR imaging has been shown to be excellent in detecting the split cord, the dural sacs, tethered cord and other accompanying vertebral or cord anomalies in both children and adults, since it provides more information and is less invasive [1,6]. As long as clinical manifestations may not be initially apparent and incidence of intraspinal anomalies is high, MR imaging is recommended as part of the initial evaluation in patients with congenital spinal deformity even in absence of clinical findings [6]. Computed tomography (CT) scan can also reveal the bony spurs and also abnormalities of the vertebral bodies and posterior arches [1]. Ultrasonography is simple imaging tool that can help diagnose the deformity in antenatal life as well as new born before going towards MRI imaging [1,2]. Ultrasonography can reveal an echogenic focus (the spur) between the spinal lamina of the fetus that spreading out the posterior elements in third trimester of intra uterine life [1]. X ray techniques are not useful in every case and can be performed for evaluation of spinal curvature [2].

Surgery is the only treatment for patients having neurological, urological or orthopedic problems [2].

Conclusion

Combination of SCMs with spinal dermoid cyst is a rare entity. Surgery is the choice for treatment in such cases.
