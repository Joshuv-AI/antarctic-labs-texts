Secular variation and risk factors of preterm birth in Hainan, the Free Trade Port in China from 2010 to 2021
Authors: Qing Luo, Weijia Wu, Chao Li, Anping Zhao, Dan Zhao, Chuican Huang, Lichun Fan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPreterm delivery rates have increased obviously worldwide over the past decade, yet reliable epidemiological studies on the incidence of preterm birth and temporal trends are not available in Hainan, The Free Trade Port in China. We aimed to describe the rate of preterm birth and trends between 2010 and 2021 and to primarily explore risk factors associated with preterm birth in Hainan, China.MethodsThis was an observational study was based on data from the Hainan Provincial Birth Certificate System (HPBCS) for live births between 1 January 2010 and 31 December 2021. We included pregnancies resulting in at least one live birth, with newborns born at a gestational age of 28 weeks or greater, or with a birth weight of 1000 g or more. The outcome were preterm birth rates and their trends over time. Potential risk factors were collected, including infant gender, maternal age, paternal age, maternal ethnicity, paternal ethnicity, home address, and single or multiple pregnancies. The logistic regression model was used to assess the relationship between preterm birth and potential risk factors.ResultsA total of 1,537,239 live births and 86,328 preterm births were investigated, giving a total preterm birth rate of 5.62%. The overall preterm birth rate increased from 4.47% in 2010 to 7.12% in 2021 (compound annual growth rate [CAGR] 4.32). The CAGR of late preterm birth is consistent with the overall preterm birth rate (4.32%). The fastest growth is observed in the rate of very preterm births (5.53%), while the rate of moderate preterm births exhibits the slowest growth (3.87%). Infant gender, multiple pregnancy, home address, parental age, and ethnicity had significant effects on preterm birth.ConclusionThe preterm birth rate was increasing year by year from 2010 to 2021 in Hainan, The Free Trade Port in China. Incidence of preterm births in Hainan Province in relation to multiple pregnancies, infant sex, parental age, parental race and residential address.Keywords: Preterm birth, Risk factors, Infant, China, The Free Trade Port

Introduction

The World Health Organization defines preterm birth as a birth occurring before 37 weeks of gestation or less than 259 days after the first day of a woman’s last menstrual period [1]. Due to different levels of neonatal care in other countries, the setting of the lower limit of gestational age is not uniform [2]. China defines preterm birth as a gestational age of 28 weeks and above but less than 37 weeks at the time of delivery [3]. Preterm birth is one of the most important causes of neonatal death and the main cause of serious complications and death in early childhood, especially a leading cause of death among newborns in China [4]. Studies have also shown that the impact of preterm birth on the health of the birth population can even extend to the long-term health of adults, such as increasing the risk of chronic diseases such as coronary heart disease in adults [4–7]. It not only puts great pressure on health systems but also imposes a huge burden on the global economy and families [5].

In 2014, the worldwide incidence of preterm birth was about 10.6% [8]. However, there was a gap in China’s preterm birth rate among different reports. According to WHO, the incidence of preterm birth in China in 2014 was 6.9% [8], while the hospital-based preterm birth rate was 7.2%, which was higher than the population-based preterm birth rate of 4.9%, according to a recent meta-analysis [4]. The incidence of preterm birth is on the rise worldwide. Preventing preterm birth and reducing the incidence of preterm birth has become a hot issue in public health worldwide [9]. Now, the preterm birth rate shows an increasing trend year by year, which has become an issue that China has to pay attention to [10]. However, the influencing factors and mechanisms of preterm birth are complex. Although sociodemographic, nutritional, biological, and environmental factors may increase the risk of spontaneous preterm birth, the causes are not completely clear [11, 12], which also leads to the lack of significant progress in the prevention of preterm birth [13].

Hainan Province is the southernmost island province in China, and it is also the country’s sole free trade island. To our knowledge, there is little study on preterm birth in Hainan Province, especially the influencing factors and the trend of preterm birth over time. Therefore, this study was the first retrospective analysis of live birth data from the past 12 years in Hainan Province, China, to clarify the occurrence of preterm birth and explore the epidemiological characteristics and risk factors of premature.

Methods

Study design and data collection

This was an observational study based on data from the Hainan Provincial Birth Certificate System (HPBCS) for live births between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2021. The HPBCS was established by the Health Commission of Hainan Province and managed by the Hainan Women and Children’s Medical Center. The management of HPBCS belongs to the Hainan Women and Children’s Medical Center, which permits the distribution of these statistics for medical research purposes.

Socio-demographic characteristics and clinical records of both mothers and newborns were available for the identification of preterm birth and risk factors. The data included the infant’s date of birth, infant’s sex, maternal age, paternal age, maternal ethnicity, paternal ethnicity, gestational age at delivery, mode of delivery, and single or multiple pregnancies. In order to ensure the continuity and coherence of preterm birth rate calculations based on previous related research, We included pregnancies with at least one live birth; the births with gestational age ≥ 28 weeks or with birthweight ≥ 1000 g, according to the definition of the perinatal period in China. Furthermore, we excluded the ineligible records: (1) births with missing gestational age or gestational age < 28 weeks or > 42 weeks and (2) births with missing maternal age or maternal age < 13 years or > 50 years.

Definition and measurements

According to criteria recommended by WHO, we defined births as preterm if they were born before 37 completed weeks of gestation. Based on gestational age, the preterm births were subdivided into three categories: gestational age of 28–31 weeks (very preterm), gestational age of 32–33 weeks (moderate preterm), and gestational age of 34–36 weeks (late preterm) [14].

We selected exposure factors via HPBCS, including sociodemographic characteristics (infant gender, maternal age, paternal age, maternal ethnicity, paternal ethnicity) and clinical records (infant’s date of birth, gestational age at delivery, mode of delivery, and single or multiple pregnancies). Based on previous literature, maternal and paternal age were respectively classified into three groups (< 20, 20–35, ≥ 35 years; <22, 22–40, ≥ 40 years, resp.). Maternal and paternal ethnicity was categorized into two groups (Han and other). Home addresses were divided into three groups (urban, rural) according to the urban-rural division code of the National Bureau of Statistics of China. We categorized multiple pregnancies into two groups (Yes or no) based on the number of fetuses during pregnancy.

Ethical statement

This study extracted routinely collected health data, and no subjects participated in the design and conduct of the research. Data collection in this work was anonymous, and no personally identifiable information was extracted for the data analysis. Informed consent was waived because all information in our study was encrypted and unidentifiable. The informed consent waiver has also been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Hainan Women and Children’s Medical Center, with the specific approval number being No. [165], 2022.

Statistical analysis

Sociodemographic characteristics and clinical records were described as numbers and percentages (%). The differences in the basic characteristics of births with and without preterm were determined by the Chi-square test.

Furthermore, we calculated the number and rate of preterm births overall and within each preterm category during the study period (January 1, 2011, to December 31, 2021). The preterm birth rate was calculated via the number of live babies in a specific preterm group divided by the total number of live babies multiplied by 100 [1]. Moreover, the Compound Annual Growth Rate= ((Preterm birth rate 2021/Preterm birth rate 2010)^(1/11)-1)×100% (1).

We further identified the associations between sociodemographic and clinical characteristics with all preterm births and each of the preterm birth subgroups using logistic regression models to identify these relationships. Because it was an exploratory analysis, we first included all of the sociodemographic and obstetric factors collected in the HPBCS in the model. First of all, crude odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were examined for each of the above factors. Secondly, multivariate logistic regression was applied to estimate the adjusted ORs. The model was adjusted for maternal sociodemographic and obstetric factors (infant sex, maternal age, parental age, maternal ethnicity, parental ethnicity, home address, and type of pregnancy [singleton vs. multiple]). All the analyses were conducted with SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). We considered p < 0.05 in the two-sided test as significant.

According to the result of univariate logistic regression analysis, male infants, parents’ age, parents’ ethnicity, multiple pregnancies, and the home address had significant effects on preterm birth in all groups. The association between multiple pregnancies and preterm birth was the strongest. When compared with singleton pregnancies, multiple pregnancies were about 11.75 times more likely to result in all preterm pregnancies (Table 2). Maternal age (≥ 35 and < 20) was about 1.73 and 1.22 times more likely to give birth prematurely than women aged 21 ~ 35. Paternal age had a similar effect; those aged ≥ 40 and <22 were approximately 1.60 and 1.14 times more likely to have a preterm birth than those aged 22–40. However, the effect of age on very preterm birth was higher than that of moderate and late preterm birth, whether below or above the appropriate age for fathers and mothers. Minority mothers are more likely to have preterm births than fathers, but differences in parental ethnicity were found to have no significant effect on very preterm births (Table 2).

Table 3 showed the association between maternal demographic characteristics, obstetric information, and the occurrence of preterm birth by the multivariate logistic regression analysis in Hainan Province, 2010–2021. Multiple pregnancies constitute a significant risk factor for both overall preterm birth and preterm births across various stages. In comparison to singleton pregnancies, multiple pregnancies substantially elevate the risks of overall preterm birth, very preterm birth, moderate preterm birth, and late preterm birth by 11.81-fold (95% CI: 11.54–12.09), 10.13-fold (95% CI: 9.44–10.88), 11.48-fold (95% CI: 10.90-12.08), and 9.72-fold (95% CI: 9.48–9.96), respectively. Ethnic minority mothers were observed to have a higher risk of preterm birth compared to fathers, while paternal ethnicity did not show a significant effect on very or moderate preterm birth. Pregnant women living in urban were the protective factors for very preterm birth and were the risk factors for late preterm birth, yet no relationship between residence and moderate preterm birth was found.

Discussion

This study, based on birth registration data from Hainan province, examines the trends and factors influencing preterm births. The findings indicate a continuous rise in the overall preterm birth rate from 4.47% in 2010 to 7.12% in 2021, reaching an average of 5.62% over the period. The World Health Organization reported a global preterm birth rate of 10.6% in 2014, with China’s rate being 6.9% during the same period. In Hainan province, from 2010 to 2021, the annual preterm birth rates remained below the global average of 10.6%. However, starting in 2020, Hainan’s rates exceeded China’s 6.9%, aligning with the global trend of increasing preterm birth rates [8, 15].

A multi-center cross-sectional survey based on the whole Chinese population showed that the weighted nationwide incidence of preterm birth was 7.3% of all births and 6.7% of live births at 24 or more weeks of gestation. Moreover, above study reported the preterm birth rate of all live births in Hainan Province was 6.6%, which surpassing the rates in this study for Hainan in 2015 (5.19%) and 2016 (6.08%) [15]. The possible reason is the difference in the definition of preterm birth, which was defined as more than 28 weeks in this study, while the former was defined as more than 24 weeks. In addition, the live births in the hospital as the survey point died after discharge, which would also lead to a higher preterm birth rate based on the hospital survey than this study.

Due to regional disparities in preterm birth rates, this study exclusively compares Hainan Province with other southern provinces and cities in China. The preterm birth rate in Hainan Province was found to be lower than that of Guangxi Province from 2017 to 2019 (7.45% and 7.34%, resp) [16]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the exclusion of data from secondary and lower-level maternity care institutions in Guangxi. The higher proportion of high-risk pregnancies in advanced medical institutions in Guangxi could explain the relatively higher preterm birth rate observed. Hainan’s preterm birth rate is slightly lower than that of Shenzhen (5.8%). This difference is explained by the fact that Shenzhen’s statistics include very preterm births before 28 weeks of gestation [17].

In this study, the preterm birth rate in Hainan increased by 4.32% per year. It was higher than the 1.05% annual growth rate of preterm births in a meta-analysis on the occurrence of preterm births in China from 1990 to 2016 [18], and It was also higher than the 1.3% annual growth rate estimated by Deng and Colleagues et al. [19]. The possible reason is the different definition of preterm birth, which was defined as more than 28 weeks in this study, compared to 24 weeks. In addition, live births would die after discharge, which also resulted in a higher rate of preterm births based on the hospital survey. Among all preterm infants, the growth rate of very preterm infants (28–31 weeks) was higher than that of late preterm infants (34–36 Weeks), and the last one was moderate preterm infants, which was the same as Deng and Colleagues et al. [19]. But the late preterm rate was higher than others studies. The possible reason is that the methods of estimating gestational age were different. The last menstruation was used to estimate preterm infants in the early years, and the last menstruation combined with B-ultrasound was used to estimate gestational age in recent years. In addition, as found in other studies [20], the cause of the higher rates of late preterm births may be linked to the maternal body condition, obstetric complications, spontaneous vaginal delivery, and other factors, and the data in this study mainly from birth certificate registration system rather than the survey design by very premature infants and the incidence of late preterm investigative study, there is a lack of maternal-related factors in this study. Further exploratory analysis and demonstration of the influencing factors of preterm birth are needed.

The main influencing factors for the increase of preterm infants in Hainan Province include multiple pregnancy, parental age (age of parents), fetal sex, and parental ethnicity, among which multiple pregnancy and parental age are the main influencing factors, which is consistent with previous studies in China [15–19], More than 50% of women with multiple pregnancies will deliver before 37 weeks [21–23]. In this study, the preterm birth rate of multiple pregnancies was higher than that of singleton pregnancies. With the emergence of super-birth technology, people’s understanding of the incidence of multiple pregnancy is significantly higher than before. With the application of assisted reproductive technology, more and more preterm infants with multiple pregnancy are successful and survive [24].Compared with singleton pregnancy, multiple pregnancy may lead to uterine transitional distension, predilection of membranes, and other pregnancy complications, and increase the incidence of premature delivery [24–26].

The second was the younger and older parents. The younger maternal physical function and reproductive function have not yet developed and are more prone to preterm birth. Some literature showed [27, 28]that for less than 20 teen pregnancy, the incidence of preterm birth is higher. The reason may be associated with body function and the development of reproductive function has not been perfect, and may also be related to their social and economic problems, not just associated with biological age, because these problems affect their health-seeking behavior, increasing the risk of preterm birth occurring.

Although the data collected in this study have a wide range of years and a large sample size, it still has certain limitations. Firstly, our study included only live births but did not analyze stillbirths, which may have underestimated the rate of preterm birth rate. Secondly, there was some information bias in the registration of birth medical certificates by the family members of newborns and medical staff. In addition, the data of this study came from the birth registration system, and we lacked the mothers’ detailed prenatal care, pregnancy complications, parents’ education level, socioeconomic factors, and other possible risk factors. Therefore, we did not have a complete archive of preterm birth risk factors, which may have a certain impact on the comprehensiveness of the study’s conclusion.

Preterm birth remains a leading cause of neonatal mortality, and morbidity [29, 30] is also a critical global public health issue. The treatment of premature birth needs a lot of medical resources and social resources support during the process of care and parenting. Among live births in Hainan province, preterm birth rates are at the general level but rising every year. To better prevent and management of premature births, further investigation on other influencing factors of contact with preterm birth rates change and the potential mechanism is needed. The risk of preterm birth could be reduced when appropriate interventions were implemented. According to related research reports [31], appropriate intervention in prolonging the time of delivery could effectively prolong the gestational age, reduce the rate of preterm birth, reduce the perinatal consequences related to preterm birth, and improve the survival rate of newborns. However, few studies have validated the effectiveness of interventions on preterm birth, so further studies are needed to investigate the impact of preventive measures on preterm birth. Although the Preterm birth rate accounts for a small proportion of the total preterm birth rate in Hainan Province, it has the fastest rising trend. Due to the increased risk of adverse outcomes, its growth and development should be closely monitored [6]. Finally, Hainan Province should make efforts to standardize the report format to increase coverage of all birth and system records, whether a live birth or stillbirth and finally, to make more feasible questionnaires. This study simplifies the responsive questionnaire, but more research is needed to mature in the end.

Conclusion

The incidence of premature birth in Hainan Province has been steadily rising. Notably, the very preterm birth rate wins the fastest increase. Premature birth can be resulted from various factors. The most important factors that contributing to premature birth in Hainan were multiple pregnancies, infant sex, parental age, parental race, and residential address. Targeted intervention measures should be implemented for women in Hainan, The Free Trade Port in China along with the reinforcement of prenatal care and services, to improve delivery outcomes.
