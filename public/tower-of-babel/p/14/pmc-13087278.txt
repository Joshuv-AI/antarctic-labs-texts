Statin use and the risk of liver-related events in older adults with steatotic liver disease
Authors: Eun Seok Kang, Hye Jun Kim, Sun Jae Park, Ju Hyun Kang, Sangwoo Park, Meng Sha, Seong Hee Kang, Seogsong Jeong, Sang Min Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStatins are widely used for cardiovascular prevention, yet their hepatic benefits in older adults with steatotic liver disease (SLD) remain uncertain, particularly regarding liver-related events (LREs) and the modifying role of cardiometabolic risk factors (CMRFs). This retrospective cohort study used data from the Korean National Health Insurance Service-Senior cohort, comprising 125,926 adults aged ≥ 60 years with fatty liver index ≥ 30, followed from 2011 to 2019. Participants with prior liver disease or LREs at baseline were excluded. Cumulative statin exposure quantified as cumulative defined daily dose (cDDD) and categorized as non-use, 1 cDDD to 89 cDDD, 90 cDDD to 364 cDDD, and ≥ 365 cDDD; statin intensity classified as low, moderate, or high. The primary outcomes were incident LREs (primary liver cancer, liver cirrhosis, and decompensated cirrhosis) identified using validated ICD-10 codes. Associations were assessed using Fine and Gray competing-risk models and expressed as subdistribution hazard ratios (SHRs). During a median follow-up of 9 years, 3,445 LREs occurred over 1,054,343 person-years. Statin use was associated with a dose-dependent reduction in LRE risk. Compared with non-users, participants with ≥ 365 cDDD had the lowest risk of total LREs (SHR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.61–0.78), primary liver cancer (SHR, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.52–0.76), and liver cirrhosis (SHR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.57–0.77). Moderate- to high-intensity statin conferred greater protection than low-intensity statin. Introduction

Statins exert cardiovascular and anti-inflammatory effects by competitively inhibiting 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase, thereby reducing cholesterol synthesis, upregulating LDL receptor expression, and lowering triglyceride-rich lipoprotein production.1–3 In addition to their well-established cardiovascular benefits, statins are associated with a reduced risk of liver diseases, including nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and liver-related mortality.4 However, the extent of the benefits and the specific patient populations that derive the greatest advantage from statin therapy remain unclear.

With the global aging population, the use of statins for primary prevention of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease in adults aged 75 and older remains a clinical challenge.5,6 Age-related changes in liver function and altered drug metabolism may increase susceptibility to hepatotoxicity and liver-related complications.7,8 However, real-world evidence of statin-associated liver-related events (LREs) in this population is scarce, as older individuals have been underrepresented in clinical trials.

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is characterized by steatotic liver disease (SLD) with low-grade chronic inflammation and at least one cardiometabolic risk factor (CMRF), indicating a systemic cardiometabolic risk.9 MASLD or SLD is not only a risk factor for liver cirrhosis and HCC but is also associated with an increased risk of extrahepatic diseases, such as cardiovascular disease, dementia, and hearing loss.10–13 Given these broad implications, patients with MASLD may represent the optimal population for statin therapy in terms of both intrahepatic and extrahepatic benefits.

Despite the potential hepatoprotective effects of statins, there remains an unmet need for evidence of their impact on LREs in older adults, particularly in relation to the number of CMRFs. This study aimed to evaluate the association between statin dose and intensity with the risk of LREs, including primary liver cancer, liver cirrhosis, and decompensated cirrhosis, in older adults with MASLD.

Methods

Study population

This dataset was provided by the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) and covers most of the South Korean population. It consists of transformed medical records, including demographic and healthcare utilization data of Korean citizens classified as health insurance subscribers.14 The NHIS senior cohort was constructed as a cohort database containing qualifications and socioeconomic information, health examination results, and hospital utilization records for approximately 511,953 individuals, representing 8% of the approximately 6.4 million elderly individuals aged 60–79 years who maintained health insurance or medical aid eligibility as of 2008.

In this study, we used data from individuals aged ≥ 60 years as of 2010 with a fatty liver index (FLI) of ≥ 30. From a total of 138,587 individuals, we excluded those who died before the start of follow-up, had missing covariate data, or had a history of LREs or underlying liver disease, including cryptogenic SLD, chronic viral hepatitis, and toxic hepatitis. After applying the exclusion criteria, the final study population comprised 125,926 individuals (Fig. 1). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Korea University Guro Hospital (2024GR0185).

Main outcomes

The main outcomes of this study were LREs, including primary liver cancer, liver cirrhosis, and decompensated liver cirrhosis. These outcomes were identified using the following ICD-10 codes: primary liver cancer was defined as C22, along with special exemption codes V193 and V194. Liver cirrhosis was identified using codes I85.9, I98.2, K70.2, K70.3, K71.7, K74.0, K74.1, K74.2, K74.4, K74.5, K74.6, and K76.6. Decompensated liver cirrhosis was defined as meeting the criteria for liver cirrhosis, along with at least one of the following additional codes: G93.4, I85.0, I98.3, K70.4, K72.0, K72.1, K72.9, K76.7, and R18.15 All participants were followed from January 1, 2011, to December 31, 2019, until the occurrence of LREs, death, or December 31, 2019, whichever occurred first.

Definition of statin use

All covariates and the primary exposure variable, statin use, were extracted based on information prior to the study baseline. The defined daily dose (DDD) is a standardized unit established by the World Health Organization (WHO), representing the assumed average maintenance dose per day for a drug used in adults. Statin use was quantified using the cumulative defined daily dose (cDDD), which was calculated as follows:

(Total amount of the drug actually dispensed to the patient in each prescription) / (WHO-assigned DDD for the drug).

This calculation was performed over the 4-year observation period during which statin use was monitored. The cDDD are recommended by the WHO for standardizing drug usage and are widely used in pharmacoepidemiologic research.16 Based on the cumulative cDDD prior to the study baseline, participants were categorized into four groups: non-users (0 cDDD), 1 cDDD to 89 cDDD, 90 cDDD to 364 cDDD, and ≥ 365 cDDD. Statins were further classified based on their intensity, with high-intensity statins including atorvastatin (40–80 mg) and rosuvastatin (20–40 mg), moderate-intensity statins including simvastatin (20–40 mg), pravastatin (40–80 mg), lovastatin (40 mg), fluvastatin (80 mg), and pitavastatin (2–4 mg), and low-intensity statins including simvastatin (10 mg), pravastatin (10–20 mg), and lovastatin (20 mg).17.

Key variables

FLI is calculated using triglyceride, body mass index, waist circumference, and gamma-glutamyl transferase values.18 It is a non-invasive method for detecting fatty liver and has good diagnostic accuracy, with an area under the curve of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.81–0.88).18 Hepatic steatosis was defined as a FLI ≥ 30.19 This threshold was chosen based on a previous validation study among Korean adults using computed tomography (CT) as the reference standard, which demonstrated that an FLI ≥ 30 provided an optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity, both 71%, for detecting hepatic steatosis.19.

MASLD was defined as the presence of hepatic steatosis in conjunction with at least one CMRF, in the absence of moderate or heavy alcohol consumption. The CMRFs considered included: (1) body mass index ≥ 23 kg/m² or waist circumference ≥90 cm;20 (2) fasting blood glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL, a diagnosis of type 2 diabetes, or prescription of antidiabetic medication; (3) blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg or use of antihypertensive medication; (4) triglyceride levels ≥ 150 mg/dL or use of lipid-lowering agents; and (5) high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) ≤ 40 mg/dL.9

Metabolic dysfunction-associated alcohol-related liver disease (MetALD) was defined as the presence of MASLD with increased alcohol intake, including moderate alcohol consumption. Alcohol-related liver disease (ALD) was defined as a further increase in alcohol consumption among individuals with MetALD. For individuals with at least one CMRF, ALD was defined as heavy alcohol use, while in the absence of CMRFs, even moderate alcohol intake met the criteria for ALD.21 Alcohol consumption was categorized as follows: ‘None’ (no alcohol consumption), ‘Light’ (≤ 30 g/day for men or ≤ 20 g/day for women), ‘Moderate’ (31–60 g/day for men or 21–50 g/day for women), and ‘Heavy’ (> 60 g/day for men or > 50 g/day for women).21.

Statistical analysis

For continuous variables, data are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) if normally distributed, otherwise, they are expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are reported as the number of participants and percentages. The variables considered in this study were as follows: age (continuous; years), sex (categorical; male and female), waist circumference (continuous; cm), household income (categorical; 1st, 2nd, 3rd, and 4th quartiles), Charlson comorbidity index (categorical; 0, 1, and ≥ 2), smoking status (categorical; never, past, and current), hypertension (categorical; yes and no), type 2 diabetes mellitus (categorical; yes and no), alcohol consumption (categorical; none, light, moderate, and heavy), moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (categorical; 0, 1–2, 3–4, and ≥ 5 times/week), and dyslipidemia duration (continuous; years).

A multivariable-adjusted Fine and Gray model was used to evaluate the association between statin dosage and LREs, accounting for the competing risk of death without the occurrence of the outcome. Subdistribution hazard ratios (SHR) were calculated after adjusting for age, sex, household income, Charlson comorbidity index (CCI), smoking status, systolic blood pressure, fasting serum glucose, alcohol consumption, moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), and dyslipidemia duration.22 Additionally, the Cox proportional hazard regression model was used as a supplementary analysis to estimate and compare the risk of LREs by calculating cause-specific hazard ratios. Absolute risk calculated as the percentage of events per population during a 9-year follow-up period, and relative risk calculated as the ratio of the incidence rate in each statin group to that in the non-user group.

In the first sensitivity analysis, to assess the consistency of the data, we compared the effects of statin use over a five-year period (2006–2010) and a three-year period (2008–2010). In the second sensitivity analysis, a washout period was applied by excluding the first one, two, and three years of follow-up, respectively, to eliminate residual effects from baseline conditions or prior interventions. In the final sensitivity analysis, to minimize reverse causality and selection bias, we evaluated the effect of statins in patients with newly diagnosed dyslipidemia, which was defined as a diagnosis made between January 1, 2006, and the date of the first statin prescription during the period between 2007 and 2010.

To adjust for confounders, a stratified analysis was conducted by including variables identified as major risk factors for LREs in the stratification groups. In addition, propensity score matching was performed to balance baseline covariates between groups, and covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences. The cumulative incidence function was analyzed using R Project for Statistical Computing (version 4.3.0; https://www.r-project.org/), whereas data extraction, processing, statistical evaluations, restricted cubic splines were conducted using the SAS Enterprise Guide (version 8.3, SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

The funders had no role in study design, data collection and analysis, decision to publish, or preparation of the manuscript.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 125,926 participants were included in the analytic cohort, with 79,042 in the non-user group, 12,207 in the 1 cDDD to 89 cDDD group, 15,278 in the 90 cDDD to 364 cDDD group, and 15,278 in the ≥ 365 cDDD group. The mean age was approximately 67 years across all groups, and the proportion of participants aged ≥ 75 years was 12.7%, 12.3%, 12.3%, and 13.0%, respectively (Table 1).

Association between DDD dosage of statin and LREs

The cumulative incidence of total LREs was lower in all cDDD groups than in the non-user group, and this trend remained consistent for primary liver cancer, liver cirrhosis, and decompensated cirrhosis (Fig. 2).

Cumulative incidence functions for incident liver-related events and its subtypes. (a) Liver-related events, (b) Primary liver cancer, (c) Liver cirrhosis, and (d) Decompensated cirrhosis. cDDD, cumulative defined daily dose.

In the analysis evaluating the risk of LREs according to cumulative statin use, a statistically significant inverse association was observed, with increasing cDDD linked to progressively lower event risk. The reference group was defined as statin non-users, and all categories of cDDD showed a reduced risk compared to this group. A total of 3,445 LREs occurred over 1,054,343 person-years of follow-up. The lowest overall risk for LREs was observed in participants with ≥ 365 cDDD (SHR, 0.69; 95% CI, 0.61–0.78). This trend was consistent across most LREs, with the most pronounced protective associations seen for primary liver cancer (SHR, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.52–0.76) and liver cirrhosis (SHR, 0.66; 95% CI, 0.57–0.77). In contrast, the strongest protective effect for decompensated liver cirrhosis was observed in the 90 cDDD to 364 cDDD group (SHR, 0.44; 95% CI, 0.33–0.68) (Fig. 3). These findings were further supported by analyses using restricted cubic splines (Supplementary Fig. 1). When evaluating the risk of LREs by SLD subtype, statin use showed a significant protective effect primarily in individuals with MASLD. Consistent with previous findings, higher cDDD was associated with a greater reduction in risk (Supplementary Table 1).

A similar trend was observed when using 60 cDDD use (SHR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.67–0.81; P < 0.001) and 90 cDDD use (SHR, 0.74; 95% CI, 0.68–0.82; P < 0.001) as cut-off values (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5).

Statin intensity was classified based on the standard 30-day prescribed dose. A protective association against LREs was predominantly observed among individuals receiving moderate- to high-intensity statin therapy, suggesting that higher-intensity statins may contribute more significantly to risk reduction (Fig. 4).

Subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis

When assessing the risk of LREs according to statin cDDD across subgroups, the overall findings were generally consistent with the main results. However, in certain subgroups, specifically individuals without hypertension (SHR, 0.67; 95% CI, 0.53–0.84) and those with suspected advanced fibrosis (BARD score ≥ 2) (SHR, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.52–0.82), the most significant risk reduction was observed in the 1 cDDD to 90 cDDD group. BMI showed a statistically significant interaction effect (P for interaction = 0.012), and a modest interaction effect was also noted with the BARD score (Supplementary Table 6). When evaluating the effects of statin use over a five-year period (2006–2010) and a three-year period (2008–2010), the trends remained consistent with previous results. Furthermore, even after applying a washout period of one, two, and three years after follow-up, the findings remained unchanged. Patients who were newly diagnosed with dyslipidemia before the date of their first statin prescription between 2007 and 2010 did not show a protective effect of statins against LREs. (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Statin use and LREs in individuals aged ≥ 75 years

Compared to individuals under the age of 75, statin use was associated with a greater protective effect against LREs in those aged 75 or older. Specifically, those who received 1 cDDD to 89 cDDD had an SHR of 0.64 (95% CI, 0.43–0.94), 90 cDDD to 364 cDDD had an SHR of 0.64 (95% CI, 0.44–0.92), and ≥ 365 cDDD had an SHR of 0.69 (95% CI, 0.48–0.98) (Table 2). Although high-intensity statins were also associated with a protective effect, the number of events was relatively small, and thus the results should be interpreted with caution (Supplementary Table 7).

Propensity score matching

Propensity score matching was performed to balance baseline covariates, and post-matching balance was assessed using standardized mean differences (Supplementary Table 8). In the matched cohorts (28,531, 21,490, and 13,551 pairs for the three comparisons), users consistently showed a lower risk of LREs than non-users in Fine and Gray competing-risk models (Supplementary Table 9), with adjusted SHRs of 0.75 (95% CI, 0.68–0.83) for User vs. Non-user, 0.72 (95% CI, 0.64–0.81) for ≥ 90 cDDD vs. Non-user, and 0.71 (95% CI, 0.61–0.82) for ≥ 365 cDDD vs. Non-user.

Discussion

Statin use significantly reduced the risk of LREs, including primary liver cancer, liver cirrhosis, and decompensated cirrhosis, in older adults in a dose-dependent manner. The protective effects of statins were more pronounced with high-intensity statin use, and persisted among those aged ≥ 75 years, while the benefits were not evident in those with low-intensity statin use. However, the beneficial effects of statin use were mitigated by a larger number of CMRFs. These findings suggest that the hepatic benefits of statins persist in older adults aged ≥ 75 years and are particularly pronounced in individuals with MASLD and smaller number of CMRFs, indicating that the extent of metabolic risk factors may play a crucial role in determining their protective effects.

The protective effects of statins may be associated with a reduced risk of liver fibrosis. Prior to this study, statin use was identified as a protective factor against NAFLD, liver fibrosis, and HCC.4,23,24 More recently, antifibrotic effects were observed for simvastatin and atorvastatin in animal models of carbon tetrachloride- or bile duct ligation-induced liver fibrosis, and prophylactic or early statin use alleviated liver fibrosis by decreasing the turnover of hepatic stellate cells.25,26 Despite accumulating evidence regarding protective effects of statin use against LREs, information on individuals most likely benefit from statin use remained uncertain, especially among older adults.

Other potential hepatoprotective mechanisms of statins, beyond fibrosis reduction, include low-density lipoprotein cholesterol and oxidative stress reduction, improvement of endothelial function, and anti-inflammatory effects.27,28 Statins may also protect against liver cirrhosis by enhancing endothelial GATA4 activity, which suppresses MYC activation and profibrotic angiocrine signaling at the chromatin level.29 The dysfunction of liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, which exhibit anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic characteristics, has been identified as a crucial factor in the progression to steatohepatitis and HCC in patients with NAFLD.30 Additionally, oxidative stress, imbalanced reactive oxygen species, and the antioxidant defense system have been recognized as contributors to hepatocellular carcinogenesis.31 These primary and pleiotropic effects may simultaneously contribute to LRE risk reduction in older adults with SLD.

According to a previous study, NAFLD and MASLD overlapped for 99%, 86.5% had at least one CMRF, and the prevalence of SLD was 37.9% in the general adult population in the US.32,33 Another study reported an 88.0% prevalence of SLD when including grade I SLD, suggesting that SLD prevalence can vary greatly across different settings and study populations.34 In addition, although the study population was not restricted to older adults, and the target population was not limited to patients with SLD, statin use was found to be protective against liver cirrhosis and HCC in patients with chronic hepatitis B.35,36 According to our findings, there were no significant benefits of statin use in patients with 5 CMRFs, while MASLD patients with 1 to 4 CMRFs showed a significantly lower risk of LREs. Therefore, caution is needed when interpreting the results in a clinical setting where not only SLD status but also other conditions, such as the number of CMRFs and chronic viral hepatitis, are present.

Statins are widely regarded as safe, with benefits outweighing the potential risks, and they have been shown to reduce aminotransferase levels and slow the progression of liver stiffness without inducing hepatotoxicity.36 Nevertheless, certain adverse effects, such as muscle weakness, increased risk of type 2 diabetes, neurocognitive disturbances, and renal toxicity, remain subjects of debate.37–39 These findings underscore the need for clinicians to optimize management and monitoring strategies when prescribing statins to patients with MASLD, considering both their well-established tolerability and potential role in mitigating LREs. However, these promising outcomes require validation through large-scale randomized clinical trials with sufficiently long follow-up periods to assess the impact of statin initiation and intensity on LREs in older adults with MASLD.

This study has some limitations. First, SLD was operationally defined using the FLI, which is an indirect surrogate, and it does not directly quantify hepatic fat accumulation. Obtaining liver biopsy or imaging-based fat fraction data remains challenging, particularly in large-scale cohort studies. Second, although adjustments were made for potential confounders, residual confounding factors such as genetic predisposition and dietary habits may have influenced the findings. Third, statin users may exhibit greater health-seeking behaviors, which could not be fully accounted for due to the retrospective nature of the study. Fourth, because statin prescription records in NHIS database were used as a proxy for actual statin use, there remains uncertainty as to whether participants adhered to their prescribed regimens. In addition, the use of prescription data may introduce immortal time bias, as exposure is defined based on future prescriptions. To mitigate this concern, we attempted to address potential immortal time bias by conducting sensitivity analyses that incorporated a lag period between prescription and exposure classification. Fifth, the study cohort was derived from the NHIS-Senior cohort, which comprised the general older adult population rather than individuals specifically diagnosed with MASLD. Sixth, we could not incorporate established non-invasive fibrosis indices such as FIB-4 (Fibrosis-4) index because platelet count data were not available in the NHIS-Senior database. As an alternative, we tried to perform stratified analyses using the BARD score to partially capture fibrosis-related risk heterogeneity.

Thus, the effect of statins on LRE risk warrants further investigation in a hospital-based outpatient setting. Finally, as the study was limited to older South Korean adults, the generalizability remains uncertain. Future studies should evaluate the effects of statins in diverse ethnic populations to establish their broader applicability.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that older adults with MASLD and 1 to 4 CMRFs, including those aged ≥ 75 years, represent a key population that may derive significant hepatic benefits from statin use, particularly in reducing the risk of LREs. These findings underscore the role of liver fat burden in modulating statin effectiveness and highlight the need for tailored therapeutic strategies in older adults with advanced MASLD. Given the observational nature of this study, future large-scale randomized controlled trials are essential to confirm these findings and comprehensively assess the risks and benefits of statin therapy across diverse clinical populations.
