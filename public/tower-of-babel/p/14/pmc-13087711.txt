Current practices of SGLT2 inhibitor use and ketone monitoring in intensive care units: A multicentre observational study
Authors: Takaya Sasaki, Sharon Micallef, Amanda Y. Wang, Fatima Butt, Naomi E. Hammond, Miranda Hardie, Serena Knowles, Takashi Yokoo, Martin Gallagher, The George Institute for Global Health and the Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society Clinical Trials Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSodium-glucose co-transporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors offer benefits for patients with chronic kidney disease, heart failure, and type 2 diabetes mellitus (with cardiovascular risk factors or atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease) but concerns exist regarding euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis (EDKA) during acute illness and surgery. We evaluated SGLT2 inhibitor use, discontinuation patterns, and blood ketone monitoring in intensive care units (ICUs).DesignProspective, multicentre, cross-sectional point prevalence study as part of The George Institute for Global Health and Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society Clinical Trials Group Point Prevalence Program.SettingFifty-two ICUs in Australia and New Zealand.ParticipantsCritically ill adult patients in ICUs on the study day.Main outcome measuresNumber of patients receiving an SGLT2 inhibitor at ICU admission, their management, serum ketone levels, and ketoacidosis monitoring procedures.ResultsAmong 786 patients, 54 (6.9 %) were prescribed SGLT2 inhibitors prior to hospital admission, mostly for type 2 diabetes mellitus (75.9 %) or heart failure (18.5 %). Of these, 28 (51.9 %) had their SGLT2 inhibitor discontinued, primarily due to EDKA concerns. Only 17 (34.6 %) of the studied ICUs had documented ketone measurement policies, with 1 (1.9 %) including SGLT2 inhibitors as an indication for measurement. Of 114 patients with blood ketone measurements, 38 (33.0 %) had elevated levels, of whom 6 (5.2 %) met ketoacidosis criteria.ConclusionsPractice regarding discontinuation of SGLT2 inhibitors and ketone monitoring varies widely. With SGLT2 inhibitor use projected to increase, better understanding their risk–benefit profile in the ICU setting and more systematic approaches to ketone measurement are needed. Future research addressing these practice variations is essential.

Introduction

The use of sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors is expanding, driven by their proven benefits for patients with heart failure and chronic kidney disease.[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6] Despite their benefits, these agents carry a risk of euglycaemic diabetic ketoacidosis (EDKA), which is a rare but potentially life-threatening adverse effect characterised by metabolic acidosis and elevated blood ketone levels occurring without significant hyperglycaemia.[7], [8], [9] This risk has given rise to a variety of recommendations to cease or avoid these agents in settings of fasting or systemic illness[10], [11], [12], [13], [14] but there are little data about current practice in real-world settings.

The risk of EDKA is particularly relevant in acute care settings, where factors such as fasting, sepsis, and surgical stress are common and recognised risk factors. Given the indications for SGLT2 inhibitor use, including type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) with multiple cardiovascular risk factors or known atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, kidney disease, and heart failure,[15], [16], [17], [18] it is to be expected that increasing proportions of patients entering intensive care units (ICUs) will be receiving them on admission. Further complicating clinical decision-making is a meta-analysis suggesting that SGLT2 inhibitors may protect against episodes of acute kidney injury (AKI; odds ratio for reduction in AKI [OR] of 0.64 [95 % confidence interval: 0.53–0.78], p < 0.001),19 several trials suggesting very low rates of EDKA in acutely unwell patients[20], [21], [22] and a recent study of patients undergoing emergency surgery that showed continuing SGLT2 inhibitors preoperatively was not associated with an increased risk of postoperative diabetic ketoacidosis.23 However, the optimal management strategy for critically ill patients remains unclear.

Accordingly, using a binational point prevalence study of intensive care unit (ICU) patients, we set out to examine the prevalence of SGLT2 inhibitor use, the determinants of discontinuation, and the extent of blood ketone testing and associated blood ketone levels.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

The Point Prevalence Program (PPP) is a collaborative initiative between the Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society Clinical Trials Group and The George Institute for Global Health. It is a prospective, cross-sectional, multicentre program conducted annually to evaluate ICU patient characteristics and elements of their treatment on specific census dates.24 As part of the PPP conducted in June 2024, we examined current practices regarding SGLT2 inhibitor use and ketone monitoring in ICU patients. The PPP has ethical approval from the Sydney Local Health District Ethics Review Committee for Australia (Reference No. X18-0511 & 2019/ETH07327) and the Health and Disability Ethics Committee for New Zealand (Reference No. MEC/09/28/EXP/AM10). A waiver for individual patient consent was approved by these committees. The study adheres to the STROBE statement.25

Procedure

Members of the investigator team (MG, SM, and AW) submitted a research proposal and case report form questions to the PPP Steering Committee for approval. All ICUs affiliated with the Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society Clinical Trials Group (ANZICS CTG) were invited to participate in the PPP in 2024 by the PPP Convenors. Research staff at each participating ICU collected data on all adult patients (aged 16 years and older), who were admitted to ICU at 10:00 h on either 4th or 19th June 2024. Study data were collected and managed using Research Electronic Data Capture tools hosted at The George Institute.26 The variables collected for the present study are listed in Appendix 1.

The data collected addressed the reasons for SGLT2 inhibitor use, continuation (or discontinuation) of SGLT2 inhibitors following admission, timing and reason for discontinuation, unit policies and practices related to blood ketone measurement, and serum blood ketone levels. For the purposes of this study, the “treating team” was defined as the admitting or primary clinical team responsible for the patient’s care. “Treating team” was also a predefined option on the case report form for identifying the person or team responsible for ceasing SGLT2 inhibitor therapy. In addition, demographic and clinical data were collected, including age, sex, primary diagnosis, COVID-19 status, and severity of illness on ICU admission measured by the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score.27 Based on the reported ketone levels, patients with available ketone measurements were classified into one of the following four categories:28 absent, <0.6 mmol/L; mild, 0.6–1.5 mmol/L; moderate, 1.6–3.0 mmol/L; and marked, >3.0 mmol/L. The category of marked hyperketonaemia was defined as equivalent to ketoacidosis for this analysis.29

Statistical analysis

This was a descriptive study without hypothesis testing or comparative analyses. Descriptive statistics were used to summarise patient characteristics and outcomes. Categorical variables were presented as proportions, and continuous variables as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs). Stratified descriptive summaries were conducted to examine variations in SGLT2 inhibitor management based on admission source and other relevant factors. All the analyses were performed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC).

Decisions regarding SGLT2 inhibitor discontinuation

Of the 54 patients receiving SGLT2 inhibitors prior to hospital admission, 28 patients (51.9 %) had their SGLT2 inhibitors discontinued during their hospital stay (Fig. 2A). Among these 28 patients, the primary reasons for SGLT2 inhibitor cessation were concerns relating to ketosis or ketoacidosis (6) or urinary tract/genital infection (1) (Fig. 2B). Other reasons include infection, sepsis, patient palliation, nature and severity of the clinical condition, surgery, AKI, or a combination of these, and in 11 patients the reasons were unknown. Incidence of starting SGLT2 inhibitors in the ICU

Among the 686 patients who were not receiving SGLT2 inhibitors prior to hospital admission, four patients (0.6 %) were started on SGLT2 inhibitors during their ICU stay.

Ketone measurement policies

Of the 52 participating ICUs, 17 (32.7 %) had a documented policy for blood ketone measurement (Fig. 3A). Among them (Fig. 3B), common indications for ketone measurements included DKA, hyperosmolar hyperglycaemia, diabetes, or hyperglycaemia. Ketone testing findings

Among the 786 patients, 115 patients (14.6 %) underwent blood ketone testing (Fig. 4A), with values available in all but one patient. Twenty-three patients (20.2 %) exhibited a mild increase in blood ketone levels, 9 (7.9 %) had a moderate rise, and 6 (5.3 %) exhibited a marked increase that would meet the criteria for ketoacidosis (Fig. 4B). Discussion

These results provide valuable and current insights into the use of SGLT2 inhibitors in patients admitted to the ICU. Our findings indicate that around 7 % of patients entering Australian and New Zealand ICUs are receiving an SGLT2 inhibitor, with the majority prescribed for T2DM or heart failure. This is a demonstrably higher rate than recent Australian population estimates of ∼1 % prevalence30 and reflects the relative medical complexity of patients entering ICU. These results also suggest that cessation of these agents in patients with acute illness does not align with the current recommendation and that departmental policies relating to ketone measurement are often not addressing SGLT2 inhibitor use.

Safety concerns relating to SGLT2 inhibitors were first publicised by the United States Food and Drug Administration in 2015 following the report of 20 cases of EDKA in the preceding 2 years.31 Since then, there have been numerous recommendations for vigilance about the risk of EDKA, including cessation or avoidance of SGLT2 inhibitors in patients who are fasting for surgical procedures or experiencing acute illnesses.32,33 Whilst many patients in ICU meet these criteria for EDKA risk, more recent data question the association between SGLT2 inhibition and EDKA34 and suggests that the absolute risk of this complication is very low.20,22

A further challenge in assessing EDKA in the ICU setting is the fact that some degree of ketosis is common in critically ill patients.28 Our data confirm this, with over 30 % of patients who had their ketones measured having values above the upper limit of the normal range, albeit that most of these elevations were mild and probably not of clinical concern. However, as ketone measurements were obtained predominantly in patients for whom clinicians already had a concern, these findings largely reflect a higher-risk subgroup and cannot be generalised to the broader ICU population. In addition, there is some evidence to suggest that stable ketosis in the setting of infections in ICU may enhance immune responses and patient outcomes35 and are unlikely to be harmful. It may be that this high prevalence of mild ketosis, along with the possible benefits from this state, are driving the continuation of SGLT2 inhibitors in patients in ICU and the relative silence of unit policies on ketone measurement.

Our findings highlight the low rate of SGLT2 inhibitor initiation in ICU patients. Whilst this reflects both the safety concerns and the lack of proven benefits for these agents in the ICU setting, there are situations where the impact of SGLT2 inhibitors needs more thorough study. The maintenance use of these agents has been associated with lower rates of AKI36 and there have been signals from small studies suggesting that short-term use may prevent AKI.22,37 More recently, a randomised study of perioperative SGLT2i use around coronary artery bypass grafting demonstrated that empagliflozin reduced postoperative AKI in patients with T2DM .38 In addition, the evidence for their rapid benefit in heart failure studies39 supports the case that these agents may have benefit in some acute medical settings.

Beyond the risk of EDKA, SGLT2 inhibitors may have additional acute benefits in critically ill patients. Published studies suggest potential haemodynamic and kidney benefits, including increased urinary output and a negative fluid balance, often reducing the need for diuretics.40,41 These findings indicate that the acute physiological impact of SGLT2 inhibitors extends beyond glycaemic control and EDKA risk, warranting further investigation in ICU settings.

This study has several limitations. First, although the findings are likely to be applicable across Australia and New Zealand due to broadly similar ICU practices and patient populations, the findings may be less generalisable to other regions or settings. Secondly, participating sites in the PPP may have been more likely to be academic or teaching hospitals, which could introduce selection bias and result in over-representation of well-resourced ICUs with established evidence-based practices. Thirdly, the study focused on descriptive outcomes and did not examine the impact of SGLT2 inhibitor management on clinical outcomes such as AKI, mortality, or length of stay. A strength of the study is its comprehensive coverage of Australia and New Zealand ICUs, such that it provides a comprehensive summary of current national practice and policies.

In conclusion, our study highlights the variable practices surrounding SGLT2 inhibitor management in patients admitted in ICUs. While concerns regarding ketoacidosis drive frequent discontinuation, the lack of standardised protocols and ketone monitoring policies suggests a need for clearer clinical guidance. Recent literature would suggest the absolute risk of EDKA in the ICU setting is low and, given the high rates of morbidity and mortality in ICU, further research is needed to more fully understand the balance of harms and potential benefits from these agents in critically ill populations.
