Clinical and Dermoscopic Features of Pigmentary Changes in Scleroderma and Its Association with Systemic Involvement - A Descriptive Study from a Tertiary Care Centre in South India
Authors: N Shijini, Anza Khader, Devi Keerankulangara, O Fathimathusalma, D Amrutha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Systemic sclerosis (SSc) is a multisystem disease with the highest case-specific mortality among other autoimmune diseases. Dermatological manifestations include skin thickening, Raynaud’s phenomenon, digital ulcers, pitted scarring and pigmentary abnormalities. Vascular abnormalities in systemic sclerosis are responsible for various systemic complications including pulmonary hypertension and scleroderma renal crisis. The underlying vascular pathology responsible for organ involvement might also result in skin manifestations including Raynaud’s phenomenon and digital ulcers. However, vascular abnormalities resulting in pigmentary changes in systemic sclerosis have not been well studied. Pigmentary changes, which are more common among the darker phenotypes, may act as an early marker for the identification of patients at risk for subsequent organ damage. We aim to study various clinical and dermoscopic features of pigmentary abnormalities in systemic sclerosis and its association with systemic features.Materials and Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted on 60 patients with scleroderma who attended the autoimmune clinic at the Department of Dermatology and Venereology Government Medical College, Kozhikode. Detailed clinical examination and investigations were performed for dermatological manifestations with special emphasis on pigmentary changes. Pigmentary changes were examined with the help of a digital dermoscope, and the different patterns observed were classified into pigmentary, vascular and scale patterns.Results:In the present study, a female predominance was observed. The mean age of the study group was 44 ± 13 years. Out of the 60 patients, 52 (86.7%) had limited cutaneous disease and 8 (13.3%) had diffuse cutaneous disease. The MRSS varied from 3 to 32 with a mean of 14.22 ± 5.76. The most common type of pigmentary change observed was vitiligo like macules (70%) followed by salt and pepper pigmentation (61.6%). Other types of pigmentation were localised diffuse hyperpigmentation, reticulate hyperpigmentation, localized hypopigmentation, melasma like hyperpigmentation and periorbital hyperpigmentation. On dermoscopy, the most common pigmentary pattern in vitiligo like macules was the petaloid pattern (57%). White homogenous areas and perifollicular pigment (89.2%) were the predominant findings in salt and pepper pigmentation. More than 50% of patients with salt and pepper pigmentation, melasma like and periorbital pigmentation and more than 25% of patients with vitiligo like macules and reticulate pigmentation had red structureless areas on dermoscopy. Only 10% of patients with localised diffuse hyperpigmentation had red structureless areas. About 70% of patients with vitiligo like macules, salt and pepper and localised diffuse hyperpigmentation had digital ulcers. Around 20% of patients with vitiligo like macules and salt and pepper pigmentation and 30% of patients with localised diffuse hyperpigmentation had interstitial lung disease.Limitations:Our study is limited by being institutional based and may not reflect the actual incidence of pigmentary disturbance in the general population with SSc.Conclusion:We have described various clinical and dermoscopic findings in all types of pigmentary changes in SSc. Dermoscopy of pigmentary changes may be as helpful as nail fold capillaroscopy in correct diagnosis and assessing the prognosis of the disease.

Introduction

Scleroderma (systemic sclerosis [SSc]) is a multisystem disease due to autoimmunological process, characterized by vascular endothelial injury, inflammation and activation of fibroblasts. The extent of organ involvement, disease progression and prognosis vary from individual to individual.[1]

SSc is classified into limited cutaneous and diffuse cutaneous forms depending upon the extent of skin involvement. In the limited form of SSc, skin sclerosis is seen on the face and extremities distal to the elbow and knee joint, whereas in the diffuse forms, sclerosis extends beyond these limits. Associated features include vascular abnormalities like telangiectasia and digital ulceration.[12]

Different patterns of pigmentary changes are also seen in scleroderma patients like (i) diffuse generalized hyperpigmentation as in Addison’s disease, (ii) salt and pepper pigmentation, (iii) hypo- and hyperpigmentation localized to sclerotic skin, (iv) streaky hyperpigmentation over blood vessels on a background of depigmentation and (v) reticulate hyperpigmentation.[34567] Abnormal thermoregulatory mechanisms of local vasculature may be responsible for the pigmentary changes, though not proved beyond doubt.[35]

Vascular abnormalities including the endothelial proliferation and deposition of scar tissue produce complications like pulmonary artery hypertension, scleroderma renal crisis and digital ulceration. Pulmonary artery hypertension is presently the most common cause of disease-related death in SSc. Pigmentary changes on the skin and organ involvement may have the same underlying vascular abnormalities.[8] Further studies are needed to support these findings.

Skin biopsy may not be useful for diagnosis of the disease due to the high rate of false negative results, especially in the early stages[9]

Dermoscopy, a non-invasive imaging technique used along with clinical examination helps with additional pigmentary and vascular information which are not visible to the naked eye.[10]

A very few data are available about the different pigmentary changes in SSc, especially in the Asian population[11]

In the present study, we try to describe different clinical types and dermoscopic features of pigmentary changes in scleroderma. We also attempt to study the association of systemic involvement with various pigmentary abnormalities in scleroderma.

Materials and Methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted at a tertiary care centre in South India and was approved by the institutional research committee and ethics committee. The study duration was 22 months, from February 2021 to October 2022. Patients satisfying 2013 ACR/EULAR criteria for systemic sclerosis attending autoimmune clinic of dermatology outpatient department were selected for the study. Patients with features of overlap syndrome or taking hydroxychloroquine for more than 1 year and those with a cumulative dose of 3900 mg of cyclophosphamide were excluded.[1213]

After the informed consent was obtained, those who met the inclusion criteria were subjected to history taking and clinical examination, and findings were recorded in a preset proforma.

The extent of skin sclerosis was measured using the modified Rodnan skin score (MRSS).[14] Pigmentary abnormalities noted were defined accordingly [Table 1]. Dermoscopic examination was performed using Dermalite Premier AM4113ZT dermoscope. Biopsy and histopathological examination were performed from the representative lesions in selected cases. Investigations including routine blood and urine, ANA profile and investigations related to system involvement were performed.

Results

Sixty patients were included in this study. The age of the patients ranged from 14 to 70 years with a mean age of 44 ± 13 years. Females outnumbered males in a ratio of 14:1. The duration of illness ranged from 0.25 years to 25 years with an average of 6.2 ± 4.5 years.

All patients had the feeling of skin tightedness from the onset itself. Pigmentary abnormalities were noticed by 90% and Raynaud’s phenomenon by 82%. At the time of presentation all patients had skin thickening, sclerodactyly and pigmentary abnormalities [Table 2].

Out of the 60 patients, 52 (86.7%) had limited cutaneous disease and 8 (13.3%) had diffuse cutaneous disease. The MRSS varied from 3 to 32 with a mean of 14.22 ± 5.76.

Vitiligo like macules (70%) was the most common type of pigmentary abnormality, followed by salt and pepper pigmentation (61.6%).

Vitiligo like macules and diffuse hyperpigmentation was most commonly observed on the upper limb. Face and neck were the most common site of salt and pepper pigmentation. Reticulate hyperpigmentation was most commonly observed on the trunk [Table 3].

On routine investigations, 28 (46%) patients were found to have anaemia and 21 (35%) had elevated ESR. ANA-IF was positive in 41 patients (68.3%). Among these, 13 (31.7%) were tested positive for anticentromere antibodies (ACA), while 7 (24.3%) were positive for anti-Scl-70 antibodies. Discussion

A female preponderance has always been noted in SSc, ranging from 1:3 to 1:14.[1] In our study, females were more commonly affected with a male-to-female ratio of 1:14. Most patients presented in the fourth decade of life, similar to the observation by previous authors but contrary to the findings in certain other studies.[15161718] Most patients had a disease duration of < 5 years, as observed in previous studies.[1516]

At the time of presentation, all patients had skin thickening and sclerodactyly, similar to other Indian studies.[1517] We observed pigmentary abnormalities also in all our patients. Pigmentary changes were present in 90% of our patients even at the onset of the disease, which was comparable to various Indian studies but was higher than the reports from Europe and Asia (65–80%).[17181920]

Among the 60 patients, 86.7% patients had limited cutaneous type of systemic sclerosis (lcSSc), whereas only 13.3% had diffuse cutaneous type of systemic sclerosis (dcSSc), comparable to the study by Sujau I et al.[21] In contrast, many studies report a higher ratio (up to 80%) of the diffuse type.[1722]

The increase in number of limited cutaneous disease may be due to severe symptoms at the onset of disease in the form of Raynaud’s phenomenon (81.7%), digital ulcers (65%), skin thickening (100%) and symptoms of GERD (90%).

In the present study, the mean MRSS was 14.22, comparable to that of the studies conducted at the same centre.[1723] Low mean MRSS observed in our study may be due to a predominance of (86.7%) limited cutaneous illness.

The commonest pigmentation noted in our study was vitiligo like macules (70%), in contrast to studies from India, Pakistan and other Asian countries where salt and pepper pigmentation predominated.[1719] Depigmented macules (37.5%) described by Sureshan et al.[17] and pseudovitiligo described by Rai et al.[24] could probably be similar to the vitiligo like macules in our study. We noticed vitiligo like macules predominantly over the extremities, the upper limb more than lower limb. Mittal et al.[25] also observed a similar finding of localization of vitiligo like macules around the joints. The depigmented macules in systemic sclerosis can be differentiated from vitiligo by their focal, well-defined, stable nature and absence of mucosal involvement. Differentiation from vitiligo is more marked with dermoscopic findings of petaloid patterns and white homogenous areas and the presence of vascular patterns like linear vessels and red structureless areas.

Salt and pepper pigmentation was present in 37 (62%) patients, higher than in other studies.[1617] The relatively low frequency of salt and pepper pigmentation in dcSSc was also observed by Asif S et al.[22] The predominant face and neck (65%)) involvement, especially near the hair-bearing area, was in accordance with the findings of Sureshan et al.[17] About 90% of patients with salt and pepper pigmentation had white homogenous areas and perifollicular pigmentation. More than 50% had linear vessels, and more than 30% had red homogenous areas signifying predominant vascular changes. Salt and pepper pigmentation has been correlated with vasculopathy and the rich capillary network around the follicle is responsible for the perifollicular retention of pigmentation.[25]

Localized diffuse hyperpigmentation was noted in 29 (48%) patients in our study which was higher than the observation (39%) by Leroy et al. but lesser than that by Sharma et al. (88%).[1526] The predominant upperlimb involvement may be explained by the accentuation of pigmentation over sunexposed areas.[8] Irregular pigment network (96%) and reticulate scales (76%) were predominant dermoscopic findings in localised diffuse hyperpigmentation. Vascular changes were not prominent in dermoscopy in this type of pigmentation.

In our study, 11 (18.3%) patients had reticulate hyperpigmentation, similar to the case reports by Ee et al. and Chuamanochan et al.[38] The predominant trunk (81.8%) involvement was similar to previous observations. Perifollicular hypopigmentation (73%) and irregular pigment network (64%) were the predominant dermoscopic findings. Less than 30% had vascular changes like red structureless areas. Only very few cases of reticulate pigmentation with vascular changes has been reported.[5]

The predominant involvement of extremities in patients with localized hypopigmentation in our study could be similar to the acral hypopigmentation described by Leroy V et al.[26] Localised hypopigmentation demonstrated reduced pigment network in a majority (63%) and vascular changes including linear vessels and red blotches (38%).

Melasma like hyperpigmentation (20%) and periorbital hyperpigmentation (6.6%) observed in our study have not been reported in SSc, to the best of our knowledge. A higher number of patients with melasma like hyperpigmentation may be due to the female preponderance, hormonal and vascular factors involved in the pathogenesis of both diseases.[272829] Superficial location of the vasculature, along with thin skin overlying the orbicularis oculi, is considered as one of the causes of periorbital pigmentation.[30] Predominant pigmentary dermoscopic features in these conditions included pseudoreticular network. Accentuated pseudoreticular network is well described in melasma.[3132] However, additional vascular patterns observed in our study including red structureless area and arborizing vessels are not known to be described in melasma.

These pigmentary abnormalities whether due to scleroderma or a mere coexistence of these conditions with scleroderma are not clear.

We found a higher percentage of patients with diffuse pigmentation had interstial lung disease (28%) than compared to those with salt and pepper and vitiligo like macules. A significant association of diffuse pigmentation with digital ulcers and interstitial lung disease has been reported by Le roy et al.[26] However, the frequency of digital ulcers in our patients with vitiligo like macules, salt and pepper pigmentation and diffuse pigmentation was almost similar.

We have described various clinical and dermoscopic findings in all types of pigmentary changes in SSc. The vascular features have been brought about clearly by dermoscopy in each of these pigmentary changes. The presence of vascular changes along with white homogenous areas and reduced pigment network will be useful to differentiate the vitiligo like macules and localized hypopigmentation in scleroderma from vitiligo and other hypopigmentary abnormalities. The less prominence of vascular changes in diffuse pigmentation can be attributed to the more severe vasoconstrictive effects of endothelin 1 in these pigmentations. A higher incidence of interstitial lung disease in patients with diffuse pigmentation may be due to the same underlying vasoconstriction.

Dermoscopy of pigmentary changes may be as helpful as nail fold capillaroscopy in correct diagnosis and assessing the prognosis of the disease. The relatively lower frequency of vascular markings in diffuse hyperpigmentation akin to absent capillaries in nail fold capillaroscopy and more severe pulmonary involvement needs further studies.

Our study is limited by being institutional-based and may not reflect the actual incidence of pigmentary disturbance in the general population with SSc.
