Tuning the microenvironment of bioinspired Fe–Cu molecular electrocatalysts for efficient oxygen reduction reaction in zinc–air batteries
Authors: Kun-Zu Yang, Jiao Dong, Chao Xu, Peng-Peng Guo, Ye-Min Zhao, Ling Jia, Yong Yang, Hao-Ning Zhu, Wen-Quan Yuan, Ping-Jie Wei, Zhi-Qiang Wang, Jin-Gang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dual-atom catalysts (DACs) with synergistic bimetallic sites are emerging as promising alternatives to the platinum group metal catalysts for the cathodic oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) in advanced energy conversion technologies. However, the rational design of DACs is challenged by the site heterogeneity and structural damage from prevalent pyrolysis methods, while the synthesis process of binuclear complexes with well-defined structures is cumbersome, making them rarely applied in practical devices. Bioinspired by the binuclear heme/Cu center in cytochrome c oxidase, herein we developed a facile stepwise assembly strategy to prepare Fe–Cu binuclear molecular catalysts via coordinating bipyridine (bpy)-derivative-functionalized copper complexes with nitrogen atoms of N-doped carbon substrates, followed by iron phthalocyanine (FePc) incorporation. The microenvironment of the Fe–Cu binuclear molecular catalysts was readily tuned by varying substitutes on bpy (2,2′-bpy-4,4′-R, R = H, Cl, CH3, COOH), among which the carboxylic acid-functionalized FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC demonstrated impressive high ORR activity and stability with a half-wave potential (E1/2) of 0.92 V vs. RHE in 0.1 M KOH and a maximum power density (Pmax) of 147 mW cm−2 in loaded zinc–air batteries, surpassing those of commercial Pt/C (E1/2 = 0.87 V, Pmax = 120 mW cm−2) under similar experimental conditions. Theoretical calculations suggested that functionalizing the Cu sites with carboxylic acid groups could significantly reduce the overpotential, thus of boosting the intrinsic ORR activity, which could be attributed to the electronic structure modulation of Fe–N4 sites and the hydrogen-bonding network formation. This work highlights the advantage of bioinspired catalyst design and assembly engineering in advancing the precise construction of DACs for energy conversion applications.

Introduction

Atomically dispersed metal–nitrogen–carbon (M–N–C, M = Fe, Co, Mn, etc.) catalysts have been intensively explored as promising alternatives to expensive and scarce Pt-based catalysts for the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) at the cathode of sustainable energy conversion devices, such as proton exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFCs) and zinc–air batteries (ZABs).1–3 Single-atom catalysts (SACs) with Fe–N4 active sites have attracted considerable attention due to their efficient ORR activity comparable to that of commercial Pt/C,4–6 while they are limited by the inherent scaling relationship in multi-electron transfer ORR processes.7,8 On the other hand, incorporating a secondary metal ion adjacent to the initial site to construct dual-atom sites represents a promising strategy for overcoming the limits of SACs due to their unique advantages in charge distribution and synergistic effects,9,10 which directs the rational design of dual-atom catalysts (DACs) for efficient ORR.

Recently, significant progress has been made in the construction of DACs through various preparation routes. The one-pot method, which involves direct pyrolysis of metal salts and nitrogen-containing organic precursors, serves as a convenient approach for synthesizing DACs;11 however, challenges remain in terms of controllable preparation. Specifically, pyrolysis of binuclear metal complexes featuring organic ligands coordinated to two adjacent metal ions to form dual-atom sites of DACs represents an impressive demonstration.12,13 Using heterometallic dual-complex pairs based on electrostatic interactions as the precursor could promote the formation of the desired dual-atom sites in DACs during pyrolysis.14 In addition, using H2O2 treatment,15 corrosive gas treatment,16 or Ar plasma etching17 of pre-formed single-atom sites to generate vacancies for capturing secondary metal ions, and subsequent thermal treatment could form dual-atom sites for boosting the ORR catalytic activity. By optimizing the precursor and preparation conditions, many thermally synthesized DACs exhibit excellent oxygen reduction performance. However, the commonly used pyrolysis method inevitably leads to heterogeneity of active sites,18,19 and the organic ligands in the precursor are destroyed under high temperature conditions, making it impossible to establish a ligand-functionalized structure–activity relationship to guide catalyst design.

On the other hand, non-pyrolyzed metal macrocyclic compounds (metal phthalocyanines, porphyrins, and corroles) with well-defined structures serve as an ideal platform for electrocatalysis, providing a clear mechanistic understanding through the modulation of substituent electronic effects and coordination environments.20–22 As natural biocatalysts, metalloenzymes such as cytochrome c oxidase (CcO, Fig. 1B) bearing binuclear heme/Cu active sites exhibit excellent activity for dioxygen activation and reduction in biological systems.23 The construction of model compounds mimicking the active sites of CcO and trapping of the ORR intermediates have demonstrated the synergistic effect of the Fe–Cu binuclear center for efficient O2 activation and reduction.24–26 Meanwhile, functional groups (e.g. imidazole, carboxylic acid and phenolic hydroxyl) in the second sphere amino acid residues could facilitate key catalytic steps by establishing hydrogen-bonding networks to modulate the microenvironment of the catalytic interface.27,28 Additionally, the carboxylic acid group offers a distinct advantage by modulating the electronic structure of the metal center through its electron-withdrawing effect.29 However, the preparation of biomimetic model compounds often involves multi-step synthesis processes with low yields of target compounds, which seriously hinders their application in practical devices. Therefore, constructing ligand-functionalized bioinspired dual-atom sites in DACs to achieve efficient electrocatalytic ORR without using cumbersome synthetic procedures remains a formidable challenge.

In this work, a facile assembly approach was developed to construct a dual-atom molecular catalyst FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC (bpyCOOH = 2,2′-bipyridine-4,4′-dicarboxylic acid) for efficient ORR by anchoring Cu complexes on N-doped carbon substrates and subsequently introducing Fe–N4-containing iron phthalocyanine (FePc) via π–π stacking (Fig. 1C). Cu-site-mediated ligand functionalization (Fig. 1A) significantly influences the intrinsic activity of Fe–N4 sites through electronic structure regulation and microenvironment modulation. The carboxylic acid-modified catalyst FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC exhibited superior ORR activity, achieving a significantly higher half-wave potential (E1/2) of 0.92 V vs. RHE and maximum power density (Pmax) of 147 mW cm−2 compared to commercial Pt/C (E1/2 = 0.87 V; Pmax = 120 mW cm−2) in 0.1 M KOH and loaded zinc–air batteries, respectively. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations further suggested that the intrinsic ORR activity enhancement of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC resulted from the lower theoretical ORR overpotential (η = 0.78 V), which could be attributed to the electron-withdrawing effect and the promotion of hydrogen-bonding networks by the introduced carboxyl groups. This work provides a valuable approach for constructing bioinspired Fe–Cu molecular catalysts with a tuneable microenvironment via assembly engineering, contributing to the rational design of DACs for advanced energy conversion technologies.

Results and discussion

The pyrolysis-free Fe–Cu dual atom catalyst FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC was prepared according to the scheme shown in Fig. 1C. Briefly, the complex formed by Zn2+ with 5-aminotetrazole, dispersed on the surface of carbon black, was used as the precursor to obtain nitrogen-doped carbon substrates (NC, Fig. S1 and SI Note 1). Subsequently, nitrogen sites on the hierarchically porous NC were utilized to anchor copper ions, which were then coordinated with 2,2′-bipyridine-4,4′-dicarboxylic acid (bpyCOOH) to form Cu–N3 sites. The final catalyst FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC was obtained by assembling FePc through π–π stacking on NC. In contrast to previous reports of planar functionalization on the periphery of macrocyclic compounds,29–31 the Cu-site-mediated ligand functionalization presented herein not only integrates substituent effects but also involves the construction of hydrogen-bonding networks to modulate the microenvironment.

The morphology and microstructure of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC were characterized via high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR TEM), which revealed a similar spherical morphology to NC and the absence of metal clusters or nanoparticles (Fig. 1D). High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) and the corresponding elemental mapping images of the FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC catalyst manifested the uniform distribution of C, O, N, Fe, and Cu elements in the composite (Fig. 1E). And the counterpart FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC (bpy = 2,2′-bipyridine) displayed similar results (Fig. S5). Furthermore, aberration-corrected (AC) HAADF-STEM analysis at atomic resolution illustrated many bright paired spots on the carbon substrates (Fig. 1F). Together with the elemental mapping results, these observations suggested the existence of Fe–Cu dual atom sites in the FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC catalyst.32–34 The distances between pairs of bright spots were randomly distributed between 2 and 9 Å (Fig. S2–S4), with the shorter distances attributed to the combined effects of multiple factors, including the bending/folding of carbon substrates under actual conditions, as well as the fact that the imaging direction in AC-HAADF-STEM characterization cannot be guaranteed to be perpendicular to the active site plane.35,36

Additionally, compared to FePc, the obvious O–H vibration signal of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC at 3460 cm−1 was attributed to the introduction of carboxyl groups. Additionally, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) survey spectrum analysis indicated the existence of C, N, O, Fe, and Cu elements in FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC with contents of 81.20, 9.18, 7.67, 1.03 and 0.92 at%, respectively (Fig. S7A). The Fe and Cu contents in FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC were accurately measured to be 2.1 and 1.9 wt%, respectively, by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) analysis. The high-resolution C 1s spectrum of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC was deconvoluted into four components of C–C/CC (284.8 eV), C–N/O (285.8 eV), CO (287.4 eV), and O–CO (289.6 eV), respectively (Fig. S7B).38 Meanwhile, the high-resolution N 1s spectrum of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC showed peaks at 399.3 and 400.1 eV that could be assigned to pyridinic-N and pyrrolic-N, respectively (Fig. S7C).39 The high-resolution O 1s spectrum of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC demonstrated three peaks with binding energies of 531.5, 532.5, and 534.0 eV, corresponding to the CO, C–O, and C–OH species, respectively (Fig. S7D),40 and the absence of the Fe/Cu–O characteristic peak (∼530 eV)41,42 confirms the absence of Fe/Cu–O coordination in the catalyst. The high-resolution Fe 2p spectrum of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC showed peaks at 710.9 eV (2p3/2) and 723.7 eV (2p1/2) (Fig. S7E), which could be further deconvoluted into Fe2+ and Fe3+,39,43 indicating an Fe oxidation state between +2 and +3. And the high-resolution Cu 2p spectrum of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC exhibited peaks at binding energies of 933.3 and 953.2 eV (Fig. S7F), which were attributed to Cu 2p3/2 and 2p1/2, respectively.44 The binding energy of the Cu 2p3/2 peak located between those of Cu2+ (934.6 eV)45 and Cu+ (932.5 eV),46 may be due to the tendency of reduction of Cu2+ to Cu+ by X-ray irradiation under high-vacuum conditions.47 Compared with FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC (Fig. S8), both the Fe 2p and Cu 2p peaks of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC shifted to higher binding energy, indicating that the carboxyl groups induce a decrease in electron density at the metal center through an electron-withdrawing effect. The oxidation states of metal ions were further determined by subsequent X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS).

The detailed electronic structure and coordination information of metal sites in FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC were further characterized by using X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra. The Fe K-edge XANES spectra of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC displayed an absorption edge position between those of FePc and the Fe2O3 reference, indicating an oxidation state of Fe between +2 and +3 (Fig. 2A). And the absorption edge position of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC in Cu K-edge XANES spectra remained similar to that of the copper phthalocyanine (CuPc) reference, suggesting the +2 oxidation state of Cu (Fig. 2B). The K-edge Fourier transformed (FT) EXAFS spectra of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC exhibited prominent peaks with radial distances similar to those of metal phthalocyanine standards (FePc and CuPc), corresponding to Fe–N and Cu–N scattering paths, respectively (Fig. 2C and D). Note that no Fe–Fe or Cu–Cu scattering paths were detected in FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC when compared to Fe or Cu foil, confirming the absence of metal aggregates. The best EXAFS fitting results revealed that the coordination numbers of Fe and Cu based on M–N coordination were approximately 4 and 3, respectively (Fig. 2E, F and Table S1). Moreover, the wavelet-transformed (WT) EXAFS plots of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC showed one maximum intensity at ∼5 Å−1, which was consistent with those of metal phthalocyanines but distinct from that of metal foil, thus corroborating the analogous first-shell M–N coordination (Fig. 2G and H). Combined with the paired sites observed in the AC HAADF-STEM images and the homogeneous dispersion of Fe and Cu elements, these results collectively indicated the configuration of Cu–Fe dual-atom metal sites in the catalyst: a Cu ion was coordinated with one nitrogen atom of NC and two nitrogen atoms of the bpyCOOH ligand to form a Cu–N3 site, and the Fe–N4 site of FePc was assembled via π–π stacking interactions with the NC substrate.

The ORR performance of prepared catalysts was evaluated using a typical three-electrode system with a rotating disk electrode (RDE) in 0.1 M KOH. The preparation methods and addition amounts of iron phthalocyanine were investigated. The linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) results indicated that equal-mass addition and stirring in solution were favorable for achieving the optimal activity of iron phthalocyanine (Fig. S10A).37 As a proof of concept, we systematically screened dual-atom catalysts featuring metal phthalocyanines (FePc, CuPc, and CoPc) with secondary metal sites, M(bpyCOOH)-NC (M = Cu2+, Fe3+, Co2+, Ni2+), to investigate the synergistic effects between different metal centers on ORR catalytic activity. The LSV measurements revealed that the combination of iron phthalocyanine with a secondary copper center exhibited the best ORR activity among the tested DACs (Fig. S9). Thus, the dual-atom FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC catalyst was explored in detail. As shown in the ORR polarization curves in Fig. 3A, FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC exhibited an excellent ORR activity with a half-wave potential (E1/2) of 0.92 V vs. RHE, outperforming FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC (0.89 V), FePc/NC (0.88 V), and Pt/C (0.87 V). Moreover, the ORR activity of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC is competitive with that of recently reported FePc-based and dual-atom catalysts (Tables S3 and S4). A similar result was also observed in the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves measured in an O2-saturated 0.1 M KOH solution (Fig. 3B), with FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC displaying a more positive oxygen reduction peak at 0.93 V than its counterpart FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC (0.90 V). It is noteworthy that the samples with only Cu sites (Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC:E1/2 = 0.77 V; Cu(bpy)-NC: E1/2 = 0.76 V, Fig. S11 and Table S2) displayed significantly lower ORR activity relative to FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC (E1/2 = 0.92 V), which suggested that the primary active sites in the Fe–Cu DACs were the Fe–N4 sites of iron phthalocyanine, with the Cu sites likely serving as the regulatory component. Compared to FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC, the physically mixed FePc + Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC exhibited a lower ORR activity (E1/2 = 0.90 V, Fig. S10B), which demonstrated that the enhanced ORR activity of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC is unlikely to be derived from the simple additive effects of the individual components.

Rotating ring-disk electrode (RRDE) measurements confirmed an approximately 4e− oxygen reduction pathway for FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC, as evidenced by H2O2 yield below 4% and electron transfer number (n) higher than 3.9 across the potential range of 0.3–0.9 V (Fig. S12A). The electron transfer number of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC calculated from the Koutecky–Levich (K–L) plots was approximately 4 (Fig. S12B and C), further confirming a nearly direct 4e− reduction of O2 to OH−, which is favorable for achieving high energy conversion efficiency in zinc–air batteries. Moreover, FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC demonstrated a Tafel slope of 55 mV dec−1, which was lower than that of FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC (63 mV dec−1) and Pt/C (84 mV dec−1), verifying its faster ORR kinetics (Fig. 3C). The electrochemical active surface area (ECSA) could be evaluated by using the double-layer capacitance (Cdl) derived from CV curves at different scan rates in the non-faradaic region.49,50 The Cdl of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC was determined to be 12.9 mF cm−2, which is higher than that of FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC (10.0 mF cm−2), suggesting a larger ECSA (Fig. S13A–C). And the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) results indicated a lower charge transfer resistance (65 Ω) of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC when compared to that of FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC (89 Ω, Fig. S13D), further manifesting its higher charge transfer efficiency toward the ORR.

To explore the influence of functional groups on bpy in the Cu sites on catalytic activity of the ORR, a series of bipyridine derivatives (2,2′-bpy-4,4′-R, R = H (bpy), Cl (bpyCl), CH3 (bpyCH3), COOH (bpyCOOH) and 2,2′-bpy-3,3′-COOH (bpyCOOH*)) were screened for the preparation of the Fe–Cu DACs (Fig. 1A). Introducing electron-withdrawing carboxylic acid groups at the para-position of bpy (bpyCOOH) afforded the FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC catalyst (E1/2 = 0.92 V) with a significantly positive shift of the ORR half-wave potential relative to the FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC catalyst (E1/2 = 0.89 V), and a less profound positive shift of E1/2 was observed for the FePc@Cu(bpyCl)-NC catalyst (E1/2 = 0.91 V) with the para-chlorine substituted bpy and the FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH*)-NC catalyst (E1/2 = 0.91 V) with the meta-carboxylic acid substituted bpy (Fig. S14, S15 and Table S2). It is worth noting that introducing electron-donating methyl groups at the para-position of bpy (bpyCH3) in the FePc@Cu(bpyCH3)-NC catalyst (E1/2 = 0.87 V) resulted in a negative shift of E1/2 (Fig. S16 and Table S2). These results strongly imply that the ORR activity of the Fe–Cu DACs could be readily tuned by the Cu-coordinated bpy ligand, and introducing carboxyl groups at the para-position of bpy is crucial for boosting catalyst activity toward the ORR, which might be attributed to both electronic configuration regulation and microenvironment modulation via hydrogen-bonding networks.

A nitrite stripping method was employed to quantify the turnover frequency (TOF) of catalysts for the ORR.51 The negligible changes in the CV and LSV curves of the Cu-based catalyst before and after poisoning confirmed that the Cu sites do not coordinate with nitrite (Fig. S17).52 Consequently, the electrochemically accessible Fe sites in the prepared Fe–Cu DACs were evaluated (Fig. S18). FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC was found to exhibit the highest intrinsic activity with a TOF value of 1.30 e site−1 s−1 at 0.8 V among the tested samples (Fig. 3D), remarkably higher than that of FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC (0.35 e site−1 s−1) and FePc/NC (0.25 e site−1 s−1). These results clearly indicate that the presence of Cu sites could significantly enhance the intrinsic activity of Fe–N4 sites in Fe–Cu DACs, and this enhancement could be further modulated via functional group substitution on the bpy ligand coordinated with Cu ions. Impressively, introducing carboxyl groups at the para-position of bpy endowed the resulting FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC catalyst with extraordinary superior intrinsic activity to the FePc/NC catalyst that only contains Fe–N4 sites.

The catalyst stability was then evaluated, where the accelerated durability test (ADT) and current–time chronoamperometric analysis were conducted in an O2-saturated 0.1 M KOH electrolyte. There was 8 and 10 mV degradation of the half-wave potential after conducting 10 000 CV cycles, observed for FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC (Fig. 3E) and FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC (Fig. S19), respectively. After 23 h of continuous operation at 0.55 V, the current of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC and FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC maintained 94% and 88% of their initial value (Fig. 3F), respectively. Besides, both catalysts exhibited excellent methanol tolerance for a potential application in direct methanol fuel cells (Fig. S20). Further TEM and XPS measurements were conducted on FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC after ADT testing for investigation. The HR TEM images and corresponding elemental mappings indicated that no nanoparticle species were formed after the stability test, and the C, O, N, Fe and Cu elements were uniformly distributed (Fig. S21). In the XPS survey spectrum of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC after the ORR accelerated durability test, a distinct F1s signal appeared (Fig. S22A). Moreover, new peaks could be deconvoluted at 292.5 eV in the C 1s spectrum (Fig. S22B) and at 536.4 eV in the O 1s spectrum (Fig. S22D), corresponding to the C–F and –SO3− species,53,54 respectively, which originated from the addition of Nafion during the electrochemical testing. Apart from these, the other chemical compositions (Fig. S22) were consistent with the initial state of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC.

The discharge performance of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC in practical devices was evaluated by loading it at 1.0 mg cm−2 as a cathode catalyst in aqueous zinc–air batteries (ZABs, Fig. 4A). The FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)–NC–loaded ZAB achieved an open circuit voltage (OCV) of 1.51 V and a maximum power density (Pmax) of 147 mW cm−2, which were much higher than those of commercial 20 wt% Pt/C (OCV = 1.44 V, Pmax = 120 mW cm−2) and FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC (OCV = 1.47 V, Pmax = 125 mW cm−2, Fig. 4B and C), indicating a superior ORR performance in practical applications. The FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC-loaded ZAB exhibited excellent dynamic responses at various discharge current densities ranging from 1 to 20 mA cm−2 and back to 1 mA cm−2 (Fig. 4D). The specific capacity of the FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC-based ZAB was determined to be 770 mAh gZn−1, surpassing those of FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC (724 mAh gZn−1) and Pt/C (708 mAh gZn−1, Fig. S23). Besides, the charging process of rechargeable zinc–air batteries involves the oxygen evolution reaction (OER),55 for which RuO2 is currently the state-of-the-art OER catalyst.56–58 Therefore, the charge–discharge stability was further investigated by assembling a rechargeable ZAB using a mixture of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC and RuO2 (with a 1 : 1 mass ratio) as the cathode catalyst with a total loading of 1.0 mg cm−2. And the reference ZAB employing the Pt/C + RuO2 benchmark59,60 was assembled following a similar procedure. Through 200 h galvanostatic charge–discharge cycling measurements at a current density of 10 mA cm−2, the FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC + RuO2-based ZAB exhibited no significant decay in charge–discharge voltage, demonstrating better stability than the Pt/C + RuO2-based ZAB (Fig. 4E). Moreover, the FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC + RuO2-based ZAB also maintained stable charge–discharge voltage after 12 000 cycles in an accelerated durability test at 30 mA cm−2 (Fig. S24), further demonstrating its stability in practical energy conversion devices. Additionally, the button-type quasi-solid-state ZABs were assembled using FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC as the cathode catalyst and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)-based gel as the electrolyte (Fig. 4F and S25),61,62 and two such batteries connected in series can light up a yellow bulb (Fig. 4G). The assembled button-type ZAB afforded an OCV of 1.37 V (Fig. S26A), a Pmax of 89 mW cm−2 (Fig. 4H), and stable operation for 1000 min after mixing with RuO2 (Fig. S26B). Furthermore, the performance of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC in aqueous and button-type ZABs were among the top for recently reported catalysts (Tables S5 and S6), and the aforementioned results highlight the potential applications of the FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC catalyst in practical devices.

The influence of carboxylic acid groups on the reaction microenvironment was further investigated by in situ Raman spectroscopy tests conducted in the potential range of 1.0–0.3 V (Fig. S27C). In the range of 2800–4000 cm−1, both FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC and FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC exhibited distinct broad peaks (Fig. S27A and B), which were assigned to the O–H stretching mode of interfacial water.63 This peak can be further deconvoluted into components at ∼3200, ∼3400, and ∼3600 cm−1, corresponding to strong hydrogen-bonded water (4-HB-H2O), weak hydrogen-bonded water (2-HB-H2O) and free water, respectively.63,64 Compared with FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC (13–15%), FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC exhibited a lower proportion of free water (7–8%, Fig. S27D), indicating that the introduction of carboxylic acid groups could facilitate the construction of hydrogen-bonding networks.65

To further elucidate how Cu-mediated carboxyl groups regulate the electronic structure of Fe–N4 sites and the corresponding ORR catalytic mechanism, we systematically performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations on the ORR activity of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC, FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC, and FePc@Cu(bpyCH3)-NC catalysts. Considering the diversity of measured distances obtained via AC HAADF-STEM, we first constructed the corresponding models (Fig. 5A and S28) based on planar N-doped carbon substrates and the calculated structure of the experimentally best-performing FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC catalyst. The calculated structures showed that the distance between the Fe and Cu atoms is 8.85 Å, corresponding to the tail of the measured distribution and the optimal 8.85 Å geometry could be considered as one of the subset sites. The bipyridine derivative-coordinated Cu site altered the electronic properties of the local carbon network, which was subsequently transmitted through the conjugated system and ultimately coupled to the Fe–N4 site via long-range interactions.66,67 In addition, Bader charge analyses of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC and FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC revealed that the introduction of the COOH group modulates the charge distribution of the Fe–N4 center, leading to a higher positive charge on the Fe atom in FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC (the calculated corresponding Bader charge is 1.137 |e|, see Table S7). Subsequently, we calculated the Gibbs free energy profiles for the ORR pathway on FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC and FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC at 0 V and 1.23 V (Fig. S29–S31), respectively. The calculated results indicated that both catalysts have the same rate-determining step (RDS), namely the formation of the *OOH intermediate. Specifically, the overpotential (η) of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC is 0.78 V, which is obviously lower than that of FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC (0.83 V, Fig. 5B) and suggests its superior ORR catalytic activity to FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC.

To further clarify the effect of the spatial configuration of the Fe–Cu sites, we tuned the Fe–Cu distance in FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC. When the Fe–Cu distance was increased to 9.76 Å (Fig. S32 and S33), the η increased to 0.96 V (Fig. S34), indicating that an excessively long Fe–Cu distance weakens the synergistic interaction between the two metal sites. This enhancement effect could be attributed to the combined effects of electronic structure modulation and the hydrogen-bonding network in the microenvironment. Furthermore, to verify how different functional groups regulate catalytic activity, we introduced an electron-donating methyl group and performed systematic calculations (Fig. S28, S35 and S36). The RDS on FePc@Cu(bpyCH3)-NC remained the step of *OOH formation, consistent with those of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC and FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC; however, the η increases to 0.97 V (Fig. S36). This indicates that electron-donating groups in the bpy ligand are unfavorable for the ORR, which is consistent with the experimental observation. Finally, to further compare the ORR activities of FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC, FePc@Cu(bpy)-NC and FePc@Cu(bpyCH3)-NC catalysts, we calculated the partial density of states (PDOS). The PDOS results showed that substituents with different electronic properties at the Cu site effectively regulated the electronic structure of the Fe center (Fig. 5C–E and S37). Moreover, electron-withdrawing groups downshifted the Fe d-band center, whereas electron-donating groups induced an upshift.

Conclusions

In summary, bioinspired Fe–Cu dual-atom molecular catalysts (DACs) for efficient ORR were prepared through a facile assembly engineering approach, in which Cu–N3 sites with copper ions coordinated with nitrogen atoms of N-doped carbon (NC) substrates and a bipyridine-derivative ligand and Fe–N4 sites of iron phthalocyanine (FePc) were assembled via π–π stacking on NC. Electrochemical evaluation showed that the presence of Cu sites significantly enhanced the intrinsic activity of Fe–N4 sites, which could be further modulated via functional group substitution on the copper coordinated bpy ligand (2,2′-bpy-4,4′-R, R = H, Cl, CH3, COOH). Introducing carboxyl groups at the para-position of bpy, which feature both electron-withdrawing properties and hydrogen-bonding network formation, could remarkably enhance the intrinsic ORR activity of the FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC catalyst. FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC displayed high ORR performance in 0.1 M KOH for half-cell evaluations (E1/2 = 0.92 V vs. RHE) and zinc–air batteries (Pmax = 147 mW cm−2), surpassing those of the commercial Pt/C catalyst (E1/2 = 0.87 V; Pmax = 120 mW cm−2) under similar experimental conditions. DFT calculations indicated that the introduction of carboxyl groups lowered the η of the *OOH intermediate formation in the rate-determining step of the ORR, which boosted the intrinsic ORR activity of the FePc@Cu(bpyCOOH)-NC catalyst. The bioinspired assembly engineering presented in this work provides a unique insight into enhancing intrinsic ORR activity via microenvironment modulation, advancing the rational design of high-performance DACs for energy conversion.
