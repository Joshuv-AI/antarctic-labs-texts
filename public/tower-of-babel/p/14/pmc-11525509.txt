Water–Rock Interaction Mechanisms and Hydrochemical Evolution in the Underground Reservoirs of Coal Mines
Authors: Haitao Zhang, Binbin Jiang, Haiqin Zhang, Peng Li, Min Wu, Jingwei Hao, Yutian Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Coal-mine underground reservoirs play pivotal roles in water purification through water–rock interactions; however, underlying mechanisms associated with water–rock interactions remain unclear to date. To address this issue, this study considered the underground reservoirs of the Daliuta coal mine, Shendong to conduct the analysis. Through onsite sampling and laboratory simulation experiments, along with hydrochemical-diagram-based analysis, multivariate statistical analysis, and hydrogeochemical simulations, the sources and evolutions of major ions associated with the water of the underground reservoirs were elucidated. Results revealed that the suspended-solid content, turbidity, electrical conductivity, and total dissolved solid concentration in the water at the outlet of the underground reservoirs decreased significantly compared to corresponding values at the inlet. Alteration in the hydrochemical type of water was observed from the Cl·HCO3–Na·Ca, Cl–Na·Ca·Mg, and Cl·SO4–Na types at the inlet of the reservoirs to SO4·Cl–Na at the outlet of the reservoirs. Further, the primary hydrogeochemical processes carried out along the water flow path in the underground reservoirs included mineral dissolution (including halite, silicate, calcite, gypsum, and anhydrite dissolution), pyrite oxidation, cation exchange, and clay-mineral adsorption. Water–rock interactions in the underground reservoirs can be categorized into three stages. The first stage was dominated by dissolution and oxidation, resulting in decreased water pH and overall increased concentrations of total dissolved solids along with major ions in the water. The second stage was dominated by clay-mineral adsorption and cation exchange, resulting in increased water pH and overall concentrations of Na+ and K+ as well as decreased concentrations of Ca2+, Mg2+, and total dissolved solids in the water body. The third stage was characterized by weak water–rock interactions, wherein the concentrations of total dissolved solids along with major ions, as well as the pH of the water stabilized. Thus, our research findings provide important theoretical references for understanding water-purification mechanisms and water-storage-duration optimization in coal-mine underground reservoirs.

Introduction

Protecting water resources remains a substantial technical challenge for green coal mining. It is estimated that coal mining in China consumes groundwater at around 8 billion tons each year, with only about 25% of the mine water being effectively employed. The unused resources associated with mine water account for 60% of China’s annual domestic and industrial water deficit, estimated at 10 billion tons.1 To report the effective mine water application, Gu et al. first introduced the concept of “underground reservoir water storage”, proposing that underground storage along with proper consumption of mine water as a viable solution.2 The approach involves employing mined-out areas for storage of water, where stable coal pillars are connected with artificial dam structures to form reservoirs. It represents a shift from the traditional method of passively “blocking” groundwater, paving the way for new strategies in coal mining and the management of water resources. The concept enabled underground storage along with the consumption of mine water, supplying more than 95% of the required water for effective mining operations.3 The approach has effectively tackled water-use challenges in mining operations and daily needs in the arid coal mining regions of Northwest China, while also providing technical and theoretical regulation for the management of water resources in coal mining areas globally.

Since the incorporation and adoption of underground reservoir technology in coal mining, studies have indicated that the interaction between collapsed rock formations and mine water within these reservoirs enhances the overall quality of the mine water. The described progress was primarily demonstrated by decreased levels of total dissolved solids (TDSs), total dissolved organic matter, suspended solids (SSs), and heavy-metal ions, along with other pollutants.4−7 Nevertheless, water environments within underground reservoirs of coal-mine are generally intricate, as evidenced primarily by uncertainties in factors such as water supply sources, inflow and outflow rates, hydraulic retention times, hydraulic head pressures, mining residues, and collapsed rock characteristics. Hence, research findings on water–rock interactions within coal-mine underground reservoirs are not entirely consistent. For instance, through onsite sampling, Han et al. discovered that water–rock interactions in coal-mine underground reservoirs are mainly dominated by dissolution and filtration, with occasional occurrence of mixing and cation exchange.6 Conversely, through indoor static immersion experiments, Zhang et al. discovered that water–rock interactions in these reservoirs predominantly involve mineral dissolution, cation exchange, and pyrite oxidation.8 This inconsistency is further aggravated by the fact that current studies overlook the influence of water–rock interaction duration on the progress of mine water quality. In reality, after the mine water enters an underground reservoir, it is stored in the reservoir for a certain period and hence affected by various water–rock interactions. Thus, ion concentrations change over time. This is because the duration of water retention directly affects the concentrations of different ions within the reservoir, ultimately affecting the subsequent treatment and utilization of water. Therefore, clarifying the variations in the major-ion concentrations in mine water during water–rock interactions over time holds theoretical importance for further adjusting the water-storage duration in underground coal-mine reservoirs to improve mine water quality.

The content of ions in groundwater depends on the precipitation, dissolution, adsorption, and exchange of rock minerals during water–rock interactions.4−7,9,10 Numerous studies have shown that major ions present in groundwater can provide geochemical information regarding groundwater and facilitate the evaluation of water–rock interactions.11−14 Two common methods used for analyzing hydrogeochemical processes are hydrochemical graphical and multivariate statistical approaches. Hydrochemical graphical methods, including those involving Piper, Gibbs, and scatter plots, are valuable tools for visualizing and interpreting massive amounts of hydrochemical data. These methods can also, to some extent, elucidate hydrogeochemical processes.15−17 Based on hydrochemical diagrams, multivariate statistical techniques including principal component analysis (PCA), correlation analysis, and hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) can identify the sources and intrinsic correlations of hydrochemical components. These methods are particularly effective for interpreting hydrogeochemical processes.11−14 In recent years, advancements in the field of earth science have led to a gradual increase in the popularity of hydrogeochemical simulations. In hydrogeochemical research, the PHREEQC software based on thermodynamic calculations is the most commonly used method to calculate mineral saturation indices (SIs) and identify the dissolution and precipitation processes of groundwater chemical components.12,17,18 Therefore, the comprehensive and integrated use of hydrochemical diagrams, multivariate statistical analysis, and hydrogeochemical simulations is anticipated to yield enhanced outcomes for onsite and experimental studies on water–rock interactions.

The Daliuta coal mine, located in the Shendong mining area, was the first in China to establish underground reservoirs. Operation data of these underground reservoirs revealed the predominance of water–rock interactions between collapsed rocks and mine water, resulting in a considerable improvement in water quality.6,19 However, field engineering practices have also revealed that after the storage of water for different durations in the underground reservoirs, the quality indicators of mine water did not consistently deteriorate compared with those of the original mine water. Some indicators even exhibited a “deterioration” phenomenon.7 Therefore, the underground reservoirs of the Daliuta coal mine were selected as the key focus of this research. Through onsite sampling and laboratory water–rock interaction experiments, the hydrochemical features of the underground reservoir water and related primary hydrogeochemical processes were analyzed. Moreover, variations in the concentrations of major ions at different stages of water–rock interactions were revealed, along with the establishment of a hydrogeochemical evolution model of the water body in the underground reservoirs.

Our previous research (ref (20)) focused on analyzing the effects of various rock-mineral components on the chemical composition of water within the underground reservoir of the Daliuta coal mine, specifically evaluating the self-purification mechanisms by sampling inlet and outlet water. However, current research addresses inconsistencies in mine water quality indicators over varying storage durations, revealing that some indicators may even deteriorate despite storage. This research employs a combination of onsite sampling and laboratory experiments to analyze hydrochemical characteristics and the primary hydrogeochemical processes involved in water–rock interactions, resulting in the establishment of a hydrogeochemical evolution model for the water body in the underground reservoirs. Therefore, while the previous work primarily evaluated the effect of mineral types on water quality, this research introduces a broader perspective by examining the temporal changes in water quality and the underlying processes that govern these changes.

Study Area Setting

The Daliuta coal mine is situated in the northwest region of Shenmu County, Yulin City, Shaanxi Province, China. The terrain of the mine features steeper slopes to the north and gentler slopes to the south, covering around 126.8 km2 of overall area. The region selected for the analysis has a northern temperate semiarid continental monsoon climate, known for its dry spells, infrequent rainfall, and significant evaporation. Particularly, the average annual evaporation rate is about 2049.6 mm, which is four to five times greater than the amount of rainfall. Additionally, two rivers, the Boniu and Wulanmulun, flow through the region without interfering with the safe operation of the coal mine.20,21

Drilling records from the mining area reveal that the strata in the well field, arranged from the most recent to the oldest, are as follows: Quaternary, Jurassic, and Triassic. The Daliuta coal mine comprises its principal coal seams within the Yan’an Formation of the Middle and Lower Jurassic periods. These coal seams are predominantly affected by water inrush from three sources during mining activities: the Quaternary loose layer aquifer, the Jurassic clastic rock fractured aquifer, and the goaf. Surface hydrological drilling and pumping tests indicate that the abundance of water in the two aquifers ranges from low to moderate, with unit water inflow rates respectively varying from 0.0026 to 0.6789 L/(s·m) and 0.00014 to 0.083 L/(s·m). Furthermore, dynamic water recharge in the aquifers is relatively limited.18 Notably, goaf water is the primary mine water source in the Daliuta coal mine and is thus an important component of the underground reservoir system. The mentioned goaf water originates from water accumulated in low-lying areas owing to mining activities in the coal seams. This accumulation process is facilitated by two zones, namely the caving zone as well as the water-conducting fracture zone, which undergo recharge from adjacent aquifers and surface water sources.22,23 The underground reservoir system leverages voids within the goaf to alleviate water storage and achieve water purification, modulating the temporal as well as spatial distribution of the goaf water as well as the utilization of mine water resources, as depicted in Figure 1a. Underground sewage enters the reservoirs via the vertical drilling at designated inlet points (W1-1, W2-1, W3-1, and W4-1, as shown in Figure 1a) followed by the extraction from the reservoirs at outlet points (W1-2, W2-2, W3-2, and W4-2 in Figure 1a), yielding a dynamic flow system for the circulation of groundwater. The functioning of the underground reservoirs can be described through four distinct pathways: path I (W1-1 → W1-2), path II (W2-1 → W2-2), path III (W3-1 → W3-2), and path IV (W4-1 → W4-2).20

Schematic of the spatial layout and roof lithology of the underground reservoirs of the Daliuta coal mine (modified according to ref (20)): (a) location of water-sample collection points; (b) 5–2-coal-seam-roof lithic column.

At the Daliuta coal mine, coal extraction primarily occurs from the 2–2 and 5–2 seams found in the Jurassic Yan’an Formation. The 2–2 coal seam is buried at depths ranging from 30.6 to 133.3 m, while the burial depth of the 5–2 coal seam varies in the range of 162.9 to 280.0 m. The two seams have an average spacing of 155 m. The immediate roofs of the 2–2 and 5–2 coal seams primarily comprise mudstone, while their upper roofs predominantly consist of medium, fine, and silt sandstone (Figure 1b). The 2–2 coal seam has been segmented into five distinct mining panels, with complete extraction. Three goaf spaces, namely Panel 4, Old Panel 6, and New Panel 6, have been transformed into underground reservoirs designated as Reservoirs 1, 2, and 3, respectively. The reservoir areas are 3.81, 1.82, and 2.23 km2, respectively, with 7.9, 5.8, and 8.3 m of average water depths along with 7.11 × 106 m3 of total water-storage capacity.20 Currently, Reservoir 4 is under construction in the 5–2 coal seam (Figure 1a), with 2.3 × 106 m3 of estimated water-storage capacity.24 Given that the water depth of Reservoirs 1–4 does not exceed the caving zone height and that the water at the inlet of each reservoir is in complete contact with air, the interior of the underground reservoirs can be considered as a semiopen oxidative environment.20

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection

Given the onsite conditions of the study area, this research focused on Reservoir 4 of the Daliuta coal mine. For analysis, eight water-sample collection points were established along the reservoir’s inflow and outflow paths. In particular, samples W1–W7 were collected at the inlet of the reservoir, while sample W8 was collected at the outlet of the reservoir. Figure 2 presents the locations of these sampling points.

Distribution of collection points for the water-sample.

The geological data associated with coal mine indicated that collapsed rocks within the reservoir primarily comprise mudstone and sandstone (Figure 1b). The rock samples for our analysis were collected from collapsed rocks around the 5–2-coal-seam roof of the reservoir, which predominantly comprises mudstone and fine sandstone. In total, 50 kg of rock samples were collected, most with particle sizes exceeding 100 mm. Given that our analysis required the rock samples to be laid on an experimental platform, the samples were first crushed at the site using a jaw crusher to achieve a particle size of <100 mm. Subsequently, they were sieved using standard Tyler sieves of 50, 25, 13, 6, and 3 mm, followed by weighing, sealing in self-sealing bags, and transporting to the laboratory under normal temperature conditions for mineral composition analysis and water–rock interaction experiments.

Experimental Design

Experimental Apparatus

Considering the vast interior of the underground reservoir along with the gradual mine water movement within it, leading to prolonged hydraulic retention durations, this study designed a water–rock interaction simulation apparatus for the underground reservoir based on the principle of similarity of simulation experiments, as depicted in Figure 3. The apparatus primarily comprised an acrylic tank, a peristaltic pump, a water-storage tank, and inlet and outlet pipelines. The acrylic tank functioned as a simulation tank for the underground reservoir, measuring 30 cm in height, 50 cm in length, and 20 cm in width. Mine water was pumped from the water-storage tank into the acrylic tank, passed through filled rock samples, and then returned to the water-storage tank through the outlet pipeline, thus establishing a closed-loop water circulation system.

Schematic of the experimental setup for studying water–rock interactions.

Experimental Materials

Mine water employed to carry out the experiment was based on sample W4, with a volume of 50 L. The rock samples used were collapsed rock samples obtained from around the 5–2-coal-seam roof of the reservoir, predominantly comprising fine sandstone and mudstone. Based on actual onsite conditions, the rock samples were crushed and evenly mixed with a mudstone:sandstone mass ratio of 1:1 before being used in the experimental apparatus. The porosity of the laid rock blocks was 0.4.

Actual onsite conditions revealed that the water:rock volume ratio in the underground reservoir varied from 1:2 to 1:1, equivalent to a mass ratio of 1:6 to 1:3. However, given that the porosity of the laid rock blocks was 0.4, the volume ratio of water to rocks was adjusted to 1:1.5, equivalent to a mass ratio of 1:4.5, aligning with the actual onsite conditions.

Experimental Scheme

Subsequently, a 1000 mL measuring cylinder was used to collect sample W4, following which the sample was gradually added to an organic glass tank until it was full. The volume of the organic glass tank with the sample was found to be 30 L. The peristaltic pump was activated, and the inlet and outlet valves were adjusted to maintain a stable water head at the inlet and outlet. At this point, the apparatus achieved a stable water circulation state with a steady flow rate of 1000 mL/min. Based on Darcy’s law25,26 and combined with known parameters, such as the steady flow rate (1000 mL/min), inlet and outlet head difference (1.99 cm; Figure 3), porosity (0.4), and dimensions of the organic glass tank, the permeability coefficient within the tank was calculated to be 0.96 cm/s. Furthermore, the seepage velocity was 0.04 cm/s, while the actual flow velocity was 0.10 cm/s. Notably, the water-storage tank was an open tank without a top surface, and the water surface was in direct contact with air, similar to the environment of underground drainage tunnels in coal mines.(5)Water-sample collection: Using the switch at the sampling port (Figure 3), continuous water sampling was conducted at the outlet of the acrylic tank until the major ions concentrations in the water samples stabilized. The experiment concluded after 40 days (960 h).

Testing and Analysis

Essential water quality parameters including pH, electrical conductivity (EC), temperature (T), and dissolved oxygen (DO) levels were measured in situ using a multiparameter water quality meter (ProDSS, USA), following the guidelines provided in the “Standard Methods for the Examination of Drinking Water” (GB/T5750-2006)27 and “Underground Water Quality Testing Methods” (DZ/T 0064-1993).28 Other water quality parameters were assessed in the laboratory using the method described previously.20 The assessment of water quality involved measuring SSs and TDSs using the gravimetric method, evaluating turbidity through turbidimetry, and determining HCO3– concentration via titration. Furthermore, the concentrations of SO42– and Cl– were analyzed using ion chromatography (IC; Thermo ICS 2100, USA), while the level of Ca2+, Na+, K+, and Mg2+ was quantified through inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (SPECTRO ARCOS, Germany). Meanwhile, the water samples were investigated predominantly for the determination of mass concentrations of various substances including TDSs, K+, Ca2+, Na+, and Mg2+ in mineral leaching experiments using the same methodologies applied to samples derived from field water. Each metric associated with water quality was evaluated at least three times, followed by the calculation of their average values for further analysis. Remarkably, all water samples demonstrated an outstanding charge balance, with errors of less than 5%.

The analysis regarding the elemental composition of the rock samples was carried out using an X-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectrometer (Rigaku ZSX Primus II, Japan). Moreover, the mineralogical composition of the rock samples was assessed through X-ray diffraction (XRD; Bruker D8 ADVANCE, Germany). To analyze the microscopic features of the rock samples, scanning electron microscopy (SEM; FEI Company Nova NanoSEM 450, Czech) was used. The elemental and mineralogical compositions of the rock samples were assessed in the laboratory using the method described previously.20

Data Analysis Methods

HCA and PCA were used in this study for the analysis of the collected hydrochemical data using SPSS 19.0, and thermodynamic calculations of the SIs of various mineral phases present in the water samples were performed with PHREEQC 3.7.3.

PCA was employed to investigate the primary factors influencing groundwater geochemistry.11 PCA included variables such as K+, Na+, Mg2+, Ca2+, Cl–, HCO3–, and SO42– concentrations. The varimax rotation technique was used to enhance the distinctions among the extracted components, yielding components with eigenvalues exceeding 1.0. Components with high loadings, accounting for a total cumulative variance surpassing 80%, were selected as the principal components (PCs) based on the Kaiser criterion29 and the elbow point identified in the scree plot.30

HCA was used to group similar water samples into distinct categories by taking into account multiple hydrochemical indicators, including the content of Na+, K+, Ca2+, Mg2+, HCO3–, Cl–, and SO42–. The study employed the Ward method for linkage rule and Euclidean distance to evaluate the geometric proximity among the samples. These methods are well-established and widely used in the field of hydrogeochemistry.11,12

The thermodynamic calculation formula for the SI using PHREEQC is expressed as follows:311where IAP represents the solution’s ionic activity and Ks(T) represents the solubility constant at a specific temperature. In practical applications, a mineral with an SI between −0.5 and +0.5 can be considered to be in a quasi-equilibrium state. When the SI falls below −0.5, it indicates that the mineral is unsaturated and likely to dissolve; conversely, SI exceeds +0.5, signifies that the mineral is supersaturated and prone to precipitate.32

Results and Discussion

Hydrochemical Characteristics of the Inflow and Outflow Water Samples of the Underground Reservoir

General Hydrochemical Indicators

Table 1 suggests that the pH values of the water samples of both inlet and outlet collected from the underground reservoir range from 7.5 to 8.2, with an average value of 7.9. The described range suggests that the mine water is in a weakly alkaline state. The TDS values ranged from 820.22 to 1560.32 mg/L, with an 1149.37 mg/L average value, indicating the slightly saline nature of mine water (1 g/L ≤ TDS < 3 g/L). The SS concentrations of the inlet water samples (W1–W7) ranged from 48.0 to 835.0 mg/L, averaging 353.87, while the turbidity ranged from 32.6 to 1974.4, with an average of 674.0. Compared with the value of SS and turbidity of the outlet water sample (W8), those of the inlet water samples demonstrated a decrease of over 99%, demonstrating the effective removal of SSs from the mine water by the underground reservoir. The EC value represents the concentration of soluble salts contained in mine water. The value of EC associated with inlet water samples (W1–W7) ranged from 1430 to 2190 μS/cm, with 1930 μS/cm of average value. The DO content in the inflow water samples (W1–W7) was in the range of 2.87–3.47 mg/L, providing an average of 3.16 mg/L. As evident from Table 1, upon comparison with those of the inlet water samples (W1–W7), the TDS concentration and the values EC associated with the outlet water sample (W8) significantly decreased while the DO content considerably increased, indicating a reduction in the ion content of the mine water with increased DO content of the water within the underground reservoir, implying an improvement in water quality.

Major Anions and Cations in the Water Samples

Table 1 shows that the main cations in the underground reservoir include Na+, K+, Ca2+, and Mg2+, with concentrations in the descending order of Na+ > Ca2+ > Mg2+ > K+. Table 1 indicates that Na+ concentrations of the underground reservoir’s water samples (inlet and outlet) range from 200.12 to 530.16 mg/L, with an average of 366.84 mg/L. Similarly, their Ca2+ concentrations range from 52.31 to 116.17 mg/L, with a 78.30 mg/L average value. The concentration of Na+ associated with the outlet water sample (W8) exceeds that of the inlet water samples (W1–W7). This trend implies that with water flow, the Na+ concentration increased while the Ca2+ concentration decreased. The K+ and Mg2+ concentrations of the outlet water samples were low. Particularly, the K+ concentration was observed over a range of 7.72 to 24.53 mg/L, providing an average value of 14.56 mg/L, whereas the content of Mg2+ ranged from 4.61 to 24.52 mg/L, averaging 12.34 mg/L. Evidently, the Mg2+ concentration exhibited a trend similar to the concentration of Ca2+: the Mg2+ concentration decreased with water flow. Based on the variations observed in the Na+, Ca2+, and Mg2+ concentrations and previous research results, these changes may be related to the alternate adsorption of cations.11−13

Table 1 indicates that the primary anions in the underground reservoir include Cl–, SO42–, and HCO3–, with concentrations in the descending order of SO42– > Cl– > HCO3–. Table 1 illustrates that the concentration of Cl– in the inlet and outlet water samples of the underground reservoir provided a range of 198.15 to 620.17 mg/L, with 336.40 mg/L of average value. Similarly, the concentration of SO42– demonstrated a range of 160.43 to 535.31 mg/L, averaging 358.76 mg/L. As evident, the SO42– concentration slightly increased at the outlet of the reservoir (W8), potentially attributed to the pyrite (FeS2) oxidation in the collapsed coal and rocks within the underground reservoir—a phenomenon also reported by studies evaluating water quality in closed mine pits.33−35 The difference between the Cl– concentrations of the inlet and outlet water samples was not significantly consistent, but the concentrations were high. The HCO3– concentration ranged from 154.18 to 336.42 mg/L, providing an average of 271.80 mg/L.

Compared with those of the inflow and outflow water samples of the underground reservoir, the Na+, SO42–, and HCO3– concentrations in the water samples tested in the water–rock interaction experiment were significantly higher, whereas those of Ca2+ and Mg2+ were significantly lower. Furthermore, the concentrations of Cl– and K+ demonstrated no significant differences.

Previous research has shown that variations in the concentrations of cations and anions in water are influenced by various factors, including rock-mineral elemental composition, mineral solubility, water-source mixing ratios, biogeochemical processes, hydrodynamic fields, and temperature fields.11−13,36

Hydrochemical Types of the Water Samples

Based on the Shchukarev classification method for groundwater hydrochemical types, major cations (Mg2+, Na+, Ca2+, and K+) as well as anions (SO42–, Cl–, and HCO3–) constituting over 25% of milliequivalent percentages were used as the grading classification standard. Subsequently, the hydrochemical types of the water samples were assessed along with their salinity (Table 1). Table 1 reveals that except for samples W1 and W7, whose hydrochemical types were Cl·HCO3–Na·Ca and Cl–Na·Ca·Mg, respectively, the hydrochemical types of all water samples of the underwater reservoir were Cl·SO4–Na or SO4·Cl–Na. These types are similar to the hydrochemical types of the water samples tested in the water–rock interaction experiment (all belonging to the SO4·Cl–Na type) and to those of the water samples obtained from the sandstone fracture of the coal-seam roof in the mining area (all belonging to the SO4·Cl–Na·Ca type).

Figure 4 shows the projection of the milliequivalent percentages of the cations and anions observed in all water samples onto a Piper trilinear diagram. All water samples are located in the central part of the right side of the diamond, corresponding to the Cl·SO4–Na and SO4·Cl–Na types, consistent with the Shchukarev classification method results. Figure 4 reveals that the position of the outlet water sample (W8) shifts toward the lower right compared with the positions of the inlet water samples (W1–W7), indicating that water–rock interactions occurred within the underground reservoir, causing the hydrochemical type to change from the incoming Cl·HCO3–Na·Ca, Cl–Na·Ca·Mg, and Cl·SO4–Na types to the outgoing SO4·Cl–Na type. The significant increase in the proportion of SO42– may be associated with the oxidation of pyrite occurring within the underground reservoir.11−13 In addition, over the course of the water–rock interaction experiment, the hydrochemical type of the water samples collected from the outlet of the experimental apparatus remained as the SO4·Cl–Na type. However, the milliequivalent percentages of Na+ and SO42– gradually increased, whereas those of Ca2+ and Mg2+ gradually decreased. This trend is possibly related to cation exchange and pyrite oxidation.11−13

Evidence of Major-Ion Sources

Ion-Ratio Evaluation

Generally, the K+, Na+, and Cl– concentrations in groundwater are relatively stable. Under these circumstances, the ratio ρ(Na+ + K+)/ρ(Cl–) is commonly employed to identify the sources of Na+, K+, and Cl– in groundwater.15,42 Typically, ρ(Na+ + K+)/ρ(Cl–) = 1 indicates that the Na+, K+, and Cl– in groundwater primarily originate from the dissolution of silicate rocks and rock salt.11,15,42Figure 7a reveals that the ρ(Na+ + K+)/ρ(Cl–) values of all the water samples exceed one, indicating that the origins of Na+, K+, and Cl– in the mine water, sandstone-fracture water, and experimental water samples may involve cation exchange processes, along with the dissolution of rock salts (eq 2) and silicate rocks (eq 3).234If the Mg2+, Ca2+, and Na+ + K+ in the water samples originate from the rock-mineral’s dissolution, their ρ(Ca2+ + Mg2+ – SO42– – HCO3–)/ρ(Na+ + K+ – Cl–) ratios must typically remain constant at 1:1.43Figure 7b reveals that all the water samples are located near or above the y = −x line, representing the occurrence of cation exchange (eq 4) in all cases. The observed result is consistent with that of onsite water quality tests, which indicate a gradual increase in the K+ and Na+ contents accompanied by a decrease in the Ca2+ and Mg2+ contents along the water flow direction. As depicted in Figure 7b, sample W8 and the experimental water sample lie the farthest from the y = −x line, indicating that cation exchange in these water samples is relatively strong.12

The molar ratio of HCO3– to Ca2+ can offer insights into the dissolution and cation exchange processes of carbonate and evaporite minerals. Several studies have demonstrated that under normal conditions, Ca2+, Mg2+, and HCO3– in groundwater primarily originate from the carbonate mineral’s dissolution.11,14,44 Typically, ρ(HCO3–)/ρ(Ca2+) = 2 implies that HCO3– and Ca2+ in water originate from the calcite dissolution. Alternatively, ρ(HCO3–)/ρ(Ca2+) = 4 indicates that HCO3– and Ca2+ result from the dissolution of dolomite.11,12 According to Figure 8c, the straight lines y = 2x and y = 4x categorized the entire plot into three regions: the upper region (above the line y = 4x), middle region (between the lines y = 2x and y = 4x), and lower region (below the line y = 2x). The lower region indicates a lack of HCO3–, and only samples W6 and W7 lie in this region (Figure 8c), indicating that Ca2+ and HCO3– in these two samples primarily originate from the calcite dissolution (eq 5). The other six samples lie in the middle region (Figure 8c). Based on the XRF and XRD results, we can conclude that Ca2+ and HCO3– in these samples primarily originate from the dissolution of both calcite and dolomite (eq 6) along with cation exchange processes.56Figure 8c indicates that SO42– originates from the dissolution of gypsum, with correlation coefficients close to 1.0 (Ca2+, R2 = 0.71; SO42–, R2 = 0.73).45 As shown in Figure 8d, the ρ(Ca2+ + Mg2+)/ρ(SO42–) value of samples W1, W7, F1, and F2 is close to 1, indicating that SO42– primarily originates from the dissolution of gypsum in these samples (eq 7). The presence of gypsum was also confirmed by the XRF and XRD detection results. The ρ(Ca2+ + Mg2+)/ρ(SO42–) values of the other water samples are below one, demonstrating that the source of SO42– in these samples is not only gypsum dissolution but also pyrite oxidation (eq 8), which increases the SO42– content in the water samples.11,12 This conclusion entirely corroborates the results of the XRF, XRD, and hydrochemical tests. We detected sufficient DO in the outflow and inflow water samples of the underground reservoir, which is essential for pyrite oxidation. Although Fe2+ and Fe3+ were not detected in this experiment, the increase in the SO42– concentration (Figure 11b) and the progressive decrease in pH (Figure 9) observed in the initial stages of the water–rock interaction experiment indicate the occurrence of pyrite oxidation.78

Variation Laws of Hydrochemical Indicators during the Water–Rock Interaction Experiment

pH and Salinity Variations

Figure 9 presents a curve depicting variations in the TDS concentration and pH value of the water samples over time. Evidently, according to variations in the TDS concentration, the water–rock interaction process may be roughly divided into three stages.

The first stage spans 0–12 h of the experiment, wherein the concentration of TDS in the mine water rapidly increases while the pH value rapidly decreases, both reaching their peak values at the 12th hour. This may be attributed to the presence of abundant nonclay minerals, such as feldspar, calcite, and pyrite, in the rocks, which initially dissolve and rapidly oxidize upon contact with the mine water. The first stage involves processes such as the dissolutions of rock salt, silicates, gypsum salt, and carbonates, as well as the oxidation of pyrite, leading to a rapid increase in the TDS concentration along with a rapid decrease in the pH value of the mine water.14,24 Thus, this stage is primarily associated with mineral dissolution and oxidation.

The second stage spans 12–480 h of the experiment, during which the TDS content of the mine water gradually decreases, and the pH value gradually increases, reaching a basic equilibrium state at the 480th hour. This phenomenon may be due to the weakening of pyrite oxidation, resulting in a slow increase in the pH of mine water. This may be attributed to the fact that clay minerals in the rocks have negatively charged surfaces that adsorb numerous cations (such as K+, Na+, Ca2+, and Mg2+) from the mine water, resulting in a reduction in the TDS concentration.24 Furthermore, some ions in the mine water may also undergo isomorphous substitutions with components in the rocks.46,47 Thus, this stage is predominantly related to cation exchange and adsorption processes.

The third stage spans 480–960 h of the experiment (end of the experiment), wherein the TDS concentration and pH value of the mine water do not considerably change and reach a stable equilibrium state. This stage may be attributed to the fact that after dissolution, oxidation, cation exchange, and adsorption, the intensity of water–rock interactions considerably weakens and reaches an equilibrium. At the end of the experiment, the mine water’s TDS concentration was slightly higher than at the beginning, possibly because the dissolution and oxidation processes dominated the cation exchange and adsorption processes during the experiment.

Anions

Figure 11 presents a curve depicting changes in the concentrations of primary anions (Cl–, SO42–, and HCO3–) in the mine water over time. In the early stages of the experiment, the Cl–, SO42–, and HCO3– concentrations rapidly increase, following which the rates of their increase reduce and gradually stabilize. Figure 11a shows that during 0–6 h of the experiment, the Cl– concentration rapidly increases, following which the rate of its increase gradually decreases, and the concentration stabilizes at the 480th hour, i.e., the end of the experiment. This may be because the solubility of rock salt present in the rocks is relatively high, leading to the rapid dissolution and depletion of rock salt during the early stages of the experiment. Owing to this, as the experiment progresses, the amount of dissolved rock salt decreases, causing a gradual increasing trend in the Cl– concentration of the mine water, followed by eventual stabilization.

Figure 11b reveals that during 0–2 h of the experiment, the SO42– concentration rapidly increases, following which the rate of its increase gradually decreases, and the concentration stabilizes at the 480th hour, i.e., the end of the experiment. This phenomenon may be attributed to pyrite oxidation and gypsum dissolution occurring during the early and middle experimental stages. As the experiment progresses, these processes gradually weaken, causing the gradual decrease and eventual stabilization of the mine water’s SO42– concentration.

Figure 11c further reveals that during 0–4 h of the experiment, the HCO3– concentration rapidly increases. Subsequently, the rate of its increase gradually decreases. From 120–480 h, the HCO3– concentration exhibits a gradual decrease, followed by stabilization until the end of the experiment. This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that mine water is weakly alkaline, leading to the dominance of HCO3–. In the early experimental stages, CO2 easily undergoes hydrolysis in water, causing a rapid increase in the mine water’s HCO3– concentration. Conversely, during the middle stages, as the HCO3– concentration gradually reaches saturation (Figure 11d) and reacts with Ca2+ to form precipitates (Figure 11d), the HCO3– concentration correspondingly decreases, stabilizing after 480 h.

Hydrogeochemical Evolution Model

Based on changes observed in the concentrations of TDSs and major ions as well as the pH value during the experiment, water–rock interactions occurring in the underground reservoir could be divided into two types: (1) dissolution and oxidation processes and (2) cation exchange and adsorption processes. Generally, collapsed rocks within underground reservoirs contained abundant soluble minerals, leading to the dominance of dissolution and oxidation processes during the early experimental stages (first stage). These processes involved the dissolutions of silicates, dolomite, rock salt, calcite, and gypsum, as well as the oxidation of pyrite (Figure 13), causing an overall increase in the major-ion concentration in the mine water (Figures 10 and 11). However, as the experiment progressed, the dissolution and oxidation processes gradually weakened. Generally, mudstone and sandstone contain abundant clay minerals (Figure 5), whose physicochemical properties favored cation exchange and adsorption with mine water, resulting in the dominance of these processes during the middle stage (second stage) of the experiment (Figure 13). The observed phenomena provided in a decrease in the Mg2+ and Ca2+ concentrations and an increase in the Na+ and K+ concentrations of the mine water (Figure 10). In the later stages (third stage) of the experiment, water–rock interactions become weak and the major ions concentrations in the mine water stabilized (Figures 10, 11, and 13). However, the TDS concentration associated with mine water at the end of the experiment was related to the intensity of the two water–rock interactions occurring during the experiment. If dissolution and oxidation processes dominate, the final TDS concentration of the mine water will be higher compared with original water sample. Conversely, if adsorption processes dominate, the final concentration of TDS in the mine water will be lower than that of the original water sample. Thus, the experimental outcomes show that to optimize water quality in underground reservoirs, the storage duration of water should be appropriate, that is, at least 480 h (20 days) or longer. However, whether longer storage durations will increase the concentrations of other ions, particularly heavy-metal ions, is currently unclear and warrants further in-depth research. Additionally, it is important to note that once these minerals achieve maximum adsorption capacities, their adsorption capacities significantly diminish.

Conclusions

The following conclusions were derived based on the investigation described above:(1)Onsite sampling and analysis revealed that along the inflow and outflow paths of the underground reservoirs of the Daliuta coal mine, Na+, and SO42– concentrations gradually increased while Mg2+ and Ca2+ concentrations gradually decreased. Moreover, the hydrochemical type of water changed from the Cl·HCO3–Na·Ca, Cl–Na·Ca·Mg, and Cl·SO4–Na types upon entering the reservoirs to the SO4·Cl–Na type upon exiting the reservoirs.(2)Water–rock interactions occurring along the inflow and outflow paths of the underground reservoirs primarily include the dissolution of rock salt, calcite, silicates, gypsum, and dolomite; oxidation of pyrite; exchange of cations; and adsorption of clay minerals.(3)Laboratory experiments revealed that the water–rock interactions in the underground reservoirs can be categorized into three stages: the first stage is dominated by dissolution and oxidation processes, leading to an overall increase in the major ion’s concentrations and TDSs as well as an overall decrease in the pH of mine water. The second stage was dominated by cation exchange and clay-mineral adsorption, leading to an increase in the K+ and Na+ concentrations and pH values as well as a decrease in the concentrations of Ca2+, TDS, and Mg2+ of the mine water. The third stage featured weak water–rock interactions, wherein the concentrations of major ions and TDSs, as well as the pH of mine water, stabilized.

The second stage was dominated by cation exchange and clay-mineral adsorption, leading to an increase in the K+ and Na+ concentrations and pH values as well as a decrease in the concentrations of Ca2+, TDS, and Mg2+ of the mine water. The third stage featured weak water–rock interactions, wherein the concentrations of major ions and TDSs, as well as the pH of mine water, stabilized.
