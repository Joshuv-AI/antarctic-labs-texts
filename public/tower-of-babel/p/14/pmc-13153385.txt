Intrinsic stabilization of synaptic plasticity improves learning and robustness in artificial neural networks
Authors: Artem Pilzak, Bobby Pennington, Jean-Philippe Thivierge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSynaptic plasticity, defined as the ability of connections between neurons to strengthen or weaken over time, is essential for learning, yet unregulated plasticity can lead to instability. Inspired by biological feedback mechanisms, we propose a framework that stabilizes synaptic changes in artificial neural networks through top-down signals derived from the network’s own output. The model, termed intrinsic Top-Down Stabilization (iTDS), augments traditional supervised learning by introducing a slower, top-down signal that tracks the network’s output over time and modulates synaptic updates. Instead of directly influencing sensory inputs, these top-down signals guide synaptic plasticity by gradually aligning with ongoing network activity. We demonstrate that this simple mechanism improves training efficiency, enhances generalization, and increases resilience to noise perturbations. The approach is evaluated across recurrent, feedforward, and reservoir networks on 16 synthetic and real-world tasks, including temporal signal encoding, synthetic data categorization, handwritten character recognition, and image classification. Our results clarify how network activity influences the alignment of top-down signals with supervised learning, providing testable predictions for how feedback projections can stabilize learning in biological networks.Subject terms: Learning algorithms, Dynamical systems

Introduction

Both neural and cognitive sciences acknowledge the role of top-down information during learning, reflecting context, sensory expectations, and beliefs about events in the environment1,2. In cortical circuits, the high density of top-down anatomical projections suggests a key role of these pathways in guiding selective attention, formulating goals, and disambiguating sensory inputs3–5. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) have been widely used to model neural dynamics across perceptual, cognitive, and motor domains, capturing essential features of learning and activity in biological circuits6–9. However, most learning in these models is implemented through supervised methods, such as Backpropagation through time10, FORCE learning8, and other gradient descent schemes11,12, which focus on reducing the discrepancy between externally provided targets and network outputs6,13,14. FORCE learning, for instance, aims to reduce the error between a desired target signal and the output of a recurrent network. These approaches are inherently bottom-up and typically do not incorporate modulatory effects of top-down feedback15–20.

Recently, efforts have sought to integrate top-down and bottom-up signals in artificial neural networks2,21–29. Yet, many of these approaches rely on modular architectures with separate compartments for feedforward and feedback processing, limiting their ability to support unified and dynamic learning.

A related line of work addresses synaptic stabilization, which describes the process where synaptic weights are preserved over time to maintain previously acquired knowledge30–33. In artificial systems, this is often achieved through regularization strategies such as quadratic penalty terms that constrain changes to strong weights25,34. While effective at mitigating forgetting, such stabilization techniques are typically passive and do not allow top-down signals to guide or evolve during learning. In biologically grounded models, synaptic stabilization can be achieved through homeostatic plasticity, where synaptic weights are adjusted to reach a target level of activity35,36. However, target levels of activity in homeostatic plasticity are determined by a fixed set point that is not dynamically modified to reflect network activity.

In this paper, we introduce a unified learning rule termed intrinsic Top-Down Stabilization (iTDS) that explicitly incorporates top-down feedback into the process of synaptic plasticity. Under iTDS, synaptic stabilization is not solely driven by error-based updates, but also by internally generated top-down signals that dynamically shape and maintain synaptic strengths throughout training. This allows top-down representations to evolve alongside bottom-up inputs, supporting both improved generalization and resistance to interference during learning.

iTDS integrates two complementary components. The first is a standard supervised term, which minimizes the discrepancy between network outputs and task-relevant targets. The second is a top-down component that compares the network output to a representation that is updated along a slow time course over training. In iTDS, top-down signals are intrinsic in the sense that they aim to approximate the network’s own output dynamics rather than encode sensory inputs. Top-down signals, however, have no direct access to the internal dynamics of the recurrent network.

In this way, iTDS functions as a plasticity rule in which a network learns regularities about itself, an idea supported by theories of internal modelling and predictive coding37. iTDS aligns with evidence that both bottom-up sensory encoding and top-down cortical modulation are modified by synaptic plasticity38,39. Importantly, top-down pathways are not fixed but evolve with learning, consistent with the adaptive nature of top-down signals.

By directly integrating supervised and top-down learning within a single rule that acts on individual synapses, iTDS offers a unified framework for exploring how these two components interact. Unlike previous approaches that treat them as separate mechanisms, iTDS allows supervised and top-down signals to either reinforce one another or exert competing influences, thereby revealing how their balance shapes learning.

Equipped with this plasticity rule, we investigated the extent to which iTDS can enhance traditional supervised learning in artificial neural networks. We trained reservoir, feedforward, and recurrent networks on a range of 16 tasks involving temporal signal encoding, pattern classification, and image recognition, and identified conditions under which iTDS improves performance, generalization, and robustness to noise.

Collectively, our findings demonstrate the computational benefits of a plasticity rule that integrates error-driven supervised learning with a top-down rule for synaptic stabilization. Despite the simplicity of the underlying mechanism, the benefits of iTDS are highly robust across tasks and network architectures, offering an analytically tractable and biologically inspired rule to stabilize synaptic modifications and yielding testable predictions about the neural basis of plasticity and feedback integration.

Results

Description of the recurrent neural network

The proposed model was based on an RNN reservoir framework where inputs are delivered to a recurrent network composed of mean-rate units that project to an output layer via trainable synapses (Fig. 1a). Extensions to feedforward networks trained with standard Backpropagation and RNNs trained with Backpropagation through time are presented in Supplementary Information.

Training a recurrent network using iTDS.

a Architecture of the recurrent network. An input is transmitted to a network that projects to an output layer via a set of adjustable connections (grey lines). Weights are trained based on a supervised signal representing a desired output as well as a top-down signal representing an approximation of the network output. b Schema of the two concomitant learning rules employed to train output weights of the recurrent network. Supervised learning aims to decrease the discrepancy between the desired target (in this case, a sine wave) and network output. iTDS adjusts the top-down signal to better match the network output. The top-down signal represents a moving average of the network output that influences the gradient of trainable weights.

In this approach, only the output weights of the RNN are adjusted8,40,41, in contrast to models where weights within the RNN are also subject to plasticity12. Computationally, restricting plasticity to the output weights of the network offers three main advantages. First, it circumvents the classic credit assignment problem associated with training connections located deep within the network42. Second, it avoids the need to formulate separate learning rules that train connections feeding into the recurrent network, within it, and projecting out from it12,43. Third, it is consistent with experimental evidence showing that not all synapses within a local circuit need to undergo plasticity when learning a task44,45.

The intent is for this framework to represent a generic, high-level mechanism that represents top-down signals conveyed by neural populations in higher neural centres. Biologically, this is consistent with evidence that downstream cortical populations integrate upstream activity over extended timescales, effectively computing a low-pass filtered representation of recent neural output. For instance, neurophysiological recordings have shown that neurons in higher-order cortical areas can maintain slowly evolving activity patterns that reflect the recent history of upstream population responses rather than instantaneous sensory input46. Such slow integration is attributed to a combination of recurrent circuitry, synaptic time constants, and cellular mechanisms that support persistent activity. In this context, Eq. (3) should be interpreted as a minimal, phenomenological description of how top-down populations can track ongoing network output on a slower timescale, rather than as a detailed biophysical model of the underlying circuitry.

A key feature of the above formulation is that top-down information does not aim to predict the input of the network or the target but rather focuses on approximating the output of the RNN itself. This approach differs from predictive coding models16,19,20, where layers are organized hierarchically, and early layers generate predictions about sensory input19,47–49. In iTDS, top-down signals strictly approximate the output of the RNN. A further distinction is that iTDS is not a prospective learning rule, in the sense that it does not attempt to predict future data points but rather focuses on building an accurate representation of the network output at each time step. Finally, predictive coding is often formulated using a free energy principle that is distinct from gradient-based approaches like the one presented here50.

Going further, a distinction should be made between iTDS and regularization by exponential moving average (EMA)51. In EMA, weights are averaged by combining multiple snapshots of the model’s synapses at different points during training. In contrast, what is averaged in iTDS is a smoothed version of the network output. In this way, iTDS influences the gradient at the output layer, rather than directly averaging weights across training steps.

Encoding temporal signals

In a first set of tasks, an RNN was trained to encode continuous time-varying inputs6,8,9,52,53. In this scenario, the network learned to linearly decode the output signal produced by the recurrent units using the iTDS plasticity rule (Eq. (4)).

In one series of simulations, an RNN was trained to trace a two-dimensional target pattern analogous to a center-out reaching task (Fig. 2a, top)54. After training, a perturbation was introduced by rotating the target clockwise (Fig. 2a, middle). The network adapted to this perturbation after the output weights were retrained (Fig. 2a, bottom). To examine the impact of iTDS, we varied the learning parameter σ, which controls the strength of top-down stabilization (Eq. (4)). Both initial training error and retraining error (measured as supervised loss, “Methods”, Eq. (16)) attained a lower asymptotic value with σ = 1 (iTDS) compared to σ = 0 (no iTDS), showing that iTDS outperformed an RNN trained with supervised learning alone (Fig. 2b). By training networks on a range of σ values, we found that σ~1 yielded optimal performance (Fig. 2c).

Retraining the output weights allowed the network to adapt to the perturbed target (bottom). b Supervised loss is lowest for an intermediate value of intrinsic top-down stabilization (σ = 1) during both the initial learning and retraining after perturbation. Training steps are dimensionless and reflect the number of discrete training iterations where weights are updated. c Performance across a range of σ values controlling the strength of intrinsic stabilization. d Lower values of time constant for intrinsic stabilization (τtd) are associated with lower supervised loss.

Next, we examined the role of the top-down integration time constant (τtd) (Eq. (3)). We fixed the integration time constant of RNN activity at τ = 0.01 (see “Methods”, Eq. (13)) and found that optimal values of top-down integration were such that τtd≪τ (Fig. 2d). Hence, the time-course of top-down integration must be set much lower than the time-course of recurrent units, suggesting that a slow evolution of top-down representations is a key component of stabilized learning. A formal analysis of the role of τtd is presented in Supplementary Information.

To examine whether these results generalized to various temporal signals employed as target, we performed simulations where an RNN encoded a 10 Hz sine wave, a series of square pulses, and a Lorenz chaotic time series6,8,9,52,53. RNNs trained with iTDS were able to reproduce these signals (Fig. 3a). Focusing on the sine wave as an example, the network gradually reduced both supervised and top-down error over the course of training (Fig. 3b). During training, the network produced a sine wave that matched the desired target within less than a full cycle, which is typical of well-performing RNNs (Fig. 3c). Networks starting from different initial conditions all converged to the target signal. Increasing the number of recurrent units sped up training (i.e., faster convergence of the loss function) but yielded a similar asymptotic performance (Fig. 3d). This speed-up was present even in the initial stages of learning (training step of 1). The optimal time-course of top-down integration was robust to alterations in the frequency of the target signal as well as additive noise (Suppl. Fig. S1). Replacing the top-down function ftd (Eq. (3)) with alternative functions worsened performance in some cases and yielded marginal improvements in performance in other cases (Suppl. Fig. S2). The ability of RNNs to replay learned temporal sequences is examined in Supplementary Information (Suppl. Fig. S3), showing an advantage of iTDS over supervised learning.

A comparison of supervised learning alone (σ = 0) versus iTDS (σ = 1) revealed faster training with the latter (Fig. 3e). Further, across all tasks, training error was lower when σ > 0, suggesting a performance advantage of iTDS (Fig. 3f). This advantage was also observed in simulations where Gaussian noise was added to the target function (Fig. 3g). An intuitive explanation for this boost in performance is that, under certain conditions, top-down error (Eq. (2)) tracks supervised error (Eq. (1)). Because both expressions have a common term yt corresponding to the network output, the supervised and top-down error will be correlated when this term exhibits high variance. We term this effect gradient matching, referring to the idea that the top-down gradient of iTDS will closely track supervised learning when the network output has high variance.

Hence, the covariance between the supervised and learning components is proportional to the variance of the network output. This effect is independent of the variance of the top-down (y*t) and target signal (zt). Hence, supervised learning will benefit from iTDS under conditions of high output variance. To be sure, the gradient matching effect depends on supervised and top-down components having the same sign, thus preventing them from canceling out. Details of the covariance between top-down and supervised error is provided in Supplementary Information.

Interestingly, increased variance in neural activity is a signature of plasticity55 and is widely reported in the brain56–59, suggesting a link between neuronal variance and learning. As learning stabilizes, neural activity reduces in variance60, suggesting that high variance may play a more significant role in the initial stages of learning. This idea is supported in simulations of the RNN, where the variance of the network output over repeated cycles of the target sine wave was high during the initial stages of learning but reduced as training progressed (Fig. 3h). This result was observed across various temporal sequences, where top-down error decreased alongside output variance (Supp. Fig. S4), except for a more complex signal involving a Lorenz attractor, where top-down error and output variance increased initially before rapidly decreasing.

Furthermore, the time-course of gradient matching, reflecting changes in the correlation between supervised and top-down learning, varied over the course of learning. The alignment between supervised and top-down errors is not a constant bias but rather a learning-dependent effect. The correlation is initially high because both errors are dominated by the high variance of the network output. As learning progresses and output variance decreases, the correlation between the two errors gradually diminishes, approaching zero at the asymptotic error (Suppl. Fig. S5a).

We note that the independence assumption used in Eqs. (6 and 7) is intended to apply to the early stages of learning. At initialization, the network output y, the top-down signal y*, and the target z are all randomly initialized and weakly correlated, such that treating y and y* as approximately independent provides a reasonable first-order approximation. As learning progresses, y* becomes a filtered copy of y (Eq. (3)), and this independence assumption no longer holds. Accordingly, the covariance derivation captures the initial-phase regime in which gradient matching is strongest, consistent with the observed decay of gradient alignment as output variance decreases over training (Suppl. Fig. S5a).

The gradient-matching effect does not exhibit a sharp threshold in output variance beyond which iTDS suddenly becomes effective. Instead, simulations and formal analyses show a graded relationship. As the variance of the network output increases, the correlation between supervised and top-down gradients increases monotonically (Suppl. Fig. S5a). During training, the output variance decreases smoothly from values close to unity at initialization to near zero at convergence while the correlation between supervised and top-down gradients decreases in parallel, from approximately 0.5 in early learning to near zero at asymptotic performance (Suppl. Fig. S5b). In turn, this leads to a near-linear relation between the output variance and supervised error (Suppl. Fig. S5c). Thus, gradient matching emerges continuously and is strongest during early learning phases when output variability dominates both error terms. The time-course of gradient matching suggests a mechanism by which networks may regulate the relative influence of top-down signals, which are strongly aligned early in learning, and progressively less as performance stabilizes.

The optimal value of top-down strength (σ) varies across datasets because the balance between the feedforward loss and the top-down regularization depends on the structure of the data. Because datasets differ in class separability, noise levels, and intra-class variability, the degree to which top-down consistency aids (or interferes with) learning is not constant. In datasets with clearer boundaries, a smaller σ may suffice, while in noisier or more overlapping datasets, a larger σ may be needed.

Comparison between iTDS and FORCE learning

In the popular FORCE learning scheme, the recurrent network is in a chaotic state8. Given that FORCE is a widely employed and a powerful learning rule to train RNNs, we aimed to compare it to iTDS. In default simulations of iTDS, the RNN receives an external input that suppresses chaotic dynamics (Fig. 1a). To examine the ability of iTDS to learn temporal sequences with chaotic activity, the following modifications were performed. First, we removed the external input to the reservoir so that the network was initialized to a chaotic state. Second, we decreased the global learning rate ζ (Eq. (5)). This strategy departs from FORCE, where the learning rate is set higher at first and decreases gradually as training progresses to prevent runaway synaptic weights. With these two modifications, the RNN produced chaotic activity (Fig. 4a), and iTDS learned to closely match the target signal (Fig. 4b). In repeated simulations with frozen initial conditions, increasing the strength of top-down stabilization from a lower value (σ = 0) to a higher value (σ = 1) reduced learning error, although a similar asymptotic value was attained (Fig. 4c). Increasing σ beyond this value resulted in synaptic instability where weights grew unbounded.

Results of iTDS were also compared to predictive alignment, a supervised rule that learns from chaotic recurrent activity21. Parameters were kept as close to iTDS simulations as possible and set to the default values from the original work. Different simulations manipulated the regularization strength of predictive alignment (α) to gauge its impact on training accuracy. Performance with predictive alignment fluctuated over time and did not attain the same degree of convergence as iTDS regardless of the regularization strength (Fig. 4d).

Further simulations examined the robustness of iTDS to alterations in parameters, including supervised learning rate (η), frequency of the target sine wave, time-course of top-down integration (τtd), and recurrent network gain (g). Performance was sensitive to learning rate and network gain but remained largely unaltered by changes in τtd and g (Fig. 4e). Finally, a total of 100 independent simulations with different initial conditions showed that 91% of networks with iTDS outperformed the baseline model (σ = 0) (Fig. 4f).

In sum, the benefits of iTDS extended to a scenario where the reservoir network generated chaotic activity comparable to FORCE learning, but without requiring an adaptive learning rate set to a high initial value that is gradually decreased over the course of training. Rather, synaptic stabilization occurred via a top-down signal that dynamically modulated supervised learning.

Of course, increasing the speed of learning and improving training accuracy could be achieved in RNNs by various means other than iTDS, for instance by applying regularization to the weight adjustment scheme61. However, as examined next, the advantages of iTDS extend beyond improvements in training speed and accuracy and include benefits such as robustness to noise and perturbations to the training data.

Contribution of top-down stabilization to pattern classification

While RNNs have been predominantly employed for tasks involving temporal signals, these models also offer robust solutions to classification tasks62–74. We therefore considered whether iTDS could serve to improve the performance of RNNs on tasks involving the classification of inputs into discrete categories.

A series of tasks were designed where a single input pattern was delivered at each time-step and paired with a target indicative of category membership. Following a batch approach, weight updates were performed once per iteration, where an iteration corresponds to the presentation of all training patterns. For simplification, the mean-rate recurrent units (“Methods”, Eq. (13)) were replaced with hypertan units (“Methods”, Eq. (14)). In a first task, the network was trained on a two-bit parity problem, chosen because it is among the simplest nonlinear classification problems. The initial performance was at chance level and good classification was attained after training the network for 100 iterations (Fig. 5a). Training error was dependent upon the strength of top-down stabilization (σ) (Fig. 5b, top left). Increasing σ yielded faster learning, but as σ approached a value of 2 (and beyond), performance became unstable, and error began to oscillate instead of reaching an asymptotic value. Following training, testing accuracy was assessed by freezing the output weights and presenting the model with training patterns that were distorted by zero-mean Gaussian noise (Fig. 5b, top right). Increasing the standard deviation of the noise yielded increased testing error. Overall, testing error was lowest for a moderate strength of top-down stabilization (σ = 1). The worst performance was obtained without iTDS (σ = 0).

Another factor influencing performance was the time-course of top-down decay (τtd, Eq. (3)) (Fig. 5b bottom left and right panels). A lower value of τtd (0.001) yielded lower training and testing error compared to higher values (0.01 and 0.1). Hence, a lower top-down decay rate conveyed performance advantages. However, lower values of τtd were associated with an under-damped oscillatory regime of synaptic weights during training, where weights fluctuate around an equilibrium without settling to fixed values (see Supplementary Information). This indicates a trade-off between keeping top-down decay low to improve performance, yet high enough to prevent synaptic instability.

Thus, both the strength (σ) and time-course (τtd) of iTDS contribute to synaptic stabilization during learning. Depending upon the values of these parameters, a formal analysis revealed three distinct regimes of learning under iTDS, namely (i) an underdamped regime leading to synaptic oscillations; (ii) a critically damped regime leading to fast, non-oscillatory convergence; and (iii) an overdamped regime with slow, monotonic convergence (see Supplementary Information). Without iTDS (σ = 0), learning follows a degenerate case at a boundary below the under-damped regime where synaptic convergence is dominated by the supervised rule alone.

Expanding to a more complex classification task, an RNN was trained to classify the MNIST dataset, composed of 10,000 hand-written characters (Fig. 6a). Each 28 × 28 image was flattened into a 784-dimensional input vector. These vectors were projected to the recurrent network through the input weight matrix. The inclusion of iTDS (σ = 1) improved learning speed, training accuracy, and generalization performance to novel exemplars using ten-fold cross-validation (Fig. 6b). Further increasing the strength of iTDS to σ = 1.5 resulted in a marked speed-up in performance but came at the cost of worst generalization. The time-course of top-down integration impacted performance, with values near τtd = 0.005 leading to minimal loss (Fig. 6c) and optimal generalization accuracy (Fig. 6d).

The out-of-distribution performance of iTDS was assessed by rotating the testing exemplars using a rotation angle θ relative to the original axis. Examples of test patterns are shown in Fig. 6e. While classification accuracy was low on these patterns, iTDS provided a slight advantage over standard supervised learning (Fig. 6f). In sum, iTDS improved the performance of RNNs trained on synthetic and real-world categorization tasks compared to a model trained with supervised learning alone.

We examined the computational consequences of this scheme and compared it to a mixed model where the same error signal encodes both positive and negative error. A network was trained on the two-bit parity problem described earlier and a sample of output weights were plotted at different time-points during training (Fig. 7a). Under normal training regimes, however, the right-hand term dominates as supervised error converges, preventing sign reversal (Fig. 7a). Moreover, the proportion of sign reversals during learning was inversely related to the percentage of output weights configured to be sign-specific (Fig. 7b).

During training, the sign-specific model resulted in lower error than the mixed model (Fig. 7c). This result was further confirmed across tasks involving different target signals, where error decreased in proportion to the percentage of sign-specific synapses (Suppl. Fig. S6). We hypothesized that a reason for this effect is that sign-specific error yields sparser output weights. Indeed, about half of weights remained at zero during training due to the linear rectification applied to the top-down error signal (Eq. (8)). Setting these weights to zero is equivalent to weight pruning, which is a well-known form of regularization in artificial neural networks79 and has been employed in reservoir computing to improve generalization80,81.

To examine whether sparse connectivity accounted for lower error in the sign-specific model, we trained a mixed model where half of randomly chosen weights were clamped at zero during learning. In this scenario, termed sparse-mixed, the performance became comparable to that of a network with sign-specific error (Fig. 7c). Importantly, the sparse-mixed control addresses whether sparsity alone accounts for the performance gains observed with sign-specific error signals. By randomly clamping the same proportion of output weights to zero, this model isolates the effect of sparsity. The comparable performance of the sparse-mixed and sign-specific models demonstrates that the main computational advantage arises from sparsity, while sign-specific error provides a mechanistic and biologically grounded means of inducing this sparsity through learning.

Going further, we trained a range of models where the proportion of sign-specific synapses was adjusted and found that increasing sign-specific synapses resulted in lower error (Fig. 7d). Hence, sparse connectivity accounted for the computational advantage associated with sign-specific top-down error. As such, sparse connectivity may be viewed as either an explanation for the role of specialized top-down error, or an alternative mechanism that yields similar computational benefits.

Robustness to counter-trials

Next, we speculated that top-down signals may provide robustness to noisy or contradictory training data during the learning process. As an analogy, an expert golfer may benefit from top-down knowledge that allows her to perform well despite adverse conditions such as wind or rain. Similarly, a person who is proficient in a certain language can follow a conversation over a noisy phone line.

To evaluate the ability of RNNs to perform accurate classification despite contradictory data, we introduced a proportion of counter-trials (5% or 10% of the training data) where inputs were deliberately assigned to the wrong class (Fig. 8a). Compared to networks trained with purely supervised learning, classification error was consistently lower with iTDS for both a two-bit parity (Fig. 8b) and a two-dimensional spiral task (Fig. 8(c)). Overall, increasing the strength of iTDS across independent runs of the model decreased classification error (Fig. 8d, e). Thus, top-down stabilization conferred robustness to contradictory trials. This effect was further examined in the MNIST dataset with 10% counter-trials, showing that testing accuracy increased with σ while τtd did not significantly impact classification performance within the range of values considered (Suppl. Fig. S7). These results are comparable with related approaches such as EMA and predictive coding in terms of both classification accuracy and estimated CPU time (see Suppl Mat. With supervised learning alone, error rose over training iterations, indicative of the inability to learn when the dataset contained counter-trials. A formal analysis shows that robustness to counter-trials requires that the top-down strength (σ) matches or exceeds the supervised learning rate η (see Supplementary Information). If σ = 0 (supervised learning alone), this condition is not met and the top-down term cannot outweigh the supervised term when a counter-trial is encountered, so weight updates to counter-trials are not reduced relative to regular trials. With iTDS, error decreased over the course of training when the proportion of counter-trials was low (5%). Error increased with a higher proportion of counter-trials (10%) but remained below that of supervised learning alone.

To explore the mechanism that endows iTDS with robustness to noisy data, we considered the possibility that iTDS dampens the magnitude of synaptic modification during counter-trials. This effect can be seen by considering the model’s combined learning rule (Eq. (4)). As a simple illustration, we examined what happens in a model trained on the two-bit parity problem when a counter-trial was introduced at training iteration t = 50, where an input pattern was assigned to the wrong label (Fig. 9a). Supervised error rose sharply during this counter-trial. At the same time, top-down error also rose because the output of the model yt no longer matched the top-down signal y*t. Because the supervised component (ηesuptrt) has opposite sign as the top-down component (−σetdtrt), they will partially cancel out, thus reducing the error gradient (ΔWout) and preventing the model from making large synaptic changes when top-down error is high on a given trial. This mechanism conveys robustness against the occasional counter-trial (Fig. 9b).

This effect can be illustrated by plotting a sample of synaptic weights during training, showing large fluctuations during a counter-trial (Fig. 9c). Increasing the absolute strength of iTDS (σ) decreases the supervised loss resulting from the counter-trial (Fig. 9d) and the mean change in synaptic weights during the counter-trial (Fig. 9e). Thus, iTDS confers robustness to counter-trials by dampening the magnitude of change in synaptic weights resulting from these trials. In this way, iTDS acts as a form of synaptic stabilization by preventing weights from being overly sensitive to counter-trials. However, if an over-representation of counter-trials was introduced, they would eventually become absorbed in the top-down signal y*t) at a rate dictated in part by τtd (Eq. (3)) (see Supplementary Information for formal analysis).

Hence, depending on the goal of the model, robustness to counter-trials could be viewed as either an advantage or an inconvenience. If the goal is to extract regularities in the training data, it would make sense to ignore counter-trials. However, if the goal is to detect novel or contradictory inputs82, a lower strength of iTDS may be desirable.

Robustness to noise perturbations

The above results were extended by examining the robustness of iTDS to iterative noise perturbation trials, defined as minimal perturbations to inputs that cause exemplars to be misclassified by the network83,84. To perturb exemplars with noise, a network was first trained on a given task until performance reached an asymptotic value (<5% improvement in performance over consecutive training iterations). Then, the output weights were frozen, and the network was prompted to classify a series of test patterns. A small amount of zero-mean Gaussian noise was gradually added to these patterns until the network no longer assigned them to the correct category. This process is illustrated in Fig. 10a. On the two-bit parity task, iTDS required a greater number of iterations before classification was altered when compared to a purely supervised learning rule (Fig. 10b), indicating an advantage of iTDS in robustness to noise perturbed exemplars. Over independent runs of the model with randomly generated input patterns that uniformly filled the four quadrants of the input space, iTDS consistently required a greater number of iterations to alter classification (Fig. 10c).

In a first scenario (labelled 1), the perturbed data point was outside of the class boundaries employed during training, leading to a problem of extrapolation that is challenging for artificial neural networks85. In the second scenario (2), the perturbed data point crossed category boundaries, leading to misclassification.

Comparable results were obtained with the MNIST dataset, where iTDS exhibited increased robustness to noise perturbed exemplars (Fig. 10e, f). As an illustration, examples of misclassified inputs show instances where a handwritten 0 was classified as an 8, and a 1 was classified as a 3 (Fig. 10g). Robustness to random perturbations is below human performance, given that the noisy 0 and 1 can still be easily recognized as such, suggesting room for further improvements to the model. Nonetheless, iTDS is a step in the right direction, providing improved resilience to noise perturbed data compared to supervised learning alone.

Discussion

This work proposed a plasticity rule for training artificial neural networks where supervised learning and top-down stabilization were combined to adjust synaptic weights. The aim of iTDS is to gradually build a top-down approximation of the network’s output and leverage that information during the learning process. In this way, the network learned not only to approximate a desired target, but also to improve its ability to generate an accurate representation of its own output. This form of synaptic stabilization regulates the magnitude of changes in weights, preventing synapses from oscillating around an asymptotic value and dampening alterations when presented with contradictory input.

Three main findings emerged from this approach. First, top-down stabilization improved learning speeds and testing accuracy across a broad range of tasks involving categorization and temporal signal encoding. These improvements were explained by a gradient matching effect where the gradient of the top-down stabilization rule aligned with the gradient of the supervised rule, an effect that depends upon the variance of network activity. Second, we found computational advantages when designing individual neurons that were specialized in tracking either positive or negative top-down error. These advantages were driven by the fact that error specialization resulted in sparser connectivity. Third, top-down stabilization increased robustness to noise perturbed exemplars by dampening synaptic alterations caused by highly unpredictable data. Together, these findings highlight the role of top-down stabilization in neural circuits and suggest that synaptic alterations during learning may arise under the confluence of different rules acting upon the same neuronal pathways.

Model predictions

Five predictions relevant to neuroscience emerged from the proposed approach:

(1) iTDS suggests an influence of combined supervised learning and top-down stabilization rules at individual synapses. In this way, both rules may affect plasticity while acting on distinct timescales. These rules may, under certain circumstances, act synergistically to promote learning. Two signatures of top-down plasticity could be tracked experimentally. First, deactivating pathways related to top-down modulation (equivalent to setting σ = 0 in the model) would result in a degradation of learning performance. Second, iTDS generates a sharp initial rise in training error before it declines (see Suppl. Fig. S8c), due to top-down error being initially high. Hence, a testable signature of iTDS is a non-monotonic error trajectory during early learning stages. Specifically, the model predicts an initial transient increase in error driven by the mismatch between top-down signals and network output, followed by a rapid decline as these signals become more aligned. This early overshoot in error is absent when top-down stabilization is removed (σ = 0). Experimentally, this predicts that intact top-down pathways should produce a brief elevation in behavioral or neural error signals at the onset of learning, whereas a disruption in feedback should eliminate this transient and yield a more monotonic but slower learning curve.

(2) Based on the gradient matching effect introduced here, top-down stabilization will yield a marked impact when recurrent networks have high variability in their output activity. In other words, the benefits of incorporating top-down signals during learning may be greater when neural variability is elevated. To test this prediction experimentally, one could use optogenetic or chemogenetic approaches to selectively increase or decrease the variance of neural activity in a targeted population and then assess the effect on learning performance or network stability86. For instance, one could use patterned optogenetic stimulation to transiently enhance trial-to-trial fluctuations in spiking activity, or conversely, recruit inhibitory interneurons to suppress variability. Behavioural readouts, such as task accuracy or learning rate, could then be measured in parallel with electrophysiological recordings to quantify the impact of altered variability on network function. Notably, there is a possible threshold beyond which excessive variability impairs learning, raising the intriguing question of whether this critical point can be quantitatively mapped in a controlled experimental setting.

(3) Specialized synapses that track positive and negative error signals lead to sparse synaptic representations that benefit learning by providing a form of weight regularization. This prediction could be tested by training animals on a learning task while performing two-photon imaging of dendritic spines to track sign-specific synaptic changes87. Positive and negative error signals could be delivered via optogenetic activation/inactivation of dopaminergic projections from the ventral tegmental area to mimic positive reward prediction errors, or inhibition/activation of noradrenergic or serotonergic pathways to model negative error signals88. If specialized synapses indeed track positive and negative errors, learning should produce a more skewed (sparser) distribution of strong synapses under normal error signalling. Conversely, disrupting sign-specific plasticity (for example, by selectively blocking neuromodulatory pathways that convey positive or negative error) should reduce sparsity and worsen generalization. This protocol would provide a direct link between sign-selective synaptic updates, sparse weight representations, and improved learning.

(4) Top-down stabilization confers robustness to noise by dampening synaptic changes to novel and unexpected training exemplars. This prediction could be evaluated by training animals on a discrimination task in which expected and unexpected exemplars can be systematically manipulated. After establishing a stable category boundary, rare outliers could be introduced to probe the impact of novelty on synaptic plasticity. Two-photon imaging in relevant cortical areas would allow measurement of synaptic weight changes following both expected and unexpected trials. To directly assess the role of top-down stabilization, one could manipulate higher-order cortical feedback (using optogenetic silencing of top-down projections) and compare how strongly synapses are updated by novel exemplars under these conditions. Intact top-down stabilization should suppress excessive synaptic modifications to outliers, thereby conferring robustness to noise.

(5) Top-down signals suppress synaptic changes during misleading trials. In contexts where sensory inputs are ambiguous or inconsistent with prior expectations, top-down signals can serve to inhibit inappropriate synaptic updates, effectively buffering the network against spurious plasticity. To test this prediction, one could train animals on a task with occasional misleading trials while performing two-photon imaging of dendritic spines. To test the causal role of top-down signals, feedback from higher-order cortex to sensory areas could be selectively perturbed by optogenetic silencing of prefrontal or secondary sensory projections on a subset of misleading trials, followed by a comparison of synaptic updates rates on those trials to unperturbed trials. A key prediction of iTDS is that suppressing synaptic updates during misleading trials will have a stronger impact during early learning, when top-down error is high. As learning progresses and top-down representations stabilize, this effect should diminish. Thus, selectively perturbing top-down feedback during early versus late training will yield an asymmetric effect, where early disruptions abolish the attenuation of plasticity on misleading trials, and late disruptions have a substantially weaker effect.

Together, these predictions form hypotheses on the combined role of supervised learning and top-down stabilization that could be targeted by experiments. Moving forward, iTDS could also be employed to study disorders of perception, including visual and auditory hallucinations that may be caused by the mismatch between sensory stimuli and their internal representation89.

Implications for computational models

This work provides two key contributions aimed at accelerating the learning times and improving the performance of artificial neural networks. First, the idea of incorporating top-down information into artificial networks is rapidly gaining interest2,90. This information can take several forms, including context, beliefs, expectations, and episodic memory. Not only can top-down signals speed-up learning compared to networks trained de novo, but these signals may also help overcome biases in the training data and selectively direct the learning process towards inputs that are more surprising and therefore least understood. In the iTDS learning rule, top-down signals serve to stabilize synaptic updates by directly interacting with bottom-up inputs. In this way, iTDS shapes learning within the same set of synaptic connections influenced by supervised learning, without the need for separate modules that require a careful hand-coded design.

The idea of combining two distinct plasticity rules in a unified circuit has been explored in related contexts, for instance examining the interplay between spike-timing dependent and homeostatic plasticity91 and the interaction between Hebbian learning and predictive coding92. However, the combination of supervised and top-down stabilizing signals in recurrent networks has received scant attention, thus prompting the development of a plasticity rule that combines these two elements.

A second contribution of the model is that iTDS combines slow and fast learning, an idea made popular several decades ago by economist and psychologist Daniel Kahneman93 and serving as the basis of recent learning algorithms94–96. In broad strokes, applying this idea to machine learning involves the design of two systems, namely a faster system that performs rapid learning, sometimes based on a one- or few-shot learning52,97, and a slower system involving more careful deliberation. The combination of these two systems provides not only a fruitful parallel to cognitive and neuroscientific findings98,99 but also yields computational advantages in learning speeds and accuracy. Indeed, results of iTDS show the benefits of slower top-down stabilization combined with faster bottom-up plasticity. Going beyond these two systems, artificial networks may benefit from learning rules that unfold over a broad range of timescales, in agreement with biological circuits100–102.

Related approaches

Incorporating top-down information into artificial neural networks has been explored in related contexts2,90. However, despite attempts at incorporating top-down learning in artificial neural networks103–105, no computational framework has been proposed to allow for a direct trade-off between supervised learning and top-down stabilization in RNNs. Below are some related models that perform top-down learning:

Impression learning106: This model features a top-down generative network that employs Bayesian learning. While a Bayesian framework provides a powerful means of training neural networks107, it has been difficult to translate into biological terms108. By contrast, iTDS is derived from error-based gradient descent, making it seamlessly compatible with classic supervised learning and providing more direct links to the biological bases of learning.

Predictive alignment21: Instead of aiming to directly minimize output error, predictive alignment tries to suppress chaos by aligning recurrent predictions with chaotic activity. The innovation of predictive alignment lies in its ability to perform online and local supervised learning through predictions. However, this approach requires that weights within the reservoir are trained using a separate plasticity rule. As such, it provides no explicit trade-off between bottom-up and top-down learning.

Slow feature encoding109,110: This approach aims to extract aspects of an input signal that vary slowly and therefore form invariant features. A parallel can be drawn between this approach and iTDS, where adjusting the top-down temporal decay can lead to slower synaptic updates, and therefore more invariance to changes in stimulus features. However, while a smaller temporal decay is associated with increased training accuracy, it may also result in synaptic instability, suggesting a potential trade-off between learning and stability.

Deep recurrent network with top-down attention2: This network architecture combines bottom-up and top-down signals using an attention mechanism. The model requires a separate explicit attention module as well as recurrence between neighbouring layers. By comparison, iTDS does not require a laminar organization with recurrent connections between layers or a separate attention mechanism.

Deep Boltzmann machines111: This model features top-down and bottom-up interactions that are adjusted through an energy function, as opposed to the error-based gradient employed in iTDS.

Helmholtz machine112: This model contains a generative and a discriminative network, where the generative module can be conceived as a top-down pathway. Helmholtz machines are an early precursor to modern generative adversarial networks, where the goal is to draw novel samples from a learned distribution113.

A few studies have aimed to combine traditional supervised learning with predictive coding28,43,76,77. In all instances, however, the supervised rule remained entirely distinct from the predictive component, given that the aim of these models was not to propose a unified rule based on the interaction between these two aspects of learning. By contrast, iTDS enables a direct trade-off between bottom-up and top-down components of learning within a unified synaptic rule.

A fruitful direction for future research would be to compare the performance of iTDS to the above approaches using standardized dataset benchmarks. Other approaches could also be added to this comparison, including regularized learning25,34 and homeostatic plasticity35,36. These efforts, however, go beyond our present contribution of showing the generalized advantages of iTDS across reservoir RNNs, feedforward Backpropagation networks, and RNNs trained with Backpropagation through time, across both signal processing and categorization tasks.

In sum, a learning rule for synaptic plasticity that combines supervised learning and top-down stabilization was shown to improve the performance of artificial neural networks trained on a variety of signal processing and classification tasks. This framework suggests the existence of brain mechanisms that serve to flexibly combine and adapt a variety of learning requisites over a range of timescales to promote synaptic stability, improve learning accuracy, and provide robustness to noise perturbed inputs.
