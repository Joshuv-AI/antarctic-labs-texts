Innovative stimulated muscle contraction signals based digital muscle marker: a reliable tool for assessing muscle in persons with stroke
Authors: Mi-Jeong Yoon, Kwangsub Song, Yerim Ji, Sangui Choi, Hooman Lee, Ji Yoon Jung, Seungyup Song, Jae Yi Kim, Miji Kim, Chang Won Won, Ilsoo Kim, Sun Im
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke leads to motor dysfunction as a result of damage to the central nervous system, often resulting in secondary muscle changes from disuse and immobilization. Existing tools for assessing these muscle changes in persons with stroke are limited, as these methods rely on voluntary muscle contractions, which are frequently impaired in hemiparesis. Additionally, these assessments often require complex and cumbersome equipment. Therefore, a novel system that can address these issues, with high levels of reliability is needed. This study introduces and evaluates the reliability levels of a novel technique using stimulated muscle contraction signals (SMCS)-based digital muscle markers.MethodsSMCS-based digital muscle markers were measured on both thighs of participants with hemiplegic stroke (n = 40). Three trials were conducted by two examiners using the exoPill device, which captures muscle contraction signals induced by electrical stimulation. Isometric knee extensor strength and hand grip strength were measured, and appendicular lean mass was estimated using bioelectrical impedance analysis. The intra- and inter-rater reliability of SMCS-based digital muscle marker measurements was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), coefficient of variance (CV), and 95% limits of agreement (LOA).ResultsThe ICC for both intra- and inter-rater reliability of SMCS-based digital muscle marker measurements exceeded 0.98 on both the affected and unaffected sides. The CV was consistently low, ranging from 1.36% to 3.07% in the unaffected leg, and remained under 3% in the affected leg. Bland-Altman plots confirmed consistent agreement, with most measurements falling within the 95% LOA and no systematic bias were observed. Mean differences for intra- and inter-rater assessments ranged from − 0.43 to 0.25 for the unaffected leg and from 0.00 to 0.05 for the affected leg. Spearman correlation coefficient showed moderate to good correlation (ρ = 0.50) between SMCS-based digital muscle markers and knee extensor strength for both unaffected and affected leg, and good to excellent correlations (ρ = 0.87) between SMCS-based digital muscle markers and leg lean mass.ConclusionsSMCS-based digital muscle markers demonstrated high reliability and significant correlations with muscle strength and mass, suggesting their potential utility for assessing muscle in individuals with stroke.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-025-01773-y.Keywords: Stroke, Muscle, Reliability, Validity, Marker, Surface electromyography

Introduction

Stroke is one of the leading causes of death and long-term disability worldwide. Motor function deficits after a stroke make it challenging to achieve mobility, perform daily activities, and engage in social participation. These impairments result from damage to the central nervous system and are primarily neurogenic. However, secondary changes also occur in the skeletal muscles, the main effector organs, after a stroke [1]. Loss of muscle mass, muscle weakness, and increased intramuscular fat deposition occur in both paretic and non-paretic limbs of stroke survivors [2–4].

These muscle changes are considered to result from a complex interplay of factors, including impaired neural control, motor neuron loss, altered muscle metabolism, inflammatory responses, and malnutrition [5]. Muscle wasting in persons with stroke negatively impacts their functional recovery, making timely and effective interventions crucial [6–8]. Consequently, regular assessment of muscle condition is essential. However, current tools for assessing strength or muscle function often rely on voluntary movement, posing challenges in accurately diagnosing muscle status in stroke survivors with motor impairment [9]. Additionally, when measuring muscle mass using equipment such as computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance image (MRI), bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), persons with stroke may have difficulty moving to the testing equipment or maintaining the required posture [10]. Therefore, a reliable method is needed that can easily and accurately assess muscle condition at any time, in any location, and regardless of the subject’s mobility.

In this context, wearable technologies that integrate various sensors with advanced digital data processing capabilities represent a promising solution for assessing muscle condition. These systems are easy to implement, highly portable, and capable of continuously capturing human activity in both clinical and real-world settings. Beyond their role in fitness tracking, wearable devices have demonstrated broad clinical utility, including real-time joint angle measurement [11], pathological gait analysis [12], and the monitoring of fetal movements in pregnant women [13].

To enable muscle assessment using wearable systems, we focused on signals emitted during muscle contractions induced by electrical stimulation, referred to as Stimulated Muscle Contraction Signals (SMCS). These signals, recorded via surface electromyography (sEMG), include responses such as compound muscle action potentials generated by muscle fibers [14]. The SMCS captures muscle contraction signals that show varying responses depending on the frequency parameter of electrical stimulation, enabling comprehensive analysis of muscle characteristics [15]. Previous research has indicated that SMCS contain valuable information on muscle strength and endurance, suggesting their potential as digital biomarkers for assessing muscle condition [15]. Furthermore, SMCS have been shown to effectively classify sarcopenia in persons with stroke, indicating this method may serve as a more accessible and convenient alternative for muscle assessment, particularly for stroke survivors with limited mobility [16]. However, evidence regarding their reliability is still lacking. Establishing high levels of reliability is essential to ensure consistent results across different settings and assessors and to validate the clinical utility of SMCS.

In this study, we first aimed to examine the reliability of measuring SMCS-based digital muscle markers in both thighs (affected and unaffected) of persons with stroke, assessing both intra-rater and inter-rater reliability. Second, this study aimed to determine the validity of the SMCS-based digital muscle marker for assessing muscle condition by correlating it with muscle strength and muscle mass.

Methods

Participants

Forty persons with stroke undergoing rehabilitation at Rhin Hospital were recruited for this study. Inclusion criteria were: (1) experiencing a first-ever stroke within the past two years, (2) being over 18 years of age, (3) having suffered a hemiplegic stroke, and (4) providing written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) the presence of neuromuscular diseases such as Guillain-Barre syndrome, Myasthenia Gravis, or myopathy, (2) unstable medical conditions, and (3) severe cognitive impairment hindering compliance with study procedures. The Medical Research Council (MRC) sum score was used to assess the severity of motor deficits due to stroke. This score is determined by manually testing muscle strength in both upper and lower limbs, with each muscle group evaluated on a scale of 0 to 5 [17]. The specific muscle groups assessed include the shoulder abductors, elbow flexors, wrist extensors, hip flexors, knee extensors, and ankle dorsiflexors on both sides of the body. The total possible score is 60 points (Supplementary Table 1). Institutional review board approval was obtained from the Korea National Institute for Bioethics Policy, and written informed consent was secured from each participant prior to their inclusion in the study (IRB number: 202210-01-032).

SMCS measurements

The SMCSs were collected for 40 hemiplegic persons with stroke using the exoPill device (Fig. 1A and B) (EXOSYSTEMS, Seongnam, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea), which had received approval from the Korea Food and Drug Administration as a medical device. SMCS measurements were conducted with subjects seated on a chair. The electrode and pad were positioned near the midpoint of the line connecting the anterior superior iliac spine and the superior border of the patella (reference line), where the muscle belly was most prominent (Fig. 1C). This placement was chosen to primarily target the rectus femoris within the knee extensor group, following the SENIAM (Surface Electromyography for Noninvasive Assessment of Muscles) recommendation [18]. The long axes of the electrode pads were aligned parallel to the reference line. Muscles were electrically stimulated, and the SMCSs were simultaneously collected using EMG sensors.

The SMCS measurement consisted of three trials, with a 30-minute interval between each. To account for inter-rater reliability, two examiners were assigned alternately in an A-B-A order. Examiner A conducted the first and third trials, while examiner B conducted the second trial. In the results, ‘A1’ and ‘A2’ indicate the values assessed by examiner A at different times. The examiners were blinded to both the electrode and pad placements from the previous trial as well as to the measurement results.

Pain and discomfort are commonly reported side effects of electrical stimulation. To evaluate safety, participants rated their pain on a visual analog scale (VAS, 0 = no pain, 10 = worst pain) during SMCS measurement. Pain scores ≤ 3 were defined as mild and tolerable, whereas scores ≥ 4 were considered unacceptable and led to termination of the procedure [19]. Additionally, the electrode attachment sites were inspected for skin irritation, burns or contact dermatitis after the procedure.

Electrical stimulation and EMG sensor setup

The frequency of electrical stimulation increases incrementally from 5 Hz to 30 Hz in 5 Hz increments over an 8-second period, with a 2-second rest period between stimulation [20]. The stimulation circuitry generated three consecutive biphasic pulse waveforms with a duration of 1.40 ms (50% duty cycle) at frequencies ranging from 5 to 100 Hz. The maximum peak-to-peak amplitude was 100 V, measured across a 500 Ω non-inductive resistor, which corresponds to a stimulation intensity of 28.24 mA. Stimulation was delivered via hydrogel surface electrode (area: 38.49 cm²; 150 mm × 70 mm), resulting in a current density of approximately 0.73 mA/cm².

All EMG signals were sampled at 1000 Hz [21, 22]. To suppress power line interference, notch filters at 50 Hz and 60 Hz were applied, as these correspond to common power line frequencies in different regions. Although there is no universal standard requiring the use of both frequencies simultaneously, several methodological guidelines, including those from Delsys and manufacturer recommendations [23], explicitly advise applying a notch filter at 50–60 Hz to reduce ambient line noise. The collected data were preprocessed by extracting signals during the electrical stimulation period.

Establishment of SMCS-based digital muscle marker using feature extraction and algorithm

The feature extraction in the time and frequency domains was conducted by segmenting the collected SMCSs based on the electrical stimulation frequencies. To characterize muscle contraction signals changing over time, we extracted four types of time domain features: zero crossing, area, correlation, and correlation of symmetric information. Frequency domain features are commonly used in EMG signal analysis for estimating muscle fatigue or muscle strength. Among various frequency domain features, total power, median frequency, correlation, and spectral edge frequency were extracted from the SMCSs. A description of features that require further explanation is presented below.

Correlation It represents the features extracted from the time and frequency domains of the cross-correlation signals for each electrical stimulation frequency segment. This helps understand the interaction between stimulation frequency segments.

Correlation It represents the features extracted from the time and frequency domains of the cross-correlation signals for each electrical stimulation frequency segment. This helps understand the interaction between stimulation frequency segments.

Correlation of symmetric information This feature represents the cross-correlation coefficient between the segment of positive peak and its symmetric signal, as well as between the segment of negative peak and its symmetric signal, and is utilized to assess the symmetry of SMCSs.

Correlation of symmetric information This feature represents the cross-correlation coefficient between the segment of positive peak and its symmetric signal, as well as between the segment of negative peak and its symmetric signal, and is utilized to assess the symmetry of SMCSs.

Spectral edge frequency It represents the frequency below which a certain percentage of the total power of the SMCS is contained, indicating the frequency boundary of significant signal components. In our case, 25% and 75% of the total power were used. In order to select the relevant features, we performed a feature selection using the Pearson correlation coefficient, with significance determined at p < 0.05.

In addition to EMG-derived features, anthropometric variables (age, sex, height, and weight) were incorporated as model inputs. Inter-individual differences in EMG signals are known to be influenced by morphological factors such as subcutaneous tissue thickness [24]. By explicitly providing anthropometric information, the model was designed to better account for individual-specific variability that cannot be explained by EMG features alone. The establishment of an SMCS-based digital muscle marker was performed using an ensemble regression model constructed using the bagging method [25]. Bagging is an ensemble learning technique that involves training multiple models on different bootstrap samples of the data, and combining their predictions to improve stability and accuracy. By averaging the predictions of individual models, bagging reduces variance, mitigates overfitting, and enhances the model’s generalization capability. The regression model was trained on a dataset comprising 122 SMCS data collected from two sources: 98 healthy adults recruited for a previous study at Kyung Hee University Medical Center [20], and 24 healthy older adults living in the community, recruited by EXOSYSTEMS. All subjects included in the model training met the exclusion criteria for the following conditions: (1) neuromuscular disease, (2) chronic kidney failure, (3) cancer within the past five years, (4) stroke within the past six months, and (5) physical disabilities. The average age of subjects from Kyung Hee University Medical Center was 40 ± 11 years, while the average age of those from EXOSYSTEMS was 76 ± 4 years. The trained model was then applied to SMCS data collected at Rhin hospital for this study, generating SMCS-based digital muscle markers that reflect muscle properties (see Supplementary Fig. 1 for detailed process).

Muscle mass measurement by BIA

Muscle mass was analyzed with BWA 2.0 (InBody, Seoul, Korea), a multi-frequency BIA device. Measurements were taken right after awakening and after 10 min of rest in the supine position to minimize the potential effects of recent food, water intake and exercise [26]. Participants were placed in supine position, and electrode were placed on both wrists and ankles. Leg lean mass was estimated using BIA measurements. To assess the relative body muscle mass, the skeletal muscle index (SMI) was calculated by dividing the sum of measured appendicular muscle mass by the square of the participant’s height (m2). For the sensitivity analysis, leg lean mass and SMI measured by BIA were converted to DXA-equivalent values using published BIA–DXA conversion equations [27]. These equations were derived from a study that used a multi-frequency BIA device in a population of the same ethnic group (Korean) as the present study.

Measurement of knee extensor strength

Maximal isometric strength in the bilateral knee extensors was measured using an isometric dynamometer, the Leg Strength Analyzer (IB-LS, Inbody, Korea). Participants were seated on a specially designed chair with 90 degrees of hip flexion and 60 degrees of knee flexion [28]. With the bar placed perpendicular to the anterior tibia of the ankle, participants were asked to perform a knee extension movement with maximal voluntary contraction. A total of 3 contractions were performed, separated by a 1-minute rest. The highest value among three trials was selected. After 5 min of rest, the same procedure was performed with the other leg.

Measurement of hand grip strength (HGS)

Maximal grip strength was assessed using a hydraulic Jamar manual dynamometer. Participants were seated with their arms adducted, elbows flexed at 90°, and wrists in a neutral position. They were instructed to squeeze the handle with maximum effort for at least 3 s. Three trials were conducted consecutively, with a 60-second rest period between each. The highest value from the three trials was used for analysis.

Statistical analysis

For continuous variables, the Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the normality. Normally distributed variables were described using mean and standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed variables were described using the median and interquartile range. The reliability of the SMCS-based digital muscle marker was determined through intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and coefficient of variance (CV) metrics. For intra-rater reliability, ICC (3,1) with a two-way mixed-effect model and absolute agreement definition was utilized [29]. Inter-rater reliability was evaluated using the ICC (2,1) with a two-way random-effect model [29]. ICC below 0.5 indicate poor reliability, between 0.5 and 0.75 moderate reliability, between 0.75 and 0.9 good reliability, and any value above 0.9 indicates excellent reliability [30]. ICC estimates and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The CV was calculated as the coefficient of variation of the repeated measurements using the follows [31]: CV = σ/µ × 100.

Here, σ represents the standard deviation of the measured SMCS-based digital muscle marker, and µ represents their mean value. The standard error of measurement (SEM) was calculated using the following formula: SEM = SD√(1-ICC), where SD represents the pooled standard deviation [32]. SEM% was expressed as a percentage of the mean. The minimal detectable change (MDC95) at the 95% CI was calculated as MDC95 = 1.96×SEM×√2 [33]. MDC95% was likewise expressed as a percentage of the mean. A repeated measures ANOVA was conducted using the three repeated measurements to demonstrate that there are no significant differences between the measurements. Bland-Altman analyses were employed to assess systematic bias across repeated measurements [34], using a Python program. To determine the limits of agreement (LOA) within a 95% CI, we used the formula: mean difference ± 1.96×standard deviation of differences. For the validity analysis, Spearman correlation coefficients (ρ) were used to determine the degree of correlation between SMCS-based digital muscle marker and muscle strength, muscle mass and SMI. The correlation was categorized as follows: values less than 0.25 were considered low, 0.25 to less than 0.5 as fair, 0.5 to less than 0.75 as moderate to good, and values greater than 0.75 as good to excellent [30]. The p-values of the test were adjusted according to the Bonferroni correction.

The CV of SMCS-based digital muscle marker measured on the unaffected leg by the same examiner A was 3.07%. Measurements by examiner B showed CV values ranging from 1.36% to 2.18%. The ICC value was above 0.98, demonstrating excellent reliability in both intra- and inter-rater measures. MDC95 for intra-rater measurements was 2.92, and for inter-rater measurements were 2.40 and 2.92. The Bland-Altman plots (Fig. 2A) illustrate the variability in SMCS-based digital muscle marker between intra-rater and inter-rater assessments in unaffected legs of participants. No systematic trend was not observed. For the measurements between examiner A1 and examiner B, the mean difference was − 0.43, with the LOA ranging from − 3.82 to 2.98. The mean difference between examiner B and examiner A2 was 0.25 (LOA range: −3.95 to 4.46). For the intra-rater measurements, the mean difference was − 0.17, with the LOA ranging from − 4.29 to 3.95.

Validity

The relationships between SMCS-based digital muscle marker, knee extensor strength, leg lean mass, SMI and HGS in the unaffected leg are presented in Table 3. The Spearman correlation coefficient revealed a moderate to good correlation between SMCS-based digital muscle marker and knee extensor strength (ρ = 0.50), as well as HGS (ρ = 0.61). Leg lean mass (ρ = 0.87) and SMI (ρ = 0.86) showed a good to excellent correlation with SMCS-based digital muscle marker. Similar to the findings on the unaffected side, significant correlations were observed between the SMCS-based digital muscle markers of the affected side and knee extensor strength (ρ = 0.50), leg lean mass (ρ = 0.87), and SMI (ρ = 0.86). However, correlation levels between the SMCS-based digital muscle marker and HGS failed to reach statistical significance.

Sensitivity analysis showed that SMCS-based digital muscle markers correlated well with DXA estimates derived from BIA. In the unaffected leg, correlations were good-to-excellent for leg lean mass (DXA-LM, ρ = 0.88) and SMI (DXA-SMI, ρ = 0.86) (Supplementary Table 3). In the affected leg, correlations were similarly good-to-excellent for both measures (DXA-LM ρ = 0.86; DXA-SMI ρ = 0.85). These findings were consistent with BIA-based correlations, supporting the robustness of the results.

Discussion

This study examined the reliability and validity of the SMCS-based digital muscle marker for assessing muscle in persons with stroke. Measurements taken from both the affected and unaffected sides demonstrated high intra- and inter-rater reliability, with all ICCs exceeding 0.98 and CVs around 3%. In addition, the SMCS-based digital muscle marker showed a positive correlation with muscle mass and strength on both the unaffected and affected sides of persons with stroke. These findings suggest that SMCS-based digital muscle markers, which have demonstrated a high level of reliability, may have potential for serving as a novel biomarker for evaluating muscle condition and function.

Reliable measurement of muscle condition is crucial for evaluating the severity of a disability and monitoring clinical progress [35]. Accurate data is essential for healthcare providers to develop effective treatment plans. For individuals with motor impairments, where progress may be gradual and challenging to detect, a technology capable of reliably capturing even small changes in strength or muscle mass is crucial [32]. SMCS-based digital muscle marker can be easily measured by attaching electrode pads to the targeted muscle. However, there were concerns that minor variations in electrode pad placement by different examiners could significantly impact measurement values.

In this study, two examiners were instructed to attach electrodes by palpating two anatomical landmarks—the anterior superior iliac spine and the patella—without the use of specialized equipment, while remaining blinded to prior placements. Despite the simplicity of this procedure, our results demonstrated excellent intra- and inter-rater reliability for SMCS-based digital muscle markers on both the affected and unaffected sides. The consistently high ICC values, all above 0.98, along with low CV values around 3%, indicate that SMCS-based digital muscle marker measurements are robust and reliable. The Bland-Altman plot also shows consistent variability across the range, suggesting stable agreement regardless of mean values. The mean differences for intra- and inter-rater assessments ranged from − 0.43 to 0.25 for the unaffected leg and from 0.00 to 0.05 for the affected leg, with the line of mean difference close to zero, indicating no systematic bias between measurements. Additionally, most values fall within the LOA, indicating reliable measurements.

EMG signals exhibit inherent variability due to factors such as subcutaneous tissue; consequently, EMG-derived features (e.g., total power and median frequency) may have limited reliability. Consistent with this, we observed relatively low reliability of these features in our study. Across different electrical stimulation frequencies, total power (ICC: 0.294–0.900; SEM%: 5.04–16.96; MDC%: 13.97–47.02) and median frequency (ICC: 0.602–0.768; SEM%: 7.63–37.16; MDC%: 21.16–103.00) reflected the inherent variability of the EMG signal. To address this, anthropometric variables including age, sex, height, and weight were incorporated into our model, allowing systematic differences in EMG responses across subjects to be captured. Consequently, SMCS-based digital markers reflected subject-specific characteristics rather than random error, resulting in high ICC values.

Electrically stimulating muscles is a highly valuable technique for understanding muscle function and its pathological changes, and it can be applied in both diagnosis and treatment [36–38]. SMCS measurements capture involuntary contraction signals in muscles induced by multi-frequency electrical stimulation, providing diverse biomedical information about muscle status [15]. A recent study reported that SMCS measurements can be used to classify sarcopenia [16]. In this study, SMCS-based digital muscle markers measured in the thigh showed significant positive correlations with leg lean mass, SMI and knee extensor strength, all important indicators related to physical function. SMI represents the relative skeletal muscle mass in the body. Low SMI is linked to functional impairment and disability in older adults [39, 40]. Leg lean mass, which is highly affected to bed rest state, serves as a reliable indicator of muscle mass loss due to reduced physical activity [41–43]. Knee extensor strength, critical for walking and closely associated with activities of daily living, reflects the functional outcome [44, 45]. Therefore, SMCS-based digital muscle markers can be used to estimate physical function by indirectly reflecting muscle mass and strength.

One point of interest was that SMCS-based digital muscle markers showed positive correlations with HGS and knee extensor strength mainly on the unaffected side. This result aligns with existing knowledge, as HGS is positively correlated with quadriceps strength and is recognized as an indicator of overall strength [46]. However, such findings were not replicated when recorded on the affected side. This lack of correlation is likely due to the fact that in stroke survivors with severe motor deficits, paretic hand function often remains unrecovered and HGS is frequently measured as zero (17 out of 40 participants, Supplementary Table 2). Similarly, HGS on the unaffected hand showed a high correlation with leg lean mass and SMI, but no significant correlation on the affected hand. Assessing hand grip strength on the affected side after a stroke may yield unreliable results due to the frequent involvement of the corticospinal tract, which disproportionately affects fine motor control of the hand. Hand movement are complex and requires precision, making them highly susceptible to impairment and potentially leading to inaccuracies in grip strength assessments for individuals with stroke. Consequently, previous research has recommended using hand grip strength data from the unaffected side when diagnosing sarcopenia in individuals with hemiplegic stroke [47].

In contrast, fewer participants exhibited zero muscle strength for the affected knee extensor (5 out of 40 participants, Supplementary Table 2), and the results showed a significant correlation between SMCS-based digital muscle markers and knee extensor strength. Prior study has indicated that paralytic knee extensor strength reflects the overall strength of the paretic lower limb in persons with stroke [48]. Therefore, our findings suggest that SMCS-based digital muscle markers measured at the affected thigh may serve as a valuable indicator for assessing and tracking changes in lower extremity strength and function in stroke survivors. In this study, the MDC95 for the SMCS-based digital muscle marker ranged from 2.40 to 3.21, indicating that changes exceeding this range can be interpreted as significant with 95% confidence. This threshold is crucial for clinical rehabilitation settings, particularly in monitoring stroke survivors with sarcopenia or reduced muscle strength, and for evaluating the responsiveness to treatment.

The ability to assess muscle condition without requiring voluntary contraction is particularly valuable for individuals with motor impairments, where traditional methods of evaluating muscle contractile function are often inadequate. Persons with hemiplegic stroke, who face a heightened risk of developing sarcopenia, especially benefit from regular muscle evaluations. This underscores the advantage of SMCS-based digital muscle markers over conventional measurement methods. Our technology not only overcomes the limitations of existing techniques but also offers significant practical benefits. Its applicability extends to individuals with limited mobility, irrespective of their location or the timing of the assessment. The lightweight and portable nature of the equipment (weighing a total of 430 g, including a 270 g cradle and four 40 g modules) enables unrestricted evaluations in various environments. As demonstrated in this study, the SMCS-based measurements displayed high intra- and inter-rater reliability, guaranteeing consistent and accurate results across different examiners. Therefore, our findings suggest that SMCS-based digital muscle markers represent a valuable tool for assessing muscle conditions in individuals with restricted mobility, such as stroke patients or those in intensive care units.

In terms of safety, all participants reported tolerable pain levels (VAS ≤ 3) during SMCS measurement, and no procedures required discontinuation. Post-measurement inspection also revealed no skin reactions such as irritation, burns, or dermatitis, and no additional adverse events were observed. These findings indicate that the SMCS measurement can be applied safely under the tested conditions.

This study has several limitations. First, some inherent differences existed between the training and testing data used to derive SMCS-based digital muscle markers. Our model was trained using SMCS data from healthy individuals and older adults, but not from individuals with stroke. Unlike age-related muscle loss, stroke survivors often exhibit a predominance of fast-twitch type 2 muscle fibers and motor unit denervation, which are not typically present in similar conditions involving muscle wasting [37]. These differences suggest that the features extracted from SMCS data may vary between the training and testing groups, potentially affecting muscle assessment in persons with stroke.

To address this limitation, transfer learning and domain adaptation techniques can be employed. Accordingly, we conducted a brief domain adaptation analysis, in which the SMCS-based model trained on healthy and older adults was fine-tuned using a subset of stroke data. A residual fine-tuning strategy enabled parameter-efficient adaptation while reducing the risk of overfitting. The adapted model showed an average 6.15% improvement in correlations with clinical measures, while the CV increased by an average of 3.74. These results indicate that domain adaptation helped correct the baseline model’s overestimation tendency. Despite this trade-off, residual fine-tuning enhanced the clinical relevance of the model and provided more realistic performance estimates. Future work should explore more advanced domain adaptation strategies and validate the approach in larger and more heterogeneous stroke cohorts to further improve reproducibility and generalizability.

Secondly, BIA was used to measure muscle mass when validating SMCS-based digital muscle marker. While MRI and CT are considered the gold standards for assessing muscle mass [49], their high cost and complexity limited their use in this study. DXA was also unavailable in our clinical setting, prompting the use of BIA. Although there is ongoing debate over the accuracy of BIA in measuring muscle mass, it has been validated for body composition assessment, showing good correlation with DXA, and has been applied to estimate appendicular skeletal muscle mass in several recent studies [50, 51]. In this study, a sensitivity analysis using published BIA-to-DXA conversion equations further showed that SMCS-based digital muscle markers correlated well with DXA estimates derived from BIA in both affected and unaffected legs, thereby addressing the limitations of BIA. Future research should explore whether SMCS-based digital muscle markers maintain strong correlations with muscle mass measured using other various methods.

Conclusions

Measurements of SMCS-based digital muscle markers in the thighs of persons with stroke demonstrated high levels of reliability and significant positive correlations with leg strength and muscle mass on both the affected and unaffected sides. These findings suggest that SMCS-based digital muscle markers hold potential for clinical use and provide a basis for future applications in the diagnosing and monitoring of conditions associated with muscle change.
