Prediction of unconfined compressive strength of fly ash and waste paper sludge stabilized clayey soil using explainable machine learning
Authors: Vandana Aggarwal, Bharpur Singh Abhi, Seema Gupta, Rippandeep Kaur, Ranjeeta Saini, Priya Sharma, Prince Sharma, Amenjor Senagah, Ankush Mehta, Rupesh Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sustainable stabilization of problematic clayey soils with industrial by-products has recently become a topic of considerable interest because of the environmental and economic issues relating to traditional binders. This paper examines the interaction of fly ash (FA) and waste paper sludge (WPS) in enhancing the unconfined compressive strength (UCS) of soil that contains Clay and trains relevant machine learning (ML) models to precisely forecast the strength of clayey soil. An overall experimental data set of 265 samples was obtained by changing the contents of FA and WPS (0–20%) under conditions of 3–90 days of curing. Three ensemble ML models (XGBoost, Random Forest and Stacking Regressor) were created to measure the nonlinear relationships between soil properties, compaction parameters and curing effects. Results indicate that the predictive performance of the models is really great with the R2 of 0.9958 (XGBoost), 0.9932 (Random Forest), and 0.9960 (Stacking); the Stacking model had the lowest error (RMSE = 4.0145 kPa, MAE = 3.2904 kPa). The residual analysis operated to reveal that there was little bias and controlled variance, which indicated a high level of generalization when used in a laboratory environment. In order to make the models easier to understand, SHAP analysis was used, which showed that the determining factors that control the strength development are the curing period and the aspects related to binder, whereas WPS predicts the physical modification, but with a comparatively smaller power. Sensitivity analysis and monotonicity analysis also supported the idea that strength behavior is controlled by coherent chemical and mechanical processes with curing-driven pozzolanic reactions having a significant role. On the whole, the paper provides a solid and explainable ML model of predicting UCS and proves the fact that the FA-WPS stabilization can be implemented as an effective alternative to geotechnical applications.

Introduction

The speed of urbanization and growth in infrastructure has amplified the need to have geotechnically sound, economical and environmentally friendly methods of ground improvement1,2. Among the major problems in geotechnical engineering is the high prevalence of problematic clayey soils that pose high plasticity, low shear strength, poor drainage, and pronounced swelling shrinkage behavior3–5; R. K. Sharma6,. All these negative features strongly deteriorate the long-term work of foundations, pavements, and earth structures, which frequently leads to excessive deformation, cracking, and premature failure7,8. Conventional stabilization methods involving lime or cement are effective but their proliferation has become a major concern on environmental issues owing to the high energy consumption and large volumes of carbon dioxide released in the method of production3,9. Therefore, creating alternative stabilization methods that would restore technical efficiency with environmentally friendly values has turned out to be a significant area of research interest.

Fly ash (FA) and waste paper sludge (WPS) are industrial by-products whose use as sustainable soil stabilizers has gained more and more popularity10–14. Fly ash is a siliceous waste product obtained in coal-fired power plants and has a pozzolanic reactivity that enhances cementitious compounds when combined with moisture, thus enhancing soil strength and decreasing plasticity15. Paper-based waste paper sludge is a fibrous and cellulosic by-product, which affects the behavior of soil in the form of microstructural alteration, filler effects, and enhanced compaction properties16,17. Whereas FA is more likely to achieve stabilization due to chemical bonding and gaining of strengths over time, WPS offers physical reinforcement and retention of moisture, which implies that their joint application will result in synergies in stabilization and help to maintain circular resource usage at the same time.

Although the number of experimental investigations on FA- or WPS-stabilized soils has been on the rise, the available literature has been mainly dedicated to single-additive systems or shorter curing periods. The interplay between FA and WPS and changing geotechnical characteristics, including plasticity, swell potential and compaction behavior, over both short-term and long-term curing periods is only partially studied. Further, strength development in these systems is directed by set of strongly coupled and nonlinear processes comprising pozzolanic reactions, moisture density interactions, and curing-dependent microstructural dynamic processes, that are hard to model using standard empirical or regression models18. Recent works have thus been utilizing ML methods to predict UCS of stabilized soils to handle this complexity19,20. Most of the UCS prediction studies utilizing MLs are, one, based on relatively small datasets; two, utilize individual stabilizers; and three, the UCS prediction models are black-box in nature, implying that the underlying geotechnical mechanisms lack interpretability. Modeling-wise, those ensembles in the form of Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Random Forest, and stacking regressors are especially effective with geotechnical data that are inflicted with multicollinearity, nonlinear dependencies, and nonuniform variables (24,]25,). Tree-based ensembles offer better generalization and are more resistant to noise as well as remain more stable to diverse parameter configurations than the more commonly used models (artificial neural networks (ANN) or gene expression programming (GEP). More to the point, together with explainable artificial intelligence methods, such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), such models allow breaking down predictions to individual feature contributions and provide insights into the effects of specific geotechnical parameters on UCS at various stabilization and curing regimes26. In contrast to traditional feature-importance metrics, which only give global rankings, SHAP analysis measures the global and local marginal effects, and thus allows the direct connection of the ML outputs to the interpretation of soil-mechanics.

Considering the foregoing, the research paper fills a definite scientific gap by constructing an understandable machine learning algorithm of forecasting UCS of clayey soil considered to be under the influence of combined FA and WPS under different curing regimes. The objective behind the work is to (i) systematically assess FA-WPS interaction effects on large, laboratory-generated data, (ii) apply ensemble ML models that have been chosen on the basis of their capability to represent nonlinear geotechnical performance in data more reliably than traditional ANN- or GEP-based (ii) integrate SHAP analysis to derive mechanistic insights into strength development beyond predictive accuracy per se. The study, based on 265 experimental available data, including material composition, index properties, compaction properties, and curing time, builds upon the current research on UCS prediction by connecting the available data to data-driven modeling and geotechnical interpretation. The rest of the paper reports the experimental program and ML methodology, shows the model performance and interpretability results and comments on the implications of the findings on sustainable and performance-based geotechnical design.

In this study, clayey soil was treated with varying proportions of FA and WPS to assess its strength behavior under different curing conditions. All materials used were procured locally, characterized in the laboratory, and combined in controlled dosages to create a comprehensive matrix of experimental samples. Soil-additive combinations

The experimental programme was planned to explore the effects of FA and combination of FA and WPS on UCS of clayey soil systematically. FA and WPS were added individually and in a mixed proportion to soil at the dosage levels of 0, 4, 8, 12, 16 and 20% dry weight of soil. The mixtures of FA–WPS were made to balance the effects of interactions with the total binder content which had to be within the target range. All mixes were tested based on 3, 7, 28, 56, and 90 days of the curing process to determine the short and long-term strength development. Each mix–curing combination generated three replicated UCS specimens and then it was tested and the average UCS value was given out. This mix design of a systematic mix also allowed the consideration of the effects of the dosage and the duration of curing of FA–WPS on the strength behaviour of stabilized clayey soil in a comprehensive manner.

Experimental analysis

The experimental program was developed in line with the Indian Standards to test the UCS of clayey soil that has different proportions of FA and WPS. The uniformity was maintained by first drying the soil in an oven at 105 ± 2 °C and then grinding it to a fine size of 4.75 mm IS sieve. The tests of standard geotechnical were performed according to IS: 2720 in order to identify the index and compaction characteristics of the soil, the liquid limit (Part 5), plastic limit (Part 5), optimum moisture content and maximum dry density (Part 7). The ratios of additives (FA and WPS) to dry soil were prepared in a given proportion that varies between 0% and 20%. UCS testing specimens were set in 38 mm diameter and 76 mm height cylindrical moulds by being compacted in three equal layers under static conditions. Water was added at the optimum moisture content achieved in the Proctor test and the materials was mixed using the hands until a homogenous blend was formed. Every layer was pressed to gain the highest dry density that was calculated. Specimens were then removed very carefully out of the mould after compaction and trimmed, so as to make the height to diameter ratio uniform, 2:1. The prepared samples were cured under that temperature inside a humility chamber that was controlled at 27 ± 2 °C with relative humidity of over 95% and the period of curing was 3, 7, 28, 56 and 90 days. The specimens were covered with plastic film and kept in airtight containers during curing so as to avoid moisture loss and to ensure that they were subjected to field curing conditions.

UCS tests were conducted at the conclusion of every curing period as required by IS: 2720 (Part 10) on a strain-controlled loading frame at a constant strain rate of 1.25 mm/min. The axial loading of each specimen was done to failure, and the highest axial loading was taken. UCS was calculated as a ratio of the peak load and the corrected cross-sectional area of the specimen in consideration of the lateral strain. Each mix and curing period was tested on three specimens and the average value of UCS was reported in order to reduce experimental variability.

Machine learning modelling

The dataset was generated by systematically testing multiple combinations of FA and WPS contents (0–20%) under five curing periods (3, 7, 28, 56, and 90 days). For each mix–curing combination, UCS tests were conducted on three replicate specimens, and the average UCS value was recorded as a single data point, resulting in a total of 265 experimental records. A data-driven modelling approach was adopted to predict the UCS of clayey soil treated with varying percentages of FA and WPS. Each of the 265 experimental observations was characterized by input parameters including soil content, FA content, WPS content, DFS, LL), PL, OMC, MDD, and CP. These parameters were selected to represent the coupled physical, mechanical, and volumetric factors governing soil strength behavior. The inclusion of index properties (LL, PL, DFS), compaction characteristics (OMC, MDD), additive contents, and curing period enables the models to learn chemical–mechanical interactions associated with stabilization and time-dependent strength development. In the developed dataset, “soil content” represents the percentage of natural clayey soil in the total dry mixture by weight. As FA and WPS contents were varied between 0% and 20%, the corresponding soil content ranged from 100% to 60% across different specimens. Prior to model development, all input features were examined through descriptive statistical analysis and normalized using Min–Max scaling to ensure a uniform range across variables. The dataset was then randomly divided into training (80%) and testing (20%) subsets to evaluate the generalization performance of the developed machine learning models. The dataset was generated through a systematic variation of mix compositions and curing periods, resulting in distinct experimental conditions rather than repeated samples. This structured design minimizes the likelihood of similar mix designs appearing in both training and testing sets and supports reliable model generalization. To avoid data leakage, the dataset splitting was performed prior to preprocessing. All normalization parameters for Min–Max scaling were derived exclusively from the training dataset and subsequently applied to the testing dataset. This ensured that no information from the test set influenced model training or parameter tuning. Although certain variables such as LL, PL, DFS, and soil content exhibit strong correlations, they collectively represent plasticity and swelling characteristics of clayey soil. Tree-based ensemble models used in this study are inherently robust to such multicollinearity and can effectively extract dominant patterns without being affected by redundancy among predictors. The UCS values exhibit a moderately right-skewed distribution, reflecting progressive strength gain with increasing fly ash (FA) and waste paper sludge (WPS) contents as well as extended curing periods. Outlier screening was performed using box plots and Z-score analysis based on the ± 3σ criterion, and no non-physical or extreme outliers were identified. Consequently, all 265 experimentally obtained data points were retained for machine learning modelling. This confirms that the dataset is physically consistent, statistically well-distributed, and representative of the stabilization behaviour across the investigated FA–WPS dosage ranges and curing regimes.

Three supervised regression models were developed for UCS prediction: XGBoost, Random Forest, and a Stacking Regressor. These ensemble-based models were deliberately selected because UCS development in FA–WPS stabilized clayey soils is governed by strongly nonlinear interactions among material composition, index properties, and curing duration. Tree-based ensembles such as XGBoost and Random Forest are particularly effective in capturing such interaction effects without requiring predefined constitutive assumptions. The stacking regressor was further employed to examine whether integrating complementary learning mechanisms could enhance robustness and generalization beyond individual models.

Random forest (RF)

Random forest (RF)

It is an ensemble learning technique that constructs multiple decision trees and aggregates their outputs to enhance predictive accuracy and mitigate overfitting (Salman et al., 2024). Unlike boosting methods, which build trees sequentially with each tree correcting the errors of the previous one, Random Forest generates trees independently by using randomized subsets of both the training data and input features. This randomization introduces diversity among the trees, thereby reducing correlation and improving the model’s ability to generalize, particularly in the presence of complex or noisy datasets. Stacking regressor

Stacking regressor

It is a meta-ensemble learning technique that integrates multiple base learners to improve predictive performance. In this framework, several models—such as Random Forest, XGBoost, or Support Vector Regression—are trained independently in the first layer. Their predictions are then combined and used as inputs for a higher-level learner, often called the meta-model or blender, which learns how to optimally integrate these outputs. This approach reduces bias and variance by leveraging the complementary strengths of the base learners, making it effective in handling complex and heterogeneous datasets. To further assess model robustness and generalization, 5-fold cross-validation was performed during hyperparameter tuning. The XGBoost, Random Forest, and Stacking models achieved mean R² values near to 0.99 with low standard deviations (< 0.003), indicating stable and consistent predictive performance across different data partitions. Model performance was evaluated using three standard statistical metrics: the coefficient of determination (R²), the root mean square error (RMSE), and the mean absolute error (MAE). The use of multiple evaluation metrics ensures both statistical robustness and engineering relevance. While R² indicates the explanatory capability of the models, RMSE and MAE quantify prediction errors in stress units, which are directly meaningful for geotechnical design and performance assessment. This combination allows model accuracy to be evaluated not only statistically but also in terms of practical engineering applicability. Results and discussion

Evaluation of model performance using actual vs. predicted UCS scatter plots

The Actual vs. Predicted UCS scatter plots (Fig. 4) show that all three models can be highly predictive based on the high clustering of the data points around 45o reference line and substantial coefficients of determination (R2> 0.99). Nevertheless, small variations in the dispersion patterns and error measure based on RMSE and MAE indicate significant differences between model behavior in various UCS ranges.

The XGBoost model has a very high prediction rate (R2 = 0.9958, RMSE = 4.1063, MAE = 3.3838), and the data points are closely distributed along the 1:1 line (Fig. 4a). The model works uniformly throughout the UCS range because of its gradient boosting framework which is used to eliminate residual errors successively. Nonetheless, at elevated UCS values (more than 250) one observes slight over- or under-predictions. This behavior can be explained by the fact that there is a sparsity of data in the stronger part of the strength area, as well as the predisposition of the model to a weak overfitting of the prominent patterns, even when regularizing. However, it has low bias and variance owing to its capability to capture nonlinear relationships.

Random Forest model also has good predictive performance (R2 = 0.9932, RMSE = 5.2058, MAE = 4.3056), but with relatively higher variability around the reference line (Fig. 4b). The spreading is stronger at the higher values of UCS as predictions are slightly below the ideal line, which means that it is slightly underpredicted. It is an established weakness of models based on bagging where extreme values are averaged out by a collection of trees, reducing the sensitivity of models to sharp non-linear trends. Random Forest can, therefore, be more erroneous in those areas with complicated interactions or poor data representation, leading to a slight rise in RMSE and MAE.

The Stacking Regressor has the highest overall performance (R2 = 0.9960, RMSE = 4.0145, MAE = 3.2904) and the narrowest distribution of points on the 1:1 line as well as the least amount of dispersion when all UCS ranges are considered (Fig. 4c). This is attributed to the hybrid learning method, which has the benefit of XGBoost capturing the complex nonlinear interactions and the Random Forest offering resistance to noise and overfitting. These predictions are combined well using the meta-learner (Linear Regression) and minimizes systematic and random errors. Consequently, stacking model demonstrates better generalization capacity especially high UCS areas where individual models are characterized by minor deviation.

This variation in the performance of the models can then be traced down to the learning mechanisms behind them. XGBoost is also good at capturing nonlinearities yet it can be a little bit sensitive to data distributions, Random Forest is robust and smooths out extreme predictions and Stacking takes advantage of the complementary nature of the two and reduces both bias and variance at the same time. The relatively bigger errors noted in Random Forest at high UCS values, and the enhanced predictability of the stacking model shows that stacking of ensemble learning offers higher predictability reliability in complicated geotechnical data sets. Although data points at higher UCS ranges are relatively sparse, the model maintains reliable predictions due to its ability to capture underlying nonlinear relationships. Slightly wider prediction intervals in these regions appropriately reflect increased uncertainty without compromising overall model stability. The present findings are consistent with previous research demonstrating the superior performance of XGBoost and ensemble stacking approaches for environmental prediction tasks27.

Residual analysis of machine learning models for UCS prediction

The residual analysis of the developed models—XGBoost, Random Forest, and Stacking Regressor—provides valuable insights into their predictive reliability and generalization behavior for estimating UCS of stabilized clayey soil (Fig. 5). The XGBoost model shows a slightly broader spread of residuals, particularly at mid-to-high UCS values, along with mild asymmetry (Fig. 5a). While still centered around zero, this distribution suggests the presence of minor systematic deviations, potentially due to slight overfitting or unmodeled interactions at certain ranges. Nevertheless, the residuals remain within acceptable bounds, affirming the model’s robust generalization and accuracy. The Random Forest model, however, exhibits a noticeably wider spread in residuals and increased variance at higher predicted UCS values, indicating mild heteroscedasticity (Fig. 5b). This behavior is typical of bagging-based methods, where prediction smoothing through averaging can dampen the model’s responsiveness to intricate patterns in the data, leading to underfitting in regions with sharp variability. In the residual plot for the Stacking Regressor, the residuals are tightly concentrated around the zero-error line, with no discernible pattern or heteroscedasticity, indicating that the model predictions are unbiased and consistent across the full UCS range. This reflects the Stacking model’s superior ability to capture complex nonlinearities and reduce both bias and variance by leveraging the strengths of its base learners (Fig. 5c). Consequently, the Random Forest model demonstrates comparatively higher prediction uncertainty. These graphical diagnostics emphasize the importance of residual behavior as a complement to statistical metrics, revealing not only the magnitude but also the distributional characteristics of prediction errors. Such analysis is critical in model selection when deploying machine learning frameworks in sensitive engineering applications like strength estimation of stabilized soils. The absence of systematic patterns, heteroscedasticity, or bias in the residual distributions confirms that the models have not memorized the dataset and instead captured the underlying relationships governing UCS behavior. This provides strong evidence against overfitting despite the high R² values. These outcomes are consistent with prior studies reporting that machine learning methods such as Gaussian Process Regression and Support Vector Machine deliver highly accurate predictions of unconfined compressive strength and related soil properties28,29.

Model performance comparison

Figure 6 measures the relative performance of the Random Forest, XGBoost and Stacking Regressor models using standard statistical measures, which are the coefficient of determination (R2), root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and mean squared error (MSE). All models had a very high predictive accuracy with a R2 of 0.9932 (Random Forest), 0.9958 (XGBoost), 0.9960 (Stacking) meaning that best models were in perfect agreement in their predicted and observed UCS values. The Stacking model had the largest R2 among them with close behind it XGBoost with a slightly lower accuracy, and lastly Random Forest recorded a lower accuracy. There is a more distinct differentiation of the models in the error-based metrics. Stacking Regressor showed the minimal values of error, RMSE = 4.0145, MAE = 3.2904 and MSE = 16.1158, which testifies to its higher predictive accuracy and reliability. XGBoost model was also very strong with RMSE = 4.1063, MAE = 3.3838 and MSE = 16.8616, which are indicators that it captures nonlinear relationships with a minimum error. Conversely, the Random Forest model had relatively greater values of error (RMSE = 5.2058, MAE = 4.3056, MSE = 27.1000) indicating that it is less sensitive to complex patterns and tends to smooth extreme predictions. The Stacking Regressor has achieved a better performance due to its ensemble learning structure, which has the merits of XGBoost and the Random Forest, with minimal drawbacks of the two. XGBoost helps with a good prediction ability of nonlinear models by boosting, and Random Forest helps to make models more robust by suppressing variance by bagging. These complementary features are successfully incorporated in the meta-learner which leads to better generalization and less prediction error within the entire UCS range. These results are consistent with recent studies that show that modern machine learning models can make highly accurate predictions of geotechnical properties, with nature-inspired and support vector regression models displaying excellent results in UCS and other forms of soil strength prediction30,31.

Correlation analysis

The correlation coefficient of the equation of the relationship between the geotechnical variables and their effect on UCS were assessed using a Pearson correlation heatmap (Fig. 8) in which the correlation coefficients of these variables were determined to be between + 1 and − 1, showing the strength and direction of the correlation. The findings reveal that UCS is moderately and positively correlated with curing period (CP, r = 0.64), optimum moisture content (OMC, r = 0.59), and fly ash (FA, r = 0.49). The high power of CP can be attributed to the time-dependent character of pozzolanic reactions whereas FA helps in strength building up due to cementitious bonding. This is because the combined system of FA-WPS, where the availability of moisture dictates the two chemical reactions and compaction efficiency, leads to a relatively higher correlation with OMC that is usually less pronounced in FA-stabilized soils which increases the strength when conditions are controlled in the laboratory setting. On the other hand, UCS has significant negative correlations with the soil material content (r = −0.69), differential free swell (DFS, r = −0.68), liquid limit (LL, r = −0.68), and plastic limit (PL, r = −0.64), which means that increased plasticity and swelling capacity have a negative impact on compressive strength. This is in line with known geotechnical behavior which states that expansive clays are likely to have lower strength because of poor inter-particles bonding and high compressibility, and therefore have to be stabilised.

Waste paper sludge (WPS) has a fairly weak positive correlation with UCS (r = 0.25) although it is supposed to enhance the soil structure. This is justified by the fact that it is largely physical as opposed to chemical in terms of its contribution, where WPS is used as a filler and alters the soil matrix without necessarily undergoing cementitious reactions. Its effect is thus indirect and, in most cases, resembles the response to its interaction with reactive binders like FA and reactive moisture-density conditions. Consequently, its independent linear correlation with UCS does not seem good as such, although it has a contribution to overall performance when other parameters are included. The inter-correlations are found to be strong between the soil properties where DFS has high positive correlations with LL (r = 0.97) and PL (r = 0.94) and soil content is strongly correlated with DFS (r = 0.98) and LL (r = 0.97). The adverse relationship between the soil content and OMC (r = −0.86) also indicates the compactioniness of clay soils. Such multicollinearity is an indicator of the weakness of traditional regression methods and justifies the application of ensemble machine learning models, which have the potential to be effective in capturing complex and interdependent relationships. The relatively lower importance of WPS reflects its indirect and interaction-driven role in stabilization, primarily through physical modification of soil structure rather than direct chemical contribution.

As far as the model performance is concerned, the model with residuals up to the values of ± 10 kPa is still regarded as competitive because of the strength of the model and its ability to generalize. Random Forest bagging-based averaging causes prediction to be smoother, potentially making it less sensitive to extreme values of UCS, especially in higher strength ranges, causing slightly larger errors. Nevertheless, it is also a behavior that reduces overfitting and leads to the creation of stable performance on the dataset. Conversely, XGBoost and Stacking models have reduced errors since they consider more complicated nonlinear relations. Thus, the basic and stable model that can be used is the Random Forest, and the apparent best results of stacking emphasize the importance of the strategy combination. Although certain input variables exhibited strong correlations (e.g., LL, PL, DFS, and soil content), these parameters represent interrelated physical characteristics governing soil plasticity and swelling behavior. The adopted tree-based ensemble models are inherently robust to multicollinearity due to their hierarchical feature selection mechanism, ensuring that redundancy among predictors does not adversely affect model stability. Furthermore, SHAP-based feature importance revealed trends consistent with established geotechnical principles, confirming that the models have captured meaningful mechanistic relationships rather than statistical artifacts arising from multicollinearity. These results are in line with prior research that indicated that boosting and ensemble learning algorithms including XGboost and stacking methods have high predictive capabilities to estimate environmental properties34.

SHAP analysis

The SHAP analysis shows that the most significant parameter is curing period (CP), as it has the largest contribution range (about − 60 to + 50) meaning it is the most significant parameter in controlling the predicted strength (Fig. 9). Positive SHAP values are always obtained in higher curing periods, which is the gradual formation of pozzolanic and hydration products, which are cementitious products, including C -S -H and C -A -H gels. These compounds improve the bonding between separate particles, decrease the voids, and increase the stiffness and strength, which explain the highly positive effect of CP on the model output. Parameters related to binders like DFS/FA also prove to be of great significance as the values of SHAP are around − 10 to + 20. Their participation is mostly under chemical stabilization processes wherein reactive alumina and silica react with calcium to produce secondary cementitious products, which enhance the cohesion and bearing capacity. The fact that both positive and negative SHAP values are present indicates non-linear behavior and a certain optimum content of binder beyond which the material may not be useful in enhancing strength. The influence of the soil index properties (LL, PL and soil type) is moderate with the SHAP values mostly falling within the range of + 10 and − 10. These are parameters that control the plasticity of the soil, mineralogy, and water affinity which also influences its reactivity with stabilizers. Increased plasticity soils are expected to exhibit negative contributions because of their weaker structure, and greater compressibility, with the exception of proper stabilization, which is consistent with the traditional geotechnical knowledge. Compaction-related parameters (MDD and OMC) are of relatively lesser significance in contrast, with SHAP values mostly under the +/−5 range. These are the variables that have the main effect on the original packing and density of the soil, and not on the strength development in the long term. When sufficient compaction is obtained, they are secondarily effective in comparison to chemical bonding processes, and this is why they have a lesser contribution in the SHAP analysis.

The waste products like WFS/WPS have minimal effect, and SHAP values are concentrated around the null (± 35). This is explained by the fact that they have predominantly a physical nature that they act as fillers, enhancing particle packing and frictional resistance and they do not play an important role in chemical bonding. Consequently, their influence on strength is indirect and is less intensive than on chemically active binders. The SHAP-based feature importance is compliant with known geotechnical principles, which is why parameters that control chemical reactions and time-dependent cementation (CP and binders) are predominant in the strength behavior, whereas the impact of factors related to physical modification (compaction and filler materials) has a relatively minor role. Such consistency increases the predictability and reliability of the predictive model developed to be used in actual geotechnical practices. The consistency of SHAP-derived feature importance with established geotechnical principles further validates that the model predictions are physically meaningful. This alignment between data-driven insights and domain knowledge reduces the likelihood of overfitting and enhances the interpretability and reliability of the proposed framework. The agreement between SHAP-derived feature contributions and known soil behavior further indicates that the model is not influenced by redundant correlations but instead learns physically meaningful relationships governing strength development. It is noted that SHAP-based interpretations are used to support physical understanding and do not imply direct causal relationships, and further validation through experimental or field studies is recommended.

Sensitivity analysis

The sensitivity analysis (Fig. 10) was carried out to measure the relative impact of each input factor on the UCS prediction in accordance with the model outputs standard deviation. The findings suggest that curing period (CP) is the most significant parameter and the sensitivity measure of the parameter is greatest (31.21), then the soil content (26.00) and DFS (8.17). The prevalence of CP emphasizes the importance of pozzolanic and hydration reaction, which occurs over a period of time, and majorly improves the strength due to the development of cementitious compounds. The fact that soil content and DFS are very sensitive, indicates the manner in which plasticity and swell properties play an innate role in the strength behavior wherein highly expansive soils are more likely to have lower UCS. Moderate sensitivity can be seen in the case of MDD (2.84) and LL (2.76), which shows that curing and consistency limits are secondary to the development of strength, when compared to curing and soil composition. Conversely BA, PL, OMC and WFS (1.86, 1.73, 1.17 and 0.81 respectively) are relatively low sensitivity parameters, indicating that they have a low direct influence on UCS. This relative weakness of WFS (or WPS) can be attributed in part to the fact that it is not a chemically reactive stabilizer but a physical modifier, the effect of which is primarily due to better particle packing and micro-structural alterations and not necessarily to the increase in strength.

Monotonicity analysis

The monotonicity analysis also gives the information on the directional relationship between input variables and UCS (Fig. 11). There is an evident positive monotonic relationship between CP (r = 0.63), which proves the hypothesis that UCS is continuously growing as the curing time goes up as a result of gradual cementation. Likewise, OMC (r = 0.59) tends to be on a generally increasing trend, which implies that sufficient moisture enables the compaction and pozzolanic reactions but the curve is a bit nonlinear because there is an optimum moisture range. Conversely, there are negative monotonic correlations between soil content (r = −0.69), DFS (r = −0.69), LL (r = −0.68), PL (r = −0.64), and MDD (r = −0.67) and UCS, which shows that an increase in plasticity, swelling potential, and some density conditions have an adverse effect on UCS. These are trends that are in line with the geotechnics requirements where expansive clay properties lower the strength unless efficiently stipulated. The adverse association between MDD can be explained by the differences in the states of compaction, and the composition of materials included in the dataset. Other parameters, like WFS (r = 0.49) and BA (r = 0.25) are weak and non-monotonic, which means that their effect on UCS is not linear and is not directly proportional to them, but rather based on their interaction. This will imply that they are affected by other factors like the content of the binder and curing conditions, which further shows that the development of sophisticated models is required to capture this kind of intricate relationships.

The existence of non-monotonic trends is also a reflection of the nonlinear and coupled nature of FA-WPS stabilization and the reason behind why ensemble machine learning models should be utilized to obtain the precise prediction of UCS.
