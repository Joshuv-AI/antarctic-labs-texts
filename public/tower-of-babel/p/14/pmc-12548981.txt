A Utility of Volume Expander Plasma in Nonhuman Primate Transplantation Models
Authors: Genki Watanabe, Izumi Yamamoto, Maiko Horikawa, Takashi Yokoo, Eiji Kobayashi
Published: 2025

We read with great interest the article entitled “Allogeneic Liver Transplantation in Nonhuman Primates: surgical technique with stable postoperative outcomes.” published in Transplantation Direct.1 This comprehensive and insightful review addresses key immunological, surgical, and perioperative challenges in nonhuman primate liver transplantation and provides a valuable reference for researchers in the field.

In the study, 3 cynomolgus monkeys (Macaca fascicularis) and 5 baboons were used. The authors noted that cynomolgus monkeys were unable to tolerate surgical complications, highlighting a significant limitation of this species. Although Macaca fascicularis is immunologically similar to humans and useful for assessing immunosuppressive agents, their small body size limits their applicability in transplant models. Notably, they are highly sensitive to intraoperative bleeding, and even minor hemorrhage can cause severe instability. Thus, meticulous hemostatic control and fluid management are essential when using this model.

Baboons’ greater resistance to surgical stress and relatively lower levels of natural antipig antibodies in some individuals make them attractive for xenotransplantation research. However, their broader use is restricted because of ethical concerns, aggressive temperament, and high maintenance costs.

In this context, we would like to share a supplementary perspective based on our recent study published in the journal Acta Cirúrgica Brasileira, in which we transplanted fetal pig kidneys into cynomolgus monkeys as part of a xenotransplantation protocol.2 To prevent early rejection and reduce anti-donor antibodies, we employed double-filtration plasmapheresis (DFPP), using Voluven (6% hydroxyethyl starch [HES]) as the replacement fluid. To safely perform DFPP, we collected blood in advance from another monkey. A total of 474 mL was drawn, including 114 mL of whole blood and 360 mL for combined whole blood and plasma collection.

Although 3% HES solutions such as Varihes or Isohes are commonly used in plasma exchange, they are associated with concerns about fibrinogen depletion and bleeding. However, current evidence indicates that fibrinogen loss and adverse events are minimal and rare.3,4 Based on these findings, we adopted 6% HES for both priming and replacement. We established a reproducible DFPP procedure in cynomolgus monkeys (3–9.2 kg), which maintained stable hemodynamics and minimized coagulation factor loss. Although our procedure was well tolerated, nevertheless, it has been reported that HES can increase the risk of acute kidney injury and the need for renal replacement therapy in critically ill patients.5 In our experimental subjects, renal function was carefully monitored, and no evidence of acute kidney injury or deterioration attributable to HES administration was observed.

Voluven offers several benefits over plasma or albumin, including reduced risks of infection and transfusion-related complications. It may also help attenuate perioperative cytokine release. We believe that synthetic colloids such as Voluven may serve as effective adjuncts in transplant protocols, particularly in small primate models where bleeding remains a major concern.

We thank the authors for their excellent contribution and hope that our findings will contribute to ongoing discussions regarding innovative perioperative approaches—such as Voluven-based plasma exchange—to enhance outcomes in both allogeneic and xenogeneic transplantation.
