A simple algebraic expression can determine if a drug’s clearance is non-specific or target-mediated
Authors: Liam V Brown, Alberto Ippolito, Mark Penney, Rhys DO Jones
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTarget-mediated drug distribution (TMDD) is typically classified by accelerated removal of drug after serum concentration drops below a critical level. Susceptible drugs would be dosed to a schedule that maintains exposure above this level, where concentration drops monoexponentially. Out-dosing TMDD often implies that target binding is saturated or degraded. T-cell engagers and other biologics are often given with low dosages which make out-dosing TMDD unrealistic, yet are also typically observed to have monoexponential elimination. It can be challenging to determine whether such a concentration-time relationship is due to TMDD or standard non-specific (intrinsic) routes of clearance, and more so to scale this clearance from preclinical settings, as TMDD-driven clearance may not scale allometrically like intrinsic clearance. In this work, we derived an algebraic expression for the proportion of clearance that is target-mediated or intrinsic, which requires only the intrinsic half-life and the target receptor’s concentration, turnover rate and binding affinity for the drug. We further show that the typical transition from monoexponential decline of serum concentrations to accelerated, target-driven clearance occurs at concentrations given by two expressions in those same parameters. We apply the equations to published data for monoclonal antibodies and T-cell engagers, finding that we consistently characterize their clearance as expected. These expressions can be used to determine if a drug’s clearance would be expected to be driven by target-mediated or non-specific routes at pharmacologically active concentrations, and hence how to accurately translate pharmacokinetic properties from preclinical species to human, a process that is essential for drug development.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10928-026-10043-w.

Introduction

T-cell engagers (TCEs) and other immune engagers have seen increasing interest as oncolytic drugs in recent years, with several now approved and on the market for haematological and solid tumour indications [1, 2]. TCEs are antibodies or other biologic molecules that are bispecific for a receptor on T-cells (such as CD3) and for a target receptor – in oncology, a tumour-associated antigen (TAA) – such as CD20. The clearance and half-lives of monoclonal antibodies are typically determined by FcRn recycling and non-specific degradation (hereafter referred to as “intrinsic clearance”), the rate of which depends on bodyweight and can be scaled allometrically between species. An example of a therapeutic use for monoclonal antibodies is to competitively block binding by another molecule to a receptor or to mark every cell that expresses that receptor for attack by the innate immune system. In this scenario, sufficiently high dosages of the antibody are given that the target is saturated and the pharmacokinetics (PK) of the free antibody is a monoexponential decay (which is a straight line on a log-scaled plot), dependent on intrinsic clearance. At low serum concentrations, target-mediated drug distribution (TMDD) becomes important, which may extend the antibody’s half-life as it is shielded from clearance, or may reduce it as the receptor turns over and destroys the bound antibody. The result of this is a characteristic interruption of the monoexponential clearance at low concentrations. TCEs are often given at low dosages, as immune-related toxic side effects such as cytokine release syndrome become more probable as dosages increase. In this scenario, it is not guaranteed that TMDD can be outdosed and so the pharmacokinetic properties of TCEs depend on their interactions with receptors on cells of the immune system. When a typical immune engager is dosed into an immunocompromised mouse, they usually exhibit antibody-like half-lives of several days, but this usually drops to an order of hours if T-cells are introduced to the animal (for example [3]), because of the turnover rates of the receptors that the engager binds to. How these interactions differ between species is essential to determine how an immune engaging drug will clear in different species, and the simple allometric (bodyweight-dependent) scaling of intrinsic clearance may not apply. The typical monoexponential PK seen for a molecule cleared intrinsically is also commonly observed for TCEs doses at low concentrations where TMDD would be expected. In order to choose the correct method to scale the PK of TCEs between species, understanding which processes are responsible for their clearance at pharmacologically active concentrations is critical.

The first model of target-mediated distribution was proposed by Mager and Jusko [4], and Peletier and Gabrielsson have previously published works [5, 6] that illustrate how the clearance of a compound passes between two regions of mono-exponential decay interrupted by a multi-exponential region, and how receptor turnover rates give rise to an “in vivo potency” that better reflects the in vivo activity of a molecule than in vitro methods. This work goes further to show how immune engagers can appear to have entirely monoexponential decay that is nonetheless governed by TMDD, and how one can predict if serum concentration data for a compound is exhibiting this.

We constructed a simple ordinary differential equation (ODE) system that implements both intrinsic and target-mediated clearance, and used it to investigate under which conditions intrinsic clearance dominates the PK of a compound and which conditions target-mediated clearance dominates instead, even when the compound’s PK looks like a simple monoexponential decay. We derived a simple expression to quantify the percentage of clearance that is intrinsic, and simple inequalities that indicate whether monoexponential-looking PK is driven by intrinsic clearance or by TMDD. The most important of these results are summarised in Table 1. We used the PK of several published monoclonal antibodies and T-cell engagers to validate these expressions. These expressions can simplify the analysis of PKPD (pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic) data by DMPK and clinical pharmacology teams, by helping them to predict which clearance mechanism dominates and thus which should be considered for scaling the PK from pre-clinical species to human, and ultimately help to produce more accurate predictions of human dosage and drug half-lives.

Effect of trimeric binding and multiple compartments

The analyses above assumed a single target for the ligand, in a single compartment. T-cell engagers, which much of the results and discussion focus on, can target at least two receptors (CD3, CD8 or similar on T-cells, and a receptor on target cells). These targets are often not in serum; T-cells are typically in secondary lymphoid tissues [9, 10] and for oncology solid tumour antigen are present in tumour lesions (or healthy tissue) by definition. The impact of including trimeric binding is explored in supplementary section A.3, in which we derive expressions for the half life due to trimer-mediated drug distribution, and a “trimeric affinity” at which trimer binding is half-maximum and acts as a switch for trimer-driven drug distribution much like \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_\textrm{D}$$\end{document} is for dimeric TMDD.

Additionally, the impact of including multiple pharmacokinetic compartments is explored in supplementary section A.4. In summary, if there are two compartments, inter-compartment transfer is faster than clearance and drug is cleared in only one of them at rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_\textrm{el}$$\end{document}, then the overall clearance rate from the body is multiplied by a fractional volume. If the drug is cleared in the central compartment, the overall clearance rate is multiplied by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{V_c}{V_c+V_p}$$\end{document}, and if the drug is cleared in the peripheral compartment, the overall clearance rate is multiplied by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{V_p}{V_c+V_p}$$\end{document}.

Results

To motivate the use of the expression for the proportion of clearance that is intrinsic (Eq. 5), we first explore simple and intuitive results that show the relationship between intrinsic or TMDD-driven clearance and model parameters. Finally, we apply the expression to T-cell engager and monoclonal antibodies.

Application to T-cell engagers and monoclonal antibodies

We applied Eq. 5 to known parameter values for several published T-cell engagers and monoclonal antibodies to confirm that they give the expected classifications. The results are shown in Table 2. For each compound, we collected an example of clinical PK from the literature, except for Glofitamab, for which we obtained cyno PK. For each compound, we identified the maximum observed Cmax concentration for the largest reported dose. We assumed that the intrinsic half-life is 15 days for human data and 10 days for cyno. We calculated the percentage of clearance that is intrinsic when ligand concentration is equal to that Cmax, 1nM or 1000nM, the terminal TMDD-driven half-life defined by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{k_\textrm{e}R_\textrm{tot}}{k_\textrm{D}+R_\textrm{tot}}$$\end{document}, and the ligand concentration above which clearance is expected to be 50% intrinsic or more. As expected, TCEs generally have a low predicted clearance intrinsic at the Cmax concentration and monoclonal antibodies have a high predicted percentage, and several of the monoclonal antibodies are predicted to have intrinsic-driven clearance for all concentrations (that is, for ligand concentrations above a negative value).

The PK found for those TCEs and monoclonal antibodies is also plotted in Figs. 4 and 5. As in Section “Time course of ligand clearance”, the two expressions that define a change in clearance regime, Eq. 7 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_\textrm{free}=k_\textrm{D}+k_\textrm{I}$$\end{document}, are plotted horizontally in each panel for comparison.

Discussion

A simple experiment shows that target mediated distribution (TMDD) is key for T-cell engagers: their half-lives are usually longer in immunodeficient mice compared to ones that have been engrafted or produce their own peripheral blood mononucleocytes (PBMCs). This could be due to binding, sequestering, or internalisation. For monoclonal antibodies and small molecules, the usual understanding of TMDD is that it is a level below which clearance rapidly accelerates due to one of these effects, and one aims to out-dose this effect. For T-cell engagers, out-dosing the TMDD may be inappropriate, because ligating all CD3 receptors for many TCEs may drive toxicity from high T-cell activation and cytokine release. Moreover, the overall concentration of CD3 receptors is small, of order 0.1nM if there are \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^9$$\end{document} CD3+ cells per litre and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$10^5$$\end{document} CD3 receptors per cell. Out-dosing this small concentration should be trivial, and yet the clearance of T-cell engagers is typically dominated by TMDD, even in the event of poor binding affinities that would indicate that most ligand should be free. We show that this can be trivially explained by fast internalisation of a small number of receptors: the switch from an intrinsic-clearance dominated regime to a TMDD-dominated one depends on the quantity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{k_{\textrm{e}}}{k_{\textrm{clear}}}R_{\textrm{tot}}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{e}}$$\end{document} is the receptor turnover rate, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{clear}}$$\end{document} is the intrinsic clearance rate, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\textrm{tot}}$$\end{document} is the total receptor count. Even for small \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\textrm{tot}}$$\end{document}, a large value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{e}}$$\end{document} can result in significant TMDD. This expression can be used even if receptor internalisation is expected to deplete receptor expression, by letting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\textrm{tot}}(t) = R_{\textrm{tot}} e^{-k_{\textrm{e}} t}$$\end{document}.

Peletier and Gabrielsson have previously illustrated [5] the characteristic ligand concentration versus time graph in which two mono-exponential clearance regions are separated by a mixed-order region. We have extended this work to show that the top and bottom of those regions can be related to known target parameters, and to consider the consequences of this for T-cell engagers and the scaling of drug clearance mechanisms from pre-clinical models to the clinic. Specifically, drugs that can be shown to be driven by intrinsic clearance can be scaled allometrically, but those whose clearance is driven by TMDD will need to have their clearance scaled by adjustment of known target parameters. In these circumstances, typical allometric scaling would give an incorrect result, as explored by Penney et al. [11]. Peletier and Gabrielsson have also published [6] the concept of an “in vivo potency”, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{\textrm{50}}$$\end{document}, defined as the serum concentration at which receptor binding reaches 50% of its maximum in the presence of receptor turnover and other dynamic processes. Adjusting our assumptions recovers this result as the lower part of the TMDD “wobble” on PK plots (Fig. 3), verifying the importance of this concept.

In practice, use of Eq. 7 and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{D}}+k_{\textrm{I}}$$\end{document} to determine if a molecule is likely to be cleared by TMDD strongly depends on one’s knowledge of the binding affinity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{D}}$$\end{document}, receptor count \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\textrm{tot}}$$\end{document}, complex elimination rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_\textrm{e}$$\end{document} and binding rate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{on}}$$\end{document}. The expressions can be formed without assumptions and they line up exactly with theoretical curves of ligand clearance in Fig. 3. However, in Fig. 4, the majority of the observed PK time courses for the TCE molecules do not have an obvious change in their monoexponential decay that cannot also be attributed to redistribution to a peripheral compartment. The ligand concentrations at which such shifts might be expected are annotated, but do not correspond to any notable features in the data. This may be because in all cases, the affinity for their target(s) is near or above all serum concentrations, and so the entire time course might be in a TMDD regime, in which case this would be a positive result. It might also be because of uncertainty or variance in parameter values that have been obtained from literature. For an example of an uncertain parameter, CD20 is said to have a fast turnover when bound to some antibodies, internalising complexes within a few hours. For others, CD20 is said to form long-lasting complexes [12]. Similarly, the number per cell of some receptors such as PD1 are highly variable, particularly if they are self-regulatory or inflammatory receptors. In these cases, selecting a single value for such a receptor is challenging. IL1R1 is reported to vary between 40 and 350 molecules per T-cell before and after stimulation [13], but for the apparent drop-off in AMG108’s PK in Fig. 5 at 100nM to correspond to the concentration of T-cell IL1R1, there would need to be 1000 per cell.

An alternative reason that figure annotations might not line up with features of the PK data is how we defined the lines at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_\textrm{free} = k_{\textrm{D}} + k_{\textrm{I}}$$\end{document} to signify 50% receptor occupancy. We assumed that receptor turnover and complex elimination rates are equal, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{deg}}=k_{\textrm{e}}$$\end{document}, as sourcing independent parameter values for each is typically challenging. Relaxing this assumption would have this concentration be \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{\textrm{free}} = L_{50}$$\end{document} from Gabrielsson et al. [6], where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{50} = \frac{k_{\textrm{deg}}}{k_\textrm{e}}\left( k_{\textrm{D}}+k_{\textrm{I}}\right)$$\end{document}. We thus might expect monoexponential TMDD-driven clearance to occur at a higher concentration or at a lower concentration than plotted, depending on the value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{e}}$$\end{document}. The use of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_\textrm{I}$$\end{document} itself also causes a problem in that a suitable value for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{on}}$$\end{document} must be selected, unlike for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{D}}$$\end{document}, which is measured independently of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{on}}$$\end{document}. In all cases, we chose a reasonable value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{on}}=5\textrm{nM}^{-1}\textrm{h}^{-1}=1.4\times 10^6\textrm{M}^{-1}\textrm{s}^{-1}$$\end{document}, but the values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_\textrm{I}$$\end{document} depend directly on this assumption and so are, in effect, arbitrary but for their relative values. Our choice of a single value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_\textrm{on}$$\end{document} might give another reason for a lack of meaning in the positions of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{D}}+k_{\textrm{I}}$$\end{document} on the TCE PK plots, and for this reason, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_{\textrm{D}}$$\end{document} was also plotted alone on each plot.

The monoclonal antibodies whose PK we presented include those with membrane-bound targets and with soluble targets. It might be expected that the mAbs with soluble targets would not have their PK meaningfully altered by being bound to their targets, but we presented the results of the same analysis on mAbs of both types of target. Anifrolumab and Sifalimumab both target molecules of the same interaction: IFNAR1 and IFNa, respectively. It might be expected that turnover is important for the former, but not the latter, in which case one would expect not to see a mixed-order TMDD phase, as the top of this phase is determined by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_\textrm{e}$$\end{document}. Indeed, Anifrolumab appears to exhibit accelerated PK typical of TMDD and Sifalimumab does not, Anifrolumab is the only molecule for which the target and turnover rate are sufficiently high for a top of the TMDD curve to be determined, and the value is above the point at which Anifrolumab’s serum concentration appears to display TMDD.

Several phenomena could impact the apparent TMDD-driven half-lives that were derived in this work. Two compartments: If the PK is best described by a two-compartment model and elimination only occurs in one of them, then the apparent half-life is changed as described in supplementary section A.4. This would not change the concentrations at which TMDD is expected to dominate clearance.

Trimeric binding: T-cell engagers and other bispecific molecules bind to two targets simultaneously, which may impact the PKPD of the molecule, but in this work we considered the targets to be independent. In supplementary section A.3, A “trimeric affinity” that is lower than the lowest of the two single-target affinities was identified, below which a bispecific molecule is predicted to exhibit mono-exponential TMDD much like \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k_\textrm{D}$$\end{document} does for a molecule with single target. We also derived a half-life due to trimer-mediated drug distribution which can be dramatically lower than the values calculated for independent receptor engagement. However, these results both depend strongly on the avidity factor that modifies the apparent affinity of the second binding step, and whose value is generally unknown. Moreover, the elimination rate of trimers may not trivially depend on the elimination rates of individual dimers. We assumed that it was the sum of both elimination rates, but it could be faster or slower in reality. TCEs rapidly deplete their target, particularly if the target is haematological, and so the trimer-driven half-life may not be representative of the terminal phase of the drug’s clearance. Indeed, the trimer-driven half-lives found in Table 4 are much faster than the actual observed half-lives, which are often closer to the half-lives predicted from CD3-driven clearance only. The results of this work are intended to give modellers a “rule of thumb” to determine whether a drug’s clearance at a particular concentration(s) exhibit TMDD, and which targets are responsible. The additional unknowns that trimeric binding brings may hinder rather than help this effort. For these reasons, we present the expressions obtained from considering trimeric binding in Table 1 alongside other important results, but focus on results obtained from assuming targets are bound independently by the T-cell engagers, and leave trimer analysis to the appendix. We have previously shown for several example TCEs that considering only dimeric binding leads to more accurate prediction of clinical half-lives from pre-clinical data than allometric scaling does [11], and adding trimeric binding had negligible effect for those examples. However, we invite the reader to use whichever expression is most useful to them.

Parameter uncertainty aside, the expressions derived in this work have worked well as a rule-of-thumb. All of the monoclonal antibodies were predicted to have clearance driven by intrinsic routes, with Anifrolumab the only one to move to a TMDD regime, as suggested by the shape of the serum data. All of the TCEs were predicted to have clearance driven at least partially by TMDD at or below the reported Cmax for the largest dose considered here. As a method to determine whether allometric scaling should be used to predict the human half-life of a compound or whether other methods should be considered, this method is shown to be fit for purpose.

Conclusions

T-cell engagers and other biologics can exhibit mono-exponential clearance that is due to target-mediated drug distribution. When faced with mono-exponential clearance it could be assumed by the unwary that the half-life of the compound would scale by bodyweight from pre-clinical species to clinical species. However, this is not the case for TMDD-driven clearance. In this work we show that two simple algebraic expressions that depend on the binding affinity, target receptor concentration and turnover rate can indicate whether the clearance of a compound is target-mediated or not, and show that the expression yields consistent results for a range of T-cell engagers and monoclonal antibodies published in the literature. This method allows DMPK and clinical pharmacology modellers to determine with much greater certainty the mechanism that governs the clearance of biological compounds such as T-cell engagers, in turn allowing the selection of appropriate scaling methods and for more accurate determination of clinical pharmacokinetics.
