Efficacy and safety of oral semaglutide in Chinese participants with type 2 diabetes: Subgroup analyses by baseline characteristics in the PIONEER 11 and 12 randomised controlled trials
Authors: Linong Ji, Guoyue Yuan, Jun Liu, Bingjie Zhang, Wenyan Liu, Zewei Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThis post hoc analysis investigates the impact of baseline characteristics on the efficacy and safety of oral semaglutide in a Chinese population with type 2 diabetes.Materials and MethodsChinese participants from the PIONEER 11 and 12 trials with type 2 diabetes aged ≥18 years were randomised to once‐daily oral semaglutide 3, 7, or 14 mg, or placebo (PIONEER 11) or sitagliptin 100 mg (PIONEER 12). Changes from baseline in HbA1c and body weight at week 26 were analysed in subgroups according to the following baseline characteristics: age, HbA1c, body mass index, duration of diabetes, and presence of gastrointestinal disease. Adverse events (AEs) by baseline subgroups were also assessed.ResultsIn total, 390 (PIONEER 11) and 1084 (PIONEER 12) Chinese participants with type 2 diabetes were evaluated. Most baseline characteristics were similar across the two trials in the different subgroups. Overall, greater reductions in HbA1c and body weight were observed with oral semaglutide versus comparators across the subgroups. A significant difference was observed for change in HbA1c by baseline HbA1c subgroups (p int <0.05), with greater reductions observed in the subgroups with higher baseline HbA1c. Most AEs were gastrointestinal‐related and mild‐to‐moderate.Conclusions/InterpretationOral semaglutide reduces HbA1c and body weight across a range of baseline subgroups in a Chinese population with type 2 diabetes without any unexpected additional safety concerns. These results support the use of oral semaglutide in a broad population in clinical practice.Keywords: body weight, GLP‐1RA, HbA1c , oral semaglutide, safety, type 2 diabetes

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 diabetes is a complex and progressive condition associated with multiple comorbidities, with the most frequent being cardiovascular and kidney diseases. 1 , 2 The risk for developing these comorbidities and associated increased mortality can be reduced by early and adequate glycaemic control. 2 , 3 Efficient management of type 2 diabetes is especially important because the incidence of diabetes globally and in China has rapidly increased over the past three decades. As of 2021, approximately 141 million people in China are living with diabetes, and this number is projected to rise to 174 million by 2045. 4

In people from East Asia, such as those from China, the aetiology of type 2 diabetes is characterised by early beta‐cell dysfunction rather than insulin resistance, as for type 2 diabetes aetiology in people from Western countries. 5 , 6 , 7 Furthermore, Chinese people with type 2 diabetes typically have higher insulin sensitivity, greater adiposity or visceral fat, and a lower body mass index (BMI) compared with global populations with type 2 diabetes, 8 , 9 as well as a higher risk of developing kidney complications and a greater predisposition to stroke. 9 The distinct differences in clinical phenotype and pathophysiology of type 2 diabetes identified in East Asian populations can impact therapeutic approaches and responses to treatments; therefore, adjustments to treatment strategies for people from specific regions and on an individual basis should be considered. 5 , 7 , 8

Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RAs) have been shown to enable reductions in both HbA1c and body weight in people with type 2 diabetes, and are recommended by the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and the European Association for the Studies of Diabetes (EASD) for the treatment of type 2 diabetes. 10 , 11 In addition, for people with type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease (CVD) or chronic kidney disease, GLP‐1RAs are recommended independent of metformin use, which has been traditionally recommended as first‐line therapy. 10 The most recent Chinese guidelines for the management of type 2 diabetes currently recommend the use of GLP‐1RAs or sodium–glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors as first‐line treatment, regardless of the glycaemic target, in people with type 2 diabetes and established atherosclerotic CVD or in those with high CVD risk, unless contraindicated. 12

Once‐weekly subcutaneous semaglutide 0.5 mg and 1.0 mg is a GLP‐1RA approved for the treatment of type 2 diabetes in several countries, including China 13 ; it is also available as an oral formulation for type 2 diabetes. 14 The efficacy and safety of three doses of oral semaglutide (3, 7, and 14 mg) have been investigated in people with type 2 diabetes in the global phase 3a Peptide InnOvatioN for Early diabEtes tReatment (PIONEER) programme, which encompassed a broad range of clinical settings, including various diabetes durations, co‐medications, comorbidities, and populations 15 ; the efficacy and safety outcomes of oral semaglutide were tested versus placebo and/or comparators in this programme. 15

In the PIONEER 11 and the PIONEER 12 trials, approximately 75% of enrolled participants were from China. 16 , 17 Although the efficacy and safety of oral semaglutide have been shown in the overall populations of the PIONEER 11 and 12 trials, it is important to understand that individual responses to treatment may vary based on demographic and baseline clinical characteristics. Therefore, in order to guide clinical practice in China, these exploratory analyses of the PIONEER 11 and 12 trials aimed to investigate the efficacy and safety of oral semaglutide in subgroups of Chinese people with type 2 diabetes.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and participants

PIONEER 11 (NCT04109547) was a double‐blind, randomised, phase 3a trial conducted at 52 sites in the China region (mainland China and Taiwan), Hungary, Serbia, and Ukraine, and included participants ≥18 years (≥20 years in Taiwan) with type 2 diabetes and HbA1c 7.0%–10.0%. Once‐daily oral semaglutide (3, 7, and 14 mg) was compared with placebo in participants with type 2 diabetes who were inadequately controlled with diet and exercise only.

PIONEER 12 (NCT04017832) was a randomised, double‐dummy, active‐controlled parallel‐group phase 3a trial conducted at 90 sites across the China region (including mainland China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong) and five other countries (Brazil, the Czech Republic, Romania, Serbia, and South Africa). Participants aged ≥18 years (≥20 years in Taiwan) with uncontrolled type 2 diabetes who had been on a stable dose of metformin (≥1500 mg or the maximum tolerated dose) and HbA1c 7.0%–10.5% were included in the study. Oral semaglutide (3, 7, and 14 mg) was compared with once‐daily sitagliptin 100 mg, both in combination with metformin.

The primary endpoint of both trials was the change in HbA1c from baseline to week 26. Key secondary endpoints were change in body weight from baseline to week 26, as well as proportions of participants achieving treatment targets. Safety was assessed via adverse events (AEs). The full methods and eligibility criteria for PIONEER 11 and PIONEER 12 have been previously published elsewhere. 16 , 17

Both trials were conducted in accordance with International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice guidelines, the Declaration of Helsinki, and applicable regulatory requirements. The trial protocols were approved by local independent ethics committees and institutional review boards at each trial site. Participants were required to provide written informed consent prior to any trial‐related activities taking place.

Subgroup analyses

Exploratory subgroup analyses using data from the PIONEER 11 and 12 trials were conducted post hoc and included participants with type 2 diabetes who were enrolled from mainland China, Hong Kong, and Taiwan. As per the prescribing information, the oral semaglutide 3 mg dose was the initiation dose, while the oral semaglutide 7 and 14 mg doses were the treatment or maintenance doses and were used for the current analyses.

Subgroups of the included populations were defined based on baseline age (<45, ≥45 to <60, and ≥60 years), BMI (<24 [normal weight], ≥24 to <28 [overweight], ≥28 kg/m2 [obesity]) as defined per Chinese BMI classification, 18 duration of diabetes (<0.5, ≥0.5 to <3, and ≥3 years [PIONEER 11], <3, ≥3 to <10, and ≥10 years [PIONEER 12]), HbA1c (≤7.5%, >7.5% to ≤8.5%, and >8.5%) and gastrointestinal (GI) disease status (with or without). In accordance with the classification in the PIONEER studies, GI diseases were defined as gastroesophageal reflux disease, chronic gastritis, gastric ulcer, and gastroparesis.

Endpoints and assessments

The endpoints assessed in this subgroup analyses were change from baseline to week 26 in HbA1c and body weight. Safety assessments included collection of AEs, treatment discontinuation due to AEs, serious AEs (SAEs) and gastrointestinal‐related adverse events, coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities.

Additional analyses were performed to evaluate whether there was a linear correlation between baseline body weight and change in HbA1c, utilising Pearson correlation coefficients.

Statistical analyses

Efficacy analyses were based on data from all Chinese participants within the full analysis set, which included all randomised Chinese study participants. Treatment effects were assessed using the trial product estimand, defined as the treatment effect for all randomly assigned participants, under the assumption that all participants remained on study treatment (oral semaglutide, placebo, or sitagliptin) for the duration of the trial and did not use rescue medication.

Efficacy data on the changes from baseline in HbA1c and body weight were analysed using a mixed model for repeated measurements (MMRM), with treatment, subgroup, and interaction of treatment and subgroup as categorical fixed factors and baseline value as a covariate, all nested within visit. Using the MMRM model, estimated treatment differences (ETD) between oral semaglutide doses 7 and 14 mg, and comparators at week 26 were reported, along with corresponding two‐sided 95% confidence intervals. The p value for interaction was calculated to assess whether the estimated treatment differences between oral semaglutide and the control group vary significantly across different baseline characteristics. A p value for interaction less than 0.05 would indicate that the treatment effect is significantly modified by these baseline characteristics.

Safety assessments were based on the safety analysis set, which included all study participants who were exposed to at least one dose of the trial product. Categorical safety endpoints were summarised as counts and relative frequencies, and additional safety endpoints were analysed descriptively.

Analyses were performed with SAS software (version 9.4).

Effect of oral semaglutide on HbA1c

Oral semaglutide reduced HbA1c across all baseline subgroups versus comparators (Figures 1 and 2). In both PIONEER 11 and 12 trials, HbA1c reductions were greater in the higher baseline HbA1c subgroups with oral semaglutide 7 mg and 14 mg, relative to the other subgroups and versus comparators (Figures 1 and 2).

In both trials, no statistically significant treatment‐by‐subgroup interactions were observed for baseline age, BMI, diabetes duration, or GI disease subgroups for change in HbA1c between oral semaglutide and the comparator (p int > 0.05). In contrast, the treatment‐by‐subgroup interactions between baseline HbA1c and the change in HbA1c were statistically significant for oral semaglutide versus the comparators in both trials (p int < 0.05; Figures 1 and 2).

The additional analysis assessing potential correlations between baseline weight and change in HbA1c from baseline in any treatment arm across both PIONEER 11 and 12 showed that there was no correlation (p > 0.05; Figure S1).

Effect of oral semaglutide on body weight

Oral semaglutide generally reduced body weight in the subgroups from baseline to week 26 with oral semaglutide 7 mg and 14 mg versus comparators in both PIONEER 11 and 12 (p int > 0.05; Figures 3 and 4). The exception to this was the change in body weight at week 26 with oral semaglutide 7 mg in the >8.5% HbA1c subgroup, in whichthe estimated treatment difference favoured placebo; although this difference was not statistically significant (PIONEER 11; Figure 3). Across the baseline BMI subgroups, greater reductions in body weight were generally observed with oral semaglutide 7 mg and 14 mg compared with placebo and sitagliptin 100 mg in PIONEER 11 and 12, respectively (Figures 3 and 4).

In both trials, there appeared to be some variation in the change from baseline in body weight versus comparators, but there was no significant treatment‐by‐subgroup interaction for any of the subgroups (p int > 0.05; Figures 3 and 4).

Safety

Most AEs observed with oral semaglutide were GI‐related, and mild‐to‐moderate in severity, with serious AEs being infrequent, generally occurring in ≤10% of participants in the PIONEER 11 trial and ≤5% of participants in PIONEER 12 (Figures S2 and S3; Tables S2 and S3). No statistically significant differences were observed in GI AE incidence across the baseline subgroups for oral semaglutide groups (Table S4).

In PIONEER 11, treatment discontinuation rates due to AEs were similar with oral semaglutide 7 mg and 14 mg compared with placebo. In PIONEER 12, however, treatment discontinuation rates were generally higher in the oral semaglutide groups versus the sitagliptin 100 mg group (Table S5; Figures S2 and S3). In both studies, most AEs leading to treatment discontinuation were GI AEs and occurred during the dose escalation phase.

DISCUSSION

In China, people with type 2 diabetes typically have a lower BMI compared with global populations; therefore, insights into the effects of diabetes treatments on HbA1c and body weight in subgroups of people with specific baseline characteristics, such as a lower BMI, are important when considering therapy options. 8 The results from this post hoc analysis showed that the reductions in HbA1c and body weight with oral semaglutide 7 mg and 14 mg versus comparators in Chinese participants with type 2 diabetes were generally not influenced by age, HbA1c, BMI, or diabetes duration at baseline in the PIONEER 11 and 12 trials. This correlation analysis supports the fact that change in HbA1c with oral semaglutide is independent of baseline body weight in people with type 2 diabetes. Although corresponding analyses are not available for the global PIONEER trials, an analysis of once‐weekly subcutaneous semaglutide in people with type 2 diabetes also found no difference in treatment effect on HbA1c change across subgroups by baseline BMI among Chinese participants. 19 Furthermore, given GI‐related symptoms are reported in approximately 20% of Chinese people with type 2 diabetes, 20 this analysis also demonstrates that the efficacy of oral semaglutide is not diminished by the presence of GI disease at baseline.

Greater reductions in HbA1c were observed in subgroups with the highest baseline HbA1c values for both doses of oral semaglutide versus comparators. This is a known phenomenon because baseline HbA1c levels are often used as a predictor of glycaemic response for many glucose‐lowering treatments, including other GLP‐1RAs such as dulaglutide and liraglutide. 21 , 22 As such, similar greater reductions in HbA1c were also observed for people with higher HbA1c baseline levels in subgroup analyses of the global PIONEER 1–5, 7 and 8 trials, as well as subgroup analyses of the once‐weekly subcutaneous semaglutide SUSTAIN trials. 23 , 24

In this analysis of Chinese participants from PIONEER 11 and PIONEER 12, it was observed that the efficacy of oral semaglutide on body weight reduction was comparable across the baseline BMI subgroups. This absence of a correlation between baseline BMI and weight loss with semaglutide was also observed in a subgroup analysis of once‐weekly subcutaneous semaglutide in the SUSTAIN China study. 19 This warrants further investigation but may be a result of the lower baseline BMI in the Chinese population and the relatively small sample size. It should be noted that in PIONEER 11, a non‐significant weight change favouring placebo was observed for the 7 mg dose in the >8.5% HbA1c subgroup. This is likely to be due to the small sample size and higher baseline weight in the placebo group (78.5 kg) compared with the 7 mg oral semaglutide group (72.9 kg), since weight loss is partly influenced by baseline weight.

A similar number of participants reported SAEs in both PIONEER 11 and 12 across the two baseline GI subgroups, suggesting safety profiles of oral semaglutide in patients with type 2 diabetes with or without previous GI disease may be comparable. Analyses also showed that baseline characteristics did not affect GI AE occurrence. Overall, most AEs in the current population were GI‐related and mild‐to‐moderate in severity, which is consistent with the GLP‐1RA safety profile reported previously. 25 However, it should also be noted that, owing to the small number of participants with GI disease at baseline, these results should be interpreted with caution.

Discontinuation rates were generally higher in PIONEER 12 than PIONEER 11. However, these studies had different study designs and different enrolled populations; therefore, cross‐trial comparisons were not possible. A wide range in discontinuation rates was observed owing to the small sample size.

In China, the use of oral glucose‐lowering drugs is generally preferred over injectable therapies and could possibly be attributed to the mode of administration. 26 By avoiding the need for injection, oral semaglutide has the potential for widespread acceptance, early treatment initiation, and increased adherence in people with type 2 diabetes. 27 However, unlike injectable formulations, oral semaglutide has specific requirements for administration, such as taking on an empty stomach and waiting at least 30 min before eating, drinking, or taking any other oral medicines. Hence, this analysis was intended to explore any impact due to GI disease, which often receives substantial attention from healthcare professionals.

Although this analysis highlights consistent beneficial effects of oral semaglutide in a Chinese population with type 2 diabetes regardless of baseline characteristics, there are a few limitations. Firstly, owing to the post hoc nature of the study, endpoints were not prespecified. This can introduce bias due to an increased risk of Type 1 errors from multiple comparisons and the potential for selective outcome reporting. As a result, the findings should be considered exploratory. Furthermore, participant numbers in some of the baseline subgroups were low, particularly in PIONEER 11, in which most subgroups included fewer than 50 participants. Small sample sizes reduce statistical power and increase the likelihood of failing to detect a true effect. They also limit generalisability, as results may not accurately represent the wider population owing to increased variability and sampling bias. Hence, given the small sample size in certain subgroups, the results should be interpreted with caution, and further research, in particular real‐world evidence, is needed to explore the impact of patient baseline characteristics on treatment outcomes when evaluating the effects of oral semaglutide in people with type 2 diabetes.

In conclusion, the current post hoc analysis of a Chinese subpopulation with type 2 diabetes included in the PIONEER 11 and 12 trials demonstrates that oral semaglutide efficiently reduces HbA1c and body weight across subgroups with variable baseline characteristics. No additional safety concerns were observed. These insights support the broad use of oral semaglutide in clinical practice, including populations with a low baseline BMI or prior GI diseases.
