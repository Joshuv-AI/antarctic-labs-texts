Role-clarity, Attitudes, and Behavioral Control Predict Stakeholders’ Involvement in Work-related Psychosocial Risk Management
Authors: Emma Steel, Katharina Näswall, Sanna K Malinen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlarming numbers of workers report mental health harm caused by work-related factors, necessitating better psychosocial risk (PSR) management practices in workplaces. This study uses the theory of planned behavior as a framework to understand the factors that influence key stakeholders’ level of involvement in work-related PSR management practices.MethodsQuantitative data were collected at two time points (T1 and T2) by surveying participants working in occupational health and safety and related roles in New Zealand. A path analysis was used to assess the effect of perceived behavioral control, perceived organizational norms, and attitudes on behavioral intention and subsequent involvement in PSR management processes. Potential moderators (role-clarity, role-conflict, and access to resources) of the intention–behavior relationship were also assessed.ResultsParticipants’ attitude and perceived behavioral control were significant predictors of their intention to be involved in PSR management practices at T1. Perceived organizational norms did not predict intention. Participants’ intention at T1 was predictive of their level of involvement at T2. Intention mediated the relationship between attitude, perceived behavioral control, and behavior. There were no significant moderation effects acting on the intention–behavior relationship; however, role-clarity had a direct effect on behavior.ConclusionThe results illuminate factors that can be leveraged to support key stakeholders to take an active role in addressing work-related PSRs. This has implications for leaders and organizations wanting to build capability and support people working in occupational health and safety and related roles to manage PSRs.Keywords: Longitudinal, Occupational health and safety, Psychosocial risks, Theory of planned behavior

Introduction

Exposure to work-related psychosocial risks (PSRs) can lead to detrimental mental [1] and physical [2] health outcomes for employees. These risks arise from the nature of how work is designed, social aspects of work, and the physical work environment. There is increasing attention and awareness of the legal obligations requiring that PSRs be addressed under occupational health and safety (OHS) and employment law in many countries. However, despite such obligations, research finds that PSR exposure is prevalent across sectors [3] and that large numbers of workers are exposed to such risks at work [4]. To meet legal obligations and to protect against the negative organizational outcomes of PSR exposure (e.g., turnover intention [5]) organizations need effective methods to address PSRs. OHS risk management systems are utilized to manage work-related risks that have the potential to harm employees. These systems can be utilized to address PSRs [6,7]. The need to integrate PSRs within OHS risk management systems in organizations has been raised by leading authorities (e.g., [8]), and research has evidenced a link between effective general OHS risk management practices and the management of PSRs [9,10]. Many countries now have national-level policy that specifically requires PSRs to be managed within OHS legal frameworks [11,12]. However, research finds that, in practice, a relatively small number of organizations adopt a systematic risk management approach to address PSRs [13], an issue that may be linked to the individualization of PSRs and the focus on workers’ behavior and characteristics as the causes of work-related mental health outcomes rather than organizational determinants [14].

To address these challenges and improve the management of PSRs, recent commentary by Schulte et al [15] has called for an increase in the number and diversity of professionals skilled in preventing and addressing PSRs. The authors point out that there needs to be a more central role of occupational health psychology within the OHS field and that the knowledge base of OHS practitioners ought to include knowledge of PSRs. Better integration of these fields in practice could improve the management of PSRs alongside physical OHS risks, leading to improved mental and physical health outcomes for employees. Empirical research supports this call, with evidence that the availability of specialist OHS assistance is predictive of PSR assessment occurrence [13]. However, there is a need to better understand the experiences of key stakeholders who can provide guidance, subject matter expertise and leadership within organizations for managing PSRs and to identify the factors that may enable their involvement in addressing these risks.

Current research indicates that PSR-related activities are not a central part of OHS practitioners’ activities. For example, only 31% of practitioners in Ireland and the UK undertake PSR assessment and management activities [16]. Further insights from an international survey of OHS professionals found that more than 60% of participants from seven of the countries surveyed did not carry out tasks relating to mental workload and stress [17]. Past research also shows a need for capability development when it comes to PSR management, with 88% of participants in a European Union stakeholder survey agreeing that there is a need to train OHS practitioners to accurately and reliably diagnose the symptoms of work-related stress [18]. To address PSRs, there also is a need for collaboration between the fields of OHS and human resource (HR) management [10] as the psychosocial work environment is inherently linked to organizational culture, leadership, and the social context of work. Therefore, activities typically under the purview of HR such as recruitment, training and development, and performance management are highly relevant to the management of PSRs. However, research conducted by Keegan and Francis [19] found HR practitioners have a diminishing role in employee support and advocacy and have difficulty engaging in discourse around employee well-being. Several scholars have discussed the conflicts HR practitioners face balancing ethical and social considerations relevant to workers with business priorities and have commented on the “dark side” (20 p. 211) of strategic HR management, where professionals may feel pressured to prioritize organizational goals at the expense of employee welfare considerations [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. The need for greater prioritization of employee wellbeing in HR management practice has been noted [24], and involvement in PSR management presents an opportunity for HR practitioners to positively impact the psychosocial work environment.

The present research focuses on improving our understanding of the conditions and factors which support key stakeholders working in OHS and related fields (i.e., employee wellbeing, HR management—hereafter OHS and related roles) to be involved in PSR management processes. We aim to provide information about the organizational conditions needed to support these stakeholders to be involved in PSR management processes and about the individual attributes that enable participants to engage in this work. Given the significant organizational costs associated with work-related mental harm [25,26] and the evidence for improving the integration of PSR management within OHS and HR management practices [10], this is a significant opportunity to better understand how to support key stakeholders to implement and improve PSR management practices in organizations. In the present study, we seek to answer the following questions:

The present study uses the theory of planned behavior [27] (TPB), which provides a structural framework for understanding specific behavioral outcomes. In the present research, the behavioral outcome of interest is the participants’ level of involvement in PSR management processes. We formulate our hypotheses based on the TPB. The key elements of the theory are provided in Fig. 1.

Visual model of the theory of planned behavior [27].

In the TPB, attitude toward the behavior is defined as the degree to which a person has a favorable or unfavorable evaluation or appraisal of the behavior in question [27]. The TPB sets out that attitude is a direct predictor of intention to engage in the specific behavior. Drawing on past qualitative research which finds that “belief in role” shapes stakeholders’ attitudes toward organizational mental health interventions [28], we measure the extent to which participants believe it is part of their roles and responsibilities to be involved with PSR management activities. We hypothesize that:

More positive attitudes toward being involved in PSR management processes will predict stronger intentions to be involved in these processes.

Subjective norms are referred to by Ajzen [27] as the perceived social pressure to perform or not to perform a behavior. Within an organizational setting, leaders have significant influence over the work activities, priorities, and the culture of the organization. A review of the qualitative literature on organizational mental health interventions using the TPB found that the subjective norm component of the TPB was best represented as an “organizational norm” that encompasses the prioritization of mental health within the organization and the level of support from upper management [28]. Considering these findings, this study measures perceptions of senior leadership’s prioritization of psychological well-being to capture perceived organizational norms relevant to our context. We hypothesize that:

More supportive organizational norms will predict stronger intentions to be involved in PSR management processes.

Perceived behavioral control refers to one’s perception of their ability to perform a given behavior. Perceived behavioral control is a latent construct consisting of two components relating to self-efficacy/perceived difficulty and perceived controllability [29,30]. Self-efficacy relates to one’s belief and confidence in one’s ability to execute a given behavior [31], while the perceived controllability component relates to the extent to which performance is up to the actor [30]. We hypothesize that:

Higher levels of perceived behavioral control (PBC) will be predictive of stronger intentions to be involved in PSR management processes.

In the TPB, one’s intention to engage in a certain behavior is theorized to be predictive of their actual behavior. However, Ajzen [27] specifies that a behavioral intention will be expressed as a behavior only to the extent to which the behavior is under the actor’s volitional control. The TPB accounts for this by also including perceived behavioral control as a direct predictor of behavior as a proxy for actual control over the behavior. Intention is also proposed to mediate the relationships between perceived behavioral control, subjective norms, attitudes, and behavior. As such, we hypothesize:

Stronger PBC will have a direct positive effect on participants’ level of involvement in PSR management processes.

Stronger intentions will be predictive of higher subsequent levels of involvement in PSR management process.

There will be an indirect effect of attitude on behavior through behavioral intention.

There will be an indirect effect of organizational norms on behavior through behavioral intention.

There will be an indirect effect of PBC on behavior through behavioral intention.

The TPB can also be used as a framework for extended models of behavior [32]. In our study context, we adopt this approach and investigate specific organizational factors that may influence PSR management practices. Here, we include measures of perceived role-clarity, role-conflict, and access to resources as factors that may influence participants’ involvement in PSR management processes. These factors have been noted in past literature as influencing occupational health interventions [28,33]. We expect that high levels of role-clarity and access to resources and low levels of role-conflict will strengthen the intention–behavior relationship. Noting that other studies have found context-specific moderators of the intention–behavior relationship to improve the theoretical model eg [34,35] we hypothesize that:

Organizational factors [(a) role-clarity, (b) role-conflict, and (c) access to resources] will moderate the relationship between participants’ intention and actual involvement in PSR management processes. Specifically, greater role-clarity and access to resources will strengthen the intention–behavior relationship and greater role-conflict will weaken the intention–behavior relationship.

Materials and methods

This study used a longitudinal survey design, involving two waves of online data collection (T1 and T2) three months apart. This interval was chosen to provide a reasonable period for participants to engage in PSR management activities while balancing the increased risk of attrition with longer intervals between data collection. Participation involved answering Likert-style survey questions through the Qualtrics [36] online survey platform. During the T1 survey, participants had the option of opting into the T2 survey, in which case they were assigned an identifier code to allow for matching of their responses. Fig. 2 provides an overview of the research design and timeline.

Overview and timeline for the present research.

Participants were recruited using social media (i.e., LinkedIn) advertizing and email invitations sent to publicly available work email addresses in April 2024. At T1, participants were required to have at least six months’ experience in their field and to be currently working in New Zealand. Participants were screened to ensure they were working in, involved with, or responsible for health and safety, HR management, or employee well-being. This research was reviewed and approved by the authors’ human research ethics committee (HREC 2023/134).

There were 201 responses to the initial (T1) survey, of which there were 165 complete surveys. The mean age of participants was 48.3 years. Fifty-seven percent of the sample was female, 42% were male, and 1% declined to answer. A total of 30.8% of participants were contractors or consultants, and 69.2% worked for an organization, of which 13% were employers. Seventy-eight percent of participants were currently working in or responsible for “Health and Safety,” 39% in “Employee Wellness/Well-being,” and 24% in “Human resources/People and Culture.” Seventy-seven percent of participants had more than 5 years of experience in their field, with a mean tenure in participants’ current role of 6.3 years. Participants worked in a range of industries. The most common industries were construction (11%), professional, scientific and technical services (11%), and healthcare and social assistance (10%). A total of 140 participants opted to receive the T2 survey, of which 97 (69%) took part. Data screening at T2 yielded 77 useable results.

Measures

The T1 survey included measures of attitude toward being involved in PSR management, organizational norms, PBC, behavioral intention, role-clarity, access to resources, role-conflict, and demographic measures. The T2 survey included measures of role-clarity, access to resources, role-conflict, and behavior (level of involvement in PSR management processes). Basic demographic and working situation measures were included at T1. Responses to all items excluding behavior and demographic questions were gathered using a 5-point Likert-style scale ranging from 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. Behavior was measured on a 5-point Likert-style scale ranging from 1 = not involved at all to 5 = highly involved. Given a lack of previously validated instruments that were suitable for our research questions and context, our survey was created using a combination of items from previous research and items designed specifically for this study. The quality of the scales was assessed using exploratory factor analysis and assessment of Cronbach’s α. See supplementary material for a copy of survey items and the results of the factor analysis.

Attitude

To gauge participants’ attitude toward being involved in PSR management processes, we used two items designed to measure the extent to which participants consider they have a role in addressing PSRs at work (a = .81).

Perceived behavioral control

Using the direct measurement approach [30], we measured participants’ PBC using two items designed to measure their confidence to be involved in PSR management processes (α = 0.73).

Organizational norms

We measured perceptions of senior leaders’ prioritization of psychological health to reflect the organizational norms relevant to our research context. To do so, we used the management priority subscale (α = .90) of the previously validated psychosocial safety climate 12-item questionnaire [37].

Intention and behavior

We included two items asking participants to rate their intention to be involved in PSR management processes over the next three months (α = .91). To assess behavior, participants were asked to rate their current level of involvement in PSR management processes (involvement was defined to include activities such as leading, advising on, developing, or contributing to PSR management processes).

Organizational factors

Role-clarity was measured using the single item reported in the study by Fisher et al [38]. Role-conflict was measured using a single item from the study by Matthews et al [39]. To contextualize these items, the stem “regarding PSR management” was added. Access to resources was measured using a single item developed for the survey.

Analyses

Jamovi Statistical Software 2.7 [40] was used to conduct exploratory factor analysis using the items for each scale. Results (see supplementary files) suggested that items for the scales loaded onto corresponding factors apart from one item from the PBC scale, which was removed. Mean index variables were computed for each of the scales. Data analysis then involved calculation of the descriptive statistics and correlations between the variables of interest.

Next, analysis was conducted to test hypotheses 1–8. Here, a path analysis was run using Mplus Muthén & Muthén version 8.3 [41] to test the hypothesized direct and indirect effects. Full-information maximum likelihood estimation was used because it has been shown to have superior properties when it comes to the proportion of convergence failures, parameter estimate bias, parameter estimate efficiency, and model goodness of fit compared to listwise, pairwise, and similar response pattern imputation [42]. Full-information maximum likelihood estimation methods deal with missing data, estimate parameters, and estimate standard errors in a single step [43]. Such imputation methods provide a useful way to utilize all available data in longitudinal studies and reduce bias due to selective attrition [44]. We used a bootstrapping procedure (10,000 samples) and assessed the path coefficients, p values, and 95% confidence intervals to test our hypotheses.

Next, moderation analyses were conducted to test H9. We tested each moderator separately on the relationship between intention and behavior using the PROCESS macro [45] in IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 29) for simple moderation (model 1) with bootstrapping (10,000 samples) and mean centering of predictor and moderator terms.

Discussion

This study sought to advance our understanding of the individual and organizational factors that support key stakeholders to be involved in PSR management processes. Drawing on the increasing calls for a risk management approach for addressing work-related PSRs [8] and research which suggests a potential for improvement when it comes to key stakeholders involvement in addressing psychosocial factors [16] and employee welfare [20], our findings have theoretical and practical implications.

First, regarding the theoretical implications of our results, we found support for several of our hypotheses based on the TPB, suggesting that some components of the TPB can help us to understand stakeholders’ involvement in PSR management processes. We show that PBC and attitude have a small but significant positive impact on intention and, in turn, involvement in PSR management activities. Although we did not find a significant effect of our measure of organizational norms on intention, this result is in line with other tests of the TPB, which tend to find that the norm component of the theory has the weakest influence on intention [46]. We also did not find a significant direct effect from PBC on behavior, which could be due to our measure of PBC being more reflective of the self-efficacy dimension of the construct than the controllability dimension [30]. That is, our measure captured information about participants’ confidence to be involved with PSR management processes rather than their actual level of control [27].

Positioning these findings within the relevant literature, we find that our results are in line with those of Friebel et al [47] who demonstrated that attitude and PBC are significant predictors of health and safety worker representatives’ intention to implement regulatory policy for psychosocial health. Aligned with our results, Friebel et al found that the subjective norm component of the TPB was not a significant predictor of intention. However, Friebel et al [47] used a cross-sectional design and did not measure the relationship between intention and subsequent behavior. In contrast, we show that intention is predictive of self-reported behavior over a three-month follow-up period. We also investigated organizational factors (role-clarity, role-conflict, and access to resources) as moderators between intention and behavior.

In contrast to other research, we did not find any moderating effects on the intention–behavior relationship. For example, past research has shown that context-specific moderators (e.g., trust [34]) and individual-level factors (e.g., mental toughness [35]) have significant effects on the intention–behavior relationship. The factors we assessed (role-clarity, role-conflict, and access to resources) were drawn from past literature that suggests they might be important factors in determining successful implementation of occupational health interventions [28,33]. Despite a lack of moderation effects, our results suggest that role-clarity does have a direct effect on behavior, signaling the importance of stakeholders having a clear understanding of their roles when it comes to addressing PSRs. We acknowledge that our use of adapted single-item measures for the job-related variables could be strengthened in future research through the development and validation of scales specific to this research context. We also note that there are other factors that may influence the intention–behavior relationship or directly influence behavior in this context. Past qualitative research indicates several barriers faced by organization when it comes to managing PSRs. These include competing priorities, lack of consensus [48], and lack of and perceived lack of scope for reducing PSRs [49]. Past research also suggests that practitioners may face value conflicts when attempting to balance employee welfare interests and business priorities [20,23]. Outside of these individual and organizational influences, there may also be macrolevel factors that impact stakeholders’ intentions and ability to address PSRs. For example, Leka and Jain’s [11] conceptual framework of determinants and impacts of PSRs sets out that macrolevel factors such as the political, social, economic, technological, and ecological context influence the psychosocial work environment. We recommend further quantitative research be undertaken, with a focus on those working in OHS and related roles, to illuminate the extent to which these contextual factors impact stakeholders’ intentions and practice.

Regarding the practical implications of this research, our findings show the importance of feeling confident to be involved in this work, believing it is part of one’s role and responsibility to be involved, and having a sense of role-clarity regarding this area of work. This has significance for organizations wanting to support people working in OHS to take an active role in addressing PSRs. Our findings suggest that improving clarity in stakeholders’ roles when it comes to addressing PSRs could enable greater involvement in PSR management processes. Creating a sense of role-clarity may be particularly important as the management of work-related PSRs requires the involvement of multiple stakeholders with potentially differing interests and perspectives [50]. Enhancing role-clarity could address some of these issues and may have additional positive effects, with past research suggesting that role-clarity is associated with job satisfaction and reduced turnover [51]. Our measure of attitude reflected participants’ belief in their role and responsibility for addressing PSRs; there may be opportunities for organizations to shape these beliefs by having role descriptions that include PSR management tasks and signaling their importance during performance discussions. Organizations could also consider ways to build confidence among stakeholders to initiate/improve PSR management processes, by providing access to training and development opportunities in this area.

Research strengths, limitations, and future directions

A strength of this study is the use of data across two time points, allowing us to assess the influence of behavioral intention on subsequent behavior. Although our sample was relatively small, our findings are strengthened by the diversity of our sample which included representation across demographic and working situations, strengthening the applicability of our findings to the population of people working in OHS in New Zealand and similar western contexts. Limitations include the use of self-reported measures of behavior, which can lead to issues including social desirability biases and common method bias [52]. We suggest efforts be made to investigate and evaluate methods to support stakeholders to build confidence, improve role-clarity, and develop supportive beliefs regarding their role in addressing PSRs. Other possible avenues for future research may include assessing the generalizability of these findings to other key stakeholder groups that can support PSR identification, assessment, and management, for example, occupational physicians, nurses, and other health professionals.

Conclusion

We show PBC and attitudes as predictors of intentions to be involved in PSR management processes and, in turn, subsequent engagement in these processes. We also find that role-clarity is a direct predictor of levels of involvement in PSR management. Our findings can inform further research in this area, which may explore whether there are other individual, organizational, or contextual factors which have effects on stakeholders’ level of involvement in PSR management processes. From a practical perspective, this research signals that feeling confident, having supportive attitudes, and having a sense of role-clarity regarding PSR management processes are important determinants of stakeholders’ level of involvement in this work. Organizations may look to encourage capability development through training opportunities and support stakeholders by ensuring roles and responsibilities are clearly defined. Supporting and leveraging those working in OHS and related roles to be active in addressing PSRs will improve the systematic management of these risks, thereby meeting legal obligations and improving employee health outcomes.
