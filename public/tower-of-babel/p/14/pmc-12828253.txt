Risk factors for coracoclavicular reconstruction failure: a systematic review
Authors: Braeden R Gooch, Josh W Thibeault, Charles R Reiter, Matthew S Smith, Joshua C Setliff, John W Cyrus, James R Satalich, J Brett Goodloe, Jennifer L Vanderbeck
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe coracoclavicular (CC) ligament reconstruction for acromioclavicular injuries is performed through a variety of surgical techniques and fixation methods. The purpose of this study is to determine the risk factors associated with failure of CC reconstruction, regardless of the fixation method used.MethodsA systematic review was performed in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. Medline, Embase, and Cochrane were searched through November 2024 for studies analyzing the failures of CC reconstructive surgery. Studies that quantified risk factors for CC reconstruction failure were included.ResultsSeven studies comprising 781 CC reconstructions were analyzed with an overall failure rate of 19.1%. Clavicle tunnel malposition in anatomic reconstructions was the most cited risk factor for failure. Position was defined as either the distance from the lateral aspect of the clavicle to the tunnels or as a ratio between this distance and the length of the clavicle. Three studies cited increased failure risk with medial malposition of the conoid or trapezoid tunnels. Specific parameters included conoid tunnel ratio >0.25, (odds ratio (OR) = 5.67), trapezoid ratio >0.15 (OR = 4.2), conoid tunnel >47 mm (OR = 4.67), and trapezoid tunnel >24 mm (OR = 4.2). Lateral conoid tunnel malposition with a conoid tunnel ratio <0.20 (OR = 40) was also noted to increase failure risk. Another study identified increased risk of failure when surgeons use tunnel ratios instead of tunnel distance in mm intraoperatively (OR = 4.609). Placement of a lateral coracoid button, as opposed to a central button, was cited in 2 studies as significant risk factors (OR = 9.614, 13.87). Other risk factors included osteoporosis (OR = 8.652), weight-bearing before 6 weeks from surgery (OR = 6.4), surgery >6 weeks from initial injury (OR = 2.65), osteolysis (OR = 4.386), patient age >40 (OR = 3.14), and open reconstruction (OR = 4.25).ConclusionsConoid and trapezoid tunnel malposition, both medially and laterally, were significantly associated with an increased risk of failure. Additionally, patient age, osteoporosis, osteolysis, time to weight bearing after surgery, delayed presentation of more than 6 weeks from initial injury, should be considered when considering surgical intervention.Keywords: Risk factors, Coracoclavicular reconstruction, Acromioclavicular reconstruction, Failure, Complications, Reoperation

Acromioclavicular (AC) joint injuries account for approximately l2% of all shoulder injuries making it one of the most injured structures of the upper extremity.4,6,16 Incidence of these injuries has steadily increased over the past 2 decades with most occurring in athletics.7 More specifically, acromioclavicular injury consists of injury to either the AC capsule, AC ligament, or coracoclavicular (CC) ligaments. Patients typically present following blunt force trauma impacting the shoulder in an adducted position or a fall with an outstretched arm leading to significant pain over the AC joint and limited range of motion.37 If not treated, symptoms can progress to chronic pain with shoulder motion and instability in the shoulder girdle.37 The Rockwood Scale is classically known as the standard grading scale used in treatment decision making. Typically, grade I and II injuries are treated conservatively while grade IV or higher are treated surgically with controversy regarding the most appropriate treatment for grade III injuries.1,37,41 When treated surgically, AC reconstruction is reported to have failure rates greater than 20%.4, 5, 6,16,18,34

Classically, surgical intervention of AC joint dislocations consisted primarily of open reduction and internal fixation before largely transitioning to an AC ligament reconstruction outlined by Weaver and Dunn.15 Since then, over 150 different surgical techniques have been developed in an effort to improve the outcomes of AC reconstructive surgery.6,15 While the Weaver-Dunn technique is still in use, current trends consist largely of anatomical reconstruction of the CC ligaments using a variety of approaches, fixation methods, graft types, and suspensory devices. Despite the efforts toward innovative surgery, postoperative outcomes tend to remain similar.34,40 Additionally with the evolution of these surgical techniques, the failure rate of AC reconstruction remains high.34 While there are many methods of failure described in the literature, the most common mechanism of failure is loss of reduction on postoperative imaging leading to chronic pain and instability of the shoulder, occasionally requiring revision reconstruction.5,21,25,35

Due to the high complication rates, many studies have analyzed outcomes and significant factors related to failure of AC (AC) reconstructive surgery.21,26,28,35 However, no systematic reviews have analyzed quantifiable risk factors associated with AC or CC reconstruction failures. The purpose of this study was to conduct a systematic review to determine which risk factors are associated with increased risk of AC joint reconstruction failure regardless of fixation method.

Methods

Literature and database search

A systematic search was conducted using Cochrane, Embase (Ovid), and Medline (Ovid) studies in English and published through November 2024. The search consisted of a variation of the following terms: AC reconstruction, CC reconstruction, and failure. The full details of the search strategy for Medline, Embase, and Cochrane can be seen in Appendix (A). All identified studies were uploaded into Covidence for screening with the literature search and subsequent review conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses reporting standards.

Eligibility criteria and study selection

Two screeners (B.G. and J.T.) manually screened titles and abstracts for studies analyzing AC or CC reconstruction and failure. Case reports, cohort studies, case series, and randomized control trials were included. Cadaveric or biomechanical studies, non-English studies, editorial commentary, systematic reviews, letters to the editor, and surgical technique papers were excluded. Studies that analyzed revision surgery, those that primarily focused on AC or CC reconstruction in the setting of concomitant clavicle fractures, or those using hook plate fixation were also excluded. Full abstract screening was performed by 2 independent reviewers to exclude further studies as needed. Conflicts between reviewers were resolved with a third-party consultation with a senior author (C.R.). Full details of the protocol for this systematic review were prospectively registered on PROSPERO and may be accessed at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/recorddashboard.

Methodological quality assessment

Methodological quality assessment was carried out using the JBI checklist for case series. Two independent reviewers (B.G. and J.T.) used the checklist on each included study to evaluate the level of bias. Any conflicts were resolved through a third author (C.R.).

Data extraction

For each of the individual studies included, 1 of 2 independent reviewers (B.G. and J.T.) extracted data into a google sheets spreadsheet (Google LLC, Mountain View, California, United States). A second independent reviewer then extracted data from the same studies. A third-party author (C.R.) was consulted as needed with continued conflicts. The spreadsheet included data as follows: study type, level of evidence, number of patients receiving either an AC or CC reconstruction respectively, number of female and male patients respectively, average mean age, mean follow-up time postoperatively, injury grade included in the study, surgical technique used, graft type used, percentage of failure, mechanism of failure.

Statistical analysis

Statistical measurements of increased risk factors were extracted from each study and tabulated. These values included odds ratios, hazard ratios, and measurements of risk from multivariate regression analysis. The outcomes of interest were tabulated in categories of similarity. A meta-analysis was not performed.

Results

Literature search and screening

A literature search of Cochrane, Embase (Ovid), and Medline (Ovid) produced a total of 1,619 studies (Fig. 1). After duplicates were identified and removed, 874 studies remained for screening. Screening of titles and abstracts related to primary AC or CC reconstruction and subsequent failure resulted in 74 studies available for full text screening. Full-text screening excluded a total of 67 studies. This yielded 7 studies for data extraction.

Methodological assessment and risk of bias

Two blind reviewers (B.G. and J.T.) analyzed studies for methodological quality and risk of bias according to the JBI Clinical Appraisal tools case series. All 7 studies were both independently scored 10 out of 10 and deemed suitable for the review.

Patient demographics

Across all 7 studies, 782 patients were included that underwent CC reconstruction. Across 6 studies, 599 patients were male and 155 were female. Cook et al did not report patient demographics by sex.10 The average age of patients was 37.5 years. Surgical techniques

The surgical techniques reported in the studies included in this review reported varying methods of both anatomic and nonatomic fixation. Three studies followed cohorts treated with suture-button fixation with Chen et al reporting both open and closed techniques.9,32,39 Of these 3, Rojas et al only analyzed double button graft fixation while both Sun et al and Chen et al. analyzed both double and triple button fixation. Einstein et al analyzed a cohort treated with open and closed allograft tunnels using semitendinosus allograft vs autograft.13 Lastly, Spencer et al reported both nonanatomic (Modified Weaver-Dunn) and anatomic techniques using synthetic, allograft loops around the coracoid, and synthetic/allograft hybrid loop graft fixations.38 The surgical techniques, graft types, and failure definitions utilized by each included study can be seen in Table II.

Reconstruction failure

The overall failure rate following primary CC reconstruction was 19.1 percent, with included studies reporting failure rates ranging from 8% to 52.6%.8, 9, 10,13,32,38,39 Three of the 7 studies reported average failure times following surgery with the average amongst them being 10 months.9,10,13 When defining the mechanism of failure, 5 of the 7 studies included defined failure as increased CC interval >6 mm compared to primary postoperative imaging. One defined failure as increased CC interval >5 mm while the other defined failure as >50% increase in CC distance compared to primary postoperative imaging.

Risk factors for failure

Risk factors for CC reconstruction failure were quantified in all included studies. Three studies reported conoid or trapezoid tunnel malposition as a risk factor for failure.13,32,39 Einstein et al reported increased failure risk with a conoid tunnel ratio <0.20 (odds ratio (OR) = 40, P = .018), conoid tunnel ratio 0.251-0.30 (OR = 39, P = .002), conoid tunnel ratio >0.30(OR 29, P = .029), conoid tunnel ratios of >25% compared to <25% (OR 5.7, P = .027), and conoid tunnel distances of >47 mm compared to <47 mm (OR 4.7, P = .028).13 Conoid tunnels were measured as the distance from the distal end of the clavicle to the conoid tunnel divided by the total length of the clavicle in mm. Similarly, Rojas et al reported increased risk of failure with trapezoid distance >24 mm (OR = 4.2, P = .024) as well as trapezoid ratio >0.15 (OR = 4.2, P = .025).32 Like conoid tunnel distance, trapezoid tunnel distance was calculated as the distance from the distal end of the clavicle to the trapezoid tunnel divided by the total length of the clavicle in mm. Sun et al reported increased risk when using tunnel ratios instead of tunnel distances during surgical planning (OR = 4.6, P = .032).39 When analyzing the coracoid button position, both Sun et al and Chen et al reported increased risk with lateralization of button position from center with ORs of 13.9 (P = .00) and 9.6 (P = .002) respectively.9,39

Osseous pathology was cited as a risk factor in 2 studies. Sun et al reported osteoporosis leading to increased risk of failure with an OR of 8.7 (P = .003).39 Chen et al reported osteolysis also leading to increased risk with an OR of 4.4 (P = .036).9

Other risk factors reported to lead to increased risk of failure include patient age over 40 years old (OR 3.1, P = .05),38 surgery later than 6 weeks from initial injury (OR 2.7, P = .004),9 open reconstruction compared to arthroscopic (OR 4.3, P = .05),10 and weight bearing prior to 6 weeks after surgery (OR 6.4, P = .011).9 A complete list of reported risk factors can be seen in Table III.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to determine factors associated with failure risk after CC reconstruction regardless of fixation method. There was an overall failure rate of 19.1% in data summated from all 7 included studies.9,10,13,32,38,39,42 Six of the 7 studies reported average follow-up time post operatively with an average time of 15.6 months.8,9,13,32,38,39 Clavicle tunnel malposition was a frequently cited risk factor for postoperative failure. Non-anatomic positioning of the conoid or trapezoid tunnels were identified as significant risk factors in multiple studies. Other risk factors were related to poor bone quality, increased patient age, open versus arthroscopic surgery, timing of surgery, and postoperative weight bearing. The findings from this review can be used to guide surgical decision-making and to identify patients at increased risk for postoperative failure. This systematic review is the first to synthesize quantifiable risk factors for CC reconstruction failure to the authors' knowledge.

Our findings suggest that variance, both medially and laterally, from the average anatomical conoid and trapezoid ligament distances and ratios leads to increased risk of CC reconstruction failure. These findings are supported by various biomechanical studies analyzing optimal clavicle tunnel placement when trying to obtain anatomic fixation. Benavent et al in a retrospective biomechanical cohort study reported the average anatomical trapezoid distance in females to be 22.1 mm and 26.6 mm in males while the average conoid distance was found to be 40 mm in females and 46.6 mm in males.3 Rios et al reported similar values with average anatomical trapezoid distance in 22.9 mm in females and 25.4 mm in males with conoid ligament position of 42.8 mm in females and 47.2 mm in males.31 With the shift from older reconstructive techniques to anatomic reconstruction, multiple biomechanical studies have analyzed the forces tolerated with anatomic and nonanatomic positions following reconstruction.23,24,27 Kocadel et al used computed tomography images to report maximal force loads based on tunnel positions, which showed maximal force tolerance at the anatomical position with decreasing tolerance with both medial and lateral malposition.23 Similarly, both Li et al and Mazzocca et al performed cadaveric studies showing reduced force tolerated through the conoid and trapezoid ligaments when misaligned from anatomic position.24,27

In this review, Rojas et al reported increased risk with trapezoid distance greater than 24 mm and a trapezoid ratio greater than 0.15 mm.32 Similarly, Einstein et al reported increased failure with conoid tunnel distance greater than 47 mm, conoid tunnel ratio less than 0.20, and conoid tunnel ratio greater than 25%.13 These findings are consistent with biomechanical studies reporting similar findings.

Coracoid tunnel malposition was also found to increase failure risk. Similar to clavicle tunnel position, the farther ligament and tunnel position deviates from anatomical position, the higher risk of failure.14 Multiple studies reporting outcomes of CC reconstruction also predicted a higher likelihood of surgical complications with both medialization and lateralization of coracoid position.14,34,42 Medialization of the coracoid tunnel has also been associated with increased risk of failure, although this risk has not been quantified.14,42

The integrity of bone plays a critical role in ensuring the success of AC and CC joint reconstruction, as evidenced by Sun et al who identified osteoporosis as a significant risk factor for loss of reduction.39 Sun et al demonstrated that osteoporosis was a statistically significant predictor of postoperative reduction failure, emphasizing the need for enhanced fixation strategies in patients with compromised bone quality.39 Geaney et al provided a biomechanical perspective, demonstrating that graft fixation strength through a clavicular tunnel in CC reconstruction is highly dependent on bone density at the tunnel site.17 Their findings suggest that placing tunnels in regions of higher bone mineral density can significantly enhance fixation strength, thereby reducing the likelihood of reduction loss. This is particularly relevant for surgical planning in osteoporotic patients, when bone composition could compromise an anatomic fixation. Healy et al advocates for a multidisciplinary approach to any shoulder surgery that includes preoperative bone health optimization, radiographic screening for osteoporosis, and consideration of pharmacologic interventions.19

Osteolysis in EndoButton (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA, USA) fixation for AC joint reconstruction presents a significant challenge, as highlighted by Chen et al, who identified it as an independent risk factor for loss of reduction and operative failure.8 Osteolysis is a commonly reported complication in numerous shoulder procedures, including CC reconstruction.2,20,29,33,36 However, further research in this area is needed to analyze possible techniques, approaches, graft types, or other factors that may contribute to osteolysis postoperatively.

Demographic factors were also associated with increased risk of CC reconstruction failure as discussed by Spencer et al who specifically reported increased risk with patients over 40 years old.38 This is consistent with literature on various shoulder procedures, including arthroscopy and arthroplasty, which found increased failure rates as age increases.11,12,22,30 Increased age is highly associated with decreased bone integrity, muscle tone and strength, and biologic healing, all of which could potentiate the effects of age on healing postoperatively.11,12,22,30 If any of these factors decrease the patient's ability to successfully follow postoperative protocols, increased failure rate can be expected to occur more regularly.22

The primary strength of this review is that it is the first review to our knowledge that synthesizes risk factors of CC reconstructive failure. By only including studies that quantified risk factors associated with failure, physicians may use these findings to quickly identify possible areas of focus in their approaches to surgical technique. Additionally, the studies in this review included 782 patients, giving increased confidence in the values reported in the respective studies.

This review is limited by the overall number of studies that met the inclusion criteria. While studies report outcomes of surgical procedures using a variety of quantification methods, our study only included those specifically reporting quantifiable risk factors in the form of odds ratios or hazard ratios. Future research that specifically quantifies risk factors is necessary for further supportive data, especially as CC reconstructive techniques are continuously evolving. Our findings are also limited by heterogeneity of reported data, with limited overlap in outcomes across studies to strengthen or refute findings. It should also be noted that of the 782 patients in this review, only 19.8% were female. Due to the differences in ligamentous and bony positions between males and females, additional studies reporting female outcomes would also contribute to increased confidence. Lastly, variations in surgical technique and changes in surgical practices over time must be considered as possible limitations.

Conclusion

A systematic review analyzing risk factors associated with increased risk of CC reconstruction failure found 782 patients across seven studies since 2013 with an overall failure rate of 19.1%. Malposition of the conoid and trapezoid tunnels was was significantly associated with increased risk of failure. Lateral coracoid tunnel malposition was also associated with increased risk of failure. Additionally, patient age, osteoporosis and osteolysis, weight bearing time after surgery, surgery after 6 weeks from initial injury, and patient age were cited risk factors. All of these factors must be considered in surgical planning prior to CC reconstruction.
