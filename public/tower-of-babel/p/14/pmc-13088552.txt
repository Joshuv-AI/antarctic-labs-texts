Latent profiles of childhood maltreatment and influence on later-life depressive symptoms trajectories: evidence from China
Authors: Tingshuai Ge, Qing Han, Zeru Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to identify latent profiles of childhood maltreatment, assess their differential impact on later-life depressive symptoms trajectories, and investigate potential gender differences in these processes in the Chinese context.MethodsData were drawn from five waves of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study spanning 2011–2020 (N = 12,856 participants aged 45–85). Latent Class Analysis and Latent Class Growth Modeling were used to identify childhood maltreatment profiles and depressive symptoms trajectories, respectively. Multinomial logistic regression models assessed the association between maltreatment profiles and depressive symptoms trajectories. All analyses were stratified by gender.ResultsFive childhood maltreatment profiles were identified: Low Maltreatment, Strict Parenting, Son Preference, Parental Neglect, and Multi-Perpetrator Physical Victimization. Four depressive symptoms trajectories emerged: Low-Stable, High-Decreasing, Moderate-Increasing, and High-Stable. The Strict Parenting, Son Preference, Parental Neglect, and Multi-Perpetrator Physical Victimization were significantly associated with higher risks of being in the three adverse trajectories. Gender differences were observed in the distribution of both maltreatment profiles and depressive symptoms trajectories, but not in the association between them.ConclusionsChildhood maltreatment, in any form, has profound and lasting mental health consequences. Life-course gender-specific interventions should be developed to eliminate maltreatment against children and mitigate their detrimental effects on later-life mental health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04326-7.Keywords: Childhood maltreatment, Depressive symptoms, Latent profiles, Trajectories, Gender differences, Later life

Introduction

The detrimental impacts of childhood maltreatment on later-life mental health have been documented in previous studies [1–5]. Childhood maltreatment encompasses various forms of abuse, neglect, and exploitation perpetrated by parents, peers, or others, which harm children’s survival, development, and welfare [6]. Experiencing such maltreatment during formative years can disrupt neurological, endocrine, and immune systems; impair cognitive, social, and emotional functioning; undermine socioeconomic attainment and health in adulthood; and ultimately resulting in elevated risks of depression in old age [3, 7–9]. Despite these insights, significant gaps persist in understanding the relationship between childhood maltreatment and later-life depressive symptoms.

First, evidence remains limited regarding the co-occurring profiles of maltreatment among older adults. The cumulative risk model posits that children are often exposed to multiple co-occurring risk factors rather than isolated adversities; and compared with a single risk exposure, multiple risks can overlap, jointly exerting more severe consequences for child development [10]. Emerging research on adolescents or incarcerated individuals provided evidence for this model, finding that individuals experience multiple forms of maltreatment concurrently or are disproportionately exposed to a particular type [11–14]. However, previous research on older adults primarily examines maltreatment in a variable-centered manner, for example, focusing on a single maltreatment exposure such as peer bullying [4, 15] or modeling dose–response associations between maltreatment experiences and subsequent health outcomes [16–18]. While informative, these approaches may not fully capture the patterned co-occurrence of different maltreatment experiences or subjectively assume that different maltreatment types have equivalent weight. As a result, they may underestimate the prevalence of childhood maltreatment and its long-term health consequences [19]. Given the context of a rapidly aging global population, it is crucial to identify the childhood maltreatment profiles specifically among older adults and to examine their potential influence on later-life psychological well-being.

Second, it remains unclear how distinct childhood maltreatment profiles shape depressive symptoms trajectories in later life. The models in life course epidemiology, such as the critical period model and the pathway model, collectively suggest that maltreatment profiles in childhood may shape trajectories of depressive symptoms in later life. Specifically, the critical period model posits that exposure to adverse events during childhood, a developmentally sensitive window, can trigger enduring physiological, psychological, and behavioral alterations with long-term implications for mental health [20]. The pathway model further proposes that early adverse experiences can set in motion a cascade of later-life social, economic, and health disadvantages, thereby influencing mental health in old age [21]. Consistent with these frameworks, empirical studies show that trajectories of depressive symptoms in later life are shaped by early-life adversities, with parental physical abuse and peer bullying associated with persistently severe or fluctuating trajectories [5, 22], while emotional neglect predicts an increasing trajectory [1]. Taken together, these models and findings motivate our central question: whether older adults exposed to different maltreatment profiles follow distinct depressive symptom trajectories in later life.

Third, gender differences may exist in childhood maltreatment profiles and their associated depressive symptoms trajectories. First, latent profiles of maltreatment may differ between men and women. Previous studies reported that men are more likely to experience physical abuse, while women are more prone to neglect [23, 24]. Research based on U.S. prison samples further found that incarcerated females are more likely than incarcerated males to be members of poly-victimization classes as children [14, 19]. Second, depressive symptoms trajectories may exhibit differential sensitivities to maltreatment profiles across genders. Variations in physiological characteristics, available resources, and coping strategies for adversity between men and women [25–27] suggest that similar maltreatment profiles may result in gender-specific depressive symptoms trajectories. However, most prior studies have relied on mixed-gender samples [2, 5, 28], potentially obscuring crucial gender differences and the need for gender-tailored mental health interventions.

The Chinese context

In China, however, context- and cohort-specific conditions may uniquely shape both the profiles of childhood maltreatment and their long-term implications. China is experiencing rapid population aging, with the population aged 60 and older reaching 310 million (22%) by the end of 2024 and projected to surpass 400 million (30%) by 2035. Declining health conditions coupled with limited social security systems place significant caregiving burdens on older adults [29], with over 30% of them experiencing depression, a proportion that continues to rise rapidly [30].

Additionally, today’s Chinese older adults grew up during a period of relatively limited socioeconomic development and under traditional childrearing norms. Under the long-standing influence of Patrilineality, Patriarchy, and Patrilocality, sons were viewed as having greater “value” in carrying on the family line and enhancing household socioeconomic standing, contributing to strong societal son preference [31]. Many couples pursued having sons through higher fertility, resulting in a high total fertility rate (about six births per woman) [32]. In multi-child households, coupled with long parental work hours needed to sustain livelihoods, caregiving time and energy were constrained, increasing vulnerability to low care and unmet physical and psychological needs—potentially more pronounced for girls in a son-preference culture [33–35]. In addition, influenced by beliefs such as “Spare the rod, spoil the child,” corporal punishment and other punitive disciplinary practices were widely regarded as acceptable and were relatively common in families of this cohort [36]. Finally, when school-based protection and anti-bullying mechanisms were relatively limited, peer risks such as bullying were more likely to be overlooked, constituting another salient source of adversity outside the family.

Taken together, these cohort-specific structural and normative conditions may have shaped not only the prevalence of maltreatment exposure but also its patterned co-occurrence in Chinese older adults, giving rise to distinct maltreatment profiles characterized by strict parenting, son preference, parental neglect, and multi-perpetrator physical violence. Additionally, these early-life profiles may contribute to persistently elevated depressive symptoms trajectories in later life by becoming embedded in life-course processes such as constrained socioeconomic attainment, cumulative health disadvantage, and limited coping resources in older age. At the same time, cultural norms and societal contexts may encourage the rationalization of parental abusive behaviors, which may attenuate the detrimental effects of maltreatment profiles on depressive symptoms trajectories [37]. Overall, these attributes provide a context-specific rationale for examining how co-occurring childhood maltreatment profiles relate to heterogeneous depressive symptoms trajectories among Chinese older adults.

In light of these limitations, we investigated the latent profiles of childhood maltreatment and their impact on depressive symptoms trajectories in later life in the Chinese context, using longitudinal data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS, 2011–2020). The goals of this study are threefold: (1) to identify the latent profiles of childhood maltreatment; (2) to examine their impact on later-life depressive symptoms trajectories; and (3) to explore gender differences in these processes.

Methods

Data

Data used in this study were drawn from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative survey of Chinese adults aged 45 years and older. Using a stratified, multistage probability sampling design, the 2011 baseline survey randomly selected 17,705 respondents from 450 communities in 150 counties across 28 provinces in mainland China. Follow-up surveys were conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. In 2014, CHARLS implemented a life history survey that collected extensive information on childhood experiences. All data were gathered through face-to-face interviews, with follow-up rates exceeding 85%.

We utilized data from 2011 to 2020. The analytic sample was derived by excluding: (1) participants younger than 45 or older than 85 years (N = 551); (2) those who did not participate in the 2014 life history survey (N = 3,134); (3) those who participated in fewer than two follow-up surveys (N = 646); and (4) those with missing covariate data (N = 518). We used listwise deletion for participants (< 3%) with missing covariates, an approach generally considered acceptable when the proportion of missingness is below 5% [38]. The final analytic sample comprised 12,856 participants, including 6,160 men and 6,696 women. The sampling procedure is illustrated in Figure S1.

Measures

Depressive Symptoms (CESD) was assessed using the 10-item short form of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10), referencing previous studies [5, 18, 39]. The scale comprises two subscales — positive affect and negative affect — and assesses the frequency of 10 symptoms experienced during the past week on a four-point scale ranging from “none of the time” (0) to “most of the time” (3). A total score was calculated by summing these item responses, ranging from 0 to 30, with a higher score indicating poorer mental health. The CES-D-10 scale has been widely validated among older adults in both Western and Chinese populations and has demonstrated adequate reliability and validity [40–42]. Following previous research [40], we allowed one missing item in the scale and then substituted the mean for the missing item. The Cronbach’s alpha values were 0.76 or higher across five waves from 2011 to 2020.

Childhood maltreatment Drawing on prior related studies [3, 18, 43], we used 12 items to measure childhood maltreatment experiences. Each item has four options ranging from “very strict” (1) to “not at all” (4). These items covered multiple domains, including physical abuse/neglect, emotional abuse/neglect, and peer bullying, and showed high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.92). Given the low response frequencies observed in the most severe categories for several items, and consistent with common practice in comparable latent-class studies [11, 14], each item was recoded into a binary indicator (0 = no, 1 = suffering). Latent Class Analysis (LCA) was then applied to identify the latent profiles of childhood maltreatment. In addition to intra-familial maltreatment, we incorporated peer bullying outside the family due to its frequent occurrence in China in recent years. We also included a measure of parental son preference, a form of maltreatment with unique Chinese cultural characteristics. While previous studies have confirmed the negative impact of son preference on women’s mental health [44, 45], recent research suggests these effects are also significant for men [46, 47]. Therefore, we included the son preference item in our assessment for both male and female participants. Specific items and their descriptive results are presented in Tables S1 and S2.

Covariates were controlled in the models, encompassing sociodemographic characteristics, health behaviors, health status, and childhood characteristics. Sociodemographic variables included baseline age, gender (0 = men, 1 = women), household registration (0 = rural, 1 = urban), education (ranging from 0 for illiterate to 10 for master’s degree), marital status, and living arrangements. For the measurements of marital status and living arrangements, participants who were married or who lived with their children during the survey were coded as 1 (otherwise 0). Health behaviors comprised smoking, drinking, and social participation. Consistent smoking or drinking during the survey was coded as 1 (otherwise, 0), while social participation was a continuous variable based on the average number of activities engaged in across the waves, with higher scores indicating greater participation. Health status was measured using three continuous variables, averaged across the survey period, with higher values indicating poorer health: self-rated health (1–5), number of chronic diseases (0–14), and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL; 5–20). Finally, we included controls for childhood health status (0 = good, 1 = poor) and childhood family economic conditions (0 = other, 1 = worse than other families).

Analytic strategy

To determine the optimal number of childhood maltreatment profiles and CESD trajectories, we used a multi-step process. First, we employed LCA to identify distinct profiles of childhood maltreatment. Second, we used Latent Class Growth Modeling (LCGM) to estimate heterogeneous trajectories of later-life CESD. We determined the optimal number of maltreatment profiles and CESD trajectories by comparing the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Sample-Size Adjusted BIC (aBIC), Entropy, Bootstrapped Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT), Lo-Mendell-Rubin Likelihood Ratio Test (LMR), and the minimum class size (MCS). The class with lower AIC, BIC, and aBIC values, higher entropy (> 0.7), significant LMR or BLRT results, and appropriate MCS (> 5%) was deemed optimal [2, 39, 48].

In this study, 12.12% (N = 2,145) of participants had missing data on CESD and childhood maltreatment, primarily due to missing responses on the maltreatment scale (N = 2,109; 11.91%). The logistic regression result indicated that older age, urban household registration, lower education, reduced social participation, poorer childhood health, and worse family economic conditions were associated with a higher likelihood of having missing data on maltreatment items (see Table S6). Following practices in prior research [2, 5, 39], we applied the Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) estimation to handle missing data in CESD and childhood maltreatment items. To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted several sensitivity analyses, including restricting the sample to participants with at least three follow-ups or excluding any cases with missing data. These analyses yielded largely consistent results (see Figures S2-S5).

Finally, we utilized multinomial logistic regression models to evaluate the influence of childhood maltreatment profiles on CESD trajectories. LCA and LCGM analyses were performed in Mplus 8.3, while all other analyses were conducted in Stata Version 17. All analyses were stratified by gender.

For both men and women, we labeled the five profiles as Low Maltreatment, Strict Parenting, Son Preference, Parental Neglect, and Multi-Perpetrator Physical Victimization. Furthermore, our findings indicate that maltreatment events are not isolated but often co-occur. For example, women in the Son Preference profile not only faced parental gender discrimination but also had higher probabilities of experiencing maternal neglect, parental favoritism toward siblings, and physical victimization (including paternal and sibling physical abuse, and peer bullying). In the Parental Neglect profile, characterized by parental physical and emotional neglect, men had a higher likelihood of experiencing parental physical abuse, while women were more likely to face maternal son preference, maternal and sibling physical abuse. Participants in the Multi-Perpetrator Physical Victimization profile experienced multiple forms of violence, including physical abuse from parents, siblings, and peers, as well as harsh parental discipline, for both genders.

CESD trajectories

Table S3 reports the fit indices for the LCGM. For men, the LMR(P) was non-significant, and the MCS was below 5% in the 5-trajectory model. For women, Entropy was below 0.7 in the 5-trajectory model. Thus, we selected the 4-trajectory model for both genders.

Figure 2 depicts the CESD trajectories by gender, labeled as Low-Stable, High-Decreasing, Moderate-Increasing, and High-Stable based on their characteristics. Notably, although the mean depressive symptom score showed a slight increase at the 2020 wave in Class 2, the overall pattern was downward. Following prior studies [5, 39], we labeled this trajectory “High-Decreasing.” Most participants’ CESD trajectories were classified as Low-Stable, followed by Moderate-Increasing, and finally High-Decreasing and High-Stable. Except for the Low-Stable group, the mean CESD scores in the three adverse trajectories (i.e., High-Decreasing, Moderate-Increasing, and High-Stable) exceeded 10, indicating clinically significant depression levels. Compared to men, women exhibited higher mean CESD scores across all trajectories and were more likely to follow the three problematic trajectories in later life (see Table S4).

Childhood maltreatment profiles and CESD trajectories

Table 3 presents the result of the association between childhood maltreatment profiles and CESD trajectories. Overall, suffering from any maltreatment profile was associated with an increased risk of belonging to the three adverse CESD trajectories compared to the Low-Stable trajectory for both genders. Among men, the Strict Parenting class had a significantly higher risk of being classified into the High-Stable trajectory compared to the Low Maltreatment class (RRR = 1.42, 95% CI [1.06, 1.89]). The Son Preference class showed elevated risks of being in the Moderate-Increasing (RRR = 1.93, 95% CI [1.45, 2.57]) or the High-Stable (RRR = 2.51, 95% CI [1.55, 4.05]) trajectories compared to the Low Maltreatment class. The Parental Neglect and Multi-Perpetrator Physical Victimization classes exhibited greater odds of being in the three adverse trajectories compared to the Low Maltreatment class. Among women, participants in the Son Preference, Parental Neglect, and Multi-Perpetrator Physical Victimization classes were less likely to have a Low-Stable trajectory and more likely to fall into one of the three adverse trajectories compared to those in the Low Maltreatment class. However, no significant association was found between the Strict Parenting class and later-life CESD trajectories in women.

Discussion

Prior research has linked childhood maltreatment to adverse mental health outcomes in older ages. Yet, it remains less clear about the co-occurring profiles of maltreatment, their implications for later-life mental health trajectories, and potential gender differences in these processes. Utilizing five waves of CHARLS data from 2011 to 2020, we identified five distinct childhood maltreatment profiles and four depressive symptoms trajectories, demonstrated the detrimental effects of maltreatment profiles on later-life depressive symptoms trajectories, and uncovered notable gender differences therein.

Childhood maltreatment profiles

Extending prior research that focused on individual childhood maltreatment events, we identified five distinct maltreatment profiles: Low Maltreatment, Strict Parenting, Son Preference, Parental Neglect, and Multi-Perpetrator Physical Victimization. Prior research has largely focused on single maltreatment types such as peer bullying [4, 15] or on the cumulative number of maltreatment events [16–18], approaches that may not adequately capture the complex patterns of maltreatment individuals experience [13]. Building on this literature, our study demonstrates that maltreatment experiences are not isolated occurrences but often co-occur, with many individuals exposed to multiple forms simultaneously. For example, we found that those who experienced peer bullying were also more likely to report physical abuse perpetrated by parents and siblings, consistent with prior evidence describing “a vicious cycle of abuse” [43, 49]. These findings not only support the cumulative risk model but also underscore that childhood maltreatment is not merely a simple sum of events; rather, different forms of victimization can overlap and reinforce one another, often with one pattern predominating, thereby exerting lasting adverse effects on mental health in later life.

In terms of distribution of membership, the finding that over 60% of middle-aged and older adults could be classified into maltreatment-related profiles suggests that childhood adversity exposure may be more widespread in older cohorts than is typically implied by studies in adolescents (15%) [50] or selected high-risk samples (25% ~ 45%) [11, 14]. Although differences in measurement may partly account for discrepancies across studies, our findings also suggest that the upbringing context experienced by middle-aged and older adults may be more conducive to the accumulation of adversities from multiple sources, underscoring the importance of incorporating a life-course perspective into research and practice on late-life mental health. In addition, among the maltreatment profiles identified, Strict Parenting being the most prevalent. The high prevalence of Strict Parenting contrasts with the situation in many Western developed countries, where any inappropriate parenting style is considered maltreatment [37, 51]. However, in China, particularly before the 1990 s, influenced by traditional beliefs, such as “spare the rod, spoil the child”, strict discipline was widely viewed as a beneficial and necessary child-rearing practice for establishing parental authority and promoting future achievements [9, 52, 53].

Maltreatment profiles for men and women, while optimally clustering in the same five classes, are unique in the proportional distribution of maltreatment profiles. Specifically, men predominantly suffered physical violence (19.71% vs. 8.48%), while women faced psychological maltreatment such as Strict Parenting, Son Preference, or Parental Neglect classes (54.12% vs. 50.26%), which is consistent with findings from both Chinese and Western studies [23, 54]. These differences are rooted in sociocultural factors and gendered expectations for socialization and behavior [23, 55]. For example, concepts like “stick education” in China led many parents to believe that harsh physical discipline was necessary for boys but not for girls [56]. Additionally, under the son preference culture, girls were often deemed less valuable than boys, resulting in their physical, emotional, and resource needs receiving less attention and investment compared to their male counterparts [35].

CESD trajectories

We identified four distinct trajectories of depressive symptoms in later life: Low-Stable, High-Decreasing, Moderate-Increasing, and High-Stable. Our findings align with several studies in China [5, 22, 28, 57], but differ from Western studies in terms of the number and distribution of these trajectories. In Western populations, depressive symptom trajectories among older adults tend to be more diverse, with studies identifying anywhere from three to six distinct patterns [58–61]. Additionally, while the Moderate-Increasing trajectory is predominant among Chinese older adults, as this study and prior research found [22, 28], Western studies more commonly report Decreasing or Subthreshold Stable trajectories [58, 59, 61]. Furthermore, the proportion of older adults in severe depressive symptom trajectory (i.e., High-Stable) is higher in our study (men: 7.78%, women: 11.17%) compared to the 2% to 6% typically found in Western studies [59–61]. These differences likely reflect the greater caregiving pressures faced by Chinese older adults due to inadequate social security systems compared to their Western counterparts. In addition, we cannot rule out the influence of cultural norms on the expression and reporting of depressive symptoms. Influenced by Confucian traditions that emphasize harmony and emotional restraint, and compounded by stigma toward depression, Chinese individuals may be inclined to suppress or underreport negative emotions [62], potentially leading to an underestimation of depressive symptom levels among older adults.

Similar to the maltreatment profiles, both men and women clustered into the same four trajectory types, yet they differed in the distribution of class membership and in mean symptom levels within each class. That is, women consistently exhibited higher mean CESD scores across all four trajectories compared to men and were more likely to be members of the three problematic trajectories. Overall, women exhibited poorer mental health outcomes than men in terms of both symptom severity and long-term progression, consistent with prior evidence [27, 63, 64]. Across the life course, women typically face greater exposure to stressors, such as higher risks of maltreatment or neglect in childhood, greater family–work conflict in adulthood, and widowhood in later life, while having fewer coping resources, which may contribute to persistently worse mental health than men in both average levels and dynamic trajectories [63–65].

Childhood maltreatment profiles and CESD trajectories

Childhood maltreatment profiles significantly associated with adverse depressive symptoms trajectories in later life, which is in line with the critical period model and the pathway model. First, we found that Strict Parenting was a significant predictor of the High-Stable trajectory among men. Recent research on Chinese children has likewise documented the psychological consequences of strict parenting, including elevated risks of anxiety, depression, and suicidal ideation, with effects that appear particularly pronounced among boys [66–68]. This pattern can be interpreted through a gendered socialization framework in the Chinese context. Historically, strict parenting has been embedded in the Confucian cultural norms, emphasizing obedience, discipline, and meeting familial expectations [69]. For boys in particular, such strictness may be more readily internalized as role-based obligation and self-suppression, consistent with authoritarian filial orientations that stress compliance with parental demands and have been linked to maladaptive outcomes including depression and anxiety [70]. In addition, traditional masculinity norms such as emotional control and self-reliance, and higher depression-related stigma among men, may encourage avoidant coping and reduce emotional disclosure and help-seeking, potentially contributing to persistent distress [62, 71]. Our finding also highlights that although harsh parenting styles have been culturally normalized and socially accepted, they can still have serious long-term mental health consequences.

Second, our study extends prior research by demonstrating that Son Preference is associated with poorer mental health not only among women but also among men, increasing their likelihood of belonging to the Moderate-Increasing or High–Stable trajectories. This extends prior research that has largely focused on the adverse effects of son preference on women’s well-being [34, 44]. On the one hand, boys raised in son-preference families are often burdened with heightened expectations to uphold the family lineage and achieve career success. These expectations can translate into strict discipline and excessive demands, which impose significant psychological burdens throughout their lives [47]. On the other hand, our findings show that men in son-preference families also have a higher likelihood of experiencing parental favoritism toward other siblings, sibling physical abuse, and peer bullying. Such experiences can trigger feelings of worthlessness and low self-esteem, ultimately impairing their ability to handle stressful situations throughout the life course [72].

Finally, both men and women in the Parental Neglect or Multi-Perpetrator Physical Victimization profiles were more likely to fall into one of the three problematic depressive symptoms trajectories, consistent with prior evidence [33]. Exposure to parental neglect during childhood can disrupt early parent–child interactions, foster insecure attachment, and elevate the risk of depression across the life course [9, 73]. Moreover, negative emotions from childhood neglect may be reactivated by adverse events in later life, resulting in persistently elevated depressive symptoms [5]. This effect may be particularly pronounced for men in a son-preference society, who feel a strong sense of entitlement to parental care and attention, making the experience of neglect especially damaging. In addition, our findings on Multi-Perpetrator Physical Victimization extend prior work that has typically examined the independent associations of peer bullying or within-family physical violence with average levels of depressive symptoms [3, 4, 15]. As the pathway model suggested, childhood physical victimization may not only dysregulate physiological functioning and undermine stress tolerance and coping, but also shape later-life mental health indirectly through education, occupation, social relationships, and coping resources [3, 7–9]. Recent evidence further suggests that even when individuals are no longer exposed to abuse or neglect, subsequent improvements may not fully offset the magnitude of earlier harms [74]. Together, these findings underscore that childhood neglect and abuse are not merely binary or static events; their timing, duration, and evolution may uniquely shape depressive symptom trajectories in later life.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the use of self-reported childhood maltreatment experiences may be subject to recall bias. In CHARLS, childhood maltreatment experiences were collected based on respondents’ recollections, which may introduce discrepancies between reported and actual experiences and raise the possibility that individuals with current psychological distress may be more likely to report adverse childhood experiences. Nevertheless, prior work indicates that retrospective reports of childhood adversity can be recalled with relatively high accuracy [75] and are not affected by respondents’ contemporaneous psychological state [76, 77]. In addition, all childhood maltreatment indicators were recoded into binary variables in this study. This dichotomization treats experiences of different severity as equivalent, which may underestimate within-profile heterogeneity and render between-profile differences more conservative. Third, due to data limitations, this study could not include other types of maltreatment, such as sexual abuse and cyberbullying, all of which have been shown to significantly impact mental health. Finally, while we found a significant association, this study did not explore the physiological, psychological, and social mechanisms linking childhood maltreatment profiles to later-life mental health trajectories. For example, the specific reasons why Strict Parenting leads to poorer mental health trajectories among men in later life remain an open question, but addressing this was beyond the scope of this study.

Despite these limitations, this study makes several important contributions to the existing literature, both empirically and theoretically. Empirically, by employing the LCA, we moved beyond the simplistic approach of treating all maltreatment events as equivalent, instead providing an objective classification of distinct childhood maltreatment profiles. Second, this study utilized five waves of data spanning approximately a decade, offering a more accurate and nuanced depiction of depressive symptom trajectories in later life than previous research, which often relied on only three time points. Finally, by stratifying all analyses by gender, we were able to capture subtle but crucial gender differences in maltreatment profiles, mental health trajectories, and their relationships in a rapidly aging society.

At the theoretical level, our findings provide explicit support and refinement of life-course epidemiology frameworks. First, our findings on childhood maltreatment profiles support and extend the cumulative risk model. The cumulative risk model emphasizes that children are often exposed to multiple co-occurring risk factors that jointly produce substantial adverse consequences for development. However, prior research has typically tested this model using the overall amount of risk exposure rather than the patterning of risks. Using the Latent Class Analysis, we identified five distinct maltreatment profiles and showed that maltreatment experiences are neither isolated events nor simply additive; instead, they co-occur. This provides clear evidence in support of the cumulative risk model. Moreover, we found that these maltreatment experiences tend to cluster around a predominant feature, forming different configurations, which in turn extends the cumulative risk model by highlighting patterned exposure beyond total burden. Second, our findings linking maltreatment profiles to depressive symptoms trajectories support and extend the critical period model. The critical period model posits that exposure to maltreatment during childhood, a key developmental period, can lead to poorer mental health in later life. Our results indicate that different maltreatment profiles are associated not only with poorer levels of mental health but also with the developmental course of mental health in later life, shaping heterogeneous depressive symptom trajectories. In this sense, our findings provide additional support for, and extend, the critical period model. Finally, in line with the pathway model, the observed associations between maltreatment profiles and depressive symptoms trajectories are plausibly maintained through cascading disadvantages across the life course, such as impaired physiological and socio-psychological functioning, constrained socioeconomic attainment, cumulative health disadvantages, and limited coping resources in older age, although this study does not test these pathways.

Conclusion

In summary, this study identified five distinct childhood maltreatment profiles and their detrimental effects on later-life depressive symptoms trajectories. Gender differences were evident in the distribution of both maltreatment profiles and depressive symptoms trajectories. However, the detrimental impacts of childhood maltreatment profiles on depressive symptoms trajectories were significant in both men and women. Our findings advance the understanding of the configurations of childhood maltreatment and their long-term mental health consequences, as well as the gender differences involved. Future research should incorporate additional maltreatment types, such as sexual abuse and cyberbullying, and conduct cross-national comparative studies to explore the mechanisms linking childhood maltreatment to later-life mental health. From a policy perspective, our results underscore the urgent need for gender-specific interventions aimed at eliminating all forms of maltreatment—both within and outside the family, in physical and digital spaces—during childhood, which is a critical developmental window.
