Submicron-oleogel particles for enhanced oral delivery of hydrophobic compounds: In vitro and in vivo proof of concept
Authors: Areen Ashkar, Rania Awad, Uri Lesmes, Alejandro Sosnik, Maya Davidovich-Pinhas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Oral administration is the preferred route for drug and bioactive delivery, although it raises great challenges due to the involvement of the gastrointestinal system and limited bioavailability. Herein, a novel submicron-oleogel particle system was developed using micro-structured edible oil to address the challenges associated with the low bioavailability of oral hydrophobic compounds. Oleogel particles were prepared using β-sitosterol and γ-oryzanol as oil structuring agents with dispersed dasatinib as a model drug. A particle size of approximately 160 nm and zeta-potential of around −25 mV were achieved along with a high drug entrapment efficiency of ∼90 %. In vitro analysis revealed greater stability of the submicron-oleogel particles during the gastric phase with minimal particle coalescence and significantly low dasatinib release. In the subsequent intestinal phase, the dasatinib bioaccessibility was enhanced through its integration into self-assembled mixed micelles formed during digestion. The presence of lipid byproducts and structuring agents played a crucial role in modulating this colloidal architecture and facilitating dasatinib solubilization, thereby improving its overall availability. An in vivo pharmacokinetic study confirmed enhanced dasatinib bioavailability in oil-containing formulations, attributed to improved micellization during digestion, which facilitated intestinal transport and overall absorption. Moreover, the submicron-oleogel particles displayed sustained release and absorption characteristics, attributed to the oleogel's mechanical durability. Overall, this study highlighted the enhanced therapeutic response while using oleogel particles, with a strong association between the in vitro and in vivo results. These findings underscore the potential of this novel carrier to advance the development of effective oral formulations for hydrophobic compounds.

Introduction

Orally administered compounds encounter various obstacles as they transit the gastrointestinal tract (GIT). This journey is influenced by the unique physicochemical characteristics of the digestive system, which faces three primary barriers, including biochemical, physical, and cellular, leading to degradation, inactivation, restricted penetration, and reduced bioavailability [1,2]. After gastrointestinal absorption, drugs and nutrients are transported to the liver via the portal vein, undergoing hepatic first-pass metabolism, which reduces bioavailability [1,3,4]. These intricacies are exacerbated for medications with limited water solubility, resulting in ineffective therapeutic outcomes. It is widely recognized that poorly water-soluble drug candidates are increasingly prevalent, with approximately 45 % of marketed drugs and 80 % of drugs in the discovery pipeline encountering challenges associated with bioavailability owing to low aqueous solubility [[5], [6], [7]]. Therefore, it is imperative to devise innovative strategies to improve the effectiveness of oral drug delivery.

Lipids have garnered considerable attention as biological materials for oral compound delivery due to their improved biocompatibility and enhanced efficacy [8]. Several research endeavors developed lipid-based carriers, including micelles [9], liposomes [10], and emulsions, aiming to optimize therapeutic outcomes while minimizing adverse effects. Recently, there has been growing interest in investigating structured lipid-based particles derived from oil-in-water emulsions, in which their organization into various physical states, either partially or fully solidified forms, is accomplished through a controlled crystallization process [11]. Importantly, the inclusion of solid matrices has the potential to enhance the physicochemical stability of encapsulated substances by restricting their diffusion into the aqueous phase [12]. Solid lipid nanoparticles are the first generation of lipid-based carriers distinguished by a fully crystallized lipid phase where the lipid is in a solid state at physiological temperature. However, a notable limitation arises from the polymorphic transition of solid lipids during storage, which leads to stability concerns regarding drug and excipient crystallinity and dissolution rate [13], which, in turn, affect bioavailability. Ongoing research endeavors aimed to mitigate these challenges by utilizing a partially crystallized lipid matrix in nanostructured lipid carriers. These carriers feature a semisolid lipid phase composed of a solid and liquid lipid blend, typically in a weight ratio ranging from 70:30 to 99.9:0.1 solid: liquid [14,15]. This approach limits the amount of drug that can be incorporated due to the relatively low liquid phase content.

A novel approach suggested for solidifying liquid lipids has been coined oil-based gel or oleogel [16]. Oleogelation is the process of imparting solid properties to liquid edible oils by incorporating various organic structuring agents. Oleogel is typically produced by dissolving structuring agents in oil at elevated temperatures, followed by cooling below its gelation transition temperature [[17], [18], [19]]. During this cooling process, a three-dimensional network forms, entrapping the oil phase. The diverse array of structuring agents can result in varied gelator constituents and diverse gelation mechanisms, yielding a spectrum of nanoscale and microscale structures alongside desirable macroscopic mechanical, textural, and rheological attributes. Furthermore, the rational design and development of oleogels, through manipulating preparation conditions and selecting structuring agents, has demonstrated the capability to achieve the desired functionality and digestibility [20,21]. This unique architecture formulates solid texture with a very high liquid content up to 98 %wt. thus allowing mechanical protection as well as high drug loading as opposed to the nanostructured lipid carriers described above.

In recent years, researchers have shown significant interest in utilizing combinations of phytosterols, particularly β-sitosterol, along with γ-oryzanol as a structuring agent due to their remarkable synergistic gelling properties and Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) status [22]. β-sitosterol is a natural component found in several vegetable oils, whereas γ-oryzanol is a phytochemical derived from rice bran oil that consists of an ester of ferulic acid with a range of closely related plant sterols. Individually, β-sitosterol can self-assemble into crystalline structures within the oil phase. Conversely, γ-oryzanol exhibits limited gelation ability on its own and typically requires specific conditions to form a stable gel [23]. However, β-sitosterol and γ-oryzanol combined undergo self-assembly, forming elongated hollow tubular structures. This arrangement entails stacking sitosterol units, while the ferulic acid group of γ-oryzanol protrudes outward from the surface of the tubule [24,25]. Subsequently, these individual tubular entities connect and autonomously organize into a sophisticated three-dimensional fibrillar network. This intricate network effectively ensnares the oil base, creating a translucent and robust oleogel [26].

In a prior investigation, we explored the utilization of oleogelation technology for the oral delivery of β-carotene, specifically focusing on the in vitro digestion process [27]. The results showed that oleogel matrices significantly improve the release and subsequent bioaccessibility of β-carotene within the GIT compared to their dispersion in free form or liquid oil. The partition coefficient (logP) of β-carotene is approximately 17.6, signifying its high hydrophobicity. This makes β-carotene highly soluble in non-polar solvents like fats and oils while showing limited solubility in aqueous mediums. Conversely, substances characterized by lower logP values demonstrate reduced solubility in natural lipids but enhanced solubility in amphiphilic compounds [28]. Consequently, encapsulating substances with low logP values within oleogel-based systems poses greater challenges.

To expand the applicability of this delivery platform beyond extremely hydrophobic molecules, dasatinib, a tyrosine kinase inhibitor used in cancer therapy, was selected as a model drug. Dasatinib is classified as a Biopharmaceutics Classification System class II compound, characterized by low aqueous solubility and high intestinal permeability [29,30]. With a logP value of approximately 3.83, dasatinib exhibits moderate lipophilicity. It has relatively low solubility in oil, while its solubility in aqueous environments is even lower, particularly under physiological conditions (e.g., <1 μg/mL at pH 6.8). This poor solubility and its tendency to crystallize under supersaturated conditions pose significant challenges for oral delivery, making it a suitable candidate for evaluating the potential of submicron-oleogel particles as carriers for poorly water-soluble drugs.

In this study, we aimed to design a delivery vehicle based on a combination of β-sitosterol and γ-oryzanol (SiOr) submicron-oleogel particles for the oral administration of dasatinib. The in vitro digestion performance of submicron-oleogel particles was compared to that of conventional oil droplet-based emulsions, with a focus on lipolytic intermediate products and dasatinib fate. Furthermore, a pharmacokinetic study in rats was conducted to evaluate the performance of this novel drug delivery platform and compare it to the free drug. Despite the promising capabilities of oleogel-in-water based technology, reports on its application in oral delivery, particularly for hydrophobic drugs, are still relatively limited. Previous studies have primarily focused on the use of oleogels to enhance the oral bioavailability of lipophilic nutrients such as β-carotene and curcumin, demonstrating improved solubilization and bioaccessibility during digestion [8,31,32]. However, most of these studies employed simple oil-based gel matrices without extensive characterization of in vitro colloidal behaviour or in vivo performance. In contrast, the present study introduces a submicron-oleogel particle approach, explores its digestive fate in detail, and includes pharmacokinetic evaluation using dasatinib as a challenging BCS Class II drug model, thus extending the scope of oleogel systems beyond nutritional compounds into the pharmaceutical domain.

Materials and methods

Materials

The oil structuring agents, γ-oryzanol (98 %) and β-sitosterol (87.7 %), were purchased from Tsuno Rice Fine Chemicals (Japan) and Sigma-Aldrich (Israel), respectively. Canola oil was purchased from a local supermarket, and Tween® 80 was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Israel). The following materials were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Israel): pepsin from porcine gastric mucosa (enzymatic activity of ≥250 units/mg protein), type II porcine pancreatic lipase (enzymatic activity of 100–650 units/mg protein), and 4-bromophenylboronic acid (4-BPB). Hydrochloric acid 32 % (HCl), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), absolute ethanol, and Dimethyl Sulfoxide (DMSO) were purchased from Bio-Lab Ltd. (Israel), whereas CaCl2· 2H2O was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Israel). Bile extract was obtained from Chem Cruz (Santa Cruz Biotechnology Inc., USA). Dasatinib-free base monohydrate was ordered from Carbosynth Ltd. (Compton, UK). Acetonitrile was purchased from Avantor Performance Materials (Poland).

Preparation of dasatinib-loaded oil droplets and submicron-oleogel particles

Structured and unstructured oil-in-water emulsions were prepared using hot high-shear homogenization technique, as described recently, with some modification [27]. Each formulation was prepared at a total volume of 10 g. The dispersed phase, consisting of 20 % (w/w) canola oil, included a blend with a fixed ratio of 40:60 w/w β-sitosterol and γ-oryzanol in two different total concentrations of 0 % and 10 % (w/w, out of the oil phase), along with dasatinib at 0.5 % (w/w out of the oil phase). Dasatinib was incorporated at 0.5 % w/w based on preliminary solubility and stability assessments to ensure adequate drug loading and analytical detectability. The continuous phase, comprising 80 % (w/w), consists of an aqueous solution with Tween® 80 (5 % w/w).

The canola oil/SiOr mixture was heated to 85 °C while continuously stirring at 200 rpm on a magnetic hot plate until full dissolution was achieved. Concurrently, dasatinib was dissolved in 2.5 mL of absolute ethanol at a temperature of 75 °C. This ethanol-dasatinib solution was gradually added to the hot molten oil phase and stirred until complete ethanol evaporation occurred, resulting in a homogenous hot dasatinib-oil suspension. Meanwhile, in a separate container, the continuous water phase was heated to 85 °C. Subsequently, the hot dasatinib-oil suspension was dripped into the aqueous phase while being stirred at 600 rpm for 1 min. Finally, the hot pre-emulsion was immediately homogenized in a high shear homogenizer (Ultra-Turrax, D-9, MICCRA GmbH, Germany) at 35,000 rpm for 1 min and 85 °C. The obtained hot emulsions were gradually cooled at room temperature (RT) for 3 h to induce the solidification of the oil particles and then stored at 4 °C for 24 h before further analysis. The hot emulsion prepared without SiOr was further treated using a probe sonicator for 3 min at 40 % amplitude (Vibracell 750 W, Sonics, USA) to attain uniformity in the particle size distribution between the two formulations.

Optical microscope

Microstructure observations of the dasatinib-loaded oil phase and the SiOr-based oleogel with and without dasatinib were carried out using a BX51TRF light microscope (Olympus, Eisenberg Brothers, Israel) equipped with a polarized light mode to ascertain the crystallinity of dasatinib. A small quantity of freshly prepared hot formulation was applied onto a microscope slide and covered with a cover glass, stored at 4 °C for 24 h prior to microstructure observation. Images were captured at RT using an Olympus DP71 digital camera.

X-ray diffraction

The nano-structural examination of the dasatinib crystal structure was carried out using X-ray diffraction analysis. X-ray diffraction was carried out using a SmartLab 3 kW X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku, Rhenium, Israel) equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.5406 Å), operated at 40 kV and 30 mA. The experiment was performed at RT with a scanning rate of 1° min−1, 0.5° scattering slit, 0.03° divergence slit, and a 12 mm receiving slit. The reflection peaks, d (Å), are calculated according to the Bragg formula (d = λ/2sin(θ)). The freshly prepared samples were directly deposited onto pre-heated glass sample holders, cooled to RT, and stored at 4 °C for approximately 24 h before analysis.

Entrapment efficiency

The amount of dasatinib encapsulated in the submicron-oleogel particles was determined using a UV–vis spectroscopy method. Briefly, formulations were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min. An aliquot of 20 μl of the supernatant was diluted in 980 μl of DMSO, mixed by vortex for 10 s, and then sonicated for 10 min in a hot water bath at 80 °C to release the dasatinib from the particles. Subsequently, the samples were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatant was collected. The dasatinib absorbance was measured using a microplate spectrophotometer (Varioskan Flash, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at a wavelength of 324 nm, following previously reported methods [33]. A calibration curve of dasatinib in DMSO was generated in a concentration range of 0.2–50 μg/mL (R2 > 0.99). Three replicates of each independent experiment were measured. A series of blank samples without dasatinib was prepared and treated similarly. The entrapment efficiency (EE%) was calculated as:(1)EE%=WeWt×100%where We denote the amount of dasatinib found in the sample, and Wt is the total amount of dasatinib added in the formulation.

Particle size analysis

The particle size distribution of the dasatinib-loaded oil droplets and submicron-oleogel particles was analyzed at RT using a laser diffraction-size analyzer (Mastersizer 3000, Malvern Instruments Ltd, Dr. Golik, Israel). Refractive Index values of 1.47 and 1.33 were selected for oil and water, respectively, for all samples. The formulation was inserted dropwise into the measurement cell filled with 125 mL of Milli-Q water until the laser obscuration achieved 7.0 ± 0.5 % at a speed of 1200 rpm to ensure homogeneity. The size information was reported by the mean volume-weighted particle diameter d43 = ∑nidi4/∑nidi3 and the mean surface-weighted particle diameter d32 = ∑nidi3/∑nidi2, where ni is the number of particles of diameter di.

Zeta-potential

The electric potential (ζ-potential) of both oil droplets and submicron-oleogel particles was evaluated at RT by measuring electrophoretic mobility using a dynamic laser scattering coupled with capillary electrophoresis (Zetasizer Nano ZS series, Malvern Instruments, Dr. Golik, Israel). For the measurement, emulsions were diluted 1000-fold in MilliQ water, and 700 μL of the resultant solution was loaded into a measurement cell (DTS1070) with a stabilization time of 2 min.

In vitro digestion model

The in vitro digestibility of the samples was conducted according to the INFOGEST international protocol with minor modifications [34]. The in vitro gastric-intestinal digestion was conducted in a glass vessel with a thermostat jacket maintained at 37 °C with mechanical agitation using an auto-titration unit (Titrando 902, Metrohm, Switzerland). The samples were placed in the reaction vessel with simulated digestive fluids while the pH was continuously monitored and adjusted by “TIAMO 2.0” software (Metrohm). Initially, the activities of the digestive enzymes were experimentally determined using recommended standardized assays. The digestion process involves sequentially exposing the formulations to gastric and small intestinal phases containing electrolytes, enzymes, and bile salts while considering factors such as dilution, stirring, pH, and duration of digestion. Based on physiological data, electrolyte stock solutions for SGF and simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) were formulated using a combination of KCl, KH2PO4, NaCl, MgCl2·6H2O, and (NH4)2CO3 at different concentrations [34].

Gastric phase

Briefly, the samples were diluted with water to achieve a final oil concentration of 1.25 % (v/v) out of the small intestine phase. Then, the diluted sample was mixed with SGF at a ratio of 1:1 (vol/vol), containing pepsin (2000 U/mL in final chyme) and CaCl2 solution (0.15 mM in SGF). The gastric phase was incubated for 120 min with continuous stirring at 200 rpm using three pH stages: pH 2.8 for 20 min, pH 1.8 for an additional 40 min, and a final gastric step of 60 min at pH 1.5. The gastric pH gradient was achieved by automatic titration of 0.25 M HCl solution.

In vitro dasatinib release during the gastric phase

The release of dasatinib from oil droplets and submicron-oleogel particles under gastric conditions was conducted via the dialysis bag diffusion technique. For this, 2 mL of lipid-based formulations, corresponding to ∼2 mg of dasatinib (based on 0.5 % w/w drug content in the 20 % oil phase), were introduced into cellulose dialysis bags with a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) of 3500 Da (Cellu Sup® T1; Membrane Filtration Products, Inc., Seguin, Texas, USA). This MWCO was selected to allow diffusion of free dasatinib molecules (MW = 488.0 g/mol) into the release medium while retaining larger colloidal structures. As a control, an aqueous suspension of dasatinib containing an equivalent drug dose (2 mg in 2 mL) was prepared and subjected to the same experimental conditions to provide a direct comparison of release kinetics from the lipid-based formulations. These bags were subsequently submerged in 50 mL of SGF with a pH of 2, serving as the release medium. The system was stirred at 100 rpm and maintained at a temperature of 37 °C ± 0.5 °C for 2 h. At specific time intervals (i.e., 5, 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, and 120 min), samples of 0.5 mL were withdrawn from the release medium and replaced with an equal volume of freshly pre-warmed SGF. Subsequently, these samples were assayed for released drug content at 324 nm against a blank using a UV–vis spectrophotometer.

Small intestine phase

At the end of the gastric phase, the pH was fine-tuned to align with the intestinal pH (∼pH 7.0). Following this, pre-dissolved bile in SIF (10 mM of digesta) and CaCl2 solution (0.6 mM in SIF) were introduced, and the pH was adjusted to 7.0 if necessary. Afterward, pancreatic lipase suspension (2000 U/mL in the final digest) was instantly added to the reaction container. Overall, an equal volume of SIF was mixed with gastric chyme to achieve a final ratio of 1:1 (v/v). During the small intestinal phase, which lasted 120 min with continuous stirring at 400 rpm, the pH was maintained at a constant value of 7.0 through automatic titration of 0.05 M alkaline solution. A control experiment, containing dasatinib dispersion, was used under the same conditions as the emulsion formulations.

Free fatty acids release

The rate and extent of lipid hydrolysis during the simulated intestinal phase were assessed by monitoring the volume of NaOH solution required to neutralize the free fatty acid (FFA) generated from the oil digestion. A blank test was carried out in the absence of samples to determine the contribution of any non-lipid components to the titration curves. The volume of NaOH was converted into the percentage of FFA (%FFA) normalized with the initial oil content for each formulation using the following equation:(2)FFA=100×((VNaOH−VBlank)×MNaOH×MWlipid2×Wlipid)where, VNaOH and VBlank are the NaOH volume titrated during the small intestine digestion of the sample and blank, respectively, MNaOH is the molarity of NaOH in molar units, MWlipid is the molecular weight of the lipid phase, and Wlipid is the initial weight of oil in the small intestine phase. The molecular weight of canola oil was taken as 882.9 gmol-1.

Micellar phase

During the simulated intestinal phase incubation, a 150 μL aliquot of digesta was withdrawn at predetermined time intervals to isolate the formed micellar phase and investigate its properties. After the collection, a lipase inhibitor was immediately added to prevent further lipid hydrolysis (9 μL/mL of a 0.5 M 4-BPB in methanol per). Afterward, the digesta samples were subjected to centrifugation (Sigma 1–14 Microcentrifuge, Getter BioMed, Israel) at 14,300 g for 20 min at 25 °C forming three distinct phases (i) at the top an oil phase containing undigested oil and non-emulsified lipids, (ii) in the middle a clear aqueous phase containing mixed lipid micelles and vesicles, and (iii) at the bottom a precipitated pellet comprised of the precipitated drug, calcium soaps of FFAs, and digestive enzymes. The micellar phase was used for further analysis of micelle size and distribution, morphology, and drug bioaccessibility.

Dasatinib bioaccessibility

Bioaccessibility refers to the fraction of dasatinib transferred into the micellar phase structure relative to the initial content. A total of 100 μL of the middle aqueous micellar phase was harvested and transferred to 900 μL DMSO, and then vortexed for 10 s. Afterward, the dasatinib extraction was done by thermal denaturation of the micellar phase by incubating the samples in a 100 °C water bath for 10 min. The solutions obtained were allowed to cool to RT and subsequently centrifuged for an additional 2 min at 5000 g. The supernatant was removed and measured for its absorbance in the wavelength range of 200–350 nm using a microplate reader (Varioskan Flash). A series of blank samples without dasatinib were digested and treated similarly. This entire analysis was conducted in triplicate. The concentration of dasatinib was determined using a standard curve of dasatinib at different concentrations in DMSO. The bioaccessibility was then calculated using the following equation:(3)Bioaccessibility=amountofsolubilizeddasatinibinmicellarphasetotalamountofdasatinibinoriginalformulation×100

Size and ζ-potential determinations

The size distribution of colloidal structures in the micellar phase was determined using dynamic light scattering (Zetasizer Nano ZS series, Malvern Instruments). To minimize multiple scattering, the samples were diluted with SIF at a ratio of 1:100 (v/v). Results were reported as the mean value by intensity of the peaks and PDI. The electrophoretic mobility of the micellar phase was also measured, as described above. Measurements of size and ζ-potential were conducted in triplicate.

Cryogenic transmission electron microscopy

The morphology of the colloidal structures in the micellar phase was investigated using a Thermo-Fisher Scientific Talos 200C, high-resolution TEM. Samples were prepared by applying a small amount of the micellar phase on 200-mesh Cu grids coated with lacey formvar/carbon films (Ted Pella Inc., Redding, USA). The grid was blotted with filter paper to form a thin film and plunged into liquid ethane at its freezing point (−180 °C) using a controlled environment vitrification system. The specimens were stored in liquid nitrogen Dewar flasks until imaging. The specimens were transferred using a Gatan 626 cryo-holder (Gatan, USA) into the high-resolution TEM, equipped with a field emission gun (FEG), using 200 kV accelerating voltage, under low-dose electron beam settings, and maintained at −180 °C during imaging.

UV spectroscopic measurement

The micellar phase composition was also investigated using a UV microplate reader (Epoch 2, BioTek, USA). Absorbance measurements of various digested formulations were performed using a UV microplate reader over a wavelength range of 190–390 nm.

Oral pharmacokinetics study

The oral in vivo pharmacokinetics were assessed in albino rats (Sprague Dawley, Envigo, Israel) following the approval of the preclinical protocols by the Committee of Animal Welfare of Technion (#IL-035-03-18 and #IL-013-01-22).

After an overnight fast with free access to water, three groups of male rats (n = 4) weighing 250 ± 20 g were administered dasatinib at a dose of 10 mg/kg via oral gavage. The dasatinib dose was chosen based on literature precedent for oral administration in rat models, ensuring relevance for comparative pharmacokinetic evaluation and maintaining consistency with established in vivo studies [35,36]. The administered volume was adjusted accordingly to ensure the same dasatinib dose across all groups, based on the quantified drug concentration within each formulation. The dasatinib was delivered in three formulations: a structured oil-based emulsion (submicron-oleogel particles), an unstructured oil-based emulsion (emulsion), and a water suspension, each at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. At predetermined time intervals (0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 24, and 48 h), blood samples were withdrawn into tubes containing potassium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid as anticoagulant (Greiner Bio-One, Danyel biotech, Israel). Next, the blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 3 min at 4 °C (HERMLE Labortechnik GmbH, Germany), and then the supernatant (plasma) was removed and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Pharmacokinetic analysis based on dasatinib detection was conducted by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Alliance, Waters Corp., USA) with a separation module e2695 equipped with an XBridge C18 column (3.5 μm, 4.6 × 250 mm, Waters Corp., Ireland) and a UV–vis detector (2998 Photoiodide Array UV/Vis 2D detector, W2998, Waters Corp.). Before analysis, 30 μL of the plasma was first mixed with 150 μL of methanol and vortexed for 1 min, followed by adding 150 μL of chloroform, and the mixture was vortexed for another 3 min, centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min, and then the organic solvents were subsequently collected. Fresh chloroform (150 μL) was added to the residue, and the procedure was repeated twice to ensure complete recovery of dasatinib from the plasma samples. The chloroform phase was pooled with the organic phase and dried under nitrogen. The residue was reconstituted with 100 μL of acetonitrile by vortexing for 1 min, and then 20 μL of the sample was injected into the HPLC system. The mobile phase consisted of 0.02M potassium phosphate buffer (pH = 7.4): methanol (20:80 v/v) and was pumped at a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min with a detection wavelength of 324 nm.

Pharmacokinetic analysis

The calculation of the following pharmacokinetic parameters was performed by non-compartmental analysis using the PKSolver add-in program [37]: (i) Cmax, (ii) the time required to reach the Cmax (Tmax), (iii) area under the curve (AUC0-∞), and (iv) half-life (T1/2). The area under the plasma concentration curve from the time of administration (AUC0-∞) was calculated using the trapezoidal rule with extrapolation to infinity, while Cmax and Tmax were directly obtained from the curves. The elimination half-life (T1/2) was calculated from the elimination rate constant (ke) estimated by a linear-square regression on the final plasmatic concentrations of dasatinib.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7.0 software. The data was compared to detect significant variances between samples through ANOVA, followed by a one-way with Tukey post-test. An average of at least three replicates, with error bars indicating the standard error of the mean, was plotted using GraphPad.

Results

Structural analysis of dasatinib-loaded oleogel

Dasatinib is a lipophilic basic drug (logP 3.83; water solubility <1 μg/mL at pH 7) with a strong crystal lattice structure [38]. Two solid crystal formulations of dasatinib tablets have been approved for clinical use, namely SPRYCEL® (Bristol-Myers Squibb) and YINISHU® (a generic formulation commercialized in China) [35]. Generally, the gastrointestinal absorption of crystalline drugs is predominantly constrained by their dissolution kinetics during their transit through the GIT. Larger crystals exhibit slower dissolution rates, while smaller ones dissolve more rapidly due to the larger surface area [39]. Furthermore, the nanonization process of dasatinib results in a notable enhancement in its oral bioavailability by increasing the drug dissolution rate and its ability to adhere to the gastrointestinal mucus [36]. In this work, we investigated for the first time a novel kind of hierarchical carrier based on edible oils and tubular sterol assemblies for the encapsulation and oral administration of dasatinib. Therefore, it is imperative to thoroughly understand the variables influencing the dimensions of dasatinib crystals within the lipidic delivery vehicle and correlate them to the performance in vivo. Canola oil was selected as the lipid phase due to its high content of long-chain unsaturated fatty acids, which promote lymphatic transport in the small intestine and undergo slower lipase-mediated degradation, contributing to sustained drug release. Moreover, its cost-effectiveness and broad availability support its suitability as an oil phase in oral drug delivery formulations. The structure of the oleogel containing dasatinib was analyzed across varying scales. On the macroscopic level (Fig. 1a), dasatinib dispersed in oil exhibited discernible crystal aggregates and large clusters. Subsequent polarized-light microscope examination of the SiOr-based oleogel revealed the existence of an unexpected microscopic sterol crystal, Fig. 1a. It seems that some β-sitosterol molecules did not effectively interact with γ-oryzanol to form nanoscale tubular structures and remained free to self-assemble and crystallize. Reports on the gelation mechanisms of SiOr-based oleogels have demonstrated that different crystalline conformations can be developed, particularly when there is an excess of β-sitosterol, leading to differences in nucleation site development [40]. It is worth noting that the fibrillar network could not be clearly visualized in the optical microscopy image due to resolution limitations, as these structures are typically on the nanometer scale and require higher-resolution imaging techniques for proper observation. More importantly, further investigation into the formation of dasatinib crystals in the presence of the fibrillar network showed a reduction in their size compared to samples without the SiOr. This reduction is believed to arise from interactions between dasatinib and the structuring agent, as well as potential influences of the fibrillar network on the dasatinib crystallization process. Such behavior suggests that the mere gelation of β-sitosterol and γ-oryzanol can affect the crystallization and organization of dasatinib in the oil phase, which may affect the oral bioavailability of the drug. On a nanoscale (Fig. 1b), the presence of crystalline structures in the dasatinib-loaded oil is evidenced by reflection peaks at 12.9, 7.2, 6.6, and 6.4 Å, as previously reported [41]. For the oleogel, two reflection peaks at 6.6 and 6.1 Å were observed, corresponding to the crystals observed in the polarized microscope analysis. Comparable reflection peaks were also observed within the range of 5.77–6.11 Å in SiOr-based oleogel [40], which closely aligns with the documented crystalline structures of pure β-sitosterol in oil [42]. The analysis indicates that the dasatinib-loaded oleogel exhibited a similar characteristic peak pattern as the dasatinib-loaded oil, suggesting the formation of a similar drug polymorphic form when integrated into the oleogel matrix.

Characterization of dasatinib-loaded oil droplets and submicron-oleogel particles

Two formulations were prepared using a hot high-shear homogenization technique with Tween 80 as a surface-active agent. Tween 80, a non-ionic surfactant with an HLB value of ∼15, is widely used in oral lipid-based formulations due to its low toxicity and ability to enhance drug absorption. Its efficacy is attributed to inhibition of P-glycoprotein efflux, increased membrane permeability, and promotion of lymphatic transport through chylomicron stimulation [43,44]. The resulting formulations differ in the physical state of the lipid phase; one is in a liquid state using solely canola oil, while the second is solidified to a gel state by incorporating 10 %wt. SiOr. Furthermore, a dasatinib aqueous suspension was employed as a control group for comparison. The characteristics of the dasatinib suspension, dasatinib-loaded submicron-oleogel particles, and -loaded oil droplets are summarized in Table 1. Particle size analysis (Fig. S1) revealed similar mean particle size values for the formulation prepared using 0 %wt. and 10 %wt. SiOr. Specifically, d43 values of 0.58 ± 0.01 μm and 0.59 ± 0.00 μm were measured for 0 %wt. and 10 %wt. SiOr, respectively, with d32 values of 0.14 ± 0.02 μm and 0.17 ± 0.02 μm, respectively. On the other hand, the crystal particles of dasatinib exhibited larger size values, measuring 15.03 ± 2.39 μm for d32 and 22.07 ± 3.79 μm for d43, aligning with the microscopy results. When contrasting the dasatinib release profiles between a free drug suspension, the oil droplets, and the submicron-oleogel particles, it is evident that the disparity becomes more pronounced over time. Specifically, at the end of the 2-h gastric incubation, the liquid emulsion manifested a release percentage of 30 %. In contrast, the submicron-oleogel particles demonstrated a significantly lower release percentage of 19 % with negligible changes during the final 30 min. This observation could be attributed to the solid physical state of the lipid core within the submicron-oleogel.

Following gastric emptying, drug-based formulations transit into the intestine, where they are exposed to different pH conditions and mixed with bile and enzymatic-rich secretions. In the small intestine, the hydrolysis of triglyceride-rich canola oil is primarily mediated by the pancreatic colipase-lipase system. This enzymatic process results in the formation of intermediate diglycerides, ultimately yielding fatty acids and monoglycerides (MAGs). These byproducts, along with bile molecules, cholesterol, and phospholipids, associate to form small and highly dispersed mixed lipid colloidal structures (Fig. 3a). Given the pivotal role of intestinal lipolysis in the breakdown of lipid systems within the GIT, this part of the study focuses on elucidating the influence of structuring agents on the release of FFAs. The FFA versus time profiles for both formulations exhibited a similar trend, marked by an initial sharp increase in FFAs within the first 20 min post-addition of pancreatic lipase, succeeded by a gradual rise during extended digestion until reaching a plateau (Fig. 3b). In general, the efficiency of lipolysis relies heavily on the accessibility of triglyceride (TAG) cleavage sites to pancreatic lipase active sites [[49], [50], [51]]. So, factors such as lipid surface area, interface composition, fatty acid distribution, and enzyme diffusion all play pivotal roles in determining the lipid hydrolysis rate. The results showed a significant increase in FFA release in the digested submicron-oleogel particles compared to the oil droplets. This behavior may be related to the droplet size after the gastric phase, affecting the particle surface area. Our previous study found that exposing liquid oil droplets to stomach conditions caused aggregation, leading to an increase in particle size compared to the initial size. Conversely, submicron-oleogel particles remained stable under stomach conditions, showing no significant change in particle size [27]. Spherical micelle, disc-shaped micelle, and unilamellar vesicle structures were observed in the cryo-TEM (Fig. 5a and b). In the absence of lipid byproducts, the formation of these colloidal structures is attributed to the self-assembly of bile salts. Bile salts, recognized as biosurfactants, can self-assemble through interactions involving their hydrophobic steroid skeleton and their ionic chain and hydroxyl groups that encounter water molecules. It is essential to note that bile extract also contains phospholipids and cholesterol alongside bile molecules [54]. Both micellar phases resulting from the digested lipid-based formulation manifested a monomodal size distribution. In the oil droplets, the main peak of the colloidal structures exhibited a significant increase, starting from ∼83 nm after 5 min and increasing to ∼100 nm after 60 min of intestinal digestion (Fig. 4b–Table 2). Moreover, there was a notable rise in the PDI from 0.144 to 0.250 during this period, indicating variations in the size and shape of colloidal structures in the micellar phase. Generally, as lipid hydrolysis advances, more FFAs are released, leading to a higher impact on the size of the resultant colloidal structure [[55], [56], [57]]. A dramatic increase in lipolysis degree can be seen, with values rising from 22 % at 5 min to 72 % at 60 min (Fig. 3b), contributing to a significant change in the size and PDI levels. At the end of the digestion, a significant increase in the mean diameter size was observed without a noticeable change in the PDI value compared to the measurements taken 60 min post-addition of the lipase. At this stage, there is a slight elevation in lipid hydrolysis, with the percentage increasing from 72 % at 60 min to 79 % at 120 min (Fig. S2). Furthermore, a transition from micelles to vesicles may occur during the digestion process, potentially leading to an augmentation in the average size diameter [58]. Subsequently, a diverse array of colloidal structures was observed in the cryo-TEM, as shown in Fig. 5c and d, which encompassed spherical micelles of various nanometer sizes, along with unilamellar vesicles, multilamellar vesicles, and multivesicular vesicles. In the submicron-oleogel particles, the main peak of the colloidal structures remained stable at around ∼89 nm during intestinal digestion ranging from 5 to 60 min. Subsequently, following a prolonged digestion period of 120 min, the main peak increased again to reach a value of ∼116 nm. A gradual rise in PDI was noted over time, resembling the behavior observed in the formulation lacking a structuring agent. This supplementary element plays a pivotal role in altering the attributes of the colloidal structures present within the micellar phase. The cryo-TEM also exhibited a predominance of spherical micelles and unilamellar vesicles, with a significantly higher abundance (Fig. 5e and f). Their population appeared quite heterogeneous, with vesicle diameters ranging from approximately 20 nm up to 200 nm. It is worth noting that some of the unilamellar vesicles exhibited an elongated morphology that was intermediate between rounded and ellipsoidal shapes. Additionally, the observation of disc-shaped micelles in face-on orientations was documented. The variation in the composition of the lipid-based formulations is implicated in driving changes in the colloidal structures and their abundance in the aqueous environment of the intestine, potentially influencing the quantities and types of hydrophobic molecules released.

The surface charge characteristics of the colloidal structures in the micellar phase during the small intestinal incubation are presented in Fig. 4c. The findings indicated that differences in the formulation composition led to distinct micellization behavior and chemical surface behavior of the colloidal structures formed. Following 5 min of lipolysis initiation, the dasatinib suspension (control group) displayed a ζ-potential of −20 mV, while the lipid-based formulations showed a value of ∼ -27 mV. After 60 min, there was a significant increase in the absolute ζ-potential in both lipid-based formulations, reaching approximately −47 mV. This value remained relatively stable throughout the subsequent 60 min of lipolysis, particularly for the oil droplets. However, the submicron-oleogel particles demonstrated a further significant increase in the absolute ζ-potential after 120 min, compared to the measurements at 60 min (∗∗p < 0.01, one-way ANOVA with Tukey's multiple comparison tests). In contrast, the control group exhibited negligible variation throughout the 2-h incubation period. Under simulated physiological conditions, bile salts undergo ionization, acquiring a negative charge due to the presence of carboxylate groups. These molecules self-assemble and form colloidal structures, thus contributing to the resulting negatively charged surface. The differences observed between lipid-containing formulations and the control group can primarily be linked to the production of anionic fatty acids resulting from the oil lipolysis, which are the key factors influencing changes in the ζ-potential.

To further elucidate the observed distinctions in the size and morphology of the colloidal structures, absorbance scans of the micellar phase were conducted for the three formulations, excluding dasatinib, following 2 h of incubation in simulated intestinal digestion. Ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy provides insight into the composition of the colloidal structures, particularly when the components have specific absorbance at certain wavelengths. The results revealed elevated absorbance values for colloidal structures generated from the digested submicron-oleogel particles within a specific spectral range that aligned with the maximum wavelength absorbance of γ-oryzanol at 327 nm (Fig. 4d). Consequently, it is inferred that γ-oryzanol is incorporated into the mixed micellar composition. These results suggest that the oleogel building blocks, especially γ-oryzanol, are incorporated into the mixed micelles after gel breakdown during digestion, thus actively participating in micelle formation.

Discussion

Addressing the low oral bioavailability of poorly water-soluble drugs and nutrients is recognized as one of the most problematic and challenging issues in pharmaceutical and food research and development [5]. Oral bioavailability refers to the fraction of a compound that enters the systemic circulation after oral administration, where it exerts its pharmacological effect. Following ingestion, the compound transits through the GIT, with the small intestine being the primary site for molecule absorption due to its extensive surface area from microvilli [62]. Hence, ensuring notable bioavailability entails compound solubility within the intestinal fluids, transport toward the milieu, and permeability across epithelial membranes.

Our study introduces a game-changing solution by leveraging oil structuring technology, which transforms edible oil into a solid-like structure using physical routes based on a three-dimensional network of self-assembled molecules entrapping liquid oil, offering the best of all worlds. More specifically, this approach relies on five fundamental principles: i) structuring edible oils providing the system a GRAS status, ii) the solid texture of the proposed oleogel-based vehicles providing a protective environment in GIT, effectively preventing degradation of the encapsulated compound, iii) high drug/nutrient load due to high liquid oil content, iv) the drug/nutrient micellization ability during intestinal digestion aided by lipid byproducts, and bile salts, highlighting its inherent spontaneity and efficacy in the gastrointestinal milieu, and v) the production process which is characterized by its simplicity, speed, scalability, and standardization, aimed at minimizing manufacturing expenses and consistency across batches. Furthermore, these oleogel-based vehicles provide a versatile platform, thus can be exploited to encapsulate a variety of hydrophobic molecules from the food and pharmaceutical field, such as health-promoting ingredients, sensitive vitamins, etc.

Oil droplets and submicron-oleogel particles were produced with identical characteristics, differing solely in their physical state. While dasatinib served as a model compound in this study, the approach is broadly applicable to the delivery of hydrophobic bioactive nutrients. Following the preparation, lipid-based and free drug formulations underwent exposure to in vitro gastric and small intestine phases. Gastric lipid digestion is less efficient for long-chain TAGs, which are the main ones in canola oil, contributing only 10–30 % of the lipolysis in adults, while long-chain TAGs hydrolysis is predominantly mediated by pancreatic lipase in the small intestine [49,63]. Therefore, our investigation focused on assessing the effect of the acidic environment on dasatinib release during the gastric phase. Our results showed that submicron-oleogel particles exhibited a relatively low release amount of 19 %, which occurred at the beginning of the gastric phase, maintaining this level throughout the 2-h digestion period. This behavior is ascribed to the complex network of the structuring agent, which forms a protective shield and impedes the diffusion of dasatinib from the lipid matrix. Moreover, the overall dasatinib release for the free drug and oil droplets was higher, with values of 60 % and 30 %, respectively. Previous research reported that the release of the antiparasitic drug praziquantel from a wax-based bulk oleogel was lower compared to our results, with a value of ∼10 %, however, similar kinetic behavior was observed [64]. It is important to highlight that the surface area of bulk oleogel is smaller than the submicron-oleogel particles, and the structuring agent gelation mechanism relies on crystallization rather than tubular arrangement. Moreover, the acidic environment may impact the physical stability of the lipid-based formulations, where oil droplets are more likely to collide and aggregate, while submicron-oleogel particles are not expected to behave in the same manner due to their solid state [27,65].

Following the gastric stage, small intestine digestion occurs, where the lipid components, primarily TAGs, undergo hydrolysis to MAGs and FFAs. This biochemical process is catalyzed by lipase enzymes at the oil-water interface, thus the properties of this interface are crucial for the efficacy of lipolysis [66]. The size of lipid particles illustrates this phenomenon, as larger particles exhibit a reduced surface area, providing fewer sites for lipase activity, leading to a reduced extent of lipolysis. As previously noted, submicron-oleogel particles are expected to retain their size, while the size of liquid oil droplets increases following gastric digestion due to coalescence and aggregation [27]. Consequently, the differences in FFA release in small intestine digestion between oil droplets and submicron-oleogel particles can be attributed to differences in size.

During the digestive process in the small intestine, the release of lipid byproducts is concomitant with bile salt secretion. These bile salts play a crucial role in augmenting lipase activity, as they efficiently eliminate interface-active molecules from the lipid interface. This process produces a range of colloidal structures encompassing lipid byproducts and bile salts [67]. Consequently, these structures are instrumental in solubilizing and transporting lipid byproducts and poorly water-soluble components, facilitating their passage to the intestinal epithelium and improving their absorption [68]. The colloidal structure's production, structure, morphology, and quantity can affect bioaccessibility and oral bioavailability [69]. Our results revealed that variations in the composition of the different formulations yielded distinct micellization behavior, resulting in different colloidal structure shapes and sizes. The dasatinib suspension exhibited bile salt micellization capability, achieving approximately 44 % drug content within the colloidal structures. This phenomenon is characterized by the self-assembly of bile salts, which is driven by hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding [70]. These structures were observed under a microscope, revealing various forms with sizes ranging from 20 to 200 nm, consistent with the size analysis data with a bimodal distribution centered at 17 ± 2 and 125 ± 25 nm. Additionally, crystalline drug structures were identified in certain vehicles, suggesting a possible transition from the amorphous to the crystalline state. This transition may be attributed to the change in pH from 2 in the stomach to 7 in the small intestine, which significantly reduces the drug solubility, potentially leading to its crystallization.

For the digested oil droplets, a diverse array of colloidal structures characterized by a monomodal distribution of sizes ranging from 17 to 500 nm, with a maximum peak at 135 ± 7 nm, with a dasatinib content of 64 %. The results emphasize the significance of lipid byproducts (FFAs and MAGs), in actively altering the size/shape of the formed colloidal structures and enhancing the dasatinib transfer. At a molecular level, electrostatic and/or hydrophobic interactions may occur between bile salts and other surface-active molecules [71,72]. A range of structural studies has consistently demonstrated that elevated concentrations of MAGs and FFAs in bile assembly trigger a transition from micelles to vesicles, thereby enhancing drug solubilization [52,73,74].

The digested submicron-oleogel particles exhibited the formation of colloidal structures characterized by smaller sizes with a monomodal distribution centered at 116 ± 9 nm and higher dasatinib content (87 %) compared to the other formulations. Furthermore, analysis of these structures revealed that γ-oryzanol is incorporated as a building block alongside bile salts and lipid byproducts (Fig. 6c). In line with this, our previous study demonstrated that increasing the SiOr concentration resulted in decreasing the size of the micellar colloidal structures [27]. Generally, physical gels exhibit a supramolecular structure formed through inter- or intramolecular noncovalent interactions [75,76]. Therefore, the enzymatic activity, presence of bile salts, and mechanical agitation within the small intestine collectively contribute to the disruption of these weak interactions, ultimately breaking down the gel network. Consequently, the structuring agent molecules released from the digested submicron-oleogel particles self-assemble into the colloidal structures due to their interfacial properties [72]. A previous study showed that γ-oryzanol has a constrained surface-activity [77], which also promotes its residence in the hydrophobic core. γ-Oryzanol consists of ferulic acid esters of phytosterols, in which the polar ferulic acid moiety preferentially orients toward the oil–water interface, while the hydrophobic sterol backbone anchors within the lipid core or micellar interior. This amphiphilic structure supports its interfacial positioning and potential role in modulating colloidal behavior during digestion. Additionally, β-sitosterol, with its tetracyclic fused ring skeleton (hydrophobic region) and a single hydroxyl group (polar region), can migrate into the colloidal structure [78]. Interestingly, a recent study demonstrated that β-sitosterol is more readily incorporated into bile micelles, leading to the expansion of their hydrophobic cores rather than self-assembling with bile into the interface [79]. Overall, the release of structuring agents from the network into the colloidal structures may improve solubilization capability, consequently increasing the dasatinib bioaccessibility.

To further investigate the effect of oleogelation technology on oral dasatinib delivery, an oral pharmacokinetic study was conducted in rats. Generally, the bioavailability of dasatinib ranges from 14 % to 34 % in mammals [35]. Thus, variability is primarily attributed to incomplete solubilization and absorption and the substantial first-pass effect resulting from its low solubility in the small intestine [80,81]. Our results revealed an improved absorption of dasatinib in lipid-based formulations compared to free dasatinib suspension, possibly due to micellization assisted by the oil digestion byproducts. This mechanism is shown to aid in dasatinib transport through the intestinal lymphatic system, thereby bypassing hepatic first-pass metabolism and enhancing its bioavailability [68]. Additionally, the structuring agent's incorporation led to a rapid increase in dasatinib levels in the bloodstream, along with sustained release and absorption properties, which can be attributed to the mechanical resilience of the oleogel. Previous in vivo studies in mice confirmed the beneficial effect of encapsulating curcumin within an oleogel-based nano-emulsion, resulting in a 9-fold increase in its oral bioavailability compared to unformulated crystalline curcumin [31]. A recent preclinical study conducted in pigs assessed the delivery efficacy of resulting gels for three distinct water-insoluble drugs. Findings indicated that oleogels successfully delivered doses equivalent to or surpassing the absorption levels achievable from tablets for each drug, underscoring their potential as effective drug delivery systems [64]. By employing submicron-oleogel particles instead of bulk oleogel, there is an increase in surface area and controlled release behavior, which can improve drug dissolution and control of drug release during the digestive process. This enhancement ultimately results in improved bioavailability and therapeutic effectiveness.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides proof-of-concept for the potential advancement of a new carrier system, inspired by the food industry, to deliver hydrophobic compounds. Converting liquid oil into an oleogel provides remarkable stability in terms of storage conditions and exposure to the challenging GIT environment. The development of submicron-oleogel particles confers a notable benefit to systems by enhancing the surface area and facilitating oil hydrolysis and hydrophobic molecules transfer processes. Importantly, our research showed that the structuring agent could actively engage in the spontaneous micellization processes and play a significant role in enhancing hydrophobic compound transfer. Interestingly, the in vitro bioaccessibility findings exhibited a significant association with the in vivo outcomes. This good correlation serves as a cornerstone in translational research, offering a comprehensive understanding of encapsulated compound behavior from bench to bedside. Altogether, we have introduced a novel platform utilizing oleogel particles, which may pave the way for simplified and scalable oral delivery technology in both the food and pharmaceutical fields.
