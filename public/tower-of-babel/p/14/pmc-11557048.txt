Global burden of thyroid cancer in 2022: Incidence and mortality estimates from GLOBOCAN
Authors: Zhangyan Lyu, Yu Zhang, Chao Sheng, Yubei Huang, Qiang Zhang, Kexin Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Thyroid cancer (TC) is the most common malignancy of the endocrine system. This study aimed to assess the global distribution of TC incidence and mortality in 2022, as well as to predict the burden for the year 2050.Methods:Data from the GLOBOCAN 2022 database were used to analyze the age-standardized incidence and mortality rates of TC by sex, age group (<55 years and ≥55 years), country, world region, and level of Human Development Index (HDI) for 185 countries. The predicted incidence and mortality burden for 2050 was calculated based on demographic projections.Results:In 2022, an estimated 821,214 new TC cases and 47,507 TC-related deaths occurred worldwide. The age-standardized incidence rates (ASIRs) and age-standardized mortality rates (ASMRs) were higher in women (ASIR: 13.60 per 100,000; ASMR: 0.53 per 100,000) than in men (ASIR: 4.60 per 100,000; ASMR: 0.35 per 100,000). The ASIR in high HDI countries was approximately ten times higher than that in low HDI countries for both sexes, with relatively similar ASMR across regions. Among 185 countries, China had the largest number of TC cases (accounting for 56.77% of total cases) and TC-related deaths (accounting for 24.35% of global TC-related deaths), with the highest ASIR in men (13.30 per 100,000). Worldwide, approximately 64.63% of TC cases occurred in populations under 55 years old, while nearly 82.99% of TC-related deaths occurred in populations aged 55 years and above. If the rates stay the same as in 2022, it is projected that approximately 1,100,000 new TC cases and 91,000 TC-related deaths will occur in 2050, indicating a 34.15% and 89.58% increase, respectively.Conclusions:TC is a highly frequent cancer worldwide with disparities across regions, genders, and age groups. Our results provide light on the worldwide TC disease burden and facilitate regionally customized prevention measures.

Introduction

Thyroid cancer (TC) is the most common malignancy of the endocrine system globally in 2022.[1] TC represents approximately 4.1% of all cancer diagnoses worldwide and generally has a favorable prognosis, with a five-year survival rate of about 98.5%.[1,2] Despite this, TC significantly affects patients’ quality of life.[3] In China, TC was the leading cancer type among adolescents and young adults aged 15–39 years for both gender, representing 46% of all cases.[4] Moreover, TC cases are projected to increase by 44.1% from 2019 to 2030 globally, with the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) per 100,000 rising by 16.3%, presenting serious concerns.[5]

The risk of TC increases with age, and the worldwide aging process may exacerbate this trend.[6] Additionally, TC is the only non-reproductive cancer that predominantly affects women, being three to four times more common in women than in men.[7] Various environmental risk factors (such as radiation exposure and iodine supply) and individual lifestyles (obesity, etc) have been identified as associated with TC risk.[8] Given the rising incidence of TC, particularly in subpopulations with different characteristics, analyzing global distribution patterns and investigating temporal trends are critical. Such analyses may aid in developing personalized prevention and control strategies for TC.

The widespread use of imaging techniques and the introduction of fine-needle aspiration biopsy have significantly improved the detection rate of TC. However, this has led to a growing concern regarding overdiagnosis.[9] Variations in the quality of medical and health services globally contribute to significant disparities in TC burden across countries. Assessing the burden of TC in different regions and specific countries can help to evaluate the fairness of health service resources and highlight the accessibility of diagnosis and treatment in nations with varying income levels.

Previous studies on TC incidence and mortality have focused on specific countries, regions, or populations.[10–13] However, limited research examined the current global burden of TC using the most up-to-date data, especially in analyzing variations across different sex and age groups. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) provided detailed estimates of cancer prevalence through the Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN), allowing for comparisons of TC burden across countries and regions.[1] In this study, we aimed to assess the worldwide epidemiological landscape of TC in 2022, considering factors such as geography and socioeconomic status, and project the future burden of TC up to 2050 using the GLOBOCAN 2022 database.

Methods

Data source

The data of new cases and deaths of TC (International Classification of Diseases 10th revision, C73) for 185 countries or territories in 2022 were extracted from the GLOBOCAN database. These data were segmented by gender or age, with a 5-year interval as the cutoff (from age group 0–4 years to 85+ years).[1] Population data for 2022 were sourced from the United Nations (UN) website.[14] The methods used in compiling the IARC’s cancer estimates for 2022 have been described in detail previously.[15] Briefly, the national (or subnational) cancer registries and national vital registries provided the best available recorded data for estimates in GLOBOCAN.[16] The methods employed to derive the global cancer incidence and mortality estimates were consistent with those used in previous studies.[17–19] The reliability of cancer data at the national level depended on the extent to which the representativeness and quality of the source information.[15] GLOBOCAN has provided detailed information on data sources and estimation methods for cancer incidence and mortality in specific countries. All data used in this study were accessible through GLOBOCAN, which offers tools for data tabulation, graphical visualization, and exploration of current and future TC burdens.

Because the GLOBOCAN project used only pre-existing, publicly accessible, aggregated, and population-level non-identifiable data, the IARC Ethics Committee determined that no ethical clearance was necessary for this work.

Statistical analysis

Tables and figures were presented based on estimated new cases and deaths, along with summary measures utilizing direct standardization, including the ASIR and age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) per 100,000 person-years, using the 1966 Segi-Doll World standard population for comparisons between populations adjusted for age structure variations.[20] Additionally, the mortality-to-incidence ratio (MIR) was calculated as a proxy for survival, serving as an indicator of healthcare performance and cancer care quality.[21] The results were shown by sex, UN-defined world regions, selected countries, and the UN’s four-tier Human Development Index (HDI) (low, medium, high, and very high). In addition to the 25 countries with the highest ASIRs of TC globally, we also provided TC-related epidemiological information regarding the five countries with the largest populations in each UN-defined region. Furthermore, we estimated ASIR and ASMR per 100,000 for TC over-age trends. In consideration of the age-related incidence trend of TC and its close association with the prognosis, we divided the population into two age groups (<55 years and ≥55 years) according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system for subgroup analysis.[22]

Following the methodologies in previous studies,[23,24] we also projected the number of future TC cases and deaths up to the year 2050, categorized by gender and HDI levels, across various scenarios starting from the base year of 2022. The projected increases and decreases of 1%, 2%, and 3% were intended to reflect potential future trends in incidence and mortality rates across all age groups, with further details available online (https://gco.iarc.fr/tomorrow/en2024).[25] Based on a critical assumption that the estimated incidence or mortality rates in 2022 will remain constant from 2022 to 2050, the projected number of new TC cases or deaths was calculated by multiplying the TC-related ASIR or ASMR for the year 2022 by the corresponding estimated global population projection. Therefore, any variations in the number of cases or deaths are solely attributable to population growth and aging.[26] The projected population data were derived from estimates provided by the United Nations Development Programme.

The analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). This study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement.[27]

Results

Estimated number of new TC cases and ASIRs in 2022

Globally, an estimated number of 821,214 new TC cases occurred in 2022. The crude incidence rate was 10.40 per 100,000, and the ASIR was 9.10 per 100,000 of the population. Worldwide, the highest and the lowest ASIRs were observed in Eastern Asia (23.10 per 100,000) and Western Africa (0.94 per 100,000), respectively [Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C133]. The ASIR increased steadily, peaked at 55 years, and then decreased [Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C133].

Approximately 74.86% of new cases occurred in women (N = 614,729). The ASIR was 13.60 per 100,000 for women, whereas 4.60 per 100,000 for men [Table 1]. Women had significantly higher ASIRs than men since the age of 20 years [Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C133]. The ASIRs exhibited a similar geographic distribution between women and men. Consistent with total population, across UN-defined world regions, the highest and lowest ASIRs were also observed in Eastern Asia (women, 34.30 per 100,000; men, 12.20 per 100,000) and Western Africa (women, 1.40 per 100,000; men, 0.40 per 100,000), respectively, except for Micronesia where no TC case was recorded in men [Table 1]. After grouping countries according to HDI levels, those with high HDI had the highest ASIR of TC, followed by countries with very high HDI. The ASIR of TC in high HDI countries was approximately ten times higher than that in low HDI countries for both women and men [Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C133].

Estimated number of TC deaths and ASMRs in 2022

Globally, an estimated number of 47,507 TC deaths occurred in 2022. The crude mortality rate was 0.60 per 100,000, and the ASMR was 0.44 per 100,000 of the population. Melanesia had the highest ASMR (1.70 per 100,000). With the exception of Micronesia, where no death was recorded, Northern Europe (0.26 per 100,000) and Southern Europe (0.26 per 100,000) had the lowest ASMRs [Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C133]. The ASMRs increased with age and increased significantly after age 60 years [Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C133].

Approximately 63.71% of all TC deaths occurred in women (N = 30,266). The ASMR was 0.53 per 100,000 for women, wheras 0.35 per 100,000 for men [Table 1]. Women had significantly higher ASMRs than men after the age of 50 years [Supplementary Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C133]. Across UN-defined world regions, with the exception of Micronesia, the ASMR for women ranged from 0.24 per 100,000 in Australia–New Zealand to 2.70 per 100,000 in Melanesia. For men, with the exception of Micronesia and Polynesia, the ASMR ranged from 0.22 per 100,000 in Western Africa to 0.61 per 100,000 in Melanesia [Table 1]. Countries with a lower HDI tended to have higher ASMRs, particularly among men. For women, the ASMR was 0.60 per 100,000 in high HDI countries and 0.50 per 100,000 in medium HDI countries [Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C133].

Nearly 82.99% of TC deaths occurred in individuals aged 55 years and older. The ASMR was 0.11 per 100,000 in individuals under the age of 55 years and 2.30 per 100,000 in those aged 55 years and older [Table 2]. TC deaths in women under the age of 55 years were higher than in men (17.75% vs. 15.72%, women vs. men). Furthermore, for women younger than 55 years, the ASMR was highest in high HDI countries, whereas for those older than 55 years, the highest ASMR was in low HDI countries. Similar differences were also observed in men. The ASMR for women aged 55 years and older was 18 times higher than those under 55 years; for men, it was 27 times higher [Supplementary Tables 2 and 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C133].

At the national level, China had the largest number of TC deaths (N = 11,564), accounting for approximately 24.35% of global TC deaths. Among the 25 countries with the highest ASMRs of TC, Vanuatu had the highest ASMR for women at 4.40 per 100,000, followed by Samoa at 3.80 per 100,000 and Fiji at 3.10 per 100,000. For men, Vanuatu had the highest ASMR at 1.10 per 100,000, followed by Turkey at 0.58 per 100,000 and Fiji at 0.53 per 100,000 [Figure 1]. Among the five countries with the largest populations in each UN-defined region [Supplementary Table 4, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C133], in Eastern Asia, the highest and the lowest ASMRs in women were observed in the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea and Mongolia, at 0.56 per 100,000 and 0.30 per 100,000, respectively. In men, the highest and the lowest ASMRs were found in China and South Korea, at 0.35 per 100,000 and 0.21 per 100,000, respectively. The ASMR was similar in women across Germany, the United Kingdom, France, the Netherlands, and Belgium in Western Europe (0.21 to 0.29 per 100,000). In men, except for Belgium (0.39 per 100,000), the ASMR was similar in the other four countries (0.22 to 0.29 per 100,000). In Northern America, the highest ASMR was observed in Mexico in women (0.80 per 100,000) and men (0.48 per 100,000).

TC care quality in 2022

MIR was used as an indicator of TC care quality. The global calculated MIR of TC in 2022 was 0.06. Western Africa had the highest MIR worldwide at 0.43. Except for Micronesia, where no death was recorded, Eastern Asia had the lowest MIR at 0.03 [Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C133]. Western Africa had the highest MIR for both women (0.44) and men (0.45) [Table 1]. Countries with a low HDI had the highest MIR, at rates of 0.29 among women and 0.34 among men. Conversely, countries with a high and very high HDI had a lower MIR [Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C133].

The MIR was higher in the individuals aged 55 years and older compared to those under 55 years (0.14 vs. 0.02, ≥55 years vs. <55 years) [Table 2]. A similar geographic distribution of MIR was observed in both women and men. The MIRs of women and men under 55 years were very similar (women: 0.01; men: 0.02). However, among individuals aged 55 years and older, the MIR was significantly higher in men than in women (0.19 vs. 0.12, men vs. women) [Supplementary Tables 2 and 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C133].

Among the top 25 countries with the highest MIR, Samoa, Vanuatu, and Fiji had the highest MIR for women at 0.23, 0.21, and 0.21, respectively. For men, Vanuatu had the highest MIR at 0.34, followed by Fiji at 0.17, and Costa Rica and Lithuania, both at 0.12 [Figure 1]. As shown in Supplementary Table 4, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C133, across countries with the largest population in Eastern Asia, the highest MIR was observed in Mongolia in both women (0.15) and men (0.36). China and South Korea had the lowest MIR in both women (both 0.02) and men (China: 0.03; South Korea: 0.04). In Western Europe, France had the lowest MIR in both women (0.04) and men (0.09). The MIR across Germany, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, and Belgium were similar in women (0.08–0.11) and men (0.13–0.19). In Northern America, Mexico had the highest MIR in women (0.07) and men (0.14). For women, the lowest MIR was found in the United States of America at 0.03. For men, the MIR was the same in the United States of America and Canada, both were 0.08.

Prediction of new TC cases and TC deaths in 2050

Assuming the incidence rates remain constant, it is predicted that 1,100,000 TC cases will be diagnosed in 2050, representing a 34.15% increase from the estimated 820,000 cases in 2022 [Figure 2]. Specifically, TC cases are expected to rise from 610,000 to 820,000 for women, and from 210,000 to 280,000 for men, reflecting an increase of 34.43% and 33.33%, respectively. Under scenarios of increasing rates (from +1% to +3% annually), projections showed a steeper increase in the number of cases. Under the highest rate scenario (+3%) by 2050, the relative incidence was up to a 206.56% rise for women and a 209.52% rise for men. Conversely, with decreasing incidence rates (from −2% to −3% annually), the number of new cases could potentially reduce to below the levels in 2022. To maintain the current number of cases seen in 2022, the annual incidence would need to decrease by almost 1% for women and men [Figures 2]. If the incidence rates remain constant, the largest relative increases are anticipated in low HDI countries, with a 130.77% rise to nearly 17,000 new cases predicted by 2050. In absolute terms, most new cases will be in high HDI countries, where cases are predicted to increase to almost 42,000 [Supplementary Figure 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C133].

Overall, the annual number of TC deaths is predicted to increase by 89.58% from 48,000 to 91,000 deaths by 2050 if mortality rates remain constant [Figure 2]. Specifically, TC deaths for women are expected to increase from 30,000 in 2022 to 57,000 in 2050, whereas for men, the numbers are projected to increase from 17,000 to 33,000. The percentage increase will be over 90% for both sexes. If mortality rates increase annually by 3%, the total number of deaths could soar up to 131,000 for women and 76,000 for men by 2050, with a relative increase of 336.7% and 347.1%, respectively. By 2050, only mortality rates decrease by 3% annually, and a substantial mitigation in the number of deaths can be expected for women and men [Figure 2]. Assuming the mortality rates remain constant, the largest relative increases are anticipated in low HDI countries, with a 159.0% increase to nearly 6200 new deaths predicted by 2050. In absolute terms, most new deaths will occur in high HDI countries, where the number of deaths will increase to almost 18,900 [Supplementary Figure 3, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C133].

Discussion

In this population-based epidemiological study, we presented the most up-to-date estimates of the worldwide incidence and mortality of TC by gender and age, providing a comprehensive picture of the current epidemiological profile of this neoplasm. The global burden of TC constituted a considerable cancer burden in 2022. The high incidence was primarily concentrated in highly developed countries. There were marked gender variations in incidence and mortality rates across countries and world regions, with the higher incidence rates reported in women and high HDI countries. Approximately 65% of TC cases occurred in populations under 55 years, while nearly 82.99% of TC deaths occurred in populations aged 55 years and older. According to the current rates, approximately 1,100,000 TC cases and 91,000 deaths are projected to occur in 2050, representing a 34.15% and 89.58% increase, respectively.

TC is the most common malignancy in the endocrine system, and its incidence is increasing rapidly.[28] Compared with the estimated results published in GLOBOCAN 2020,[29] the incidence rates of TC have increased by 3.5 per 100,000 for women and 1.5 per 100,000 for men. This high incidence is particularly notable in countries with a high and very high HDI. The HDI measures a country’s social and economic development levels and has been identified as an independent factor associated with TC incidence.[30] Moreover, the ASIR of high HDI countries has increased significantly by 10.1 per 100,000 for women and 4.2 per 100,000 for men.[29] However, the mortality rate of TC has remained relatively low and stable. The large discrepancy between incidence and mortality is primarily attributed to the effects of overdiagnosis.[9,31] Overdiagnosis, which reflects increased access to health care and diagnosis, explains the association between the high incidence of TC and national development. For instance, in women, a significant proportion of TC cases can be attributed to overdiagnosis, with an estimate of approximately 93% in South Korea, 91% in Belarus, 87% in China, 84% in Italy and Croatia, and 83% in Slovakia and France.[9] The MIR serves as a valuable indicator of 5-year survival rates across different countries.[32] Consistent with previous findings, this study found that higher MIR values were prevalent in low HDI countries, particularly in Africa.[30] Overdiagnosis may lead to the identification of numerous indolent TC cases, thereby artificially inflating survival rates[33]; however, it can also increase the detection rate of advanced-stage cancers. Given that Africa exhibits low incidence but high mortality rates for TC, further investigation is essential to identify the causes of elevated mortality and to develop appropriate strategies to improve the quality of treatment and accessibility of health care resources.

In 2022, the global geographic distribution of TC incidence and mortality remained essentially unchanged compared with the findings reported in GLOBAOCAN 2020.[29] In addition to the HDI, other risk factors may also contribute to the high incidence of TC in certain regions. Radiation exposure is a significant environmental risk factor for TC that affects the susceptibility of the thyroid to radiation-induced carcinogenesis, particularly in childhood. This could explain the high TC incidence rates observed in some Pacific Ocean Islands due to atomic testing conducted between 1966 and 1996.[34] Regions near volcanoes have reported pollution with specific heavy metals,[35] which act as endocrine disruptors and pose direct toxic and carcinogenic risks to living cells.[36] This may explain the high TC incidence in volcanic regions such as Vanuatu and French Polynesia. However, the link between volcanic ash and TC has not been demonstrated in all volcanic areas, which may be due to variations in gas, ash, lava emissions, and toxic compounds from different volcanoes.[8] Exposure to ionizing radiation due to medical procedures is a well-known risk factor for TC, and the increase in medical imaging procedures in countries with wide coverage of medical services may result in a higher incidence of TC.[37] Obesity has been associated with the risk of TC,[38,39] and a positive correlation between HDI and obesity has been suggested.[40] Consequently, regions with high TC rates coincide with those where obesity is prevalent. Regarding mortality, areas with clinical or subclinical iodine deficiency, such as some areas of Central Asia and Africa, may result in higher TC rates.[41] Overdiagnosis cannot be universally applied to all countries with elevated TC rates. Therefore, in countries showing an increase in the incidence of TC, investigating factors related to environmental exposure or modifiable risk factors linked to TC could further help its control and prevention.

TC is more common in women than in men. Such noticeable gender differences may be related to obstetric and gynecological factors and sex hormone levels.[42,43] Hormone-related events such as pregnancy may also contribute to increased exposure of women to medical care. The rate of overdiagnosis of TC is higher in women than in men.[44] The discrepancy in TC burden across age groups could be interpreted from the perspective of age-period-cohort (APC) effects. Age effect highlights that TC risk changes with age, partly due to cumulative exposure to various risk factors. Previous APC analysis based on TC burden between China and America found that the peak age of TC incidence was 55–59 years old, which was similar to our study’s peak age.[45] However, another study found that the peak age of TC incidence tended to be younger. A study conducted in China showed that individuals aged 10–24 years will be the main population accounting for the increase in TC incidence over the next 20 years.[11] Period effect accounts for changes in TC risk with the time in the same age group. Such factors include advancements in medical technology and improved diagnostic techniques. One APC analysis proved that the TC risk has slowed significantly since 2007, which was consistent with the changes in TC management guidelines in South Korea and the United States of America.[46] The cohort effect describes how the different historical, social, and environmental conditions in which individuals were born affect their lifetime exposure to TC risk factors. Furthermore, in the current study, we found that individuals under the age of 55 had the highest ASMR in high HDI countries for both sexes. In individuals aged 55 years and older, the highest ASMR was found in low HDI countries. This may indicate that the stage and pathological type of TC at diagnosis could differ between younger and older patients, suggesting the need for tailored prevention and management strategies across different age groups, which requires further research.

This study had several limitations. First, data collected from GLOBOCAN are estimates. Thus, the results should be interpreted cautiously, particularly for countries without available population data; in these cases, regional information was aggregated to provide national proxies. The lack of diagnostic facilities or the failure to report cases treated in an outpatient setting may also have contributed to the underreporting of TC incidence in some regions. Therefore, the potential contribution of underdiagnosis or poor registration to underestimating cases and deaths in certain groups cannot be ruled out. Meanwhile, these distortions may be offset by overreporting and overdiagnosis caused by increased TC screening in high-income settings. Second, the GLOBOCAN estimates do not account for the impact of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic due to extrapolations of cancer data collected in previous years. Third, the absolute ranking of age-adjusted rates might vary slightly if an alternative standard rather than the world population were applied, especially in high HDI countries in which the age structure differs significantly from that of the applied world standard population. Finally, the future projections were estimated based on the assumption that incidence and mortality will remain unchanged between 2022 and 2050. The results only considered the anticipated demographics (population growth and aging) change among or within countries. Temporal variation, such as risk factors, treatments, and population composition, was not considered, which may yield a more thorough and precise prediction.

This research offers a thorough overview of the worldwide burden of TC, which is valuable for cancer control efforts and clinical decision-making. The results of this study suggest the existence of high geographical variability of TC incidence, particularly the high incidence in countries with a high and very high HDI. Although the mortality from TC is relatively low, the higher incidence among women and the high proportion of cases among individuals younger than 55 years are noteworthy. These findings emphasize the need and the power of balancing overdiagnosis with primary and secondary prevention interventions, which could be the most valuable strategies for managing TC now and in the future.
