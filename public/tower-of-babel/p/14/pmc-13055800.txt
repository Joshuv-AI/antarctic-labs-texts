Domain‐Specific Paradox of Physical Activity and Depression Among Patients With Cardiovascular Disease or Related Conditions
Authors: Su Jin Yeon, Ji Yong Byeon, Dong Hoon Lee, Sae Young Jae, Chan Joo Lee, Ansuk Jeong, Justin Y. Jeon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDomain‐specific physical activity (PA) may relate differently to depressive symptoms in adults with cardiovascular disease. We examined associations of work, transport, and leisure PA with depressive symptoms and assessed modification by sex and income.MethodsWe analyzed cross‐sectional data from 5788 adults with cardiovascular disease or related conditions in the Korean National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (2016–2020). PA was measured using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire and categorized by weekly metabolic equivalent task minutes. Depressive symptoms were defined as Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 ≥11. Multivariable logistic regression estimated adjusted odds ratio (OR) and 95% CI.ResultsCompared with no work PA, low (1–600 metabolic equivalent task‐min/week) and high (≥600 metabolic equivalent task‐min/week) work PA were associated with higher odds of depressive symptoms (OR, 2.61 [95% CI, 1.57–4.36]; OR, 3.17 [95% CI, 2.03–4.95]). High leisure PA was associated with lower odds (OR, 0.45 [95% CI, 0.28–0.72]), and high transport PA showed a modest protective association (OR, 0.73 [95% CI, 0.54–0.98]). Associations between work PA and depressive symptoms were stronger in women, whereas the protective association of leisure PA was stronger in high‐income individuals (P for interaction <0.001).ConclusionsIn adults with cardiovascular disease, work PA was positively, whereas leisure PA was inversely, associated with depressive symptoms, supporting the PA paradox and highlighting the need for domain‐specific PA strategies.

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of death worldwide, with 18.6 million deaths, accounting for ∼32% of all deaths in 2019. 1 In addition to physical problems, patients with CVD commonly encounter mental health issues such as depression. Up to 41.9% of patients with heart failure and 18.4% of patients with CVD suffer from depression. 2 Considering the high risk of depression among patients with CVD, reducing risk factors for depression in this population warrants attention. 3 Low levels of physical activity (PA) are known to be a risk factor not only for depression but also for unfavorable CVD prognosis. 4 , 5 , 6 Although regular PA is generally associated with improved physical and mental health outcomes, leisure and work PA may have contrasting health effects, 7 a phenomenon referred to as the PA paradox.

Recent research has increasingly focused on categorizing PA into distinct domains, such as work, transport, and leisure, rather than treating it as a singular concept. This approach recognizes that different PA domains may have varying effects on physical and mental health. 8 , 9 , 10 According to a previous study, individuals who engaged in leisure PA for >150 min/week experienced a lower prevalence of depression than those who did not. 11 Additionally, individuals who were highly active in both leisure and transport PA showed a lower prevalence of depressive symptoms than those who did not participate in any leisure or transport PA. 8

Although previous research has linked different PA domains to depressive symptoms, these relationships have not been fully explored in individuals with CVD who are at a higher risk for depression. 9 , 12 Understanding whether the PA paradox applies to this population is crucial for the development of more targeted interventions. In this regard, the purpose of this study was to examine the association between domain‐specific PA and prevalence of depressive symptoms among patients with CVD and related conditions, including hypertension and dyslipidemia.

Methods

Transparency and Openness Promotion Statement

The data, analytic methods, and study materials will be made available to other researchers for purposes of reproducing the results or replicating the procedure. This study used data from the KNHANES (Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey), 13 which are publicly available from the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency but cannot be redistributed by the authors. The analytic code used for this analysis is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population

Data were obtained from the KNHANES in 2016, 2018, and 2020. The data from each year represent an independent cross‐sectional sample of the Korean population. To minimize potential bias, a stratified multistage probability sampling method was applied, and standardized face‐to‐face interviews were conducted by trained interviewers. 13

Among 15 991 respondents, 9933 patients with CVD and related conditions were identified. We excluded individuals aged <19 years, those with missing data for any item on the Korean version of the Patient Health Questionnaire‐9, and those with incomplete data for key covariates. The final sample consisted of 5788 participants (2952 men and 2836 women). The survey was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Korean Center for Disease Control and Prevention, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Data Collection

Data were collected using self‐report questionnaires and interviews. A detailed description of the data‐resource profile can be found elsewhere. 13 The parts of the data set relevant to this study are demographics, socioeconomic variables, and PA, obtained through a self‐report questionnaire; and depressive symptoms were collected through face‐to‐face interviews conducted by KNHANES research staff.

Measures

PA was assessed using the validated Korean version of the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire, 14 originally developed by the World Health Organization. 15 The questionnaire included 16 items covering 3 PA domains: work, transport, and leisure. In the work and leisure domains, participants reported both the frequency and duration of moderate‐ and vigorous‐intensity activities. Vigorous activities were defined as those requiring hard physical effort and causing substantial increases in breathing or heart rate, whereas moderate activities involved moderate effort with slight increases in breathing or heart rate. In the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire, the work domain includes both paid and unpaid activities performed at moderate‐to‐vigorous intensity for ≥10 minutes, such as household chores, caregiving, farming, fishing, and livestock care, regardless of employment status. 14 In the transport domain, the participants reported the time spent walking or cycling, regardless of the intensity. The weekly PA volume for each domain was calculated in metabolic equivalent task (MET)‐min/week by multiplying the reported minutes by the standard MET value, using 4 METs for moderate‐intensity and 8 METs for vigorous‐intensity activities, based on the 2011 Compendium of PA. 16 Domain‐specific PA was categorized into 3 levels according to international PA guidelines: no PA (0 MET‐min/week), low PA (1–600 MET‐min/week), and high PA (≥600 MET‐min/week).

CVD or related conditions were defined as having at least 1 of the following conditions: myocardial infarction, angina pectoris, heart failure, hypertension, or dyslipidemia. These conditions were ascertained using questionnaire items regarding physician diagnosis, current status, and treatment. For hypertension and dyslipidemia, medication use was additionally considered: participants who reported taking antihypertensive medication were classified as having hypertension, and those taking at least 1 lipid‐lowering medication were classified as having dyslipidemia.

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire‐9, a questionnaire recommended for depression screening in primary care settings. The Korean version of the Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 has been validated and found reliable. 17 This questionnaire comprises nine items that align with the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders V. 18 Participants rate their symptoms on a scale from 0 (“not at all”) to 3 (“nearly every day”), with the total score ranging from 0 to 27. Studies have suggested a cutoff score of 11 for depressive symptoms, which was used in our analyses. 19

The covariates were as follows: age (<40, 40–64, and ≥65 years), education (none or elementary school, middle school, high school, and college or above), income (low, lower middle, higher middle, high), working status (employed group, unemployed group), marital status (yes, divorce or bereavement or separation, no), smoking (never smoked, smoked previously but not currently, and smoking currently), and alcohol consumption (never, previously but not currently, and currently). Body mass index was categorized into 3 groups according to body mass index cutoff points widely used for Asian populations (<23 kg/m2, 23–24.9 kg/m2, and ≥25 kg/m2). 20

Statistical Analysis

Participants' characteristics were compared using descriptive statistics and an independent t test. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± SD, and categorical variables as counts (n) and percentages (%). Between‐group differences were assessed with an independent t test for continuous variables and a chi‐square test for categorical variables. The equality‐of‐variances assumption for the t test was assessed using Levene's test; when the assumption was violated, Welch's t test was applied. We summarized the distribution of CVD subtypes by depressive symptom status (Table S1). Additionally, we quantified between‐group differences in participant characteristics by reporting effect sizes (Phi, Cramer's V and Cohen's d) (Table S2).

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to estimate odds ratios (OR) and 95% CI for the association between domain‐specific PA and depressive symptoms. We fitted 4 nested logistic regression models: unadjusted model—crude association; Model 1 (sociodemographic factors)—adjusted for education, income, work status, marital status, and age; Model 2 (behavioral and anthropometric factors)—additionally adjusted for body mass index, smoking status, and alcohol consumption; and Model 3 (mutual adjustment for PA domains)—further adjusted for the other PA domains to isolate domain‐specific effects. The Tables S3 and S4 indicate that saturated fatty acid, cholesterol, sodium, and carbohydrate (continuous variables) were additionally controlled for as covariates.

Stratified analyses were conducted to test the association between domain‐specific PA and depressive symptoms according to sex (male, female) and income (low, middle, high) using multivariate‐adjusted regression. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Among the 5788 patients with CVD, 484 (8.4%) reported depressive symptoms, whereas 5304 did not. As shown in Table 1, depressive symptoms were significantly more prevalent among women (P<0.001), those with body mass index ≥25 kg/m2 (P=0.025), individuals with no or only elementary school education (P<0.001), and those in lower‐income group (P<0.001). Unemployed participants (P<0.001), participants unmarried or not living with a spouse (P<0.001), current smokers (P<0.001), and nondrinkers (P<0.001) were also more likely to report depressive symptoms. Additionally, those with depressive symptoms had significantly lower levels of transport PA (327.67 ± 31.50 MET‐min/week) compared with those without symptoms (409.76 ± 9.17 MET‐min/week; P<0.001). Association Between Domain‐Specific Physical Activity and Depressive Symptoms in Patients With CVD and Related Conditions

As shown in Table 2, compared with individuals with no work PA (0 MET‐min/week), those engaged in low (1–600 MET‐min/week) and high (≥600 MET‐min/week) levels of work PA had significantly higher odds of experiencing depressive symptoms in the fully adjusted model. Specifically, the odds were 2.61 times higher in the low work PA group (95% CI, 1.57–4.36) and 3.17 times higher in the high work PA group (95% CI, 2.03–4.95). In contrast, high leisure PA (≥600 MET‐min/week) was associated with significantly lower odds of depressive symptoms (OR, 0.45 [95% CI, 0.28–0.72]; P for trend <0.001), while no significant association was observed for the low leisure PA group. For transport PA, only the high PA group demonstrated a significant inverse association with depressive symptoms (OR, 0.73 [95% CI, 0.54–0.98]); the low transport PA group did not show a statistically significant association (Figure1).

Stratified Analyses of the Association Between Domain‐Specific Physical Activity and Depressive Symptoms by Potential Effect Modifiers

Table 3 presents the stratified associations between domain‐specific PA and depressive symptoms by sex and income level. The association between work PA and depressive symptoms was more pronounced in women than in men. Among participants with high work PA (≥600 MET‐min/week), women had 3.38 times higher odds of depressive symptoms (95% CI, 1.90–6.02), whereas men had 2.91 times higher odds (95% CI, 1.43–5.93), with a statistically significant interaction by sex (P for interaction <0.001). A similar pattern was observed across income groups; participants in the low‐income group with high work PA had 3.27 times higher odds of depressive symptoms (95% CI, 1.92–5.55), compared with 2.73 times higher odds in the high‐income group (95% CI, 1.14–6.52; P for interaction <0.001). In contrast, high leisure PA (≥600 MET‐min/week) was associated with significantly lower odds of depressive symptoms in both men (OR, 0.50 [95% CI, 0.26–0.94]) and women (OR, 0.44 [95% CI 0.22–0.89]), with significant interactions by both sex and income level (P for interaction <0.001 for both).

Discussion

We found that the association between PA and depressive symptoms in patients with CVD and related conditions varied significantly according to the PA domain. Higher levels of work PA were associated with increased odds of depressive symptoms, whereas greater engagement in leisure PA was associated with decreased odds. These associations remained robust after adjusting for potential confounding variables including demographic, socioeconomic, and behavioral factors. The adverse association between work PA and depressive symptoms was especially pronounced among women and individuals with lower incomes, underscoring the role of social context in shaping the mental health impact of PA. These findings highlight the importance of evaluating domain‐specific PA when addressing psychological well‐being in individuals with CVD and provide further support for the PA paradox. 21 , 22

As presented in Table 2, participants who engaged in either low (1–600 MET‐min/week) or high (≥600 MET‐min/week) levels of work PA had significantly higher odds of reporting depressive symptoms compared with those who reported no work PA. Specifically, the odds were 2.61 times higher among those in the low activity group (95% CI, 1.57–4.36) and 3.17 times higher among those in the high activity group (95% CI, 2.03–4.95), suggesting a potential dose–response relationship. These findings are consistent with the PA paradox, in which higher levels of work PA may be detrimental to mental health. Although previous research has reported mixed results, some studies suggest protective effects and others indicate adverse outcomes. Our findings align with studies that emphasize the negative mental health impact of work PA. For example, one study found that any level of work PA was associated with significantly increased odds of depressive symptoms, with ORs of 2.74 in men and 2.84 in women. 8 In contrast, other studies have suggested weaker or even inverse associations, depending on factors such as PA intensity, occupation type, and broader contextual influences. 23 In contrast to work PA, higher levels of leisure PA was significantly associated with lower odds of depressive symptoms. Specifically, individuals engaging in ≥600 MET‐min/week of leisure PA had 55% lower odds of reporting depressive symptoms (OR, 0.45 [95% CI, 0.28–0.72]) (Table 2). This inverse relationship was consistent with the results of previous studies. For example, Schuch et al. found that engaging in 60 to 89 minutes of leisure PA per week was associated with nearly a 50% reduction in the risk of depression (OR, 0.47 [95% CI, 0.29–0.76]). 24 A meta‐analysis further demonstrated that engaging in 2.5 hours of brisk walking per week significantly reduced depressive symptoms. 25 Other studies have suggested that the protective effect of leisure PA may plateau at higher volumes, ∼25 MET‐hours/week, equivalent to ∼8 to 9 hours of walking, beyond which additional benefits may be limited. 26

Although both work and leisure PA may involve similar physical energy expenditure, the physiological and psychological responses to each seem to differ. 27 Work PA has been linked to increased stress, elevated cortisol and epinephrine levels, and heightened vascular tension and blood pressure. 28 In contrast, leisure PA is associated with reductions in stress and elevations in endorphins and serotonin, which are neurotransmitters linked to improved mood and well‐being. 29 Moreover, an individual's perception of PA may moderate its effect on health. Crum et al. showed that activity enhanced physiological outcomes when people viewed it as beneficial, suggesting that the context and perception of PA matter. 30 In this light, the adverse association between high work PA and depressive symptoms may be explained by chronic occupational stress, disruption of the hypothalamic‐pituitary‐adrenal axis, and sustained cortisol secretion. 31 Patients with CVD and related conditions may experience both physical and emotional fatigue from excessive work PA, which, combined with limited recovery, contributes to poorer mental health outcomes. Interestingly, the association between higher work PA and depressive symptoms was more pronounced in women (OR, 3.38 [95% CI, 1.90–6.02]) than in men (OR, 2.91 [95% CI, 1.43–5.93]), with a significant interaction by sex (P for interaction <0.001) (Table 3). One possible explanation for the stronger association between work PA and depressive symptoms in women is the inclusion of household labor in the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire's work PA domain. 15 Women often engage in both occupational work and unpaid domestic tasks, a combination that can contribute to increased workload, stress, physical fatigue, and psychological distress. 32 Previous research suggests that excessive physical exertion without sufficient recovery can lead to increased cortisol levels and chronic stress responses, particularly in demanding work environments. 33 Moreover, the dual burden of employment and household responsibilities may create additional mental health strain in women. 34 These findings highlight the need for personalized interventions targeting sex‐specific risk factors for depressive symptoms in patients with CVD and related conditions. Strategies for women should focus on reducing excessive work PA and ensuring recovery time, particularly for those managing both occupational and domestic workloads. These tailored approaches may help mitigate depressive symptoms and improve the overall mental well‐being of patients with CVD and related conditions.

These findings suggest that the mental health effects of PA differ depending on both its domain and the individual's socioeconomic status. In particular, higher levels of work PA were associated with an increased risk of depressive symptoms among lower‐income individuals (Table 3). This may be explained by the nature of work PA, which is often characterized by low autonomy, high physical demands, and limited recovery opportunities. Such conditions may contribute to chronic stress and adverse mental health outcomes, as reported in population‐based studies. 22 , 35 In contrast, leisure PA demonstrated a more robust protective effect against depressive symptoms in individuals with a higher income. This group may benefit from greater time flexibility, access to exercise facilities, and supportive social and environmental resources, which enable more autonomous and enjoyable participation in PA. 36 Furthermore, previous research suggests that self‐selected and voluntary PA is more likely to elicit positive affective responses and enhance psychological well‐being. 37 Taken together, these results underscore the need for tailored interventions that consider the socioeconomic context of patients with CVD and related conditions. Interventions for lower‐income individuals should focus on improving working conditions and reducing excessive physical workload, whereas strategies targeting higher‐income individuals may benefit from promoting consistent and enjoyable engagement in leisure PA to support mental health.

Additionally, previous studies have reported that dietary patterns are closely associated with depressive symptoms. 38 In line with this evidence, we conducted a sensitivity analysis to address potential dietary confounding. After adjusting for 4 major dietary components—saturated fat, cholesterol, sodium, and carbohydrate intake—previously linked to depression, the main associations between work and leisure‐time PA and depressive symptoms remained largely unchanged, supporting the robustness of these findings. In contrast, the initially significant inverse association between transport‐related PA and depressive symptoms became statistically nonsignificant after dietary adjustment. This attenuation may reflect the tendency of individuals with higher levels of active transport to also maintain healthier dietary habits, suggesting that clustering of health behaviors may have partially contributed to the observed association. These findings highlight the importance of considering co‐occurring health behaviors, such as diet, when interpreting the mental health benefits of PA.

Our findings highlighted the differential associations between domain‐specific PA and depressive symptoms. Leisure PA was associated with lower levels of depressive symptoms, whereas work PA was associated with higher levels. These results suggest that evaluating domain‐specific PA provides greater insight into its mental health effects than a generalized assessment of total PA. Although previous research using KNHANES data has explored the association between PA and depression in the general population, 8 our study uniquely focused on individuals diagnosed with or at high risk for CVD. By analyzing domain‐specific PA patterns and conducting interaction analyses by sex and income level, we identified important subgroup differences in the mental health impacts of work and leisure PA. These findings extend existing evidence by emphasizing the clinical relevance of tailored PA recommendations, particularly for vulnerable populations such as women and individuals with lower socioeconomic status.

This study had several limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the cross‐sectional study design inherently limits the ability to infer causal relationships between PA and depressive symptoms. Longitudinal studies are required to determine the temporal sequences and directions of these associations. Second, the prevalence of PA was assessed using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire, a self‐reported measure. Although widely used, self‐reported data are subject to recall bias, which may affect the accuracy of the findings and requires careful interpretation. Third, some subgroups of work and leisure PA (ie, low and high levels) had limited sample sizes, warranting cautious interpretation of the observed associations in these categories. Finally, the distribution and types of PA domains may vary significantly across cultural and social contexts. Therefore, future research should include individuals with CVD from diverse ethnic and cultural backgrounds to better understand the variations in PA patterns and their associations with depressive symptoms. This would offer critical insights into the cultural and social influences on the relationship between PA, CVD, and mental health. Despite these limitations, this study makes valuable contributions to our understanding of how domain‐specific PA affects depressive symptoms in patients with CVD. The use of a large, nationally representative sample of Korean patients enhanced the reliability and generalizability of the results. Moreover, the comparative analyses within this cohort make this study one of the few to specify the complex relationship between domain‐specific PA and depressive symptoms using a robust and meaningful sample size. Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of tailoring PA interventions by domain and individual context to improve the mental health outcomes of patients with CVD.

Conclusions

Our study highlighted the contrasting associations between different PA domains and depressive symptoms in individuals with CVD, thus supporting the concept of the PA paradox. Although leisure PA was associated with a lower level of depressive symptoms, work PA was positively associated with depressive symptoms, potentially due to occupational stress and sustained, physically demanding work‐related activity. These findings underscore the importance of prioritizing leisure PA as a part of mental health management strategies, particularly for individuals with CVD. However, owing to the observational nature of this study, causal relationships could not be established. Future research should explore the mechanisms underlying these associations and evaluate the effectiveness of targeted interventions that account for the paradoxical effects of different PA domains. Addressing these complexities may help optimize mental health outcomes in patients with CVD.
