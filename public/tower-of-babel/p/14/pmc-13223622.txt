Clinician experiences of organisational compassion in healthcare: a qualitative study
Authors: Teresa Pestian, Jhansi Chandra Ellakula, Eli Awtrey, Adam C Carle, Naomi Winick, Jason Kanov, Rachel Thienprayoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundWhile compassion is widely recognised as an essential component of high-quality patient care, the compassion needs of clinicians often go unrecognised and unmet. Clinicians face multifaceted sources of workplace suffering, both sources inherent to working with the sick and avoidable sources due to healthcare systems and leadership challenges. Organisational compassion, defined as the continuous and systematic identification, prevention and alleviation of sources of suffering for healthcare workers, offers a paradigm shift in mitigating and preventing clinician suffering and burnout. Yet little is known about how clinicians experience suffering and compassion from their organisations, teams and leaders.ObjectiveOur overarching goal is to develop a clinician-reported experience measure of organisational compassion. The purpose of this study was to explore how clinicians experience suffering and compassion in healthcare organisations.Design and participantsThis qualitative study used semistructured interviews of interdisciplinary paediatric hospice and palliative care clinicians from across the USA. A moderator’s guide was developed based on the literature of organisational compassion in management and healthcare and validated through practice interviews with clinicians. 22 participants were recruited via national paediatric hospice and palliative care email list serves. Video interviews were conducted via Zoom. Transcripts were analysed using a hybrid grounded theory-thematic analysis methodology to identify themes and to construct a theoretical framework of compassion experiences.ResultsFive major themes of experiencing compassion emerged: (1) Feeling cared about, characterised by authentic, empathetic responses to clinician distress; (2) Dignity, encompassing being valued, respected and recognised as a whole person and professional; (3) Proximal (team) compassion, including camaraderie, shared workload and mutual support within teams; (4) Structural (organisational) compassion, reflecting policies, practices and benefits that alleviate or exacerbate suffering and (5) Compassionate leadership behaviours, such as presence, empathy and connection to frontline staff needs.ConclusionsHealthcare work includes sources of both inherent and avoidable suffering for clinicians. In this study, we sought to understand how clinicians experience compassion from their organisations, leaders and team members during times of distress. We found five themes of experiencing compassion in healthcare organisations: feeling cared about; dignity; proximal (team) compassion; structural (organisational) compassion and compassionate leadership behaviours. These qualitative data and results will provide an empiric foundation for the development of a clinician-reported experience measure of compassion for use in healthcare settings. Such a measure will enable future research examining how compassion experiences in healthcare may predict workforce outcomes such as burnout, satisfaction, engagement and thriving. Ultimately, this work may support the design of interventions aimed at strengthening compassionate organisational cultures and improving conditions for the healthcare workforce and both experiences and outcomes of the patients they serve.Keywords: PALLIATIVE CARE, Organisation and Administration, Burnout, Occupational Stress, Organisation of health services

Background

Clinicians enter the workforce seeking careers defined by meaning and purpose, and throughout medical training their professional roles become core parts of their human identities. If suffering is defined as ‘anything that threatens the intactness of the whole person’,1 then arguably, modern clinicians suffer from myriad sources including a high frequency of workplace aggression and violence,2 moral distress,3 salient anti-science sentiments in society, degrading autonomy,4 vicarious trauma5 and burnout.6,9 The impact of these collective sources of suffering is evidenced by the recent mental health crisis in healthcare and related epidemics of burnout and resignation in the healthcare workforce.10 While some sources of distress such as caring for the sick and dying are inherent to healthcare work, many sources are avoidable and, therefore, it is the responsibility of healthcare leaders to systematically identify and mitigate or prevent them.11 This is how organisational compassion—the continuous and systematic identification, prevention and alleviation of all sources of workplace suffering for clinicians—can be operationalised.11

Compassion means noticing that another person is suffering, making an empathetic connection with that suffering, and then acting to relieve or mitigate that suffering.12 Patients and quality experts alike identify compassion as a cornerstone and predictor of high-quality healthcare,13 yet we fail to approach clinician needs for compassion with the same urgency as patient needs. Therefore, whereas clinicians are expected to demonstrate innate and unceasing compassionate behaviours towards their patients, they do so in a system that causes them to suffer without a concomitant compassionate response directed towards them—a disturbing juxtaposition.14 Rather than focusing on individual clinicians’ behaviours (eg, mindfulness, resiliency training) as an antidote to workforce stress, a model of organisational compassion shifts the locus of responsibility in addressing the prevailing challenges to the healthcare workforce onto the system itself.14 Further, unlike traditional management paradigms which emphasise efficiency and productivity, organisational compassion centres on creating a culture of empathy, support and emotional care within the healthcare workplace. This holistic and humanistic approach acknowledges the emotional toll of caregiving roles and emphasises the well-being of employees as an essential aspect of organisational success and compassionate patient care. By designing and nurturing environments that minimise unnecessary sources of suffering and promote compassion for workers who experience inevitable suffering, healthcare organisations can provide their staff with the resources necessary to cope with the emotional demands of their profession, to maintain compassion for their patients and to deliver high-quality, safe and reliable patient care over the course of sustainable careers.

Previous conceptualisations of organisational compassion in healthcare define it as a four-part process of: (1) Noticing of a colleague’s suffering, (2) Empathising with the colleague’s distress, (3) Assessing to understand the causes of suffering and (4) Responding to mitigate or alleviate the suffering (NEAR).15 This defines organisational compassion from the perspective of the actor, or the person who noticed that another person was suffering and attempted to relieve that suffering.16 17 Yet the effectiveness of the compassion intervention from the perspective of the sufferer—or in the context of healthcare, the clinician—must also be considered to understand how the response is experienced and whether suffering was indeed relieved. Further, a recent systematic review of organisational compassion-based interventions to improve clinician well-being highlighted the need for both an empirically grounded framework and methodologically sound tools to measure and evaluate compassion within healthcare organisations.16 Therefore, the purpose of this study was to explore clinician experiences of suffering and compassion in a healthcare setting. We sought to understand clinician suffering at work and whether their organisations, leaders and team members responded in a way that was experienced as compassionate or non-compassionate. As the study was qualitative in nature, there were no a priori hypotheses stated.

Methods

We used purposeful (extreme) sampling17 18 and recruited paediatric hospice and palliative care (HPC) clinicians for this study because paediatric HPC is a clinical environment with the potential for high levels of both patient and clinician suffering, necessitating high levels of compassion by and for clinicians. Much of the research literature in clinician suffering and patient compassion has been evaluated in HPC settings for these reasons.19 20 Participants were recruited via various HPC listservs including the American Academy of Hospice and Palliative Medicine Connect platform; the Hospice and Palliative Nurses Association list serve; the Social Workers in Hospice and Palliative Medicine listserv, the Health Care Chaplaincy Network list serve; the Association of Professional Chaplains listserv and the Physician Assistants in Hospice and Palliative Care list serve. A study advertisement was also shared on social media. Potential participants who responded to a recruitment email or ad were sent a follow-up survey to determine whether inclusion criteria were met; those who met criteria were scheduled for video interview calls. Inclusion criteria were: clinicians (physicians, social workers, child life specialists, nurses, nurse practitioners (NP), physician assistants (PA) and chaplains) who had practised paediatric HPC in the USA for at least 12 months; were currently practising paediatric HPC for the majority of their clinical time; were part of an interdisciplinary HPC team (‘interdisciplinary’ defined for study purposes as including at least one physician, at least one social worker and at least one nurse, NP or PA); had been in their current organisation for at least 6 months; and who attended regular HPC interdisciplinary meetings (defined as at least every other week). Exclusion criteria were current employees at the primary investigator (PI)’s home institution to limit the potential bias or withholding of experiences due to personal relationships with the research team. Recruitment began in the fall of 2021 and interviews were conducted in the fall and winter of that year; qualitative analysis began concurrently with interviews and concluded in 2022.

A moderator’s guide (online supplemental file A) evaluating experiences of suffering and compassion in the workplace was developed by experts in paediatric palliative care, organisational compassion, teamwork and compassion in healthcare and informed by a literature review of organisational compassion and patient experiences of compassion. The guide was tested with several HPC clinicians and iteratively revised based on feedback before participants were enrolled. Virtual interview calls were conducted via the Zoom platform. Participants were offered the choice of having their video on or off during the interviews according to their comfort. Because the field of paediatric HPC is small, as a practising paediatric HPC clinician, the PI did not have access to identifying information of participants to protect their confidentiality given the sensitive nature of the interviews. Field notes were not taken as part of this study. Participants did not receive their transcripts. All interviews were done by a qualitatively trained research coordinator interviewer (TP) and only the participant and interviewer were present. Two team members (RT and TP) maintained audit trails throughout the study including raw data, memos, reflexive notes and methodological decisions. The Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) reporting guidelines21 were used to draft this manuscript, and the SRQR reporting checklist22 when editing, included in online supplemental file B.

22 participants representing varying clinical roles participated in qualitative interviews. Most (73%) identified as female and as White/Caucasian in race (86.5%). Physicians (41%) were the largest clinical role represented, with registered nurses (13.6%), NPs or PAs (18.3%), chaplains (13.6%), social workers (9%) and a child life specialist (4.5%) also included. No participants repeated interviews. Participant demographic information is summarised in table 1. Average transcribed interview length was 8610 words, and average duration was 62 min and 55 s.

Patients and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this study as our focus was on the healthcare workforce.

Analysis

Zoom transcription software provided first-pass transcription of each interview, then transcripts were validated and deidentified by study staff. We used a qualitative analysis using a hybrid grounded theory-thematic analysis occurred concordantly as interviews were held until saturation was reached.23 This hybrid approach combined grounded theory-informed coding techniques with thematic analysis. Grounded theory is the method of analytic choice when no prior theoretical model exists from which to develop hypotheses.24 The method is frequently employed in healthcare and has been used extensively by the authors.25,28 For the purposes of this study, open and axial coding was used in the initial phase of analysis to identify relationships among concepts to develop a preliminary codebook. This codebook then informed the thematic analysis of the remaining interviews as data was mapped to existing codes and iteratively revised as new concepts emerged.29 The authors used a constant comparative method (ie, a constant process of taking information from data collection and comparing it with emerging categories). Categories, subcategories and themes were iteratively revised by the study team until consensus was reached. Qualitative analysis was done using NVivo V.12 (QSR International, Victoria, Australia) software.

Results

Five categories of experiencing compassion emerged from the data: feeling cared about; dignity; proximal (team) compassion; structural (organisational) compassion and compassionate leadership behaviours. Due to the sensitive nature of the qualitative data and potential for deductive disclosure, the data set is not publicly available; participants were assured of confidentiality as part of the informed consent process and even deidentified data sharing could potentially violate that agreement.

Feeling cared about

Participants consistently described feeling cared about as a core component of experiencing compassion. Subcategories within feeling cared about included: the sense of being genuinely cared about by coworkers, leaders and others in the workplace; the experience that a compassionate response to suffering is motivated by authentic caring; and caring as the experience that when they were distressed or suffering, someone noticed, responded and acted to mitigate the suffering. Exemplar quotes include:

All the things that’s always made me feel like this group of people just genuinely cares about each other as much as we care about the work we do.

Our culture is really caring about each other, like I think people truly care about each other on an individual basis.

At our next team debriefing, I shared this story, and my team members were, like, completely silent while I was telling the story. They were, they were just very kind and not trying to make it okay…just doing it out of kindness.

Participants emphasised their judgement of the intentions of a responder (eg, their colleague, a leader or the organisation broadly) in determining whether a caring response was experienced as compassionate. The belief that a colleague or a leader was motivated by authentic caring when offering support was experienced as more compassionate; if their behaviour was perceived to be motivated by something other than a genuine desire to relieve the individual’s suffering, the response was not experienced as truly compassionate. Specific statements which support this include,

If I started experiencing our leadership as authentic individuals who are meeting us where we are in the work we do, I think I’d have a very different experience.

Just the way that it was, it felt very genuine and felt very open ended, like, I want to help. I don't know how to…they didn't know exactly what to do with it. It was just very real and very welcomed.

The demeanor in which he does it, like, shows that there’s, like, he enjoys doing it. And it’s not just to check a box on his to do list.

Dignity

A central domain that emerged from participants’ narratives was the vital role of dignity in shaping their experiences of both compassion and suffering in the workplace. Participants described that experiences of compassion in their organisation were tied to feelings of being valued, respected and supported not only as a clinician, but as a unique human being. Participants articulated that when dignity was upheld, it fostered a sense of purpose, belonging and value. Conversely, when dignity was violated, through dismissiveness, exclusion or lack of recognition, this produced deep emotional distress and a sense of professional erasure. Subcategories that emerged within this dignity category included feeling valued as a unique human being in the workplace; feeling valued as a clinician; feeling as though one’s voice and opinion are respected in patient care; recognition for work well done; being adequately resourced to do one’s job well; and experiences of justice, belonging and inclusion.

For example, one participant said,

It was nice that somebody acknowledged that our work was…meaningful and important and difficult and making a difference.

Another shared,

Make sure that it’s an environment that values who we are as people and who we are as workers.

Participants also invoked ideas of dignity violations or being systematically devalued as experiences of suffering. One participant shared the act of being physically moved away from a parent by a coworker, which was experienced as active devaluation of both their innate humanity and their role in patient care:

I was holding his hand, and I had my arm around the mom and the social worker came in and like physically moved me out of the way to be able to hug the mother. And so, you know, often we feel overlooked and dismissed.

Another participant was asked about experiences of suffering, and described a lack of voice and respect, or components of dignity which were dishonoured by the other teams involved in the patient’s care:

It didn’t really matter how much our team was trying, it felt like we weren’t being heard when we were trying to advocate for the patient and her mom.

Proximal or team compassion

Participants consistently shared examples of compassionate practices between team members or within the team broadly, both scheduled and as needed, to continually acknowledge and respond to sources of suffering related to patient care or personal issues. Subcategories that emerged within this category include experiences of team camaraderie, humour and connectedness; experiences of workload sharing within the team; feeling able to ask for emotional support from team members; feeling able to ask for instrumental support from team members in the course of work; and experiences of team debriefings. Participants shared that because of the nature of paediatric palliative care and the inherent suffering encountered in caring for dying children, it was necessary for their teams to cultivate a culture that normalised supporting one another emotionally and by sharing work responsibilities. The notion that without these conditions, clinicians would not be able to sustain themselves in the work of paediatric HPC over long periods of time was common. Exemplar quotes include:

I think that maybe why and how we all keep doing this is because we do have that support from each other.

When there’s been a lot of hard cases in a row, probably, I mean like the tragic consequences or death of a child, I think we, you know we try to protect each other and pick consults differently, maybe we give someone a break.

Several participants shared stories about how the team responded compassionately when a specific team member suffered a personal tragedy or crisis, such as by bringing food, providing coverage for work, or simply providing emotional support. One participant said,

I recently had a death in the family. And my colleague, the other physician that I work with…she made sure I didn’t do like an end-of-life patient this week.

Participants described that such practices fostered team camaraderie and belonging. For instance, one participant shared:

I think, just, that trust. I know that our team is really close-knit. And it’s been like the same people…we've been through a lot together and so we know each other well and I know that I can lean on them.

The importance of team leadership in prioritising this culture and these behaviours was noted, both by participants who were leaders themselves and by those who were team members. A participant said,

We just had a lot of open conversation about setting the example and the tone for the team and how we were going to work together and how we were going to encourage each other to be open, be honest, and really look at what our teamwork is like.

Structural or organisational compassion

Participants distinguished experiences of ‘the organisation’ from behaviours of specific leaders; benefits, policies or practices available to all employees and more diffuse cultural constructs like psychological safety emerged in the category of structural or organisational compassion. Subcategories that emerged in this category include the feeling that organisational policies, practices and/or benefits meet the needs of unique individuals; experiences of systems-level debrief opportunities or mental health support such as peer support programmes after difficult events; effectiveness of employee assistance programmes; feeling that systems, policies and/or practices prevent and/or mitigate recurrent sources of suffering, distress and stress; feelings of physical safety, psychological safety and emotional safety in the organisation; and the perception that the organisation was willing to change in response to salient sources of suffering. Participants also frequently noted that organisational gestures to support staff were at times difficult to access or not what was truly needed at that moment:

It’s hard to utilize any of the tools and resources and things they set up because there’s no allotted time…with no time outside of the job hours, it’s often hard to want to engage in any of those things.

I think that the organization is trying, and yet sometimes really misses the mark on things like that.

Organizations could do a better job of…we’re just so driven by performance that people don’t get a chance to just take a break, to breathe, to step away from the bedside.

When organisational gestures ‘missed the mark’, some participants shared that those efforts in fact worsened workforce suffering:

There have been some gestures of gratitude by the organization to all of us, not just palliative care, but all staff. Some of which have been helpful and some of which have actually felt more depleting.

Like why keep trying? Why keep exerting energy into this that never changes that never makes a difference? Things don’t actually get resolved.

Participants emphasised effective communication as a key dimension of compassion from the organisation.

The organization’s also responsible for maintaining good communication with people, because not having good or accurate or honest or truthful information produces a lot of anxiety for everybody.

There were a lot of opportunities for transparency of information about things going on during the pandemic, which I, you know, maybe would not have looked at that as an act of compassion, but I think the context of this actually, I do kind of look at that way.

Compassionate leadership behaviours

Participants described the importance of compassionate leadership behaviours when experiencing organisational compassion. In this category, ‘leadership’ included team leaders, leaders of other teams, hospital managers and organisational executives. Subcategories included experiencing compassionate leadership as personal and individualised, leadership as physically present and connected to the needs of frontline staff, and leaders setting a tone of genuine compassion or sincere caring. The importance of seeing leaders physically present during or after stressful or distressing events was a consistent theme among participants:

Presence. Show up. Come around, and acknowledge, because you know, let’s be honest. All of these things get reported…our leaders know…so be present. Show up on those units. Just walk through every now and then.

It was nice that he, one, came to my office, and two, knew my name and what I do and thank me for the work that I do. Recognized that it was hard, recognizing that this week is particularly bad, not only for our patients and families but, like the entire hospital.

Participants described feeling an organisation was more compassionate when organisational leadership was connected to the needs of frontline staff, and less compassionate when leaders were perceived to be disconnected from their needs and experiences. For example:

I have leadership, who like unless I tell them, they don't know that I've been to five deaths this week, and even if I tell them they just kind of stare at me and they don't know what to do.

And no one really gets it…my director, my director’s manager, director and then the CEO…just real disconnected.

For leaders who had moved away from bedside care into organisational leadership positions, participants emphasised the importance of these leaders remaining connected to their prior clinical experiences, noting that this proximity fosters a deeper sense of empathy and understanding. Drawing on memories of times when they felt unsupported or overwhelmed, participants described how these formative experiences shaped their commitment to leading with compassion. By staying grounded in the realities of frontline work, leaders were seen as more attuned to the needs of their staff and better equipped to create supportive, responsive work environments. One participant shared,

She remembers being the only person there and…feeling burnt out and being there all the time and there was no one to like keep her accountable or tell her you need to go take a day off, and …she felt taken advantage of.

Many participants expressed compassion for their leaders, describing an understanding that leadership roles were complex but still desiring authentic connections with their leaders.

We know they’re human. We know they feel these things. I don’t think that any of them are heartless. But it’s hard sometimes to get them to that place where they can actually engage that and show that emotion.

Discussion

This is one of few studies to focus on the receiving side of compassion in organisations24 25 and the first to specifically evaluate compassion experiences in the healthcare context. Whereas clinician experiences of suffering and compassion in the workplace have not yet been described, there is a body of comparative literature describing how patients experience compassion from clinicians.1320 26,28 Sinclair et al identified seven domains of patient experiences of compassion: virtues (eg, genuineness, authenticity, love); relational space (eg, engaged caregiving); virtuous response (eg, knowing the person), seeking to understand; relational communicating; attending to needs; and patient reported outcomes.28 We found similar emergent themes in how clinicians experience compassion but with a few distinctions. For example, the domains of virtues and relational space are similar to our emergent categories of feeling cared about and team compassion, and relational communication is included in compassionate leadership behaviours. But the experience of compassion between a patient and clinician is primarily dyadic whereas the way a clinician experiences compassion in the workplace is multifaceted and inclusive of interpersonal, team and organisational relationships. The emergent five categories of clinician experiences of organisational compassion described here are therefore more diffuse in structure than those found for patient experiences, reflecting the layered and systemic dynamics between a clinician and their team, other teams, leaders and the organisational culture.

Feeling cared about has been identified as a core component of patient experiences of compassion19 28 and our participants likewise experienced caring from their colleagues as the empathic component of compassion. Yet our participants emphasised a step of judging another’s intention as genuine caring or not in whether they experienced a response as compassionate, which is a novel finding. This focus on intention in receiving compassion is particularly salient in healthcare where many experience burnout: the cynicism that accompanies burnout may increase the likelihood that the sufferer will fail to recognise the intentions of a colleague who authentically desires to help. This suggests that the burnout syndrome itself is a barrier to experiencing compassion. This dynamic could create significant challenges for leaders who desire to positively influence organisations in which burnout is prevalent and frustrate clinicians who seek to respond compassionately to coworkers during times of duress.

Dignity as an essential element of how clinicians experience compassion emerged from the data, which is a novel finding in both the healthcare well-being literature and organisational compassion literature.30 Hicks identified ten essential elements of dignity: acceptance of identity, recognition, acknowledgement, inclusion, safety, fairness, independence, understanding, benefit of the doubt and accountability.31 When asked about suffering and compassion, our participants similarly described themes of feeling valued as a unique human being and for the work they do, themes of justice and inclusion, and feeling recognised for their contributions. Feeling devalued or inadequately resourced for the demands of the job were likewise described as sources of suffering. Much of the literature on dignity in medicine is focused specifically on the end-of-life period in HPC settings and a concern for loss of dignity in the dying.32,34 Research regarding dignity in the workplace remains nascent, but key dimensions of workplace dignity include safe and healthy working conditions, providing just reward for work and providing secure terms of employment.35 Similarly, when workers are harmed by unsafe working conditions, companies have been accused of violating human dignity.36 More research is needed to elucidate the relationship between human dignity and experiences of compassion in the workplace.

Previous organisational compassion research has focused on creating a compassionate climate or cultivating conditions for compassion to arise between two workers1537,39 emphasising the act of compassion rather than its experience. Simpson et al conceptualised a SEND-NEAR model of organisational compassion which includes four steps between a sufferer and responder of Sending-Noticing, Emoting-Empathising, Narrating-Appraising and Desiring-Responding.40 In our study, participants consistently described the first necessary step of their suffering as being noticed by their colleagues or leaders. Yet the culture of stoicism in medicine teaches clinicians to actively suppress sending signals of distress in the workplace; similarly, emoting or indicating that one is struggling is commonly considered unprofessional.41 Despite this culture, many of our participants described a willingness to be vulnerable with team members and shared examples of a protective team culture of support, caring and compassion that sustained them through difficult times. Participants consistently described scheduled and ad hoc debriefing sessions as mechanisms of team compassion and caring experiences. The consistent modelling of vulnerability and suffering and the concomitant compassionate response which happens in well-facilitated debriefing sessions may facilitate a culture of trust in paediatric HPC which mediates future compassionate experiences and counters the suppression of emotion found elsewhere in medicine. In a scoping review of organisational compassion in healthcare, we previously found that Schwartz Rounds—events in which clinicians model vulnerability and narrate40 stories of suffering with concomitant compassionate response by the audience—are one of few evidence-based mechanisms of organisational compassion in the US healthcare system.42 We hypothesise that the other themes of proximal or team compassion such as humour, belonging and camaraderie, workload sharing and being able to seek support from team members may be byproducts or predictors of trust within the team. In nurses, low organisational trust has been correlated with scoring higher in all dimensions of burnout43 and higher trust in leaders and organisations is protective against burnout.44 More research is needed to elucidate the relationship between trust and compassion experiences in healthcare and to evaluate what other factors may be necessary for clinicians to feel comfortable sharing distress with colleagues. In a medical culture which suppresses signalling or emoting, though, the burden remains on clinicians and leaders alike to proactively notice, prevent and respond to recurrent or predictable sources of suffering or distress in their colleagues.

A recent systematic review of compassionate leadership found that ‘literature on compassion reveals a variety of ways in which compassion has been defined…but one hardly finds an article which defines compassionate leadership clearly’.45 Our participants described experiencing multiple behaviours by healthcare leaders as compassionate, including leaders being physically present during difficult times, being connected with the needs of frontline staff and demonstrating sincere caring for them. Shuck, et al interviewed leaders and identified six similar elements of compassionate leadership behaviours: integrity, accountability, presence, empathy, authenticity and dignity.46 Presence is also a ‘compassionate virtue’ noted by Sinclair et al20 but when demonstrated by leaders, reflects the ability of a leader to ‘stay attuned to people and situations by focusing their attention on the present moment’ and is characterised by a leader’s ability to ‘deeply listen’.46 Thus, our findings validate previous literature and also contribute to the body of literature on compassionate leadership behaviours, as our focus was on the lived experience of workers rather than leader perceptions of their own behaviours.

Strengths and limitations

Interviews were conducted in the autumn of 2021 closely following the COVID-19 pandemic, a period characterised by widespread clinician distress across the world. Many participants reflected on the pandemic as a concrete example of how compassion was or was not expressed by leaders, within teams and across organisations during a time of collective strain. This timing therefore offered participants recent and salient examples of both compassionate and non-compassionate organisational responses to highly visible clinician suffering and distress. We do not believe these results are confounded by the pandemic, but rather that the timing offered a methodological advantage as what may have otherwise been subtle compassionate or non-compassionate behaviours were made obvious as organisations were forced to respond to widespread suffering in real time.

It is possible that clinicians with more strongly held experiences of suffering and/or compassion were drawn to volunteer for this study, which could bias results. Our recruitment strategy was designed to promote diversity in paediatric palliative care roles, demographics and setting, and the resultant sample reflected a range of such experiences. Further, our sampling strategy was purposefully oriented towards a population in which suffering is a salient and accessible experience, which enabled us to elicit detailed and nuanced responses regarding the effects of compassion (or lack thereof) on clinicians in the workplace. As our goal was to develop a phenomenological understanding of how compassion functions, we believe that our purposeful (extreme) sampling approach17 18 is a methodological strength. Interviews were conducted via video calls, which allowed flexibility for participants and allowed recruitment from a national sample but may have limited verbal communication and rapport building compared with in-person interviews. Data regarding the composition of the field of paediatric HPC including racial/ethnic data are scarce, but available data indicate that physicians are the most common clinical role represented in teams, followed by PAs/NPs, social workers, nurses and chaplains.47 Therefore, we do feel the study sample is generally representative of the field regarding role composition, but as most participants were White and identified as female, it is possible that sampling more male clinicians or those in underrepresented races/ethnicities may have resulted in themes not represented in these results.

All participants were intentionally drawn from the field of paediatric HPC. While this might seem to limit applicability to other healthcare settings, this context represents a field in which both patient suffering and concomitant clinician suffering are prominent, and clinicians in this setting are typically at a lower power valence organisationally than other teams. The combination of these aspects highlights the salience of organisational compassion (or the lack thereof), making this an ideal field to investigate clinician experiences of suffering and compassion in healthcare organisations. But it is possible that clinicians in other countries or clinical fields with lower emotional interdependence may experience suffering and compassion somewhat distinctly from those represented in this sample. Future research should examine whether these findings are generalisable beyond the field of paediatric HPC and evaluate whether these and/or other domains are experienced in fields in which suffering and compassion are less explicitly recognised. It will also be important to evaluate these constructs in healthcare systems external to the United States to understand how differing structures, cultures and care delivery systems may influence suffering and compassion for clinicians.

Conclusions

Healthcare work includes sources of both inherent and avoidable suffering for clinicians. In this study, we sought to understand how clinicians experience compassion from their organisations, leaders and team members during times of distress. We found five themes of experiencing compassion in healthcare organisations: feeling cared about; dignity; proximal (team) compassion; structural (organisational) compassion; and compassionate leadership behaviours. These qualitative data and results will provide an empiric foundation for the development of a clinician-reported experience measure of compassion for use in healthcare settings. Such a measure will enable future research examining how compassion experiences in healthcare may predict workforce outcomes such as burnout, satisfaction, engagement and thriving. Ultimately, this work may support the design of interventions aimed at strengthening compassionate organisational cultures and improving conditions for the healthcare workforce and both experiences and outcomes of the patients they serve.
