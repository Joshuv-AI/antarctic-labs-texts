Fractional caputo modeling of delayed disease dynamics: sensitivity analysis and control strategies incorporating environmental influences
Authors: Sushil Pathak, Venkata Ratnam Kota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents a fractional-order epidemic model incorporating Caputo derivatives and time delays to capture the environmental effects and incubation periods inherent in disease transmission dynamics. We analyzed the qualitative properties of the system, such as the existence, uniqueness, and boundedness of solutions, ensuring both mathematical consistency and biological relevance. The basic reproduction number is derived as a threshold parameter governing the stability of equilibria. Local and global stability analysis of the disease-free and endemic equilibria has been discussed, revealing conditions for disease eradication or persistence. Furthermore, sensitivity analysis is performed to identify the most influential parameters affecting the threshold parameter and transmission dynamics, providing valuable insights for designing effective control strategies. Numerical simulations are presented to illustrate and validate the analytical results.

Introduction

Mathematical modeling has become an indispensable framework for analyzing the transmission dynamics and control of infectious diseases. Traditionally, compartmental models based on classical differential equations have been employed to study the evolution of epidemics by capturing the transitions among susceptible, infected, and recovered individuals1,2. While these models have provided fundamental insights, they often fail to incorporate the environmental and hereditary characteristics inherent in biological processes. To overcome these limitations, fractional-order models, particularly those utilizing the Caputo fractional derivative, have gained prominence3,4. Unlike classical derivatives, the Caputo derivative accounts for the historical states of the system, making it especially well-suited for epidemiological modeling, where past disease progression, partial immunity, and time-lagged responses significantly influence current and future dynamics5,6. In this study, we construct a fractional-order compartmental model governed by Caputo-type derivatives to explore the spread and control of infectious diseases. This framework enables a more nuanced representation of critical epidemiological factors, including nonlinear transmission dynamics, delayed onset of symptoms, vaccination interventions, and time-dependent control measures7,8. In recent years, fractional-order differential equations have been widely used to model complex real-world dynamical processes more accurately than classical integer-order models. The main motivation for adopting a fractional-order formulation is its ability to capture memory and hereditary properties of the underlying system. Unlike the standard integer-order derivative, the fractional derivative is a nonlocal operator, meaning that the present state of the system depends not only on its current value but also on its entire past history. This feature is particularly suitable for biological and epidemiological processes, where the evolution of the disease may depend on previous exposure, immunity development, treatment history, and delayed physiological responses. Therefore, the inclusion of fractional-order derivatives provides a more realistic framework by incorporating the long-term memory effect, which cannot be effectively represented using classical models. For these reasons, we employ the Caputo fractional derivative of order \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$0<\alpha \le 1$$\end{document} in the proposed model, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} represents the strength of memory in the system, and the classical model is recovered as a special case when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha = 1$$\end{document}. The fractional approach is particularly effective for diseases such as influenza or covid-19, where immunity may be temporary, and the risk of reinfection or recurrent outbreaks persists9,10. The non-local nature of the Caputo derivative allows the model to incorporate long-term environmental effects, reflecting how past exposures and interventions influence current susceptibility and disease progression11. This characteristic is crucial for evaluating the timing and effectiveness of public health interventions, such as quarantine enforcement or vaccination campaigns, which rely on understanding both immediate and delayed responses within a population12–14. Furthermore, fractional-order modeling provides enhanced flexibility for capturing heterogeneous transmission pathways, variable infectious periods, and complex feedback mechanisms driven by social behavior, immunity loss, or pathogen evolution15. These elements are often oversimplified in classical models, but are essential for accurately simulating real-world epidemic scenarios. Throughout history, humans have continuously struggled against evolving pathogenic threats. Despite advancements in antibiotics, antiviral drugs, and vaccination programs, pathogens exhibit an extraordinary capacity for adaptation and resistance16. The iterative nature of epidemic response, where disease characteristics must first be understood before effective interventions are designed, creates a critical time window that can result in significant morbidity and mortality. Here, mathematical models play a vital predictive role by enabling in silico experimentation before real-world implementation17,18. The roots of epidemiological modeling trace back to the pioneering efforts of Bernoulli, Ross, McKendrick, and Kermack19–22, whose foundational models continue to influence contemporary research. In recent years, fractional calculus, particularly models based on Caputo derivatives, has been extensively applied to infectious disease dynamics, offering a more comprehensive perspective on emerging infections, vaccine-preventable diseases, and cyclical epidemics23–27. By integrating Caputo fractional derivatives into epidemic modeling, we propose a robust and environmental aware framework that captures the intricate temporal behavior of infectious diseases28. This approach not only enhances theoretical understanding but also supports the design and assessment of intervention strategies. The resulting model serves as a valuable tool for policymakers and public health professionals, enabling the simulation of diverse scenarios and facilitating data-driven decisions for effective epidemic control. With this background, in the present paper, we explore the capacity of a recently proposed mathematical model in which the roles of vaccination and treatment put together in combating a disease for the survival of a susceptible population26,29,30. The model proposed in31 is general and claimed to be applicable to any situation of infectious disease. The model consists of three compartments of the population, namely the susceptible, the infected, and the recovered part. The authors in32 studied the existence of both endemic and disease-free equilibria and their stability. Further exploring the model given in31, the authors estimated the treatment and vaccination rates with which the spread of disease could be controlled to a desired level16. The main motive of the present study is to further strengthen the model in31. In the present study, we modify the model by introducing time delays, which are realistic and natural in disease dynamics but were ignored earlier. These delays might be due to the delay in the production of suitable vaccines, the supply of vaccines in different areas, and most importantly, the applicability and fears of side effects from person to person in a society33,34. Moreover, a delay in infection, which represents the incubation period of the disease, along with the recovery delays35–38. Further, the limitations expressed by authors in31, establishing the global stability of the endemic equilibrium, are addressed here. The popular notion of estimation of the basic reproduction number for a disease environment is also explored for this model. Local stability of an equilibrium depends on the initial conditions of disease spread and eventually determines whether the same state of disease is maintained or not, whereas global stability of an equilibrium decides the status of the disease, whether under control or not, irrespective of its initial capacity39. Thus, the study of global stability is important, which is highlighted here. At the same time, the local stability and basic reproduction number clearly distinguish between the conditions on the parameters for a spread or disease-free environment.

To study the dynamics of the proposed model in terms of Caputo fractional derivative, we begin by examining the influence of key parameters and time delays on its qualitative behavior. A detailed formulation of the model, along with its underlying assumptions and constraints, is presented in Section 2. Section 3 focuses on establishing the fundamental analytical properties of the system, including the positivity, boundedness, existence, and uniqueness of solutions. Additionally, the basic reproduction number is derived, and both local and global stability of the equilibria are analyzed in terms of this threshold parameter. The global stability results are rigorously obtained through the construction of suitable Lyapunov functionals. In Section 4, sensitivity analysis is performed to identify the most influential parameters affecting the transmission dynamics. The corresponding numerical simulation plots have been included in each section to validate the theoretical results and demonstrate the model’s behavior under various parameter settings. Finally, Section 5 concludes the study and outlines potential directions for future research.

Description of the model

In this study, we develop a fractional-order epidemiological model to investigate the dynamics of a contagious disease within a closed population over a specified temporal domain. The modeling framework extends the classical Susceptible-Infected-Recovered compartmental functional structure, in which the total population is divided into three disjoint classes named susceptible individuals x(t), infected individuals y(t), and recovered individuals z(t). The proposed fractional-order model not only generalizes the classical integer-order SIR model, but also includes nonlinear functional terms to represent vaccination strategies, infection mechanisms, and recovery processes through medical treatment. Local stability analysis

In this subsection, we investigate the local stability properties of the proposed fractional-order epidemiological model formulated using the Caputo derivative. The incorporation of the Caputo fractional operator introduces environmental effects into the system, thereby allowing the model to capture long-term dependencies and hereditary characteristics inherent in real-world disease dynamics48. Unlike classical integer-order systems, fractional-order models reflect the fact that the future evolution of the epidemic is influenced not only by its current state but also by the entire history of the population’s exposure, infection, and recovery processes. Local stability analysis is crucial for understanding the behavior of the system in the vicinity of its equilibrium points, particularly the disease-free equilibrium and endemic equilibrium45,49. Due to the fractional nature of the model, the stability criteria differ from their classical counterparts. Specifically, the eigenvalues of the Jacobian matrix must satisfy conditions determined by the argument of the eigenvalues and the order of the fractional derivative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}. The analysis presented herein provides a foundational step for understanding the qualitative behavior of the system and evaluating the effectiveness of potential control strategies under environmental-influenced epidemic progression.

Remark 1

The basic reproduction number, denoted by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}_0$$\end{document}, serves as a fundamental threshold parameter in epidemiological modeling, representing the average number of secondary infections generated by a single infectious individual in a fully susceptible population. The stability analysis presented in the preceding theorems and Figs. 1(a)–(d) reveals how the qualitative behavior of the disease dynamics is governed by the value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}_0$$\end{document}, particularly within the framework of fractional-order differential equations that incorporate environmental effects through the Caputo derivative \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${}^C D^\alpha$$\end{document}. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}_0 < 1$$\end{document}, the infection cannot sustain itself in the population each infected individual leads to less than one new case on average. Consequently, the disease tends to die out, and the system asymptotically approaches the disease-free equilibrium. However, due to the non-local nature of the Caputo derivative, which encapsulates the historical behavior of the system, the decline of the infectious population follows a slower, power-law decay rather than an exponential one. This behavior reflects the sub-diffusive dynamics commonly observed in real-world epidemic processes. In contrast, when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}_0 > 1$$\end{document}, the infection has the potential to spread, as each infectious individual produces more than one secondary case on average. In such scenarios, the disease-free equilibrium becomes unstable, and the system may evolve toward an endemic state. The incorporation of environmental effects in the model introduces temporal delays such as those arising from delayed immune responses or behavioral adaptations, which can decelerate the epidemic spread but do not prevent it. Instead, the fractional-order dynamics modulate the pace of epidemic progression while preserving the threshold nature of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}_0$$\end{document} observed in classical models50. The unusually large peak observed in the infected population arises primarily from the dominance of the nonlinear infection parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_1$$\end{document}, together with insufficient damping effects and the influence of numerical discretization. In particular, larger values of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_1$$\end{document} significantly amplify the infection growth term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$b_1 f(x(t-\tau ), y(t))$$\end{document}, which can dominate the removal terms \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r p(y(t)) + d_1 y(t) + \beta (y(t))$$\end{document}. This imbalance results in a rapid increase in the infected population at the discrete level. This behavior is further intensified when combined with relatively large time step sizes, smaller fractional orders \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha \in (0,1)$$\end{document}, and the presence of delay \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tau$$\end{document}, all of which contribute to increased sensitivity and stiffness of the system. Consequently, the numerical scheme may generate artificial spikes due to instability, rather than representing true dynamical behavior. It is important to emphasize that the numerical scheme remains reliable for sufficiently small time step sizes and moderate parameter values. All principal conclusions of this study are therefore drawn within such stable regimes, where the numerical results are consistent with the theoretical analysis. The anomalous behavior observed in Figs. 1(c)(d) is included to illustrate the limitations of the explicit discretization, rather than to indicate genuine epidemic dynamics.

To address these numerical instabilities, more robust approaches, such as implicit fractional schemes or predictor-corrector methods of Adams-Bashforth-Moulton type, may be employed, particularly for stiff fractional delay systems. However, these methods involve higher computational complexity and are beyond the scope of the present work. Numerical simulations under varying initial conditions further support these theoretical findings, demonstrating how both the value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}_0$$\end{document} and the inclusion of environmental effects jointly influence the transient and asymptotic behavior of disease transmission.

Global stability analysis

Building upon the local stability results, the present subsection focuses on the global stability properties of the fractional-order epidemic model formulated with the Caputo derivative. Global stability analysis aims to characterize the long-term behavior of the system for any admissible initial condition within the biologically feasible region, thereby providing comprehensive insights into the overall disease dynamics beyond local neighborhoods of equilibrium points51. The presence of environmental effects, inherently captured by the Caputo fractional derivative, complicates the analysis since the system’s future state depends on its entire past trajectory rather than solely on its instantaneous configuration34,52. This nonlocality necessitates the use of advanced mathematical tools such as suitable Lyapunov functionals tailored for fractional-order systems or fractional comparison principles, which generalize classical stability concepts to accommodate the fractional dynamics53,54. The methods and results presented herein provide rigorous criteria under which the epidemic model exhibits global asymptotic stability, thus offering valuable guidance for effective disease control strategies under the influence of environmental-dependent transmission and recovery processes.

Role of the fractional order \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} and its epidemiological interpretation

The fractional order \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha \in (0,1]$$\end{document} plays a fundamental role in characterizing memory-dependent epidemic evolution. In the classical integer-order formulation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha =1$$\end{document}), the rate of change of each compartment depends only on the present state of the system. However, many infectious disease processes exhibit long-term memory effects due to delayed immune response, cumulative exposure, behavioral adaptation, environmental persistence, and treatment history. Fractional derivatives provide a natural mathematical framework to incorporate such hereditary effects into the model. When \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha <1$$\end{document}, the Caputo fractional derivative introduces a fading-memory kernel, meaning that the current dynamics depend on the entire past history of the system with decreasing influence from older states. This property modifies the transient dynamics compared to the integer-order case and typically results in slower epidemic evolution and delayed convergence toward equilibrium. In particular, fractional dynamics can generate long-tail persistence of infection, which cannot be reproduced by the corresponding integer-order model.

Remark 3

The numerical simulations demonstrate that the fractional order \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} fundamentally governs the memory intensity embedded in the Caputo derivative and, consequently, the transient dynamics of the epidemic system. In this case, the susceptible population decreases rapidly, the infected class exhibits a sharp and early peak, and the system converges quickly to its equilibrium state. As \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} decreases from 1 to 0.8, the susceptible population declines more gradually, the infected population attains a lower and delayed peak with a flatter profile, and the recovered population increases more smoothly over an extended time interval. Although the qualitative stability of the equilibrium remains unchanged for different fractional orders, the convergence rate transitions from exponential (when \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha = 1$$\end{document}) to sub-exponential for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha < 1$$\end{document}. Therefore, the fractional-order model provides a more realistic epidemiological framework by incorporating behavioral inertia, biological immune memory, and delayed transmission responses that cannot be captured by the classical integer-order system.

Conclusion and future scope

In this study, we analyzed a fractional-order epidemic model incorporating Caputo derivatives and time delays to effectively capture environmental-dependent dynamics and latency inherent in infectious disease spread61,62. The inclusion of time delays reflects realistic biological processes such as incubation and delayed intervention effects, while fractional derivatives model the hereditary nature of disease progression more accurately than classical integer-order approaches. The model also integrates critical epidemiological factors, including vaccination, treatment, and abrupt environmental changes, enhancing its alignment with real-world complexities63. We established the existence, uniqueness, and boundedness of solutions, ensuring the model’s mathematical rigor and biological relevance. A key contribution is the derivation of the basic reproduction number \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}_0$$\end{document}, serving as a threshold parameter that governs disease persistence or elimination. Stability analyses of the disease-free and endemic equilibria were performed based on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}_0$$\end{document}, providing insights into long-term disease behavior. Complementary sensitivity analysis identified parameters with the greatest impact on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {R}_0$$\end{document} and epidemic outcomes, offering valuable guidance for targeted interventions. Numerical simulations verified the theoretical results and demonstrated the practical implications of the model in epidemic prediction and control64. This work underscores the utility of fractional-order modeling for capturing complex interactions between environmental effects, delays, and nonlinear transmission, thereby advancing the design of effective disease control strategies.

Looking ahead, future work will focus on rigorous parameter estimation and validation through data-driven calibration. Both integer-order and fractional-order models will be fitted to the same real epidemiological dataset, and their performance will be quantitatively compared using metrics such as the Mean Square Relative Error (MSRE) and Akaike Information Criterion (AIC)65,66. The integration of real-time data will further enhance predictive accuracy and practical relevance67. Additionally, developing discrete-time analogues that preserve key properties such as positivity, boundedness, and stability remains an important direction. This combined data-driven and structure-preserving approach will strengthen the applicability of epidemic modeling.
