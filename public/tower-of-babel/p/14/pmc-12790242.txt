3-Dimensional Wiring Allows for ARCADIA Technique Followed by Rotational Atherectomy for Calcified Nodules
Authors: Kiwamu Sudo, Masakazu Yasuda, Tomohiro Yamasaki, Kota Tanaka, Satoshi Watanabe, Mutsumi Iwamoto, Nobuaki Tanaka, Yoshitaka Iwanaga, Heitaro Watanabe, Atsunori Okamura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aggressive wire recanalization in calcified atheroma and dilation technique is performed to treat eccentric calcified lesions in the peripheral arteries.Case SummaryWe present a clinical case in which this technique was successfully applied to treat an eccentric calcified nodule (CN) in the right coronary artery.DiscussionCN is an independent predictor of poor clinical outcomes after percutaneous coronary intervention. Our experience suggests that this technique may be an effective treatment for CNs.Take-Home MessagesCNs in the coronary artery remain an issue for percutaneous coronary intervention, and insufficient lesion modification of the CN is associated with unfavorable results. The aggressive wire recanalization in calcified atheroma and dilation technique is an effective option to treat CNs in terms of intentionally creating brand-new guidewire bias.Key Words: aggressive wire recanalization in calcified atheroma and dilation, angiography-based 3-dimensional wiring, eccentric calcified nodular lesions, rotational atherectomy

Calcified nodules (CNs) are challenging lesions to manage using percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), and they are associated with poorer clinical outcomes after PCI.1 CNs are also a known cause of acute coronary syndrome.2 In recent years, several reports have described the effectiveness of modification techniques for CNs, such as rotational atherectomy (RA), orbital atherectomy system (OAS), intravascular lithotripsy, and directional coronary atherectomy. However, mid- and long-term results after PCI for CNs using debulking devices are insufficient.3, 4, 5

The aggressive wire recanalization in calcified atheroma and dilation (ARCADIA) technique has been reported for the treatment of eccentric severe calcified lesions in peripheral artery disease.6 In this technique, a guidewire is advanced into and crossed through the calcification and returned to the distal residual lumen. The lesion is expanded from the inside of the calcified plaque with balloon catheters, which may provide concentric lumen expansion. As the effects of debulking techniques, such as RA and OAS, depend on the bias of the guidewire, optimal plaque modification with sufficient debulking for CN lesions is less certain. The idea of intentionally creating the new route using a guidewire is efficient for reducing the volume of calcification by using debulking devices.

The ARCADIA technique for calcified lesions in the coronary artery was performed in a limited number of cases in Japan more than 10 years ago. However, its widespread adoption has been limited because of the difficulty of controlling guidewires within calcified lesions and the associated risk of coronary perforation. In recent years, the technological development of high-penetration-efficacy guidewires,7 angiography-based 3-dimensional wiring (3D wiring),8 and intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) using the tip-detection method9 have enabled us to more accurately and safely control guidewires even during calcification.

Herein, we report the first successful treatment of a restenotic and eccentric CN lesion using the ARCADIA technique, followed by RA.

History of Presentation

A 77-year-old woman with a history of angina pectoris, sick sinus syndrome, and type 2 diabetes mellitus presented to the emergency department with the primary symptom of worsening chest pain. We performed coronary angiography to diagnose acute coronary syndrome.

Past Medical History

The patient had received first PCI in the left anterior descending coronary artery 8 years previously and second PCI in the right coronary artery (RCA) by RA using a 1.75-mm Rotablator (Boston Scientific) 7 years previously. She received additional PCI for in-stent restenosis in the RCA 3 years previously. The patient also underwent pacemaker implantation for sick sinus syndrome.

Differential Diagnosis

Heart failure, pulmonary embolization, aortic dissection, and pneumothorax were considered as differential diagnoses.

Investigations

The electrocardiogram demonstrated ST-segment depression in leads II, III, aVF, V4, V5, and V6. The levels of myocardial deviation enzymes, including creatine kinase, creatine kinase-myocardial band, and troponin T, were not significantly elevated. Echocardiography revealed normal left ventricular function.

Management

Coronary angiography revealed severe stenosis with delayed flow in the mid-RCA (Figures 1A and 1B). IVUS demonstrated the eccentric CN in the most stenotic segment (Figure 1C). After predilation using a 2.75-mm scoring balloon (Figure 1D), the coronary flow was improved to the thrombolysis in myocardial infarction grade 3. However, luminal gain remained insufficient on both IVUS and angiography (Figures 1E and 1F), and suboptimal lesion preparation is related to stent underexpansion. Although debulking devices are necessary to reduce the eccentric CN and optimize lesion preparation, their use on unstable plaques and thrombus can lead to slow flow. To minimize the risk of complications, we decided to perform additional procedures the following day.

We performed an additional procedure for the RCA 1 week after the first procedure. An 8-F Amplatz left-type guiding catheter was engaged in the RCA, and a workhorse guidewire (SION blue, ASAHI INTECC) was inserted into the residual lumen. An eccentric CN was located on the ventricular side according to angiography and IVUS, and wire bias was not suitable for RA or OAS (Figure 2). Moreover, the patient was already treated with RA using a 1.75-mm Rotablator (Boston Scientific) several years previously. It is possible that directional coronary atherectomy reduces the volume of the CN; however, the catheter is bulky and sometimes difficult to insert into severe stenosis with calcification.

We attempted to puncture and insert another guidewire into the CN and expand the lesion from inside the CN. The proximal end of the CN that started from the distal edge of the previous stent was clearly visualized using IVUS from the residual lumen, and the Conquest Pro 12 Sharpened Tip (CP12ST, ASAHI INTECC) supported by a microcatheter could enter the CN with real-time IVUS guidance using the tip-detection method (Figure 3). We intentionally controlled the CP12ST using 3D wiring while referring to projections from 2 perpendicular directions (Figures 3A and 3B). CP12ST was in intraplaque on IVUS immediately after the CN (Figures 4A and 4B), but it could not re-enter the residual lumen from the CN. The location of CP12ST was close to the residual lumen, but the radiopaque part of CP12ST was separated because the component of the core wire was damaged by severe resistance from the lesion (Figure 4C). The CP12ST was pulled out without complete separation to cover the radiopaque part using the microcatheter as much as possible.

We changed the guidewire from CP12ST to Gaia Next 4 (GN4, ASAHI INTECC) to repuncture the CN using real-time IVUS guidance. GN4 ended up in the CN through a route similar to that of CP12ST and finally re-entered the residual lumen through the CN using 3D wiring (Figure 5). IVUS after predilating 1.5-mm balloons revealed that the new hole made by GN4 was at the center of the CN (Figure 6A). After RA using a 1.75-mm Rotablator and additional dilation with a 3.25-mm scoring balloon, optimal concentric expansion and acute lumen gain were confirmed on IVUS images (Figure 6). A 3.5-mm everolimus-eluting stent was implanted, and the final angiography showed sufficient coronary flow without any complications (Figure 6).

Follow-Up

The patient had not experienced any chest pain after PCI at 3-month follow-up.

Discussion

CNs are independent predictors of stent underexpansion and unfavorable results after PCI. To obtain optimal lesion modification, we can use limited options, such as RA, OAS, intravascular lithotripsy, and directional coronary atherectomy. The effects of RA and OAS are uncertain because the effect of debulking depends on guidewire bias. Intravascular lithotripsy is a safe and effective tool for CN,4 but the eccentricity of the lesion affects the results of intravascular lithotripsy modification.

Konishi et al6 were the first to report the ARCADIA technique for peripheral lesions. In terms of creating another route using another guidewire, the ARCADIA technique is sometimes effective for plaque modification, particularly eccentric calcification in coronary arteries. Puncturing the calcification vertically is sometimes difficult, even when CP12ST is used, which is one of the guidewires with the highest penetration efficacy that is used in Japan. Real-time IVUS guidance using the tip detection method7 is useful for puncturing the ideal position and less calcified area of the CN during this procedure. In addition, the ARCADIA technique has a potential risk of coronary perforation and dissection; however, the IVUS-guided technique, which confirms the tip of the stiff wire from the remaining lumen side, can avoid complications, such as coronary perforation and dissection.

After successfully puncturing the CN, advancing the guidewire from within the CN to the residual lumen using IVUS was sometimes difficult because we could not see the guidewire position inside the CN on IVUS due to the acoustic shadow on the IVUS image. 3D wiring using the calcification and the guidewire in the residual lumen as landmarks is effective for returning the guidewire from the inside of the CN to the residual lumen when the guidewire position inside the CN cannot be seen.8,10 If the guidewire position is visible in a less calcified area on IVUS, then IVUS guidance using the tip detection method9 is helpful for returning the guidewire from the inside of the CN to the residual lumen.

Debulking and modification techniques, such as RA, OAS, intravascular lithotripsy, and directional coronary atherectomy, are required to modify the CN after the guidewire crosses the inside of the CN. Confirming the position of the guidewire inside the CN is an important step in avoiding vessel injury. If the guidewire is close to the opposite edge of the CN, then RA and OAS cause dissection and vessel perforation due to unfavorable guidewire bias.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of the treatment of CN of the coronary artery using the ARCADIA technique. Further studies investigating the mid- and long-term outcomes of this treatment are warranted.

Conclusions

The ARCADIA technique is an additional option for treating an eccentric CN with unfavorable guidewire bias in the residual lumen. In terms of creating another route using the guidewire and brand-new guidewire bias, this technique can be effective in treating eccentric lesions, including CNs. To accurately penetrate CNs in coronary arteries, real-time IVUS-guided wiring using the tip-detection method and 3D wiring are effective.
