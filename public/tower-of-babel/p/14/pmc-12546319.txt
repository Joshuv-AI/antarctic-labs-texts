A novel antibacterial approach: targeting methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus with carvone nanoemulgel
Authors: Wafaa E. Soliman, Nancy S. Younis, Shaimaa K. Mostafa, Maged E. Mohamed, Soha M. El-Masry, Mounir M. Bekhit, Alanood S. Algarni, Fakhria A. Al‑Joufi, Heba A. Ramadan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMethicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) remains a challenging issue in the treatment of diabetic wounds. The current researchinvestigates the formulation, antimicrobial, and wound healing efficacy of D-carvone-based nanoemulgel against clinical MRSA strains collected from diabetic wound infections. Nanoemulsion and nanoemulgel preparations for carvone were prepared and assessed. The antibacterial activity was assessed using both the cup method and microtiter assay, in addition to electron microscopy and molecular techniques. An excision wound model was used to evaluate the diabetic wound healing activity of NPs carvone. Diabetic rats, induced via streptozotocin (STZ, 65 mg/kg, i.p.), were separated into three groups: diabetic negative control, carvone, and NPs carvone groups, in which diabetic rats were allowed to heal spontaneously or treated by applying carvone alone or NPs carvone ointment, respectively. NPs D-carvone nanoemulgel demonstrated favorable characteristics suggesting enhanced tissue penetration; therefore, it was selected for further assessment. The antibacterial activity of D-carvone oil exhibited weak activity against all MRSA strains except for the AH-7 strain. On the other hand, both nanoemulsion and nanoemulgel formulations showed improved antibacterial effects, with the highest effect detected in strain AS-8 and the lowest in NCI-5, with MIC values ≤ 0.25 µg/ml for all strains. SEM images showed morphological changes in MRSA strains after treatment with NPs carvone. NPs carvone exhibited higher wound contraction %, faster epithelialization, and instigated collagen deposition, demonstrating enhanced healing processes. Additionally, NPs carvone intensified angiogenesis-related factors and antioxidant enzymes and reduced lipid peroxidation and inflammatory mediators. D-carvone nanoemulgel formulations exhibit effective antimicrobial activity against MRSA strains derived from diabetic wound infections.Key points• D-carvone NP formulations exhibit effective antimicrobial activity against MRSA.• Nanoencapsulation improved efficacy and stability of D-carvone.• D-carvone nanoemulgels demonstrated superior diabetic wounds healing ability.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00253-025-13623-x.

Introduction

Diabetic infections present a significant clinical problem, with delayed healing, increased morbidity, and increased healthcare costs. Among the complicating factors in managing such infections is the intensifying occurrence of MRSA, a resistant organism well known to be antibiotic-resistant with the capability of producing biofilms that evade host defense (Craft et al. 2019). MRSA remains a challenging issue in the treatment of diabetic wounds, resulting in chronic infection, delayed healing, and increased risk of systemic complications (Al-Joufi et al. 2020). The chronic nature of diabetic wounds, along with impaired immune function and poor tissue perfusion, creates an optimal environment for the proliferation of MRSA, and this typically ends in severe outcomes such as amputations or systemic infection (Buch et al. 2019). With increasing antimicrobial resistance undermining traditional therapeutic choices, there is an urgent need for innovative, effective, and safe alternatives to address clinical MRSA strains and promote wound healing in such a high-risk population.

As the limitations of traditional antibiotics arise, researchers explore alternative therapeutic agents, including essential oils (EOs) derived from plants. One of the promising agents is dill, a member of the Apiaceae family, which demonstrates multifaceted roles including antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties (Yazdanparast and Bahramikia 2008). Dill essential oil has shown significant antimicrobial efficacy against numerous bacterial and fungal pathogens, with key active compounds including γ-Terpinene, p-Cymene, pinene, cuminaldehyde, limonene, and especially carvone (Al Masoody et al. 2023). Carvone, a major essential oil component, contributes to its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticonvulsant properties and has shown potential antibacterial effects (Bhavaniramya et al. 2019). These properties are, however, restricted in their therapeutic applications by low solubility, stability, and bioavailability. To overcome these constraints, nanoemulsion-based drug delivery systems have emerged as potent delivery vehicles for enhancing the efficiency of bioactive molecules (Preeti et al. 2023). Nanoemulgels, being the combination of the advantages of nanoemulsions and hydrogels, offer superior drug penetration, sustained release, and enhanced stability and therefore are the best possible candidates for topical delivery (Farooqui and Sharma 2024). Topical drug delivery systems (TDDS), including nanoemulsions (NEs) and nanoemulgels (NEG), facilitate targeted delivery of active compounds directly to affected tissues. These systems enhance drug solubility, stability, and bioavailability while minimizing systemic exposure and side effects (Bujubarah et al. 2023; Elsewedy et al. 2022). The aim of the current research is to investigate the formulation and antimicrobial efficacy of encapsulated D-carvone nanoemulgel against clinical MRSA strains isolated from diabetic wound infections. By integrating nanotechnology with natural antimicrobial agents, the study seeks to create a healing-friendly environment that supports wound recovery and presents a promising alternative to conventional antibiotics for diabetic wound management.

Nanoemulsion preparation

A spontaneous emulsification method was employed at room temperature to create a nanoemulsion of D-carvone, as previously detailed in Azadi et al. (2023). The oil phase was initially prepared by combining 100 µL of D-carvone with 100–250 µL Tween 80 using a magnetic stirrer (2000 rpm, 5 min). De-ionized water was gradually added until the final volume (5 mL) was reached, then stirred for 40 min (2000 rpm). The prepared nanoemulsion’s particle size (PS), polydispersity Index (PDI), and zeta potential (ZP) were measured using a dynamic light scattering (Zeta Sizer Nano-series, Worcestershire, UK).

Nanoemulgel preparation

The D-carvone nanoemulgel was developed by blending the nanoemulsion with 3% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC-Na) powder, as a cross-linking agent, and mixed until a uniform gel consistency was attained. The mixture was left undisturbed for 24 h to facilitate adequate hydration and gel formation.

Physical appearance

Nanoemulgel formulation was observed visually for its color, homogeneity, consistency, and phase separation.

pH determination

One gram of gel was weighed and mixed with 10 ml of water; then the pH was assessed via a digital pH meter (Thermo Scientific, USA).

Spreadability

D-carvone nanoemulgel spreadability was assessed using the horizontal plate method (Dantas et al. 2016).

Viscosity

The viscosity was defined via the viscometer (Brookfield spindle no. 32) at 32 ± 2 °C and 8–200 rpm.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

D-carvone, Tween 80, CMC-Na, nanoemulsion, and nanoemulgel were examined via FTIR (Agilent Technologies Cary 630). To prepare the samples, the compressed disc method was used, blending a small amount of each sample with potassium bromide (KBr) and then compressing it with a hydraulic press. The discs created were scanned across a spectral range of 400–4000 cm⁻1, and their respective peaks were identified.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

D-carvone, Tween 80, Blank Gel, nanoemulsion, and nanoemulgel thermal behaviors were identified using Discovery DSC 25, TA instruments, USA. The analysis was performed at 20 °C/min, from 50 to 400 °C, using inert nitrogen as a purging drying gas. For the analysis, the samples weighed ranged from 3 to 5 mg, placed in aluminium pans that were sealed tightly, then heated from 30 to 280 °C at a steady rate of 10 °C per minute before cooling them back down to 25 °C while maintaining a nitrogen purge at 40 mL per minute. Afterwards, the resulting thermograms were analyzed and compared with those of the drug in physical mixtures to check for any potential thermal interactions.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

To analyze the nanoemulsion and nanoemulgel using TEM, the samples were first pipetted onto a carbon-coated grid and allowed to adsorb. Excess liquid was carefully removed, and the grid was placed in a TEM holder for imaging.

Culturing of the microorganisms

This study utilized various clinical strains of Staphylococcus aureus previously isolated from diabetic foot ulcer patients, following the methodology of a prior investigation conducted among diabetic patients at five tertiary care teaching hospitals in Cairo, Egypt (Al-Joufi et al. 2020). S. aureus (ATCC 29213) was used as a Gram-positive control strain. All microbial strains were in freeze-dried form and refreshed using brain heart infusion broth (Oxoid, Hampshire, UK). To maintain the viability of the microorganisms, MRSA strains were plated onto sheep blood (5%) and mannitol salt agar (Oxoid, Hampshire, UK) and incubated overnight (36 ± 1 °C). Phenotypic characterization at the species level was confirmed using the API system (Biomerieux, France) of S. aureus.

MRSA detection

MRSA detection was performed as mentioned in the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (Howe and Andrews 2012) through the oxacillin agar screen test. The inoculated plates were incubated at 35 °C for 48 h; MRSA-positive and -negative standard reference strains were performed as controls in parallel. One or more colonies in each inoculated spot were considered a positive result. Where the results were equivocal, confirmation was attempted by determining the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of oxacillin by the agar dilution method. All the isolates were also confirmed for the presence of the methicillin-resistant S. aureus (mecA), β-lactam-resistant strains (blaZ), erythromycin-resistant isolates (ermA), and tetracycline-resistant strains (tetA) genes by polymerase chain reaction (PCR).

DNA extraction

All the S. aureus isolates were cultured on mannitol salt agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. One bacterial colony was transferred from each plate and suspended in 200 µL of deionized distilled water. According to the manufacturer’s guidelines, genomic DNA was isolated using the QIAamp Mini DNA Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany).

PCR amplification

PCR for detecting the mecA gene was carried out on six phenotypically oxacillin-resistant S. aureus isolates. The reaction mixture for the PCR amplification (total volume of 20 µL) was made up as mentioned before (Herold et al. 1998). The PCR products (10 µL) were electrophoresed on 2% agarose gels in 1X TAE buffer (40 mM Tris–HCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1.14 mL glacial acetic acid, pH 7.8) at 70–100 V.

Further characterization of the isolates was achieved through PCR amplification of several virulence and regulatory genes, including blaZ, ermA, and tetA. The resulting amplicons were separated on 1.7% agarose gel with 0.5 µg/mL ethidium bromide in 0.5X Tris–EDTA buffer at 5 V/cm for 3 h and then observed under UV illumination. Data on primer pairs and thermal cycling conditions employed in the PCR assays are presented in Supplementary 1.

Antimicrobial studies

Using the cup diffusion agar method, the antibacterial activity of carvone NPs was compared with carvone oil against clinical MRSA isolates and S. aureus (ATCC 29213) as a control strain. The method procedures were performed according to the CLSI guidelines (Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute 2020). The samples were tested for antibacterial activity against six clinical bacterial strains, which were cultivated in Cation-Adjusted Mueller–Hinton (CAMH) broth (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) according to the CLSI guidelines. After incubation, the diameters of the inhibition zones were measured in millimeters to ascertain the antibacterial action of the formulations (Howe and Andrews 2012). The test was performed in triplicate, and the inhibition zone diameter mean was calculated and recorded. The experiments were conducted in triplicate, and results are represented as mean value ± standard deviation (SD).

MIC determination

MIC was determined for active formulations against antimicrobial activity, which was established by the cup plate method. For each experiment, a single colony of each bacterial strain was transferred into 7 mL of CAMH broth and grown overnight in a shaking incubator. The bacterial cultures were adjusted to a bacterial concentration of 106 CFU/mL. A double-fold serial dilution of carvone nanoparticles (NPs) was prepared, starting at a concentration of 40 µg/mL. For carvone oil, the starting concentration was 8000 µg/mL when testing S. aureus. Then, 50 µL of the diluted bacterial culture was added to each well of a 96-well plate, except for the negative control wells. Each treatment was repeated three times on the plate. After incubating the plates at 36 ± 1 °C for 24 h, 20 µL of a 0.015% resazurin solution was added to each well. The plates were incubated again at 36 ± 1 °C for 2–4 h, and the color change in the wells was monitored as an indicator of bacterial viability (Soliman et al. 2022).

Minimum bactericidal concentration determination

After MIC analysis, the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) was determined. Fifty microliters of liquid were taken from every tube, and no bacterial growth or change in optical density was observed at 600 nm. The aliquots were spread on nutrient agar plates and incubated at 36 ± 1 °C for 24 h. The MBC was the lowest concentration of the antimicrobial agent that resulted in the death of 99.9% of the population of bacteria (Soliman et al. 2022). The MBC was the lowest concentration of the test formulation, resulting in no growth on the agar plates, indicating bactericidal activity (≥ 99.9% reduction in viable cells). Experiments were conducted in triplicate, and results were recorded as the mean of the replicates.

SEM analysis of carvone NP-treated bacterial morphology

To confirm the antibacterial effect of carvone NPs, the changes in bacterial morphology before and after treatment were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Briefly, a mixture containing 100 µg of NPs carvone, 100 µL of Mueller–Hinton broth, and 10 µL of bacterial suspension (approximately 1.5 × 106 CFU/mL) was prepared and incubated for 1 h. After incubation, the mixture was centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 20 min to separate the supernatant, leaving the bacterial pellets. These pellets were then resuspended in normal saline. Approximately 50 µL of the suspension was spread onto a slide and allowed to dry. The sample was fixed using 3% glutaraldehyde for 3 h and then examined under a scanning electron microscope to detect any morphological changes. For comparison, untreated bacteria were also prepared and examined under the SEM as a control. This process helped visually assess NPs carvone’s influence on bacterial structure (Elsewedy et al. 2023).

Network pharmacology

Carvone’s pharmacokinetics, drug-likeness, and physicochemical properties were obtained from the Swiss Target website. Carvone’s probable targets were collected using Swiss Target Prediction and Pharmmapper. Wound healing targets’ possible genes were attained from GeneCards and OMIM using the phrase, “Wound healing.” Carvone and wound healing targets were composed and proved via UniProt. Carvone and wound healing targets were instituted in a Venn Diagram to obtain the shared genes, which were pasted into the STRING database to inspect the protein–protein interaction (PPI). Cytoscape 3.7.2 is utilized for PPI network visualization, specifying the “Degree” filter to assess the interaction intensity. The intersected targets were introduced into Metascape to analyze GO bioprocess and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment.

In vivo studies

Animal’s ethical approval

Male Wistar rats (200 ± 20 g, 6–8 weeks) were maintained in the animal house and fed laboratory chow and water ad libitum. All experiments were appropriately executed per the “Ethical Conduct for Use of Animals in Research” Guidelines at King Faisal University with an ethical approval no. (KFU-REC-2024-MAY-ETHICS2318).

Induction of diabetes

Diabetes was induced in overnight-fasted rats through an intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of streptozotocin (STZ) at a dose of 65 mg/kg. The STZ, freshly prepared in citrate buffer (pH 4.5) and obtained from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA), was administered to the rats. To prevent severe hypoglycemia caused by the sudden surge in insulin release, a 10% glucose solution was provided 6 h post-injection and continued for the following 24 h (Prasad et al. 2010; Younis et al. 2022).

Excision wound establishment

Anesthetized rats were positioned prone on a dissection pad. A sterile scalpel was used to create a circular, open wound on the back of each rat, measuring approximately 10 mm2 in diameter and 0.2 mm in depth. The animals were then randomly assigned to various groups, as outlined in the experimental design.

Experimental design

Diabetic rats were arbitrarily separated into three groups (n = 6). Group I: diabetic negative control, in which diabetic animals were allowed to heal spontaneously without any treatment. Group II: diabetic + carvone, in which diabetic rats were treated by applying carvone-alone ointment. Group III: diabetic + NPs carvone, in which diabetic rats were managed with the topical application of NPs carvone on wounds once a day. After the complete wound healing, the animals were sacrificed using xylazine (10 mg/kg) and ketamine (25 mg/kg), and blood and wound tissue samples were collected. Blood samples were centrifuged (3000 × g for 15 min), and serum was preserved.

Macroscopic investigation

Wound photographs were captured on days 0, 3, 7, and 14 using a digital camera, and the epithelialization period was recorded (Ren et al. 2019). The wound area was assessed on days 3, 7, and 14 post-excision using tracing paper placed directly over the wound. The % of wound contraction was determined as previously described (Kant et al. 2015):

Wound contraction (%) = [(Wound area on day 0 – Wound area on day n)/Wound area on day 0] × 100, where n represents the number of days until complete healing.

Skin tissue–measured parameters

The hydroxyproline, collagen, and hexosamine were measured within wound tissues as mentioned earlier (Elson and Morgan 1933; Reddy and Enwemeka 1996).

Measurement of angiogenesis-related factors

Angiogenesis-related factors, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF, Cat No. LS‐F5482), transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF‐β1, Cat No. LS‐F24972), and hypoxia‐inducible factor 1‐alpha (HIF‐1α, Cat No. LS‐F4225), were assessed in the tissue homogenates using ELISA-based kits that were obtained from LifeSpan Bio‐Sciences, Seattle, WA, USA.

Measurement of lipid peroxidation and antioxidant enzyme activities

Malondialdehyde (MDA, Cat. No. ab 118,790), glutathione content (GSH, Cat. No. 703002), glutathione peroxidase (GPx, Cat. No. 703102), superoxide dismutase (SOD, Cat. No. 706002), and catalase (CAT, Cat. No. 707002) ELISA kits were purchased from Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA, and used.

Measurement of the inflammatory cytokine

Inflammatory cytokines, including Tumor Necrosis Factor‐alpha (TNF‐α, Cat. No. LS‐F23150), Interleukin 8 (IL‐8, Cat. No. LS‐F9753), and Interleukin 1β levels, are ELISA-based kits that were obtained from LifeSpan Bio‐Sciences, Seattle, WA, USA.

Statistical analysis

Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical comparisons among groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software, version 8 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

FTIR analysis

D-carvone, Tween 80, CMC-Na, nanoemulsion, and nanoemulgel underwent FTIR analysis (Fig. 1B). The FTIR spectrum of pure D-carvone exhibited characteristic peaks corresponding to various functional groups, such as C = O stretching (around 1700 cm⁻1), C-H stretching (around 2900–3000 cm⁻1), and perhaps some characteristic peaks associated with the cyclic structure of oil. Tween 80 showed C-H stretching vibrations (around 2850–3000 cm⁻1), C-O stretching vibrations (around 1000–1300 cm⁻1), and potentially some peaks related to the ester linkage present in the molecule. D-carvone nanoemulsion typically exhibits distinct peaks in its FTIR spectra corresponding to various chemical bonds and functional groups as C-H stretching vibrations (around 2850–3000 cm⁻1), C = O stretching vibrations (around 1700–1750 cm⁻1 for ester or carbonyl groups), in addition to potential peaks associated with D-carvone. The FTIR spectrum of CMC-Na exhibited key bands around 2850–3300 cm⁻1 for –OH and C–H stretching vibrations. Strong absorption bands at 1602 cm⁻1 and 1423 cm⁻1 indicated asymmetric and symmetric COO⁻ stretching. Additionally, bands at 1328 cm⁻1 and 1060 cm⁻1 were assigned to –OH bending and CH–O–CH₂ stretching, respectively. D-carvone nanoemulgel typically exhibits distinct peaks in its FTIR spectrum corresponding to various chemical bonds and functional groups of its components. These peaks may include C-H stretching vibrations (around 3324 cm⁻1) and C = O stretching vibrations (around 1700–1750 cm⁻1 for carbonyl groups), along with those associated with CMC-Na. Additionally, a subtle shift or broadening observed around 1667 cm⁻1 suggests an overlap between D-carvone nanoemulsion and CMC-Na.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

DSC curves for D-carvone exhibited two endothermic peaks at approximately 55 °C and 170 °C, which may be related to the melting and evaporation of D-carvone (Fig. 1C). DSC analysis of the blank CMC-Na gel exhibits an endothermic peak around 65 °C, which corresponds to water evaporation from the gel matrix and the breaking of hydrogen bonds (de Lima et al. 2020; Souza et al. 2016). This thermal event signifies the removal of both free and bound water within the gel. The DSC analysis of the nanoemulsion demonstrated an endothermic peak at approximately 72 °C, exhibiting a greater peak size relative to pure D-carvone. This observation is likely attributable to the volatilization endotherm of D-carvone being shifted to 72 °C within the nanoemulsion matrix. The shift to a slightly higher temperature, along with an increase in peak size and intensity, suggests enhanced intermolecular interactions within the nanoemulsion. This phenomenon can be attributed to the presence of Tween 80, which is hypothesized to fortify the intermolecular interactions, thereby enhancing the stability and structural integrity of the nanoemulsion (Chiang et al. 2023). Meanwhile, the lack of a D-Carvone endothermic peak in the nanoemulgel may be a sign that the drug is trapped inside the gel.

Antibacterial study

The testing of D-carvone and its nanoparticle formulations against clinical MRSA strains revealed remarkable differences in antimicrobial activity as revealed in Table 2 and Fig. 4. Classical D-carvone oil demonstrated weak antibacterial activity, with 8 to 10 mm zones of inhibition against the strains under test. Remarkably, the measurable minimum inhibitory concentrations (MIC) were achievable only for strain AH-7 at 125 µg/ml, while other strains were resistant at tested levels, and thus, their MIC values were indeterminable. In sharp contrast, the nanoemulsion formulation of D-carvone exhibited much enhanced antimicrobial activity. Inhibition zones were much enhanced to 33–37 mm in diameter, representing greater than three-fold enhancement over control D-carvone. The highest effect was seen against the strain AS-8, which exhibited a 37 mm inhibition zone. The preparation of the nanoemulsion was found to be highly effective with MIC values of ≤ 0.25 µg/ml in all MRSA strains, indicating an increase in antibacterial activity relative to native D-carvone oil. D-carvone nanoemulgel formulations were also found to exhibit good antibacterial activity with zones of inhibition ranging from 32 to 36 mm. The nanoemulgel formulation had comparable activity to the nanoemulsion against all strains tested. Strain AS-8 was most susceptible to both nanoemulsion and nanoemulgel formulations, with inhibition zones of 37 mm and 36 mm (p < 0.05), respectively. However, strain NCI-5 exhibited slightly reduced susceptibility, with inhibition zones of 33 mm and 32 mm (p < 0.05) for nanoemulsion and nanoemulgel formulations, respectively. Nevertheless, all strains exhibited high sensitivity towards both nanoformulations, at MIC values that were always at or below 0.25 µg/ml. Scanning electron microscope (SEM) analysis

SEM images revealed extreme morphological alterations in clinical S. aureus (MRSA) strains following treatment with D-carvone nanoemulsion compared to untreated controls (Fig. 5). The surface of control S. aureus cells was smooth and intact with normal cocci morphology (Fig. 5A). S. aureus treated with D-carvone nanoemulsion (Fig. 5B) exhibited a noticeable decrease in the number of bacterial cells, along with visible changes and irregularities in their structure after treatment with D-carvone nanoemulsion compared to the control sample. Discussion

In this study, spontaneous emulsification was employed to prepare nanoemulsion because it is simple and able to produce nano-sized droplets spontaneously without high-energy input when oil is added to the aqueous phase that contains surfactants under controlled conditions (McClements 2012). As for nanoemulgel assessment, the pH level of gel formulations is crucial for skin compatibility, aiming to align with the natural pH range of human skin (6–7) to prevent skin irritation or redness (Nurman et al. 2019; Saryanti and Zulfa 2017). Findings indicated that the pH of the prepared gel was within the acceptable pH range for human skin. Spreadability in gel formulations refers to their capacity to evenly spread over the skin’s surface. A larger scatter diameter signifies greater coverage (Danimayostu et al. 2017). Optimal spreadability, typically falling within the range of 5–7 cm, ensures uniform distribution upon application (Saryanti and Zulfa 2017). The pseudoplastic or shear-thinning feature of the prepared gel could be advantageous for topical applications, facilitating easier spreading and application of the gel, and potentially improving patient adherence (Alqarni et al. 2023). According to the FTIR spectral data of D-carvone and all excipients, it has been verified that the distinctive peaks of D-carvone remain intact in the nanoemulgel formulation, indicating the absence of any interaction among the constituents of the nanoemulgel. Recent advances in nanomedicine consistently demonstrate that well-engineered nanocarriers can significantly enhance therapeutic efficacy, bioavailability, and targeting precision. Our findings are supported by several studies employing green synthesis and biopolymer-based platforms. For instance, plant-extract-mediated silver and zinc oxide nanoparticles have shown selective cytotoxicity against cancer cells, enhanced antibacterial activity, and low systemic toxicity features (Balusamy et al. 2025; Mori et al. 2025; Rani et al. 2025). Biocompatible nanocarriers constructed from chitosan, Moringa oleifera, or Nigella sativa derivatives have likewise demonstrated prolonged drug release, antioxidant effects, and anti-inflammatory efficacy across various in vivo and in vitro models (Dehnoee et al. 2023, 2024; Li et al. 2023; Nejad et al. 2024). Other investigations confirm that nanoscale manipulation, especially with hybrid or magnetically active structures, enhances cellular uptake, drug solubilization, and tissue penetration (Ma et al. 2022; Shi et al.; Zarafshan et al. 2022; Zhao et al. 2022). Moreover, polysaccharide-based and ocular nanodelivery platforms have been reported to improve pharmacokinetics and patient compliance through sustained action and targeted bio-distribution (Abdoli et al. 2020; Bai et al. 2021; Dou et al. 2021; Li et al. 2020). These comparative insights place our results within a validated framework of nanoparticle research and highlight the potential of D-carvone encapsulated nanogel to meet the current therapeutic challenges.

The enhancement in antibacterial potency observed with D-carvone nanoformulations is due to a variety of physiochemical advantages of nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems. The nanometer dimension could promote penetration of the active compound within bacteria (Lingayat et al. 2017). Encapsulation of D-carvone NPs overcomes many limitations associated with the conventional delivery of essential oils. The tremendous enhancement in surface area-to-volume ratio achieved by nanoformulation enhances the antimicrobial activity of cells (Liang Rong et al. 2012). Additionally, nanoencapsulation protects D-carvone from degradation or volatilization, hence prolonging its action in bacterial cells, as previously reported by Jayari et al. (2022). The comparable efficacy displayed by formulations suggests their therapeutic application, as nanoemulgels typically offer more stability, controlled release properties, and higher compatibility compared to liquid nanoemulsions (Mulleria et al. 2021). The potential activity against a range of clinical MRSA strains underscores the therapeutic potential of D-carvone nanoformulations in combating antibiotic-resistant infections. Conventional D-carvone was ineffective, as reflected by MIC values (Zare-Zardini et al. 2025). The action mechanisms of the antimicrobial activity of D-carvone are likely to involve disruption of cell membrane integrity in bacteria and interference with intracellular metabolic processes (Mun et al. 2014). Nanoformulation may increase the effectiveness through better delivery of the compound to the point of action. Further, the nanoscale properties of these formulations would potentially enhance certain bacterial defense systems against antibiotic-resistant pathogens (Parvin et al. 2025).

As for the in vivo study, the enhanced healing of wounds in diabetic animals treated with NPs carvone suggested great therapeutic importance for this nanoformulation. The improved efficiency of NPs carvone compared to standard carvone suggests enhanced bioavailability and tissue penetration, as has been previously reported for some nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems (Hamdan et al. 2017). The augmented collagen deposition, as evidenced by greater hydroxyproline and hexosamine content, is of noteworthy importance because maturation and collagen synthesis are compromised in diabetic ulcers in the first place (Singh et al. 2023). Nanoencapsulation would naturally protect carvone from unnecessary metabolism and release sustained action, perhaps in part explaining greater efficacy in the NPs carvone (Mazumder and Majumdar 2021). Enhanced epithelialization also means NPs carvone can increase keratinocyte proliferation and migration more effectively compared to free carvone, in agreement with more recent literature (Kumar et al. 2023). These results suggest nanoparticle formulations of carvone as an effective means for diabetic wound healing.

Decreased levels of angiogenesis-related factors in the control diabetes group align with diabetic wound profiles of impaired vascular regeneration (Ullah et al. 2025). Greater than with conventional carvone, the robust induction of these parameters post-treatment with carvone-encapsulated NPs also implies that nanoencapsulation allows for greater bioavailability and longer release of carvone in the wound. An improved delivery mechanism would enable better activation of complementary angiogenic pathways since VEGF stimulates endothelial proliferation, TGF-β1 stimulates fibroblasts, and HIF-1α regulates cell adaptation to hypoxia (Qadir et al. 2021).

The improved antioxidant profile exhibited by NPs carvone-treated animals is an important therapeutic advantage, likely resulting from increased bioavailability and targeted delivery of the bioactive compound to the injury site (Pisoschi et al. 2018). The enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity upon treatment with NPs carvone suggests the effective elimination of ROS, a most important pathophysiologic event in diabetic wound complications. The noteworthy concern regarding the considerable lowering of MDA levels indicates alleviated lipid peroxidation as well as membrane damage (Comino-Sanz et al. 2021). These findings are consistent with current research indicating that nanoformulated phytochemicals are more effective than their native counterparts in treating oxidative stress-mediated pathologies, particularly diabetic complications (Kumar et al. 2023).

Nanoparticle-encapsulated carvone maximized carvone’s anti-inflammatory action by increasing its bioavailability and precisely targeting it to inflammatory cells in the wound microenvironment (Sharma et al. 2024). The simultaneous reduction in a number of inflammatory cytokines as well as the transcription factor NF-κB suggests that NPs carvone may be having complementary anti-inflammatory actions (Sharda et al. 2024). By controlling this central inflammatory signalling node, NPs carvone can facilitate the resolution of the inflammatory stage to the proliferative stage of wound healing, overcoming a critical obstacle to effective tissue repair in diabetic conditions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study confirmed that D-carvone nanoemulgel formulations possess significant antimicrobial activity against MRSA strains isolated from diabetic wound infections. The nanoencapsulation approach enhanced both the stability and efficacy of D-carvone, resulting in superior antibacterial performance compared to its free form. These findings support the potential of D-carvone nanoemulgels as a promising and innovative therapeutic strategy for managing antibiotic-resistant infections and promoting the healing of diabetic wounds.
