Manipulation of the nucleoscaffold potentiates cellular reprogramming kinetics
Authors: Benjamin A Yang, Camila Vesga-Castro, André Monteiro da Rocha, Isabel Newton, Anna Shcherbina, Sing-Wan Wong, Paula M Fraczek, Jacqueline A Larouche, Harrison Hiraki, Brendon M Baker, Jae-Won Shin, Shuichi Takayama, M D Thouless, Carlos A Aguilar, Tejal Desai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSomatic cell fate is an outcome set by the activities of specific transcription factors and the chromatin landscape and is maintained by gene silencing of alternate cell fates through physical interactions with the nuclear scaffold. Here, we evaluate the role of the nuclear scaffold as a guardian of cell fate in human fibroblasts by comparing the effects of transient loss (knockdown) and mutation (progeria) of functional Lamin A/C, a core component of the nuclear scaffold. We observed that Lamin A/C deficiency or mutation disrupts nuclear morphology, and the mechanical properties of the nucleus when measured by a microfluidic cellular squeezing device. We also show that transient loss of Lamin A/C promotes opening of previously silenced heterochromatin domains and increases access to DNA in lamina-associated domains. These alterations in Lamin A/C resulted in acceleration of the kinetics of cellular reprogramming to pluripotency, while genetic mutation of Lamin A/C into progerin was found to induce a senescent phenotype that inhibits the induction of reprogramming genes. Our results highlight the physical role of the nuclear scaffold in safeguarding cellular fate.

Nuclear lamins and heterochromatin physically interplay at the nuclear envelope, but how this relationship influences cellular fate remains underexplored. A comparison of nuclear lamin modifications revealed changes in chromatin accessibility, heterochromatin-associated histone modifications, nuclear mechanical properties, and efficiency of reprogramming from a somatic cell state towards pluripotency. These findings establish a unique link between subnuclear organization and cell fate control.

Introduction

All mammalian cells strictly regulate gene expression to retain their identity and function through genomic organization of chromatin (1, 2). Recent studies have shown that the spatial segregation of transcriptionally active and inactive chromatin domains is achieved in part through physical interactions with subnuclear structures at the nuclear periphery (3–6). The periphery of the nucleus is bounded by the inner and outer nuclear membrane and the nucleoskeleton, a complex mesh of type V intermediate filaments called lamins (7). Nuclear lamins are comprised of A-type (LMNA, LMNC) and B-type (LMNB1, LMNB2) isoforms (8), and their expression is lineage restricted such that all somatic cells express at least one B-type lamin (7), while A-type lamins are lacking from embryonic stem cells and restricted to differentiated cells (9). A-type lamins also scale in abundance according to cell type (10–12) and tissue stiffness (13). Accordingly, the animate functions of the genome and cell-type specificity (10, 14) are primarily mediated through dynamic interactions with lamins, whereby genes associated with alternate lineages are condensed into transcriptionally inactive heterochromatin and physically tethered to the nuclear periphery in lamina-associated domains (LADs) (12, 15–17). LADs are 0.1–10 Mb in size (17), cover more than one-third of the human and mouse genomes (18), and are associated with transcriptionally inactive domains (15). During stem cell differentiation or activation, hundreds of repressed genes in LADs detach from lamins to increase in expression and promote cell fate transitions (10, 14, 19, 20). The loss of functional lamins results in laminopathies such as Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome (HGPS), which is driven by mutations in the LMNA gene (21). HPGS has been shown to result in disruption to LADs, abnormal nuclear morphologies, loss of peripheral heterochromatin, and premature aging (22, 23). Thus, nuclear lamins are a mechanical safeguard for cellular fate and a molecular gateway to previously repressed genes.

HGPS and other laminopathies have displayed altered nuclear stiffness (24) and impairments in the ability to resist mechanical loads, which in turn render these cells susceptible to changes in chromatin conformation (25). The ability of the nucleus to resist mechanical deformation is specified in part by separate contributions from lamins and heterochromatin domains, in which chromatin is responsible for the elastic response at small deformations (<3 μm), while A-type and B-type lamins regulate the elastic and strain-stiffening responses, respectively, in response to larger mechanical stimuli (26–28). Taken together, these studies suggest that lamins and heterochromatin sit at the nexus between mechanical properties of the nucleus, intracellular mechanical signaling and cellular fate but this interdependent relationship remains underexplored.

In this article, we evaluate how changes in the composition of nuclear lamina impact the stability of cellular fate using somatic cell reprogramming as a model system. We compare the reprogramming kinetics (29) of healthy fibroblasts, HGPS fibroblasts possessing permanently dysfunctional LMNA, and fibroblasts that transiently down-regulate Lamin A/C to evaluate how chromatin state, elasticity of the nucleus (16), and cell fate plasticity are coupled. We first show how loss of functional Lamin A/C proteins affects morphology, distributions of repressive histone modifications, and chromatin accessibility. Next, we assess differences in the mechanical properties of a nucleus using a novel micro-mechanical cell-squeezing device and contrast the results with atomic force microscopy (AFM). Finally, we demonstrate that Lamin A/C modifications potentiate changes in reprogramming efficacy and kinetics. To understand the molecular mechanisms that confer these changes, we mapped transcriptional dynamics of single cells across conditions and show that Lamin A/C knockdown produces varied transcription factor (TF)-binding dynamics. These results shed light on how mechanical properties of a nucleus focalized through lamins contribute to chromatin organization and cellular fate.

Results

Disruptions in nucleoscaffold affect nuclear morphology and heterochromatin

To evaluate the effect of disruptions to the nuclear lamina on nuclear morphology, we examined three different systems. First, we utilized dermal fibroblasts possessing a heterozygous point mutation in exon 11 of the LMNA gene (c.1824C>T; p.Gly608Gly) obtained from patients with HGPS (22). This mutation activates a cryptic splice donor site, resulting in the synthesis of a truncated, immature form of Lamin A/C called progerin (22) (Fig. S1A and B) that has been shown to produce aberrant nuclear morphology (30) and loss of peripheral heterochromatin (23). We confirmed the presence of progerin in HPGS fibroblasts (Fig. S1A) and compared HGPS fibroblasts to healthy human dermal fibroblasts (controls) as well as fibroblasts that had temporarily reduced levels of Lamin A/C (LMNA KD). Knockdown of Lamin A/C was achieved through delivery of Dicer-substrate small interfering RNAs (31) (DsiRNAs) targeting LMNA in lipid nanoparticles. Following LMNA KD, we observed >75% reduction of mRNA levels and significant reductions in LMNA levels (Figs. 1A, B and S1C, E, F) with a slight decrease in cell proliferation (Fig. S1D–F). Immunostaining for Lamin A/C and two different markers associated with constitutive heterochromatin (H3K9me3 and heterochromatin protein 1a [HP1a]) in HGPS, LMNA KD, and control fibroblasts revealed that both HGPS and LMNA KD fibroblasts displayed alterations in nuclear morphology when compared with unmodified controls (Fig. 1A). Specifically, multiple protrusions or cavities were observed along the contour of the nucleus for HGPS and LMNA KD cells when compared with controls, likely reflecting punctate areas of localized protein depletion (Fig. 1C and D). In addition, we observed ruptures of the nuclear membrane in Lamin A/C staining for LMNA KD (Fig. 1A and C), indicating elevated instability in nuclear architecture. LMNA KD also resulted in increased H3K9me3, independent of the HP1a (Fig. 1A and B), which was in contrast to HGPS fibroblasts that displayed reductions in H3K9me3 and HP1a (5).

Nuclear scaffold alterations modulate chromatin accessibility and transcription factor binding

Inducing somatic cells to reprogram or change fate requires the concerted re-expression and binding of previously silenced TFs, which physically untether from the nuclear envelope back towards interior nuclear compartments (32). Previous research has shown that HGPS cells display reductions in heterochromatin and increased chromatin accessibility in LADs (33), but less is known about how transient loss of lamins impacts chromatin accessibility and gene accessibility within LADs. To probe further whether transient knockdown of LMNA facilitates heterochromatin detachment that results in changes in transcription factor binding (Figs. 2A and S2A–C), we utilized an assay for transposase-accessible chromatin followed by high-throughput sequencing (34, 35) (omni-ATAC-Seq) on control fibroblasts, LMNA KD, induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs), and human embryonic stem cells (hESCs). We generated 25,786,327 read pairs on average for each sample, resulting in 208,451 accessible sites across all conditions. Principal component analysis (PCA; Fig. 2B) showed clear separation between groups with LMNA KD fibroblasts between control fibroblasts and stem cells along the first component, and LMNA KD fibroblasts separated from controls along the second component. Partitioning each group by their shared and unique sites (Fig. 2C) revealed that uniquely accessible sites were mostly distal to gene promoters (Fig. 2D). LMNA KD fibroblasts and hiPSCs showed similar patterns of extensive chromatin opening relative to control fibroblasts (Fig. S2D and E) around promoters, enhancers, and polycomb repressed regions (36) (Fig. S2F). To investigate the functional significance of altered chromatin accessibility between groups, we annotated differentially accessible sites between control and LMNA KD fibroblasts with GO terms using the GREAT toolbox (37). This analysis revealed that transient loss of lamins promoted increased accessibility around genes associated with cell fate commitment and reduced accessibility to genes associated with fibroblast proliferation (Fig. 2E). We next inferred differences in TF binding in LMNA KD fibroblasts and hiPSCs with respect to control fibroblasts using differential TF footprinting analysis (38). LMNA KD fibroblasts and hiPSCs showed strongly concordant enrichment (Pearson's r: 0.887, P < 2.2e−16) of TFs that promote stem cell function, including the SNAI1/2/3 and ZEB1 factors, which regulate the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) during embryogenesis (39), the GLI and GLIS families, which have been recently reported to modulate reprogramming efficiency and stem cell maintenance (40–42), and PATZ1, which supports stem cell maintenance (43, 44) and titrates reprogramming efficiency (45). In contrast, control fibroblasts were enriched for AP-1 family members, which are known to guard the somatic genome against reprogramming (46, 47), MEF2 transcription factors, which regulate cellular differentiation across a wide range of cell types (48), and HOX genes, which function as master regulators of positional identity throughout development and adulthood (49) (Fig. 2F). These results show transient knockdown of the nuclear scaffold alters the chromatin landscape that results in new TF-binding patterns associated with stem cell self-renewal.

To evaluate whether transient loss of lamins induced loss of lamina-associated heterochromatin, we integrated datasets of facultative LADs (fLADs) that are specific to human Tig3 lung fibroblasts, and constitutive LADs (cLADs), which are conserved across cell types and species (50). Control fibroblasts showed a clear decrease in chromatin accessibility within fLADs compared with flanking regions, while the remaining groups showed increased accessibility within and between fLADs, indicating a loss of peripheral heterochromatin that safeguards fibroblast identity (Fig. 2G and H). Similarly, LMNA KD, hESC, and hiPSC cell types showed increased accessibility across cLADs compared with controls, consistent with global changes in chromatin architecture due to differences in lamina composition (Fig. S2G and H). Similar chromatin accessibility changes were also observed across a larger atlas of LAD types in human cell types from different germ layers (51) (Fig. S3). Integrating these results shows that loss of lamins promotes alterations in chromatin organization and increased access to previously silenced motifs required for adoption of a new cellular fate.

To gain further insight into alterations in heterochromatin that manifest from loss of Lamin A/C, we performed Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation (Cut&Tag) for facultative heterochromatin (H3K27me3) across control, HGPS, and LMNA KD fibroblasts. We generated on average 10.5 M paired-end reads for each H3K27me3 sample and replicate profiles for each library were highly similar (Figs. 2I and S4A, B). We detected comparable numbers of H3K27me3 enrichments for HGPS and LMNA KD cells, which contrasted with control cells that displayed fewer and smaller peaks when compared with both conditions (Fig. S4C–E), respectively. PCA of H3K27me3 signal revealed a clear separation between HGPS and the remaining groups as the dominant axis of variation, with the second axis containing differences between the controls and the remaining groups with altered lamina (Fig. 2I). In agreement with our ATAC analyses, we found that the boundaries of fLADs and cLADs were enriched with H3K27me3 and that loss of Lamin A/C weakened the association between lamins and H3K27me3 marks (Figs. S3 and S4F–I). To characterize differences in gene regulation between groups, we identified domains with differential H3K27me3 binding and annotated the overlapping genes with GO terms. Consistent with the role of H3K27me3 as a facultative heterochromatin mark, the pooled set of peaks was generally enriched for developmental processes (Fig. 2J). Additionally, we found that genes marked by increased H3K27me3 signal in LMNA KD and HGPS fibroblasts compared with controls, including those related to nervous system and limb development, also displayed increased chromatin accessibility across the gene body, suggesting that these genes may be opening for re-expression (Figs. 2J and S4J). To further probe this effect, we investigated gene promoters that have been associated with cell fate commitment and reprogramming, we found significant reductions and gains in H3K27me3 and ATAC signal, respectively, across all groups relative to control fibroblasts. This result suggests that the loss of functional lamins promotes the priming or re-expression of cell fate–related genes (Figs. 2K, S2I–K, S4K, L, S5, and S6). We further explored H3K27me3-related changes in cell fate regulation by identifying H3K27me3-rich regions (MRRs) which have been suggested to function as transcriptional silencers of cell fate–related genes through long-range chromatin interactions (52). H3K27me3 peaks within MRRs across all groups covered genes that were enriched for cell fate–related processes compared with typical peaks (Fig. S7A–C), and LMNA KD and HGPS fibroblasts showed consistent increases in the number and locations of MRRs relative to controls (Fig. S7D–G). These results are congruent with previous studies (5, 53) and the notion that modifying the amount and type of lamins engenders alterations to nuclear morphology and distributions of facultative heterochromatin that regulates cell fate commitment.

Development of micro-mechanical squeezing assay to assess nuclear deformations

Nuclear mechanical properties are typically assessed in bulk by confining cells between parallel plates (54), stretching an elastic substrate on which cells are adhered (55, 56), or using AFM (57–59), micropipette aspiration (60), micropost arrays (61), or microfluidic devices (62). While these approaches have begun to tease apart the relationship between nuclear morphology and stiffness in three-dimensions (3Ds), they typically rely on highly specialized equipment and setups. We improved upon an easily configurable micro-engineered poly(dimethylsiloxane) (PDMS)-based device (63) with custom stretching mechanisms to precisely trap and reproducibly deform single cells with detailed 3D optical read outs enabled by confocal microscopy (Fig. 3A). The microfluidic device uses a substrate of PDMS that contains micro-patterned notches rigidly attached to a thin film of hard PDMS (h-PDMS; Fig. S8A). Application of tensile strain to the device generated micro-cracks (64) (Fig. S8B) owing to both a lower toughness and 3-fold increase in the modulus of the thin h-PDMS layer compared with the underlying substrate (Fig. S8C). The resultant cracks can then be opened and closed in a reproducible manner by varying strain of the device (63) according to the thickness of the h-PDMS layer (Fig. S8D and E). Three-dimensional confocal imaging of the channels when the device was strained and relaxed showed the fully reversible nature of the channel cross-section (Fig. 3B).

To test the system's ability to resolve morphological changes during compression, hydrogel spheres with comparable sizes (∼25 μm diameter) and mechanical properties (2.4 kPa) to cells were fabricated from alginate polymers and conjugated to rhodamine using a cross-junction microfluidic device (65) (Fig. S9A and B). The hydrogel spheres were loaded into the system and their deformation observed at varying strains using confocal microscopy (Fig. S9C). Bead deformation was assessed by fitting an ellipsoid at each channel width. The alginate hydrogel was observed to deform linearly (65) in proportion to decreases in channel width and partially recover its undeformed morphology after the channel was reopened (Fig. S9C–E). The direction of bead deformation was primarily parallel to decreasing channel width with minimal changes in morphology along the length of the channel (Fig. S9F).

We next used this system to mechanically assess nuclei with perturbed Lamin A/C proteins. Control, HGPS, and LMNA KD fibroblasts were captured in the micro-mechanical device and compressed at four different strains until the channel was completely collapsed (Fig. 3C). Confocal microscopy at each strain resolved fine differences in nuclear morphology during compression such that folds and wrinkles in the nuclear surface were observed to flatten as they contacted the channel walls (Fig. S10A). HGPS and LMNA KD fibroblasts showed decreased resistance to nuclear lysis upon mechanical compression compared with control fibroblasts (Fig. 3D). LMNA KD fibroblasts were particularly susceptible to lysis, which is in line with our observation that loss of Lamin A/C promotes nuclear rupture even in the absence of mechanical stress. In contrast, HGPS fibroblasts, which are less able to reorganize their nucleoscaffold in response to mechanical stress (60), were able to resist lysis until the channel was completely closed.

To quantify nuclear deformation, ellipsoids were fitted to nuclei at each stage of compression and the elongation and flatness factors of each ellipsoid were calculated. Comparing these two factors during compression revealed a trend across all cell types in which nuclei transition from elongated morphologies towards flat, round morphologies (Fig. S10B and C). To determine whether the device imposes mechanical stress on nuclei before the cells are captured in the channel, we assessed the morphologies of cells sitting in the inlet outside of the channel and those of cells sitting on glass coverslips (Fig. S10C). We observed minimal differences between these populations. We further assessed each cell type's ability to resist mechanical deformation by visualizing ellipsoid flatness and elongation as a function of channel width distal to the cell. Changes in fitted ellipsoid radii during compression showed that nuclear strain occurs both along and orthogonal to the channel, in contrast to the hydrogel beads (Fig. S10D). HGPS and LMNA KD nuclei showed steeper increases in ellipsoid flatness with decreasing channel width compared with control nuclei, indicating reduced resistance to mechanical deformation (Fig. 3E). These results were supported by AFM indentation experiments, which revealed significantly reduced Young's moduli in HGPS and LMNA KD nuclei compared with control fibroblasts (Fig. 3F). The maximum indentation depth was also greatest in HGPS nuclei, highlighting reduced mechanical stiffness (Fig. 3F). These results are consistent with previous studies showing alterations in lamins confer changes in mechanical properties of nuclei (66).

Transient nuclear scaffold alterations accelerate cell fate transitions

To assess whether the induced changes in nuclear morphology and chromatin organization with Lamin A/C modification in fibroblasts impacted their ability to adopt new cellular fates, control, LMNA KD, and HGPS fibroblasts were transduced with a nonintegrative Sendai virus system to overexpress reprogramming factors (OKSM) (67) (Fig. 4A). The number and size of reprogrammed stem cell colonies were assessed by TRA-1-60 live cell staining at 7, 15, and 22 days after transduction, in addition to immunostaining for Oct3/4, Sox2, and Nanog at 15 and 22 days (Fig. 4B and C). Despite robust initiation of the reprogramming process, as evidenced by strong TRA-1-60 expression, HGPS cells did not form sizable colonies over 22 days (68). In contrast, LMNA KD fibroblasts produced more TRA-1-60-positive colonies at days 15 and 22 than controls, suggesting accelerated reprogramming kinetics. To further assess the extent of reprogramming between conditions, we performed immunostaining for Oct3/4 and Nanog followed by flow cytometry (Fig. 4D–F). On day 15, we found comparable levels of Oct3/4 and Nanog for LMNA KD and control fibroblasts, respectively, confirming a strong induction of the reprogramming program for both conditions (Fig. 4F). We also observed a significant increase in Nanog expression for LMNA KD cells on day 22 compared with control fibroblasts, signifying an accelerated rate of genuine reprogramming. Immunofluorescence staining for Sox-2 and Nanog further supported these findings whereby Sox-2 and Nanog expression were strongly activated in colonies that had formed by day 22 (Fig. S11). HGPS fibroblasts were excluded from this analysis, as the samples were too sparse to acquire reliable results. Overall, these results suggest that transient loss of Lamin A/C promotes accelerated colony formation with elevated Nanog expression, while permanently dysfunctional LMNA in HGPS cells promotes strong initiation of reprogramming that is hampered by complications from reduced proliferation and disorganized chromatin (69).

Early reprogramming kinetics are altered by loss of functional lamins

To further understand changes in the kinetics of early reprogramming from modifications to Lamin A/C, we generated and analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing datasets of control, HGPS, and LMNA knockdown fibroblasts after 7 days of reprogramming (Figs. 5 and S12A, B). We identified 6,073 high-quality cells (886 HGPS; 2,390 LMNA KD; 2,797 control), with each cell expressing a mean of 35,095 unique molecular identifiers and 6,242 genes. After dimensionality reduction with a variational autoencoder (70), we performed unbiased clustering and detected nine clusters (Figure 5B). We visualized the datasets in a uniform manifold approximation projection (UMAP) latent space (71) (Fig. 5B), and detected control and LMNA KD fibroblasts tended to co-localize within each cluster while HGPS fibroblasts were primarily restricted to clusters 6, 7, and 8. Visualizing the expression of somatic and pluripotency gene programs in the embedding revealed distinct populations of fibroblasts at the initial (clusters 2, 3, and 6-Fibronectin 1) and later stages (cluster 0-Nanog, Sox2) of reprogramming (Figs. 5C, D and S12C). In line with previous studies (72, 73), we detected enrichment of the EMT and reactive oxygen species (ROS) pathways in the initial stages of reprogramming, both of which decreased during the late stages of reprogramming (Fig. 5D).

To further analyze gene expression changes during reprogramming, we ordered cells by increasing expression of the pluripotency program (Fig. S12D) using partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) (74) and diffusion pseudotime (75) (Figs. 5E and S12E, F), which largely recapitulated differences in developmental potential measured by CytoTRACE (76) (Fig. S12G). Next, we calculated pseudotime-informed cell–cell transition probabilities using CellRank2 (77) and identified five distinct terminal fates at the extremes of pseudotime (Fig. 5F). To characterize each lineage, we performed gene-set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of genes that were correlated with the probability of reaching each terminal state (Fig. 5G). The reprogramming lineages (A and B) were similarly enriched for terms related to loss of the somatic state, such as decreases in inflammatory signaling and an increase in cell cycling, DNA repair, chromatin remodeling, and glycolysis (78). However, the B state exhibited lower NANOG expression compared with the A state and up-regulated genes related to autophagy and mTORC1 signaling (79, 80), suggesting the A and B states correspond to cells in the late and early stages of reprogramming, respectively. The C and D lineages were associated with down-regulation of the cell cycle, chromatin remodeling, histone acetylation, and an up-regulation of programs related to keratinization. HGPS fibroblasts were enriched in the C state, while control and LMNA KD fibroblasts were enriched in the D state, indicating basal differences in cell state transition potentials due to permanent loss of functional lamins. Gene expression patterns in the E state were largely opposite to those seen in states A and B, indicative of cells that had failed to reprogram and instead retained a somatic fate. Combining these results suggest that changes in mechanical properties of the nucleus from lamin modifications may alter reprogramming propensity.

To gain insights into whether lamin modifications impacted the probability of each cell to reach the reprogrammed state, we quantified both conditions as a function of diffusion pseudotime and found two branch points in the trajectory. At cluster 4, cells separated into the reprogramming (A, B) and somatic (C, D, E) lineages, and cluster 1 subsequently diverted reprogramming cells towards the late (A) or early (B) reprogramming state (Fig. S12H). Similar branching points were observed in the PAGA graph (Fig. S12E). While most of the control and LMNA KD fibroblasts were associated with the reprogramming trajectory (Fig. 5F), LMNA KD fibroblasts were enriched in both the initial state in cluster 3 and the late reprogramming terminal state (Figs. 5H and S12I), suggesting that transient loss of lamins imbues only a subset of fibroblasts with accelerated reprogramming kinetics. HGPS fibroblasts primarily transitioned towards somatic terminal states C and E, consistent with the lack of appreciable sized colonies after 22 days of reprogramming. Cells transitioning along the main reprogramming trajectory gradually increased the expression of OSKM and pluripotency genes while down-regulating fibroblast, mesenchymal, and AP-1 factor genes, consistent with our ATAC-Seq footprinting analysis (Fig. 2F) and with these genes behaving as guardians of somatic cell fate (46, 81, 82) (Fig. 5I). Moreover, we observed that several members of the linker of nucleoskeleton and cytoskeleton (LINC) complex and the Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) decreased and then increased in expression as cells transitioned towards the reprogrammed state. To detect gene programs with different reprogramming dynamics between control and LMNA KD fibroblasts, we identified genes with significant interactions between each sample and the probability of reaching the late reprogrammed state (A), yielding 412 dynamic genes (Fig. S12J). Genes that showed a greater increase in expression in control fibroblasts with reprogramming probability were primarily related to extracellular matrix organization (e.g. DCN, LUM, and BMP1), while LMNA KD fibroblasts up-regulated genes related to the cell cycle, transforming growth factor β signaling, and interferon signaling (Fig. 5J and K). Among the interferon signaling components, we observed up-regulation of STAT2 early in the reprogramming trajectory, followed by sustained expression of STAT3, which synergizes with NANOG to promote pluripotency programs (83–85) (Fig. 5K). To infer changes in gene regulatory networks, we submitted these genes for TF enrichment analysis (86) (Fig. S13A). We found increased activity of AP-1 factors (JUN, FOS) and TFs that regulate the EMT (SMAD3, ZEB1) in control fibroblasts relative to LMNA KD fibroblasts. In contrast, LMNA KD fibroblasts showed increased activity of inflammatory factors (IRF1, IRF7, IRF9, STAT1, STAT2), Krüppel-associated box domain zinc fingers (ZNF93, ZNF878, ZNF659, ZNF367, ZNF107), which regulate iPSC self-renewal (87), and FOXM1, which has been shown to promote rejuvenating chromatin landscapes through suppression of AP-1 factor expression (88) (Fig. S13B). Summing these results shows that permanent modification of Lamin A/C, as observed in HGPS, reduces competency for changing cellular fate while transient loss of functional lamins modulates early reprogramming kinetics by up-regulating unique pathways supportive of pluripotency in a subset of cells.

Discussion

Cellular identity is regulated by the coupled actions of lineage-specific TF binding, and a chromatin landscape that represses genes associated with alternate fates (2). Here, we show that modifying Lamin A/C changes nuclear morphology and elasticity (16, 89), and opens condensed chromatin to facilitate alterations in the ability to change cell fate (12, 18). Recently, mechano-transductive strategies (90) have been shown to enhance the efficiency of the reprogramming process through reduction of heterochromatin (91, 92), which is a well-known barrier to reprogramming (93) and contributes to proper expression of lineage-specific programs (94, 95). Our results show that temporary perturbation of the nuclear scaffold from LMNA KD can transiently increase constitutive heterochromatin markers such as H3K9me3 (94, 95), as well as increase chromatin accessibility in constitutive LADs. These chromatin changes occurred around genes related to PIWI RNA metabolism, which are known to repress transposable elements that are typically silenced within LADs. Thus, while H3K9me3 may be temporarily increased in LMNA KD cells, lineage-specific genes that are typically repressed within LADs may also be opening. This result indicates that the kinetics of reprogramming somatic cells back to pluripotency may be mediated by heterochromatin detachment within LADs, which is in line with previous improvements in reprogramming efficiency by treatment with vitamin C (96) that specifically targets heterochromatin at the nuclear periphery (97).

HGPS is a devastating rare condition in which functional Lamin A/C is replaced by mutant progerin. Replacement of Lamin A/C with progerin contributes to aberrant nuclear morphology, loss of H3K9me3, and opening of genes normally repressed in LADs (33, 98, 99) suggestive of an increased propensity to change cellular fate. Our results show reductions in the ability of HGPS cells to reprogram back to pluripotency and attribute this to reductions in proliferative capacity from accumulation of progerin (99). Cellular reprogramming to pluripotency requires concerted proliferation and multiple passages of HGPS cells (100) have been shown to contribute to senescence (101). Single-cell RNA sequencing of fibroblasts early in the reprogramming process further substantiate this effect and showed that while control and LMNA KD fibroblasts traveled along similar trajectories towards pluripotency, HGPS fibroblasts did not appreciably express pluripotency genes and varied in their trajectory. These results suggest that temporary changes in lamina-associated heterochromatin facilitate formation of new chromatin accessibility patterns that are permissive to cell fate changes, while permanent LAD detachment and progerin accumulation contributes to dysfunctional cell cycle regulation and senescence programs (102).

Mammalian cells are sensitive to tissue stiffness (13) and adjust the mechanical properties of their nuclei by modifying expression of lamin isoforms and re-distributing heterochromatin. Techniques to connect biophysical properties of nuclei with morphological changes have been developing (103) and our microfluidic squeezing approach facilitates facile cell trapping in optically transparent microfluidic channels and controllably deforming nuclei. Monitoring the squeezing process with confocal microscopy facilitated observation of increases in nuclear deformation for HGPS and LMNA KD fibroblasts when compared with controls, which mirrored results obtained with AFM indentation. Given this system is adaptable to integrate with light-sheet microscopy (104) for tracking of faster cellular processes, is scalable to probe deformation of individual nuclei in multinucleated cells such as muscle, and displayed sensitivity to follow the unfolding of nuclear wrinkles, which can be used to predict mechano-transduction ability (105) and resistance to nuclear lysis; we envision additional aspects of nuclear mechanobiology can be accessed by this novel approach. Our observations revealed HGPS and LMNA KD fibroblasts displayed increased propensity to lyse under smaller nuclear strains, and we speculate that cells with mutated LMNA in mechanically active tissues such as the myocardium, skeletal muscle, and vasculature may attempt to remodel their matrix as a protective mechanism. In line with this, recent evidence suggests that cell types in mechanically active tissues with laminopathies exhibit pathological symptoms at faster rates than tissues with less deformation (106). While other mechanisms such as stretch-induced ion channels or nuclear actin alterations could also contribute to differences in nuclear mechanics from loss of LMNA, we believe our system can begin to investigate these intricately coupled mechanisms.

The biophysical changes in nuclear structure that are imparted from modifications to lamins present an exciting opportunity to further understand links between chromatin organization and epigenetic factors that enforce lineage-specific gene expression (69, 107, 108). Future work in this area will focus on how changes to the nuclear scaffold impact DNA interactions with other nuclear bodies and self-interacting topological-associated domains, as well as posttranslational modifications such as phosphorylation and TF interactions with other chromatin enzymes that shuttle into the nucleus with mechanical deformation (109, 110).

Materials and methods

Additional methods can be found in the Supplementary material.

Cell culture

Dermal fibroblasts from a de-identified normal adult and a patient with HGPS were obtained from The Progeria Research Foundation Cell and Tissue Bank. Prior to seeding cells, culture plates were coated in a 1:30 dilution of Matrigel (Corning) in DMEM/F12 for 1 h at room temperature. Extra plates were kept at 4 °C for at most 2 weeks and allowed to equilibrate for 1 h at room temperature before use. The growth media consisted of DMEM/F12 (Gibco), 10% fetal bovine serum, 0.1 mmol/L nonessential amino acids (Gibco), 1 mmol/L L-glutamine (Gibco), 0.1 mmol/L beta-mercaptoethanol, and 1× penicillin–streptomycin. Fibroblasts were passed at ∼80% confluency using 0.25% Trypsin-EDTA (Gibco).

hESCs were grown on ESC-qualified Matrigel (BD) in human ES media consisting of KO-DMEM (Invitrogen), 10% KOSR (Invitrogen), 1% glutamax or L-glutamin, NEAA (Thermo), penicillin/streptomycin, 0.1% 2-mercaptoethanol, and 10 ng/mL human bFGF (Gibco). All cells were passaged using Accutase (Invitrogen).

Fibroblasts from proband (HGADFN167) and genetically relate control (HGDFN168) were obtained from the Progeria Research Foundation Cell and Tissue Bank (MTA-19UFA01804), and studies with hiPSC lines were conducted with institutional approval of the University of Michigan's Human Pluripotent Stem Cell Oversight Committee (HPSCRO #1047).

siRNA transfection and real-time PCR analysis

Lmna knockdown in human dermal fibroblasts

One day prior to transfection, human dermal fibroblasts were seeded in 12- or 24-well Matrigel-coated plates at 15,000 or 10,000 cells per well, respectively. Cells were seeded on glass coverslips for immunostaining. On the day of transfection, cells were treated with 50 nM of DsiRNA against Lamin A/C (IDT; hs.Ri.Lmna.13.3) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher) and allowed to recover for 5 days. The medium was replaced 3 days after transfection.

Quantitative PCR

Titration of Lmna DsiRNAs and knockdown efficiency were confirmed in part using quantitative PCR. Cells were harvested using Trizol, and a phenol-chloroform RNA extraction was performed using the Qiagen miRNeasy Micro Kit according to the manufacturer's protocol. cDNA was then synthesized using the Superscript III First-Strand Synthesis kit and quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using Power SYBR Green PCR mix (Thermo Fisher) on a QuantStudio 3 thermocycler (Applied Biosystems). The Lmna primer was purchase from IDT (PrimeTime qPCR assay, Hs.PT.58.27794).

Proliferation assessment

To confirm that lipid nanoparticle transfection has minimal effect on cell proliferation, we compared the proliferation of control and Lmna knockdown cells using the BioSpa Live Cell Analysis system (BioTek) and performed Ki67 immunofluorescence staining (Santa Cruz Biotechnology).

Cell immunostaining and analysis

Immunostaining

Cells were fixed using 3% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 10 min and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min at room temperature. The cells were then washed and blocked using a solution of 0.005% Triton X-100 and 0.1% normal donkey serum in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. Primary antibodies were added in the same buffer for 1 h at room temperature in the dark followed by secondary antibodies using the same procedure. DAPI stain was diluted to 1.5 µg/mL in PBS, and the cells were incubated with diluted DAPI for 1 min at room temperature, in the dark. Cells cultured on glass coverslips were then lifted and mounted on microscope slides using Fluoromount-G Mounting Medium (Southern Biotech) and stored at 4 °C overnight. The primary antibodies used in this study were: Rabbit anti-HP1a (Invitrogen, PA529879), Rabbit anti-H3K9me3 (Abcam, ab8898), and Mouse anti-Lamin A/C-Alexa Fluor 546 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-376248). Secondary antibodies were Goat anti-Rabbit Alexa Fluor 555 or 647 (Fisher Scientific).

Fluorescence imaging

Fluorescence images were acquired using a Zeiss AxioVert epifluorescent microscope at 40× magnification or a Nikon A1R inverted confocal microscope at 60×. The same acquisition parameters were used across samples for quantitative microscopy. All image processing was done in ImageJ. Nuclei were segmented using the DAPI stain and used to quantify the integrated fluorescence intensity of the other stains (111). Varying segmentation and image processing protocols were implemented depending on the objective magnification and sample type. Background regions of interest (ROI) were drawn by hand in each image and used to calculate corrected total cell fluorescence (CTCF):

Nuclei morphology analysis

Image analyses were performed in ImageJ and processed using R (v4.1.3). Cells for each condition were fixed and immunostained for Lamin A/C. The Huang threshold algorithm was used to segment and threshold nuclei in the corresponding Lamin A/C channel to produce a binary image. A pixelated ROI boundary was acquired for each nucleus using the wand tool and smoothed by subpixel interpolation with one pixel resolution. The resulting cartesian coordinates were saved for further analysis. For each nucleus, a circle with the same centroid and area was constructed, and the ratio between the centroid-to-perimeter distance and the radius of the equivalent circle was calculated as its relative radius. To reduce noisiness in the data, a cubic smoothing spline was applied using the R stats package with a smoothing parameter of 0.45. Smoothed relative radii were graphed as a function of degrees in a clockwise direction. Invaginations and protrusions in the nuclear envelope were quantified by identifying local minima and maxima of the smoothed profiles, respectively, with a sliding window of 10°. The average number of invaginations and protrusions for each LINC knockdown were compared against that of wild-type nuclei using Mann–Whitney U tests.

Western blots

Cell pellets from control and HGPS fibroblasts were resuspended in 2× Laemmli buffer and vortexed. Equal volumes of lysates were run on NuPAGE 4–12% Bis-Tris 1.5 mm protein gels (Invitrogen) in MOPS SDS buffer (Invitrogen) for 2 h at 80 V then transferred to nitrocellulose paper. The resulting membranes were cut to isolate the proteins of interest. Lamin A/C and progerin were located around 70 kDa while glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was located around 40 kDa. Membranes were then washed three times in PBS-T (1 mL Tween 20 in 1 L 1× PBS) for 5 min each and blocked with 5% milk in PBS-T at room temperature for 30 min. Primary antibodies were then incubated on each membrane in 5% milk in PBS-T overnight at 4 °C (1:1,000 GAPDH, 1:200 Lamin A/C). The following day, membranes were washed five times in PBS-T and incubated with HRP secondary antibody (1:500 Lamin A/C, 1:200 GAPDH). After washing, the membranes were imaged on a Chemi-Doc using the Pierce ECL kit (Thermo Fisher) for GAPDH and the SuperSignal Pico PLUS kit (Thermo Fisher) for Lamin A/C. Blot intensities for GAPDH were measured using the ImageLab software (Bio-Rad) and used to normalize sample volumes. The protocol was repeated with these normalized sample volumes for quantification.

Microfluidic assessment of nuclear mechanical properties

Fabrication of micro-engineered devices

We utilized deterministic cracking (112) of thin polymeric films at patterned sites to generate fluidic channels that are deformable under uniaxial tension and to trap single cells (113). First, we used photolithography to create a mold with two horizontal arrays of v-shaped grooves that were positioned across from each other with the tips of each “v” facing inwards. Each pair of grooves defined the positions of a single crack and served as inlets and outlets into the cracked channel. h-PDMS, which is composed of vinyl and hydrosilane end-linked polymers (114) that increase its Young's modulus and render the material more brittle, was poured over the mold and spun on a Brewer Science Spin Coater to create a thin film (step 1: 500 RPM, 5 s, 250 RPM/s ramp; step 2: 2,000 RPM, 40 s, 250 RPM/s ramp). The mold was then incubated in a 120 °C oven for 2 min and allowed to cool for 30 s at room temperature. Twenty grams of 1:10 PDMS was then poured over the h-PDMS film and incubated in a 60 °C oven for at least 4 h. The mismatch in moduli between the h-PDMS film and PDMS substrate promoted crack formation between each pair of grooves (63, 115). Channels were created by applying uniaxial tension across the resulting device using a custom fabricated clamp integrated to a micrometer displacement stage. Each device was observed under a dissection microscope during tension to monitor crack formation. Inlets and outlets were then created with a 0.75-mm punch. To form the bottom wall of the channels, a thin film of 1:10 PDMS was spun onto glass slides (step 1: 500 RPM, 5 s, 250 RPM/s ramp; step 2: 1,000 RPM, 60 s, 250 RPM/s ramp) and bonded to the h-PDMS/PDMS device using a plasma asher (Femto Science).

Measurement of bulk PDMS and h-PDMS stiffness

The Young's moduli of the bulk PDMS and h-PDMS layers in the device were measured under strain-controlled uniaxial tension using a Dynamic Mechanical Analyzer (TA Instruments, RSA III). Samples were cast into a 3D-printed mold following the ASTM D638 type 1 standard. Samples were stretched at 0.01 mm/s for 10 s and normal stress was measured at 300 time points assuming a temperature of 21 °C.

Production of alginate hydrogel beads

Alginate hydrogels with a 2.4-kPa Young's modulus were generated as previously described (65). Briefly, a gel solution of DMEM and 2% w/v lyophilized alginate conjugated with Rhodamine B was encapsulated in microfluidic water-in-oil droplets with an outer oil phase comprising 1% acetic acid in PFPE surfactant. A secondary flow of CaCO3 solution in DMEM was added to form alginate hydrogel emulsions that were filtered through a 40-μm cell strainer.

Loading and squeezing samples in the device

Each device was washed with 70% ethanol and 1× PBS prior to sample loading. Devices were stretched in the custom clamp, which opened the channel, and a pair of pipettes generated a negative pressure gradient across the length of the channel by dispensing cleaning solution in the inlet and aspirating the solution through the outlet. Cells and hydrogel bead samples were loaded in the same manner. To trap samples, the strain on the device was relaxed to constrict the channel. The applied strain was recorded at rest before the channel was opened and at the moment of sample capture to monitor channel dimensions. To compress samples, uniaxial strain on the device in the clamp was incrementally removed in 0.5 mm steps until the channel was completely collapsed.

Image acquisition

All confocal images were acquired on a Nikon A1R confocal microscope with a 20× air objective. After the channel cleaning and prior to sample loading, the channel was filled with an aqueous solution of fluorescein to visualize channel dimensions. To image cells, the channel was filled with a solution of fluorescein in PBS without calcium or magnesium. To image alginate hydrogel beads, the channel was filled with a solution of fluorescein in DMEM with ∼1.8 mM calcium chloride to minimize swelling. Prior to loading, cells were stained with Calcein AM Red-Orange and Hoechst 33342, and resuspended at 0.5 × 106 cells/mL in complete growth media. Cells and hydrogel bead suspensions were kept on ice during image acquisition. Confocal z-stacks (1 µm z-steps) were acquired after cell trapping and at each strain during compression.

Image analysis

All image analysis done in ImageJ. Briefly, samples were segmented from z-stack images using the MorphoLibJ (116) (https://imagej.net/plugins/morpholibj) and 3D Objects Counter (117) (https://imagej.net/plugins/3d-objects-counter) plugins. This allows surface area and volume measurements to be recorded. Next, the thickness of each sample in orthogonal directions is recorded in addition to measurements of the orthogonal cross-sections (e.g. aspect ratio, area, and perimeter). Finally, an ellipsoid is fitted to each sample using the 3D ROI Manager (118) (https://imagejdocu.list.lu/plugin/stacks/3d_ij_suite/start). Three-dimensional visualizations of the channel and samples during compression were generated using the 3D Viewer plugin (119) in ImageJ without resampling. STL files of the bead and channel surfaces were exported from 3D Viewer and opened in Fusion 360 for 3D renderings. The imported meshes were adaptively re-meshed using density and shape preservation settings of 3 and 1, respectively, and rendered images were captured using the orthographic camera setting.

Atomic force microscopy

To measure nuclei stiffness, AFM nanoindentation in contact mode was performed using a Nanosurf FlexBio atomic force microscope with a HYDRA6V-200NG (AppNano) probe tip (k = 0.0348 N/m) affixed with an 11.39 μm diameter glass microsphere (Fisher). Cells were cultured on 18 mm glass coverslips and stained with Hoechst dye (1:1,000) to visualize nuclei location. The glass coverslips were then adhered to a 50 × 55 mm glass coverslip that was then placed onto the AFM stage. The probe tip was equilibrated in the cell media above the culture monolayer for 20 min prior to indentation measurements. Indentations were performed on 15 nuclei per condition. Force–displacement curves were fit to the Hertz model assuming a Poisson's ratio of 0.5 to determine Young's Modulus values.

CUT&Tag sequencing and analysis

Library generation

About 50,000–75,000 human dermal fibroblasts before and after Lmna knockdown and HGPS cells were processed for CUT&Tag library creation as per manufacturer's instructions (CUTANA, Epicypher). Briefly, cells were centrifuged at 600 × g for 3 min at room temperature in a fixed angle centrifuge. After removing the supernatant, the cell pellets were re-suspended in 100 µL of ice-cold nuclei extraction buffer (CUTANA), incubated 10 min on ice, and centrifuged at 600 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to pellet the nuclei. The supernatants were carefully removed and the pellets were re-suspended in antibody buffer (CUTANA), incubated overnight on a rocker followed by a series of washing steps and tagmentation, amplification, and purification. The molarity of each DNA library was determined (Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer), pooled into a single tube and sequenced on a NextSeq (Illumina) using 76-bp paired-end reads.

Library preprocessing and count matrix generation

CUT&Tag FASTQ files were trimmed and aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38) with the nf-core/cutandrun processing pipeline (https://nf-co.re/cutandrun/3.2.2/) (120) with a read mapping quality threshold of 20. Mitochondrial reads were removed. Peaks were called using the SEACR (121) with the “stringent” setting and a threshold of 0.01 without control normalization. To create a consensus peak set across samples, peaks were pooled and merged to create a naive overlap set. The count matrix was generated by counting reads in each peak using the featureCounts program in the subread suite (122).

Differential region analysis and PCA

Differentially bound regions were identified using the quasi-likelihood framework implemented in the edgeR package (123). Peaks were filtered to those with at least 10 counts within biological groups and trimmed-mean of M-value (TMM) normalization factors were calculated. Empirical Bayes estimates of the quasi-likelihood dispersions were robustified against outlier peaks. Thresholds of adjusted P-value <0.05 and absolute log-fold-change >0 were used to identify differentially bound regions. Latent batch effects were identified using the sva package (124) and removed using the removeBatchEffect function in limma. PCA was performed using log counts-per-million (logCPM) with a prior count of 5.

Signal track generation

H3K27me3 signal tracks were created for each sample using the bamCoverage function in the deeptools suite (125) with a bin size of 1 and scaling factors derived from the TMM factors calculated with edgeR. Unmapped reads, nonprimary alignments, reads failing quality checks, reads with a mapping quality <20, and reads overlapping the ENCODE blacklist regions (126) were removed prior to track generation. Tracks were averaged across replicates within each biological group using the bigwigAverage function in the deeptools suite.

Shared and unique peaks

To define cell type–specific peaks, SEACR peaks were pooled and merged within cell types and compared with the naive overlap peak set used for count matrix generation. Overlaps between cell-type peaks and naive overlap peaks were combined into a combination matrix for the make_comb_mat function in the ComplexHeatmap package (127) using the “distinct” mode. Shared and unique peaks were defined as naive overlap peaks that overlapped any peaks across all cell types or one cell type, respectively.

MRR identification

Overlapping H3K27me3 peaks were merged within each cell type and merged peaks within 4 kb were stitched together. The resulting regions were ranked by total TMM-normalized H3K27me3 signal, and the rank corresponding to a slope of 1 was used as the cutoff for defining MRRs. The slope was calculated using scripts from Cai et al. (52).

Gene-set enrichment

Genes marked by H3K27me3 were identified by extending gene bodies 5 kb upstream and downstream, and filtering for regions that overlapped H3K27me3 peaks. The resulting genes were annotated to GO Biological Process terms using the goana command in the limma package. The naive overlap peak set was annotated using the full genome universe. Peaks with differential H3K27me3 coverage were annotated using all H3K27me3-marked genes as the background. This identified terms that were enriched between biological groups. Significant terms were identified using a P < 1e−3 threshold.
