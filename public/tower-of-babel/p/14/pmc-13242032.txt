Current opinions on CEA in the diagnosis and prognosis of colorectal cancer
Authors: Qi Han, Yajuan Zhu, Fangzhou Xing, Yun Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•CEA is a widely used but imperfect biomarker in clinic.•Cut-off value of CEA should be flexible and condition-dependent.•Combining CEA with liquid biopsy improves its value in CRC.•Comprehensive review of CEA biology, mechanisms, and clinical history were included.Keywords: Carcinoembryonic antigen, Colorectal cancer, Diagnosis, Prognosis

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a significant public concern with the second leading cause of cancer death. There are approximately 1.9 million new cases and 0.9 million deaths estimated in 2022 worldwide [1]. Only 10-20% of cases are applicable for surgery, and the 5-year survival rate is as low as 30% [2]. After the curative treatment, recurrent diseases will incur in about 25% to 40% of patients, contributing to cancer death [3]. As for CRC metastasis, the most common is liver metastasis, which is also an important cause of CRC-related deaths [4]. On the whole, the incidence and mortality of CRC are decreasing, especially in high-income countries. The five-year survival in the USA and some European countries has improved to 65% [5,6]. However, the condition in undeveloped countries is not so optimistic with a five-year survival of less than 50% [7,8]. CRC is strongly linked to age that the incidence rises with age, and the screening usually begins when a person turns 50. Notably, the early onset colorectal cancer (eoCRC), which is diagnosed before 50 years old, has been increasing rapidly [9].

The survival of CRC patients is contingent on early diagnosis and treatment. The five-year survival rate is notably higher for patients diagnosed at early stage compared to those with advanced-stage CRC. Patients diagnosed at an early stage have a significant higher 5-year survival rate compared to those with advanced-stage CRC. Tissue biopsy is still the gold standard for tumor identification. However, it is difficult to monitor disease progression and detect heterogeneity based on one single biopsy. Repeated biopsies are also hard to conduct due to the poor compliance of patients resulting from the injury [10]. And the removal of tumor tissue, accuracy, and high costs are limited factors. Therefore, it is necessary to use invasive methods to screen the high-risk population and evaluate the risk of relapse. Reliable, easily-used, and no-injury tumor biomarkers are urgently needed. The serum biomarkers, especially tumor-associated proteins are most commonly used in CRC diagnostics. Among these, CEA has great clinical utility as a tumor marker. The serum CEA level is crucial for screening CRC because it is upregulated in individuals with CRC. It also plays an important role to be a prognostic factor in supervising CRC after treatment [11]. However, previous studies found that realizing simultaneous excellence of sensitivity and specificity for CEA was difficult. Besides, it seems that CEA alone is not reliable enough to be used for CRC screening in the asymptomatic population [12]. There are controversies on the CEA usage in CRC.

In this review, we concentrate on the current opinions on CEA and discuss its attractive potential to serve as a noninvasive diagnostic biomarker in CRC. We highlight its important clinical influence, and try to conclude novel insights on the applicated value of this classical biomarker.

The biological characteristics of CEA

CEA is a glycoprotein belonging to a subgroup of the CEA immunoglobulin (Ig) superfamily [13]. The CEA family is broadly divided into two distinct categories: CEA-related cell adhesion molecules (CEACAMs) and pregnancy-specific glycoproteins (PSGs). PSCs are thought to be secreted proteins, while others are mainly membrane-bound proteins. The CEA protein has a variable (V)-type, I-type, and six C2-type Ig domains, and it is heavily glycosylated. Each Ig domain was evenly replaced by four oligosaccharide chains containing L-fucose or sialic acid at the non-reducing ends and linking to the peptide core via N-acetyl glucosamine. The V-type domain is known as the N domain, with repeated pairs of C2-type domains (A1, B1, A2, B2, A3 and B3) followed [14] (Fig. 1B). In addition, CEA has an exceptional highly conserved amino sequence, and each repeat unit contains four conserved cysteine residues [15].

Most CEACAMs are cell membrane-bound through GPI, such as CEACAM 5, 6, 7, 8, or through transmembrane helices, such as CEACAM 1, 3, 4, 20, 21.

There are at least 17 active genes encoding members of the CEA family clustered on chromosome 19q13.2 [16] (Fig. 1A). The cis-acting sequence responsible for the control of cell-specific expression of the CEA gene was identified. It is approximately 400 base pairs upstream from the translation start [17]. Hauck and Stanners also demonstrated that the regulatory elements conferring cell-type-specific expression were within -403 to -124 bp. The 5′ upstream region contains four positive and one negative regulatory elements [18]. It was found that no classic TATA or CAAT box resided at the expected position upstream of the CEA gene transcription sites [17]. A truncated Alu repeat sequence is present in the 3′ untranslated region of the CEA mRNA.

All CEACAMs bind to the cell surface membrane and CEA achieves it through glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) lipid moieties (Fig. 1B). In healthy individuals, most CEA protein is produced in colons and displays the selective epithelial expression [19]. The high-level CEA was observed in mature columnar epithelial cells facing the gut lumen, as well as in highly differentiated epithelial cells at the crypt mouth [20]. The approach of the CEA secretion is from the apical surface of these cells into the enteric cavity, then it will be exited with feces (Fig. 2A). This is the reason why healthy individuals sustain a low CEA level. In patients with CRC, CEA protein is secreted at the whole surface of malignant cells. With the increase of CEA level in blood and tumor area, patients with CRC can be screened out (Fig. 2B). Besides, the distribution of CEA mRNA was identical to CEA protein [21]. The highest levels of CEA mRNA were found in the tumor regions with the highest levels of CEA protein [22]. This suggested that CEA expression, rather than differential protein secretion, is responsible for the heterogeneous distribution.

Intercellular cell adhesion and signaling have been considered as roles of CEA [16]. By protecting the colon from the extensive microbial exposure, CEA also involved in innate immune defense [19]. Furthermore, CEA is implicated in pathological functions, such as the inhibition of differentiation progress, disruption of cell polarization, and tissue architecture and carcinogenesis [23]. It was reported that a variety of apoptotic stimuli to CRC cells could be defensed by the over-expression of CEA [24]. CEA can promote the dissemination of tumor cells. Mechanically, CEA can influence various pathways to upregulate tumor-progression molecules and depress tumor-inhibit molecules [25].

The performance of CEA in CRC

In 1965, CEA was first found as a tumor-related antigen in both primary and metastatic CRC by Gold and Freedman [26]. CEA was also exposed in the pancreas, liver, and gut tissues of 2–6 months human fetuses, therefore they named this marker “carcinoembryonic antigen” [27]. A sensitive radioimmunoassay was used to measure the serum CEA level in patients with cancers including CRC [28]. Compared with the healthy group, patients with cancer displayed a higher CEA level, which attracted people’s interest to use it as a tumor marker. Before using it effectively in the clinic, there were two major jobs finished. CEA was a glycoprotein containing approximately 50% carbohydrate. It was not easy to be purified and characterized. And one step accomplished further exploration about it [29,30]. Another work was the discovery of CEA-cross-reactive antigens such as non-specific cross-reacting antigens (NAC) and biliary glycoproteins (BGP) [31]. These antigens interfere with CEA test and the cross-reactive antibodies should be noticed. With the emergence of monoclonal antibodies, the studies about applying CEA as a target antigen for radioimmunolocalization and radioimmunotherapy became more [32]. CEA has been considered as a suitable target antigen for the detection of primary and metastatic CRC and some other carcinomas.

The most frequently used immunoassays for CEA detection are enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), electrochemiluminescent immunoassay (ECL) and chemiluminescence immunoassay (CLIA). Although radioimmunoassay (RIA) can achieve a sensitivity of 10⁻¹² mol/L [33], its application has declined due to radiation exposure hazards and inconsistent sensitivity, with microparticle enzyme immunoassays and CLIA becoming more common alternatives. CLIA is one of the most sensitive immunoassay platforms and prevalent method. Its detection limits can reach as low as 10⁻¹⁸ to 10⁻²¹ mol/L. Owing to its low chemiluminescent background, CLIA offers high precision and produces fewer false-positive results than microparticle enzyme immunoassays [34]. The detection limit of enzyme immunoassay for CEA is 0.5 ng/mL [35] and sandwich ELISA format is widely employed for CEA measurement. However, these antibody-based assays have drawbacks, such as costly, high immunogenicity, and short-term stability [36]. More excellent detecting methods have been explored, such as chemically synthesized oligonucleotides called aptamers, and biosensors that are used in either an optical, mechanical, or electronic immunoassay. ECL offers enhanced temporal and spatial control compared with conventional chemiluminescence, and improved selectivity via adjustments in electrode potential. The detection threshold of ECL for CEA is 0.2 µg/L [37]. These methods are promising as featuring precise specificity and affinity for various targets [38,39]. Notably, during the entire follow-up period, the same assay technique should be used. Accounting for data from clinical studies requires considering the actual method used, especially the threshold [40].

Nowadays, a growing number of studies have clarified that the serum CEA was an unqualified marker for health checkup population and not sensitive enough to diagnose early gastrointestinal cancers [41]However, for CRC in stage II-IV, CEA is valuable if 0.7 of the area under curve (AUC) was defined as the qualified line [42]. There was reliable evidence that routinely monitoring the serum CEA in postoperative CRC could reveal early recurrence. The CEA level after treatment was contacted with survival rate and the continued rise often implies incomplete resection or occult metastasis [43]. Some scientists indicated that cell-associated CEA could be utilized as a tumor marker because it was able to trace circulating tumor cells (CTCs) for recurrence prediction [44]. The results in tumor tissue were consistently to that in the blood. For CRC patients with metastatic lymph nodes, the CEA expression in the metastasis was higher in the short-term survivors [45]. And the positive CEA mRNA in lymph nodes also related to shorter survival time, about 60 months than patients with negative CEA mRNA [46].

For individuals with CRC, CEA level elevation in serum and tumor region is not surprising. The expression of CEA is observed on the whole surface of the malignant cells that are unpolarized and located deep inside tumor gland. CEA can exfoliate as plasma membrane-derived vesicles, which provide access to lymphatic or blood vessels and accumulate in blood. It has been demonstrated that increasing serum CEA was associated with a hypomethylation level of CEA DNA. The hot sites for DNA methylation were demonstrated in the CEA promoter regions of −200 to −500 and −1000 to −1400 bp [47]. The changes in the structure of transcription factors interacting with the regulatory elements of the CEA promoter may also work [18].

Obviously, CEA does relate to the progression and metastasis of CRC, and its mechanisms have attracted lots of researchers to explore. CEA in patients with CRC expresses a high level of the carbohydrates, including Lewis X and Lewis Y. Their binding to dendritic cell-specific intercellular adhesion molecule 3-grabbing non-integrin (DC-SIGN) realizes the connection of immature dendritic cells (DCs) with tumor cells. It leads to the suppression of DC functions and intensive T-cell response which was speculated to be responsible for the immune tolerance to cancer cells and tumor progression [48]. And CEA protein, especially the mutant type, may involve in increasing the abundance of CRC-associated bacteria in the intestine and promote colorectal carcinogenesis, through inhibiting transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) signaling [49]. The bridges formed by CEA between tumor cells help their adhesion and are beneficial to tumor growth. CEA participates in cancer metastasis mainly through three pathways. Firstly, CEA can improve the adhesion skill of circulating CRC cells by indirectly increasing related cell molecules, such as intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) and vascular adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1). These molecules are upregulated by pro-inflammatory cytokines, which are secreted more because of the binding of CEA to liver Kupffer cells [[50], [51], [52]]. Secondly, CEA can bind to TGF-β Receptor 1 (TGF-β R1) which is responsible for growth inhibition [53], thus enhancing the metastasis. Thirdly, the anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-10 (IL-10) that can be stimulated by CEA alleviates the ischemic injury of cancer cells, raising the survival rate of metastatic cells [50] (Fig. 2C).

Apply and monitor CEA level with more flexibility

In fact, the elevation of serum CEA level shows not only in several cancers including CRC, gastric cancer, pancreatic cancer, and lung cancer, but also in many kinds of non-malignant conditions. These non-malignant conditions can be systematically categorized into physiological conditions and benign pathological conditions. Physiological factors include age, cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, and fasting glucose [54]. Benign pathological conditions comprise COVID-19, Alzheimer's disease, chronic lung disease, cirrhosis, ulcerative colitis, hypothyroidism, and chronic renal failure [[55], [56], [57], [58]]. Other metabolic and lifestyle factors also influence the CEA increase, such as There are some confusions when facing the elevation of CEA. For example, an elevated CEA level in pleural fluid can give a clue about malignancy, but the identification of lung infections accompanying exudates should also be noticed because 9% of benign pleurisy results in the CEA elevation [59]. Commonly, 5 ng/mL as the cutoff level is used to define CEA elevation, which has limitations in predicting recurrence, as benign factors can elevate CEA in 5-10 ng/ml. Therefore, clarifying the causes of false-positive CEA will help to better apply this tumor marker.

When serum CEA level was used to predict the risk of recurrence, the accurate condition of patients should be considered. Preoperative serum CEA reliably predicts recurrence in CRC patients treated with curative surgery. It was 10 ng/ml to predict recurrence in a study comprising 638 patients of stage I–III CRC after curative surgery. The no-disease survival was significantly different between the two group CEA levels >10 ng/mL and <10 ng/mL, except for patients in stage I [60]. A study including 1543 stage IIA CRC patients in South Korea showed that those with increasing CEA preoperatively had a lower 5-year relapse-free survival (RFS) and overall survival (OS), with the optimal cutoff level 6 ng/mL [61]. Another study, however, suggested that the optimal CEA level for predicting high risks of recurrence was 2.5ng/mL for patients at that stage after treatment [62].

What’s more, the surveillance of postoperative CEA is also a useful tool to help find early recurrence for patients after resection. When following up 318 patients with resected colorectal liver metastases (CRLM) during ten years, over half of them were found to have relapses within two years and the postoperative CEA ≥15 ng/mL indicated the highest hazard ratio [63]. Additionally, Araujo et al. also considered the cutoff level as 15 ng/mL. The study included CRLM patients in the follow-up during the first six months after liver resection [64]. Another study showed that serum CEA levels above 50 ng/mL predicted a poorer prognosis for patients undergoing the first hepatectomy [65]. Therefore, the optimal cutoff level of CEA is not consistent under different conditions (Table 1). Using a low threshold of 2.5 ng/mL would reduce the number of missed recurrences to 36.5%, but increase the false alarms to 84.2% [12]. In fact, the risk of missed lesions increases as the cut-off level increases, which should be noticed when using this tumor marker. And there are clinical variations in the cut-off levels applied to decide whether further investigations are needed.

Therefore, the optimal cut-off balancing of CEA should be finally settled [77]. The optimal CEA cut-off value depends on the clinical objective. A low threshold (5 ng/mL) is recommended when high sensitivity is required to rule out disease, such as in population screening. Of note, healthy individuals aged >70 years may exhibit CEA levels slightly exceeding 5 ng/mL [78]. The performance of CEA in detecting colorectal cancer recurrence is significantly influenced by baseline levels, with a much higher detection rate in patients who had elevated initial CEA (59.4%) than in those with normal initial levels (23.4%) [79]. Given this variability, a multi-modality approach is recommended for monitoring recurrence, employing a higher CEA cut-off of 10 ng/mL [41].

The optimal time for monitoring CEA seems controversial, too. Whether bowel preparation has a remarkable influence on CEA levels or not, some studies have made comments on it. A slight increase of serum CEA was observed after bowel preparation, so the variation in CEA level relating to bowel preparation would have a negligible impact on clinical screening results [77]. Concerning postoperative monitoring, CEA measurement was recommended within 4-12 weeks [80] or 1-12 weeks after surgery [81]. A study found that CEA at 2-3 months after surgery behaved best for patients in stage II CRC. The relationship between CEA levels at various perioperative times and RFS was plotted as an upside-down U-curve, which means CEA status was influenced by the time of measurement [82].

The innovative usage of CEA as a tumor marker

Different cutoff levels selected by decision makers may bring different sensitivity and specificity of CEA prediction, and hence uncertainties exist in the clinical performance of CEA. Because the large number of false alarms will generate, it is not a good strategy to improve sensitivity by setting a low threshold. And it is insufficiently sensitive to use CEA alone, as the sensitivity may range from 50% to 80%, and negative predictive value (NPV) was above 80%. To date, a consensual cut-off value has not yet been validated. The CEA-negative tumors account for 49%-75% of CRC, as about 70% of colorectal tumors are mainly composed by CEA-negative cell lines with low or no CEA secretion [83].

Thus, augmenting CEA with other monitoring methods to avoid missed cases is an emerging trend. It is relatively common to combine the CEA test with imaging, and the sensitivity is significantly better when using together compared to CEA alone in resected CRC patients [84]. National comprehensive cancer network (NCCN) advises CRC patients to undergo measurement of CEA and computer tomography (CT) regularly for five years [85]. But in the aspect of survival benefit, the combination of CEA and CT had no advantage [86]. The validity of next-generation sequencing (NGS) in the identification of tumor genomic and transcriptional heterogeneity, as well as the detection of cell-free DNA (cfDNA), improves the development of precision medicine. cfDNA consists of circulating double-stranded DNA fragments primarily derived from normal leukocytes and stromal cells, while the tumor-specific fraction shed from apoptotic or necrotic circulating tumor cells in cancer patients is defined as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) [87]. According to the recent study, although ctDNA methylation alone had good specificity (0.902) for CRC screening, combining it with CEA significantly improves diagnostic performance (AUC from 0.8851 to 0.9269), supporting the superior efficacy of the combined strategy. In addition to ctDNA detection, CTC measurement also serves as a vital component of liquid biopsy. It has been verified that combining CTC counts and CEA levels can achieve higher accuracy in predicting the prognosis of CRC patients. The cutoffs of four CTCs per 2 mL of blood and a serum CEA concentration of 5 ng/mL were adopted [88]. Emerging evidence indicated that CTC clusters could also serve as a valuable biomarker for prognostic evaluation. For patients simultaneously presenting CTCs > 3, CEA > 5 ng/mL, CA19-9 > 37 U/mL and CTC clusters, the recurrence rate was predicted as 100% [89]. Therefore, integrating ctDNA or CTC with CEA offers a more sensitive and reliable approach for CRC patient monitoring than CEA alone or liquid biopsy alone.

The addition of other tumor markers to the CEA assay is also popular. The combination group of differentiation antagonizing non-protein coding RNA (DANCR), CEA and CA19-9 had better sensitivity and negative predictive values (87.5%, 81.5%) than using them alone [66]. It was recommended that dynamic measurements of multiple biomarkers including CEA, CA125, and CA19-9 at the same time should be used to supervise the prognosis of CRC patients through longitudinal trajectories [90]. For those with negative CEA-level baseline, the combination of CEA and CA19-9 could improve the monitoring sensitivity of both overall recurrence and distant recurrence (49.2% vs 41.3%, 50.0% vs 39.6%) [91]. In patients with radically resected CRC who received adjuvant treatment, the elevation of CEA, CA19-9, IL-6, CRP, and chitinase-3-like protein 1 (CHI3L1) combination was 27.3 months before recurrence confirmed by imaging, while CEA was 7.8 months earlier [92]. A novel risk score combining CEA and sICAM-1 serum concentrations was applied to predict the high risk of poor survival in CRC patients [93]. Though peritoneal cytology was the standard for indicating recurrence in peritoneum, the accuracy of predicting local recurrence could acquire improvement through combining positive cytology and high peritoneal CEA level [94,95].

Special combination strategies are also proposed. A CEA-CA19-9 chimeric protein designed through bioinformatics approaches could be used to test autoantibodies generated against tumor-associated antigens. It had a stronger immune reactivity than each of CEA or CA19-9 alone [96]. Increasing evidence illustrated that inflammatory markers are associated with poor prognosis of CRC patients. For example, albumin-to-globulin ratio (AGR), the neutrophil percentage to albumin ratio (NPAR), and Immune-Nutritional Prognostic Ratio (INPR) were proved to be potential prognostic biomarkers in stage I-III CRC [97,98]. Especially, cancer-inflammation prognostic index (CIPI), through multiplying CEA by the values of neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), has been confirmed as an independent factor for non-metastatic prognosis of patients with CRC after curative treatment. The higher CIPI value, the worse RFS and OS [71]. However, the tie-up application is not superior to CEA alone from the economic perspective.

Due to about 50% of CRC patients having no obvious elevation of CEA, it’s necessary to divide patients into groups to determine the surveillance project. Patients getting negative baseline CEA levels at diagnosis were extremely possible to sustain a low serum CEA level until recurrence was found. And additional surveillance methods should be applied for them, such as CA19-9 assay, imaging, and medical visit to avoid the delayed discovery of recurrence. It was advised that the CEA alone is proper to monitor patients with elevated baseline CEA levels, whereas the combined CEA/CA19-9 is appropriate for those with negative baseline CEA levels. Furthermore, the group that has negative baseline CEA and increased CEA with relapse was considered to have an association with aggressive diseases. It indicates a worse outcome and the reason was speculated as cancer evolution [91]. And studies have shown that patients with early‐rising and later‐rising CEA had lower OS and RFS [99]. The familiar assessing way has been used in the NLR method. Researchers stratified patients into three groups depending on the preoperative and postoperative levels of NLR [100]. Thus, the subgroup analysis when using CEA as a tumor marker can’t be neglected and the individualized CEA surveillance strategy is necessary.

There was no inevitable statistical correlation between serum CEA levels and CEA content in carcinoma tissues in some studies [101]. However, tissue CEA also works for diagnosis. Tissue CEA staining was speculated as useful for indicating possible vascular invasion even at an early stage. As larger tumors invade vascular or metastasis, elevated serum CEA levels may be detectable. Organ-specific immunostaining profiles with high sensitivity, specificity, and positive predictive value could detect primary adenocarcinomas, and CEA were enrolled. It contributed to identifying primary tumors in patients with metastatic cancer, which is important, and difficult. Stained tissue CEA has been proven to be an independent prognostic factor for gastric cancer. Among intensely positive, moderate, and negative stained-tissue, the 5-year survival rates were 40.1, 53.9 and 67.6% respectively [102]. The application of tissue CEA may be a great candidate for predicting survival in CRC.

Conclusions and perspectives

We discussed the current applied significance and strategy of CEA as a tumor marker in CRC, resulting from its value in incomplete unity. Though it is not recommended to use alone in primary screening, it has played an important role in predicting survival rate and monitoring recurrence. Multiple analyses have proved it as an independent prognostic factor in CRC. CEA has been regarded as a marker of tumor burden that can reflect the biological activity of the tumor. Its significance is different from what imaging can assess including tumor bulk or number and it can’t be replaced. CEA can monitor micro-metastasis and detect hidden metastases that are not obvious on conventional imaging, especially in hepatic metastasis [103]. As a classical and widely-used biomarker in the hospital, CEA is worthy to be explored for more effective usage and made full of its value.

Rather than serving as a one-off diagnostic test, CEA is employed as a monitoring method. The perioperative CEA trajectory is more instructive instead of preoperative CEA [99,104]. Researchers have employed CEA kinetic instead of CEA baseline and confirmed CEA kinetic more validated as a prognostic marker. The threshold of the CEA slope was 0.05 and the survival rate is lower when surpassing 0.05 in this prospective study [105]. Notably, analyzing CEA from the aspect of the genome is prevalent and more accurate. CEA mRNA becomes more and more attractive as a biomarker and reverse transcription associated technology was functional CEA mRNA can be detected by reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR). The positive incidence is higher for CEA mRNA than serum CEA level in CRC patients. Through monitoring before and after surgery, some patients with metastatic diseases eventually were positive in CEA mRNA and negative in serum markers [106]. Positive CEA mRNA was found to have a highly overall recurrence rate in stage II and III CRC [107]. Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) technology, achieving quantitative analysis has been widely used. The level of serum CEA mRNA detected through qRT-PCR was positively correlated with the TNM stage, and it was higher in patients with stage lll and lV than those in stage l and ll [108]. What’s more, CEA gene quantification in blood also help to conquer the difficulties of CTC detection, such as six-gene assay and CEA gene assay [109]. A combination of CEA mRNA and CTC had better performance than CTC alone in predicting postoperative recurrence of digestive tract cancer [108]. New biomarker patterns established by genomic or proteomic techniques will enable a more reliable diagnosis of CRC. However, Current mRNA detection methods usually require expensive machinery, strict preservation environments, and time-consuming processes to guarantee detection sensitivity, specificity, and stability. Variability exists in blood collection, RNA extraction, reagents and primer design, leading to significant inter-laboratory discrepancies [110]. The choice of internal control genes also remains inconsistent. The lack of standardization of RT-PCR techniques across institutions is a major barrier to the clinical application of CEA mRNA detection. Despite the current challenges, the continued evolution of liquid biopsy and CEA-related genetic testing holds the promise of guiding traditional methods into a new and more precise future.
