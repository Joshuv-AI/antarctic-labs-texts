Clinical and radiographic outcomes of anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) with a 3D-printed porous titanium intervertebral cage
Authors: J. Alex Thomas, Neil A. Tayyab, Kaveh Khajavi, R. Alden Milam, Jeremy Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe availability of evidence supporting the use of 3D-printed porous titanium interbody implants for lumbar spinal fusion varies by procedure and implant design, with relatively few reports to-date of patient outcomes in anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF). This retrospective study aims to investigate the clinical and radiographic outcomes of an interfixated 3D-printed, porous titanium ALIF implant used to treat DDD, spondylolisthesis, degenerative scoliosis, and stenosis conditions.MethodsA multicenter retrospective chart review of all patients who underwent ALIF surgery with the 3D-printed, porous titanium ALIF intervertebral cage for degenerative pathologies of the lumbar spine from 2020 to 2023 was performed. Patient demographics, surgical details, fusion status, complications data, and visual analog scale (VAS) back pain were collected and evaluated from all available patient records.ResultsA total of 114 patients were included with a total of 136 levels treated with the 3D-printed porous titanium ALIF cage. Patients were followed for an average of 19.7 months. The fusion rates of patients with assessable imaging data by 12 and 24 months postoperative were 99.0% (n = 100) and 98.2% (n = 110). There were 6 device-related complications in 6 patients (5.3%), with 4 interbody device-related complications (3.5%) attributable to loosening of interfixated screws (1.8%) and/or cage subsidence (2.6%), with one removal of the ALIF device (0.9%). At 6-month and 12-month follow-up, VAS back pain was improved by 56% (n = 43) and 41% (n = 24), respectively as compared to the preoperative VAS back pain scores.ConclusionsThe interfixated 3D-printed titanium ALIF cage resulted in a high fusion rate, significantly improved back pain, and low rates of device-related complications in a real-world, multicenter patient series. Future prospective, comparative studies are warranted to assess the relative safety, efficacy and value of differing cage designs.

Introduction

Spinal fusion is a surgical treatment option for lumbar degenerative disc disease (DDD), stenosis, spondylolisthesis, and degenerative scoliosis. Anterior lumbar interbody fusion (ALIF) is one approach to spinal fusion that allows for complete discectomy, avoids dissection of the paraspinal musculature, and provides greater improvement in radiologic outcomes compared to other spinal interbody fusion techniques [1–3]. The ALIF approach allows for the use of large intervertebral body spacers, which generally feature a large footprint, large grafting windows, various height options to indirectly decompress nerves through restoration of disc height, and a range of lordosis angle options for correcting sagittal deformities.

There are many intervertebral body spacer options available, with notable differences in design, materials, and manufacturing methods. Structural bone allografts, such as femoral rings, and autograft wedges harvested from the patient’s iliac crest are traditional options offering good biological incorporation but carry the risks of disease transmission or donor-site morbidity, respectively [4]. Spacers made of bone also tend to be limited in their sizes and lack compatibility with integrated fixation due to the natural geometry and strength of the source bones. Synthetic spacers have commonly been machined from solid titanium, or titanium alloy, and polyether ether ketone (PEEK). Based on synthetic material choice and overall spacer design, these implants may vary in biomechanical properties and consequently influence stability, load sharing, and the risk of subsidence [5]. Moreover, neither material is particularly conducive to bone in-growth or the development of strong osseointegration at the bone-implant interface, which puts the onus on the accompanying bone graft filler to drive fusion between vertebrae [6–8].

Porous titanium intervertebral fusion devices built by additive manufacturing methods such as direct metal laser sintering or electron beam melting represent some of the most recent innovations in spinal implant design and manufacturing in recent years. These innovative 3D-printed fusion devices allow for the design and production of titanium alloy structures which can target specific criteria for strength, modulus of elasticity, porosity, or surface roughness [9, 10]. Porous titanium implants have been observed in preclinical testing to improve osseointegration and reduce the risks of subsidence and implant expulsion compared to traditionally made implants of solid titanium or PEEK [6, 11, 12].

Different design approaches are reflected in the variety of structures seen in 3D-printed titanium interbody implants. Currently available implant designs incorporate porous lattices varying in size, geometry, and position within the implant, and some designs include integrated fixation components for enhanced stability. These variations raise questions about the collective assessment of 3D-printed implants and generalization of findings, since they may differ in biological and biomechanical properties and perhaps even clinical outcomes [12, 13]. One specific approach to 3D-printed cage design consists of the combination of a computationally stress-optimized body lattice and microporous endplates [10, 13]. To-date, radiographic fusion and patient reported outcomes have been evaluated in patients treated with this type of porous titanium cage in lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF) and, more recently, ALIF [14–18]. However, the total number of evaluable patients across the ALIF studies remains small (< 100 patients) compared to those treated with LLIF.

There is a need to better understand and document the safety and performance of specific 3D-printed implant designs in selected spinal procedures to guide clinical decision making between various design options. Additionally, clinical outcomes data are relevant to understanding the performance and value of 3D-printed implants compared to more well-established implant technologies like subtractively manufactured solid PEEK and titanium cages. This retrospective chart review aims to investigate the clinical and radiographic outcomes of a 3D-printed, porous titanium ALIF intervertebral spacer used to treat DDD, spondylolisthesis, degenerative scoliosis, and stenosis conditions.

Methods

A multicenter retrospective chart review was undertaken of patients who underwent ALIF surgery between 2020 and 2023 with an interfixated 3D-printed, porous titanium ALIF intervertebral spacer (Modulus® ALIF, NuVasive, Inc. San Diego, CA) for degenerative pathologies of the lumbar spine. The study was designed as an on-label, real-world evidence study using data previously collected and documented per the standard of care of each participating surgeon. The primary outcomes of interest were interbody fusion rates and device-related complications. Data were extracted from patient records according to an IRB-exempted protocol from five participating centers. Inclusion in the study required patients who: (1) were ≥ 18 years of age at time of surgery; (2) had a regimen of at least six months of nonoperative care prior to surgery; (3) were treated with the Modulus ALIF spacer with supplemental fixation at 1–2 adjacent levels for DDD, spondylolisthesis, or spinal stenosis or any number of levels for degenerative scoliosis or sagittal deformity; and (4) had ≥ 6 months postoperative clinical and radiographic follow-up. Patients treated with bone graft materials, including recombinant human bone morphogenetic protein-2 (rhBMP-2), that have not been cleared or approved by the FDA for use in the specified interbody cage were excluded. Patient demographics, surgical details, radiographs and/or CT scans, visual analog scale (VAS) back pain, and adverse event data were collected from all available patient records.

For each radiographic imaging follow-up available, fusion status was evaluated by the treating surgeon using predefined criteria. Radiographic fusion was defined as the presence of continuous bridging bone from endplate to endplate across the disc space, anterior to, posterior to, lateral to, and/or through the interbody implant on AP/lateral lumbar radiographs and/or CT scan, with the additional criteria of absence of a radiolucency > 2 mm thick at > 50% of the bone-implant interface for those patients without CT scan imaging available [19, 20]. For those patients with flexion/extension lateral radiographs, fusion additionally required no discernible motion (i.e., less than 5° of angular motion at the assessed level) [20]. All ALIF levels were required to be fused for the subject to be considered fused.

The analysis population included all subjects who satisfied the inclusion and exclusion criteria and previously underwent the ALIF procedure as previously described. A paired t-test with an alpha of 0.05 was used to evaluate the statistical significance between preoperative and postoperative VAS back pain. Counts of subjects fused using the latest imaging and the corresponding percentages per total subjects with assessable radiography by 6, 12, and 24 months were calculated.

Patient demographics

Patients were treated with ALIF at 136 spinal levels across 114 patients with L5-S1 as the most commonly treated (66.2% of levels). Ninety-two (80.7%) patients were treated at one level, and the remaining patients were treated at two contiguous levels. Patient positioning for the ALIF procedure was closely split between supine (50.9%) and lateral decubitus (49.1%). The median ALIF cage dimensions included a nominal height of 8 mm (range 6–10 mm), width of 38 mm (range 34–42 mm), length of 28 mm (range 24–32 mm), and lordosis of 15 degrees (range 10–25 degrees). Fifty-one adverse events were documented in the medical records of 35 patients (30.7%). There were six interbody or fixation device-related complications in six patients (5.3%), including screw loosening (n = 2 for interfixated screws and n = 2 for pedicle screws) and subsidence (n = 3). Of these complications, one patient who developed subsidence and loose interfixated screws secondary to a wound infection underwent device removal and conversion to a corpectomy with extended posterior instrumentation at 6 months. Two patients had revision of posterior instrumentation. There were 26 procedure-related adverse events in 22 patients including new radiculopathy/neuropathy (n = 10), new back pain (n = 5), and adjacent level degeneration (n = 3). Discussion

The objective of this study was to assess the fusion rates and complications associated with the use of a 3D-printed titanium cage in ALIF, given few prior publications of results of this practice. The characteristics of the 3D-printed titanium ALIF cage used by investigators in this study reflect recent engineering innovations in spinal implant design. The ALIF cage incorporates the specific design elements of a novel, algorithmically designed, stress-optimized body lattice, microporous endplates, and integrated fixation. By combining iterative improvements in implant biomechanical properties, porosity, and surface texture, this approach to 3D-printed titanium implant design has been shown through preclinical benchtop and animal testing to yield improvements in stress distribution across vertebrae, bony in-growth, and osseointegration strength relative to traditional solid titanium and PEEK implants [6]. Furthermore, robust fusion through the porous lattice structures has been observed in an ovine models of spinal interbody fusion and in clinical implant retrievals with 3D-printed titanium cages [7, 8, 10, 12, 21].

A key finding of the current ALIF study was the high rate of fusion (99.0% and 98.2% by one and two years, respectively) with 3D-printed porous titanium cages which is consistent with the preclinical findings of enhanced bone conduction and implant stability with similar implants. Radiographic fusion performance was largely equivalent across clinical sites and across the patient cohort whose breadth included various real-world demographics, indications, and case complexity. The fusion results reinforce those previously reported for two patient cohorts receiving the identical ALIF cage which ranged from 88.4% when used in a standalone interfixated manner to 97.3% when used with supplemental posterior fixation at 1-year with CT-based assessments [14, 15]. Additionally, the ALIF results are comparable to those achieved in LLIF cohorts using a similarly designed 3D-printed porous titanium cage from the same implant family. Maalouly et al. reported a fusion rate of 88% at 6 months by CT when using the Modulus XLIF cage filled with a synthetic bone substitute (Attrax Putty, NuVasive, Inc.) in anterior-to-the-psoas LLIF [16], while Malone, et al., reported a 98% fusion rate at 12 months by CT for the same implant and graft combination in transpsoas LLIF [17].

When assessed, the patient reported VAS back pain improved substantially by 6-months postoperative, and that improvement was maintained through one year postoperative similar to a previous report of lowered pain scores with use of this ALIF cage [15]. Together with the radiographic fusion results, these outcomes suggest that 3D-printed, porous titanium cages can be used effectively in ALIF operations to achieve surgical goals of relieving pain and creating stability through at least one year postoperative. This conclusion is further supported by the reported safety outcomes in this study, in which there was an overall interbody device-related complication rate of 3.5% (4 out of 114 patients) attributable to loosening of interfixated screws and/or cage subsidence and a device removal rate of 0.9% (1 out of 114 patients). In prior studies of this ALIF cage with a minimum of 1-year follow-up, the revision rate for cage subsidence, migration, or pseudarthrosis has been reported as 0% to 3.8% [14, 15]. These findings also build upon those of Corso, et al., who reported no early revisions through 6 months in 93 patients treated at one lumbar level with an alternative 3D-printed porous titanium cage design, of which 27% were ALIF or LLIF procedures [22].

Implant subsidence is a potential complication of lumbar interbody fusion which can reduce the effectiveness of neural decompression and result in loss of stability or correction of spinal alignment. Risks for subsidence are multifactorial and include aspects of surgical technique, patient characteristics, and implant design [23]. In recent studies of LLIF, 3D-printed, porous titanium cages were associated with significantly lower rates of subsidence versus solid PEEK cages and a significantly lower revision rate for subsidence in one study [24, 25]. Ohiorhenuan et al. attributed this difference in subsidence between cage types to the ratio of cage to vertebral endplate surface area, which was the only significant predictor of subsidence in their multivariate analysis [25]. This finding is further reinforced by benchtop mechanical testing of representative implants from the same 3D-printed titanium and PEEK cage systems which demonstrated more evenly distributed endplate pressures and lower subsidence displacements with the 3D-printed titanium implants [6]. The 3D-printed titanium ALIF cage shares design similarities with its previously studied LLIF counterpart, including a large footprint and high microporous endplate surface area. These features may help reduce the risk of implant subsidence and account for the low subsidence rate reported in this study of 2.6%. Comparatively, the subsidence rate is lower than the range (6%—23.1%) for ALIF with various PEEK cages as reported in a recent systematic review [26], but it is in line with those of studies investigating 3D-printed titanium cages in lumbar interbody fusion, including the 3.8% subsidence rate reported for the identical ALIF cage by Ajello et al. [11, 15, 27].

The synergistic combination of cage technology and bone grafting material has received renewed interest with the availability of newer material technologies and evidence. Clinical selection of these implant combinations is likely to influence not only the safety and performance outcomes for patients but also the economic outcomes for spinal fusion surgery across the healthcare landscape. In this study, patients were excluded from analysis if they received rhBMP-2, which is not indicated for use in this 3D-printed ALIF cage in the US. Regardless, the fusion outcomes with these cages support their use with alternative autogenous, allogeneic, or synthetic bone grafting materials which are widely recognized as lower-cost options compared to rhBMP-2. Malone, et al., reported that their outcomes with 3D-printed porous titanium LLIF cages and a low-cost synthetic bone substitute may justify the elimination of expensive biologics, including rhBMP-2, in the majority of cases [17]. The current study supports the expansion of such principles to the setting of ALIF, though additional comparative studies and economic value analyses may further strengthen this conclusion.

There are several limitations to this study which largely result from the retrospective and non-comparative study design. While the multicenter approach to the study helps ensure generalizability of the findings, the standard follow-up practices of the investigators were not standardized with respect to the collection of radiographic imaging and patient reported outcomes. Radiographic follow-up of at least 6 months was required for subject inclusion, but available patient reported outcomes were limited to only VAS back pain for a subset of subjects. Uniform radiographic imaging modalities were not used across sites nor centrally and independently reviewed. This was mitigated in part by the investigators’ use of shared definitions for fusion which incorporated the most commonly used criteria as reported in a systematic review of 442 published spinal fusion studies [20]. Overall, the data collected in this study are similar to those found in clinical registries and reflect real-world evidence collected by individual healthcare providers and collated across sites. Furthermore, the study results are contextualized with comparisons to relevant published outcomes from other studies since no comparative arm was evaluated in this study.

Future opportunities abound for prospective studies to address these limitations through predetermined follow-up intervals and assessments. The inclusion of control groups within prospective study designs would further enable higher quality comparisons to either traditional cages or alternative 3D-printed cages to address key questions related to their relative safety, performance, and economic value. Notably, prospective studies comparing 3D-printed titanium cages to alternative cage materials and designs in lumbar interbody fusion are in progress with participation by several of the authors. In review, the study limitations are balanced by the need for timely evidence of the clinical performance of 3D-printed porous titanium cages in ALIF, which is sparsely reported in the current literature.

Conclusion

In this multicenter study, patients treated with an interfixated 3D-printed titanium ALIF cage consisting of a stress-optimized body lattice and microporous endplates demonstrated a high rate of fusion, significantly improved patient-reported back pain, and low rates of device-related complications.
