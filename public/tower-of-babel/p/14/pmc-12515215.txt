Methamphetamine Pharmacotherapy: A Need to Re-focus on the Complex Neurobiological Changes that Occur Both During and After Methamphetamine Use Disorder
Authors: Rebecca McKetin, Jee Hyun Kim, Alyna Turner, Michael Berk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The aim of this article is to review the neurobiological changes associated with methamphetamine use disorder (MUD) and consider what implications they have for the development of effective pharmacotherapies. Novel pharmacotherapies are urgently needed to address the complex neurobiological changes that occur both during and after MUD. Current approaches are modelled on maintenance therapies for opioid use, particularly with the use of prescription stimulant medications to substitute for illicit methamphetamine. Existing evidence suggests that these have limited effectiveness, and enthusiasm has been dampened by concerns about the risk of toxicity. We identify an opportunity to look beyond maintenance therapies and to consider alternative models of pharmacotherapy that support the initiation and maintenance of abstinence. In doing this, we highlight the complexity of the neurobiological changes that occur both during and after cessation of methamphetamine use. We identify a need for pharmacotherapies that can address the lasting neurobiological changes from long-term methamphetamine use to improve treatment engagement and retention as well as functional recovery and to reduce relapse risk.

Introduction

Illicit methamphetamine and amphetamine (hereafter collectively referred to as methamphetamine) are highly addictive stimulant drugs used by an estimated 30 million people globally [1]. Around 11% of people who try methamphetamine develop a methamphetamine use disorder (MUD), totalling an estimated 7.4 million people worldwide [2]. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, MUD includes symptoms of craving, an inability to curtail use despite negative consequences, continuing to use methamphetamine at the expense of other activities, the development of tolerance and experiencing a withdrawal syndrome on abrupt cessation of use [3]. MUD is a chronic relapsing condition associated with an elevated risk of suicide, homicide, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) and other blood-borne virus infection, accidental injuries, psychosis, depression and death [2].

Effective treatments are essential to mitigate the adverse health outcomes associated with MUD. Treatment currently relies entirely on psychosocial approaches, such as counselling and therapeutic communities. Successful engagement with these psychosocial therapies is challenged by the neuropsychiatric and cognitive changes experienced by people with MUD (e.g. deficits in impulsivity, social cognition and reward processing [4, 5]). Psychosocial treatments also carry substantial access, cost and scale capacity constraints, meaning that most people in need do not receive minimally adequate treatment [6, 7].

Pharmacotherapies provide the theoretical potential to improve treatment coverage and ameliorate aspects of MUD that hinder treatment engagement and retention (e.g. alleviate withdrawal symptoms, reduce craving and improve cognitive function to engage in psychological therapies) [8]. Despite this potential role, no medications have yet been approved by regulatory authorities (e.g. the Food and Drug Administration [FDA]) for MUD. Of the 23 individual pharmacotherapies that have been trialled [9], none have yet provided clear and replicable signals.

In this paper, we overview existing approaches to pharmacotherapy development for MUD and their shortcomings. We then consider the potential role for pharmacotherapeutic interventions to respond to the key clinical challenges in treating MUD. In doing this, we identify an opportunity for the development of pharmacotherapies to manage both withdrawal from methamphetamine and the longer-term neurobiological sequalae associated with MUD. Addressing these issues holds great potential to enhance treatment engagement and retention, improve post-treatment functioning and prevent relapse.

The Current Focus on ‘Maintenance’ Therapies and Repurposing Medications

To date, the focus of pharmacotherapy research has been on the use of prescription stimulant medications that substitute or replace the pharmacological effect of methamphetamine, particularly dextroamphetamine (the active enantiomer of amphetamine) and methylphenidate, which have a similar mechanism of action to methamphetamine (increasing catecholamine activity via interaction with the dopamine transporter mechanism [10]). These substitution or replacement medications are modelled off of the success of opioid maintenance therapies, such as methadone and buprenorphine, for opioid use disorders [11]. Opioid maintenance pharmacotherapies minimise opioid withdrawal and reduce the reinforcing effects of heroin, leading to a reduction in the use of illicit heroin [12, 13] and harms associated with illicit drug use (e.g. HIV risk from injecting drugs and involvement in the illicit drug market) [11].

The use of prescription stimulant medication as a maintenance therapy for MUD has received a corresponding groundswell of support from researchers and clinicians [14]. However, it is not without critics [15–17]. One of the primary criticisms is the lack of compelling evidence for effectiveness. Although most trials marginally favour the use of prescription stimulants over placebo, with potential benefits apparent for both reducing craving for methamphetamine and reducing methamphetamine use, the small scale of trials, low medication adherence and high dropout make it difficult to confirm these benefits or attendant risks. A meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials (seven of methylphenidate and three of dexamphetamine) suggests a potentially small (7%) non-statistically significant reduction in methamphetamine use [18]. Findings suggested that higher doses may yield more robust benefits. A recent trial using high doses of the long-acting amphetamine analogue, lisdexamfetamine, yielded similarly modest outcomes (2.2 days of use over 28 days after 12 weeks of treatment) [19]. There are also concerns about toxicity if prescription stimulants are co-consumed with illicit methamphetamine (i.e. neurotoxicity, cardiovascular toxicity and adverse psychiatric reactions), particularly with the use of high-dose preparations [16, 20–22].

Most other candidates are re-purposed psychiatric medications. These have also been trialled as maintenance therapies. There is very low-quality evidence that bupropion, an antidepressant and antismoking agent that inhibits catecholamine re-uptake, may produce small reductions in methamphetamine use and craving [23]. There is stronger evidence that the tetra-cyclic antidepressant mirtazapine probably reduces methamphetamine use, but, similar to bupropion, treatment effects are small [24, 25]. The μ-opioid receptor antagonist naltrexone has been trialled for MUD on the premise that it will diminish the rewarding properties of methamphetamine [26]. Available evidence suggests that it does not reduce methamphetamine use or craving [27]. However, a single large trial has found that combining extended-release injectable naltrexone with oral extended-release bupropion produced a modest but significant reduction in methamphetamine use [28]. The cystine/glutamate exchange transporter system xc has been targeted using the precursor of the antioxidant glutathione N-acetylcysteine, with disappointing results [29]. Collectively, evidence to date suggests that the benefits of these alternative maintenance pharmacotherapies are likely to be modest, as with prescription stimulant therapy.

A challenge in this area of research is that these maintenance therapy medications are not tailored to the pharmacology of methamphetamine (Fig. 1) or the neurobiological changes seen with MUD (Box 1 and 2). Successful maintenance pharmacotherapies for other substance use disorders limit the rewarding properties of their target drug and mitigate against the risk of toxicity. For example, the opioid maintenance pharmacotherapies buprenorphine and methadone have complex pharmacological profiles at opioid receptors that reduce the reinforcing effects of heroin and reduce the risk of opioid overdose [30, 31]. Pharmacotherapies for tobacco cessation similarly reduce the reinforcing effects of tobacco (e.g. varenicline [32] and bupropion [33]) while maintaining a dopamine level sufficient to ameliorate withdrawal symptoms and craving [34]. By contrast, the most trialled prescription stimulant for MUD, methylphenidate, does not attenuate the rewarding properties of methamphetamine [35, 36] or reduce the severity of withdrawal symptoms [18]. With the exception of a potential protective effect on neurotoxicity [37], the action of methylphenidate does not protect against toxicity if co-consumed with illicit methamphetamine [20–22, 38]. Opportunities for Pharmacotherapies to Address MUD Withdrawal and Relapse Risk

In addition to the development of maintenance therapies for MUD, opportunities exist to consider pharmacotherapies that address acute withdrawal from methamphetamine and the more protracted neurobiological changes that can persist for weeks to months thereafter. Pharmacotherapies that target these more protracted changes have the potential to greatly improve both treatment retention and functional outcomes during recovery. These are discussed below.

Detoxification and the Management of Acute MUD Withdrawal Symptoms

The level of tolerance that can develop to methamphetamine, and the severity of the withdrawal syndrome that occurs following abrupt cessation of heavy use, is an important and underappreciated aspect of methamphetamine use disorder that implies a need for pharmacological management. This state involves severe mood disturbances (including negative affect and anhedonia), fatigue, paranoia, appetite and sleep disturbances and severe craving for methamphetamine [39, 40]. These symptoms are an important catalyst in the resumption of use [41, 42], undermining the success of completing detoxification, which, in practice, is often a necessary antecedent to engaging in psychosocial treatment options, such as residential rehabilitation or counselling, that facilitate longer-term recovery.

There are currently no medications that are specifically indicated for the treatment of methamphetamine withdrawal [43], leaving management to be on a symptomatic basis (e.g. using benzodiazepines, antipsychotics or antidepressant drugs to manage withdrawal symptoms). Compounds that have been trialled include mirtazapine, modafinil, ibudilast, amantadine, varenicline and amineptine [43]. Only amineptine, a dopamine reuptake inhibitor, has shown significant benefit, although the evidence supporting this benefit is of low to very low quality [43]. Tapering of prescription stimulants (dexamphetamine) has been piloted and found to alleviate craving relative to placebo during withdrawal, without clear signals for other withdrawal symptoms [44]. More recently, an open-label pilot has found tapering from a high dose of the long-acting lisdexamfetamine, a precursor of dextroamphetamine, to be feasible and well-tolerated [45]. A controlled trial is now underway (ACTRN12624001061527). Aside from this very recent effort, trials of pharmacotherapies for methamphetamine withdrawal have stalled during the past decade [43].

More vigorous research attention is needed to identify neurobiological targets for treating methamphetamine withdrawal. In addition to managing changes in dopamine function during acute withdrawal (Box 1), potential candidate pharmacotherapies could also target brain corticotrophin-releasing factor (CRF) systems, which mediate a stress response in substance use disorders [46, 47]. These are triggered in methamphetamine withdrawal in animal models and activate sympathetic pathways in the heart [48]. CRF activation in the amygdala has also been found to be involved in reinstatement of methamphetamine seeking in response to stress in animal models [49]. Another potential target is the dynorphin-κ opioid aversive system in the ventral striatum and extended amygdala which is activated during drug withdrawal [46]. Evidence for this system’s involvement in drug withdrawal derives mostly from opioid studies [46], although there is some evidence of opioid receptor dysfunction in the amygdala in animal models of methamphetamine withdrawal [50] which occurs alongside changes in oxytocin function [50]. These systems are thought to be important in mediating the dysphoria experienced during withdrawal, and their effective management may improve retention in withdrawal management and the successful completion of detoxification.

The Post-Acute Withdrawal Syndrome and the Protracted Down-Regulation of Monoamine Function

An additional knowledge gap is how to manage the more protracted sequalae that follow acute withdrawal from methamphetamine. Withdrawal trials are brief (range 5–28 days, with most lasting 1–2 weeks [43]). By contrast, the neurobiological adaptations that are apparent following abrupt cessation of heavy methamphetamine use suggest a far more protracted sequalae that would benefit from pharmacological management (Box 1). The downregulation of dopamine function seen in this context appears to last at least weeks if not months [51]. Mood and cognitive disturbances may be similarly protracted [51, 52], both of which can be a catalyst for relapse. Longer-term pharmacotherapy could play an important role in managing affect and reducing the risk of relapse during the months following withdrawal from methamphetamine.

To date, there has been little if any attention paid to pharmacotherapy options that could ameliorate longer-term monoamine down-regulation that occurs for the months after withdrawal from methamphetamine. Existing maintenance pharmacotherapy options that interact with catecholamine transporter receptors, such as methylphenidate and modafinil, may be able to play a role in restoring functional deficits in the absence of ongoing methamphetamine use. Management of craving is also critical because it can trigger relapse, as discussed in the next section.

Despite the prevalence of methamphetamine use, molecular research in neuroadaptations due to chronic methamphetamine use lags behind other illicit substances [55], and what we know largely focusses on regional activities in the brain [56–58]. Withdrawal from methamphetamine use is accompanied by an overall reduction in dopamine 2 and dopamine 3 receptor availability [59], a reduction in dopamine transporter density (DAT) in multiple regions (striatum [60], amygdala [52], the dorsolateral and orbitofrontal prefrontal cortex [52] and the orbitofrontal cortex [52]), and a reduction in 5-HT transporters (e.g. midbrain, thalamus, caudate, putamen, cerebral cortex and cerebellum [61]). These neural changes have been correlated with functional decrements, including dysphoria during withdrawal [62, 63], impairments in vigilance [64] and motor function [60], psychiatric symptoms [52] and aggression [61]. During the early phases of withdrawal, changes in cerebral glucose metabolism can also been seen, implicating brain regions involved in mood disorders and negative affect (perigenual anterior cingulate gyrus and amygdala) [65]. During the later weeks of abstinence, there are shifts in cerebral glucose metabolism that extend beyond the ventral striatal areas implicated in addiction (e.g. the right parietal lobe and amygdala and the dorsal striatum). These changes are positively correlated with the severity of self-reported depressive symptoms [66]. Several neurobiological changes (e.g. DAT density) appear to show some recovery with longer-term abstinence (12–17 months [51]), suggesting the potential for at least partial recovery, although functional impairments appear not to improve to the same extent [51].

Reducing Craving to Reduce Relapse Risk

Although many people can withdraw from methamphetamine use, few maintain abstinence. Relapse rates following treatment are discouragingly high (e.g. 61–80% of people relapse to use within 1 year of treatment [62, 67, 68]). Relapse is triggered by cravings for methamphetamine which are most intense during the later phases of acute withdrawal [39, 40]. Although cravings diminish over time, cues related to drug use can continue to induce strong cravings for months after withdrawal [69, 70], triggering relapse even after extended periods of abstinence [71]. Pharmacotherapies that reduce craving for methamphetamine are therefore essential.

Craving for methamphetamine has a different pharmacological basis to the rewarding effects of acute methamphetamine use (Box 2). While the immediate rewarding effects of methamphetamine intoxication are thought to be mediated by its ability to increase dopamine activity in brain regions critical to learning about reward (i.e. dopamine projections into nucleus accumbens and surrounding ventrostriatal regions), the development of craving and drug-seeking is a learned response that is underpinned by neuroplastic changes (Box 2). These neuroplastic changes are thought to be mediated by changes in glutamate receptor sensitivity [72, 73] which modulate dopamine firing in the ventral tegmental area and the nucleus accumbens region [62].

Human imaging studies have been additionally informative in identifying a neural signature for craving which shares commonalities across drug types and with natural rewards such as food [63, 74, 75]. Koban et al. used machine learning to identify what they refer to as a ‘neurobiological craving signature’, which involves activation of the ventromedial prefrontal and cingulate cortices, ventral striatum, temporal/parietal association areas, mediodorsal thalamus and cerebellum [75]. Although several of these regions have been long-implicated in addiction [47], others have received relatively less attention (e.g. cerebellum and temporal and parietal areas) [76]. Importantly, areas of the brain involved in craving appeared to be similar across drug types and to overlap considerably with those involved in food reward, suggesting that drug craving depends on brain systems integral to primary reward processes.

The commonality between reward systems involved in drug addiction and natural rewards has led to a search for novel agents that can modify these macro-level brain reward systems. This has included recent attention on the use of glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor antagonists, which are used to treat obesity, as a potential modulator of dopaminergic reward processes in substance use disorders [77]. Benefits have been found for alcohol use disorder [78], but this approach has not yet been tried for MUD [77]. In a similar vein, the sigma-1 receptor system has been targeted [79], with this system interacting with dopamine 1 and 2 receptors; potential benefits are apparent for both excessive consumption of food and drugs [80].

The identification of pharmacotherapies that can reverse the neuroplastic changes that underpin craving is another area that is ripe for development [72, 73, 81]. Attempts to modulate glutamate regulation to restore homeostasis have yielded some success in reducing drug craving [82], including for methamphetamine [83], but have failed to reduce methamphetamine use in people with MUD who are actively using the drug [29]. Another agent that targets methamphetamine-induced neuroplasticity is the molecular adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase) motor, non-muscle myosin II [79], which has been found to reduce methamphetamine-seeking in preclinical models [84] and is currently under development [79].

Acute reinforcement from methamphetamine is thought to be due to excess dopamine activity, particularly in the nucleus accumbens. This excess activity occurs through multiple mechanisms, the net effect of which is the accumulation of dopamine in the synaptic cleft (Fig. 1), signalling reward by shifting dopamine firing from tonic to phasic via the relative activation of D1 over D2 receptors [62]. Similar dopamine firing patterns accrue to environmental stimuli associated with drugs (i.e. drug cues), eliciting drug craving and drug seeking [62]. Craving is extinguished when dopamine firing patterns triggered by environmental drug cues are not accompanied by the expected reward (i.e. the drug) [85]. These processes are mediated by neuroplastic changes that are similar to synaptic changes associated with learning and include changes in dendritic morphology, epigenetic modification, ionotropic glutamate receptors (predominantly AMPA and N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptors) that result in long-term potentiation and long-term depression [62, 86]. These dynamics are reflected at a macro level by a relative shift in activity in different neural systems [74], with craving involving the activation of the ventromedial prefrontal and cingulate cortices, ventral striatum, temporal/parietal association areas, mediodorsal thalamus and cerebellum [75]. Importantly, areas of the brain involved in craving appear to be similar across drug types and overlap considerably with those involved in food reward, suggesting that drug craving depends on brain systems integral to primary reward processes [76].

Neuro-inflammation, Neurotoxicity and Cognitive Impairment—A Longer-Term Scenario

In addition to the development of addiction, long-term methamphetamine use drives multiple pathways including inflammation, oxidative stress and apoptosis that in concert lead to neurotoxicity (Box 3). These neurotoxic pathways are all associated with structural brain changes, particularly in regional grey matter. People who use methamphetamine display reductions in volumes of the left superior temporal gyrus and the right inferior parietal lobe. With longer use of the drug, volume reductions are seen in the left precentral gyrus and the right supramarginal gyrus [87]. Pathological changes reflecting neuroinflammation, oxidative stress and apoptosis have also been found in the post-mortem brain tissue of people who have a history of chronic methamphetamine use [88–90]. Structural brain changes are clinically manifested by cognitive impairment, which is a well-documented feature of long-term use [4].

These longer-term brain changes are likely to impact not only treatment engagement and retention [91] but also functional recovery following long-term methamphetamine use [92–94]. Agents that have demonstrated cognitive enhancing capacity in other psychiatric disorders (e.g. methylphenidate, modafinil and vortioxetine) may be useful in this context, although few have been tested for their ability to relieve cognitive deficits in people with MUD [8]. To date, two studies suggest that modafinil may be able to improve executive function and memory in people with methamphetamine use disorder [95, 96], while appreciating that the evidence does not support modafinil as a pharmacotherapy to reduce methamphetamine use per se [97]. We hope to see more pharmacotherapy clinical trials in methamphetamine use disorder that examine cognition and quality of life.

Methamphetamine increases several proinflammatory factors including interleukin (IL)-6, interferon (INF)-γ, tumour necrosis factor (TNF)-α, and nuclear factor (NF)-κB [98]. It also compromises the blood–brain barrier, impairing brain plasticity and driving neurotoxicity. It similarly compromises the gut–brain barrier and alters the microbiome, which operates in tight concert with the immune system [99]. Methamphetamine use is also associated with oxidative stress, indexed by total antioxidant power measured via the ferric reducing ability of plasma assay and increased lipid peroxidation measured via serum level of malondialdehyde [100]. Several oxidative changes persist in the withdrawal period [101]. These include higher malondialdehyde levels, lower superoxide dismutase activity as well as higher catalase activity and glutathione levels. The net result of these cascades is apoptosis [102]. This is effected by endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria-dependent death cascades.

Implications for Future Research

We have explored opportunities to develop pharmacotherapies that go beyond maintenance therapy to address the management of acute withdrawal and the more protracted changes seen following acute withdrawal which hinder treatment engagement, increase the risk of relapse and undermine functional recovery (e.g. down-regulation of monoamine function, brain inflammation, oxidative stress, neurotoxicity and longer-term neuroplastic changes that mediate craving).

Preclinical research on the neurobiology of MUD is developing rapidly and has the potential to transform the development of pharmacotherapies. Ongoing research is needed to clarify the nature of the more complex neurobiological changes that occur both during and following MUD and how these relate to clinical and functional outcomes. This includes a clearer understanding of the acute and longer-term neuroplastic, neuro-inflammatory and neurotoxic changes that can persist for months to years after withdrawal from methamphetamine.

A significant challenge will be enabling the translation of preclinical neurobiological research into early phase human clinical trials. Current clinical trial research has developed in parallel to preclinical research, and the former remains largely focussed on repurposing drugs as maintenance therapies, particularly drugs that either mimic the acute effects of methamphetamine or block those effects. This focus does not fully embrace our emergent understanding of the complex neurobiological responses to acute drug exposure nor does it address the need to remediate the longer-term neuroplastic changes associated with addiction or the neuro-inflammatory and neurotoxic effects that can accrue to chronic methamphetamine use.

Closing Remark

Current MUD pharmacotherapy research has largely focussed on the development of maintenance therapies. Evidence to date suggests that these have limited effectiveness. In this review, we have identified an opportunity to look beyond maintenance therapies to consider alternative models of pharmacotherapy that support the initiation and maintenance of abstinence for people who want to cease use. We highlight that the neurobiological changes that occur following cessation of methamphetamine are lasting, and there is great potential to develop pharmacotherapies that improve functional outcomes during recovery (e.g. reduce dysphoria and craving and improve mood, cognitive function and quality of life) as well as reduce relapse risk. We also identify a need for pharmacotherapies that are better tailored to the complex pharmacological profile of methamphetamine and the typical comorbidities of MUD. This will require more precisely targeting the neurobiological mechanisms that underpin reward processes involved in MUD as well as the lasting neurobiological adaptations and neurotoxic changes that can develop with long-term use.
