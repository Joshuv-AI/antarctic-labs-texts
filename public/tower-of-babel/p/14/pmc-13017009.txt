Post-infection sleep syndrome: long COVID as an example
Authors: Haoqi Sun, Janet M Mullington, Robert Joseph Thomas
Published: 2025

Infections can directly or indirectly damage the nervous system. The post-infection syndromes are especially challenging, as the range of clinical syndromes can involve the central and peripheral nervous systems and the muscles. Bacterial, spirochetal, and viral infections can all trigger such syndromes. Examples [1] include Guillain–Barré syndrome (Campylobacter jejuni, Cytomegalovirus, Epstein–Barr virus, Influenza, HIV, Hepatitis A, E. Chlamydia pneumonia, Mycoplasma pneumonia, Haemophilus influenzae, Zika virus, COVID-19), autoimmune encephalitis (Herpes simplex virus, Japanese encephalitis virus, West Nile virus, COVID-19, Mycoplasma pneumoniae), myasthenia gravis (West Nile virus), Neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorders (Varicella Zoster virus), acute disseminated encephalomyelitis (Measles, Mumps Coxsackie B, Varicella Zoster virus, HIV, Mycoplasma pneumonia, Legionella), Sydenham’s chorea (Group A streptococcus), and stiff person syndrome (West Nile virus).

Clinical sleep syndromes have been described mostly in association with the post-infectious state over the past 100 years [2, 3]. Sleep disturbances can range from severe sleep fragmentation and severe insomnia to persistent hypersomnia with long sleep, either during the acute phase or as temporally varying in severity, but with persistent sequelae. Dr. Christian Guilleminault reported in 1986 a hypersomnia syndrome that followed infectious mononucleosis [4] and remains one of the best reports of this association.

Both sporadic and epidemic forms of infection-induced sleep disorder syndromes are possible. To capture the whole clinical spectrum, we suggest the term “post-infection sleep syndrome,” or PISS. The most famous PISS is hypersomnia, and less common is insomnia associated with classic Von Economo’s disease [3]. Much has been written about this condition, but it suffices to say that the critical site of involvement is likely the hypothalamus [5]. Other PISS include persistent hypersomnia following Epstein–Barr virus infection, such as mononucleosis [6], and several case reports of Kleine-Levin syndrome [7] with an infectious prodrome (including EBV-like illnesses), and more recently, associated with Long COVID [8–10].

Recently, Ibrahim et al. [11] reported a retrospective analysis of polysomnographic recordings from 150 patients across three matched groups: post-COVID-19 insomnia, chronic insomnia during the pandemic without a history of COVID-19 infection, and pre-pandemic chronic insomnia. They did not find significant differences across groups in classical polysomnographic variables. However, they found that patients with post-COVID-19 insomnia had significantly increased rapid eye movement (REM) sleep without atonia (RWA), higher nocturnal heart rate, and shorter N3 bouts. Their results are consistent with our clinical practice from a Long COVID-Sleep program, with an increase in both REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) and syndromes of sleep–wake cycle instability (loss of sleep regularity) including hypersomnia with and without long sleep, persistent insomnia, and type 2 narcolepsy [12]. There have been case reports of hypersomnia and type 2 narcolepsy after COVID-19 infection [13]. In parallel, an international survey of subjective patient symptomatic reports following COVID-19 infection in 2020 [14] found a 3-fold increase in insomnia, 2.5-fold increase in night sweats, and 10% more vivid dreams.

These symptoms in Long COVID PISS suggest multiple central nervous system involvements. Of specific interest to RWA, brainstem involvement has been reported but not systematically studied [15–17]. Other differences also hint at brainstem dysfunction, including higher REM density [18] indicative of phasic REM, lower nocturnal blood oxygen [19] especially during REM without lung symptoms or pathology, and elevated heart rate found in our previous study [19] and others [20]. The key unanswered question is whether RWA in Long COVID PISS is a harbinger of something worse to come, some form of permanent or progressive neural injury/neurodegeneration/RBD. How far one could go with testing is also uncertain—should this be considered a prodrome of RBD? Then, could/should autonomic testing, skin biopsy for α-synuclein, or a dopamine transporter scan be considered? Indeed, these tests, except for skin biopsy, are not yet standard of clinical care for RBD.

Meanwhile, other brain regions could be involved, including alpha intrusions into N3, N2, and REM stages [18] that hint at cortical involvement, and changes in spindles and spindle-slow oscillation coupling (unpublished) that hint at thalamocortical involvement. Indeed, there are several reports of brain injury, especially white matter changes post-COVID [21–23], including the brainstem. MRI studies [24, 25] showed that regions with the highest discriminatory power include both white matter (including corpus callosum and frontal white matter) and gray matter (including putamen and left thalamus).

It is also important to consider the roles of medications in Long COVID. Due to the stressful nature of the disorder, patients often take antidepressants, which can suppress REM and are associated with RWA [26]. Clinically, some patients can develop antidepressant-induced RBD [27]. One strength of Ibrahim et al. [11] was that they compared medications across both groups, and the groups were balanced.

The COVID-19 pandemic has resulted in an unfortunate increase in PISS. For practical reasons, there is limited objective data on the acute effect of COVID-19 infection [28, 29]. Nevertheless, when there is an evolution into Long COVID, sleep symptoms and sleep disorder syndromes can cause substantial morbidity. In this context, perhaps the pervasiveness of RWA could guide the next steps. At a minimum, those who have developed Long COVID with or without demonstrated RWA (and certainly if there are dream enactment behaviors) should ideally be part of a registry for long-term follow-up [30].
