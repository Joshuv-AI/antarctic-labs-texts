Development and in vitro evaluation of teicoplanin PEGylated (TEC-PEGylated) niosomes for antimicrobial and anti-biofilm activity against vancomycin-intermediate Staphylococcus aureus (VISA)
Authors: Jaber Hemmati, Zahra Chegini, Iraj Sedighi, Mehdi Azizi, Raha Zare Shahraki, Mohsen Chiani, Mohammad Reza Arabestani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVancomycin-intermediate Staphylococcus aureus (VISA) is a prominent pathogen in burn wound infections, mainly known for biofilm formation. Recently, infections associated with antibiotic-resistant VISA isolates have increasingly posed life-threatening risks. Accordingly, there is an urgent necessity to investigate and develop efficient strategies against the rapid spread of VISA strains in the healthcare system. In this research, a niosomal drug delivery system was developed using the thin-film hydration method and then surface‑modified with polyethylene glycol (PEG). Teicoplanin (TEC) was further incorporated into PEGylated niosome (TEC-PEGylated niosome), and key physicochemical properties, including encapsulation efficiency (EE %), drug release profile, particle size, surface zeta charge, morphology, polydispersity index (PDI), and 30-day stability were measured. The antimicrobial potential of TEC-PEGylated niosome against five VISA strains isolated from burn wounds was investigated by determining the minimum inhibitory/bactericidal concentrations (MIC, MBC) and the time-kill assay. Furthermore, the anti-biofilm properties of the synthesized niosomal formulation were evaluated with a microtiter-plate (MTP) method and compared to those of free drug. FE-SEM results revealed that the niosomal formulation had spherical morphology with an approximate size of 250 nm. Hydrodynamic size, surface zeta charge, and EE% of the formulated niosomes were found be 278.8 ± 5.0 nm, + 9.5 ± 3.4 mV, and 65.9% ± 1.6, respectively. Also, TEC-PEGylated niosome demonstrated a significantly improved antibacterial ability compared to free drug. Additionally, TEC-PEGylated niosome effectively inhibited the biofilm formation capacity in all VISA strains. It was concluded that synthesized niosomal system could be an effective drug delivery system owing to several advantageous characteristics, including sustained-release profiles, acceptable stability, and other desirable features. These properties enable PEGylated niosomes to effectively deliver a diverse array of antimicrobial agents, including TEC. This capability could present a promising novel strategy for addressing burn infections, particularly those caused by VISA strains.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Niosome, Polyethylene glycol (PEG), Drug delivery system, Burn wound infections, Vancomycin intermediate Staphylococcus aureus (VISA), Teicoplanin, Biofilm

Introduction

Vancomycin-intermediate Staphylococcus aureus (VISA) is a serious human bacterial pathogen, particularly well‑known for causing skin and soft tissue infections [1, 2]. The rapid emergence of drug resistance among VISA isolates complicates the treatment of infections associated with this superbug [3]. VISA strains exhibit a high propensity for biofilm production, which poses substantial challenges in healthcare settings, particularly in the management of wound infections [2]. Biofilm is characterized as a community of living bacteria within an extracellular polymeric substance [4], facilitating bacterial survival under adverse conditions, particularly under a high dosage of antibacterial agents [5]. This structure represents a key mechanism of bacterial resistance to different antimicrobial agents. Due to limited drug permeation into the biofilm matrix, the embedded bacteria could withstand high concentrations of antibiotics, posing a significant challenge to effectively treating wound infections [6].

Currently, burn treatment remains a significant challenge in medicine, as compromised microcirculation hinders systemic circulation to the burn tissues [7]. VISA is one of the primary bacterial pathogens that colonizes burn wounds, causing chronic and potentially life-threatening infections, especially among individuals with impaired immunity [8, 9]. The restricted penetration of topical medications through the skin layers complicates efficient drug administration. Furthermore, several limitations, including in vivo instability, low absorption, and water-insolubility, cause deficient drug delivery to the target microorganism, contributing to failure of conventional dosage forms in managing burn wound infections [10]. These phenomena underscore the critical importance of efficient topical drug delivery in combating burn wound infections.

Nano-transdermal drug delivery system may represent a highly novel and effective strategy for the successful management of therapeutic difficulties related to burn wound infections [11, 12]. Niosomes are vesicular carriers formed from nonionic surfactants consisting of one or more concentric bilayer membranes [13]. This bilayer structure is created through the self-assembly of amphiphilic components in an aqueous environment, allowing for the incorporation of materials with a wide spectrum of solubility [14]. Furthermore, biodegradability, negligible toxicity, flexible structure, and sustained drug release are among the advantages resulting in the appropriate application of a niosomal drug delivery system for topical administration [15].

Teicoplanin (TEC) is a glycopeptide antibiotic crucial in treating VISA infections, acting through inhibiting bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding to its precursors [16]. Clinically, TEC offers a broad spectrum of activity similar to vancomycin, making it a preferred choice for severe VISA infections, especially in high-risk patients. Studies indicate that TEC exhibits favorable pharmacokinetic features, especially extensive tissue penetration, which is critical for successful management of skin- and deep-seated infections [17, 18]. However, variable pharmacokinetics, emergence of resistance, potential nephrotoxicity, and cutaneous reactions could pose challenges in the treatment efficacy of TEC [18]. Overall, TEC remains a vital component in the antibiotic arsenal against VISA infections, requiring ongoing investigations to optimize its administration and to combat resistance mechanisms.

Surface modification with PEG polymer could provide favorable properties for niosomes by enhancing their stability, biocompatibility, and delivery efficiency [19, 20]. The current investigation relies on the hypothesis that incorporation of TEC into a PEGylated niosomal system could improve the therapeutic efficacy of the loaded drug. According to the excellent characteristics of this nano-drug delivery system [21, 22], we aim to propose a TEC‑PEGylated niosome formulation, as a novel anti-VISA agent, for solving the therapeutic challenges related to VISA burn wound infections by in vitro anti-microbial and anti-biofilm analysis including minimum inhibitory/bactericidal concentrations (MIC, MBC), time-kill, and microtiter plate (MTP) biofilm assays.

Materials and methods

Materials

Span 60 (sorbitan monostearate), mPEG 2000, cholesterol, chloroform, methanol, crystal violet, teicoplanin (TEC, ≥ 80%, HPLC), Spectra/Por® dialysis membrane (MWCO 12–14 kDa), Amicon® ultra centrifugal filter, and all culture media were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich Company.

Preparation of TEC-PEGylated niosome

This study examines the hypothesis that surface modification of niosomes by PEGylation can enhance the pharmaceutical activity of the antibacterial agents. For this purpose, the thin-film hydration method was utilized to prepare the niosomal formulation [14, 15, 23]. Briefly, specific amounts of Span 60 (335.9 mg), cholesterol (150.8 mg), and mPEG 2000 (46.8 mg) were dissolved in 25 mL of a chloroform-methanol solution (2:1 v/v) using a magnetic stirrer (50 min, 25 °C, 150 rpm) to obtain a homogeneous suspension. Subsequently, the organic solvent was removed using a rotary evaporator (WB Eco Laborota 4000 Model, Germany) under vacuum at 60 °C for 50 min at a rotation speed of 150 rpm. The remaining solvent was removed by purging with nitrogen gas over a 10-minute period. The dried lipid film was then hydrated in 20 mL of PBS (pH 7.4, 5 mM) containing TEC (100 mg) for 45 min at 25 °C under continuous magnetic stirring (150 rpm). The molar ratio of total lipids to TEC was 15:1. In the final step, the niosomes were sonicated for 10 min at 30% amplitude and 25 °C using an ultrasonic processor (Hielscher UP50H, Germany) to obtain a homogeneous formulation [24, 25]. After visually assessing the formulations for turbidity and flocculation, the synthesized niosomes were stored at 4 °C for subsequent experiments. Using the same protocol, blank niosomes were prepared without adding TEC.

Characterization of the physicochemical features of TEC-PEGylated niosome

Hydrodynamic size, PDI, and surface zeta potential

Hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and surface zeta potential of prepared TEC-PEGylated niosome were analyzed using dynamic light scattering (DLS) protocol. For this purpose, the samples were examined by using a SZ100 zeta-sizer instrument (Horiba, Japan) at 633 nm in a polystyrene cuvette under identical conditions of concentration, temperature, and pH (0.1 mg/ml, 25 °C, 7.4) [26].

Morphological evaluation

In our study, field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, Hitachi S4160, Japan) was employed to analyze size and morphology of TEC-PEGylated niosome. For imaging with FE-SEM, a drop of synthesized niosomal formulation, diluted 1:100 in double distilled water, was placed on the plate and subsequently covered with a conductive layer of gold. Finally, ImageJ software was utilized to analyze images captured by FE-SEM [26, 27].

Assessment of drug entrapment efficiency (EE %)

The EE% of TEC-PEGylated niosomal formulation was evaluated using an ultra-filtration assay. Briefly, 1 ml of synthesized niosomal formulation was centrifuged in an ultra Amicon® tube (Cut-off 30 range kDa) for 15 min at 14,000 × g at 4 °C. Afterward, the amount of TEC in the filtrate solution was evaluated using a standard curve and a UV–Visible spectrophotometer (Jasco V-530, Japan) at 280 nm. In vitro drug release study

In this research, the dialysis assay was employed to study the release kinetics of the TEC-PEGylated niosomal formulation [28]. At first, un‑entrapped TEC was removed by ultracentrifugation, and subsequently, a dialysis bag (Spectra/Por®; MWCO 12‑14 kDa; membrane width 16 mm; surface area approximately 8 cm²) containing 1 ml of prepared niosomes was suspended in 30 ml of recipient medium (PBS, 5 mM, pH ~ 7.4). Notably, the same amount of free TEC solution was used as control. After magnetically stirring for 48 h (150 rpm, at 37 °C), 0.5 ml of recipient medium was sampled at different interval times, and the absorbance of each sample was measured spectrophotometrically at 280 nm. In the final step, the amount of released TEC was determined by equation of standard curve. At each sampling time point, 0.5 mL of the release medium was withdrawn and replaced with an equal volume of fresh PBS (5 mM, pH 7.4, 37 °C) to maintain sink conditions throughout the experiment.

Stability analysis of prepared niosomes

Stability analysis of the synthesized TEC-PEGylated niosome was conducted by monitoring the modifications in hydrodynamic size, PDI, and EE% over a 30-day period under refrigerator and room temperature storage conditions.

Microbial analysis

Bacterial isolation

In this research, five VISA strains isolated from clinical burn wounds were applied for antimicrobial analysis. All patients, and the parents or legal guardians of participating children, gave informed consent before taking part in the study. The isolates were first approved as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) using cefoxitin disc and mecA amplification [14]. Subsequently, VISA strains were confirmed by determining the vancomycin minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [29]. Also, MRSA ATCC 6538, provided from the Department of Microbiology, Hamadan University of Medical Sciences, Hamadan, Iran, was considered as a standard strain.

Biofilm formation and resistance profile

The biofilm formation capability of VISA strains was assessed using MTP method [2]. Overnight cultures in 1% glucose-supplemented tryptic soy broth (TSB) medium were adjusted to a specific concentration and incubated in a 96-well plate at 37 °C overnight. Following washing, biofilms were fixed with absolute methanol and subsequently stained with crystal violet (1.5% w/v) for 15 min. Afterward, the bound stains were solubilized with 33% v/v acetic acid solution, and optical densities (ODs) were measured using an ELISA reader (BioTek, Germany) at 570 nm. Notably, the biofilm-forming profile of isolates was tested in triplicate, and S. aureus ATCC 6538 and uninoculated TSB served as positive and negative controls.

Following the disk diffusion method, multidrug-resistant (MDR) profile of VISA isolates was evaluated against aminoglycosides, macrolides, tetracyclines, glycopeptides, fluoroquinolones, and lincosamides. The MDR profiles were screened by non-susceptibility to at least one antibiotic in three or more antimicrobial classes [30].

Antibacterial analysis

The antimicrobial potential of TEC-PEGylated niosomes against VISA isolates was performed using the MIC and MBC methods recommended by CLSI [2, 30]. In this regard, Mueller-Hinton broth (MHB) medium containing increasing concentrations of TEC-PEGylated niosome was dispensed into sterile microtiter plates. Afterward, the bacterial suspensions were cultured in the wells. After overnight incubation of plates at 37 °C, the lowest concentrations showing no visible bacterial growth were reported as MICs. MBCs were also considered as the lowest concentration with no growth (> 99.9%) on Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA) medium following overnight incubation at 37 °C. All assays were carried out in triplicate, and microbial strain and uninoculated culture medium were positive and negative controls, respectively.

Time-kill analysis

The time course of five VISA strains viability was assessed by microtiter plate protocol. For this purpose, sub-MIC concentrations of free TEC and TEC-PEGylated niosome were exposed to a suspension of VISA isolates with 105 CFU/ml and the microtiter plate was incubated at 37 °C. At predetermined time intervals, aliquots were withdrawn from each well and spread onto MHA plates. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, the number of viable colonies was counted, and the results were expressed as log₁₀ CFU/mL [26–32].

Anti-biofilm activity

To analyze the anti-biofilm ability of TEC-PEGylated niosomes, 200 µl of a specific concentration of bacterial suspensions diluted with TSB was cultured into a sterile 96-well plate. Subsequently, a sub-MIC concentration of the samples were introduced into each well, and the plate was incubated overnight at 37 °C. Following washing of the wells, the biofilms were fixed and stained. Finally, the ODs were measured in triplicate at 570 nm using an ELISA reader [33–35].

Statically analysis

Tukey test and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were performed to investigate the significant differences among the analyzed groups. Notably, all graphs were generated using the GraphPad Prism version 9.0, and statistical significance was established as a P-value of less than 0.05.

Results

Physicochemical characterization of synthesized nanoparticles

Particle size, morphology, and surface zeta potential

The hydrodynamic size and PDI of TEC-PEGylated niosome were reported to be 278.8 ± 5.0 nm and 0.383 ± 0.010, respectively, using the DLS technique (Fig. 1). The reported PDI value of 0.383 ± 0.010 indicated a moderately homogeneous dispersion, which is acceptable for the antimicrobial evaluations performed in this study. Furthermore, the surface zeta potential of TEC-PEGylated niosome, as measured by DLS method, was found to be + 9.5 ± 3.4 mV (Fig. 2). Using FE-SEM microscopy, the morphology of the prepared niosomal formulation was investigated, and Fig. 3 shows the FE-SEM micrograph of TEC-PEGylated niosome with a smooth surface and spherical structure. Additionally, ImageJ analysis revealed that the synthesized niosomes had an approximate size of 250 nm.

EE% of formulated niosome

Incorporation into niosomal vesicular nanoparticles is known to enhance pharmaceutical efficacy of loaded contents, which is considered a primary reason for the development of drug delivery systems [2]. Our findings represented that the amount of loaded TEC in PEGylated niosome was found to be 65.9% ± 1.6, showing a significant EE% for the prepared niosomal formulation in comparison with other formulations [36, 37].

Release profile and kinetics

In our research, the dynamic dialysis method was employed to examine release kinetics of the free and niosomal formulations [2]. Release analysis was performed using different mathematical techniques according to the equations of kinetic models, including zero- and first-order polynomials, Korsmeyer–Peppas, Higuchi, and Hixson-Crowell. The coefficient of determination (R²) was calculated for each model, indicating that the drug release profile was best described by the first-order model for free TEC. Furthermore, the niosomal formulation followed the Korsmeyer-Peppas model (R² = 0.977) with n = 0.385, indicating Fickian diffusion as the dominant release mechanism due to the drug’s transport through the lipid bilayer [38].

It should be noted that the dialysis method may introduce diffusion barriers that are not directly related to the intrinsic release kinetics of the niosomes. Therefore, the observed release profile may represent a combination of nanoparticle release and diffusion across the membrane (Table 1).

Stability study

Physical stability, a key characteristic of formulated niosome, was evaluated throughout a 30-day period at 4 °C and 25 °C by monitoring modifications in hydrodynamic size, PDI, and EE%. Our results indicated superior stability at refrigerated temperature, as evidenced by minimal variations in particle size, PDI, and EE% compared to niosomes stored at room temperature (Fig. 5). Furthermore, the greater stability of our niosomes compared to other formulations [36, 37] could be related to surface modification by PEGylation. Nevertheless, the synthesis methods, the molar ratio of ingredients, the presence of cholesterol, and the type of surfactants also are effective in the stability of niosome nanoparticles [39–41].

MIC and MBC

The MIC and MBC of TEC-PEGylated niosome against five VISA strains were tested and compared with those of free TEC (Fig. 7). It was revealed that incorporation into the niosome significantly decreased MICs of free TEC against all VISA strains by 2- to 8-fold (P-value < 0.01). Moreover, TEC-PEGylated niosome had significant bactericidal activity in comparison with free TEC, as it reduced the MBC values at lower concentrations (P-value < 0.05). Notably, the MIC and MBC values of free TEC and TEC-PEGylated niosome against all VISA strains were measured in triplicate, and the bare niosome had no antibacterial activity.

Comparison of MIC and MBC values of free TEC and TEC-PEGylated niosome against VISA strains

Time-kill study

The bacterial viability over time was assessed after the tested bacteria were exposed to free TEC and TEC-PEGylated niosome at sub-MIC concentrations. The comparison of time-kill kinetics profiles of both free drug and niosomal formulation against VISA strains showed that TEC-PEGylated niosome resulted in a significant reduction in the number of viable bacterial cells at the same concentration (overall P-value < 0.05) (Fig. 8). Also, the time-kill assay was performed in triplicate, and the bare niosome showed no antibacterial activity against any of the tested strains.

In our study, the inhibitory effectiveness of TEC-PEGylated niosomes against five VISA biofilms was analyzed and compared with that of freeTEC. Discussion

The increased propensity for biofilm formation in VISA isolates has a crucial role in the emergence of drug resistance. In this research, all VISA isolates exhibited strong biofilm patterns, and there was a significant association between biofilm production and MDR pattern. This result is consistent with other studies, where MDR isolates had a significant tendency to biofilm production [42, 43]. Addressing the drug-resistant challenge necessitates the investigation of innovative therapeutic and pharmaceutical strategies. Our study indicated that the formulated niosomes had high activity against MDR S. aureus strains. Our results were in line with other studies, where niosomal formulations had lower MIC/MBC values compared to free drug [44, 45]. Indeed, the integration of established antibiotics with nanoparticles has demonstrated the potential to circumvent resistance mechanisms. Also, the niosome could increase the efficacy of loaded agents and improve their bioavailability through a sustaining release profile [13]. Therefore, niosomal nano-carriers could be proposed as powerful strategies for overcoming therapeutic challenges associated with VISA strains.

The drug release kinetics is a considerable factor affecting pharmaceutical properties of niosomes, offering beneficial insights into their in vivo effectiveness. By comparing the drug release profiles from both free TEC and synthesized niosomal formulation, it was demonstrated that the niosome had a prolonged release pattern by limiting the initial burst release [46, 47]. The mechanism of drug release from niosomes involves a controlled and sustained process, differing significantly from rapid release profile of free formulation. Niosomes, composed of non-ionic surfactants, encapsulate drugs within their vesicular structure, protecting them from the biological environment, thereby enhancing bioavailability [48]. The release of drugs from niosomes is mainly governed by diffusion through the vesicle membrane, which can be influenced by parameters such as shape, vesicle size, and composition, particularly concentration of cholesterol that affects membrane rigidity. This controlled release mechanism allows niosomes to prolong drug action and reduce systemic toxicity compared to free drug solutions [49]. It should also be noted that in the present study, the sustained release profile of the synthesized niosome was evaluated only at physiological pH (7.4), whereas burn wounds typically exhibit an acidic microenvironment with pH ranging from 5.5 to 6.5 [50]. Acidic pH conditions can significantly affect the stability and release behavior of niosomal formulations, as previous studies have demonstrated pH-sensitive release profiles from niosomes at pH 5.5 [51]. Therefore, the observed release kinetics may not fully represent the conditions at the burn wound site. The interpretation of drug release kinetics is essential for understanding the behavior of nanocarriers in biological environments. Regarding the Korsmeyer-Peppas model, the release exponent n = 0.385 obtained in our study is below the threshold of 0.43, which confirms that the dominant release mechanism is Fickian diffusion [52], where drug transport occurs through passive diffusion across the lipid bilayer with no significant contribution from swelling or erosion of the niosomal vesicles. This finding is consistent with a previous report showing that n values ≤ 0.43 indicated diffusion-controlled release in niosomal and polymeric systems [26, 53, 54]. Overall, while our data confirm Fickian diffusion as the primary release mechanism, future studies should investigate the release behavior of TEC-PEGylated niosomes at acidic pH to better mimic the burn wound microenvironment and to evaluate the potential pH-sensitivity of the formulation.

TEC is an antibiotic that can prevent and cure serious complications caused by Gram-positive bacteria, such as VISA. The action profile of this semi-synthetic glycopeptide antibiotic is similar to that of vancomycin. Inhibiting the formation of bacterial cell walls is one of its modes of action, and incorporating glycopeptide antibacterial agents within nanoparticles offers advantages in enhancing their antibacterial activity with favorable outcomes [55]. Researchers have recently developed nanocarriers for various pharmaceutical applications, particularly in delivery of TEC. In this research, the anti-VISA activities of TEC-PEGylated niosome were examined, and it was confirmed that niosomal drug delivery holds significant promise as a substitute approach against VISA strains with 2-8-fold reduction in MIC/MBC concentrations of free TEC. In this regard, Armenia et al. approved that the antimicrobial activity of TEC conjugates with silver nanoparticles was greater (2-4-fold) than free TEC against S.aureus strains [56]. In addition, Kavruk et al. synthesized TEC-encapsulated mesoporous silica nanoparticles, which had improved anti-S. aureus acitivity with a 3.6-fold reduction in MIC of free TEC [57]. Also, Ucak et al. prepared TEC-loaded PLGA nanoparticles, where fabricated nano drug delivery system had 2-fold and 8-16-fold more ability against different Staphylococcus spp. strains, respectively, in comparison with free drug [58].

Niosomal vesicular system is capable of delivering loaded antibacterial agents directly into the bacterial cell by utilizing bilayer fluidity and fusogenic characteristics [59]. This strategy aims to enhance the effectiveness of antimicrobial therapy and minimize dose-dependent side effects. In Gram-positive bacterial pathogens, niosomes can facilitate intracellular drug delivery by leveraging the fluidity of their bilayer structure, allowing them to establish a concentration gradient at the peptidoglycan barrier. Niosomal vesicles not only facilitate considerable drug permeation into bacterial environment but also shield antimicrobial agents, which could be a useful tactic for combating drug resistance [60].

Our results from time-kill assay approved the antibacterial potential (MIC/MBC) of the formulated niosome, which was in line with other conducted studies [26, 36, 37]. The significant antibacterial activity of our formulation could be attributable to surface modification of niosomes via PEGylation, which is approved by other studies [61, 62]. Moreover, previous study revealed that PEGylated niosomes enhanced the antibacterial and anti-biofilm capabilities of encapsulated vancomycin against S. aureus strains [63]. Notably, although the release of TEC from PEGylated niosomes was lower than that of free drug at 6 h, the significantly lower MIC and MBC values of the niosome formulation explain the superior bactericidal effect observed in our time-kill assay. Other studies have also shown that the anti-bacterial efficacy of niosome nanoparticles against bacterial pathogens could be increased by incorporating positively charged agents into their formulations [15, 62]. It should be noted that extending the time‑kill assay duration beyond 6 h would have provided deeper insights into the long-term antibacterial activity after exposure to the niosomal formulation.

Based on electrostatic principles, it is proposed that positively charged niosomal vesicles could exhibit prolonged and enhanced attraction to the negatively charged bacterial surface. Moreover, we hypothesize that the enhanced encapsulation capacity of cationic nanocarriers contributes to an increased pharmaceutical index of the antimicrobial agent [15]. However, further studies are recommended to fully validate these proposed mechanisms. Future investigations should include direct mechanistic experiments such as bacterial surface charge measurement after niosome treatment, fluorescence microscopy of niosome‑bacteria interactions, bilayer interaction studies, and qRT‑PCR analysis to better clarify the role of surface modifications in enhancing antibacterial efficacy. Considering potential drug‑lipid interactions, such studies are also warranted to fully understand the precise mechanism of vesicle‑mediated delivery [64]. Nevertheless, our findings offer the concept that PEGylated niosomes may serve as a promising platform for topical delivery of TEC against VISA strains. However, the potential skin irritation associated with the positive surface charge of cationic niosomes should be considered in future formulations and safety evaluations [65].

In this research, the inhibitory potential of TEC-PEGylated niosome on VISA biofilms was analyzed, and it was proven that TEC incorporation into niosomal vesicles significantly increased the anti-biofilm capability of free drug against all VISA strains. In line with this research, other studies [45, 66, 67] approved that niosomal encapsulation could considerably reduce biofilm-producing ability of S. aureus strains at the same concentration as free drugs. The hypothesis that niosomes, as a physical barrier, compete with biofilm-forming bacteria for surface attachment reinforces the conclusion that the niosomal formulation exhibits improved anti-biofilm qualities. By decreasing bacterial adhesion, this competition greatly suppresses biofilm formation [68–70]. Additionally, by facilitating diffusion inside biofilm matrix, niosomes efficiently prevent the formation of biofilms and enable the effective delivery of their incorporated contents to the embedded bacteria [13]. It should be noted that the MTP assay used in this study measures total biofilm biomass rather than specifically quantifying viable bacterial cells within the biofilm. While this method is widely accepted for initial screening of anti‑biofilm activity, it does not distinguish between live and dead cells. Complementary techniques such as confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), CFU quantification, and metabolic activity assays would provide more robust evidence. Future investigations should incorporate these methods to further validate the anti‑biofilm potential of PEGylated niosomes.

The early prevention of bacterial attachment to burn wounds constitutes a substantial problems within healthcare systems, as biofilm has been identified as a prominent factor in the pathogenicity of VISA strains [43, 71]. The sustained presence of drug-loaded niosomal systems within the biofilm matrix could potentially overcome resistance mechanisms in embedded bacteria [13, 60]. Also, PEGylated niosomes could improve the pharmacokinetic profile of loaded agents at infected sites through high physical stability and prolonged release pattern [13]. This process could prevent biofilm formation and hold promise for tackling the difficulties associated with VISA burn wound infections.

The physicochemical properties of nanocarriers, including zeta potential and PDI, play a critical role in their stability and biological performance. In the present study, the TEC-PEGylated niosomes exhibited a zeta potential of + 9.5 ± 3.4 mV and a PDI of 0.383 ± 0.010, with a hydrodynamic size of 278.8 ± 5.0 nm. Although nanoparticles with zeta potential values below − 30 mV or above + 30 mV are generally considered highly stable [72, 73], we achieved a moderate positive charge to enhance electrostatic attraction between the niosomes and the negatively charged surface of Gram-positive bacteria [13]. A comparison of our data with previous studies demonstrated that surface modification played a significant role in maintaining particle size and EE% [2, 15, 36]. However, it should be noted that surface modification may have an adverse effect on PDI, potentially increasing particle size distribution over extended periods. Although our surface charge may not ensure long‑term colloidal stability comparable to that of highly charged systems (e.g., ± 30 mV), the formulation remained adequately stable during the 30‑day study period. Nevertheless, further studies are required to evaluate the long‑term effects of surface modification on PDI and overall stability. Despite the neutral zeta potential and moderate PDI value, our formulation exhibited physicochemical characteristics within the acceptable range reported in the literature for similar niosomal drug delivery systems.

It should be noted that while the current study focused on antimicrobial and key physicochemical evaluations, additional characterization analyses such as FTIR, XRD, DSC, and long-term stability studies could provide deeper insights into drug-polymer interactions and the physical state of TEC within the PEGylated niosomal formulation. Although we provided robust evidence for the antimicrobial activity of TEC-PEGylated niosome using standard phenotypic assays (MIC, MBC, Time-Kill, and MTP), complementary techniques such as SEM or TEM could have helped to better clarify the precise role of niosomes in their interaction with bacterial cells and biofilms. Also, molecular studies such as qRT-PCR to investigate resistance- or biofilm-related gene expression were not conducted. Additionally, animal experiments were not performed in this study, and the relatively small sample size may limit the generalizability of our findings to the broader patient population. Future studies should therefore employ SEM, TEM, qRT-PCR, and in vivo studies to evaluate the therapeutic potential and safety of the formulated niosomes before clinical translation.

Conclusion

This study indicates that encapsulation within niosome significantly augments the pharmaceutical effectiveness of free TEC, highlighting its promise as a delivery system for antimicrobial agents. Moreover, incorporation of TEC into a PEGylated niosomes has been demonstrated to enhance the antibacterial properties of free drug, establishing it as a highly promising drug delivery system for combating burn infections related to VISA isolates. Furthermore, MTP exhibited showed that the niosomal drug delivery system improved free TEC’s anti-biofilm properties against VISA clinical strains, which could decrease the biofilm-related challenges in VISA burn wounds. However, the antibacterial and anti-biofilm efficacy of our synthesized niosomes needs to be further explored through cytotoxicity, pharmacokinetic, and in vivo studies, as these could provide critical insights for therapeutic development against bacterial burn infections.
