Formulation of a Modified Aloe vera-Based Emulgel for Sustained Release of Methotrexate: Integrated Experimental and Theoretical Approaches
Authors: Tanjila Begum, Moushumi Hazarika, Arpita Das, Pranjal Bhuyan, Akalesh Kumar Verma, Bikram Das, Sangeeta Agarwal, Ankur Kanti Guha, Mausumi Ganguly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic autoimmune disorder characterized by chronic inflammation of synovial joints, for which methotrexate remains the first-line therapy. However, conventional methotrexate administration is often limited by systemic toxicity and uncontrolled drug release. In this study, a methotrexate-loaded Aloe vera-based emulgel was developed to achieve sustained drug delivery and enhanced anti-inflammatory efficacy. Four emulgel formulations with varying compositions were prepared and evaluated for physicochemical characteristics, including appearance, rheological behavior, spreadability, average globule size, zeta potential, drug content, and in vitro drug release. Aloe vera-containing formulations demonstrated sustained methotrexate release, with more than 60% of the drug released over 6 h, and the release kinetics were best described by the Weibull model. In contrast, non-Aloe vera formulations exhibited rapid drug release (>80% within 2 h) following Michaelis–Menten kinetics, indicating the critical role of Aloe vera in modulating release behavior. The optimized emulgel showed significant anti-inflammatory activity by inhibiting protein denaturation, achieving up to 93% inhibition of bovine serum albumin and 95% inhibition of egg albumin, comparable to diclofenac. Cytotoxicity studies revealed minimal to negligible toxicity toward normal peripheral blood mononuclear cells and splenocytes over 24–96 h, confirming good biocompatibility. Density functional theory calculations indicated stable adduct formation between methotrexate and bioactive constituents of Aloe vera, supporting their role as effective drug carriers. Molecular docking studies further suggested enhanced binding affinity of these adducts toward cyclooxygenase-2 compared to methotrexate alone. Overall, the findings suggest that methotrexate-loaded Aloe vera-based emulgel is a safe and promising therapeutic platform for improved management of rheumatoid arthritis.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease, which causes joint pain, swelling, stiffness, disability, and joint deformities in severe cases. It is a progressive disease accompanied by infiltration of inflammatory cells and proliferation of synovial fibroblasts. Rheumatoid arthritis affects about 1% of the world population, and among them, women are three times more prone to the disease when compared to men. The exact etiology of RA is not known; several factors such as aging, genetics, environment, and lifestyle are thought to have a role in the disease’s development. Although several therapeutic strategies are available to control disease progression, a definitive cure has not yet been established. , Analgesic and anti-inflammatory agents, including corticosteroids, are primarily employed for symptomatic relief, whereas disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) are administered to suppress the underlying immunopathological mechanisms and to slow or prevent irreversible joint and tissue damage. ,

Leflunomide, sulfasalazine, methotrexate, and hydroxychloroquine are well-known DMARDs available for rheumatoid arthritis. Methotrexate (MTX) is one of the first-line drugs most frequently used as a DMARD that suppresses the disease progression and reduces the pain and joint damage. MTX is a folate antagonist, but the exact mechanism of action is not clear. It is thought to inhibit purine and pyrimidine syntheses, which consequently prevent cellular proliferation of lymphocytes involved in the inflammation process. This inhibits the activation of T and B cells, which are capable of autoantibody production resulting in the deactivation of the enzyme activity related to immune system. , Additionally, a few studies suggest that the anti-inflammatory activities of MTX may result from its inhibition of cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2). COX-2 is an inducible isoenzyme responsible for the conversion of arachidonic acid to prostaglandin, and its activity is strictly controlled at the sites of tissue damage and inflammation. The concentration of prostaglandin is highly increased at the synovial fluid during inflammation due to the overexpression of COX-2and hence is one of the major components for the pathogenesis of RA. Therefore, the inhibition of COX-2 is considered to be a key target for the management of RA.

However, oral administrations of MTX have various side effects that cannot be ignored. The most common adverse effects include hepatotoxicity, hematologic toxicity, gastrointestinal toxicity, increased risk of infection, hair loss, nausea, loss of appetite, etc. , Hence, it is a challenge for researchers to formulate a safe and effective drug delivery method for hydrophobic MTX. To overcome these limitations, an emulsion-loaded gel can be utilized to incorporate and deliver a hydrophobic therapeutic agent through a topical route. Emulgels are dosage forms that consist of gels and emulsions in 1:1 proportion. They are easy to apply, have a high penetration capacity, and can be readily removed from the skin. There are various advantages of emulgels like thixotropic behavior, emollient properties, extended shelf life, high drug loading capacity, incorporation of hydrophobic drugs, controlled release, better stability, and enhanced patient compliance. −

Aloe vera (Aloe barbadensis Miller), which is a perennial succulent belonging to Liliaceae family, grows in hot and dry climates. For many years, Aloe vera has been reported to possess immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, antipyretic, antioxidant, UV protective, moisturizing, and wound-healing properties. − Moreover, Aloe vera can enhance skin permeation of formulated codrug and can be used as vehicles for other substances. −

Although topical delivery of MTX using conventional gels, nanoemulsions, and vesicular carrier systems has been previously investigated − and Aloe vera-based emulgels have also been independently reported, ,, the integration of MTX within an Aloe vera-incorporated emulgel matrix is an incremental formulation study with combined utilization of Aloe vera and MTX in a single emulgel platform. In the present study, Aloe vera simultaneously acts as (1) the structural gelling/emulsion-stabilizing medium, (2) a natural penetration enhancer, and (3) a modulator of both the drug release kinetics and the anti-inflammatory activity of the formulation.

In the present study, four MTX-loaded Aloe vera-based emulgels were formulated, optimized, and physicochemically characterized. Their in vitro drug release profiles and antiarthritic potential were evaluated using bovine serum albumin (BSA) and egg albumin denaturation assays. In silico anti-inflammatory studies were additionally carried out to investigate the inhibitory interactions of the formulation constituents with the COX-2 enzyme. The effects of Aloe vera and the type and concentration of emulsifying agents on MTX release kinetics from the emulgel matrices were also studied. Furthermore, the cytotoxicity of the emulgels was examined via an MTT assay using normal peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and splenocytes. The theoretical component of the study comprised quantum mechanical density functional theory (DFT) calculations and ab initio Born–Oppenheimer molecular dynamics (BOMD) simulations to elucidate the structural stability and intermolecular interactions of adducts formed between MTX and six major Aloe vera-bioactive constituents, namely aloin, aloe-emodin, aloesaponarin, esculetin, chrysophanol, and aloesin.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Aloe vera (Aloe barbadensis Miller) plants were collected from Nalbari District, Assam, India. Span 20, Tween 20, triethanolamine 98%, and Carbopol 934 were purchased from Loba Chemie Pvt. Ltd. Paraffin liquid light was purchased from Merck. Bovine serum albumin was purchased from HIMEDIA. Methotrexate hydrate was purchased from TCI. The dialysismembrane-110 was procured from HIMEDIA having molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) in a range of 12000–14000 Da, with a pore size of about 2.4 nm. Biological samples, including peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC), were donated by Professor Surya Bali Prasad from the Department of Zoology at North Eastern Hill University (NEHU), Shillong, India. All of the experiments were carried out using distilled water.

Method Development

Determination of λmax

The UV–vis spectrum of methotrexate was measured using a Shimadzu UV-2600 spectrophotometer from 200 to 1200 nm range to determine its λmax.

Standard Calibration Curve of Methotrexate

Accurately weighed 20 mg of solid methotrexate hydrochloride was dissolved in 20 mL of buffer solution to get the strength of 1 mg/mL (1000 μg/mL), which was further diluted with buffer to obtain the final stock solution of 100 μg/mL. A calibration curve for methotrexate was constructed over a concentration range of 5–25 μg/mL. Standard solutions of known concentrations were analyzed spectrophotometrically, and the corresponding absorbance values were recorded. Calibration curve was generated by plotting methotrexate concentration on the X-axis and their respective absorbance on the Y-axis.

Preparation of Aloe Vera-Carbopol Gel

Aloe vera-Carbopol gel was prepared by the methods described by Vandana et al. with slight modifications. Briefly, fresh Aloe vera leaves were collected and washed repeatedly with distilled water. Aloe vera leaves were peeled off, and pulp was collected and washed with distilled water and then ground in a mechanical mixer. The content was then filtered through Whatman filter paper no. 42. The filtrate was used for the preparation of an Aloe vera-Carbopol gel.

The A loe vera-Carbopol gel was prepared by dispersing Carbopol in Aloe vera gel with continuous stirring using a magnetic stirrer until a uniform solution was obtained. The pH of the solution was adjusted in 6–7 range with triethanolamine. The content was allowed to stand overnight. Blank gel was prepared by dispersing Carbopol in distilled water. Preparation of MTX-Loaded Emulsion

The oil phase of the emulsion was prepared by dissolving Span 20 in light liquid paraffin. Since MTX is lipophilic in nature, the drug was added to the oil phase. The aqueous phase was prepared by dissolving Tween 20 in purified water. Both the oily and aqueous phases were separately heated to 70 to 80 °C; then, the oily phase was added to the aqueous phase dropwise with continuous stirring and the mixture was cooled to room temperature. −

Three different emulsions were prepared by varying the concentration of emulsifying agents, i.e., in 1:2, 2:1, and 1:1 ratio. The ratios of the ingredients in the emulsions are shown in Table .

Preparation of MTX-Loaded Aloe Vera-Carbopol Emulgel

The emulgel was prepared by mixing the Aloe vera-Carbopol gel and MTX-loaded emulsion in 1:1 ratio and homogenized using a high-speed homogenizer at 10,000 rpm speed for 5 min.

In preparing the formulations, the drug concentration was selected to achieve therapeutically relevant levels, while maintaining adequate solubility within the delivery system. The gelling agent, Carbopol 934, was incorporated at concentrations ranging from 0.5% to 2.5% (w/w), and its impact on the physicochemical properties and drug content of the formulations were evaluated. A reduction in Carbopol 934 concentration (0.5%, 1%, and 1.5%) resulted in decreased drug content, which corroborate earlier findings. Therefore, the proportions showing the best drug loading were selected for the preparation of formulations.

In F1, F2, and F3 formulations, the ratios of emulsifying agents (Tween 20:Span 20) were 1:2, 2:1, and 1:1, respectively. In F1 and F4 formulations, the emulsifying agent ratio was the same (1:2), but the concentration of Carbopol varied.

Evaluation of Emulgel

Physical Examinations

The prepared formulations were examined visually for color, homogeneity, phase separations, etc.

Rheological Study

The rheology of formulations (viscosity, shear stress, shear rate gradient, etc.) was studied in the Anton Paar rotational rheometer, and the relevant results were obtained.

Spreadability

The spreadability of the formulations was determined by the apparatus described by Mutimer et al. Briefly, the apparatus consists of a wooden block attached to a pulley and two glass slides. One glass slide was fixed at the wooden block, and the other glass side was attached to the pulley. A predetermined quantity of the formulated sample was positioned between two clean glass slides, after which a 500 g load was applied over the slide for 5 min to eliminate entrapped air and to ensure the formation of a uniform formulation film. Subsequently, a standardized load of 80 g was applied to the upper glass slide to assess the spreading behavior of the formulation. The time required for complete separation of the two glass slides was recorded. Spreadability was calculated using the following formula S=M·LT where S is the spreadability, M is the weight tied to pulley, L is the length of glass slides, and T is the time taken to separate the slides completely from each other.

Determination of pH

The pH of the formulations (F1, F2, F3, and F4) was determined using a Systronics digital pH meter (μ Controller Based pH system 361). A 1 g portion of the formulation was taken in a beaker, and a minimum amount of distilled water was added to it and sonicated until a homogeneous solution was obtained. The pH of the formulations was measured by dipping the electrode in the solution. At the beginning, the pH meter was calibrated using solutions of pH 4 and pH 7. The experiments were performed in triplicate for each formulation.

Zeta Potential

Zeta potential of the formulations was measured by using a Litesizer DLS 500 instrument of Anton Parr. The samples were prepared by dissolving 0.5 g of each formulation in deionized water and sonicating for 1 h until homogeneous solution was obtained. That suspension was used for the analysis.

Droplet Size Measurement and Polydispersity Index (PDI)

Droplet size and polydispersity index were measured by a dynamic light scattering instrument (ZS90 instrument, model no. ZEN3690). The samples were prepared by dissolving 0.5 g of formulations in deionized water and sonicated for 1 h until homogeneous solution was obtained. That suspension was used for analysis.

Drug Content of the Formulation

For the estimation of drug content in the formulations, 1 g of formulation was mixed with a suitable solvent, which was sonicated and then filtered through Whatman filter paper. The absorbance for the blank (solvent without drug) was recorded in a UV–vis spectrophotometer. A calibration curve was obtained for determining the concentration of the drug. The absorbance was fitted in the calibration curve to obtain the concentration of the drug.

In Vitro Drug Release Studies

For the study of in vitro drug release, 2 g of each formulation was taken in a dialysis bag (dialysis membrane-110, pore size 2.4 nm), and the two ends of the bag were sealed. The receptor medium consisted of freshly prepared buffer solution of pH 7.4 taken in a 250 mL beaker. The sealed dialysis bag containing the formulations was dropped into the release medium. The entire system was kept at 37 °C with continuous magnetic stirring of the release medium. A 3 mL portion of solution from the receptor compartment was withdrawn at predetermined time intervals, and samples were analyzed using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer at 303 nm to assess the drug released. Calibration curves of MTX were used to calculate the concentration of the drug.

Evaluation of the Anti-inflammatory Effect of MTX-Loaded Formulations

The anti-inflammatory effect of MTX-loaded formulations was evaluated using bovine serum albumin (BSA) and egg albumin denaturation assay. These assays evaluate the ability of the formulations to inhibit heat-induced protein denaturation; together, they provide complementary evidence regarding the capacity of the formulations to prevent protein structural alteration, thereby indicating their potential anti-inflammatory activity.

Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) Denaturation Assay

The test solution (0.5 mL) was prepared by mixing 0.45 mL of bovine serum albumin (5%W/V aqueous solution) and 0.05 mL of different concentrations of F1, F2, F3, and F4. To minimize interference from overlapping absorbance between BSA and the test samples, we prepared controls were prepared. The absorbance values of these blanks were subtracted from the corresponding sample readings to ensure that the final measurements reflected protein denaturation rather than the intrinsic absorbance of the test compounds. For test control, 0.05 mL of distilled water was used instead of emulgels (F1, F2, F3, and F4), while product control lacked BSA. Diclofenac sodium was used as a standard for this assay.

The pH of all prepared solutions was standardized to 6.3 using 1 N HCl. The samples were initially incubated at 37 °C for 20 min, followed by a controlled temperature increase to 57 °C for 3 min. After allowing the solutions to return to room temperature, 2.5 mL of phosphate buffer was added to each sample. − The percentage inhibition of protein denaturation of different concentrations at 280 nm was calculated using the formula percentageinhibition=100−[absorbanceoftestsolution−absorbanceofproductcontrolabsorbanceoftestcontrol]×100%

Egg Albumin Denaturation Assay

The test solution (5 mL) consisted of 0.2 mL of egg albumin, 2.8 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and 2 mL of the formulations (F1, F2, F3, and F4) at different concentrations of 50, 100, 250, and 500 μg/mL, respectively. Mathematical Modeling of the Drug Release Kinetics

To understand the drug release kinetics of the emulsifying agent, a model-dependent mathematical approach was used. Two different kinetic models were employed to elucidate the drug release behavior.

Weibull Model

The Weibull model is one of such approaches useful for matrix-type drug delivery typically written as M(t)=M∞[1−exp(−atb)] where M(t) is the cumulative amount of drug released at time t, M ∞ is the total drug to be released, "a" denotes a scale parameter that describes the time dependence, and "b" describes the shape of the dissolution curve progression. These Weibull parameters offer direct mechanistic interpretation (shape parameter b indicates release mechanism: b < 1 for diffusion, b ≈ 1 for first-order, and b > 1 for complex erosion/swelling), making it invaluable for comparing release profiles of different matrix-type drug delivery systems. To estimate the optimal values of the parameters M ∞, a, and b in this equation, a nonlinear curve fitting procedure in MATLAB using the Nelder–Mead simplex algorithm via the fminsearch function was employed. This method minimizes the weighted sum of squared residuals between the experimental dissolution data and the model predictions. To quantitatively assess the quality of the fits of the Weibull model to the experimental dissolution data, the coefficient of determination, R 2, was calculated for each formulation. The R 2 metric measures the proportion of variance in the observed data explained by the model, provides a standardized measure of goodness-of-fit, and is expressed as R2=1−∑i=1n(Mi−M̂i)2∑i=1n(Mi−M̅i)2 where Mi is the observed cumulative drug release values, M̂ i is the model-predicted values, and M̅ i is the mean of the observed data. A value of R 2 close to 1 indicates an excellent fit, implying that the model reliably represents the drug release kinetics. Conversely, lower values suggest model inadequacies or data variability not captured by the Weibull parameters.

Michaelis–Menten Equation

The basic Michaelis–Menten equation for a concentration-dependent rate process is V(C)=Vmax+CKm+C 1 where V(C) is the rate of the process (e.g., rate of drug metabolism or elimination), C is the drug concentration, V max is the maximum achievable rate when all binding sites are saturated, and K m is the Michaelis constant, defined as the concentration at which the rate is half of V max. In the present work, we use the same functional form as an empirical model to fit cumulative drug release profiles. Instead of V(C) as a function of concentration, we consider the cumulative amount of drug released M(t) as a function of time t, and we model it by a Michaelis–Menten-type saturation curve: M(t)=Vmax+tKm+t 2 where V max now represents the asymptotic maximum cumulative release (for example, in % of dose) and K m is an empirical time constant that indicates how quickly the system approaches saturation.

Cytotoxicity Assay

MTT-Based Cytotoxicity Assay in Healthy PBMC and Splenocytes

The cytotoxic effects of F1, F2, F3, and F4 were tested on healthy peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) and splenocyte cells using the MTT assay. The MTT assay is a widely used method to assess cell viability by measuring the enzymatic reduction of MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) into an insoluble purple formazan product in metabolically active cells. For this assay, PBMC and splenocytes (1 × 106 cells) were treated with 100 μM/mL of the compounds over a time course ranging from 24 to 96 h. After a 24 h incubation at 37 °C in a 5% CO2, 20 μL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added to each well and incubated for an additional 2 h in the dark. The resulting formazan crystals were dissolved in 100 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader.

The percentage of cell inhibition was calculated using the following formula %cellviability=[absorbanceoftestabsorbanceofcontrol]×100

Computational Details

To understand the role of the major components, present in Aloe vera gel in carrying and subsequent delivering of MTX, we have selected six components of Aloe vera, viz., aloe emodin, esculetin, aloin, aloesaponarin, esculetin, and aloesin and investigated their possible interaction with the MTX drug. All the structures were fully optimized in the gas phase using the B3LYP-D3­(BJ)/def2-TZVP level. − This method uses hybrid GGA functional , with third order dispersion correction and Becke–Johnson damping function. Harmonic vibrational frequency calculations confirmed that they were local minima on the potential energy surface. All these calculations were performed using Gaussian 16 suite of program. Implicit solvent (water and formic acid)-phase calculations were performed using a polarizable continuum model (PCM). Topology of electron density was analyzed within the realm of atoms in molecules (AIM) using Multiwfn program code. Energies were corrected for the basis set superposition error (BSEE) by using counterpoise of the order two method. Dynamic stability of the adducts was investigated using Born–Oppenheimer molecular dynamics (BOMD) simulations at room temperature (300 K) using the B3LYP/def2-TZVP level for a duration of 20 ps with a time frame of 0.5 fs. BOMD calculations were also performed using the Gaussian 16 suite of program.

Molecular Docking Studies

Molecular docking was carried out for the six optimized adducts, viz, MTX-Aloin, MTX-Aloe emodin, MTX-Aloesin, MTX-Chrysophanol, MTX-Aloesaponarin, and MTX-Esculetin using AutoDock Vina to understand the binding ability of these adducts into the active site of the target COX-2. The 3D crystallized structure of the target COX-2 (PDB ID: 6COX) was obtained from RCSB-PDB. The grid box was constructed in such a way that it covers all of the active site residues. It was centered at 23.531, 24.497, and 43.373 Å in x, y, and z coordinates, respectively, and the box size was 66 × 68 × 58 points along with an exhaustiveness of 8. Using the Discovery Studio Visualizer, the findings were manually analyzed.

All the formulations were yellowish white in color with a creamy, homogeneous texture with a glossy appearance. There was no phase separation observed in any of the formulations.

Rheological Study Results

Rheological parameters, including the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″), play a critical role in the emulgel formulation. The storage modulus (G′) reflects the elastic behavior of the system, whereas the loss modulus (G″) represents its viscous response. Increased formulation viscosity can reduce drug permeation, while shear-thinning behavior enhances the spreadability and permeability under applied stress. The rheological behavior of the formulations is illustrated in Figures and .

Dynamic mechanical analysis shows the variation of storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) of the four formulations.

Viscosity vs shear rate curve of the emulgel formulations.

Dynamic mechanical analysis using frequency sweep tests were performed to study the gel strength of the formulations. The value of G′ for all four formulations is larger than G″ in the entire frequency range examined, indicating the presence of a strong gel structure of the formulations. Moreover, G′ and G″ do not intersect (no crossover point at which G′ is equal to G″) at the frequency range studied, which indicates that formulated gels do not show gel-to-sol transformation. This is a sign of strong internal forces keeping the components of formulation together. ,

Flow measurements showed that all the formulations exhibited shear-thinning properties as the viscosity of all the formulations decreased with increasing the shear rate. Despite the high shear-thinning property of the formulations, none of them had viscosities higher than 1400 mPa·s even at high shear rates (100 s–1). This confirms the well-structured texture of the formulations.

Zeta Potential Results

Zeta potential represents the surface charge of particles and contributes significantly to the stability of a formulation. Higher absolute zeta potential values generate stronger electrostatic repulsive forces, which prevent particle aggregation and thereby enhance the formulation stability. All formulations exhibited negative zeta potential values ranging from −54 ± 3.93 mV to −68 ± 1.80 mV (Figure ). The high negative zeta potential observed in these formulations was primarily attributed to the presence of ionizable acidic polysaccharides (such as acemannan, glucomannan, and pectic substances) and phenolic compounds in aloe vera gel, which impart negative surface charges upon ionization. This pronounced negative zeta potential enhances electrostatic repulsion between particles, thereby improving the physical stability of the emulgel. Based on recent literature, formulations with zeta potential values exceeding ± 30 mV are generally regarded as electrostatically stable, whereas those with values below ±10 mV tend to be unstable due to insufficient electrostatic repulsion. ,

Evaluation of the Antiarthritic Effect of MTX-Loaded Formulations on Inhibition of Protein Denaturation

Inflammation, which is characterized by redness, discomfort, swelling, and a loss of function in the affected area, has been linked to protein denaturation. This primarily occurs when a protein loses its molecular structure and activities, causing it to become denatured. As a result, formulations that can stop these alterations and stop protein denaturation may be useful as anti-inflammatory drugs.

Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) Denaturation Assay Results

Bovine serum albumin (BSA) is a commonly used model protein for drug delivery due to its high similarity (76%) to human serum albumin, the main protein in human plasma. In the BSA denaturation assay for anti-inflammatory activity, absorbance is measured at 280 nm because BSA naturally absorbs strongly at this wavelength due to aromatic amino acids. Heat causes BSA to denature and increase absorbance, while anti-inflammatory compounds reduce this effect, resulting in lower readings. Therefore, changes at 280 nm provide a simple and reliable measure of sample’s ability to inhibit protein denaturation.

Several studies have reported the anti-inflammatory potential of Aloe vera using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as a model protein for protein denaturation assays. In the present study, the pronounced anti-inflammatory activity was primarily attributed to the combined Aloe–MTX system rather than to Aloe vera or MTX administered individually. Moreover, additional BSA denaturation assays were performed with appropriate experimental controls, enabling a clear differentiation between the effects of MTX alone and those of the combined Aloe–MTX formulation.

From the experimental findings, it was found that there was not much difference between the formulated gels and standard diclofenac gel. Standard gel showed minimum 97 ± 0.52% to maximum 100 ± 0.29% ability to inhibit the BSA protein denaturation, − and formulated gels have 88 ± 0.51% to 94 ± 0.15% ability to inhibit protein denaturation in dose-dependent manner (Figure ).

Egg albumin was employed as a model protein in the study. The primary objective of the egg albumin denaturation assay was to evaluate the ability of the formulations to prevent or suppress protein denaturation under the defined experimental conditions. In this assay, the anti-inflammatory potential of the formulations was assessed based on their capacity to inhibit egg albumin denaturation. The UV–visible absorbance spectrum of the protein exhibited λmax at 280 nm, which is consistent with previously reported studies. Based on the experimental results, no substantial difference was observed between the formulated gels and standard diclofenac gel. In Vitro Drug Release

The in vitro drug release efficiency was checked for each formulation for the period of 30 h, and the results are represented in Figure . Each of the formulations was different in the percentage of the drug delivered with time. In the case of all the formulations, more than 60% of the drug was released in the first 6 h of treatment and the sustained release behavior continued even up to 30 h, indicating that the drug is released from the formulation slowly. Increasing the emulsifying agent ratio increases the drug release from the formulation. The order of drug release was found to be F2 > F1 > F3 > F4. In F1 and F2, the concentration of the emulsifying agent is higher than that in F3, but F4 contains a higher concentration of Carbopol. From the results, it was observed that higher concentration of emulsifying agents lowers the interfacial tension between oily and aqueous layer in the dispersion medium. As a result, the penetration of the dissolution medium into the formulation increases with increase in the drug release capacity.

In vitro drug release by formulations F1–F4.

Drug release from the formulation is inversely proportional to the concentration of gelling agent, which may be due to the higher concentration of Carbopol in the gel network making it more complex, leading to a longer pathway for the drug to diffuse through the membrane.

These results accord with those of the Fong Yen et al. investigation. In their investigation, raising the Carbopol percentage to 1.00% w/w, 2.00% w/w, and 5.00% w/w resulted in a considerable reduction in drug release. They concluded that the percentages of Carbopol quantity and drug release are inversely proportional.

Effects of Aloe Vera on the Drug Release Profile of the Formulation

To elucidate the influence of Aloe vera gel on the drug release behavior, four corresponding methotrexate-loaded control formulations devoid of Aloe vera (non-Aloe vera–MTX formulation) were prepared, maintaining the same composition as shown in Table . These formulations were systematically evaluated for their in vitro drug release profiles and compared with those of the Aloe vera-containing formulations.

The in vitro drug release profiles of the non-Aloe vera–MTX formulations exhibited a markedly different pattern compared with the Aloe vera-based MTX emulgels. More than 80% of drug was released within the first 2 h, indicating a pronounced burst release effect (Figure ). In contrast, the presence of aloe vera gel significantly modulated the release kinetics, resulting in a controlled and sustained drug release extending up to 30 h.

Mathematical Modeling of the Drug Release Kinetics Results

In order to validate the experimental results, the Weibull model was fitted to four experimental dissolution data sets (F1, F2, F3, and F4) spanning a 30 h time course. Figure illustrates the experimental dissolution data alongside the corresponding curve fits obtained using the Weibull model. The associated R 2 values indicate that the Weibull function provides a reliable fit across all experimental data sets. The parameter b for each data set (F1, F2, F3, and F4) has values in a range of 1.5–2.0, indicating a sigmoidal release pattern with an initial lag phase. This behavior can be attributed to hydration, swelling, and structural relaxation of the Aloe vera-based emulgel matrix prior to effective drug diffusion. Despite the initial delay, methotrexate release proceeded gradually over the entire study period without a burst release, confirming a slow, matrix-controlled release mechanism. The sigmoid curve in Figure illustrates how the release starts off slowly before rising sharply and leveling off as it gets closer to its maximum value, which supports the experimental findings represented by Figure .

Curve fitting using the Weibull function for the drug release kinetics by the formulations F1, F2, F3, and F4.

Mathematical Modeling of the Drug Release Kinetics of Non-Aloe Vera–MTX Formulations

To corroborate the experimental dissolution behavior, the cumulative release data of four non-Aloe vera–MTX formulations (F1–F4) were mathematically modeled using the Michaelis–Menten kinetic equation over a 30 h release period. Figure presents the experimental dissolution profiles together with the corresponding nonlinear regression fits generated using the Michaelis–Menten model. The high coefficients of determination (R 2 = 0.88–0.98) obtained for all data sets demonstrate a good agreement between the experimental data and the proposed kinetic model, confirming the suitability of the Michaelis–Menten function in describing the release behavior of the non-Aloe vera–MTX formulations.

The analysis revealed that the non-Aloe vera– MTX formulations followed Michaelis–Menten kinetics, whereas the Aloe vera–MTX formulations were best described by the Weibull model. This change in the release kinetics indicates that the incorporation of Aloe vera alters the release behavior.

Computational Results

The DFT and molecular docking analyses were performed to evaluate the theoretical feasibility of noncovalent interactions between methotrexate and representative Aloe vera phytochemicals under idealized molecular conditions. Six major components of Aloe vera, viz., aloe emodin, esculetin, aloin, aloesaponarin, chrysophanol, and aloesin, were chosen, and their possible interaction with MTX drug was investigated using DFT. Figure shows the optimized geometries of the adducts formed. Aloe emodin forms three O···H hydrogen bonds with MTX with O···H distances of 1.808, 2.339, and 2.906 Å. The BSSE-corrected binding energy for aloe emodin was found to be −10.3 kcal/mol. Similarly, esculetin, chrysophanol, and aloesaponarin bind with MTX through the formation of three O···H hydrogen bonds with binding energies of −12.5 kcal/mol, −13.2 kcal/mol, and −15.3 kcal/mol, respectively. Aloin and aloesin bind with MTX forming two O···H hydrogen bonds with binding energies of −14.2 and −16.3 kcal/mol. The binding energies in water medium increase, while those in a formic acid medium decrease. For example, the binding energy for aloe emodin in a water medium becomes −12.1 kcal/mol, while it becomes −9.7 kcal/mol in a formic acid medium. Similarly, the binding energies of esculetin become −13.4 and −11.1 kcal/mol, respectively, in water and formic acid media. Binding energy for aloin increases in the water medium (−15.4 kcal/mol) and decreases in formic acid (−12.8 kcal/mol). The same trend is also observed for aloesin (−17.1 kcal/mol in water and −15.3 kcal/mol in formic acid). Thus, it indicates that a neutral polar medium such as water enhances the binding energy, while an acidic medium decreases the same. However, the changes in binding energy are not significant and thus, all the considered Aloe vera components can be considered as good carriers of MTX drug. The calculated O···H distances are all smaller than 3.1 Å, suggesting formation of hydrogen bonds. To confirm the presence of hydrogen bond formation, we performed topological analysis of electron density within the realm of atoms in molecules (AIM). Figure shows the molecular graphs of the adducts. Clear formation of O···H bond paths along with their (3, −1) bond critical points was observed confirming the formation of hydrogen bonds along with other noncovalent interactions.

Optimized geometries of the adducts of the considered components present in Aloe vera with the MTX drug. Bond lengths are in Å.

AIM analysis of the adducts. Bond critical points are colored red dots.

To investigate the dynamic stability of the hydrogen bonded adducts, we performed ab initio Born–Oppenheimer molecular dynamics (BOMD) simulations at room temperature (300 K) using the B3LYP/def2-TZVP level for a duration of 20 ps with a time frame of 0.5 fs. For the BOMD analyses, we choose aloe emodin and aloesin components, as they showed the smallest and largest binding energies with MTX drug. Figure shows the root-mean-square deviation versus time plot. It is evident from Figure that there is no significant reversible jump in the BOMD plot, indicating that the adducts are dynamically stable, at least within this time scale considered in the ab initio dynamics simulation. Although a small-time scale is considered for the ab initio dynamics simulation, however, the prediction herein may be considered realistic due to the fact that both nuclear and electronic fluctuations are considered in ab initio dynamics. Hence, it may be envisioned that the components present in Aloe vera may act as good carriers for the MTX drug and may be released in due course with sufficient efficiency. However, these results do not constitute direct evidence of such interactions occurring within the formulated emulgel system but rather provide a hypothesis-generating framework that may guide future experimental validation.

Conclusions

Transdermal drug delivery systems, such as emulgels, have gained significant attention for delivering drugs through the skin while bypassing the gastrointestinal tract and first-pass metabolism, and Aloe vera is an excellent candidate for formulating transdermal emulgels. Present study clearly reveals that the antiarthritic drug MTX can be loaded in Aloe vera-based emulgel efficiently. All the prepared formulations showed acceptable physical properties, pH, drug content, viscosity, and anti-inflammatory activity. Among the formulations, F2 showed lesser globule size and highest drug release rate. The in vitro release studies demonstrated sustained MTX release from the Aloe vera-based emulgels. In contrast, non-Aloe vera MTX formulations exhibited a rapid release profile, with more than 80% of the drug released within 2 h, indicative of a pronounced burst effect. Kinetic modeling showed that non-Aloe vera formulations followed Michaelis–Menten kinetics, whereas Aloe vera–MTX emulgels were best described by the Weibull model, suggesting that Aloe vera modulates the drug release behavior. The anti-inflammatory activity of the preparation was experimentally evaluated by measuring its ability to inhibit bovine serum protein and egg protein denaturation. The results proved that the formulations show high ability (around 90% of diclofenac) to inhibit protein denaturation leading to significant anti-inflammatory effect. Cytotoxicity study of the MTX-loaded Aloe vera emulgels F1, F2, F3, and F4 showed minimal to negligible cytotoxicity in normal/healthy PBMCs and splenocytes over a period of 24–96 h, indicating the safety for normal cell lines. Moreover, six major components of Aloe vera, viz., aloe emodin, esculetin, aloin, aloesaponarin, chrysophanol, and aloesin, were chosen and their possible interaction with MTX drug was investigated using DFT. The studies revealed that the components show H bonding and other noncovalent interactions with MTX thereby acting as good carriers for the drug and helping in its sustained and targeted release. In addition, the theoretical studies including docking and molecular dynamics simulation indicated that the adducts formed by these components with the drug MTX show better COX-2 inhibitory activity than that of the drug taken alone. The computational results indicate a theoretical propensity for noncovalent interactions between methotrexate and aloe vera phytochemicals. However, these findings are hypothesis-generating and require experimental validation.
