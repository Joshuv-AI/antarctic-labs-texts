Microglia-specific NLRP3 inhibition mitigates hippocampal neuroinflammation and cognitive deficits after hemorrhagic shock with resuscitation
Authors: Ya-Mei Zhang, Meng-Yue Wang, Zhong-Yong Zhang, Xin-Yu Yang, Hong-Lian Duan, Jian-Kai Sun, Xiao-Yi Ma, Rong-Xin Song, Jing-Yu Hui, Dong-Xue Zhang, Li-Min Zhang, Hui-Tao Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCognitive dysfunction is a prevalent mental health problem following hemorrhagic shock with resuscitation (HSR). Our previous work indicated that neuroinflammation caused by nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3), which is modulated by glial cells, such as microglia, is potentially significant in developing emotional and cognitive dysfunction. However, little is known about the potential of microglial NLRP3 to treat HSR-induced cognitive dysfunction. Therefore, this study established an HSR rodent model to investigate whether microglial NLRP3 represents a potential therapeutic target for improving cognitive dysfunction after HSR.MethodsAn HSR model was developed by inducing bleeding and retransfusion in mice. The Morris water maze and Novel object recognition tests were used for behavioral evaluation. To selectively knock out the NLRP3 inflammasome in microglia, the AAV‑CX3CR1‑Cre (pAAV‑CX3CR1‑NLS‑Cre‑P2A‑EGFP‑3xFLAG‑WPRE) virus was stereotaxically injected into the hippocampal CA1 region of male C57BL/6 mice with NLRP3flox/flox. Immunofluorescence and local field potential recordings were used to assess pathological alterations at different intervals after injury.ResultsOur findings indicated that the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 significantly reversed HSR-induced lower recognition index, increased escape latency, reduced platform crossings, decreased θ and γ power and θ–γ phase coupling, decreased intensity of PSD95 and Synaptophysin, increased number of Iba1+ cells, normalized soma size and total process length cell of Iba1, and increased colocalization of cleaved caspase-1 and interleukin-18 with Iba-1 in the CA1 area of the hippocampus. It also significantly alleviated the HSR-induced cognitive dysfunction. Moreover, we observed that HSR-induced cognitive dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and synaptic plasticity damage may be reversed by knocking out NLRP3 in the hippocampal CA1 microglia.ConclusionOur study demonstrates that inhibition of microglia‑specific NLRP3 can mitigate cognitive impairment following HSR, identifying microglial NLRP3 as a promising therapeutic target. This effect may be associated with suppressing pyroptosis via the NLRP3 signaling pathway in microglia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12993-026-00354-w.Keywords: Hemorrhagic shock with resuscitation, Microglia, Cognitive dysfunction, NLRP3, Inflammation

Introduction

Hemorrhagic shock is common in emergency departments and operating rooms [1]. It significantly impairs social and occupational functioning and is the leading cause of death and disability among adults [2]. It can be effectively managed with hemostasis, fluid resuscitation to maintain tissue oxygenation and coagulation support [3]. However, hemorrhagic shock with resuscitation (HSR) results in incomplete cerebral ischemia, leading to brain reperfusion injury [4]. Survivors frequently experience adverse effects such as chronic critical illness, which includes various functional disorders such as anxiety, depression, and cognitive dysfunction [5]. Cognitive dysfunction, a prevalent symptom, is characterized by impaired memory, attention, executive function, reasoning, thinking, and other functional abnormalities [6]. However, a complete understanding of the best therapies for patients with memory and cognitive deficits following HSR is lacking. This emphasizes the importance of investigating fundamental causes and considering novel and efficient approaches to address this issue.

The hippocampus, a crucial limbic system component, is involved in pattern recognition, long-term memory, near-memory circuits, novelty perception, sleep-dependent memory consolidation, and spatial navigation [7, 8]. Research has indicated that neuroplasticity in memory acquisition, storage, and retrieval is mediated by the CA1 area of the hippocampus [9]. Furthermore, recent evidence suggests that neuronal loss and synaptic degradation in the hippocampal CA1 area are directly associated with HSR-induced cognitive deficits [10].

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3)-mediated pyroptosis in the inflammatory response to HSR [11, 12]. The formation of cleaved caspase-1 is triggered by NLRP3 activation, which amplifies the inflammatory response by producing inflammatory molecules such as interleukin 18 (IL-18) [13]. NLRP3, caspase-1, and IL-18 have been demonstrated to be activated in rodent models of cognitive impairment and individuals with cognitive disorders [14]. Microglia are immune cells in the central nervous system (CNS) that monitor the neural environment and remove cellular debris and pathogens [15]. However, study has indicated that HSR may overactivate reactive microglia, resulting in increased neuroinflammation and neurofunctional disorders [3]. The inhibition of abnormal microglial activation has been reported to have an excellent neuroprotective effect against cognitive dysfunction [16]. Our previous work indicated that glial cell-mediated chronic neuroinflammation linked to NLRP3 is probably a key factor in the development of late-stage inflammation after traumatic stress [3, 17]. In a chronic moderate stress (CMS) mouse model, neurofunctional abnormalities can be successfully corrected by targeting reactive microglia and subsequent NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis [18]. However, the mechanism through which microglial NLRP3 contributes to post-HSR cognitive dysfunction remains unclear. Therefore, we explored the putative role of NLRP3 in microglia during HSR-induced cognitive dysfunction, and evaluated whether microglial NLRP3 represents a viable molecular target for future therapeutic development.

Notably, pharmacological inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome using MCC950 has been shown to reduce acute brain injury in models of intracerebral hemorrhage [19]. However, it remains unknown whether microglial NLRP3—as opposed to NLRP3 in other CNS cell types or peripheral immune compartments—is the critical pathogenic driver of long-term cognitive dysfunction following global ischemic insults such as HSR. Moreover, no prior study has examined whether microglia-specific NLRP3 deletion can restore hippocampal synaptic integrity, neural network oscillations, and cognitive function at chronic time points after systemic hypovolemic injury. The present study was designed to address these specific questions.

In this study, we developed an HSR rodent model to evaluate the modulation of NLRP3 in microglia as a potential therapeutic option for improving the behaviors associated with cognitive impairment following HSR. Given that cognitive dysfunction in HSR survivors manifests over weeks to months, we specifically examined chronic time points (30–36 days post‑HSR) to evaluate persistent neuroinflammatory and synaptic pathology.

Materials and methods

Animals and grouping

The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Hebei Province Cangzhou Hospital of Integrated Traditional and Western Medicine approved the experimental methods used in this study (approval number: CZX2022091). The Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines were strictly followed for all aspects of this study. Supplement File 1 provides comprehensive information on the required resources, such as software, reagents, and antibodies. Male C57BL/6 wild-type and NLRP3flox/flox mice, aged 9–10 weeks and weighing 25–30 g, were housed in standard cages at 24 ± 1 °C with 12-h light/dark cycles and unrestricted access to food and water.

In the first stage, wild-type mice were randomly assigned to two groups: (1) Sham (n = 12) and (2) HSR (n = 12). In the second stage, wild-type mice were randomly assigned to four groups: (1) Sham + vehicle (n = 12), (2) Sham + MCC950 (n = 12), (3) HSR + vehicle (n = 12), and (4) HSR + MCC950 (n = 12). In the third stage, NLRP3flox/flox mice were randomly assigned to two groups: (1) HSR + AAV-vehicle (n = 12) and (2) HSR + AAV-Cre (n = 12).

HSR model

Sevoflurane anesthesia was used to induce intermittent bleeding and retransfusion during the HSR operation in mice. The three steps of the HSR procedure were the same as in our previous studies [3, 4]: (1) Hemorrhagic Phase (15 min): A hypotensive level of 30–35 mmHg was purposefully achieved by lowering the blood pressure. (2) Maintenance Phase (15 min): A hypotensive blood pressure level of 30–35 mmHg was maintained. (3) The Resuscitation Phase (15 min) allowed the blood pressure to recover to physiologically normal levels gradually. Mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation if their blood pressure did not return to normal after the transfusion. 2 µl of MCC950 (10 mg/kg, # HY-12815 A/CS-5427, MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) was administered into the left lateral ventricle (0.5 mm behind the anterior fontanelle, 1 mm left to the median, 2.5 mm below the skull surface) immediately after model establishment (Fig. 1). ICV injection was chosen to achieve high and consistent brain bioavailability of MCC950, bypassing the blood-brain barrier, which the compound penetrates poorly, and to restrict NLRP3 inhibition primarily to the CNS compartment for mechanistic specificity.

Injection of AAV adenovirus

A stereotactic device (Beijing Zhongshi Technology Co., Ltd., China) was used to locate the hippocampal CA1 area of the mice 21 days before HSR modeling. Mice in the HSR + AAV-Cre group were injected with the adenovirus pAAV-CX3CR1-NLS-Cre-P2A-EGFP-3xFLAG-WPRE (Cat#SD0314, OBiO Technology Corp.,Ltd, Shanghai, China) in the hippocampal CA1 region 21 days before HSR modeling to establish a microglia-specific NLRP3 gene knockout model. Mice in the HSR + AAV-vehicle group were only injected with pAAV-CX3CR1-EGFP-3xFLAG-WPRE (Cat#SD0311, OBiO Technology Corp.,Ltd, Shanghai, China) in the same region as a control. The coordinates were 1.5 mm in depth, 1.2 mm lateral to the midline, and 2 mm posterior to the bregma. A microinjection pump (Baoding Longer Peristaltic Pump Co., Ltd., China) was used to bilaterally inject adenovirus (Shanghai Hivogene Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) with a volume of 0.5 µL on each side into the hippocampus CA1 area at a rate of 0.05 µL/min.

Behavioral tests

Novel object recognition (NOR)

Cognitive and memory alterations in mice were evaluated using NOR scores obtained 30 days after HSR development [20]. The mice were habituated to the test setting for 30 min before the test. On the first day of NOR, each mouse was positioned in the middle of a 40 × 40 × 50 cm white box for 5 min to adjust to its new surroundings. After an hour of rest, the mice were placed back in the box containing the symmetrically positioned pair of identical objects and were allowed 5 min to explore them. On the second day of the NOR, item C was used instead of the first, and the investigation was videotaped for 5 min. Labmaze 3.0 (Beijing Zhongshidichuang Science and Technology Development Co. Ltd., Beijing, China) was used to record and evaluate the time spent exploring the novel object, the familiar object, and the overall distance covered on the second day of the NOR. The recognition index (RI) was calculated as follows: RI = time spent exploring the novel object/(time spent exploring the novel object + time exploring the familiar objects) × 100%.

Morris water maze (MWM) test

The MWM test was performed following NOR to assess cognitive and memory alterations in mice 32 days after HSR [20]. The MWM test was performed following NOR to assess spatial learning and memory in mice 32 days after HSR. The apparatus consisted of a circular tank (120 cm in diameter, 50 cm in height) filled with water (22 ± 1 °C) to a depth of 30 cm. The water was made opaque with non-toxic white titanium dioxide to conceal the platform. The tank was divided into four equally sized quadrants (I–IV), and a transparent escape platform (8 cm in diameter) was placed in the center of the target quadrant, submerged 2 cm below the water surface. Distinct visual cues of different shapes and colors were affixed to the interior walls of the tank and remained constant throughout the experiment.Training phase (days 32–35 post‑HSR): Each mouse received four trials per day (one from each quadrant, with the order of starting positions pseudo‑randomized across days), for a total of 16 trials over four consecutive days. Labmaze 3.0 (Beijing Zhongshidichuang Science and Technology Development Co. Ltd., Beijing, China) was used to measure escape latency during the training phase, periods spent crossing the platform, distance and time spent in the target quadrant, overall distance, and average speed during the testing day.

Immunofluorescence

Iba-1 intensities, colocalization of NLRP3, IL-18 and cleaved caspase-1 with microglia, and intensity of PSD95 and Synaptophysin were evaluated using immunofluorescence methods. Six mice from each group were anesthetized 36 days after the modeling procedure using 7%–8% sevoflurane. Subsequently, the mice were administered normal saline and 10% neutral-buffered formalin via the left cardiac apex. Brain tissue samples were fixed for 48 h in 10% neutral-buffered formalin and dehydrated using graded alcohol. Tissues were embedded in paraffin and incubated with xylene. Paraffin-embedded tissues were cut into 4 μm thick slices in the coronal plane. Following hydration, sections were incubated in sodium citrate for 5 min at 0.15 MPa to retrieve the antigen. After permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 20 min, the sections were blocked with a blocking solution for 1 h. Sections were incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C. These antibodies included polyclonal rabbit anti-cleaved caspase-1 (1:200, AF4022; Affinity Biosciences, Jiangsu, China), primary monoclonal rabbit anti-postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD95) (1:200, R10175046; Wanleibio, Shenyang, China), primary monoclonal rabbit anti-Synaptophysin(1:200, GB11553-100; Servicebio, Wuhan, China), polyclonal goat anti-Iba-1 (1:100, GB15105-100, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), primary polyclonal rabbit anti-NLRP3 (1:200, 30109-1-AP; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), primary mouse monoclonal anti-NeuN antibody (1:100, ab104224, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), primary rabbit polyclonal anti-EGFP antibody (1:100, K106580P, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and polyclonal rabbit antibody against IL-18 (1:200; 10663-1-AP; Proteintech, Wuhan, China). The sections were then treated with secondary antibodies, including goat anti-mouse IgG conjugated to CyTM3 (1:500), goat anti-rabbit IgG conjugated to FITC (1:500), and anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 IgG (1:500). Nuclei were stained with 4’-6’-diamino-2-phenylindole. To maintain consistency, all immunostaining techniques were performed under the same conditions. Images were acquired using a motorized-stage confocal laser scanning microscope. Double-labeled cells were manually counted by two blinded researchers separately in four randomly chosen 400X microscopic fields of view. To ensure data reliability, the averages of these numbers were computed and verified for consistency. Furthermore, ImageJ software was used to assess the fluorescence intensity and improve the accuracy of our cell counts. This program improved the precision of our immunofluorescence analysis by facilitating the quantification of double-labeled cells.

Electrophysiological recordings: local field potential (LFP) recordings

After being anesthetized with 6%–8% isoflurane, the mice were placed firmly on a stereotactic device. After the head hair was removed, a 1% lidocaine incision block was applied, and the area was cleaned with alcohol. A longitudinal incision was made from the midline of the skull to reveal the fontanelle and the occipital bone. The CA1 region of the hippocampus was located on the right parietal bone of the mouse (2 mm posterior to the bregma, 1.2 mm lateral, and 1.5 mm deep) using stereotactic equipment and a mouse brain atlas. The site was marked meticulously with black ink. Using a micro-hole dental drill, a 3 mm circular bone window was created in the left parietal bone at the desired site. Using a bone window, a manipulator was used to carefully lower the 8-channel microwire array electrode (Jiangsu Brain Medical Technology Co. Ltd., Nanjing, China) into the hippocampal CA1 region.

Field potentials (local field potentials, LFPs) were measured using a NeuroLego amplifier (Jiangsu Brain Medical Technology Co. Ltd., Nanjing, China) during the NOR test on the 31st day following HSR surgery. The recorded signals were passed through a bandpass filter in the frequency range of 0.3–300 Hz. These signals were amplified and digitally recorded at a sampling rate of 2 kHz. A 50 Hz notch filter was added to the recorded LFP to reduce any power-line interference. The broadband data were downsampled to 1000 Hz for LFP analysis, emphasizing changes in LFP during the first 5 s after exposure to a novel item in the NOR test. NeuroExplorer software (Plexon Inc., Dallas, TX, USA) was used to analyze the EEG data in this study.

Statistical analysis

Based on the impact sizes predicted from our preliminary data, statistical analysis was conducted using SAS software (version 9.1), with a significance threshold of 5% (α = 0.05) and an objective of 80% power (1 - β = 0.8). Descriptive statistics are presented as the mean ± SD. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0.1). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to determine the normality of the data, and Levene’s test was used to assess the homogeneity of the variance. When heteroscedasticity was detected in the data, it was corrected using logarithmic data transformation. The t-test was used to compare the means of the two groups. One-way or two-way analysis of variance with Tukey’s post-hoc test was used for group comparisons. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (Supplemental File 2).

Results

HSR induces cognitive dysfunction

Analysis of NOR revealed that HSR-treated mice exhibited a significantly lower recognition index than Sham-treated mice (Fig. 2A–B). Furthermore, no discernible variation was observed in the average field speed of mice across the two groups (Figs. 2C). The MWM test results revealed a significant interaction in escape latency in the training phase between time and group (Fig. 2D–E). On the third and fourth days of the training period, mice exposed to HSR exhibited a significantly longer escape latency than the Sham group (Fig. 2D–E). During the testing phase, we observed that HSR significantly decreased platform crossing times compared to Sham (Fig. 2F). Consistent with the impaired memory retrieval, the time spent in the target quadrant during the probe trial was significantly reduced in the HSR group compared with the Sham group (Fig. 2G). Interestingly, the average speed of mice in the two groups did not differ significantly (Fig. 2H).

HSR induces reduced θ and γ power, θ-γ phase coupling in LFP recordings, and synaptic impairment

LFP in CA1 under exploratory settings was recorded within the first 5 s of the first exposure to a novel item. The NOR test revealed that HSR significantly decreased the θ and γ power in CA1 (Fig. 3A–F). Research has highlighted the importance of θ (4–8 Hz) and γ (30–100 Hz) oscillations in higher brain processes, including memory, emotion, and cognition [21, 22]. A mouse model demonstrated decreased theta-gamma phase-amplitude coupling linked to cognitive impairment [23, 24]. During the NOR test, the HSR group demonstrated a significant decrease in θ (Fig. 3G–H), γ power (Fig. 3I–J), and θ-γ phase coupling (Fig. 3K-L). The integrity of synaptic plasticity is crucial for maintaining normal neuronal electrophysiological activity, and the expression levels of PSD95 and Synaptophysin serve as key indicators reflecting alterations in synaptic plasticity. After HSR, mice showed significantly reduced fluorescence intensities of PSD95 and Synaptophysin in the hippocampal CA1 region, suggesting a corresponding effect on synaptic function (Fig. 4A-D). To determine whether HSR-induced cognitive and synaptic deficits are accompanied by overt neuronal loss, we quantified the density of NeuN positive pyramidal neurons in the hippocampal CA1 region at 36 days after HSR. The results showed no significant difference in the number of NeuN positive neurons between groups (Supplemental Fig. 2). These data indicate that the cognitive and synaptic impairments observed at this time point are primarily driven by HSR-induced synaptic dysfunction and neuroinflammation in the CA1 region, with the resultant electrophysiological disturbances ultimately contributing to cognitive dysfunction, rather than by overt neuronal cell death.

HSR exposure leads to the activation of microglia and neuroinflammatory response in the CA1

Abnormally activated microglia can dramatically accelerate traumatic stress-induced neuroinflammation and neurotoxicity by releasing proinflammatory mediators and cytokines [25]. Our previous research demonstrated a relationship between emotional dysregulation in HSR conditions and activation of NLRP3-induced pyroptosis pathways in microglia [3]. Accordingly, we evaluated the levels of IL-18 and caspase-1, which are two critical downstream molecules of the NLRP3 inflammasome and key components of pyroptosis [26]. At 36 days post-HSR, mice in the HSR group exhibited a significantly higher number of Iba1+ cells, normalized soma size and total process length cell of Iba1 in the CA1 area of the hippocampus (Fig. 5A–D) compared with the Sham control. After HSR, there was a significant increase in the colocalization of cleaved caspase-1and IL-18 with Iba-1, a microglial marker, in the CA1 regions compared to the Sham group (Fig. 5E–G).

MCC950 alleviated cognitive dysfunction, improved electrophysiological experiments and synaptic impairment, and reduced activation of microglia neuroinflammatory response in the CA1 of mice after HSR

The precise mechanism underlying HSR-induced cognitive impairment was investigated using MCC950, an NLRP3 inhibitor, in light of the activation of the NLRP3-induced pyroptosis pathways. Thirty-one days after HSR, NOR findings revealed no discernible difference in the average speed of the mice in the four groups (Fig. 6A–C). The NOR analysis revealed that mice treated with HSR exhibited a significantly lower recognition index than the Sham-treated group, whereas mice treated with HSR + MCC950 exhibited a significantly higher recognition index (Fig. 6B). The recognition index indicated no significant differences between the mice in the Sham + vehicle and Sham + MCC950 groups (Fig. 6B). According to MWM test findings, mice exposed to the HSR + vehicle exhibited a significantly longer escape latency on the third and fourth days of the training period than those in the Sham + vehicle group (Fig. 6D–E). However, in mice exposed to HSR + MCC950, MCC950 treatment reversed this increase (Fig. 6D–E). During the testing phase, we noticed that the HSR + vehicle significantly decreased the platform crossing times compared to the Sham + vehicle (Fig. 6F). Conversely, mice in the HSR + MCC950 group traversed the platform far more often than those in the HSR + vehicle group (Fig. 6F). Likewise, the target‑quadrant dwell time was significantly restored by MCC950 treatment (Fig. 6G). Notably, the average speed of mice in the four groups did not differ significantly (Fig. 6H).

Depletion of NLRP3 in microglia alleviated neuroinflammation involved in the pathological processes after HSR

Our next goal was to determine whether NLRP3 signaling plays a role in microglial activation after HSR. To selectively delete the NLRP3 inflammasome in microglia, the pAAV-CX3CR1-NLS-Cre-P2A-EGFP-3xFLAG-WPRE virus was stereotaxically injected into the hippocampal CA1 area of male C57BL/6 mice that were NLRP3flox/flox. The immunofluorescence results showed that, compared to AAV-CX3CR1-vehicle-treated mice, there was almost no colocalization of Iba1, a microglial marker, and NLRP3 in the hippocampal CA1 region of AAV-CX3CR1-Cre -treated mice, which demonstrated that NLRP3 was successfully and specifically knocked out in the microglia of the hippocampal CA1 region in this study (Fig. 10A–C). To rule out off‑target Cre expression and confirm the microglial specificity of the knockout, we performed double‑immunofluorescence labeling for EGFP (reporter of viral transduction) with NeuN (neuronal marker) and GFAP (astrocytic marker). EGFP fluorescence was detected exclusively in Iba1⁺microglia and was never observed in NeuN⁺ neurons or GFAP⁺ astrocytes (Supplemental Fig. 1). These data demonstrate that the AAV‑CX3CR1‑Cre approach achieves near‑complete, microglia‑specific ablation of NLRP3 in the hippocampal CA1 region, with no detectable off‑target effects in other major cell types. The NOR analysis revealed that the average speed of the mice in the two groups did not differ significantly (Fig. 10D–F). HSR + AAV-CX3CR1-Cre significantly increased the recognition index in mice exposed to HSR + AAV-CX3CR1-vehicle (Fig. 10D–F). Mice exposed to HSR + AAV- CX3CR1-vehicle exhibited significantly longer escape latency than those in the HSR + AAV- CX3CR1-Cre group, according to the results of the MWM test (Fig. 10G–H). During the testing phase, we discovered that mice in the HSR + AAV- CX3CR1-Cre group crossed the platform much more often than those in the HSR + AAV- CX3CR1-vehicle group (Fig. 10G–I). Concomitantly, the HSR + AAV‑CX3CR1‑Cre group spent significantly more time in the target quadrant than the HSR + AAV‑CX3CR1‑vehicle group (Fig. 10J), confirming improved spatial memory retention.

Discussion

We explored the potential roles of activated microglia in cognitive impairment and provided persuasive data highlighting the vital role of the inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis-related NLRP3 signaling pathway in HSR-induced cognitive impairment. We provide compelling evidence that treatment with the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 significantly reversed the HSR-induced lower recognition index, higher escape latency, reduced platform crossings, reduction in θ and γ power, and θ-γ phase coupling. Furthermore, our data indicated that treatment with the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 significantly reversed the HSR-induced decrease in the intensity of PSD95 and Synaptophysin, a remarkable increase in number of Iba1+ cells, normalized soma size and total process length cell of Iba1, and increased colocalization of cleaved caspase-1and IL-18 with Iba-1 in the hippocampal CA1 area. Moreover, we observed that cognitive dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and synaptic plasticity damage induced by HSR could be reversed by knocking out NLRP3 in the microglia of the hippocampal CA1 region. Our results identify microglia‑specific NLRP3 as a critical mediator of post‑HSR cognitive impairment and suggest that targeting this pathway may represent a promising therapeutic target for future development.

Previous studies have revealed that HSR may increase the risk of acquiring Parkinson’s disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and chronic traumatic encephalopathy, in addition to causing immediate brain damage [4, 27]. Following injury, the CA1 region of the hippocampus, a critical component linked to memory and learning, undergoes significant pathological changes in various cognitive dysfunction disorders [28, 29]. Previous studies have demonstrated that traumatic stress-induced neuroinflammation causes neuronal death and impaired synaptic plasticity in the CA1 region of the hippocampus, resulting in cognitive impairment [30, 31]. Consistent with other studies, we observed cognitive deficits in the HSR mice in our study.

Research suggests that the phase coupling between θ and γ oscillations improves information transmission and is important for memory and cognitive processes [32–34]. Studies have demonstrated a strong correlation between the phase coupling of the θ and γ oscillations and synaptic plasticity [35]. The stability of synaptic plasticity is crucial for maintaining normal phase coupling between the θ and γ oscillations [36, 37]. In traumatic stress and neurodegenerative diseases, synaptic plasticity is impaired, which results in aberrant phase coupling [20, 38]. Our previous study demonstrated that cognitive impairment alters the phase coupling of γ and θ oscillations in a mouse model of traumatic stress produced by traumatic brain injury [20], which is associated with reduced synaptic plasticity in this pathway. This study revealed that the HSR group exhibited poor synaptic plasticity due to a significant reduction in θ and γ power and θ-γ phase coupling and lower fluorescence intensity of PSD95 and Synaptophysin.

Microglia, the core immune cells of the CNS, play a dual role in cognitive impairment [39, 40]. They exert a protective effect by clearing pathogens and abnormal proteins. Conversely, overactivated or dysfunctional microglia can trigger neuroinflammation and synaptic damage [41]. Studies have indicated that traumatic stress can trigger the overactivation of reactive microglia, resulting in increased neuroinflammation, impaired synaptic plasticity, and, ultimately, neurofunctional disorders [42, 43]. Notably, inhibiting aberrant microglial activity has a strong neuroprotective effect against cognitive impairment [44, 45]. Our findings indicate that HSR can trigger microglial activation in the hippocampal CA1 area, manifesting as increased number of Iba1 + cells, normalized soma size and total process length cell of Iba1.

Several studies have focused on pyroptosis-induced persistent inflammation in animal models of severe stress [46, 47]. Previous studies have emphasized the critical role of pyroptosis in aggravating the inflammatory burden and causing tissue damage [48]. Therefore, pyroptosis inhibition has emerged as a viable therapeutic target for reducing inflammatory reactions. As a component of several pyroptosis-related inflammasome complexes that promote the maturation and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including cleaved caspase-1 and IL-18, NLRP3 is an important regulator of various inflammatory responses in CNS trauma [49, 50]. These inflammatory reactions frequently result in impaired synaptic plasticity, causing brain injury and disruption of CNS homeostasis [51]. Our earlier research indicated that NLRP3-induced pyroptosis contributes to the development of anxiety-like behaviors under HSR conditions [3, 5]. Following HSR, we identified that cleaved caspase-1 and IL-18 colocalized with activated microglia in the hippocampal CA1 region, implying that activated microglia produce their effects via pyroptosis-related pathways. These findings suggest that pathogenic microglia promote persistent inflammation associated with pyroptosis after HSR, eventually resulting in cognitive impairment.

Notably, we did not observe significant neuronal loss in the hippocampal CA1 region at 36 days post‑HSR. This finding suggests that the cognitive dysfunction at this time point is predominantly driven by synaptic pathology and neuroinflammation rather than by overt neurodegeneration. This is consistent with the concept of synaptopathy, wherein cognitive decline results from synaptic dysfunction that can occur before or even in the absence of — overt neuronal death [52]. In the present study, although the number of NeuN⁺ neurons did not differ significantly between the Sham and HSR groups, HSR mice exhibited markedly reduced expression of the synaptic proteins PSD95 and Synaptophysin in the hippocampal CA1 region, along with significantly impaired θ and γ oscillation power and θ-γ phase coupling, indicating compromised structural and functional synaptic integrity. Concomitantly, microglia in the CA1 region of HSR mice displayed a distinctly activated state, evidenced by an increased number of Iba1⁺ cells, enlarged soma size, and shortened processes, as well as significantly elevated expression of the NLRP3 downstream effectors cleaved caspase‑1 and IL‑18 in microglia, reflecting an active neuroinflammatory response. These results suggest that, even in the absence of overt neuronal death, microglia‑mediated neuroinflammation and the ensuing synaptic pathology are sufficient to cause cognitive impairment. Recent studies in models of traumatic brain injury have similarly reported that cognitive deficits can arise without significant neuronal loss and are closely associated with microglial activation, synaptic pruning, and impaired synaptic plasticity [53, 54]. Therefore, our findings further support the view that, at a stage when hippocampal neuronal numbers are still largely preserved, synaptic pathology and neuroinflammation may already serve as key drivers of cognitive dysfunction, and early intervention targeting these processes may effectively delay or prevent the onset of cognitive decline.

Furthermore, MCC950, a specific inhibitor of the NLRP3 inflammasome, enhances neurological outcomes and reduces inflammatory responses. Our study suggests that MCC950 therapy significantly reversed the HSR-induced lower recognition index, higher escape latency, reduced platform crossings, reduction in θ and γ power, and θ-γ phase coupling. Furthermore, our data indicated that MCC950 treatment significantly reversed the HSR-induced decrease in the intensity of PSD95 and Synaptophysin, a significant increase in number of Iba1+ cells, normalized soma size and total process length cell of Iba1, and colocalization of cleaved caspase-1and IL-18 with Iba-1 in the hippocampal CA1 area.

In this study, MCC950 was administered via intracerebroventricular (ICV) injection to achieve robust central nervous system target engagement while avoiding confounding effects from concurrent peripheral NLRP3 inhibition. HSR triggers both systemic and central inflammatory cascades; systemic MCC950 administration would simultaneously suppress NLRP3 activity across multiple organs, precluding mechanistic attribution of cognitive improvements specifically to microglial NLRP3 inhibition within the hippocampus. ICV delivery bypasses the blood‑brain barrier, which MCC950 penetrates poorly and unpredictably—particularly in the setting of acute hemodynamic instability—thereby ensuring reliable drug exposure at the target site during the critical post‑injury period. This route has been widely employed in preclinical studies of CNS NLRP3 biology [20, 55]. We acknowledge that ICV injection is not a clinically translatable route; however, our findings establish a strong mechanistic rationale for developing brain‑penetrant, systemically bioavailable NLRP3 inhibitors suitable for clinical use.

An important feature of this study is its focus on chronic outcomes at 30–36 days post‑HSR. This time window was selected for several reasons. First, cognitive impairment following HSR in patients is not an acute phenomenon but rather emerges and persists over weeks to months as a component of post-intensive care syndrome [10]; our chronic time point models this clinically relevant trajectory. Second, although NLRP3 activation is canonically associated with acute innate immune responses, recent evidence demonstrates that microglial NLRP3 can remain chronically activated after diverse CNS insults, sustaining a low-grade neuroinflammatory state that drives progressive synaptic loss and cognitive decline [56]. Third, the electrophysiological and synaptic plasticity endpoints evaluated in this study (θ–γ phase coupling, synaptic protein expression) reflect structural and functional integrity that requires extended timelines to recover [57]; demonstrating preservation at a chronic time point provides stronger evidence for durable neuroprotection than acute assessments alone. Finally, by showing that a single intervention at the time of injury produces sustained benefits detectable one month later, our data suggest that microglial NLRP3 inhibition disrupts the transition from acute injury to chronic neuroinflammatory pathology.

Prior work has demonstrated that pharmacological NLRP3 inhibition with MCC950 attenuates acute brain injury in intracerebral hemorrhage and other focal CNS injury models [19]. The present study extends these findings in three important respects. First, we demonstrate efficacy of NLRP3 inhibition in a model of global cerebral ischemia-reperfusion (HSR) that is mechanistically distinct from focal hemorrhagic or ischemic stroke, broadening the therapeutic relevance of NLRP3 targeting. Second, using a microglia-specific genetic knockout strategy with rigorous cell-type specificity controls, we establish that microglial NLRP3 is the critical cellular source driving post‑HSR cognitive dysfunction, synaptic damage, and impaired neural oscillations. This cell-type resolution cannot be achieved with pharmacological approaches alone. Third, we show that inhibition of microglial NLRP3 produces durable benefits at chronic time points (30–36 days post‑injury), consistent with the clinical trajectory of post‑resuscitation cognitive impairment. Collectively, these findings provide a novel mechanistic framework and cell-type-specific target for therapeutic intervention in HSR‑associated neurological sequelae.

Moreover, we identified that cognitive dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and synaptic plasticity damage induced by HSR could be reversed by knocking out NLRP3 in microglia in the hippocampal CA1 region. Our findings suggest that inhibiting NLRP3 specific to microglia has significant potential for treating HSR-induced cognitive deficits in clinical settings.

Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in microglia triggers a cascade of molecular and cellular events, including pyroptotic cell death and the maturation and release of pro‑inflammatory mediators such as IL‑1β and IL‑18 [58]. Once released into the extracellular space, these cytokines further amplify neuroinflammation, creating a persistent inflammatory microenvironment that impairs synaptic plasticity [59]. In our study, this process was accompanied by a significant reduction in PSD95 and synaptophysin fluorescence intensity. The structural integrity of synapses is essential for maintaining normal neural circuit function and oscillatory activity, particularly θ and γ power rhythms, which are crucial for memory encoding, information processing, and cognitive performance [60]. The electrophysiological deficits we observed were closely associated with microglial activation and NLRP3‑mediated neuroinflammation; notably, these abnormalities were markedly attenuated either by pharmacological inhibition of NLRP3 with MCC950 or by microglia‑specific NLRP3 gene knockout via viral delivery. These findings suggest that activation of microglial NLRP3 may disrupt synaptic plasticity and neuronal oscillations through inflammatory mechanisms, ultimately leading to cognitive impairment. Therefore, our results support the notion that targeting microglial NLRP3 could restore neuroinflammatory homeostasis and synaptic plasticity, thereby re‑establishing oscillatory dynamics and improving cognitive outcomes following HSR.

While our work focuses on microglial NLRP3 in the hippocampal CA1 region, we recognize that the inflammatory cascade in HSR involves complex bidirectional communication between central and peripheral immune compartments. Microglia engage in reciprocal signaling with astrocytes, neurons, endothelial cells, and infiltrating peripheral leukocytes, shaping the neuroinflammatory milieu [61]. Activated microglia-derived cytokines (e.g., IL‑1β, IL‑18) can prime astrocytes and peripheral monocytes, propagating a feed-forward loop of inflammation that exacerbates synaptic deficits and cognitive dysfunction [62]. Similar cross-cell inflammasome activation has been documented in traumatic brain injury and neurodegenerative conditions, supporting the generalizability of the observed mechanism. Although our viral strategy achieved specific deletion of microglial NLRP3, we acknowledge possible contributions from astrocytic NLRP3, peripheral myeloid cells, and other CNS cell types. Future studies incorporating temporal mapping of multi-cell immune activation will clarify these interactions and help delineate the sequence and hierarchy of immune events driving post-HSR pathology.

From a translational standpoint, cognitive impairment following HSR remains a critical, unmet clinical need. Our findings demonstrate that both pharmacologic inhibition (MCC950) and microglia-specific genetic ablation of NLRP3 improve performance in spatial memory tasks, restore oscillatory coupling, and preserve synaptic integrity. Several next-generation NLRP3 inhibitors with improved safety profiles are in advanced preclinical or early clinical stages [63]. Repurposing these agents for HSR-associated cognitive impairment, potentially guided by biomarkers such as CSF or serum IL-18 or caspase‑1 activity, could enable early intervention in at-risk patients. By linking electrophysiological recovery to inflammasome modulation, our study provides a robust preclinical foundation for developing therapeutic strategies targeting NLRP3 to attenuate the long-term neurological impact of critical illness and resuscitation.

In summary, our study confirms that blocking NLRP3 specific to microglia may reduce cognitive deficits after HSR. This action may be linked to the inhibition of pyroptosis in microglia through the NLRP3 signaling pathway. This study highlights the important role of NLRP3 in microglia in relation to HSR and provides opportunities for further investigation to understand the intricate neuroinflammatory processes fully. It may be helpful to investigate inflammatory indicators more thoroughly and evaluate drugs that target the NLRP3 pathway. These studies might improve our knowledge of neuroinflammation following HSR and help to develop new therapeutic strategies for HSR-related cognitive deficits. Consequently, this study provides a preclinical rationale and identifies a specific cellular target for the future development of therapies aimed at addressing the neurological consequences of HSR.

Limitations

While this study provides compelling evidence for the role of microglial NLRP3 in HSR-induced cognitive impairment, certain limitations should be noted. The use of a male-only cohort, while controlling for sex hormone variability, warrants future validation in female subjects to assess potential sexual dimorphism in the observed effects. Additionally, although our viral approach achieved targeted knockout in hippocampal microglia, potential minor off-target effects cannot be entirely excluded. Future studies employing inducible, microglia-specific knockout models could further confirm these findings. Additionally, MCC950 was administered via ICV injection, which, while providing robust central target engagement and clean mechanistic interpretation, is not a clinically translatable delivery route. Future studies employing brain-penetrant, systemically administered NLRP3 inhibitors are needed to advance these findings toward clinical application. The single-dose administration paradigm used here also differs from the repeated or sustained dosing that might be required in a clinical setting; optimizing the timing, duration, and route of NLRP3 inhibition represents an important direction for future research. Finally, while our experimental design focused on long-term outcomes, a more detailed temporal analysis of NLRP3 activation dynamics would provide deeper mechanistic insights.
