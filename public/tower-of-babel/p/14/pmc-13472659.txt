Introduction

Traditional medical education has long relied on a teacher-centered, lecture-based model that is increasingly viewed as mismatched for the modern surgical trainee. Adult learning theory suggests that learners are self-directed, motivated by immediate clinical relevance and learn best via active problem-solving rather than passive listening1. Historical estimates indicate that learners in traditional lecture-based formats may retain as little as 5% of covered material2,3. Research shows that 50% to 80% of information is forgotten if not reinforced within a 24-hour window4,5. This “forgetting curve” has prompted a broad call to action within the medical community to transition from a “sage on the stage” to a “guide on the side,” emphasizing active learning over passive observation3,6.

The “flipped classroom” (FC) model emerged as a robust solution by reversing the traditional educational structure7. Instructional content is delivered for self-study before class, while in-person time is reserved for interactive, small-group case-based discussions. Meta-analyses across STEM have shown active learning strategies significantly increase student performance and reduce failure rates3. Within medical education, this has led to a call for “lecture halls without lectures,” to free up classroom time for interactive clinical reasoning8.

Historical efforts to improve objective metrics demonstrated that structured reading and preparatory examinations could yield significant score improvements9. While FC models have shown promise in various residency settings10,11, data regarding their specific impact on the Orthopaedic In-Training Examination (OITE) remain limited. One transition to a flipped classroom curriculum resulted in significant increase of overall OITE percentile rank from 29th to 75th percentile against national peers12. Despite promising program-wide results, there is lack of data investigating how this model impacts subspecialty-specific performance.

This study sought to examine association between a flipped classroom foot and ankle curriculum and resident performance on the foot and ankle subsection of the Orthopaedic In-Training Examination. The primary aim was to retrospectively compare foot and ankle OITE scores between residents who had a traditional lecture-based curriculum and a flipped classroom curriculum. Secondarily, we sought to normalize these results against overall OITE performance to isolate the curriculum's impact from general cohort aptitude.

Methods

A retrospective cohort analysis was conducted reviewing OITE scores for residents within a single orthopaedic surgery residency program approved by the IRB. All examination scores for postgraduate year-2 (PGY2)-PGY5 residents in the program from 2019 to 2024 were deidentified, and the scores for all categories of the OITE were recorded. The observational study compared the preintervention group of 49 residents trained under a traditional lecture-based curriculum against the postintervention group of 61 residents trained under a flipped classroom curriculum. Beginning July 2020, the foot and ankle curriculum was transitioned to a flipped classroom model, while other subspecialty curricula continued to be taught using traditional lectures. Treatment was defined as any resident who received foot and ankle flipped classroom training as a part of their PGY2 curriculum. Residents who were PGY3-PGY5 at the time of implementation were considered part of the traditional lecture cohort. PGY2 cohorts were excluded from the primary subgroup analysis as testing occurred midcurriculum, creating a confounded sample.

Curriculum Design and Implementation

The foot and ankle flipped classroom curriculum was implemented for PGY2 residents in July 2020. The curricular goal was to move foundational knowledge acquisition outside the classroom and reserve in-person time for case-based clinical reasoning, imaging interpretation, treatment decision making, and board-style knowledge application. Didactic time, rotations schedule, and OITE preparation remained unchanged. Three faculty members participated unchanged throughout the study period. Before implementation, faculty reviewed the learning objectives, case format, and expectations for resident prework to standardize delivery across sessions.

Each session included assigned presession material distributed in advance through an online recorded lecture, followed by an interactive faculty-moderated case discussion during in-person conference time. The finalized foot and ankle flipped classroom curriculum was mapped to the ABOS Part I Examination Blueprint and corresponding AAOS OITE foot and ankle content domains, with 11 modules organized around major ankle/leg and foot topic areas (Appendix 1)13. Sessions used a consistent structure: brief review of learning objectives, resident-led discussion of a representative clinical case, interpretation of relevant radiographs or advanced imaging, discussion of nonoperative and operative management, complications, and consolidation using OITE-style questions.

Faculty buy-in was obtained by presenting baseline data, emphasizing that the intervention would preserve existing didactic time while shifting faculty effort from slide-based content delivery to case-based expert facilitation. The curriculum was designed to be sustainable by reusing a core set of cases and updating content annually. Implementation coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, reducing elective volume-facilitating curriculum development. The clinical slowdown occurred before the second OITE examination date for the traditional cohort; however, the intervention itself remained unchanged after normal operations resumed.

Faculty Survey

To gauge the perceptions of the foot and ankle faculty, a formal survey was distributed through the Research Data Capture (RedCAP) platform14. Six questions used a 5-point Likert scale yielding a composite score from 6 to 30.

Statistical Analysis

Since OITE performance may be influenced by resident ability, PGY level, examination difficulty, and secular trends in OITE performance, 2 complementary resident-level adjustment strategies were used. First, a mixed-effects model with a resident-level random intercept accounted for repeated OITE administrations within individual residents and differences in baseline performance, allowing for cohort comparison. Second, for each OITE administration, foot and ankle performance was normalized to the same resident’s contemporaneous performance on all non–foot-and-ankle OITE sections, producing a ratio where values above 1.0 indicated superior foot and ankle performance. This within-examination normalization allowed each resident’s performance in other domains to serve as an internal control for global test–taking ability and year-specific examination factors. Because residents did not cross over between curricula, this approach reduces but does not eliminate confounding inherent to a before-after observational design.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1.

Results

The residents who experienced the flipped classroom curriculum, as a part of foot and ankle training, demonstrated an average increase of 7% (95% confidence interval: 4%, 10%; p < 0.001) in the foot and ankle questions correctly answered on the OITE compared with the residents who experienced traditional lecture-based foot and ankle training. In PGY 3, the mean percentage correct was slightly higher but not statistically significant, in the flipped classroom compared with the traditional group (66.07% vs. 62.26%, p = 0.16). In PGY4, the mean percentage correct was higher in the flipped classroom compared with the traditional group (74.83% vs. 69.16%, p = 0.013). For PGY5, the mean percentage correct was higher in the flipped classroom compared with the traditional group (75.53% vs. 69.03%, p = 0.006) (Fig. 1 and Table I).

Discussion

Traditional lecture-based models in medical education are widely used yet have demonstrated notable limitations regarding long-term knowledge retention and examination scores2,3. This single-institution retrospective study found implementation of a foot and ankle flipped classroom curriculum was associated with higher OITE foot and ankle scores after adjustment for PGY and each resident’s contemporaneous non–foot-and-ankle OITE performance. The association was strongest in PGY4 and PGY5 residents, suggesting that repeated exposure to presession content and faculty-led case discussion may have a cumulative effect. These findings support active-learning methods as a targeted intervention for subspecialty areas with historically lower in-training examination performance, while acknowledging the retrospective before-after design does not establish causality.

Assessing score improvements, residents trained using the flipped classroom method demonstrated an average increase of 7% in foot and ankle questions correctly answered compared with those in the traditional lecture-based cohort. This improvement aligns with adult learning theory, in which self-directed learning combined with practical application leads to better learning outcomes15,16.

Approximately 75% of PGY5 residents in the traditional lecture-based group scored lower on the foot and ankle section than the rest of the examination. After implementation of the flipped classroom, PGY4 and PGY5 residents had approximately 75% of their cohort score higher on the foot and ankle questions than the rest of the examination. PGY3 residents tended to score the same on the foot and ankle section as the rest of the test if they had undergone the flipped classroom training; however, if they underwent traditional lecture-based training, they scored worse on the foot and ankle section than the rest of the examination. By using a foot and ankle–trained orthopaedic surgeon to guide case-based discussions, the flipped classroom program provided contextualized cognitive modeling that standard lectures lack, potentially allowing residents to outperform their baseline across other concurrently tested orthopaedic domains.

This within-examination normalization is important because secular trends in OITE performance and annual variation in examination content could otherwise confound interpretation of score changes. The improvement in foot and ankle performance occurred despite lower contemporaneous performance on the remainder of the OITE in the flipped classroom cohort. This finding suggests that the observed improvement was relatively specific to the foot and ankle domain rather than simply reflecting a global improvement in resident test performance.

Faculty survey results supported the feasibility and acceptability of the flipped classroom transition: All 3 participating foot and ankle faculty members returned the maximum composite score of 30, indicating strong satisfaction with the curriculum format.

An important finding in this study was the cumulative educational benefit that occurred throughout postgraduate years. Under traditional lecture, mean foot and ankle scores progressed from 62% in PGY3 to 69% in PGY4 and PGY5, an expected baseline gain. The flipped classroom model amplifies this growth trajectory, with mean foot and ankle scores accelerating from 66% in PGY3 to 75% in PGY4 and 76% in PGY5. Even when comparing foot and ankle performance with the performance on the rest of the test, we see a greater maintained trend in the flipped classroom group (1.01 in PGY3s to 1.08 in PGY4s and 1.07 in PGY5s) than the traditional lecture-based group (0.92 in PGY3s to 0.97 in PGY4s to 0.94 in PGY5s). This compounding success supports recent literature investigating long-term knowledge retention in graduate medical education. The asynchronous active learning methods lead to improved long-term retention and confidence over time, outperforming the slower gains from traditional didactic formats17,18.

Several limitations should temper interpretation of these findings. First, this was a retrospective, nonrandomized before-after study, and curriculum exposure was inherently confounded with calendar time. Differences among resident cohorts, year-to-year variation in OITE content and difficulty, COVID-era educational changes, and changes in clinical exposure may have contributed to the observed association.

Second, while the flipped classroom curriculum was associated with improved foot and ankle OITE performance, the OITE is a written assessment of cognitive knowledge and does not directly measure operative skill, clinical judgment, surgical decision making, or patient outcomes.

Third, adherence to presession preparation, resident engagement during didactic sessions, and use of supplemental educational resources were not measured and may have influenced outcomes. Fourth, implementation of a flipped classroom curriculum may represent an administrative and financial burden, requiring fee-based platforms and substantial faculty development time11,12. Programs with limited faculty availability, educational support infrastructure, or financial resources may face challenges reproducing this model.

Finally, this study was conducted within a single orthopaedic surgery residency program, which may limit generalizability. In addition, because the OITE is administered during the academic year, some PGY2 residents had not completed the full foot and ankle curriculum before testing. Consequently, the most interpretable PGY-stratified comparisons begin with PGY3 residents who had received complete exposure to the intervention.

Conclusion

Implementation of a foot and ankle flipped classroom curriculum was associated with improved foot and ankle OITE performance compared with prior lecture-based instruction, particularly among senior residents. The curriculum may be a feasible targeted strategy to strengthen subspecialty knowledge, but prospective multi-institutional studies with measures of engagement, knowledge retention, and clinical performance are needed before further broad conclusions can be drawn.
