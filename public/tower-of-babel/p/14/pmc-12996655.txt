Neuronal alkaline phosphatase promotes the spread of Tau-induced pathology, and its blockade prevents neurodegeneration and memory loss
Authors: Lucía Soria-Tobar, Daniel Ouro-Corredera, Laura Roman-Valero, Félix Hernández, José Luis Millán, Beatriz Álvarez-Castelao, Álvaro Sebastián-Serrano, Paloma Aivar, Miguel Díaz-Hernández
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tauopathies, such as Alzheimer's disease (AD), are neurodegenerative disorders marked by abnormal intraneuronal aggregates of phosphorylated Tau protein. Unfortunately, no effective treatment is currently available. Since extracellular Tau (eTau) is essential for the spread of cerebral tauopathy, immunotherapy approaches using specific antibodies against Tau have been investigated. However, these strategies have shown limited applicability and benefit. Because previous in vitro studies reported that dephosphorylation of eTau by tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (TNAP) enhances its neurotoxicity, here we evaluate how neuronal TNAP contributes to Tau-induced neurotoxicity in vivo. To address this, we generated new transgenic mouse lines using Cre-lox technology to i) specifically delete TNAP in excitatory neurons of P301S mice, a well-characterized tauopathy model, or ii) induce neuronal TNAP overexpression in WT mice. Moreover, we compare the in vivo spreading capacity of phospho-eTau and dephospho-eTau-induced neurotoxicity. Our findings show that neuronal TNAP deletion in P301S mice reduces i) neuronal and synaptic loss, ii) the number of neurons with neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), iii) reactive astrogliosis and microgliosis, and iv) brain calcifications; collectively, these changes lead to v) improved memory function in these mice. Conversely, overexpression of neuronal TNAP in WT mice alone is sufficient to cause i) loss of thalamic neurons and synaptic contacts, ii) formation of intracellular NFTs, iii) reactive gliosis, iv) brain calcifications, and v) memory impairment. These results demonstrate that neuronal TNAP promotes Tau-induced neurotoxicity spreading by facilitating eTau dephosphorylation, which confirms this ectoenzyme as a promising therapeutic target for tauopathies.

Introduction

Tauopathies are a family of neurodegenerative disorders characterized by the presence of aberrant intraneuronal aggregates of phosphorylated microtubule-associated protein Tau, namely neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs). These diseases include Alzheimer's disease (AD) and Pick's disease (PiD), among others [1]. In addition to this histopathological hallmark, the loss of neuronal and synaptic contacts, as well as general neuroinflammation, are also standard features observed in the brains of these patients [1,2]. Emerging evidence suggests that Tau-induced pathology is mediated through the spreading of the Tau protein from cell to cell. Supporting this hypothesis, it has been reported that the Tau protein can be released into the extracellular space through both physiological and pathological mechanisms. In the brain parenchyma, Tau protein can be toxic to neighboring cells, thereby promoting the spread of Tau-induced toxicity between interconnected brain areas, following a well-defined anatomical pattern [3,4]. While the specific underlying mechanisms mediating Tau-induced toxicity propagation remain poorly understood [5,6], immunotherapeutic approaches aimed at reducing extracellular Tau protein (eTau) levels have been proposed as a novel strategy to halt the progression of tauopathies, including AD [6]. However, active immunotherapy approaches have been associated with adverse effects [7,8], and although passive immunotherapy reduces Tau-induced pathology, it has failed to extend lifespan in tauopathy mouse models [9,10]. These limitations prompt us to invest additional efforts in researching alternative strategies that are both effective and safe in combating these terrible diseases.

Recent studies have reported that Tauopathy patients, including those with AD, exhibit increased expression of the alkaline phosphatase isoform tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (TNAP) in both brain and plasma [11,12]. This enzyme is encoded by the ALPL gene and is expressed by neurons (especially at nerve terminals), microglia, oligodendrocytes, astrocytes, and endothelial cells in the brain [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. Because it was suggested that a close relationship exists between the cerebral increase and plasma elevation of this enzyme [12], TNAP has been proposed as a novel plasma biomarker for AD [12,17,18]. Furthermore, other studies have linked the elevated cerebral TNAP levels to eTau-induced neurotoxicity [11,[19], [20], [21]]. Specifically, it has been reported that: i) TNAP dephosphorylates phosphorylated eTau at multiple sites [19], promoting activation of muscarinic receptors in a non-desensitizing way which may alter intracellular calcium homeostasis leading to cell death [20,21]; ii) local hippocampal TNAP overexpression promotes intracellular Tau phosphorylation and aggregation in neighboring neurons [11]; iii) TNAP haploinsufficiency or systemic pharmacological inhibition reduces neuronal hyperactivity, hippocampal neuronal loss, and brain atrophy in P301S mice, a mouse model mimicking Tau pathology observed in AD, which also prevented the decrease of anxiety-related behavioral symptoms or disinhibition profile, the motor deficiency, and increased both memory capacity and life expectancy of these mice [11]; iv) an inverse correlation between the elevated TNAP plasma levels and cognitive functions has been reported in AD patients [18,22]. Nevertheless, these beneficial effects may be countered by the detrimental impact that a sustained systemic inhibition of TNAP may cause outside the CNS, such as i) disruption of the blood-brain barrier (BBB) [23]; ii) promotion of systemic age-associated inflammation and/or local inflammation in different tissues [24], and iii) slow down bone formation and exacerbate risk of age-related osteoporosis [24]. Given the potential therapeutic benefits of cerebral TNAP blockade in patients with tauopathy and the need to avoid peripheral side effects, this study aims to investigate the therapeutic potential of selective neuronal TNAP inhibition in tauopathies. To address this challenge, we generated P301S mice with neuron-specific TNAP depletion to elucidate how TNAP ablation affects symptoms and signs associated with Tau-induced pathology. Furthermore, given that TNAP dephosphorylates eTau in the brain, we will also investigate whether this process may influence the spread of Tau-induced neurotoxicity. Finally, we will develop a transgenic mouse line overexpressing TNAP specifically in neurons to assess whether its overexpression is a cause or a consequence of the neuronal alterations associated with Tau-induced neurotoxicity, and to identify which brain regions are most susceptible.

Methods

Human brain tissues

Human samples were provided by the Banco de Tejidos Fundación CIEN (BT-CIEN, Madrid, Spain). Written informed consent for brain removal after death for diagnostic and research purposes was obtained from brain donors and/or next of kin. Procedures, information, and consent forms were approved by the Bioethics Subcommittee of Fundación Cien Madrid, Spain (S19001). Frozen samples were obtained from hippocampus and thalamus of four patients clinically diagnosed with AD (three men aged 73, 87, and 86 years old and one woman aged 86 years), four patients with clinical diagnosis of Pick disease (three men aged 70, 79, and 64 years old and one woman aged 73 years), and four non-demented controls (three men aged 58, 61, and 83 years old and one woman aged 70 years). The postmortem delay in tissue processing ranged from 4 to 5 h in all groups.

Animals

All animal procedures were carried out at the Universidad Complutense de Madrid (UCM) in compliance with National and European regulations (RD 1201/2005; 86/609/EEC) and in accordance with the guidelines of the International Council for Laboratory Animal Science. The protocol for animal experiments was approved by the Committee of Animal Experiments at the UCM and the Environmental Counseling of the Comunidad de Madrid, Spain (PROEX 185/17 and PROEX 137.8/22).

Floxed Alpl (Alplf/f) mice, where the murine alkaline phosphatase gene (Alpl) is flanked by loxP sites, enabling its conditional deletion using the Cre/loxP system [25]; and the HprtALPL mice, which harbor a floxed “stop cassette” and the human ALPL cDNA inserted into the Hprt locus on the X chromosome [26] (both strains on a C57BL/6J genetic background), were provided by Prof Jose Luis Millan from Sanford Burnham Prebys, La Jolla, California, USA. B6.Cg-Tg(Camk2a-cre)T29-1Stl/J strain mice (CKIICre mice) that have the mouse calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II alpha (Camk2a) promoter driving Cre recombinase expression in the forebrain [27] were obtained from Jackson Labs (Catalog # 005359). The tauopathy mouse model, P301S mice, was obtained from The Jackson Laboratory: line B6; C3-Tg(Prnp-MAPT∗P301S)PS19Vle/J (Catalog #008169). The original B6C3H/F1 genetic background was altered to C57BL/6J by backcrossing it with C57BL/6J animals in our laboratory.

Alplf/f; CKIICre/P301S+/− animals, a new tauopathy mouse model with a conditional deletion of Alpl in neurons of the forebrain, were generated by crossing Alplf/f, CKIICre, and P301S mice. HprtALPL;CKIICre animals, which present a conditional overexpression of human TNAP (encoded by ALPL) in neurons from the forebrain, were generated by crossing HprtALPL and CKIICre mice.

Animals were housed in an environment with controlled light, temperature, and humidity, featuring a 12-h light-dark cycle with light onset at 8:00 a.m. Mice were grouped 4–6 per cage and allowed free access to water and food (ad libitum). All surgeries were performed under isoflurane anesthesia, and all efforts were made to minimize suffering. Investigators were blinded to the group allocation throughout the experiments and data analysis.

We used 89 mice, aged 8–11 months, divided into the following groups: 33 wildtype (WT) mice, 19 Alplf/f;CKIICre mice, 12 P301S+/− mice, 11 Alplf/f;CKIICre/P301S+/− mice, and 14 HprtALPL;CKIICre mice. Additionally, we used 12 WT mice, aged 3–4 months. When the animals were divided into different groups for a particular procedure, we ensured that the gender variable was evenly distributed among all groups.

PCR genotyping

According to the manufacturer's protocol, genomic DNA was extracted from tail biopsies using the Wizard SV Genomic DNA Purification System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA).

Simple PCR reactions were carried out using: for P301S mice, DNA Kapa2G Fast HS Master Mix from Sigma-Aldrich (Madrid, Spain), specific primers (0.5 μM each) and two μL of genomic DNA in a final volume of 12 μL; for Alplf/f; mice Amplitools Master mix (Biotools, Madrid, Spain), specific primers (25 μM) and five μL of genomic DNA in a final volume of 25 μL; for HprtALPL mice, Extraction Kappa kit Amplitools master mix (Biotools, Madrid, Spain), specific primers (25 μM) and two μL of genomic DNA in a final volume of 25 μL; for CKIICre mice Kapa2G Fast HS Master Mix from Sigma-Aldrich (Madrid, Spain), specific primers (20 μM) and two μL of genomic DNA in a final volume of 12 μL.

Animals were genotyped using specific primers for P301S Fw 5′-GGCATCTCAGCAATG TCTCC-3′ and Rv 5′-GGTATTAGCCTATGGGGGACAC-3′; for Alplf/f; Fw 5′-GTTGCGATGTGTGAAGATGTCCTCG-3′ and Rv 5′-CTTGGGCTTGCTGTCGCCAGTAAC-3´; for HprtALPL Fw 5′-TGTCCTTAGAAAACACATATCCAGGG-3′ and Rv 5′-CTGGCTTAAAGACAACATCTGGGAGA-3′ and Fw 5′-AATGCCCAGGTCCCTGACAGC-3′ and Rv 5′-GGTTCCAGATGAAGTGGGAGT-3´; for CKIICre 5′-GCGGTCTGGCAGTAAAAACTATC-3′ and Rv 5′-GTGAAACAGCATTGCTGTCACTT-3′ and internal control Fw 5′-CTAGGCCACAGAATTGAAAGATCT-3′ and Rv 5′-GTAGGTGGAAATTCTAGCATCATCC-3´. PCR was carried out over 28 cycles 94 °C for 15 s, 65 °C for 15 s, 72 °C for 10 s for P301S primers; over 40 cycles 94 °C for 1 min, 64 °C for 2 min, 72 °C for 2 min for Alplf/f primers; over 40 cycles of 94 °C for 1 min, 60 °C for 2 min, and 72 °C for 3 min for HprtALPL primers; and 35 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 51,7 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for °1 min for CKIICre primers.

PCR amplification products were run on a 1.5 % (w/v) agarose gel and stained with SYBR Safe DNA Gel Stain (Life Technologies, CA, USA). PCR bands were visualized using a Gel Logic 200 Imaging System (Kodak, Rochester, NY, USA).

Cell cultures and Tau purification: phosphorylation and dephosphorylation

SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (Rockville, MD) and were grown in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (v/v; FBS), 2 mm glutamine, and 50 mg/mL gentamicin (all from Invitrogen) in a humidified atmosphere with 5 % CO2.

Phosphorylated Tau42, the longest tau isoform present in the central nervous system with four microtubule binding domains and two N-terminal inserts, was purified from SH-SY5Y cells treated with 10 μm okadaic acid for 1 h at 37 °C. Phosphorylated Tau was dephosphorylated by TNAP treatment as previously reported [19]. Briefly, phosphorylated Tau was incubated with one enzyme unit of TNAP for 1 h at 37 °C in PBS buffer, pH 9.8. After dephosphorylation, the mixture was heated to 100 °C for 5 min to inactivate the enzyme. All proteins were characterized by gel electrophoresis followed by staining with Coomassie Blue.

Intrahippocampal administration

Three-to four-month-old WT mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (1-chloro-2,2,2-trifluoroethyl-difluoromethylether; Isovet; Braun, Rubi, Barcelona, Spain), diluted in 50 % O2. Mice were kept under anesthesia during the surgery, with their body temperature monitored and maintained at 37 °C using a thermal blanket (ThermoStar, RWD). The scalp was incised along the midline, and one hole was made at the appropriate stereotaxic coordinates from bregma (mediolateral, −1,4 mm; anteroposterior, −2 mm; dorsoventral, −2.2 mm). Subsequently, we infused intrahippocampally two μL of phosphorylated Tau, two μL of dephosphorylated Tau, or two μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with inactivated TNAP at a rate of ≈0.2 μL/min. After the injection, the needle was kept for an additional 5 min and then slowly withdrawn in three steps of 2 min. After surgery, their scalps were sutured, and mice were then returned to their original home cages.

Behavioral assays

All mice were handled for at least 3–4 consecutive days before testing. They were tested randomly, and the experimenter conducting the tests was blinded to genotype and group. All behavioral devices were in a soundproof room, and the experimental area was uniformly illuminated at approximately 70 lx. All used devices were carefully cleaned with alcohol (70° proof) after each trial.

Open field test

is an experimental test used to assay general locomotor activity levels, anxiety, and willingness to explore within a novel environment. The apparatus consists of a transparent polycarbonate tank (42 cm × 42 cm). There is no need to habituate the mouse before the test. A single adult mouse was gently placed into the setup, and its position was video tracked for 10 min. The analysis measured the time the mouse spent in the center (17 × 17 cm) and the periphery (the surrounding area of the tank). The tendency to avoid the center of a novel environment and to remain close to the tank wall is an index of anxiety. Data acquisition and analysis were performed automatically using Any-Maze software (Stoelting Co., Wood Dale, IL, USA).

Novel object recognition

This test exploits mice's inherent preference for novelty to reveal their memory of previously encountered objects. In this regard, it evaluates spatial learning, which relies heavily on hippocampal activity. Mice were habituated to the open field test box (10 min). During the memory acquisition trial, each mouse was allowed to explore two identical objects for 10 min. For the memory retention phase, animals were exposed for 10 min to two objects localized at the same previous location, but one of them was new, and the other was the same that had been previously explored. Object exploration time was recorded when the mouse touched the new object directly with its nose, mouth, or forepaws. The discrimination index was calculated as the time spent near the new object divided by the time spent around the familiar object. Results are expressed as a recognition index.

Elevated plus maze test

This test measures anxiety based on the animal's aversion to open spaces. The test uses an elevated, plus-shaped (+) apparatus with two open and two enclosed arms. There is no need to habituate the mouse before the test. Each adult mouse was gently placed in the center of the setup (at the intersection of the open and enclosed arms), and its position was video-tracked for 5 min. The analysis was conducted using Any-Maze software, which measured the amount of time spent by the mouse in the open versus closed arms of the maze.

T-maze test

The device was a symmetrical, dark-grey PVC T-shaped maze. The arms were 30 cm long, 9 cm wide, and 20 cm high, and were connected at the center at a 90° angle to a 9 cm by 9 cm square. Both arms of the T could be blocked with a barrier. In the first part of the test, one of the two arms was blocked (alternated). The mouse was placed at the base of the T-maze leg and allowed to freely explore the leg and the open arm for 5 min. Afterward, it was returned to its home cage. One hour later, the barrier was removed from the T-maze, and the mouse was allowed to freely explore the maze again for 5 min, this time with the blocked arm also open (novel arm). Videos were analyzed using Any-Maze software. An automated setup was used to detect the mouse within the maze, and the software assigned areas to the left, novel, and familiar arms. When the center point of the mouse entered one of these areas, it was counted as an arm entry. The procedure is based on rodents' natural tendency to prefer exploring a novel arm over a familiar one. During the task, the animal must remember which arm was visited in the previous trial, making the spontaneous alternation T-maze an optimal test for spatial working memory.

Barnes maze test

We adapted the shortened Barnes maze protocol described by Attar et al. [28]. Briefly, the Barnes maze was a circular, white PVC table with a diameter of 1 m, elevated 1 m above the ground. In the maze's periphery, there were twenty holes of 5 cm in diameter spaced at regular intervals. A rectangular black plastic box 16.2 cm long, 6.6 cm wide, and 8.5 cm high was attached under one of the holes, serving as an escape chamber. The escape chamber included a small staircase for the mice to descend. The Barnes maze test consisted of four consecutive days, covering three phases: day 0 (habituation), days 1–3 (training), and day 4 (probe). For later analysis using Any-Maze software, all sessions were video-tracked. During the habituation phase, mice were placed in the center of the maze and allowed to explore it freely for 2 min. If the mouse did not enter the escape chamber within 2 min, it was gently guided toward the escape hole through a glass beaker. When the mouse entered the escape chamber, the escape hole was covered, and it remained in the chamber for 2 min before being returned to its cage.

In the training phase, mice were placed inside an opaque box at the center of the Barnes maze for 15 s. This allowed the mice to face a random direction when the cylinder was lifted, marking the start of the trial. At the end of the holding period, the box was removed, and the mice were allowed to explore the maze for 2 min. If a mouse found the target hole and entered the escape cage during that time, the endpoint of the trial, it was allowed to stay in the escape cage for 1 min before being returned to the holding cage. If it did not find the target hole, the mouse was guided to the escape hole and allowed to enter the cage independently. We performed five trials in total. During the training phase, measures of primary latency (the time to identify the target hole the first time) were recorded.

On the probe day, 24 h after the last training day, the escape cage was removed. Mice were placed inside the opaque box at the center of the maze for 15 s before the box was removed. Each mouse was given 2 min to explore the labyrinth, then returned to its holding cage. During the probe phase, we analyzed the time spent and the distance traveled to find the hole that previously contained the escape box.

Rotarod test

is a behavioral task that assesses balance and coordination capacity. In this study, we used an accelerating rotarod apparatus (Ugo Basile, Varese, Italy). After a three-day training period (first day: four rpm for 1 min, four repetitions; second and third days: 4–8 rpm for 2 min, four repetitions), the rotarod was accelerated from four to forty rpm over 5 min, also with four repetitions. The mean latency to fall was calculated.

Tail suspension test

This test is used to assess “depression-like” or “despair-like” behavior. The apparatus consists of a white-walled box divided into four rectangular compartments, each measuring 55 cm in height, 15 cm in width, and 11.5 cm in depth, and crossed by an aluminum suspension bar on its upper side. It is used to suspend the tail of each mouse. Each mouse was suspended in the middle of those compartments, and the width and depth were sufficiently sized so that the mouse could not touch any of the three walls. The approximate distance between the mouse's nose and the apparatus floor is 20–25 cm. Twelve-centimeter fragments of tape were cut, and a mark was made two cm from one end. This 2-cm portion was used for attaching the tape to the tail, while the remaining 10 cm was used for suspending the mouse. Plastic clear hollow cylinders, 4 cm in length, are placed around the tails of mice to prevent the mice's tail-climbing behavior. Once animals were suspended by placing the free end of the tape on the suspension bar, they were video tracked for 5 min. At the end of the session, we returned the animals to their home cage and carefully removed the tape from each tail by gently pulling it off. We analyzed the percentage of time spent immobile.

Clasping test

This test assesses motor function by gently lifting a rodent by its tail and observing the posture of its hindlimbs. Healthy animals typically extend their hindlimbs, while those with motor deficits may clasp or retract them toward their body, indicating potential motor impairment. Mice were video-tracked for 30 s for their analysis using the following score system: zero points when hindlimbs splayed outward, away from the abdomen; one point, one hindlimbs retracted partially toward the abdomen and toes exhibit standard extension; two points, both hindlimbs show incomplete extension and loss of mobility but toes show standard extension; three points, both hind limbs exhibit stiffness with curled toes and immobility; four points, the forelimbs and hindlimbs are stiff and crossed, with bent fingers and immobility.

Collection of CSF samples

The CSF samples from all the analyzed genotypes were collected as described by de Diego-Garcia et al. [29], with the experimenter being blinded to the genotype of the mice. Briefly, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane diluted in 50 % O2 and placed in a stereotaxic frame with the head forming a nearly 135° angle with the body. Mice were kept under anesthesia during the surgery, with their body temperature monitored and maintained at 37 °C using a thermal blanket (ThermoStar, RWD). The CSF was collected from the cisterna magna with a pulled capillary. Skin and muscle were dissected away to expose the dura mater overlying the cisterna magna before withdrawing CSF with a pulled capillary. Blood vessels were carefully avoided when penetrating the dura mater with the capillary tube to prevent contamination by plasma proteins. CSF was immediately frozen on dry ice.

Tissue processing for immunofluorescence and immunohistochemical studies, and blood collection

Mouse brain

Mice were deeply anesthetized using a mix of ketamine (200 mg/kg) and xylazine (12.5 mg/kg) diluted in PBS, administered as a single intraperitoneal injection. Anesthetized mice were perfused transcardially with cold PBS followed by cold PFA (pH 7.4) (all reagents from Sigma; MS, USA). Using the anesthesia explained above, blood samples were collected via cardiac puncture from the left ventricle using a 23–25-gauge needle (0.3–0.4 mL per mice). Blood was withdrawn slowly to prevent the heart from collapsing. Brains were dissected immediately and, in some cases, placed in 4 % PFA at 4 °C for 48 h for post-fixation. Excess PFA was removed with three washes in PBS. Next, the fixed brains were placed in 30 % sucrose in PBS for 48 h at 4 °C for cryoprotection. Samples were then embedded in OCT compound (Sakura, Finetek, Torrance, CA) and frozen using dry ice. Finally, 25-μm floating sections were cut in parasagittal or coronal planes with a cryostat (CM1950, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) and stored in a solution of 30 % ethylene glycol, 30 % glycerol and 0.1 M PBS at −20 °C until processed.

Human brain

Whole brain from AD subjects was separated into two hemispheres, through a sagittal interhemispheric incision, and fixed in 10 % buffered formalin for at least 3 weeks. Samples (2 mm thick) of the hippocampus and thalamus were then obtained through dissection, embedded in paraffin according to standard protocols (Leica, HistoCore Pearl), and later sectioned (5 μm) using a microtome (Microm HM 355 S). The sections were stored at −80 °C until use.

Serum samples

Eppendorf tubes containing the whole blood samples were kept at 4 °C until the formation of the blood clot, which was removed by centrifugation at 1,000–1,500×g for 10 min at 4 °C. Following centrifugation, serum samples were immediately transferred into sterile polypropylene tubes 1.5 mL clearly labelled with the animal ID and date of collection.

Synaptosomal preparation

A set of WT, Alplf/f;CKIICre, Alplf/f;CKIICre/P301S+/−, and HprtALPL;CKIICre mice, 8–10 months old, were sacrificed by cervical dislocation and decapitated. Their brains were then extracted to obtain the brain cortex to isolate synaptosomes. The synaptosomal isolation was made through a Percoll gradient, as previously described [30].

TNAP activity

Serum samples

20 μL of plasma were brought to 0.2 mL with the following reaction conditions: 0.2 M Diethanolamine and 1 mM MgCl2 (Merck Life Science S.L.U, Madrid, Spain), pH 9.8, incubated with 4 mM p-nitrophenyl phosphate (Merck Life Science S.L.U, Madrid, Spain) at 37 °C with agitation. All samples were assayed in duplicate. To confirm that the detected phosphatase activity was specifically due to TNAP plasma samples were pre-incubated with 150 μM levamisole for 5 min at 37 °C, before adding 4 mM p-nitrophenyl phosphate in the continued presence of levamisole. Samples absorbance was measured at 405 nm, 25 °C, in a spectrophotometer.

Mouse brain samples

A portion of fresh cortical or cerebellar tissue was homogenized with a Teflon glass homogenizer in 10 mM Tris-HCl buffer, pH 8.0, supplemented with 0.25 M sucrose and protease inhibitor mixture (EDTA-free CompleteTM, Roche Diagnostics) or with a protease inhibitor mixture containing 1 mM PMSF, 10 μg/mL aprotinin, 10 μg/mL leupeptin, and 50 μg/mL pepstatin. 30 μL aliquots of cortical or cerebellar homogenates were assayed at 25 °C in the following reaction mix: 0.2 M diethanolamine buffer, pH 9.8, 1 mM MgCl2, and 5 mM p-nitrophenyl phosphate in the presence or absence of 5 mM levamisole (all reagents from Sigma; MS, USA). Reactions were stopped after 20 min with 0.1 M NaOH. Protein concentrations were quantified by Bradford assay. TNAP activity was determined as the absorbance of the liberated p-nitrophenol at 405 nm and normalized to cellular protein content.

Synaptosome samples

30 μL aliquots of cortical synaptosomes were assayed similarly to that previously described for cortical or cerebellar brain samples.

For immunofluorescence studies

Parasagittal or coronal mouse sections were boiled in citrate buffer, washed in PBS, blocked for 1 h at room temperature (RT) with blocking solution (5 % FBS, 1 % bovine serum albumin, 0.2 % Triton-X100 in PBS) When using mouse antibodies, an additional blocking step with goat anti-mouse IgG (Millipore Catalog AP124, 1:50) was performed overnight at 4 °C. Later, slices were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h or overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted as follows; chicken anti-GFAP (Aveslabs, 1:1500 Catalog 4937976), rabbit anti-NeuN (Millipore Catalog ABN78, 1:400), mouse anti-AT8 (phospho Tau, Ser202/Thr205, catalog MN1020 Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:500) and mouse anti-MC1 (kindly provided by Albert Einstein College of medicine on behalf of Prof Peter Davies, 1:100) in blocking solution. Subsequently, brain sections were washed with PBS buffer and incubated with secondary antibodies at the following dilutions: goat anti-rabbit IgG labeled with Alexa 555 (1:400), goat anti-chicken IgG labeled with Alexa 488 (1:400), goat anti-mouse IgG labeled with Alexa 488 (1:400), and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) staining (1:5,000). Finally, brain sections were washed with PBS and mounted in FluorSave (Calbiochem).

For immunohistochemical studies

Parasagittal or coronal mouse sections were pre-treated for 45 min with 1 % H2O2 in PBS to inactivate endogenous peroxidase. Afterward, sections were washed in PBS, blocked for 1 h in block solution (1 % bovine serum albumin , Sigma-Aldrich, 5 % FBS, and 0.2 % Triton X-100, Sigma-Aldrich, in PBS) When using mouse antibodies, an additional blocking step with goat anti-mouse IgG (1:50) was performed ON at 4 °C. Later, slices were incubated ON at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted as follows; rabbit anti- Iba-1, (Wako catalog 019–19741, 1:500), mouse anti-MC1, rabbit anti-NeuN (Millipore Catalog ABN78, 1:400), mouse anti-Tau (not recognize tau human, clone T49 Merck MABN827, 1:1,000), or anti-XPR1 (Invitrogen catalog PA5-34337, 1:200). Subsequently, brain sections were washed with PBS buffer and incubated for 1 h with biotinylated antibodies. After being washed twice with PBS, sections were incubated with the avidin-biotin complex using the Elite Vectastain kit (Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA). Chromogen reactions were then performed with diaminobenzidine (DAB; Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.003 % H2O2 for 10 min. Once washed with distilled water, the sections were mounted on glass slides and dried. Afterward, they were covered with FluorSave (Calbiochem). To avoid considering any possible nonspecific signal in our analysis arising from nonspecific immunoglobulin binding, some mouse brain slices were treated as described above, but in the absence of primary antibodies.

Brain calcifications staining

Von Kossa staining

For mouse samples, parasagittal brain sections were stained using the Von Kossa technique to detect phosphate ions (PO43−). Briefly, after rinsing with deionized water, slices were incubated in a 1 % silver nitrate solution in a clear glass Coplin jar, placed under ultraviolet light, for 20 min. Next, after being rinsed with deionized water, slices were treated with 5 % sodium thiosulfate for 5 min to remove unreacted silver. Later, the slices were counterstained with toluidine blue for 2 min, treated with 95 % ethanol, and then covered with DPX (Sigma).

For Human samples; Human sections were pre-incubated at 55 °C overnight and then sequentially washed for 10 min in Xylene (catalog 131769.1611, Panreac, Barcelona, Spain), ethanol 100 %, ethanol 96 %, ethanol 70 % and finally distilled H2O to remove paraffin and rehydrated them. Later, the same protocols described for mouse samples were followed.

Alizarin staining

To detect calcium ions in the mouse brain, we used Alizarin Red staining solution from Millipore (catalog number 2003999) and followed the manufacturer's instructions.

Golgi staining

We used the FD Rapid GolgiStain™ Kit from FD Neurotechnologies (catalog number PK401) and followed the manufacturer's instructions.

Evans blue treatment

To assess BBB integrity, we followed the protocol described by Goldim et al., [31] based on intraocular administration of Evans Blue.

Images acquisition

Confocal images were acquired at RT with a TCS SPE microscope from Leica Microsystems equipped with a Plan Fluor 10 × dry objective lens NA = 0.30, 40 × Apochromat NA = 1.15 oil objective lens and 63 × Apochromat NA = 1.3 oil objective lens (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) and four different lasers lines (405, 488, 565 and 647 nm). Photomicrographs were acquired and representative images were converted to TIFF files using the Leica LAS AF v2.2.1 software (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany), and representative slices' images were converted to TIFF files using ImageJ software. For the comparative studies, all confocal images were acquired with identical settings, including the number of Z-sections and thickness.

Transmitted light images were acquired using a microscope (DM 1000, Leica) with DFC450 CCD camera (Leica Microsystems GmbH) using Leica Application Suite (v4.1). Sections were photographed with Plan 10 × dry objective lens (NA = 0.1) and insets with Plan S-Fluor 40 × dry objective lens (NA = 0.90, Nikon) at RT.

Quantification of total neurons, neurons bearing NFTs, microglia cells or area covered by glial cells

For each mouse, six series of parasagittal brain sections were selected for immunostaining with antibodies against AT8, MC1, NeuN, Iba-1, or GFAP, or for Von Kossa staining. Every series contained seven sections, each 30 μm thick and 250 μm apart, approximately spanning from Bregma 0.6 mm–2.3 mm (lateromedial axes), according to Paxinos' atlas [32]. In each analysis, at least four mice per genotype were included. For the intrahippocampal-treated mice, because the inoculation point was at coordinates mediolateral, −1.4 mm; anteroposterior, −2 mm from bregma, coronal brain sections series containing 11 slices of 30 μm thick, 250 μm apart, approximately spanning from Bregma −0.6 mm to −3.4 mm (anteroposterior axe) according to the atlas [32] were used. In this way, the analyzed area covered 1.2 mm from this point, extending anteriorly and posteriorly. Data are presented in box plots representing the distribution of the number of neurons bearing NFTs, total neurons, microglial cells, or area covered by glial cells within hippocampal or thalamic sections from each group (median, IQR, maximum, and minimum values). The tissue slices were mounted on slides with blind codes for the analyzer. Images of the whole hippocampal area or whole thalamic area were constructed from 20 to 30 individual images acquired at low magnification. The background signal was determined by visual analysis, yielding a range value of 165–170 on a 0–255 scale, where 0 represents white and 255 represents black.

Morphological analysis

For the microglial morphological analysis, brain sections were immunohistochemically stained with a rabbit polyclonal anti-Iba-1 antibody. For each slice, four random images of the structure of interest were taken using the DM 1000 microscope coupled with a DFC450 CCD camera (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Images were analyzed using FIJI software (US National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, United States).

Skeleton analysis binary images were obtained using a 40 × objective. Binary images were then skeletonized and analyzed through FIJI Analyze Skeleton (2D/3D) plugin, as previously described [33]. The number of cell ramifications and branch length were calculated for each analyzed genotype (with at least four animals per condition, and graphs showing at least eight randomly analyzed microglial cells per mouse).

For fractal analysis, at least 18 random microglia cells from each experimental condition were analyzed using the FracLAc FIJI plugin. The area covered by cells was calculated for each experiment and condition.

For neuronal morphological analysis, we used the Sholl technique. This analysis required brain sections to have a thickness of 100 μm to cover most of the brain area covered by a single neuron. The study was conducted using transmitted light images of the thalamic area acquired from a Golgi-stained brain section, which enables visualization of the entire neuron structure. For each slice, five random images were captured from the analyzed structure using the DM 1000 microscope, coupled with a DFC450 CCD camera (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Binary photos acquired using a 100x objective were analyzed using the Sholl plugin incorporated in the FIJI software following the available instructions (US National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, United States).

Tissue processing for western blot

Mouse samples: Extracts for Western blot analysis were prepared by homogenizing fresh mouse hippocampi in ice-cold extraction buffer containing 20 mM Hepes, 100 mM NaCl, 50 mM NaF, 5 mM EDTA, 5 mM Na3VO4 (all reagents from Sigma; MS, USA), 1 % Triton X-100, okadaic acid (Calbiochem), and Complete TM Protease Inhibitor Cocktail Tablets (Roche Diagnostics GmbH), pH 7.4. Samples were homogenized at 4 °C, and protein content was determined using the Bradford assay before adding loading buffer and boiling at 100 °C for 5 min.

Human samples

Fresh samples from the hippocampus were obtained at the time of autopsy and immediately frozen in dry ice, then stored at −80 °C for biochemical studies. Control, AD, and PiD brain samples were processed following the same protocol as described for mouse samples.

Synaptosome samples

The protein content of the synaptosomal-enriched fraction was determined by using Bradford assay before adding loading buffer and boiling at 100 °C for 5 min.

Western blot analysis

Samples from mouse, human or synaptosomes corresponding to 20 μg of total protein were run by SDS-PAGE on 10 % Tris-Glycine-SDS gels and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Amersham Biosciences). The experiments were performed using the following primary antibodies: mouse anti-α-Tubulin (catalog T5168, Sigma-Aldrich, 1:10,000), rabbit anti-TNAP (catalog GTX100817, Gentex, 1:500), mouse anti-AT8 (phospho Tau, Ser202/Thr205, catalog MN1020 Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:100), mouse anti-Tau1 (dephospho Tau at Ser 195/198/199/202 catalog MAB3420, Millipore, 1:500), mouse anti-PHF-1 antibody (phospho Tau, Ser 396/404, kindly provided by Albert Einstein College of medicine on behalf of Prof Peter Davies, 1:1,000), mouse anti-Tau5 (total Tau catalog. 577801 Millipore, 1:10,000), mouse anti-Synaptophysin (catalog S5768, Sigma-Aldrich, 1:10,000), mouse anti-Postsynaptic density 95 (PSD-95, catalog 124011, Synaptic Systems, 1:1,000), rabbit anti-Syntaxin (catalog sc-13994, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, 1:2,000), mouse anti-β-actin (catalog. A5441, Sigma-Aldrich, 1:10,000), rabbit anti-M1R (catalog AB5164, Merck Millipore, 1:250), and rabbit anti-PiT2 (catalog. A304-474A, Bethyl, 1:2,000). Next, the membranes were washed three times for 10 min with PBS-Tween and then incubated with secondary antibodies (1:10,000) conjugated to fluorophores (emitting at 700 or 800 nm) for 1 h at RT. Protein bands were detected using the LI-COR Odyssey Classic and associated Image Pro analysis software (LI-COR Biosciences, Cambridge, UK). In the figures, we present representative Western blot images, which were used for quantification using ImageJ software (v1.52n, NIH, Bethesda, MD, United States). In all cases, the average intensity value of the pixels in a background-selected region was calculated and subtracted from each pixel in the samples. The densitometry values obtained in the linear range of detection with these antibodies were normalized to the values obtained with an anti-β-actin or anti-α-tubulin antibody to correct for any deviation in the loaded amounts of protein.

Statistics

Data in box plots represent the distribution of numeric data values per group (median, IQR, maximum, and minimum values). The numbers of mice per group used in each experiment are annotated as “n” in the corresponding figure legends. Figures and statistical analyses were generated using GraphPad Prism (v6.00, www.graphpad.com). To assess whether the data met the normal distribution, the Shapiro-Wilk or Kolmogorov-Smirnov tests were performed. The ROUT method was used to identify outlier values. For a two-group comparison, data were analyzed using a two-tailed, unpaired Student's t-test. For multiple comparisons, when only one variable was compared, data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test. The statistical test used, and p-values are indicated in each figure legend. Significance was considered at ∗P ≦ 0.05 throughout the study. Marginal significance was considered at #P ≦ 0.08.

Conditional neuronal TNAP deletion ameliorates cognitive decline in a tauopathy mouse model

To elucidate whether a specific neuronal TNAP blockade may be an effective therapeutic strategy for preventing the spread of Tau-associated pathology, we generated a new transgenic mouse model by crossbreeding the well-characterized tauopathy mouse model, P301S+/− mice [34], with a conditional deletion of Alpl in neuronal cells, the gene that encodes TNAP. To achieve this, we crossed the heterozygous Alpl floxed mouse (Alplf/+) [25] with CKIICre mice, which drive Cre recombinase expression in excitatory neurons under the control of the calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II alpha (Camk2a) promoter in the forebrain [27]. When characterizing the newly generated transgenic murine lines, as expected, both Alplf/f;CKIICre and Alplf/f;CKIICre/P301S+/− mice showed significantly lower cortical TNAP activity than WT mice (56.7 ± 4.4 % and 48.2 ± 7.5 %, respectively). On the contrary, as previously reported [11], P301S+/− mice exhibited higher cortical TNAP activity than WT mice (40.2 ± 14.8 %, Fig. 1B, left panel), however, no significant differences in cerebellar TNAP activity were observed between all analyzed genotypes, in accordance with the specificity of the Camk2a promoter (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Neither it was observed differences in plasma TNAP activity between WT, Alplf/f;CKIICre, or Alplf/f;CKIICre/P301S+/− mice. However, P301S+/− mice presented higher plasma TNAP activity than WT mice (an increase of 28.1 ± 8.7 % compared to WT mice, Fig. 1B). To confirm the specific lack of neuronal TNAP in Alplf/f;CKIICre mice, we isolated synaptic terminals (synaptosomes) from the cortex of WT, Alplf/f;CKIICre and Alplf/f;CKIICre/P301S+/− mice. It is worth noting that although the TNAP protein was not detected by the Western blot technique in the synaptosomes-enriched fraction from Alplf/f;CKIICre mice (Supplementary Fig. 1C), however, a very low residual TNAP activity was detected in these mice (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Similar results were observed in the synaptosomes-enriched fraction from Alplf/f;CKIICre/P301S+/− mice (Supplementary Fig. 1C).

Once we demonstrated that our strategy led to a specific neuronal deletion of TNAP, we aimed to evaluate how this condition affected cognitive responses in P301S+/− mice. Because previous studies reported that P301S+/− mice exhibited cognitive decline and impaired spatial memory in adulthood [35], we analyzed the behavior of WT, P301S+/−, Alplf/f;CKIICre, and Alplf/f;CKIICre/P301S+/− mice at 8–10 months of age. To evaluate spatial memory capacity, mice were subjected to three tests: the Novel Object Recognition (NOR), T-Maze (TM), and Barnes (BT) (Fig. 1C). In the NOR or TM, P301S+/− mice spent less time exploring the new object or the new arm than the WT mice (Fig. 1D and E), suggesting impaired short-term spatial memory (Fig. 1E). In agreement with this hypothesis, P301S+/− mice took longer to find the escape hole than WT mice during both the test training sessions and the test day in the BT (Fig. 1F). Notably, Alplf/f;CKIICre/P301S+/− mice spent a similar time to WT mice and less time than P301S+/− mice to explore the new object in NOR or find the escape hole during the training sessions and test day of the BT (Figs. 1D and F). Curiously, in the T-M, although Alplf/f;CKIICre/P301S+/− mice spent more time exploring the new arm than P301S+/− mice, they spent less time than WT or Alplf/f;CKIICre in this task (Fig. 1E). These results suggest that selective neuronal TNAP depletion protects against spatial memory impairment associated with Tau-induced pathology. Next, to evaluate the anxiety/disinhibition profile, mice were assessed by the elevated plus maze (EPM) and Open field (OF) tests (Fig. 1C). Our analysis showed that P301S+/− mice spent more time exploring the open arms in the EPM than their WT littermates, suggesting a decreased anxious behavior or an increase in the disinhibitory phenotype (Fig. 1G). However, the OF test failed to detect an increased disinhibitory phenotype in P301S+/− mice, as observed in the EPM (Supplementary Fig. 1D). It is worth noting that selective neuronal TNAP depletion did not prevent the disinhibition profile in P301S+/− mice (Fig. 1G). We also assessed the depressive-like status of P301S+/− mice using the tail suspension test (TST). As previously reported [36], P301S+/− mice spent less time immobile than WT mice, suggesting that they suffer from an opposite state to depression, such as mania. Interestingly, neuronal TNAP blockade did not prevent this condition in P301S+/− mice (Fig. 1H). Finally, balance and coordination capacity were analyzed by the rotarod test (RR) (Fig. 1C). Our analysis did not reveal any significant differences between the analyzed genotypes (Fig. 1I). It is to be noted that Alplf/f;CKIICre mice did not show significant differences compared with WT mice in any assessed behavioral tests (Fig. 1D–F). Finally, no significant differences by sex were found when behavioral tests were analyzed considering this condition (Fig. 1D–I).

Neuronal TNAP deletion prevents the loss of neurons, reduces the incidence of neurofibrillary tangles, and avoids microgliosis and astrogliosis in the hippocampus of P301S+/− mice

In agreement with the recovery of neuronal survival and increased connectivity, Alplf/f;CKIICre/P301S+/− mice did not exhibit the hippocampal gliosis and microgliosis observed in P301S+/− mice (Fig. 2J–M). Dephosphorylation of extracellular Tau by TNAP favors the spreading of Tau-induced pathology

Given that TNAP dephosphorylates eTau at multiple sites, promoting its neurotoxicity [19], we asked which eTau species —dephosphorylated eTau (dpTau) or phosphorylated eTau (pTau) —might be more effective at spreading Tau-associated neurotoxicity. To achieve this goal, we used a dephosphorylated eTau as previously reported [19]. Specifically, we intrahippocampally administered (i.h.) phosphorylated human Tau treated with TNAP (dpTau) or not (pTau), or a vehicle solution (containing TNAP and buffer solution) to a new small set of 4-month-old WT mice. TNAP treatment caused a significant reduction in the phosphorylation rate of Tau protein both at Ser202/Thr205 sites and Ser 396/404, detectable by AT8 and PHF-1 antibodies, respectively, as well as a significant increase in dephosphorylation rate at Ser 195/198/199/202, detectable by Tau1 antibody [37] (Fig. 3A). 90–100 days after i.h. mice were behaviorally analyzed using a similar battery of behavioral tests previously used (Fig. 3B and C). Remarkably, dpTau-treated mice spent less time exploring the new object in the NOR or the new arm in the T-Maze test than WT mice (Fig. 3D and E), suggesting that this treatment caused an impairment in short-term spatial memory. Nevertheless, no significant differences were observed between pTau-treated mice and vehicle-treated mice in either of the tests mentioned above (Fig. 3D and E). Curiously, non-induction of decreased anxiety/disinhibition profile was detected in neither the dpTau-treated nor the pTau-treated mice using EPM and OF tests (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. 2H). Interestingly, when mice were tested with the RR test, dpTau-treated mice exhibited shorter latency times than pTau-treated or vehicle-treated mice, indicating a motor deficiency (Fig. 3G). Confirmation of this condition was achieved using the limb-clasping test (CT) (Fig. 3H), which is also used to assess this capacity. In concordance with these results, dpTau-treated mice traveled a shorter distance than vehicle-treated or pTau-treated mice in the OF test (Supplementary Fig. 2F). These results indicate that dpTau, but not pTau, treatment, at least in the temporary paradigm used here, can induce memory and motor impairment in WT mice. After the behavioral assessment, we evaluated how pTau or dpTau i.h. impacts on the spreading of Tau-induced toxicity. To achieve this goal, we analyzed the incidence of hippocampal cells bearing intracellular NFTs using two specific antibodies, MC1 and AT8. In line with the behavioral results, immunological studies revealed that the hippocampi of dpTau-treated mice exhibited a higher incidence of neurons bearing positive MC1 NFTs than pTau-treated mice (Fig. 3I and J). Similar results were obtained using the AT8 antibody (Fig. 3K). Using the anti-Tau clone T49 antibody, which does not recognize human Tau protein, we observed that exogenous injection of human dpTau affects the native mouse Tau protein and promotes its aggregation in NFTs (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Our results suggest that extracellular dpTau has a higher neurotoxic spreading capacity than pTau.

Conditional neuronal TNAP overexpression causes cognitive impairment in mice

Since previous studies reported that focal overexpression of TNAP can induce intracellular Tau phosphorylation and aggregation in hippocampal cells neighboring those overexpressing the ectoenzyme [11], we asked whether selective TNAP overexpression in neurons, by itself, can trigger Tau-associated neurotoxicity. To this end, we generated a new transgenic mouse line by crossing HprtALPL mice, which harbor a floxed “stop cassette” and the human ALPL cDNA inserted into the Hprt locus on the X chromosome [26], with CKIICre mice. In the resulting double transgenic mice (HprtALPL;CKIICre), Cre expression excises the stop cassette specifically in excitatory neurons, leading to TNAP overexpression. All possible genotypes were detected in the generated offspring, and no alteration in predictive rates was observed (Fig. 4A). Because in the initial crosses we used male carriers of floxed ALPL, only females that overexpressed neuronal TNAP were obtained. As expected, HprtALPL;CKIICre mice exhibited almost twice TNAP activity in the cortical brain area compared to WT mice (Fig. 4B). However, only a very slight tendency to increase the plasmatic TNAP activity was detected in HprtALPL;CKIICre mice (Fig. 4B). Consistent with the specificity of Camk2a promoter, no significant difference in cerebellar TNAP activity was observed between HprtALPL, CKIICre, and WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 3B). In line with these results, cortical synaptosomes from HprtALPL;CKIICre showed a higher expression of TNAP and four times more TNAP-activity than that detected in WT mice (443.2 ± 57.1 % compared to that observed in WT synaptosomes, Supplementary Fig. 3C). Next, using a similar battery of behavioral tests to those previously described, a new subset of HprtALPL;CKIICre and WT mice was tested at 10 months of age (Fig. 4C). Notably, the newly generated transgenic line, HprtALPL; CKIICre mice, developed an evident memory deficiency, as detected by NOR and TM tests (Fig. 4D and E). However, inconsistent results were obtained regarding the anxiety-related profile. Whereas no apparent differences between HprtALPL, CKIICre mice, and WT mice were observed in the EPM test (Fig. 4F), a marginally significant increase in the number of entries in the arena's center was detected by HprtALPL, CKIICre mice compared to WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 3D). Non-evident motor deficiencies were detected in HprtALPL;CKIICre mice, neither by the RR test nor the CT test (Fig. 4G and H). More surprisingly, in the CT test, HprtALPL;CKIICre mice obtained a significantly lower score than WT mice (Fig. 4H). In line with these results, both genotypes showed similar traveled distances in the OF test (Supplementary Fig. 3D). Considering the accumulating evidence over the last few years indicating that the thalamus plays a significant role in the development and progression of AD and other Tauopathies [[38], [39], [40], [41]], we examined this structure using the newly generated transgenic mouse line. Surprisingly, immunological and histological studies revealed that HprtALPL;CKIICre mice presented severe thalamic damage, characterized by neuronal loss (Fig. 5D) and severe neuronal dendritic pruning (Fig. 5E). Moreover, the significant reduction in presynaptic marker STX levels detected in these mice (Fig. 5F) strongly suggests that HprtALPL;CKIICre mice exhibit a substantial decrease in the number of thalamic synaptic contacts. Interestingly, the reduction of thalamic neurons was matched with a significant decline of muscarinic M1 receptor expression in the thalamus (Supplementary Fig. 4D). Further analysis also revealed that HprtALPL;CKIICre mice exhibited a significant increase in the phosphorylation rate of intracellular Tau protein at the Ser202/Thr205 sites compared to WT mice (Fig. 5G). In line with these findings, a reduced number of scattered thalamic neurons (less than 8 % of the analyzed thalamic neurons) showed intracellular aggregates identified by the MC1 antibody in the younger HprtALPL;CKIICre mice, but were absent in the aged-matched WT mice (Fig. 5H). In accordance with this neurodegenerative scenario, both significant astrogliosis and microgliosis were detected in the thalamus of HprtALPL;CKIICre mice (Fig. 5I and J). Further analysis of microglia morphology revealed that HprtALPL;CKIICre mice have less ramified thalamic microglia compared to WT mice and therefore cover less thalamic area than WT mice (Supplementary Fig. 4E–G). These morphological changes suggest microglial activation.

Neuronal TNAP contributes to the brain calcification observed in patients with tauopathy

Over the years, evidence has accumulated pointing to tauopathy patients, including AD patients, suffering from brain calcifications in the hippocampus and other affected brain areas [42,43]. Considering that TNAP degrades pyrophosphate, a natural inhibitor of calcification, thereby promoting the formation of calcium phosphate deposits, we analyzed whether our transgenic mouse model develops cerebral calcifications using both Von Kossa staining to detect phosphate ions (PO43−) and Alizarin Red S staining to detect calcium ions. Interestingly, we found that HprtALPL;CKIICre mice develop brain calcifications in various brain areas, including the thalamus, striatum, and hippocampus, which are detectable by both techniques (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. 5A). To simplify the analysis of calcifications, we divided them into microcalcifications (diameter <30 μm) and macrocalcifications (diameter> 30 μm). We also focused on only two brain structures: the hippocampus and the thalamus. Our analysis revealed that the brain area with the highest incidence and largest calcifications was the thalamus (Fig. 6A–E). It is worth noting that macrocalcification was absent in both the hippocampus and thalamus of WT mice, which exhibited only microcalcifications in these structures (Fig. 6A–E). Interestingly, the HprtALPL;CKIICre mice exhibited increased PiT2 expression in the hippocampus, but not in the thalamus, compared to WT mice (Fig. 6K and Supplementary Fig. 5B). Moreover, although no evident alterations in the XPR1 distribution pattern were observed between WT and HprtALPL;CKIICre mice in the thalamus, or in different hippocampal regions, like CA1 and CA3, a significant increase in expression of XPR1 was detected in the dentate gyrus of HprtALPL;CKIICre (Supplementary Fig. 5C–5G). Discussion

Our studies have shown that an acute, whole-body TNAP blockade induces beneficial effects in the tauopathy mouse model by preventing the interneuronal propagation of Tau-induced toxicity [11]. However, potential side effects outside the CNS arising from a foreseeable chronic general TNAP blockade raise questions about the benefit-to-risk ratio of this strategy [23,24]. To evaluate the effectiveness and safety of a therapeutic approach based on the selective TNAP blockade at the CNS, we depleted neuronal TNAP in a well-characterized mouse model of tauopathy, P301S+/− mice. Our results demonstrate that selective blockade of neuronal TNAP not only reproduced but even improved the beneficial effects already associated with a general TNAP blockade [11]. Thereby, this approach not only reduced neuronal loss in the hippocampus of P301S+/− mice as previously observed by a general TNAP blockade [11], but also decreased the burden on neurons bearing NFTs, prevented the loss of synaptic contacts, avoided reactive astrogliosis and microgliosis, and prevented memory loss in these mice. However, unlike the effect observed with a general TNAP blockade [11], specific neuronal TNAP downregulation failed to avoid the decreased anxiety-like behavior or disinhibition profile in P301S+/− mice. These unexpected results suggest that the neuronal TNAP is not involved in the molecular mechanisms underlying anxiety/disinhibition profile, but instead in those related to memory. In line with this hypothesis, different studies have linked different mood disorders, including depression, chronic stress, and decreased anxiety/disinhibition, with alterations in astrocyte cellular features or gene expression in this neural lineage [47]. Given that TNAP is expressed, among other lineages, in astroglia cells [13,14], additional studies should be done to explore the specific contribution of astrocytic TNAP in the decreased anxiety-like behavior or disinhibition profile developed by P301S+/− mice [11]. However, the preferential localization of neuronal TNAP in nerve endings [48], where it plays a key role in regulating neurotransmission [13], aligns well with the beneficial effects observed in memory dysfunction. Furthermore, the absence of behavioral alterations or significant biochemical changes in Alplf/f;CKIICre mice suggests that selective neuronal TNAP blockade is a safe approach, avoiding the potential side effects associated with a chronic whole-body TNAP blockade. Moreover, the fact that neuronal TNAP depletion avoided the elevation of plasma TNAP activity observed in P301S+/− mice proposes that cerebral TNAP contributes to increasing plasma TNAP activity detected in tauopathy patients, as previously proposed in AD patients [12]. Therefore, these findings consolidate the value of this parameter as a plasma biomarker for AD [12,17,18]. On the contrary, the fact that HprtALPL;CKIICre mice only showed a slight rise in plasma TNAP activity suggests that additional concomitant factors, such as a compromise of the BBB, would be required for an elevation in neuronal TNAP to contribute to the increase in plasma TNAP activity. Supporting this hypothesis, we did not detect a significant compromise of the BBB in HprtALPL;CKIICre mice. Nevertheless, further studies are needed to clarify this.

Although the factor(s) causing the increased cerebral TNAP in tauopathies remain unknown, accumulating evidence supports the notion that this condition plays a critical role in the progression of tauopathy, as it favors Tau-induced neurotoxicity by dephosphorylating the eTau [5,11,[19], [20], [21]]. Consistent with this hypothesis, our data suggest that in vivo inoculation of dephosphorylated eTau by TNAP (dpTau) in the hippocampus promotes a greater spread of NFTs in this area than that caused by inoculation of phosphorylated eTau (pTau). Although to be sure that dpTau induces a greater seeding capacity than pTau, additional experiments using more specific assays to address this question, such as FRET-based biosensor assays, should be conducted. Nevertheless, our results strongly support this hypothesis by evidencing that dpTau-treated mice exhibited a higher incidence of hippocampal neurons bearing NFTs than pTau-treated mice, as well as a clear and significant memory loss and motor dysfunction, which were not observed in pTau-treated mice, at least under our experimental conditions (only three months post-treatment). In agreement with these results, beneficial effects detected in Alplf/f;CKIICre/P301S+/− mice were associated with a significant increase in CSF pTau levels. Nevertheless, although our results support the role of dpTau as the eTau neurotoxic species, the question arises as to whether dpTau alone induces toxicity or whether both species are neurotoxic. Considering the first hypothesis, the absence of cognitive dysfunction in pTau-treated mice suggests that pTau must be dephosphorylated before it induces neurotoxicity. If so, it would point to pTau-induced neurotoxicity occurring later or more slowly than that caused by dpTau. Otherwise, if both forms are neurotoxic, they may share or not common underlying mechanisms, which complicates the scenario and opens the possibility that several underlying mechanisms contributing to Tau-induced neurotoxicity coexist. In any case, given the preferred location of neuronal TNAP in synaptic contacts [13,48], it is reasonable to think that synaptic TNAP is one of the factors that determines that Tau-induced neurotoxicity depends mainly on synaptic connectivity rather than on spatial proximity, as it has been previously postulated [49]. Supporting this assumption, the reduction of neuronal loss and the number of neurons bearing NFTs observed in Alplf/f;CKIICre/P301S+/− were associated with a significant increase in synaptic contact markers. Additionally, the fact that neuronal TNAP depletion prevented the reduction in M1R expression levels further supports the notion that extracellular Tau induces neurotoxic effects by activating the muscarinic receptor M1R, as previously reported [5,20,21,48]. Similarly, thalamic neuron loss in HprtALPL;CKIICre also correlated with reduced M1R expression levels. Finally, our results confirm that dpTau-induced neurotoxicity is faster than that caused by pTau, suggesting that dpTau may be related to early neurotoxic events in the disease, making it a particularly interesting target from a pharmacological perspective for halting disease progression. Supporting this hypothesis, it has recently been reported that, in late-onset AD patients, although CSF pTau and total Tau levels are significantly increased, the pTau/totalTau ratio decreases compared to that observed in older healthy controls. Interestingly, authors correlated the worsening of episodic memory observed in these patients with thalamic atrophy [38].

Since previous studies have proposed that neuroinflammation associated with neurodegeneration also contributes to pathological Tau dissemination [34,50,51], we explore the impact of neuronal TNAP blockade in glial cells. Our results indicate that selective depletion of neuronal TNAP prevents astroglial and microglial activation in P301S+/− mice. Although our results suggest that neuronal TNAP is not a key factor in neuroinflammation associated with neurodegeneration, we can infer that, under our experimental conditions, astrogliosis and microgliosis are consequences of primary neuronal degeneration driven by TNAP-promoted dpTau-induced neurotoxicity. Supporting this conclusion, we observed that selective neuronal TNAP overexpression leads to neuronal degeneration and the activation of astroglia and microglia.

Considering that previous studies reported that local hippocampal TNAP overexpression causes intracellular Tau phosphorylation and aggregation [11], perhaps one of the most unexpected findings reported here is that neuronal TNAP overexpression in the forebrain did not induce any apparent effects on the hippocampus but severely affected the thalamus. This apparent discrepancy may be explained by considering technical issues. In a previous study, a lentivirus overexpressing human TNAP was used, thereby limiting TNAP overexpression to the hippocampal area infected with the virus. In contrast, the present study uses a Cre-lox system to overexpress TNAP in all forebrain neurons, thereby extending the evaluation to multiple forebrain nuclei. Beyond this consideration, these unexpected data also raise relevant questions; i) why are there selective thalamic effects in HprtALPL;CKIICre mice if they overexpress TNAP in forebrain neurons, and ii) what is the relationship between thalamic degeneration and the cognitive impairment developed by these mice? To answer these questions, we have to keep in mind that despite the associated memory impairment has been traditionally related to alterations in the medial temporal lobe, assuming that the hippocampus is the pre-eminent brain region controlling episodic memory [52], several clinical and behavioral findings indicate that the Papez circuit, which contains different thalamic nuclei among other, plays a critical role in episodic memory [53,54]. Within the Papez network, beyond the classic concept of a serial circuit, both anterior thalamic nuclei and the hippocampus have direct reciprocal connections, that support a balanced role for these anatomical structures [55]. Therefore, it is postulated that thalamic neuropathology occurs at the same time, or even before, that of those in the medial temporal lobe in AD patients [56]. Supporting this hypothesis, Braak and Braak reported that the anterodorsal thalamic nucleus presents a marked NTFs burden at the same time as these lesions spread toward the hippocampus (Stages III-IV) [57]. Furthermore, because the pattern of thalamic NFTs burden is preferentially observed in those nuclei with reciprocal hippocampal connections, it is plausible to postulate that the disruption of these connections may strongly contribute to the interruption of episodic memory associated with AD [56,58]. In agreement with this postulation, in the absence of appreciable hippocampal damage, including NFTs formation, neuronal loss, or neuroinflammation, we observed that neuronal TNAP-overexpressing mice exhibit a significant memory deficiency but do not develop a decreased anxiety/disinhibition profile or motor dysfunction, also associated with AD. Supporting the relevance of thalamic nuclei in the memory decline observed in HprtALPL;CKIICre mice, we detect severe thalamic damage in these mice, including neuronal loss, dendritic pruning, loss of synaptic contacts, increase in Tau phosphorylation leading to the formation of intracellular aggregates identified by MC1, NFTs-like, as well as significant reactive astrogliosis and microgliosis, and calcification in brain parenchyma. Although these findings again suggest that TNAP plays a key role in the early stages of AD, primarily affecting the thalamus, further studies are needed to confirm this hypothesis. One possibility would be to perform longitudinal studies evaluating whether thalamic neurodegeneration ultimately reaches the hippocampus and other anatomically connected structures in older HprtALPL;CKIICre mice, thereby exacerbating the associated symptoms. Nonetheless, supporting the early thalamic dysfunction associated with AD, various studies of presymptomatic familial AD (FAD) have found that the earliest sites of amyloid deposition are the thalamus and striatum [[59], [60], [61]]. Finally, using neuroimaging techniques, it was also found that thalamic volume loss appears to be one of the first signs of cognitive decline, already present during early mild cognitive impairment (MCI). In contrast, this volume loss does not further progress from late MCI to the AD dementia stage [41].

Although previous studies have reported brain calcifications in tauopathy mouse models [46], no evidence has been proposed regarding the triggering factors. Here, we demonstrate that neuronal TNAP upregulation is sufficient to induce brain calcifications, while its depletion significantly reduces calcifications in the tauopathy mouse model. This may be caused by TNAP hydrolysis of the extracellular pyrophosphate to inorganic phosphate (Pi), which may be then transported inside the cells by sodium-dependent phosphate transporters, like PiT2 [44], buffering in this way the extracellular Pi levels. However, TNAP overexpression may alter this equilibrium, affecting the PPi/Pi ratio and leading to precipitation as calcium phosphate deposits or “brain calcifications”. Notably, we found that, both in tauopathy mouse models and postmortem brains of tauopathy patients, the thalamus is a more susceptible brain area to develop larger calcifications than the hippocampus. One possible explanation will be that, in this structure, the compensatory mechanisms observed in the hippocampus do not occur, specifically the increased expression of PiT2. It is worth noting that the increase in hippocampal PiT2 expression was associated with elevated XPR1 levels, specifically in the dentate gyrus, suggesting a regulatory effect, as previously reported [62]. Remarkably, mutations in the gene encoding PiT2 may induce brain calcifications by altering phosphate homeostasis [63], and brain calcification has been correlated with impaired spatial learning and memory [64], suggesting that TNAP-induced brain calcification may be involved in the behavioral alterations observed in HprtALPL;CKIICre mice. Another possibility would be found in the Papez network, in which the thalamic hub would be more severely affected in HprtALPL;CKIICre mice, as reflected by the presence of macrocalcifications. Given the great complexity of afferent projections to the thalamus arising from different brain areas that overexpress TNAP [55], deciphering the specific CamKII-excitatory-neuron projection responsible for this thalamic susceptibility is highly challenging. On the other hand, the fact that these calcifications were associated with a significant increase in intracellular Tau phosphorylation levels, leading to the development of NFTs “like”, together with the finding that selective neuronal TNAP depletion reduces both parameters, suggests a plausible cause-and-effect relationship between neuronal TNAP overexpression and Tau-induced neurotoxicity. In this regard, the absence of robust Tau pathology in the HprtALPL;CKIICre mice, as observed in P301S mice, would argue that calcifications can be a primary pathology that may promote subsequent Tau pathology, perhaps by favoring a neurotoxic environment that could eventually predispose to tauopathy. Although additional experiments should be conducted to disentangle the sequence and potential synergy between these two pathological processes, in support of a possible synergy between both events, it was reported that tauopathy patients with diffuse NFTs and brain calcifications exhibit early but progressive memory disturbances [46].

Over the past few years, various immunotherapeutic approaches aimed at reducing eTau levels have been evaluated [65]. However, neither passive nor active immunization has proven to be an efficient or effective therapeutic strategy to control the disease, at least yet, and they have even been associated with adverse effects [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Therefore, additional therapeutic approaches should be considered [66]. Responding to this need, our previous work suggested that pharmacological inhibition of TNAP is a suitable therapeutic strategy for treating AD and other tauopathies [11]. However, potential side effects outside the CNS raised concerns about the safety of this strategy [23,24]. The results presented here demonstrate that selective neuronal TNAP blockade could be a safe and effective strategy to halt Tau-induced toxicity. Therefore, our findings emphasize the need to develop new, selective prodrugs against TNAP that release the active form of the TNAP inhibitor after crossing the blood-brain barrier.

Summarizing, the results presented here demonstrate that neuronal TNAP has a profound impact on brain cellular homeostasis. Exacerbated function of neuronal TNAP promotes i) the formation of brain calcifications, similar to those observed in patients with tauopathy, and ii) accelerates the spread of tau-induced neurotoxicity by favoring the dephosphorylation of eTau. Given that the thalamus is one of the brain areas most affected in the early stages of AD, its heightened sensitivity to TNAP-induced neurotoxicity suggests that this ectoenzyme plays a crucial role in the initial stages of this pathology and is a key molecular player in the disease's development. Finally, our present work confirms the effectiveness and safety of neuronal TNAP blockade in rescuing tau pathology in the P301S mouse model. Since TNAP blockade has also been demonstrated to be effective in reducing the cerebral senile plaque burden [16] and improving cognitive deficits associated with it, the development of selective brain TNAP blockers may be considered a priority strategy for treating AD patients, potentially even halting disease progression.
