Sivelestat and Incidence of Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome After Cardiovascular Surgery

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionDoes postoperative administration of the neutrophil elastase inhibitor sivelestat reduce the incidence of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) among patients undergoing cardiovascular surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass?FindingsIn this randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial of 424 patients, sivelestat significantly reduced the incidence of postoperative ARDS (16.8% vs 31.2%) and 90-day all-cause mortality (1.1% vs 5.2%) compared with placebo.MeaningThese findings suggest that sivelestat may be an effective pharmacologic strategy for preventing ARDS and improving survival in patients undergoing cardiovascular surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass.

Introduction

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is a severe postoperative complication after cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB).1 Its reported incidence varies widely, from less than 2% in large retrospective cohorts to more than 30% in prospective studies of patients at high risk, with associated mortality rates as high as 40% to 80%.1,2,3,4,5,6 This variability underscores the importance of surveillance intensity for specific patient populations. A systemic inflammatory response triggered by surgery and CPB plays a central role in the pathogenesis of ARDS.7,8,9,10,11

Among various inflammatory mediators, neutrophil elastase (NE) serves as both a key amplifier of pulmonary inflammation and a marker of neutrophil activation.9,10,11 CPB activates neutrophils via complement, thrombin, endotoxins, and cytokines such as interleukin 6 (IL-6), interleukin 8 (IL-8), and tumor necrosis factor (TNF).12,13,14,15 Activated neutrophils infiltrate lung tissue and release NE along with other proteolytic enzymes such as myeloperoxidase and matrix metalloproteinases.16,17 These enzymes degrade extracellular matrix proteins, damage alveolar-capillary barriers, and disrupt pulmonary function.16,17 The sustained elevation of proinflammatory cytokine and NE levels contributes to alveolar damage, alters vascular permeability, and exacerbates pulmonary edema—hallmarks of ARDS.18 NE not only causes direct tissue injury but also propagates a positive feedback loop, further amplifying neutrophil activation and inflammation.17,19 Given the central role of NE in the pathophysiology of ARDS, targeting NE may offer a promising strategy to prevent or mitigate lung injury after cardiovascular surgery with CPB.

Sivelestat sodium, a highly selective NE inhibitor, has been shown to be a promising agent for the treatment or prevention of ARDS with minimal adverse effects.20 It reduces pulmonary edema and lung injury, improves oxygenation, shortens the duration of mechanical ventilation, and potentially improves overall survival in acute lung injury and respiratory distress.21,22,23,24 A randomized clinical trial by Morimoto et al19 demonstrated that sivelestat significantly shortened the duration of mechanical ventilation among patients undergoing thoracic aortic surgery with deep hypothermic circulatory arrest. In addition, a retrospective study reported that administration of sivelestat at the initiation of CPB improved the oxygenation index (Pao2/Fio2 [fraction of inspired oxygen] ratio) and reduced the duration of mechanical ventilation among patients undergoing hypothermic circulatory arrest.25 An observational study found that sivelestat sodium significantly improved postoperative oxygenation among patients with high risk of lung injury after cardiovascular surgery.10,11

The effect and mechanisms of sivelestat in reducing the risk of ARDS among patients after cardiovascular surgery requiring CPB is largely unknown. We report a randomized clinical trial to evaluate the efficacy of sivelestat in a population who underwent major cardiac and vascular procedures requiring CPB.

Methods

Trial Design

This randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial was conducted from February 15, 2024, to April 16, 2025, at the Cardiovascular Medical Center of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital, a major referral center serving a population of more than 150 million across Jiangsu and Anhui provinces in China (trial protocol in Supplement 1). The cardiac surgery department performs more than 2500 major procedures annually, supported by a dedicated 42-bed cardiac intensive care unit (ICU) and integrated with translational research at the Nanjing University Institute of Cardiothoracic and Vascular Diseases. Of 424 randomized patients (1:1), a total of 42 were excluded prior to receiving the study intervention: 22 in the sivelestat group (12 withdrew consent and 10 discontinued due to concerns about the blood sampling protocol) and 20 in the placebo group (2 withdrew consent and 18 declined to continue, citing anxiety or reconsideration regarding trial participation). These withdrawals were related to factors such as the preoperative admission period, during which patients awaiting surgery in multibed rooms could experience uncertainty or anxiety about trial procedures or allocation. All withdrawals occurred while the study blinding was fully intact. The reasons for withdrawal reflected participants’ subjective perceptions and tolerance of the trial procedures themselves, and were not indicative of any knowledge regarding treatment allocation. The blinding was strictly preserved at all critical stages: during the preoperative period when withdrawal decisions were made, and during the postoperative intervention in the ICU. Key practical measures included the use of identical opaque covers for all infusion bags to prevent visual identification by ICU staff. Final analyses included 190 patients receiving sivelestat and 192 patients receiving placebo. The study was approved by the institutional ethics committee of Nanjing Drum Tower Hospital. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants through a face-to-face discussion led by the attending surgeon, involving the patient and direct family members. Participants and families received a comprehensive information package (including the informed consent document, a patient information booklet, and compensation details). For participants unable to write, consent was documented by a family member’s signature or a joint signature. The informed consent process included permission to use clinical and serologic data collected during hospitalization for research purposes. An independent data and safety monitoring board oversaw the study and reviewed a prespecified interim analysis to ensure patient safety and trial integrity. Before the manuscript was submitted for review, the protocol and statistical analysis plan had been submitted to this journal as part of the registration process. The reporting of this trial adheres to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guideline.

Patient Population

We prospectively screened all patients scheduled to undergo cardiac surgery (Figure 1). As a tertiary referral cardiac center, we receive many transfers with severe or complex cardiovascular conditions, resulting in a naturally high-risk study population; no preenrollment risk stratification or selective enrichment of patients at high risk was performed. Eligible patients were between 50 and 80 years of age, of either sex, and scheduled for elective cardiac surgery. Exclusion criteria were as follows: emergency surgery; deep hypothermic circulatory arrest required for the procedures; hepatic or renal dysfunction (Child-Pugh class B or C, or estimated glomerular filtration rate <35 mL/min/1.73 m2); elevated baseline inflammatory markers (IL-6 > 10 pg/mL, procalcitonin >0.5 ng/mL, or C-reactive protein [CRP] >1.0 mg/dL [to convert to milligrams per liter, multiply by 10.0]); active autoimmune disease, uncontrolled infection, or active malignant neoplasm (requiring treatment within 6 months); receiving other NE inhibitors (eg, ulinastatin, α1-antitrypsin); known allergy or intolerance to sivelestat sodium; pregnancy; or EuroSCORE II (European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation II) value indicating an estimated mortality risk >3%.

Blinding and Randomization

This study used a randomized, placebo-controlled design, in which participants and clinicians were blinded to their group allocation. To maintain the blinding of participants and clinicians, sivelestat and placebo were identical in appearance, packaging, and labeling, and were confirmed to be clear, colorless solutions on preparation by the independent pharmacist (W.-X.S.). We recognized that sivelestat sodium solution could produce foam upon agitation during routine nursing handling, posing a potential risk of unblinding. To proactively eliminate this, our protocol mandated that after preparation, all study infusion bags (for both the sivelestat and placebo groups) be immediately placed inside identical, white, opaque covers before being dispensed to the clinical units. Stratified block randomization was used to allocate participants in a 1:1 ratio to the sivelestat or placebo group. Stratification was based on the primary surgical procedure category: (1) isolated coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG); (2) isolated valve surgery or simpler intracardiac repair (valve replacement or repair, atrial septal defect or ventricular septal defect repair); or (3) complex cardiac or aortic procedures (aortic surgery, combined procedures, tumor resection). Randomization sequences were generated using SAS, version 9.4, software (SAS Institute Inc) by an independent statistician (H.-T.X.). Randomization occurred after a patient provided informed consent and was assigned a study ID; the treatment allocation was then determined sequentially from the pregenerated concealed list. Within each stratum, patients were randomized in blocks of 4 to ensure balanced allocation. Each patient received a unique study ID number and was assigned to their group based on the randomization list. Clinicians responsible for recruitment followed the inclusion and exclusion criteria and assigned patients sequentially according to the pregenerated list. Drug administration, including distribution and retrieval, was performed by designated unblinded personnel (E.J.-Z.), who maintained accurate documentation of all procedures. The preparation, dispensing, and retrieval of the study drug were managed by designated unblinded research pharmacists (W.-X.S.). Administration of the infusion at the bedside was performed exclusively by the blinded ICU nursing staff (W.-Z.X., W.-W.Z., J.-X.Y., and M.G.), ensuring that all patient-facing clinicians remained unaware of the treatment assignment. Participants remained unaware of their treatment assignment throughout the study period.

Intervention

A total of 382 patients were enrolled in this study. Randomization occurred on the day informed consent was provided. After surgery, the study infusion was initiated within 6 hours of admission to the ICU. All participants completed preoperative evaluations 5 to 7 days before surgery and laboratory tests, including echocardiography, complete blood count, creatine kinase-myocardial band (CK-MB), cardiac troponin I, procalcitonin (PCT), IL-6 and IL-8, CRP, myoglobin, B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), and d-dimer. These data, along with demographic information, were collected from the electronic medical record.

The cannulation technique, CPB management, and myocardial protection were performed according to our institutional standard, as previously detailed.9,10 In brief, CPB was initiated after achieving an activated clotting time greater than 480 seconds.

The CPB circuit was primed with a standardized crystalloid-based solution (lactated Ringer or acetate-buffered balanced salt solution) with a total volume of approximately 1200 to 1400 mL. To maintain oncotic pressure and reduce tissue edema, 10% human albumin was routinely added; gelatin-based solutions were used as alternatives when indicated, while hydroxyethyl starch was avoided in adults. The priming solution also included adjuncts: heparin for anticoagulation, mannitol as an osmotic agent to reduce CPB-induced edema and oxidative injury, and sodium bicarbonate to correct potential metabolic acidosis.

Conventional ultrafiltration was routinely applied during CPB to remove excess fluid, reduce hemodilution, and mitigate the accumulation of inflammatory mediators. In cases of significant hemodilution or fluid overload, modified ultrafiltration was performed after CPB at the surgeon’s discretion. Hematocrit was maintained between 22% and 25% throughout CPB to optimize oxygen delivery and blood viscosity. Core temperature was maintained between 32 °C and 34 °C.

For myocardial protection, Del Nido cardioplegia solution was administered to achieve and maintain complete electrical arrest. Retrograde cardioplegia was used after the initial antegrade Del Nido cardioplegia among patients requiring CABG.

A highly restrictive transfusion strategy was consistently applied, reflecting both institutional guidelines and the need for judicious use of blood products. Intraoperatively, red blood cell transfusion was typically triggered at a hemoglobin level of less than 7.0 to 8.0 g/dL (to convert to grams per liter, multiply by 10.0), while in the ICU, the threshold for stable patients was less than 7.5 g/dL, unless active bleeding or myocardial ischemia was present. Platelet and plasma transfusions were strictly guided by viscoelastic testing (rotational thromboelastometry and thromboelastography) and standard coagulation assays.

All patients were transferred to the cardiac ICU for postoperative management. The following standardized, evidence-based protocols were uniformly applied:

Mandatory lung-protective ventilation was applied with a tidal volume of 6 to 8 mL/kg of estimated body weight. Positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) was initiated at 5 cm H2O and titrated (typically 5-8 cm H2O) based on lung compliance and hemodynamic tolerance to maintain a Pao2 greater than 60 mm Hg and oxygen saturation of 95% or more, while simultaneously aiming to reduce Fio2 to 0.50 or less as quickly as tolerated.

A conservative fluid management strategy was used. Fluid administration was guided by bedside ultrasonographic assessment of the inferior vena cava collapsibility index (IVC-CI), targeting approximately 50% as an indicator of optimal intravascular volume, reflecting normovolemia under our conservative protocol. The goal during the initial 1 to 3 postoperative days was to achieve a net even-to-negative cumulative fluid balance, supported by maintaining adequate end-organ perfusion (urine output >0.5 mL/kg/h).

Weaning and extubation from mechanical ventilation were conducted based on standardized criteria. Patients were considered for a spontaneous breathing trial when meeting the following criteria: Pao2/Fio2 ratio greater than 200 mm Hg with PEEP of 5 cm H2O or less and Fio2 of 0.50 or less, hemodynamic stability (vasoactive-inotropic score <10), and adequate mentation (Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale score of −2 or more). A 30-minute spontaneous breathing trial on T-piece or continuous positive airway pressure 5 cm H2O was conducted. Extubation was performed upon spontaneous breathing trial success, defined as the absence of tachypnea (respiratory rate <35 breaths/min), hypoxia (oxygen saturation ≥95%), tachycardia (heart rate <140 beats/min or <20% sustained increase), hemodynamic instability (systolic blood pressure 90-180 mm Hg), and no signs of increased work of breathing.

All patients received a standardized perioperative prophylactic antibiotic regimen, which included an initial dose administered 30 minutes before skin incision, with intraoperative redosing as indicated, followed by a continuous 48-hour postoperative course (a second-generation cephalosporin, or vancomycin if allergic to cephalosporins). Decisions regarding therapeutic antibiotic escalation beyond this period were guided by a validated machine-learning algorithm for diagnosing early infection after cardiovascular surgery,26 ensuring an objective and protocolized approach.

The cardiac surgery ICU is staffed with in-house intensivists providing 24-hour coverage, 7 days a week. Noninvasive ventilation (NIV) and reintubation were strictly used as a rescue therapy based on objective criteria. All decisions regarding NIV, reintubation, or advanced rescue therapies were made after multidisciplinary consultation by clinicians (W.-Z.X., W.-W.Z., J.-X.Y., and M.G.) blinded to study group assignment. For patients with refractory hypoxemia meeting institutional criteria, additional rescue therapies were available, regardless of prior NIV or reintubation. These rescue therapies included hemodynamically guided diuresis, prone positioning, or veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. Low-dose corticosteroids were administered to patients with persistently elevated inflammatory markers after active infection had been ruled out.

Decision criteria for NIV or reintubation were as follows. NIV was trialed for patients developing acute or worsening respiratory insufficiency meeting at least 1 of 3 criteria: (1) tachypnea (respiratory rate >25 breaths/min); (2) hypoxemia (oxygen saturation <90% with Fio2 ≥0.5 via face mask, or Pao2 <60 mm Hg); or (3) hypercapnia (Paco2 >50 mm Hg with pH <7.35). Patients were directly reintubated if they presented with immediate life-threatening respiratory failure, such as severe hypoxemia (Pao2/Fio2 ratio <150), hemodynamic instability (mean arterial pressure <65 mm Hg requiring increased vasopressors), depressed mental status (Glasgow Coma Scale score <8), or inability to protect the airway. NIV failure—persistence or worsening of criteria despite NIV—also mandated immediate reintubation. All these decisions were made under the same blinded, multidisciplinary consultation process.

The study drug infusion was initiated within 6 hours of ICU admission for 3 primary reasons. First, it ensured initial hemodynamic stabilization after CPB and surgery, minimizing the risk that early postoperative instability would be confounded with a potential drug-related adverse event. Second, it allowed a standardized, controlled start point for the protocol across all participants. Third, and most critical from a pathophysiological perspective, while neutrophil priming begins during CPB, the peak of neutrophil-mediated pulmonary sequestration, endothelial injury, and the clinical manifestation of ARDS typically escalates in the early postoperative hours. Initiating sivelestat within this window targets this critical phase of injury escalation.27,28,29 This timing aligns with the intervention window used in previous positive studies conducted in similar high-risk cardiac surgery populations.10,11 Sivelestat was administered as an initial daily dose of 4.8 mg/kg diluted in 50 mL 0.9% sodium chloride, delivered continuously at 0.2 mg/kg/h for up to 7 consecutive days or until ICU discharge, whichever occurred first. Control patients received an identical volume and infusion of placebo (0.9% sodium chloride). Postoperative blood samples were collected on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 to assess hematologic and biochemical parameters, including complete blood count, CK-MB, myoglobin, BNP, d-dimer, cardiac troponin I, PCT, IL-6 and IL-8, and serum NE.

Primary Outcome

The primary outcome is the incidence of postoperative ARDS. We adopted a protocol based on the Berlin Definition for the diagnosis of ARDS, which explicitly excluded hydrostatic (cardiogenic) pulmonary edema as the primary cause of respiratory failure.30 In our fast-track cardiac surgery population, patients are routinely extubated early, whereas postoperative ARDS typically manifests later. To ensure rigorous and equitable application for patients—regardless of their respiratory support status at the time of hypoxemia assessment—we prospectively implemented a standardized, stepwise diagnostic protocol. This protocol was based on the patient’s respiratory support status at the time of hypoxemia assessment: (1) for patients undergoing invasive mechanical ventilation, the Pao2/Fio2 ratio was measured with a PEEP of 5 cm H2O or more; (2) for patients receiving noninvasive ventilation (NIV or bilevel positive airway pressure), the Pao2/Fio2 ratio was measured with an expiratory positive airway pressure or PEEP set at 5 cm H2O or more, thus fully adhering to the Berlin Definition; and (3) for extubated patients receiving high-flow nasal oxygen—a common clinical scenario in our cohort (eResults in Supplement 2)—the Pao2/Fio2 ratio was measured at a standardized flow of 50 to 60 L/min. This approach, which represents a pragmatic adaptation of the Berlin Definition for clinical practice, is supported by physiological data showing that high-flow nasal oxygen at these flows generates mean positive airway pressures of 3.1 to 5.0 cm H2O,31,32,33,34,35 thereby fulfilling the physiological intent of the Berlin Definition to standardize assessment under positive pressure. This protocol ensured that all ARDS diagnoses were made under a defined level of respiratory support, and it was applied consistently across both treatment groups.

Echocardiography was performed postoperatively to assess cardiac function. Inclusion criteria for ARDS required a left ventricular ejection fraction greater than 50% and an E/e′ ratio less than 15, indicating preserved systolic and diastolic function. Intravascular volume status was evaluated using the IVC-CI. Patients with IVC-CI less than 15% were considered to have elevated central venous pressure or volume overload and were therefore excluded from the ARDS group.

In addition, we integrated clinical data, serial BNP measurements, and chest radiographic findings to identify noncardiogenic causes. BNP levels were monitored daily for 3 consecutive days, and a consistent downward trend (reduction in BNP <0) was required to support the diagnosis of ARDS. Chest radiographs were reviewed to ensure the absence of classical features of cardiogenic pulmonary edema such as bat-wing–shaped or butterfly-shaped central infiltrates, cardiomegaly, and bilateral pleural effusion. In contrast, ARDS-compatible infiltrates were defined as bilateral, patchy opacities with peripheral or nonspecific distribution. Through this rigorous multiassessment protocol, we ensured that all ARDS diagnoses were made according to our standardized protocol, which operationalized the principles of the Berlin Definition across all respiratory support states.

Secondary Outcomes

The secondary outcomes of this study include abnormal laboratory parameters, such as inflammatory markers (white blood cell count, neutrophil count, IL-6, IL-8, TNF, CRP, PCT, systemic immune-inflammation index [SII], and NE) and myocardial injury markers (myoglobin, CK-MB, and cardiac troponin I), measured on postoperative days 1, 3, 5, and 7. Additional secondary outcomes include clinical scoring systems such as the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) and the Murray Lung Injury Score, as well as duration of mechanical ventilation, ICU length of stay, total hospital length of stay, 90-day mortality, and 180-day mortality. ARDS-related complications, including postoperative pneumonia, reintubation, and death, were also recorded.

Postoperative pneumonia was defined using a combination of clinical, radiologic, and laboratory criteria. Diagnosis required both the appearance of new or progressive pulmonary infiltrates on chest radiography and at least 2 of the following clinical criteria: fever (>38.0 °C) or hypothermia (<36.0 °C); leukocytosis (>12 000 cells/µL [to convert white blood cells to cells × 109 per liter, multiply by 0.001]) or leukopenia (<4000 cells/µL); or purulent tracheobronchial secretions. Microbiological confirmation (via endotracheal aspirate or bronchoalveolar lavage culture) was sought but was not mandatory for diagnosis. Patients were actively screened for pneumonia daily throughout their ICU stay and until hospital discharge. A sequential adjudication process was used by clinicians blinded to treatment allocation to ensure clear differentiation between ARDS and pneumonia. This process mandated that the diagnosis of ARDS be first established according to the Berlin Definition. Subsequently, the presence of pneumonia was assessed for all patients using the predefined criteria, with the critical requirement that pneumonia diagnosis necessitated new infectious signs not fully explained by the initial inflammatory response. This blinded methodology ensured unbiased distinction between patients with ARDS alone, those with ARDS complicated by secondary pneumonia, and those with pneumonia alone.

Sample Size Calculation

This study is a randomized clinical trial, with the primary outcome being the incidence of postoperative ARDS after cardiovascular surgery. According to previous literature, the incidence of ARDS ranges from 20% to 33%.2,3,4,11 In a previously published cohort study, the incidence of ARDS was 33% in the control group.3 This baseline risk aligns with recent prospective studies in rigorously monitored, high-risk cardiac surgery populations (eg, 29.9% in a study by Tamura et al4 and 33% in a study by Wang et al3). For this trial, we hypothesized that treatment with sivelestat would lead to a substantial reduction in ARDS incidence—from 33% in the placebo group to 18% in the treatment group (a 45% relative reduction). This effect size (≥40% relative risk reduction) was selected as it represents a clinically meaningful target for a severe complication and permitted a feasible sample size within a single-center design. Based on this assumption and using a 2-sided α level of .05 and 80% power, we calculated the required sample size using PASS software, version 15.0.5 (NCSS Statistical Software). The calculation yielded a minimum of 192 patients per group. Accounting for an anticipated 10% attrition rate (eg, loss to follow-up or withdrawal of consent), we determined that at least 212 participants per group would be needed, for a total of 424 participants. Ultimately, 190 patients were enrolled in the sivelestat group and 192 in the placebo group, which is considered acceptable for the analysis under the revised effect size assumption. A post hoc calculation indicated that with the final sample size and the observed placebo group event rate, the study’s power for the primary end point was approximately 78%, slightly below the planned 80%.

Statistical Analysis

Analysis was performed on an intention-to-treat basis using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0 (IBM Corp), and R software, version 4.2.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing). Continuous variables were expressed as mean (SD) values for normally distributed data or as median (IQR) values for nonnormally distributed data. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For group comparisons, the t test was used for continuous variables that followed normal distribution, while the Mann-Whitney test was applied for nonnormally distributed continuous variables. Categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test or the Fisher exact test, as appropriate. For the longitudinal analysis of biomarker levels (SII, IL-6, IL-8, TNF, NE, PCT) and Pao2/Fio2 ratios from postoperative days 1 to 7, linear mixed-effects models were used to appropriately account for within-patient correlation and temporal dependence. These models included group, time, and their interaction as fixed effects, with a random intercept for each patient. The results are reported as interaction P values, group main effect P values, and time main effect P values. Between-group comparisons at individual time points (postoperative days 1, 3, 5, and 7) were adjusted using the Bonferroni method and are reported as adjusted P values. For the analysis of 90-day survival, the final database lock occurred on October 12, 2025. This date ensured a complete 90-day follow-up period for all enrolled patients (the last patient was enrolled on April 16, 2025, with follow-up through July 15, 2025).

Univariable logistic regression was conducted to identify potential risk factors for ARDS. Variables with P < .05 in univariable analysis and without significant multicollinearity (based on collinearity diagnostics) were subsequently included in multivariable logistic regression models to identify factors independently associated with ARDS. Due to high multicollinearity among biomarker levels measured on postoperative days 1, 3, and 5, separate multivariable models were constructed using biomarkers from each of the time points individually. Based on the Pao2/Fio2 ratio, ARDS severity was classified as mild, moderate, or severe. Spearman correlation analysis was then used to examine the association between ARDS severity and biomarkers identified as independently associated with ARDS in the multivariable logistic regression. A 2-tailed P < .05 was considered statistically significant. Missing data were minimal and confined to scheduled research biomarker measurements, with an overall missing rate of 2.5%. A complete-case analysis was used for all models incorporating biomarkers. No data were missing for clinical outcomes or variables in the primary analysis.

Results

From February 15, 2024, to April 16, 2025, a total of 2987 patients underwent cardiac surgical procedures. Of these, 1633 were excluded due to procedure- or patient-related reasons, leaving 1354 patients potentially eligible for enrollment (Figure 1). After screening, 930 patients were further excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria, geographical limitations, or other reasons. Ultimately, 424 patients were randomized. In the sivelestat group, 12 patients withdrew consent before receiving the intervention and 10 discontinued due to concerns about frequent blood sampling. In the placebo group, 2 patients withdrew consent prior to intervention while 18 participants declined to continue due to anxiety or reconsideration regarding trial participation. Consequently, 382 patients (mean [SD] age, 62.9 [6.2] years; 210 men [55.0%] and 172 women [45.0%])—190 patients in the sivelestat group and 192 in the placebo group—received the allocated intervention, completed the study, and were included in the final analysis (Table 1; eTable 1 in Supplement 2). Formal testing confirmed no statistically significant differences in baseline demographic characteristics, preoperative laboratory values, or surgical details between groups.

The sivelestat group, compared with the placebo group, showed significantly lower 90-day mortality (1.1% [2 of 190] vs 5.2% [10 of 192]; P = .02) (Figure 2), reintubation rates (6.3% [12 of 190] vs 13.0% [25 of 192]; P = .03), NIV application rate (4.7% [9 of 190] vs 10.4% [20 of 192]; P = .04), and pneumonia incidence (13.2% [25 of 190] vs 29.2% [56 of 192]; P < .001). In terms of disease timing, the onset of ARDS was significantly delayed in the sivelestat group compared with the placebo group (mean [SD], 35.2 [12.3] vs 27.0 [11.4] hours; P < .001), whereas the timing of pneumonia onset was similar between groups (approximately 5 days; P = .49). No patient developed pneumonia prior to ARDS diagnosis. We interpret the parallel reductions in NIV and reintubation as robust evidence that sivelestat mitigated the overall severity of lung injury, thereby diminishing the need for both intermediate and ultimate respiratory rescue. The fact that some patients required direct reintubation (sivelestat, 3 vs placebo, 5) further illustrates the spectrum of respiratory failure severity, which was favorably influenced by sivelestat. No statistically significant intergroup differences were observed in ventricular fibrillation, cerebrovascular events, major bleeding, acute kidney injury, or allergic reactions. For patients discharged from the ICU before completing the 7-day treatment course, representing most patients (median ICU stay, 4 days [IQR, 2-5 days]), safety monitoring continued for 24 hours after treatment discontinuation, with all nonfatal adverse events captured during this defined monitoring period (eTable 2 in Supplement 2).

The survival benefit in the sivelestat group was statistically significant (log-rank P = .02).

Patients in the sivelestat group demonstrated a significantly lower incidence of ARDS compared with the placebo group (16.8% [32 of 190] vs 31.2% [60 of 192]; odds ratio, 0.45; 95% CI, 0.27-0.73; P < .001) (Table 2). Radiographic assessment revealed significantly less severe bilateral pulmonary opacities in the sivelestat group than the placebo group (no opacities: 83.2% [158 of 190] vs 68.7% [132 of 192]; P < .001). During the ICU stay, the sivelestat group showed significantly higher lowest Pao2/Fio2 ratios (>300 mm Hg: 83.2% [158 of 190] vs 68.7% [132 of 192]; P < .001), shorter median mechanical ventilation duration (10 hours [IQR, 6-18 hours] vs 17 hours [IQR, 8-20 hours]; P < .001), reduced median total hospital stay (19 days [IQR, 16-23 days] vs 22 days [IQR, 18-26 days]; P < .001). The onset of ARDS occurred substantially later than the median time to extubation, underscoring the clinical rationale for the standardized diagnostic protocol applied across different respiratory support modalities. No significant differences were observed between groups in PEEP levels and markers of noncardiogenic pulmonary edema (left ventricular ejection fraction, E/e′ ratio, IVC-CI, or number of reductions in BNP less than 0).

A key finding from the linear mixed-effects model was a highly significant group-by-time interaction effect specifically for NE, indicating that sivelestat dynamically altered the temporal trajectory of its primary pharmacologic target. In contrast, for downstream inflammatory biomarkers (IL-6, IL-8, TNF, SII, and PCT) and Pao2/Fio2 ratio, the group main effects were highly significant while the interaction effects were nonsignificant, indicating a consistent overall treatment benefit without a significant change in the slope of their postoperative course.

Given sivelestat’s specific mechanism as an NE inhibitor targeting neutrophilic inflammation, we performed comprehensive risk factor analysis. Univariable logistic regression identified several potential ARDS risk factors: treatment assignment (sivelestat vs placebo), the preoperative Society of Thoracic Surgeons score for prolonged ventilation risk, diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide, forced expiratory volume in 1 second to forced vital capacity ratio, and total intraoperative input (eTable 3 in Supplement 2). Multivariable analysis revealed specific postoperative biomarkers that were independently associated with ARDS risk: IL-6 and IL-8 on postoperative day 1; SII, IL-8 and NE on postoperative day 3; and SII and NE on postoperative day 5 (eTables 4-6 in Supplement 2). These findings were further validated by Spearman correlation, which demonstrated consistent associations between these inflammatory biomarkers and ARDS development. Furthermore, adjusted multivariable logistic regression models accounting for the baseline imbalance in chronic lung disease confirmed the independent protective effect of sivelestat (eTables 7 and 8 in Supplement 2). Analysis of postoperative supportive care demonstrated balanced management between groups (eTable 9 in Supplement 2). To corroborate the primary model findings and explore mechanisms in depth, a multilevel analytical strategy was used, with further results presented in Supplement 2. These include detailed temporal analysis of ARDS onset (eFigure 1 in Supplement 2), biomarker density distributions (eFigure 2 in Supplement 2), analysis restricted to the ARDS subpopulation (eFigure 3 in Supplement 2), and biomarker correlation networks (eFigure 4 in Supplement 2), as well as a sensitivity analysis of outcomes in patients not receiving high-flow nasal oxygen (eTable 10 in Supplement 2), all supporting a coherent narrative centered on NE inhibition (eResults in Supplement 2).

Discussion

This single-center randomized clinical trial demonstrates that sivelestat significantly reduces the incidence of ARDS and 90-day all-cause mortality among patients undergoing cardiovascular surgery with CPB. These findings position sivelestat as a promising preventive pharmacologic strategy for this severe complication.

The reported incidence of postoperative ARDS varies widely, ranging from 0.4% to 2% in large retrospective cohorts5,6 to more than 20% to 30% in prospective studies of patients at high risk.2,3,11 The observed ARDS rate in our placebo group (31.2%) is consistent with the higher end of this spectrum, reflecting the high-risk profile of patients referred to our tertiary center and the intensive prospective surveillance of this trial, a methodology recognized to more sensitively capture postoperative pulmonary dysfunction.36 Systemic inflammation triggered by surgery and CPB is a key pathogenic driver of ARDS,5,6,7,8,9 yet current management remains predominantly supportive. Sivelestat, a selective NE inhibitor, addresses this need by targeting a central mediator of endothelial injury, vascular permeability, and inflammatory amplification.19,21,22,23 Our findings further support the plausibility of this mechanism by demonstrating both clinical and biomarker-level improvements after treatment.

The incidence of ARDS after cardiac surgery demonstrates a bimodal distribution, heavily influenced by patient risk profile and surveillance intensity. Large, retrospective studies of unselected populations report low incidences of ARDS, typically below 2%,5,6 while prospective studies focusing on high-risk cohorts at tertiary centers consistently report substantially higher rates (20%-55%).4,10,11 This spectrum contextualizes our study: as a regional tertiary referral center, our enrolled patients represent a higher-risk segment. Accordingly, the observed ARDS incidence in our placebo group (31.2%) aligns with rates reported in similar high-risk populations, including a 33.5% incidence in a large contemporary cohort3 and a 29.9% incidence in a recent prospective study.4 This elevated risk profile provided the rationale for evaluating a preventive intervention. Building on prior evidence showing sivelestat’s benefit in high-risk settings,10,11 we hypothesized that sivelestat would reduce ARDS incidence by 45% relative to placebo—a target that was successfully achieved. These findings indicate that sivelestat is a targeted and effective preventive strategy for high-risk cardiac surgery populations, in whom the burden of ARDS is substantial.

Our findings regarding the intermediate-term safety of sivelestat are particularly noteworthy in light of the prior Sivelestat Trial in ALI Patients Requiring Mechanical Ventilation (STRIVE),37 which was halted due to increased mortality. In contrast, our study demonstrated a favorable safety profile during the critical postoperative period, with no excess mortality at 90 days. For the primary survival analysis, we selected the 90-day time point as it represented a period of complete follow-up for the entire cohort, ensuring methodological rigor. With a complete 180-day follow-up, no deaths occurred in either group after day 90, confirming the durability of the early survival benefit and the absence of the late mortality signal observed in STRIVE. This divergence can likely be attributed to fundamental differences in trial design; we investigated sivelestat as an intervention initiated early after CPB in a homogeneous cardiac surgery population at risk for ARDS, whereas STRIVE evaluated its therapeutic use in patients with established, heterogeneous ARDS. Our complete 90-day follow-up data provide robust evidence for the safety of sivelestat within this clinically decisive timeframe in our specific clinical context. Thus, our results help clarify previous safety concerns by situating sivelestat within a distinct preventive—not therapeutic—use case.

The choice to initiate sivelestat within 6 hours of ICU admission balanced targeting the peak postoperative inflammatory phase27,28,29 with ensuring post-CPB hemodynamic stability and protocol adherence. Earlier administration merits future study. Sivelestat significantly reduced ARDS incidence, pneumonia, and reintubation, and improved clinical outcomes. The observed reduction in infectious complications aligns with the pathophysiological concept that mitigating the initial inflammatory lung injury could reduce the need for and duration of invasive support, thereby potentially lowering the risk of secondary pneumonia. Our longitudinal analysis using linear mixed-effects models provided a nuanced understanding of its mechanism: sivelestat produced a dynamic, time-dependent suppression of its direct target, NE (significant interaction effect), while exerting a stable, overall reduction (significant group main effect) on a broad spectrum of downstream inflammatory mediators (IL-6, IL-8, TNF, PCT, and SII) and clinical Pao2/Fio2 ratios. The linear mixed-effects model analysis specifically highlighted that the suppression of NE over time was dynamic and distinct from the pattern observed for other biomarkers. This finding supports NE’s pivotal role and validates its inhibition as a preventive strategy. The strong correlation of IL-6 and PCT with treatment response is clinically useful, as these biomarkers are more readily assayed than NE. These biomarker patterns provide mechanistic coherence to the observed clinical benefits and underscore the centrality of neutrophil-driven injury in postoperative ARDS.

Our application of the Berlin Definition included standardized assessment under positive pressure (via NIV or high-flow nasal oxygen at 50-60 L/min),31,32,33,34,35 ensuring equitable diagnosis across respiratory support states in this fast-track population. This protocol was uniformly applied under blinding, preserving internal validity. Such standardized diagnostic rigor is essential in postoperative cohorts where respiratory support modes frequently fluctuate. For patients who progress to hypoxemic respiratory failure after extubation, adjunctive supportive measures such as prone positioning have been shown to improve oxygenation and may reduce reintubation rates,38 underscoring that ARDS management in this population often requires a multimodal strategy beyond pharmacologic prevention alone.

The generalizability of our positive findings must be interpreted within the context of this being a single-center study. Although the randomized design ensures robust internal validity, the patient population, high-volume surgical expertise, and standardized perioperative protocols, including specific lung-protective ventilation, fluid management, and transfusion practices, at our tertiary center in China may not be fully representative of other health care settings. These factors could influence both the baseline incidence of ARDS and the observed magnitude of treatment effect. Therefore, our results, while providing strong proof of concept, require confirmation through external validation in multicenter settings with varied clinical practices and patient demographics. Future multicenter collaborations will be essential to determine the reproducibility and scalability of this preventive strategy.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although withdrawals before intervention occurred under strict blinding and the numbers were comparable between groups (sivelestat, 22; placebo, 20), the reasons for withdrawal (eg, anxiety about trial procedures) were not evenly distributed. This postrandomization attrition, while occurring before any treatment exposure, could theoretically introduce an imbalance in unmeasured baseline characteristics or risk perceptions between the analyzed groups. We have no evidence that this imbalance occurred, but it remains a limitation of the pragmatic trial conduct. Second, its single-center design at a tertiary Chinese academic hospital may limit the generalizability of the findings; the high ARDS incidence observed in the placebo group reflects the specific high-risk profile of our cohort and intensive prospective surveillance at our center, and may not be representative of lower-risk settings. External validation in multicenter settings with varied clinical practices is warranted. Third, biomarker measurements were performed in our research laboratory using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay, which may introduce variability. Fourth, Pao2/Fio2 ratio–based severity stratification may be affected by ventilatory parameters—including PEEP levels—and by perioperative fluid status, potentially introducing classification variability. Fifth, the optimal timing for initiating sivelestat remains an open question. Sixth, due to budget constraints, several planned assays (eg, myeloperoxidase, IL-1β, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid analysis) were omitted. Seventh, biomarker sampling was limited to postoperative days 1, 3, 5, and 7, leaving gaps in daily inflammatory dynamics (eg, days 2, 4, 6, and 8). Eighth, a protocol-allowed, low-dose corticosteroid rescue therapy was administered to a small subset of patients with persistently elevated inflammatory markers after active infection was ruled out. Although this therapy reflects real-world practice for managing refractory inflammation, it introduces a concurrent intervention that could potentially confound the interpretation of biomarker trajectories and clinical outcomes among these specific patients. Ninth, the final analyzed sample size was marginally lower than the original recruitment target. A post hoc calculation indicated an achieved power of approximately 78% for the primary end point, which is slightly below the planned 80%. Although a statistically significant effect was observed, this minimal reduction in power is acknowledged. Tenth, sivelestat is not approved for use in many countries, including the US. Therefore, the immediate clinical applicability of our findings may be limited to regions where the drug is currently available and approved. Eleventh, large-scale multinational trials are needed to confirm the efficacy of sivelestat across diverse populations and health care systems. Despite these limitations, the consistency of clinical and biomarker findings strengthens confidence in the observed preventive effect.

Conclusions

In this single-center, randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial, postoperative infusion of sivelestat significantly reduced the incidence of ARDS and 90-day all-cause mortality among patients undergoing cardiovascular surgery. These results suggest that sivelestat may be an effective preventive strategy for postoperative ARDS in high-risk populations. Our findings require confirmation in larger, multicenter trials to establish generalizability.
