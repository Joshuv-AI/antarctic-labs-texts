Colistin-induced transition from non-mucoid to mucoid phenotype in resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa ST3351

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPseudomonas aeruginosa is an opportunistic pathogen frequently infecting the lower airways of individuals with cystic fibrosis (iwCF). We investigated colistin (CST) resistance mechanisms in P. aeruginosa ST3351 (Psa-ST3351) and their impact on bacterial virulence. Psa-ST3351, a novel CST-resistant sequence type, was isolated from a child with CF. We demonstrate that CST resistance in Psa-ST3351 depends on the addition of 4-amino-4-deoxy-L-arabinose to lipid A. Moreover, exposure to CST induced a non-mucoid-to-mucoid phenotypic switch characterized by enhanced swarming motility, decreased susceptibility to aminoglycosides, impaired ExoS secretion via the type-III secretion system, and increased bacterial competition, all hallmarks of a chronic infection phenotype. CST-treated Psa-ST3351 also exhibited reduced phagocytosis by macrophages, despite the induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines remaining unaffected, and enhanced the pathogenicity in a mouse pneumonia model. These findings suggest that CST-induced mucoid conversion in CST-resistant Psa-ST3351 may contribute to disease progression in iwCF, particularly when CST resistance and mucoid conversion remain undetected during CST nebulized therapy.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa, a non-fermenting Gram-negative rod, is an opportunistic pathogen that causes acute and chronic human infections [1]. It is a common cause of nosocomial infections, particularly pneumonia, in patients with compromised immune defenses, critical illnesses [2], or individuals with Cystic Fibrosis (iwCF) [3].

CF is an autosomal recessive genetic disorder that affects multiple organs [4]; it is caused by mutations in the gene encoding the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR), a transmembrane ion channel that regulates Cl− and Na+ transport [5]. CFTR dysfunction, manifested by reduced Cl− transport and increased Na+ reabsorption in secretory epithelia, leads to osmotic imbalance and thick, sticky mucus in the airways, creating a hospitable environment for bacterial colonization [6,7]. During chronic infection in the lower airways, P. aeruginosa downregulates the expression of genes involved in acute infection, thus escaping from recognition by the host immune system [8,9]. The bacterial chronic infection phenotype results from a complex adaptation involving physiological and genetic changes, ultimately selecting for highly persistent bacterial populations. Central to this adaptation is a shift from a non-mucoid to a mucoid phenotype, characterized by reduced pyoverdine production, but not pyocyanin, decreased protease secretion via the type-II secretion system (T2SS), diminished exotoxin (ExoS, ExoT, ExoU, and ExoY) secretion via the T3SS [8], and increased interbacterial competition mediated by the T6SS [10]. Concurrently, P. aeruginosa increases alginate production and decreases its antimicrobial susceptibility [8].

The emergence of multidrug-resistant, high-risk P. aeruginosa clones is a global health threat [11]. Therefore, P. aeruginosa is on the World Health Organization’s high-priority list of pathogens that require new antimicrobials [12]. Colistin (CST), a polymyxin-class antibiotic, is often used as a last-resort treatment for multidrug-resistant Gram-negative pathogens [13,14]. A common mechanism of CST resistance in these bacteria, including P. aeruginosa, involves modifications of the lipid A structure of the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) by the addition of 4-amino-4-deoxy-L-arabinose (L-Ara4N) or phosphoethanolamine groups [15]. Previously, we reported the isolation of a CST-resistant P. aeruginosa strain (CF053) from a child with CF; this strain was later designated as Psa-ST3351 and was found to harbor mutations in the two-component regulatory system prmAB [16].

In this study, we investigated the CST resistance mechanism in Psa-ST3351 and the effect of CST exposure on its pathogenicity. We demonstrate that CST resistance in Psa-ST3351 is associated with the addition of L-Ara4N to the lipid A. Furthermore, CST exposure induced a shift to a mucoid phenotype and other features consistent with a chronic infection phenotype, including reduced phagocytosis, increased bacterial competition, and enhanced bacterial virulence in a mouse pneumonia model. Together, our results suggest that the use of CST in iwCF colonized with undetected CST-resistant P. aeruginosa may exacerbate lung disease by selecting pathogenic mucoid variants, ultimately increasing morbidity and mortality.

Material and methods

Bacterial strains and culture conditions

Psa-ST3351 was isolated from a Mexican child with CF [16,17]. Previously designated CF053, this isolate was highly resistant to CST (MIC > 128 μg/mL). P. aeruginosa strain PAO1 was kindly donated by Dr. Maria del Rosario Morales-Espinoza, Facultad de Medicina, UNAM. From stocks stored at −70°C, bacteria were grown in 5 mL of Lysogeny Broth (LB) (Sigma-Aldrich) overnight at 37°C with shaking at 180 rpm. Psa-ST3351 was also grown on 5 mL of LB supplemented with a sublethal dose of 1 µg/mL CST (Sigma-Aldrich) [18].

MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry analysis

The lipid A from CST-exposed [Psa-ST3351(+CST)] and untreated Psa-ST3351 was analyzed by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry (MS) using a Bruker Autoflex MALDI-TOF spectrometer. Lipid A was isolated from log-phase bacteria grown overnight in 10 mL of LB. Bacterial cells were pelleted at 3,220 × g for 10 min at room temperature, resuspended in 1 mL of 0.1 M citric acid, and centrifuged at 14,500 × g for 1 min. The supernatants were discarded, and the pellets were washed twice with 1 mL of 0.1 M citric acid. The lipid A for mass spectrometry (MS) was isolated as described by Hamidi et al. [19]. MS spectra were recorded in reflectron mode and negative-ion polarity mode using an ABSCIEX TOF/TOF 5800 mass spectrometer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). The instrument was equipped with a neodymium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet laser (λ = 349 nm) featuring a 3-ns pulse width and a repetition rate of up to 1000 Hz. Delayed extraction technology was utilized to enhance resolution. Each spectrum was generated by accumulating 2,000 laser shots, ensuring high signal quality and reproducibility [20].

Determination of bacterial diameter and zeta potential using dynamic light scattering

For these studies, we used bacterial suspensions of Psa-ST3351 grown for 8 h in Müller-Hinton (MH) broth with or without 1 μg/mL CST. After centrifugation (5000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C), bacterial pellets were washed twice, suspended in 2 mL of sterile 1 mM NaCl solution, and adjusted to the turbidity of a 0.5 McFarland standard solution. An additional 1:4 dilution was performed in 1 mM NaCl for each bacterial sample, and particle size and zeta potential were determined in 2 mL aliquots. Mean diameter (nm) and zeta potential (millivolts) of bacterial cells were measured at 25°C with a ZetaPALS ZetaPotential Analyzer (Brookhaven Instruments Corporation, Holtsville, NY), which was equipped with a 677-nm laser and dynamic light-scattering (PCS) at 90°C for particle sizing. Zeta potential was determined from electrophoretic mobility using Smoluchowski’s equation, ζ = μη/ε, where μ is the electrophoretic mobility, η is the viscosity of the medium, and ε is the medium dielectric constant [21,22]. Each particle size value was obtained as the mean of 10 individual measurements ± the standard deviation. In contrast, each Zeta potential value was obtained as a mean of at least five individual measurements ± the standard deviation [23].

Pigment quantifications

Overnight cultures were diluted at an OD600 of 0.05 in 5 mL of LB medium with or without 1 µg/mL CST and incubated at 37ºC for 24 h with continuous shaking (180 rpm). The supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 5 min. Pyoverdine production by Psa-ST3351 and Psa-ST3351(+CST) was quantified in filtered supernatants using spectrophotometry at OD405 nm and normalized by the OD600 nm as reported [24]. Pyocyanin was extracted from cell-free supernatants using an acidic solution protocol [25] based on the pyocyanin uptake profile. Briefly, bacterial pre-cultures were washed and diluted to 0.05 OD600 nm in 125 mL flasks with 30 mL of LB medium and incubated at 37°C and 225 rpm for 24 h. The bacterial culture was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C. For each biological replicate, 5 mL of the supernatant was used to extract pyocyanin with 3 mL of chloroform and then acidified with 1 mL of 0.2 N HCl. Pyocyanin concentrations were calculated using a molar absorption coefficient of 17.072 mL mol−1 cm−1 and normalized to the bacterial growth at 600 nm [25].

Rhamnolipids production

The production of rhamnolipids was assessed using thin-layer chromatography (TLC) as previously reported [26]. Briefly, supernatants from Psa-ST3351 and Psa-ST3351(+CST) cultures were acidified to pH 2 with HCl and extracted twice with chloroform-methanol (2:1). After centrifugation, the organic phase was dried and dissolved in 50 μL methanol. TLC was performed using silica 60 F254 plates (Merck) with chloroform: methanol: acetic acid (65:15:2) as the mobile phase, and rhamnolipids were visualized using the orcinol reagent after heating at 140ºC for 2 min.

Biofilm formation

Biofilm production was assessed as previously described [27]. Briefly, bacterial overnight cultures of Psa-ST3351 or Psa-ST3351+CST were adjusted to 107 CFUs/mL, and triplicate samples of 500 μL were dispensed into polystyrene tubes (Falcon). Tubes inoculated with Psa-ST3351+CST contain LB broth plus 1 μg/mL CST. After 24 h of static incubation, the media was removed carefully; every tube was washed three times with 500 μL of sterile-deionized water. Adherent bacteria were stained with 500 μL of crystal violet 1% (w/v) (T.J. Baker®) for 5 min at room temperature. Biofilms were washed three times with 500 μL of sterile deionized water. To quantify bacteria-bound crystal violet, the cells were incubated with 1 mL of cold methanol (T.J. Baker®); the crystal violet released was quantified by measuring OD540 nm and normalized by the OD600 nm.

Swarming motility assay

Swarming motility was determined as previously described [27]. Briefly, Petri dishes were prepared with 15 mL of LB broth supplemented with nutrient agar 0.95% (w/v). Then, they were inoculated with 10 μL of a bacterial suspension of 107 CFUs/mL Psa-ST3351 or Psa-ST3351+CST. Petri dishes inoculated with Psa-ST3351+CST contain LB broth plus 1 μg/mL CST. Inoculated plates were incubated for 18 h at 37°C.

Western blots

Secreted ExoS in culture supernatants of Psa-ST3351 or Psa-ST3351(+CST) was detected by western blotting. Bacterial overnight pre-cultures with or without 1 μg/mL CST were diluted at an OD600 = 0.05 in 125 mL flasks with 15 mL of induction or non-induction medium and incubated at 37°C with shaking (225 rpm). Samples were collected and centrifuged at 4°C (14,000 rpm, 2 min); proteins in the supernatants were precipitated with trichloroacetic acid (TCA) 100% overnight at 4°C. After centrifugation at 4°C (14,000 rpm, 30 min), pellets were resuspended in SDS-PAGE loading buffer. Samples were denatured at 90°C for 5 min and loaded in 12% SDS-PAGE gels. Resolved proteins were transferred to 0.2 μm nitrocellulose membranes (Amersham) and blocked with 5% BSA in Tris-HCl buffer pH 7.4 supplemented with 0.1% Tween-20 detergent (TBS-T) at 4°C, for 2 h. To determine the molecular weight of immunodetected ExoS and GroEL, we used precision plus protein standards (Cat 161–0374) from BioRad®. The membranes were washed trice for 15 min with 15 mL of TBS-T. Subsequently, membranes were incubated with an anti-ExoS [26] (kindly donated by Dr. Bertha Gonzáles-Pedrajo, Instituto de Fisiología Celular, UNAM) at 4°C for 1 h. The membranes were washed trice, and then an anti-rabbit IgG conjugated to alkaline phosphatase (anti-IgG-AP) (Abcam) was added and incubated at 4°C for one h. Finally, the membranes were washed and developed using a 1-step NBT/BCIP solution (Thermo Scientific). GroEL was used as a loading control [28]. For GroEL detection, the membranes were washed with 15 mL of stripping buffer (25 mM glycine, 1% SDS, pH 2.0) at 4°C for 1 h with shaking and washing. The membranes were blocked and incubated for 12 h at 4°C with the anti-GroEL; then, the membranes were washed and incubated with an anti-IgG-AP. Bands were revealed using a 1-step NBT/BCIP solution.

Bacterial competition

The bacterial competition assay was performed as described [29], with some modifications. Briefly, Escherichia coli DH10B, Psa-ST3351, and Psa-ST3351(+CST) were grown in 5 ml LB broth. Bacterial cultures were adjusted to an OD600 of 1.0 and then mixed 1:1 and 2:1 (prey/predator). Serial dilutions were performed and plated in LB agar supplemented with 200 μg/mL of streptomycin. E. coli DH10B is intrinsically resistant to streptomycin; in contrast, Psa-ST3351 and Psa-ST3351(+CST) are susceptible. The recovered pray CFUs/input pray CFUs ratio was used to determine the bacterial competition activity.

Minimal inhibitory concentration assays

The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were determined according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute guidelines 2023 [30]. MICs for amikacin, gentamicin, tobramycin, levofloxacin, and tetracycline (Sigma-Aldrich) were determined using the broth microdilution method in 96-well plates with Muller-Hinton (MH) broth. Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922, a CST-sensitive reference strain, was used as quality control. Psa-ST3351, with or without CST treatment (1 µg/mL), was adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard in antibiotic-free MH broth. Antibiotic stock solutions (256 µg/mL) were serially diluted two-fold in MH broth to obtain final concentrations ranging from 0.125 to 128 µg/mL. For MIC determination of Psa-ST3351(+CST), all antibiotic dilutions contained CST (1 µg/mL). Bacterial suspensions were inoculated into wells with the antibiotic dilutions, and plates were incubated at 37°C for 18–20 h. MIC was defined as the lowest antibiotic concentration (in the presence or absence of CST) that inhibited visible bacterial growth.

Cells and infection conditions

Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) was obtained from Invitrogen. Three x105 THP-1 human monocytes (TIB-202; ATCC) were differentiated into macrophages with 200 nM of phorbol myristate acetate for 24 h in 24 well plates. On the day of infection, cells were washed twice with PBS and then incubated in a medium without antibiotics. THP-1 cells were infected with a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 50. To synchronize the infection, the plates were centrifuged at 1400 rpm for 1 min, incubated for 30 min, and washed thrice with PBS to remove the extracellular bacteria; infected cells were returned to the incubator for 30 min (to evaluate phagocytosis) or 24 h (to evaluate bacterial survival) in RPMI media supplemented with 100 μg/mL gentamicin. The survival index was calculated by quantifying CFUs at 30 min (bacterial phagocytosis) and 24 h post-infection (bacterial survival). The adherence was determined by incubating the cells on ice (4°C) for 30 min; then, the cells were washed, and the CFU was determined.

Mouse pneumonia model

All mice experiments were conducted according to the ARRIVE guidelines and the standard operating protocols approved by the Comité de Investigación y Ética from the Facultad de Medicina, of the Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México (UNAM). The use of animals was conducted under a protocol approved by the Comité Interno para el Cuidado y Uso de Animales de Laboratorio (CICUAL). Seven groups of five mice by cage (BALB/c) were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection [20 μL of ketamine (6.7 mg/mL) and xylazine (1.3 mg/mL)]. On day 0, mice were inoculated with either 20 μL of PBS (control group) or 10 μL of a bacterial suspension containing 1x106, 1x107, and 1x108 CFUs [Psa-ST3351 or Psa-ST3351(+CST)] per nostril. Animals were maintained under controlled temperature conditions to ensure their comfort and minimize stress, and circadian cycles were regulated through controlled light–dark periods. The humane endpoints to cull mice before the end of the six-day infection were as follows: significantly reduced or loss of activity, stooped position, bristly coat, short and high-frequency breathing pattern, lack of grooming, decreased alertness, reduced food intake, reduced water intake, weight loss (0, 10 and more than 15%), loss of mobility, presence of dirty orifices (ocular, nasal, and urinary secretions, diarrhea), and cyanotic extremities. Three scores were given for each of the signs above (0, normal; 1, mild; 2, accentuated). Values from 0 to 1 indicated that the mouse was healthy. Two–4 indicated a need for close monitoring every 6 hours. Five–8 indicated the need for analgesic treatment (meloxicam 1.5 mg/kg subcutaneously every 12 h). Animals reaching a score of 9 or above were immediately euthanized by exposure to CO2, followed by cervical dislocation.

Lungs from control and infected mice (24 h post-infection) were carefully removed and fixed in p-formaldehyde 10% for 24 h at room temperature. Tissue samples were blocked, embedded in paraffin, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and visualized with a Nikon Microphot-FXA microscope using a 60X objective. Each microphotograph was analyzed using ACT-1 Nikon software. The presence of neutrophils was digitally analyzed using Fiji ImageJ software. Fifteen images from three mice were analyzed.

Cytotoxicity assay

Supernatants from infected THP-1 cells were used to quantify the activity of the cytosolic enzyme lactate dehydrogenase (LDH; Promega, Madison, WI). The percentage of LDH activity was determined using the following formula: % of LDH release = (OD490-experimental LDH release OD490-spontaneous LDH release)/(OD490-maximal LDH release – OD490-spontaneous LDH release) ×100% [27].

Cytokine measurements

Supernatants of infected THP-1 cells were used to quantify the release of TNFα, and IL-1β (Invitrogen®), IL-6, IL-8, IL-13, IL-17A, and IL-22 (TONBO Biosciences) by ELISA following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

The statistical distribution of the data, derived from at least three independent biological replicates for each strain, was assessed using normality (Shapiro–Wilk’s test, p < 0.05) and homoscedasticity (Levene’s test, p < 0.05) analyses. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between groups were conducted using paired t-test or one-way ANOVA, with a confidence level of 95% (α = 0.05). Post-hoc analyses were performed using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (Tukey-HSD) test when significant differences were detected.

Results

Colistin resistance in Psa-ST3351 is mediated by adding 4-amino-4-deoxy-L-arabinose to lipid A

Resistance to CST in Gram-negative bacteria is commonly linked to chemical modifications of lipid A [15]. To investigate the high-level CST resistance of the Psa-ST3351 isolate, we first quantified the expression of arnB, pmrA, and cptA by RT-qPCR in Psa-ST3351 grown with or without CST. In Psa-ST3351(+CST), the transcription of arnB and pmrA increased nearly 500-fold and 12-fold, respectively (Supplementary Figures S1A and S1B), while cptA transcription increased two-fold (Supplementary Figure S1C). We analyzed the lipid A structures of Psa-ST3351 and Psa-ST3351(+CST) using MALDI-TOF MS. Ion peaks consistent with the addition of L-Ara4N were identified in the penta- and hexa-acylated lipid A forms of Psa-ST3351 (+CST) (Figures 1A,B). Ion peaks associated with the addition of phosphoethanolamine (pEtN) were not found. These results indicate that CST induces the incorporation of L-Ara4N into the Psa-ST3351 lipid A, consistent with its CST-resistant phenotype. Psa-ST3351 exposed to cst exhibits a mucoid phenotype

To evaluate the effect of CST on the phenotype of Psa-ST3351, we incubated both Psa-ST3351 and Psa-ST3351(+CST) on cetrimide agar plates. In contrast to Psa-ST3351, Psa-ST3351 (+CST) exhibited a mucoid phenotype and a marked loss of pigmentation (Figure 2(A)). Bacterial cells that produce a mucoid phenotype showed a two-fold increase in average size by dynamic light scattering (from 388.5 ± 10.0 nm to 898.1 ± 57.1 nm after 8 h of interaction with CST). This increase, also confirmed by flow cytometry (Supplementary Figure S2), suggests that CST induces capsule production or bacterial aggregation in Psa-ST3351. The zeta potential of Psa-ST3351 was −20.47 ± 1.34 mV, while Psa-ST3351(+CST) decreased to −27.44 ± 1.81 mV after 8 h of CST exposure, indicating an increased overall membrane negative charge of Psa-ST3351(+CST) cells. The adaptation of P. aeruginosa to chronic infection is associated with the downregulation of the secretion of exotoxins (ExoS, ExoT, ExoU, and ExoY) through the T3SS [31,32]. Whole-genome sequencing of Psa-ST3351 reveals that this clone carries the exoS and exoT genes [16]. ExoS was detected in the supernatants of both PAO1 and Psa-ST3351 under growth conditions that stimulate T3SS expression (Figure 3(A)). However, exposure to CST significantly reduced ExoS secretion into the bacterial supernatant (Figure 3(A)), suggesting that CST inhibits ExoS release via the T3SS. Furthermore, Psa-ST3351 exposed to CST enhanced their bacterial competitive ability (Figure 3(B)), possibly by an increased activity of the T6SS. Together, these results suggest that CST induces a phenotype in Psa-ST3351 consistent with P. aeruginosa’s adaptation to chronic infection [8,9,33]. Psa-ST3351(+CST) impairs phagocytosis by macrophages

Since the mucoid phenotype of Psa-ST3351(+CST) may help evade macrophage phagocytosis, we examined the adhesion of Psa-ST3351(+CST) to macrophages. The results showed an approximately 80% reduction in adhesion of Psa-ST3351(+CST) compared to Psa-ST3351 (Figure 4(A)). Similar levels of reduced adhesion were observed with HeLa and A549 epithelial cells (Supplementary Figures S5A and S5B). The decreased adhesion of Psa-ST3351 (+CST) to macrophages correlated with poor internalization, not only by THP1 human macrophages (Figure 4(B)) but also by RAW264.7 and IC21 murine macrophages (Supplementary Figure S6A and S6B). Furthermore, over 95% intracellular Psa-ST3351 or Psa-ST3351 (+CST) were killed without affecting the macrophage viability (Figure 4(C and D)). CST exposure does not alter Psa-ST3351 pro-inflammatory properties

To investigate whether phagocytized Psa-ST3351(+CST) can induce pro-inflammatory responses, we analyzed macrophage culture supernatants for cytokine release at 24 h post-infection. Pro-inflammatory cytokines TNFα, IL-8, IL-1β, and IL-17A were detected in supernatants of both in Psa-ST3351- and Psa-ST3351 (+CST)-infected macrophages (Figures 5(A-D)), while IL-13, and IL-22 were absent (Figure 5(E and F)). Despite small variations in some cytokines (TNFα and IL-1β), there were no significant differences between the two bacterial strains. However, a slight reduction of IL-17A and an increased release of IL-8 were detected. We also performed similar experiments by treating macrophages with purified LPS from Psa-ST3351 and Psa-ST3351(+CST) at 100 ng/mL for 24 hours. No cytotoxicity was observed at these LPS concentrations (Supplementary Figure 7(A)), but both LPS preparations induced higher secretion of TNFα, IL-8, IL-1β, and IL-17A (Supplementary Figure 7B-E). However, an increased production of IL-1β was shown (Supplementary Figure 7C). These results indicate that the presence or absence of the L-Ara4N modification in the LPS does not significantly affect inflammatory responses in macrophages. Psa-ST3351(+CST) is more pathogenic in a murine pneumonia model.

The phenotypic changes observed in vitro suggest increased virulence of Psa-ST3351(+CST). We used an intranasal mouse pneumonia model [34] by infecting mice with either Psa-ST3351 or Psa-ST3351(+CST). Infected mice were monitored daily for up to 6 days. Animals reaching the humane endpoint were euthanized as described in Materials and Methods. Mice infected with 106 CFUs of Psa-ST3351 or Psa-ST3351 (+CST) did not display any signs of illness up to 6 days post-infection (p.i) (Figures 6(A and B)). At a dose of 107 CFU, 60% infected mice with Psa-ST3351 reached the humane endpoint at 48 h p.i. (Figure 6(A)), while all infected mice with Psa-ST3351 (+CST) had to be sacrificed at this time (Figure 6(B)), while at a dose of 108 CFU, all mice infected with either Psa-ST3351 or Psa-ST3351 (+CST) had to be culled at 24 h (Figure 6(A,B)). Histological analysis of lung sections from animals infected with 107 CFUs at 24 hours post-infection was performed to assess neutrophil infiltration in the peribronchial regions (Figure 6(C)). Quantifying immune cells in lung sections of mice infected with Psa-ST3351(+CST) revealed a higher density of neutrophil infiltration than similar lung sections of mice infected with Psa-ST3351 (Figure 6(C)). In summary, the infection results suggest that Psa-ST3351(+CST) increases the neutrophil infiltration into the mice lungs, suggesting that the phenotype changes induced by exposure to CST enhance pathogenicity. Discussion

The remarkable adaptability of P. aeruginosa drives its success as an opportunistic pathogen, frequently colonizing the lower airways of iwCF [35]. An initial acute infection typically prompts the bacterium to alter the expression of virulence factors. This helps evade host immune defenses and facilitates progression to a chronic infection state [9,36–38]. During chronic infection, P. aeruginosa shows increased biofilm production and resistance to antimicrobials. The bacteria also exhibit reduced acute-phase virulence traits, including T3SS expression, protease secretion via T2SS, flagellar motility, and pyoverdine production. Switch to the chronic state involves the appearance of mucoid variants arising from alginate overexpression, enhanced efflux pump activity, and lipid A modifications [8]. Over time, high-level multidrug-resistant strains emerge. This led to reliance on last-resort antibiotics such as CST. Recently, CST-resistant strains have become more prevalent [39]. In this study, we examine a CST-resistant strain (Psa-ST3351). We show that CST exposure induces a transition from a non-mucoid to a mucoid phenotype. This is accompanied by characteristics associated with chronic infection.

CST resistance in P. aeruginosa involves mutations in two-component regulatory systems, such as PmrA/PmrB [40] and PhoP/PhoQ [41]. Psa-ST3351 has several of these mutations [16], but the specific role of these mutations in CST resistance is unknown. We found that resistance does not depend on constant lipid A modification. Instead, CST exposure greatly increases (~500-fold) expression of the arnBCADTEFpmrE operon. This results in the addition of L-Ara4N to lipid A, matching previous studies. Antimicrobial peptides, such as CST, can activate the PmrAB system, even in the presence of PmrAB mutations [42,43]. These lipid A changes (by L-Ara4N and palmitoylation modifications) promote CST resistance. They also affect how the host immune system detects P. aeruginosa [44,45]. L-Ara4N and palmitate on penta-acylated and hexa-acylated lipid A boost Toll-like receptor 4/MD2-mediated responses [46,47]. Consistent with this, we show that macrophages exposed to Psa-ST3351 or Psa-ST3351(+CST) LPS release TNFα, IL-1β, IL-8, and IL-17A. Both Psa-ST3351 or Psa-ST3351(+CST) have penta- and hexa-acylated lipid A that contains L-Ara4N but lacks palmitate.

The adaptive changes that occur during the transition from acute to chronic infections are central to P. aeruginosa persistence. Acute infections rely on virulence factors, including pyoverdine, flagellar motility, and T3SS activation. These enable the invasion and dissemination of pathogens [38]. In contrast, in a chronic infection, these factors are suppressed, and persistence traits are upregulated, including exopolysaccharide production (alginate, Pel, and Psl), biofilm formation, and T6SS activation [33]. Generally, non-mucoid colonies correlate with acute phenotypes, while mucoid colonies, driven by alginate overproduction, are characteristic of chronic infection [48–51]. Our data align with these patterns. CST exposure induces a mucoid phenotype characterized by increased alginate production and a more negatively charged outer membrane. These variants resist immune clearance due to inhibited phagocytosis [36,52–54]. With CST treatment, Psa-ST3351 exhibited increased production of pyocyanin and swarming motility. There was a decrease in pyoverdine production, a slight reduction in biofilm formation, suppressed T3SS activity, and activation of the T6SS. Thus, CST appears to act as both an antibiotic and an environmental cue. This supports pathogen persistence and virulence.

Changes in biofilm formation and motility following CST exposure highlight regulatory shifts in colonization strategies. Notably, there is a slight reduction in biofilm formation and an increase in motility. This implies a reallocation of resources toward bacterial dissemination. This process is often reciprocally regulated by c-di-GMP levels, whereby low levels enhance motility and high levels promote biofilm formation [55–57]. Recent studies suggest that motile and biofilm states may represent sequential rather than exclusive stages of colonization [58–62]. In the context of our findings, CST exposure may prompt a transition toward a motile, pre-biofilm phenotype that facilitates long-term colonization [63,64].

Pyocyanin is linked to severe CF disease [65–67]. Higher pyocyanin production in Psa-ST3351(+CST) could be due to Rhl and PqsE activation, which controls phz1 and phz2 expression [68–72]. CST-resistant variants also show increased pyocyanin production through QS [73–76]. Lower pyoverdine production by Psa-ST3351(+CST) may result from altered AlgZR signaling. As Little et al., found, unphosphorylated AlgR raises prrf2 expression, a miRNA that suppresses pvdS. This gene is needed for pyoverdine synthesis and T3SS activation [77,78]. Our data support this finding. Psa-ST3351(+CST) fully suppresses ExoS release and reduces pyoverdine production, suggesting repression of the pvdS gene. This regulation likely induces GacS/GacA, which activate rsmY/Z RNAs. These RNAs bind RsmA, a mucoid repressor and acute trait activator [79–82]. Phosphorylated AlgR also repress rsmA expression. This further enforces the chronic state [83,84].

The observed increase in T6SS activity in the CST-treated Psa-ST3351 aligns with the mutually exclusive T3SS and T6SS systems. These systems are regulated in opposite directions by GacS/GacA [33,81,85,86]. These findings confirm a CST-driven move toward mucoid, persistent phenotypes characterized by intra-species competition over invasion.

The clinical implications of CST-induced phenotypic modulation are significant, particularly for the management of patients with CF. Our results suggest that prolonged use of CST may drive the emergence of mucoid, highly pathogenic P. aeruginosa variants. This potentially worsens lung injury and complicates treatment. Importantly, such a phenotypic shift may not be detected by standard sputum culture or antimicrobial susceptibility testing. This increases diagnostic challenges in clinical practice. These findings underscore the need to reassess CST use as a long-term treatment for chronic infections. They also suggest the potential risk of selecting for more virulent strains, highlighting the importance of conducting further clinical research.
