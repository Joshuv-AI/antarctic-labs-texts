Hantaviruses and rodent-borne diseases: Distinct ecologies at the human–environment interface
Authors: Cyrille Gourjault, Bernard Davoust, Renaud Piarroux, Loïc Epelboin, Nadim Cassir
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Rodents have shaped the history of infectious diseases more profoundly than almost any other mammalian reservoir, perhaps second only to bats. However, “rodents” should not be considered as a homogeneous group. A major ecological distinction exists between invasive synanthropic rodents, such as Rattus rattus and Rattus norvegicus, which thrive in degraded urban environments, and wild native rodents that constitute part of natural ecosystems and biodiversity [1].

Synanthropic rodents are closely associated with zoonoses linked to rapid urbanisation, informal settlements, poor waste management, and inadequate sanitation. These invasive rats are reservoirs or amplifying hosts for Yersinia pestis, Leptospira spp., Rickettsia typhi, rat hepatitis E virus, rat encephalomyocarditis virus, Coxiella burnetii, and Angiostrongylus cantonensis [1]. Although plague is often perceived as a relic of the past, Yersinia pestis continues to circulate in endemic foci, particularly in Madagascar, where invasive rodents and their flea vectors perpetuate transmission cycles at the human–animal interface [2]. It may also facilitate regional dissemination through maritime trade, cargo transport, and increasing human mobility between Madagascar, neighbouring Indian Ocean islands, and East African coastal regions [2].

In contrast, hantaviruses illustrate a fundamentally different ecological paradigm. Andes virus (ANDV) remains the only hantavirus for which human-to-human transmission has been convincingly demonstrated [3]. Its principal reservoir, Oligoryzomys longicaudatus, is a native rodent widely distributed in southern Chile and Argentina [4]. The recent alert regarding a cluster of ANDV infections aboard a cruise ship in the South Atlantic, involving multiple deaths and secondary transmission, highlights the continuing epidemic potential of hantaviruses in a highly interconnected world [5]. The presumed source of infection for the two index cases, was environmental exposure to aerosolised excreta from wild rodents carrying ANDV, possibly during birdwatching activities near Ushuaia before embarkation. Local health authorities noted that O. longicaudatus is not usually described in the Ushuaia area, suggesting either earlier exposure during travel, or a possible ecological expansion of the reservoir range. Other pathogens associated with wild rodent reservoirs include the lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus, Lassa virus and other arenaviruses in Africa, orthopoxvirus, and Francisella tularensis [1]. Wild rodents are integral components of local ecosystems. Human disease therefore frequently reflects increasing intrusion into natural habitats through deforestation, agricultural expansion, ecotourism, or environmental disruption.

Consequently, rodent-borne diseases reflect two complementary aspects of planetary health: failures in sanitation and urban infrastructure on one hand, and increasing human encroachment into natural ecosystems on the other, further amplified by the growing popularity of pet rodents. In the twenty-first century, mitigating the impact of rodent-borne epidemics will depend not only on ecological prevention strategies, but also on rapid detection, early outbreak recognition, and coordinated public health responses.
