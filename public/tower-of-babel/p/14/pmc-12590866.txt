Coping styles, resilience, and mental health symptoms in first-year medical students: a structural equation modeling approach in a cross-sectional study
Authors: Shuang Xu, Qianningzi Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study examines the associations among coping styles, resilience and mental health symptoms among Chinese first-year medical students. It highlights the significance of understanding coping mechanisms and resilience in relation to the unique academic stressors medical students face, particularly during their first year.MethodsThe study involved 2,000 first-year medical students aged 16 to 22 from medical universities and colleges in China. Participants completed validated questionnaires, including the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (for resilience), the Simple Coping Styles Questionnaire (for coping styles), and the Symptom Checklist-90 (for mental health symptoms). Regression analysis was conducted to identify associations with mental health symptoms, and structural equation modeling was used to explore the mediating effects of coping styles on the relationship between resilience and mental health symptoms.ResultsBoth resilience and coping styles were significantly associated with mental health symptoms. Structural equation modeling indicated that positive and negative coping styles partially mediated the relationship between resilience and mental health symptoms.ConclusionsThis study suggests that resilience is related to mental health through its association with coping styles among first-year medical students. The findings may inform the design of tailored psychological interventions to support this population’s mental well-being.

Background

First-year medical students are tasked with mastering complex subjects and developing critical clinical skills immediately after passing their university entrance exams, extending throughout their medical education. Compared to their peers in other fields of study, they show consistently greater levels of mental distress, which may result from the rigorous demands of medical education, a vast courseload, and the high expectations placed on medical occupations [1]. Existing research [2] has indicated that medical students experience significant mental health challenges, with reported rates of 39.4% for depression, 47.1% for anxiety, and 11.1% for experiencing suicidal thoughts [3]. Chronic and severe psychological symptoms significantly increase the risk of mental disorders [4].Given that these symptoms are closely related to the intense and multifaceted stressors inherent in medical training, it is crucial to contextualize the discussion within the broader theoretical frameworks of stress research. The classic research on stress by Hans Selye emphasized the body’s general physiological response to demands [5], while contemporary stress research, such as Lazarus and Folkman’s transactional model [6] conceptualizes stress as a dynamic process involving primary and secondary appraisal and subsequent coping responses, rather than a unitary state of distress. This perspective highlights distinctions between acute and chronic stress and underscores that outcomes depend on how individuals appraise demands and resources and on the strategies they enact. This view underscores that stress arises from the dynamic interaction between external stressors and individual appraisal, and that coping involves not only internal regulation but also, at times, efforts to modify stress-inducing conditions. We therefore frame resilience and coping within an appraisal-based, process-oriented model, in which the first year of medical education—the transitional phase from high school to university—represents a particularly critical period for adapting to the rigorous demands of future academic studies. This perspective highlights the importance of providing tailored psychological interventions and support at this stage.

As defined by Luthar et al. [7], resilience is an active process that includes constructive adjustments in the face of severe adversity. It is an indicator of stress-coping capacity in the face of challenging situations and is utilized as a focus in addressing mental health issues [8]. Studies have shown a substantial correlation between resilience and mental health symptoms among university students, especially those in medical programs, highlighting its role in buffering stress, promoting mental health, and enhancing academic success and quality of life [8–11]. Resilience also predicts psychiatric symptoms and protects against stressful life events [12]. Medical students often encounter a unique set of stressors, including rigorous and intensive schedules, prolonged lecture hours, extensive laboratory work, and demanding study sessions. Those with higher levels of resilience are not only able to effectively deal with academic training challenges, avoid stress and burnout, but also tend to appraise stressful situations more adaptively and implement personally preferred coping strategies more effectively, resulting in better mental health outcomes, and lower susceptibility to various negative psychological symptoms [13–15]. However, compared to their peers, medical students often exhibit lower resilience [16]. Therefore, research promoting resilience represents an advantageous strategy to alleviate the adverse consequences of stressors and support students through challenging experiences.

The cognitive and behavioral strategies applied to handle both internal and external demands in stressful situations are termed “coping styles” [17, 18] and are generally classified as positive and negative. Positive coping styles often involve active problem-solving, seeking social support, and effective emotion regulation [17]. On the other hand, negative coping styles include avoidance, social withdrawal, and negative emotional expression [19]. Studies have demonstrated the significant impact of coping styles on mental health; positive coping serves as a protective factor, shielding individuals from the negative impacts of adversities, promoting greater mental health, and reducing stress and mental distress [19, 20]. The detrimental effects of negative coping styles have also been identified [21]. Resilience and coping styles are interrelated yet distinct constructs, each significantly affecting mental health. Research demonstrates that stronger resilience correlates with the adoption of positive coping styles [22], and engaging in support-seeking behaviors allows these individuals to address stressors directly and mitigate their impact [23]. Increased psychological distress and diminished resilience are linked to negative coping styles [24]. Zhao et al. [19] examined how coping styles influenced resilience and depression among medical students amid the COVID-19 pandemic. These studies imply that while resilience directly affects mental health, coping styles also play a crucial role. Despite methodological differences, these findings underscore the crucial roles of resilience and coping in mental wellness. At the same time, it is important to recognize that coping strategies are developed within specific educational and social contexts, and that their effectiveness can be determined by institutional demands and responses.

While the existing body of work has highlighted the significant effects of resilience and coping styles on mental health symptoms across various populations, few studies concentrate specifically on first-year medical students. The intricate interplay between these two factors underscores the challenges involved in improving the mental well-being of these students. Given the demanding nature of medical training, it is essential to gain deeper insights into the interplay between resilience and coping styles and the mental health symptoms experienced by these students. This understanding is essential for the development of effective promotion strategies tailored to their needs and to address a substantial gap in current academic literature.

This study sought to explore two primary hypotheses related to the mental health of first-year medical students:

Methods

Participants

A total of 2,000 first-year medical students from a large medical university in Guizhou Province, China, volunteered for this research study and completed the questionnaire. Of these, 92 responses were not returned or were excluded as incomplete, leaving 1,908 valid participants (1,331 (69.8%) female, 577 (30.2%) male). Their average age was 18.75 years (standard deviation 1.06, range 16–22 years).

Measures

Symptom checklist-90 symptom

Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90) is a widely used self-reporting instrument designed to assess psychological symptoms in adolescents and adults [25] and is a valuable tool for evaluating mental health in both clinical and research settings. Developed by Derogatis in the mid-1970s, it consists of 90 items that measure nine distinct symptom dimensions: somatization (SOM), obsessive-compulsiveness (OC), interpersonal sensitivity (IS), depression (DEP), anxiety (ANX), hostility (HOS), phobic-anxiety (PANX), paranoid ideation (PI), psychoticism (PSY) and others [25]. This scale is widely used by researchers worldwide [26–28] and has become the most commonly used tool for assessing the mental health status of Chinese university students in China [29–31]. However, despite its widespread adoption, there has been a notable lack of research focusing specifically on the mental health status of first-year medical students.

Connor-davidson resilience scale

The Connor-Davidson resilience scale is a widely used self-reporting instrument for measuring resilience in various populations [32–34]. Participants evaluate how often they experience each of the ten items in the previous month using a five-point scale, where 0 represents “not true at all” and 4 indicated “true nearly all the time.” A higher score indicates greater resilience in an individual. This scale enables effective resilience measurement and typically exhibits good psychometric qualities [35].

Simple coping style questionnaire

The Chinese version of the Simplified Coping Style Questionnaire (SCSQ) is widely used to assess coping strategies in the Chinese population. The assessment examines positive coping styles (12 items) and negative coping styles (8 items). Each item is rated using a four-point Likert scale. The SCSQ has proven to be a valuable tool for assessing coping in the Chinese context [31, 36].

Procedure

A total of 2,000 first-year medical students from a large medical university voluntarily participated in this research study. The participants were recruited using random sampling. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (approval number: KL2024-004). Prior to participation, all individuals were informed about the (1) research purpose, (2) anonymity of responses, and (3) voluntary participation and the right to withdraw. Informed consent was obtained before their inclusion in the study. This study adhered to the National Health Commission of China’s ethical guidelines (2023) and the Declaration of Helsinki. All self-reporting questionnaires were completed in a classroom. Of the 2,000 distributed questionnaires, 1,908 were valid, yielding an effective response rate of 97.3%.

Data analysis

In this research, the statistical analysis employed two software packages: SPSS version 22.0 and AMOS version 24.0, both developed by IBM Corporation. The tools were used to conduct a correlation analysis aimed at identifying the relationships between the main variables. A hierarchical linear regression analysis was employed to assess the mediating effect of coping styles on the relationship between resilience and mental symptoms. This methodological approach allowed for a thorough investigation into how much variance in mental symptoms could be explained by incorporating coping styles into the model of resilience. Mental health symptoms served as dependent factors, while resilience and coping techniques functioned as independent variables. The variables were put into models following a sequential procedure: Step 1 involved resilience, and Step 2 concerned coping styles. According to Baron and Kenny [37], the following criteria must be satisfied to establish mediating effects: (1) the independent variable (resilience) must exhibit a significant relationship with the dependent variable (mental health symptoms) and the mediator (coping styles); (2) the mediator (coping styles) must demonstrate a significant relationship with the dependent variable (mental symptoms); and (3) when the mediator (coping styles) is incorporated into the analysis model, the effect of the independent variable (resilience) on the dependent variable (mental symptoms) is either significantly diminished or completely eliminated.

The mediation effects were assessed using the two-step approach suggested by Anderson and Gerbing [38]. Each latent variable represented by the observed indicators was evaluated by applying the measurement model. To enhance the psychometric properties of the indicators, we adopted an item-parceling approach based on content homogeneity, whereby items from the same theoretically and empirically supported subdomain were aggregated into parcels. This approach has been shown to yield more reliable and normally distributed indicators for latent variables and improve overall model fit [39]. In present study, it was used to divide resilience, positive style, and negative style into three parcels. This approach has been widely adopted in prior studies involving structural equation modeling (SEM), as evidenced by the work of Kong et al. [34] and Zhao et al. [40]. Once the measurement model was deemed acceptable, maximum likelihood estimation was applied to evaluate the structural model. The significance of understanding the mediating roles of both positive and negative coping styles was tested by a bootstrap estimation approach. According to MacKinnon et al. [41], this approach is advantageous because it generates more accurate confidence intervals for indirect effects, allowing for more reliable interpretation. To assess the fit of the proposed model, various statistical measures were employed, including chi-square statistics and fit indices: RMSEA < 0.08, SRMR < 0.08, CFI >0.90, NFI >0.90, and GFI >0.90. Furthermore, 5,000 bootstrap samples were extracted from the entire dataset. This process enabled a more robust examination of the mediation effect. To determine the significance of this effect, a 95% confidence interval was utilized, which is a widely accepted statistical method for evaluating the reliability and precision of estimated effects in research.

Results

Correlations for all variables

Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics (means and standard deviations) and Pearson correlation coefficients for the main study variables: resilience, positive coping styles, negative coping styles, and SCL_90. As shown in the table, all variables were significantly correlated. Specifically, the SCL_90 scores were negatively correlated with resilience (r = −.34, p <.01) and positive coping styles (r = −.23, p <.01), and positively correlated with negative coping styles (r =.37, p <.01). Additionally, resilience was positively associated with positive coping styles (r =.54, p <.01), and negatively correlated with negative coping styles (r = −.11, p <.01). A statistically significant but negligible positive correlation was also observed between positive and negative coping styles (r =.06, p <.05).

Linear regression models

Table 2 summarizes the results of two linear regression models examining associations with psychological symptoms (SCL-90 scores). In Model 1, resilience alone accounts for 11% of the variance in SCL-90 scores (R2 = 0.11, F = 240.84, p <.001). In Model 2, the inclusion of positive and negative coping styles increased the explained variance to 23% (R² = 0.23, F = 193.36, p <.001). In the final model, all three predictors (resilience, positive and negative coping styles) were significant.

Direct effect

The direct effect of resilience on SCL-90 was β = −0.37 (p <.001) when coping styles were not included into the model, indicating a significant negative association between resilience and SCL-90. The model fit indices were as follows: χ² = 691.23, df = 64, p <.001; RMSEA = 0.072; SRMR = 0.033; CFI = 0.97; GFI = 0.94; AGFI = 0.92; NFI = 0.95.

Measurement model

The measuring model included 19 observable variables and four latent components: SCL-90, resilience, positive coping styles, and negative coping styles. An initial test of the measurement model demonstrated a satisfactory fit for the data, with the following results: χ² = 1068.87, df = 146, p <.001; RMSEA = 0.058; SRMR = 0.043; GFI = 0.94; CFI = 0.96; AGFI = 0.92; NFI = 0.95. Each latent factor was adequately represented by its corresponding indicators, demonstrated by the significance of all factor loadings for the latent variables (p <.001).

Structural model

The structural model (Fig. 1) with two mediators—positive and negative coping styles—and a direct path from resilience to SCL_90 fit the data well: χ2 = 1098.41, df = 147, p <.001; RMSEA = 0.058; SRMR = 0.047; CFI = 0.96; NFI = 0.95; GFI = 0.94, AGFI = 0.92; NFI = 0.95. Bootstrapping results confirmed that both coping styles significantly mediated the association between resilience and SCL_90: positive coping styles (95% CI = [0.62, 0.71]) and negative styles (−95% CI = [−0.23, −0.11]). The path coefficient from resilience to SCL-90 was significant (β = − 0.21, SE = 0.18, P <.001). These findings supported the hypotheses that coping styles may function as mediating variables in the association between resilience and mental symptoms among first-year medical students.

Discussion

This study sought to elucidate the mediating role of coping styles in the relationship between resilience and mental health symptoms as measured by the SCL-90 among a large sample of first-year medical students. These results support the hypothesis that coping styles and resilience can significantly predict mental symptoms in these students. Furthermore, the link between resilience and mental symptoms was mediated by both positive and negative coping styles. This study indicates that coping styles can serve as crucial mediating mechanisms for enhancing or undermining the protective effects of resilience.

Resilience exhibited a notable negative correlation with SCL-90 scores, suggesting that stronger resilience correlates with lower SCL-90 scores. This aligns with other prior research indicating that resilience serves a critical role as a protective factor against psychological distress [8, 9]. Further, these findings suggest that individuals who exhibit higher levels of resilience are better equipped to manage stress and adversity, ultimately reducing their risk of suffering mental health problems. Moreover, positive coping styles positively correlated with resilience and negatively correlated with SCL-90 scores. This indicated that students with higher resilience may employ more positive coping strategies, such as problem-solving. Therefore, they tended to experience fewer psychological symptoms, which corroborates the literature suggesting that such coping strategies are beneficial to mental health [23, 42]. Conversely, a negative coping styles presented a positive connection with the SCL-90 scores, suggesting that maladaptive coping strategies are related to higher degrees of psychological distress. This result is consistent with the findings of Zhao et al. [19] and Dai et al. [31]. A small but statistically significant positive association between positive and negative coping styles (r =.06) suggests that, while conceptually distinct, participants may adopt multiple strategies simultaneously when facing stress, depending on situational demands. For example, the SCSQ item “comforting oneself” is classified as a negative coping strategy, but depending on the stressor and appraisal stage, it can provide short-term emotional regulation and facilitate engagement in more adaptive coping. This overlap may also reflect the measurement properties of the SCSQ, which captures general coping tendencies rather than context-specific responses. These findings highlight the complexity of coping behavior and the need to interpret coping styles in light of situational and appraisal factors.

The findings of the regression analysis (Table 2) also made it clear that the mental health status of first-year medical students was significantly associated with coping styles and resilience. In Model 1, resilience contributed 11% of the variance in the SCL-90 scores, showing a significant negative association with psychological distress. When coping styles were added to Model 2, the explained variance increased to 23%, suggesting that both positive and negative coping styles contributed independently to psychological outcomes. More specifically, a decrease in SCL-90 scores was linked to positive coping styles, whereas an increase was linked to negative coping styles. These results emphasize the importance of cultivating resilience and positive coping strategies among medical students.

Results from SEM indicated that positive and negative coping styles both mediated the link between resilience and mental symptoms, measured by SCL-90 scores among first-year Chinese medical students. The path coefficients from resilience to positive and negative coping styles were both significant, illustrating that those who exhibited greater resilience used more positive coping strategies and fewer negative ones. The indirect effects of resilience on mental symptoms through positive and negative coping styles were significant. These findings underscore the dual mediating roles of coping styles, suggesting that resilience was associated with fewer mental health symptoms and resilience may influence mental health symptoms through its association with coping styles. Research indicates that individuals adopting positive coping strategies generally report superior mental health outcomes compared to those utilizing negative coping methods [17, 24]. Our findings align with and expand upon this theory, underscoring the mediating role of adaptive coping strategies in the resilience-mental health status relationship. Medical students with higher psychological resilience at the academic outset are more likely to have a clearer understanding of the effectiveness and value of positive coping strategies when confronted with stressful circumstances. Such appraisal-based recognition may facilitate greater use of adaptive coping, which, in turn, was related with fewer mental health symptoms [20]. Conversely, the positive association between maladaptive coping styles and SCL-90 scores suggests that strategies like self-blame and behavioral disengagement tended to co-occur with higher mental symptom reports, consistent with previous findings [43]. This dual-pathway model, which includes both positive and negative coping styles, offers a comprehensive understanding of how resilience influences mental health through different coping mechanisms. The mediating effect of the coping styles elucidates the multifaceted nature of resilience and its impact on mental health. This research supports the transactional model of stress and coping proposed by Folkman et al. [6, 44], which asserts that coping strategies play an important role in the stress response and subsequent health outcomes. Building on Hans Selye’s early conceptualization of stress as a general physiological response, contemporary stress and coping theory [6] claims that stress is not merely a stimulus or response but a transactional process involving an individual’s appraisal of a situation and their ability to cope with it. After primary appraisal, evaluation of the situation and secondary appraisal assess the available resources to manage the stressor. Coping strategies, either problem- or emotion-focused, are employed based on these appraisals. Accordingly, first-year medical students with high psychological resilience are more adept at cognitive appraisal, which enables them to select more effective coping strategies in response to stress and adversity. However, it should also be recognized that the effectiveness of these strategies is shaped by the broader educational environment, as institutional demands and responses can either support or hinder the successful implementation of coping efforts. In addition, coping may also extend beyond internal regulation to efforts aimed at modifying stress-inducing conditions, consistent with contemporary stress research.

Understanding the potential mediating role of coping styles has significant practical implications for mental health interventions among medical students. Given the substantial pressure and challenges medical students face, targeted interventions that address resilience and coping styles are essential to improve their mental well-being. Interventions aimed at enhancing resilience should focus on promoting positive and reducing negative coping strategies. Resilience-building alone may be insufficient without opportunities to practice effective coping in real academic contexts; it is equally important to equip students with effective coping strategies that focus on building problem-solving skills and reducing avoidant coping to handle the multifaceted stressors they face. By integrating resilience-building activities and coping strategy training into the medical curriculum, educators may equip students with the resources to manage the demands of medical school while prioritizing their mental well-being. This holistic approach may not only benefit students’ well-being but also help their academic performance and professional development.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences; future longitudinal studies are needed to explore the dynamics among resilience, coping styles, and psychological distress. Second, the reliance on self-reported measures may have introduced response bias. Third, the sample was limited to first-year medical students from a single institution, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other student populations or educational contexts. Fourth, the SCSQ captures coping behaviors but does not assess primary or secondary stress appraisal, nor the subjective perception of stress, which limits the extent to which coping can be understood within the broader stress process. Future research should consider adopting a multicenter or cross-regional design and integrating appraisal-focused instruments to enhance external validity and provide a more comprehensive understanding of stress, coping, and resilience.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides valuable insights into the role of resilience and coping styles in relation to psychological distress among first-year medical students. The findings indicate that both resilience and coping styles are significantly associated with mental health symptoms, and that coping styles mediate the relationship between resilience and mental health symptoms. This study contributes to both theoretical understanding and practical application by highlighting the dual pathways through which resilience may influence mental health symptoms through its association with coping styles. Moreover, our findings suggest that fostering resilience and promoting positive coping strategies may be helpful for supporting mental health. However, causal interpretations are not warranted given the cross-sectional design, and longitudinal or experimental research is needed to evaluate efficacy. Future studies should also integrate measures of stress appraisal with coping assessments to provide a more comprehensive understanding of resilience and coping processes. Such an integrated approach could offer valuable insights into students’ stress experiences and mindsets, thereby informing curriculum development and institutional support strategies aimed at promoting mental health in high-stress academic environments.
