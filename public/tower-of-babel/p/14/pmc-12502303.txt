Navigating turbulence: analyzing the resilience of Lebanon’s healthcare system in a multi-crisis scenario
Authors: Rouham Yamout, Joanna Khalil, Joanna Raven, Fouad M. Fouad, Wesam Mansour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionLebanon’s healthcare system, historically reliant on privatization and public–private partnerships, faces unprecedented challenges owing to a compounded crisis environment. Since 2019, economic collapse, the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic and the Beirut port explosion have placed immense strain on an already fragile system, highlighting the necessity to understand its resilience in sustaining service provision. This study aims to examine the capacities of the local health system to absorb, adapt and potentially transform in response to ongoing crises, in Majdal Anjar, a municipality located in the Zahle District.MethodsThis qualitative study employed key informant interviews (KIIs) with nine local healthcare stakeholders, including healthcare providers, officials from the Ministry of Public Health (MoPH) and representatives from the United Nations and non-state actors, and was conducted from May to June 2022. The interviews focused on stakeholders’ insights into the actions taken to address challenges triggered by the crisis. Data were coded according to the Health System Resilience Framework, developed by the Rebuild Consortium, and analyzed through a deductive approach to classify identified responses according to the resilience capacities of absorption, adaptation and transformation.ResultsWithin the adopted resilience framework, the capacities identified in the responses appear as follows: Absorption: International organizations and non-state actors provided crucial financial and operational support, compensating for the government’s disengagement from funding and regulation. While this support sustained essential services, it had also introduced inequities and coordination challenges, as aid priorities sometimes diverge from local needs. Adaptation: The MoPH and municipal entities have implemented policy adaptations, including tolerating informal healthcare providers and allowing contracting of health institutions with humanitarian actors directly, while healthcare facilities innovated to manage medication shortages. The MoPH sought to bolster local pharmaceutical production and reform reimbursement policies. Transformation: Local stakeholders, particularly municipalities, demonstrated capacity to manage healthcare services independently during COVID-19 crisis cells, but this autonomy remained underutilized.ConclusionsLebanon’s healthcare system has demonstrated resilience through absorptive and adaptive responses amidst multiple crises. However, these strategies have been insufficient to ensure equitable and sustainable access. Reliance on humanitarian funds and centralized, profit-driven governance has deepened structural weaknesses, hindering long-term resilience. Achieving genuine resilience requires governance reforms, strategic resource allocation, decentralization and a shift towards a needs-based health approach. Future strategies should prioritize equity, ensuring all populations access quality healthcare regardless of financial or geographic barriers.

Introduction

The rising frequency of new diseases and pandemics, along with global economic crisis, climate change and political instability, has emphasized the critical role of developing resilient health systems [1, 2].

Over the past couple of decades, Lebanon has confronted a series of profound crises that have had a profound impact on its health system, compromising the progress achieved towards universal health coverage (UHC) since the end of the civil war. The Syrian refugee crisis since 2011 marked the beginning of these challenges [3], exacerbated by an economic collapse that commenced in 2019, with a peak of inflation in 2021 compounded by shortages of fuel and essential goods (including medications), and lead to widespread poverty [4]. The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic (2020–2022), followed by a cholera epidemic in 2022, further weakened the already strained healthcare system and economy [5]. In addition, the Beirut blast in August 2020, attributed to improperly stored ammonium nitrate, caused extensive casualties, population displacement and destruction in the capital [6, 7]. Since 8 October 2023, an armed conflict has escalated between Israel and Hezbollah, evolving into a broader attack by Israel starting 23 September 2024 [8]. This escalation has resulted in over 4000 civilian deaths, widespread injuries and destructions, and the displacement of approximately 1.5 million people [9].

Evolution and challenges of Lebanon’s healthcare system

Historically, Lebanon succeeded in establishing advanced, state-of-the-art healthcare services, considered as the best in the Arab world, attracting patients from the entire Middle East [10]. It also developed a state-funded healthcare infrastructure, including at least one governmental hospital in each district, and a network of ambulatory healthcare institutions mandated officially to provide essential and critical healthcare services free of charge [11]. However, the civil war (1975–1990) resulted in the collapse of the public health sector, significantly increasing the role of private healthcare providers and civil society organizations (CSOs) [12, 13].

While the Ministry of Public Health’s (MoPH) reforms since the late 1990s successfully standardized healthcare and improved access, they coincided with massive privatization of healthcare service delivery [14, 15]. By 2016, 68% of primary healthcare (PHC) centres were operated by CSOs – local or national non-governmental organizations (NGO) or faith-based charity organizations, and 80% of hospitals were privately owned [16]. Nowadays, the quasi-totality of health services – including those at low cost in the civil sector, MoPH- and Ministry of Social Affairs (MOSA)-owned facilities are amenable to payment on a fee-for-service basis [17]. As a result of privatization, healthcare coverage has never fully recovered its pre-civil war levels of accessibility and inclusion [18].

Reforms have involved establishing strategic partnerships with private stakeholders, automating public hospitals and engaging CSOs in the operation of over 1500 low-cost ambulatory centres [19, 20]. The PHC Network exemplifies partnerships between the MoPH and the civil sector. It was established in 1993 to develop into a large network of 300 CSO-run facilities, supported by the MoPH for free vaccines and essential medications, including treatments for chronic illnesses [21]. The chronic medications program notably increased access to treatment, contributing to some of the lowest rates of premature mortality from cardiovascular diseases in the region [11]. The government also supported six public insurance schemes to cover secondary and tertiary care for roughly 50% of Lebanese citizens [22]. In addition, MoPH established subsidies for hospitalization and critical care for uninsured Lebanese and addressed catastrophic health costs for all, advancing towards Universal Health Coverage (UHC) [14]. Publicly subsidized hospitalizations are mostly outsourced to the private sector, and rarely to the autonomous public hospitals [23–25].

However, significant challenges in achieving equitable and standardized care persisted, including discrepancies in access and quality of care between subsidized and private services, as well as between facilities in peripheral areas and those in the capital [26].

Since 2011, the United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) has assumed responsibility for essential healthcare services for Syrian refugees, adopting a model similar to the pre-existing model of outsourcing and collaboration with the civil and private sectors [1].

Conceptual background

Resilience in health systems refers to the ability of the system to maintain essential functions, absorb shocks, adapt to changing conditions and ultimately, transform to reduce future vulnerability [2, 27]. The crises occurring back-to-back in Lebanon have had a profound effect on the Lebanese health system, already facing significant challenges of fragmentation, privatization and clientelism, compounded by weak organizational structure and inadequate infrastructure [28–32]. Since 2019, when the financial collapse began – further exacerbated by the COVID-19 pandemic starting in 2020 – the MoPH has struggled to mount an effective response. It has faced structural obstacles, including decision-making challenges, financial hardships, cuts to public spending and reductions in ministerial budgets [33, 34]. These issues have led to the MoPH becoming disengaged from its commitments [35]. Poor strategic planning, characterized by a weak and unclear vision as well as a lack of coherence across sectors, has undermined the functions of the MoPH [36, 37]. Since the beginning of the economic collapse, reduced public subsidies for low-cost health centres and declining reimbursements for outsourced hospitalizations have significantly decreased coverage, resulting in a marked increase in out-of-pocket expenses for patients and jeopardizing the control authority of the MoPH [38, 39]. Specifically, the National Social Security Fund (NSSF) coverage has dropped from 85% to 10%, while out-of-pocket expenditure on health has risen from 33.1% in 2017 to over 85% in 2022 [33, 40]. This situation has occurred despite a rise in public insurance premiums, leaving beneficiaries with substantial contributions, but without adequate, if any, coverage [41]. Access to healthcare services was largely compromised, even for middle-class families, who were subsequently deprived of the cash of their savings, insurance coverage and MoPH assistance. Concurrently, owing to currency devaluation and active exfiltration of imported goods purchased with preferential exchange rates, acute shortages of imported medical equipment and medications have emerged, affecting 70% of patients who remained unable to access or afford prescribed procedures and medications [42, 43]. Healthcare workers faced drastic income cuts, increased workloads and deteriorating working conditions, which drove many to seek opportunities abroad, further impacting health services availability and quality [44, 45]. While the challenges facing the Lebanese health system are well-documented, there remains limited understanding of how the resilience capacities were mobilized to navigate these crises in real-life conditions. This paper examines these capacities in a Lebanese locality using the ReBUILD for Resilience Consortium’s Health System Resilience Framework (Fig. 2). ReBUILD for Resilience is an international consortium that studies the resilience of health systems in fragile and conflict-affected settings. The framework is a model of feedback loops that builds on foundational work by Kurk and colleagues [2] and Blanchet and colleagues [1] to map how health systems can manage shocks, through enablers that activate resilience capacities [46]. Specifically, absorption is the system’s ability to respond to population needs with available resources and processes; adaptation involves adjusting resource use without changing the fundamental structures; and transformation denotes the capacity to fundamentally alter system structures, processes and resource utilization to meet both ongoing and emerging needs [47] (Fig. 2).Fig. 2ReBUILD for Resilience Health System Resilience Framework (43)

We use this framework to examine how these capacities can be built and maintained to create responsive, effective, inclusive, gender-equitable and sustainable health systems [48].

Methods

Study design

This study is qualitative, cross-sectional research based on key informant interviews. It is part of a Participatory Action Research Learning Site, implemented within the ReBUILD for Resilience program to assess the resilience of the local health system and collaboratively develop interventions to strengthen it.

A key part of the Learning Site involved a mapping exercise aimed at evaluating the health system’s ability to withstand and adapt to multiple crises as perceived by local health actors. During this exercise, we conducted interviews with local stakeholders to gather real-life insights on how responsive measures are implemented in practice. We present here the results of those interviews.

The assessment focused on the resilience nodes outlined in the framework, representing healthcare provision components, and the interplay among system actors and their connections to the broader context (Fig. 2).

Study setting

Lebanon is divided into eight governorates (Mohafazat) with a Public Health Unit in each one except Beirut. These governorates are further divided into 26 districts (Qada’a) each of which, except Beirut, has an MoPH office called District Physician Office (DPO) [49]. Districts are further divided into cadasters, generally managed by municipalities. There are 1029 municipalities, varying widely in size and population across urban, suburban and rural areas [50].

Majdal Anjar is a municipality situated near the Syrian border in the Zahle District of the Beqaa Governorate. It was selected as a Learning Site owing to its classification as a vulnerable cadaster by the UNHCR [51]. Approximately half of Majdal Anjar’s population comprises Syrian refugees, some of whom are living in substandard housing or informal tented settlements. The area also hosts long-standing Palestinian refugees [52].

Data collection and sampling

This study used key informant interviews (KII) with local stakeholders involved in various levels of healthcare service provision at the Learning Site. KIIs were conducted between May and June 2022. At that moment, the external conflict had not started yet, and Lebanon was experiencing a peak in disruptions to healthcare services, including drug supply, human resources and insurance coverage, in addition to the disengagement of the MoPH from its regulatory role and a consequent disorganization of health services provision. The primary objective was to capture local actors’ perceptions about the responses adopted to maintain healthcare provision.

Key informants (KI) were selected from different levels within the local healthcare system, including coordinators, officers, administrators and practitioners from MoPH, UNHCR, local and international non-governmental organizations (NGOs), and the informal sector. It is noteworthy that the study targeted individuals in more leading or managerial roles within these institutions, which was a deliberate choice to gain insights from those directly involved in developing and/or implementing directives.

Participants were identified and proposed by local partners within the Learning Site participatory action research (PAR). They were subsequently contacted by phone, with some requiring additional time to obtain approval from their supervisors. A total of 11 potential respondents agreed to be interviewed, with necessary authorizations secured where applicable. However, two potential interviewees were unable to schedule interviews despite multiple follow-ups. Ultimately, nine key informants were interviewed, comprising five women and four men. During the interviews, respondents provided insights into various healthcare provision components represented by the nodes of the framework, describing the status of healthcare services amid challenges posed by the crisis – including the COVID-19 pandemic and the economic/financial downturn – and outlining the responsive measures implemented to mitigate their impact on health service delivery.

The interviews followed a topic guide, developed collaboratively by the research team and the Learning Site team, on the basis of a literature review and the Health System Resilience Framework. The aim was to explore the crisis’s impact on key components of the healthcare provision, and to identify responses implemented to strengthen resilience. During the interviews, respondents provided insights into various healthcare provision components, describing the status of healthcare services amid the challenges triggered by the COVID-19 pandemic and the economic/financial downturn, and outlining the responsive measures undertaken to mitigate their impact on health service delivery. The interviews were conducted by two of the authors, along with four local partners involved in the Learning Site. All interviewers underwent meticulous training on qualitative data collection methods to ensure consistency and rigor. The data collection process adhered to established guidelines in the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research to enhance transparency and quality of reporting [53].

Six interviews were conducted face-to-face in a private setting. Three additional interviews were conducted by phone for participants unable to participate in face-to-face interviews. Interviews were conducted in Arabic, lasted between 30 min and 90 min, and were recorded with participants’ consent. The interviews were then transcribed in Arabic by the interviewers and subsequently translated into English by a professional translator and checked by the research team for accuracy.

Data analysis

The narratives were analyzed using a deductive approach to qualitative analysis, applying the ReBUILD Health System Resilience Framework [54]. The research team collaboratively developed the coding framework, unfolding the nodes of the Health System Resilience Framework and the topics of the interview guide (Fig. 2). The second author primarily coded the transcripts and extracted data into an Excel sheet. To ensure reliability, the co-authors reviewed the coding, and any discrepancies were discussed until consensus was reached. The first author then analyzed the coded data to identify responsive measures reported by the respondents. Subsequently, the entire team of authors collaboratively classified the identified responses, into the three resilience capacities – absorption, adaptation or transformation – on the basis of their descriptions [1, 2, 46, 47], with multiple discussions to ensure accuracy and coherence.

Ethics

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Institutional Review Boards of the American University of Beirut (2021–0449) and the Liverpool School of Tropical Medicine (21–092). Written consent was obtained from participants interviewed in person, while verbal consent was secured for phone interviews, with consent forms shared via WhatsApp. Consent for recording interviews was systematically obtained.

Results

The findings are organized into two parts. The first part provides an overview of the health system landscape within the studied locality on the basis of stakeholders’ perceptions gathered through interviews. This overview aims to contextualize the subsequent analysis and enhance the interpretability of the results. The second part examines the local response to the multifaceted crises, highlighting how the health system demonstrated resilience through absorptive, adaptive and transformative responses.

Overview of the local health system landscape

The structure of the healthcare provision in the Learning Site was described by the respondents as overseen by different sectors (KI-04, KI-05, KI-06, KI-08). The MoPH regulates and supports three low-cost ambulatory facilities: two operated by local NGOs – Fath el Islam, which is affiliated with the regional religious authority, and Karagueusian PHC Centre, managed by a national charity organization – and the Majdal Anjar Social Development Centre, which is owned and operated by MOSA and supported by the municipality. The three are connected to the MoPH through subsidy channels for vaccines and essential medications, and a training and reporting system, which includes the electronic intranet system PHOENIX, established in 2010 for the PHC Network. In addition, the MoPH manages the private sector through agreements for outsourcing subsidized services to accredited private and autonomous public hospitals, and monthly reporting. Private doctors practice in their clinics, in private and public hospitals, and treat private patients and those covered by insurance schemes and MoPH. The UNHCR and its implementing partners supervise humanitarian funds assisting Syrian refugees within both the civil and private sectors, in coordination with the MoPH. UNHCR also coordinates ad hoc health programs implemented by local and international NGOs, through the Beqaa Health Working Group. For Palestinian refugees, healthcare is provided through the United Nations Relief and Works Agency for Palestine Refugees (UNRWA) clinics (there is one in Barrelias serving Palestinian dwellers of Majdal Anjar) and a network of facilities operating under UNRWA auspices, including those affiliated with the Palestinian Red Crescent, which has a hospital near Majdal Anjar. Furthermore, informal healthcare services are provided by Syrian medical professionals who operate outside of MoPH regulations. Gherass el Kheir informal health centre located in Majdal Anjar, functions without authorization and serves a large number of Lebanese and Syrian residents in Majdal Anjar and nearby communities. This centre is associated with the Multi-Aid Programs (MAPS) – a refugee-led local humanitarian organization, funded by Syrian diaspora and foreign governments, promoting dignified living conditions of refugees. Given the high proportion of Syrian refugees in the area, humanitarian actors operate numerous health initiatives and programs. Most remain ad hoc, time-bound and target specific population groups.

Response of the local healthcare system to the multi-crisis

Rapid deployment of absorptive responses

Given the unprecedented crisis confronting health actors, it is important to highlight how local stakeholders utilized available resources and different arrangements to address urgent demand on healthcare services and secure the survival of their institutions amid the ongoing economic and political turmoil.

The most notable response to the abrupt cessation of both publicly sourced and out-of-pocket payments for healthcare services was the rapid mobilization of humanitarian funds. This involved both existing organizations that had been supporting Syrian refugees and new local and international organizations that emerged at the onset of the economic crisis.

The local health actors immediately leveraged available resources and organizational processes, while humanitarian actors, encouraged by the MoPH, intensified their appeals to mobilize international funds to maintain the functioning of health infrastructure and expand their coverage to those in need.The MoPH and the state of Lebanon in general encouraged international donors to donate to Lebanon, to allow an escape from the trap of economic crisis. You can find this appeal in all the documents issued by the MoPH, and all the activities of the MoPH are directed towards increasing the humanitarian fund allocated to Lebanon. KI-08, MoPH Officer

The MoPH officer noted that many entities prioritize establishing new health centres simply because funding is available. According to them, this approach represents a misallocation of resources that could instead be used to strengthen existing health services, improve quality and expand coverage to underserved populations (KI-08).

Moreover, the new sources of funding and the conditions of the new collaborative arrangements inadvertently created new inequalities. Individuals suffering from illnesses, especially critical ones, not covered by the health programs proposed at this point in time, cannot access care, while communities served by facilities that failed to secure alternative funding lost access to their usual healthcare provider (KI-01, KI-07, KI-08).

Meaningful adaptive responses

Following the initial absorption of shock using available resources, adaptive measures emerged within Lebanon’s health sector to reconfigure resources and adapt laws and regulations to mobilize capacities amid the deepening crisis.

According to narratives, although the Lebanese government largely failed to address the financial crisis – particularly in implementing protective measures such as safeguarding savings, regulating the market for subsidized goods and preventing smuggling and speculation – many KIs acknowledged the efforts of the MoPH. They emphasized that, in contrast to other sectors, the Ministry was able to sustain functional health services during this period, which was viewed by stakeholders as a demonstration of resilience (KI-02, KI-04, KI-05, KI-06, KI-08). This perception was notably acknowledged even by the DPO representative, who, despite their usual critique, recognized positive aspects of the MoPH’s response during this difficult period.We are calling for the state to plan and be ready, instead of merely reacting to the crisis. Because in our country, every crisis is followed by another crisis. However, I need to point out that the MoPH is one of the rare state institutions that had the right reaction, and succeeded in uniting everybody and delegating its role subtly to maintain the health services. As soon as the MoPH identified budget constraints and anticipated cuts, they immediately modified their directives and actively encouraged health institutions to collaborate directly with humanitarian funders, while appealing to international donors to assist healthcare institutions operating under MoPH affiliation. The UNHCR representative emphasized: “Since 2020, we received directives from the MoPH to shift our priorities and secure maximum funding to maintain the front-line health institutions functioning” (KI-02). The MoPH officer emphasized: “The Ministry issued internal circulars permitting health centers and hospitals to collaborate directly with international donors and report to them – an arrangement that had not been allowed prior to the crisis” (KI-08).

The MoPH, local health authorities and municipalities adapted their policies regarding the informal sector by facilitating its activities as a recognition of its essential role in addressing the immediate health needs of the population, including Lebanese. In practice, however, the core regulation remained unchanged: All non-Lebanese health workers continue to lack the right to practice, and those without legal residency are subject to deportation. In addition, informal health facilities still require authorization from the MoPH to operate on Lebanese territory, even if they cater to the needs of Syrian refugees exclusively. Nevertheless, during the COVID-19 pandemic, authorities exhibited increasing tolerance towards informal health workers and facilities. In some areas, collaborations between local authorities and the informal sector emerged. The officer at Gherass el Kheir highlighted this shift, remarking: “The Municipality proposed close collaboration, and they even entrusted us with distributing donations coming to the village” (KI-05). The MAPS representative noted that this increasing tolerance appeared to be widespread in all the areas where they operate informal health facilities.There has been a noticeable shift in perspective. Recent internal directives have eased the movement of Syrian healthcare workers, even those without legal residency, through checkpoints. Besides acquiring direct funds, individual facilities, civil and private, intensified their fundraising within their localities to secure the necessary resources for their operations, an action they were not used to undertaking before the crisis. The administrative officer in a private hospital highlighted: “Can you imagine that at the peak of the financial crisis, we secured the fuel and water for the hospital through local donations?” (KI-03).

The availability of medications was a pervasive concern in each interview since the drug shortage was at its highest point when the team conducted interviews. The healthcare providers improvised a new task in their job description, which was to support their patients in the search of prescribed medications. The PHC officer stated: “Helping our patients to find the prescribed medications has become the most time-consuming task of our job.” (KI-05). The UNHCR adapted their protocols to assist patients navigating drug shortage as relayed by their officer: “We have created a dedicated platform that every healthcare provider can consult to indicate to their patients where each medication can be found.” (KI-02).

However, adaptive responses sometimes fall short owing to institutional inertia, economic volatility and implementation gaps.

The representative of DPO relayed that the current minister seems to be seeking to strengthen the local pharmaceutical industry, positioning it as a preferred solution for enhancing the availability and affordability of treatments, particularly for chronic conditions: “This initiative is overdue and should have been adopted a long time ago. But the Ministry’s line before was to satisfy the importers of pharmaceutical products. This is why I’m not sure he (the Minister) will make it through. They will not let him” (KI-01).

The officer in a private hospital mentioned that the MoPH attempted to adapt its reimbursement rates for outsourced hospitalizations to account for the devaluation of the national currency. However, they did not keep up with rapid inflation, leading hospitals to limit and sometimes completely ban admissions for patients with public insurance or public funding.Honestly, we, and all the other hospitals, including governmental ones, no longer accept publicly subsidized patients, not from the Ministry or the NSSF. We only admit those covered by organizations that pay or private patients who can afford to pay out of pocket. KI-03, Officer in a private hospital

Since the end of 2022, Lebanon’s central bank has stopped providing preferential currency exchange rates for all subsidy programs, including medications. This new policy neutralized the financial interest of smuggling and consequently increased the availability of drugs in the market. However, this measure had negative repercussions on the cost of drugs as remarked by the DPO representative: “This measure will make imported medicines more available in the market. But will they remain affordable for patients, struggling to secure their basic needs?” (KI-01).

Aborted transformative response

Researchers identified a pattern within the narratives that could be classified as a transformative response. During the COVID-19 pandemic, when the state was unable to provide technical support, means of subsistence, supplies or consistent feedback, municipalities stepped into the breach. They utilized their decision-making space to bolster efforts in overseeing essential services to meet their communities’ needs, including health services. This period saw the spontaneous creation of municipal crisis cells, which were frequently mentioned and viewed positively by local stakeholders who were interviewed (KI-01, KI-03, KI-04, KI-05, KI-09).

The DPO representative, who also experienced a rise in their role when the central MoPH could not assume full responsibilities, willingly engaged with these crisis cells.It was easier for me to contact the municipalities…because they are official bodies and are supposed to respond more quickly than CSOs, who need time to deal with their own headquarters. Moreover, they are close to their communities and understand their needs better than anyone. Our office contributed to creating crisis cells in many municipalities of the district. KI-01

The officer in a private hospital, who collaborated extensively with the Majdal Anjar crisis cell during the pandemic, witnessed their work: “You would see them during corona; they managed everything – food, medications, hospitalizations, oxygen – and succeeded in saving the village from devastation. Yet, nobody recognizes their efforts” (KI-03).

This sense of unacknowledged contribution highlights a broader issue: Despite their vital role during the crisis, municipalities’ efforts were not fully leveraged for long-term benefit. Once the immediate crisis subsided, municipalities reverted to their pre-pandemic roles, with limited participation in health decision-making. As the MoPH officer put it:

“They (the municipalities) are not involved in decision-making for health. They do not want to be involved; they are afraid they will have to pay” (KI-08). Similarly, the UNHCR regional officer noted that municipal contributions were mostly limited to enabling humanitarian programs to implement their activities (KI-02).

Some respondents advocated for elements of decentralized health governance (KI-05, KI-06, KI-09). This was most clearly articulated by the officer in a private hospital:

“I believe it is critical to establish local medical teams that monitor local populations and initiate actions in collaboration with municipalities, district officials, and the Ministry of Health” (KI-03). This respondent emphasized that failing to adopt decentralization principles represents a missed opportunity to enhance health services and promote social justice – an idea echoed by the representative of DPO who highlighted the potential of proximal offices to strengthen future crisis preparedness. He explained:We (at the MoPH District Office) succeeded in managing the pandemic’s catastrophic early phase alone – mobilizing municipal staff, volunteers, doctors and businesses to fund supplies and enforce lockdowns. Since we learned how to proceed, this model could work for every crisis. But it was an exception, only possible because we could bypass the system in those extraordinary circumstances. KI-01

By analysing these responses through the lens of resilience, particularly absorptive, adaptive and transformative approaches, the discussion contextualizes the broader implications for health system governance, resilience and long-term sustainability.

Our findings confirm that absorptive response relies on the availability of human, physical and financial resources [1, 46]. In Majdal Anjar, the health actors managed to sustain operations primarily through humanitarian funds, direct collaboration between healthcare facilities and donors and the MoPH’s facilitation of partnerships. These efforts prevented system collapse by ensuring continued service delivery. However, over-reliance on external aid can foster dependency and undermine local capacity. As Shroff and colleagues [55] pointed out, this dependency can hinder the development of sustainable, locally driven solutions, an important consideration when assessing the long-term impact of humanitarian interventions in settings such as Majdal Anjar. The reliance on short-term external support, instead of pre-established buffer stock mechanisms such as building financial reserves and stabilization funds pre-crisis, in addition to building local capacities, reveals a lack of long-term planning, leaving the system vulnerable to future shocks [56]. This issue has also been observed in crisis-prone settings such as Sierra Leone and Syria, where emergency funding temporarily sustained healthcare services but failed to build lasting resilience, as well as in post-conflict Iraq and South Sudan, where dependence on humanitarian aid has created long-term governance and equity challenges [57–60].

In addition, absorptive responses depend on the availability and motivation of diverse healthcare professionals [46]. While international support provided temporary relief for workforce shortages through training programs and financial incentives, ongoing economic distress failed to reduce attrition rates, with many healthcare workers continuing to leave the country [61]. This depletion of the health workforce reduced service quality and increased workload burdens on remaining staff, necessitating adaptive capacities to retain personnel and maintain quality healthcare. This mirrors findings from Venezuela and Myanmar, where economic crises caused mass migration of health workers, undermining healthcare systems [62, 63]. Moreover, the impact of health worker migration extends beyond immediate service delivery. Burau and colleagues [64] argued that the loss of skilled professionals significantly weakens a health system’s ability to respond to future crises and implement long-term health programs.

The MoPH encouraged direct engagement between health facilities and humanitarian actors, allowing facilities to secure independent funding. In addition, growing tolerance for informal health services demonstrated an unplanned but critical adaptation to healthcare gaps. However, the lack of structured governance mechanisms to formally integrate these actors led to inefficiencies, duplication of services, and inequities in service provision and healthcare access. This lack of formal governance structures and regulatory oversight over informal health services is a clear shortcoming. While informal providers can temporarily address gaps, the absence of quality control and reporting systems poses significant risks. A more robust integration of these actors would lead to better-coordinated care and minimize potential risks to patient safety. Similar challenges have been documented in Afghanistan, where weak governance resulted in fragmented health service delivery, limiting the effectiveness of adaptive responses [65]. To enhance adaptive capacity, fostering collaborative governance and integrated healthcare delivery is essential. Gilson and colleagues [66] identified that effective health system governance requires the participation of multiple stakeholders and clear accountability mechanisms. Moreover, introducing decentralized governance alongside collaborative strategies could offer more responsive and context-specific solutions that enhance health system equity, efficiency and resilience [1, 36, 66].

Despite some positive resilience indicators, our findings also highlight significant maladaptive responses. A heavy reliance on humanitarian aid has reinforced a fragmented healthcare system, where private and NGO-led facilities operate with minimal coordination by public health authorities. This lack of integration has exacerbated healthcare inequities, leaving many populations unable to access essential services. Moreover, the selective nature of humanitarian aid has created uneven coverage, with certain groups receiving better care on the basis of funding priorities, further deepening inequalities. The proliferation of these unregulated services poses serious public health risks, leading to inconsistencies in care and potential harm to patients. As Kumah [67] noted, ensuring the quality and safety of healthcare requires robust regulatory mechanisms. Without regulation, informal providers can compromise patient safety and undermine broader efforts to achieve UHC [67, 68]. In addition, rising out-of-pocket costs have left vulnerable populations excluded from essential healthcare, directly contradicting the goal of equitable access. These maladaptive responses emphasize the dangers of relying on short-term solutions such as humanitarian aid and the informal health sector without addressing the need for long-term, integrated health system reforms.

A truly resilient health system must prioritize universal access, ensuring that financial, geographic and systemic barriers do not exclude vulnerable groups. Research in Ghana, Mexico and Cambodia has shown that resilience strategies must focus on equity, ensuring comprehensive service provision across all population groups [69]. Ultimately, strengthening system integration and regulatory oversight is key to addressing these systemic challenges and moving towards a more equitable, sustainable healthcare system. Achieving UHC requires a long-term commitment to health system strengthening. As Kruk and colleagues [68] and Kumah [67] emphasized, investing in primary healthcare, improving health financing mechanisms and strengthening the health workforce are essential steps towards building resilient and equitable health systems.

Healthcare systems often reveal their underlying weaknesses most sharply during crises, as observed in Sri Lanka, the United Kingdom post Brexit and Europe during the COVID-19 pandemic [70–72]. This is why a resilient health system is not merely about the technicalities of implementing reactive responses but also about the underlying values that shape responses [73–75].

In Lebanon, the historical emphasis on profit generation within the healthcare industry has led to fragmentation and inequitable access [76, 77]. When the crisis hit, this approach persisted, with humanitarian funds directed towards outsourcing and benefiting the private and low-cost civil sectors, obstructing the development of a more efficient, needs-based health framework [24, 41]. While the model adopted is benefiting the quality of health services, it ultimately falls short in promoting equitable access to healthcare [78–80]. Illustratively, the profit-centric choices are still absorbing humanitarian funds to outsource services to the private hospitals and the low-cost sector, a phenomenon observed clearly during the COVID-19 response [41, 81].

International evidence demonstrates that investing in transformative resilience – particularly through strengthened public health systems and progress towards UHC – significantly bolsters a nation’s ability to withstand and recover from crises [82, 83]. In Majdal Anjar, within the multi-crisis context, all three resilience capacities emerged with varying success: Absorptive responses ensured immediate survival but struggled with efficiency and sustainability. Adaptive responses allowed temporary policy adjustments yet suffered from fragmented leadership and poor coordination. Transformative responses – essential for structural shift – remained largely absent. The sole exception was COVID-19’s temporary decentralization of health services via municipal crisis cells. Responsible for small communities, these local bodies leveraged their knowledge of community needs to pool resources and prioritize service delivery, which inadvertently improved equity in access to healthcare services and efficiency of processes. However, this short-lived arrangement, triggered by necessity, failed to disrupt Lebanon’s centralized and profit-driven healthcare model. Ultimately, while the system exhibited resilience in preserving infrastructure and institutional stability, it still lacks equitable and inclusive service delivery.

Implications for policy and practice

To enhance health system resilience, several key policy recommendations emerge. First, strengthening absorptive capacity through the establishment of emergency financial reserves and improved supply chain logistics will ensure that necessary resources are available during future crises. In addition, implementing workforce retention strategies, such as offering incentives, improved salaries and better working conditions, can reduce attrition and ensure continued service delivery. The formal integration of informal health providers, supported by regulatory frameworks, would improve service quality and coordination. Lastly, adopting decentralized governance models, as demonstrated by municipalities during the COVID-19 response, can empower local governments to make decisions and enhance service delivery in their communities.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study lie in the inclusion of perspectives from a range of local stakeholders working in the complex health system in Lebanon. They provide rich insights into the real-life challenges confronting the local health system during crises and the responses that the health system was able to make. The diverse perspectives of researchers with long-standing engagement in Lebanon’s health system, including the academically knowledgeable embedded research team and the contextually informed local partners within the learning site team, were essential in shaping a robust study design and reaching key stakeholders for interviews. This experience informed the development of contextually appropriate interview guides, facilitated trust-building with participants, and enhanced the cultural and political sensitivity of the analysis. It also enriched the data collection, analysis and interpretation, supporting the broader generalizability of the findings. However, there are several limitations.

We conducted the study at a single time point and framed our analysis and discussion accordingly. This cross-sectional approach provided important insights into stakeholder perceptions at a specific moment of compounded crises; however, it limited our ability to capture how responses and systemic responses have evolved.4 Future research would benefit from longitudinal, ethnographic, historical and economic approaches to better understand the evolving nature of healthcare governance in Lebanon, particularly in relation to privatization trends, profit motives and policy shifts. Such interdisciplinary work could offer a more dynamic and systemic view of how health systems adapt under pressure.

The absence of community and patient perspectives limits triangulation. This focus on stakeholders in managerial positions may have shaped the narratives obtained and different perspectives might have emerged had the study included institutional staff at various levels or beneficiaries of these services. Recognizing this, a limitation of the current study is that it does not encompass the views of front-line staff or service recipients. In addition, the study was conducted in a single region, which may limit the transferability of findings beyond Lebanon. Future research could explore these other stakeholder groups to provide a more comprehensive understanding of adaptive practices and perceptions across the healthcare system.

Conclusions

The findings highlight that Lebanon’s healthcare system has demonstrated resilience through the deployment of absorptive and adaptive responses during recurrent crises. These strategies have allowed the system to persist in the face of significant challenges; however, they have not been sufficient to guarantee equitable and sustainable healthcare access for all populations. The heavy reliance on humanitarian funds and partnerships with the private sector has exacerbated pre-existing structural weaknesses within the health system, limiting its capacity for long-term resilience. Addressing these vulnerabilities requires a fundamental paradigm shift towards comprehensive governance reforms, strategic and needs-based resource allocation, increased decentralization and a reorientation towards a health framework centred on equity and inclusion. Moving forward, resilience efforts must prioritize ensuring that all individuals, regardless of their financial situation or geographic location, have access to quality healthcare services, thereby laying the groundwork for a more resilient and just health system.
