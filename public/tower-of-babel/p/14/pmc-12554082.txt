Formation mechanism and characterization of hesperidin-stabilized Pickering emulsion gels
Authors: Sheng Geng, Guoyang Liu, Yuxiang Wang, Chunyan Wang, Benguo Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study systematically compared the Pickering emulsifying capacity of four citrus flavonoids (hesperetin, Hpt; hesperidin, Hpd; neohesperidin, Neohpd; neohesperidin dihydrochalcone, Neohpddic) using combined experimental and molecular dynamics (MD) approaches. A stable Hpd-based O/W Pickering emulsion gel was successfully developed. The Hpd rod-shaped particles exhibited the smallest particle size and demonstrated the highest emulsifying activity, primarily arising from its distinct rutinose glycoside group. But molecular dynamics simulation revealed that Hpd could significantly reduce interfacial tension and rapidly migrate to the oil-water interface, forming molecular aggregates via hydrogen bonding, which was consistent with laser confocal scanning microscopy observation. Stable emulsion gels formed when Hpd concentration (w) ≥2 % and oil phase volume fraction (φ) ≥50 %. With the increase of w and φ, the system exhibited enhanced droplet aggregation, and the apparent viscosity, gel strength, and rheological properties significantly ascended, indicating a marked enhancement in emulsion stability.Keywords: Flavonoids, hesperidin, Pickering emulsions, Molecular simulation

Introduction

Citrus fruits are the largest category of fruit in the world, with 50 %–60 % consumed as fresh fruit and the remainder utilized for industrial processing. During citrus processing, peels and pomace represent the primary by-products. These by-products not only contain abundant nutritional components such as dietary fiber, natural pigments, and volatile compounds but also concentrate various high-value-added bioactive substances, offering potential for further development and utilization. Hesperidin (Hpd), a flavonoid compound abundantly present in the outer peel of citrus fruits, serves as a typical representative of citrus flavonoids (Putnik et al., 2017). Extensive studies have demonstrated its significant biological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antibacterial, immune-regulatory, anti-tumor, and lipid metabolism-modulating effects as a natural extract. However, the low water solubility of Hpd severely restricts its oral bioavailability, thereby limiting its widespread application in food, pharmaceutical, and other industrial sectors (Choi et al., 2022). Therefore, enhancing the bioavailability of Hpd has become a critical issue for expanding its application scope. Food emulsions, as a common delivery system, are widely employed to improve the oral bioavailability of nutraceutical formulations, demonstrating promising application prospects (Ting et al., 2015).

While assemblies such as liposomes and microcapsules have been employed for the encapsulation of bioactive substances, emulsion delivery systems offer distinct advantages in enhancing the oral bioavailability of hydrophobic nutrients. Emulsions generally have a higher lipid-loading capacity and greater physical stability compared to liposomes, and their preparation processes are more suitable for large-scale production. Unlike the solid powder form of microcapsules, the oil phase in emulsions more closely resembles the digestive pathway of dietary fats, significantly enhancing the bioaccessibility of hydrophobic bioactive compounds, by facilitating bile salt secretion and micelle formation (Gorantla et al., 2022; Salvia-Trujillo et al., 2017; Ameta et al., 2023). Consequently, to address the challenge of Hpd's extremely low solubility, developing an emulsion delivery system emerges as a promising and effective strategy.

Pickering emulsions are a type of emulsion system stabilized by solid particles, exhibiting significantly enhanced stability compared to conventional surfactant-stabilized emulsions. They not only retain the fundamental properties of emulsions but also possess a remarkable ability to suppress coalescence and Ostwald ripening (Harman et al., 2019). Despite the widespread use of inorganic particles such as silica and alumina in Pickering emulsions, their application in food is restricted due to their failure to meet clean-label requirements (Tavernier et al., 2016). In recent years, food-derived particle emulsifiers have emerged as a research focus. These natural particles can effectively stabilize Pickering emulsions while efficiently delivering or encapsulating bioactive compounds (Mwangi et al., 2020), such as lutein, anthocyanins, and resveratrol. Additionally, they offer advantages such as ease of digestion and low toxicity. Consequently, Pickering emulsions developed by natural biopolymer particles represent an emerging trend in research.

Currently, research on food-grade Pickering particle emulsifiers has primarily focused on natural biomacromolecules such as proteins and polysaccharides. Additionally, some flavonoid compounds have garnered attention from researchers due to their safety, environmental friendliness, and dual functionality in providing both emulsifying properties and biological activity. In our previous report, dihydromyricetin was utilized to construct edible Pickering emulsion gels. Lu et al. (2023) employed quercetin to establish a stable Pickering emulsion system. Shi et al. (2024) utilized acylated EGCG derivatives to stabilize Pickering emulsions. Dammak and José do Amaral Sobral (2018) successfully encapsulated Hpd using chitosan nanoparticles enhanced with phospholipids, which extended the shelf life of the product, though this study did not focus on the emulsifying properties of Hpd. Our prior research revealed that Hpd possessed emulsifying properties, but the underlying mechanism remains unclear. Hpd is the predominant flavonoid present in citrus fruit peels, characterized by hesperetin (Hpt, Fig. 1A) linked to a disaccharide on the A-ring (Fig. 1B). This disaccharide is composed of rhamnose and glucose molecules. The chemical structure of Hpd is amenable to various reactions, yielding structurally related compounds with distinct physicochemical properties suitable for diverse applications. By isomerizing the glycosidic bond within Hpd, it can be converted into Neohpd (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, through hydrolysis and catalytic hydrogenation, Hpd can be transformed into Neohesperidin dihydrochalcone (Neohpddic, Fig. 1D), a synthetically produced compound known for its intense sweetness. Neohpddic is chemically stable, non-toxic, and safe for human consumption, offering an effective means to mask bitterness in food products, thus emerging as a promising green food sweetener. In light of this, this study compared the abilities of four structurally similar citrus flavonoids—Hpt, Hpd, Neohpd, and Neohpddic—in forming Pickering emulsion gels. By integrating particle characterization techniques with molecular dynamics simulations, the relationship between emulsification performance and molecular structure was explored. On this basis, employing Hpd as an emulsifier, the effects of oil phase volume fraction (φ) and particle concentration (w) on the microstructure and mechanical properties of Pickering emulsion gels were investigated. The findings of this research not only promote the comprehensive development of citrus resources but also provide a novel strategy for constructing food-grade Pickering emulsion gels.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Hesperetin (Hpt, purity≥98 %), hesperidin (Hpd, purity≥98 %), neohesperidin (Neohpd, purity≥98 %), neohesperidin dihydrochalcone (Neohpddic, purity≥98 %), and medium-chain triglycerides (MCT) were all obtained from Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Nile Blue A and Nile Red were the products of Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Preparation of Pickering emulsion gels

Distilled water was used as the aqueous phase, and MCT as the oil phase to investigate the effects of flavonoid addition levels (w = 0.5 %, 1.0 %, 2.0 %, 3.0 %, 4.0 %) and oil phase volume ratios (φ = 20 %, 40 %, 60 %, 80 %, 90 %) on the formation of Pickering emulsion gels. The obtained mixtures were subjected to high-speed shearing at 15,000 rpm for 3 min using an IKAT18 high-speed shear machine, then transferred into 20 mL flat-bottomed glass vials. After resting for 24 h, the vials were inverted for 2 h to confirm the formation of emulsion gels.

Contact angle measurement

The amphiphilicity of Hpt, Hpd, Neohpd, and Neohpddic was determined using a ThetaLite optical contact angle meter based on the sessile drop method. Initially, samples were compressed into cylindrical tablets using a tablet press at 15 MPa. Subsequently, the tablets were placed in cubic quartz vessels (with a side length of 5 cm) containing medium-chain triglycerides (MCT). An automated high-precision droplet dispensing device delivered 25 μL of water onto the surface of the tablets, and the state of the droplets was recorded. The recording duration was 15 s, during which the contact angles were calculated based on an elliptical fitting model (Geng et al., 2021).

Interfacial tension measurement

The influence of Hpt, Hpd, Neohpd, and Neohpddic on the interfacial tension of MCT/water was investigated using a ThetaLite optical contact angle meter based on the pendant drop method. Excess samples were first dissolved in distilled water, followed by ultrasonication at room temperature for 10 min and subsequent static incubation for 15 min. The supernatant was then filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane. Subsequently, 40 mL of MCT was added to a quartz dish. A single drop of 15 μL of the flavonoid solution was added into the quartz dish containing MCT using a high-precision droplet dispensing device for measurement. The state of the sample droplet was recorded, and the interfacial tension was automatically determined (Zhou et al., 2018). To better elucidate the relationship between interfacial tension and structure, the solubility of flavonoids was determined using the ultraviolet (UV) method.

Observation of particle morphology

An appropriate amount of particles were evenly distributed on the sample stage and sputter-coated with gold. Subsequently, the morphologies of Hpt, Hpd, Neohpd, and Neohpddic particles were observed and rapidly recorded using a Quanta 200 environmental scanning electron microscope under low vacuum conditions at an accelerating voltage of 15.0 Kv (Lv et al., 2022).

Determination of particle size

The particle size distribution of Hpt, Hpd, Neohpd, and Neohpddic in water was determined using a Malvern Mastersizer 2000 laser particle sizer. A small amount of the test sample was added into the sample cell, and measurements were conducted based on the principle of laser light scattering within a 10 %–15 % obscuration range. The results were processed using the Malvern Mastersizer 2000 software to obtain the particle size distribution (Li et al., 2022).

Molecular dynamics simulation method

This study employed Gromacs 2018.04 software to simulate the emulsifying behavior of Hpt, Hpd, Neohpd, and Neohpddic in the MCT/H2O system. The system included three layers: the upper layer (8 nm × 8 nm × 4 nm) and the lower layer (8 nm × 8 nm × 4 nm) represented the oil phase, each filled with 160 molecules of 1,3-octanoyl-2-decanoyl-glycerol molecules. The middle layer (8 nm × 8 nm × 8 nm) represented the water phase, filled with 16,000 SPC (simple point charge) water molecules. The topology files for the flavonoids and 1,3-octanoyl-2-decanoyl-glycerol were generated based on the GAFF field using the Antechamber tool. Prior to initiating molecular dynamics simulations, 32 flavonoid molecules were randomly placed in the water phase. During the simulation process, energy minimization was first performed to eliminate unreasonable conformations. Subsequently, a 30-ps heating dynamics simulation was conducted from 0 K to 298.15 K to prevent system collapse caused by unreasonable contacts. For the subsequent 100-ns isothermal-isobaric (NPT) equilibration simulation, the pressure was maintained at 1.01325 bar using the Berendsen barostat with isotropic coordinate scaling. After confirming system equilibrium, a 10-ns simulation was performed to obtain trajectories for analysis under equilibrium conditions. The simulation time step was set at 2 fs, with data recorded every 500 steps. In the molecular dynamics analysis, system equilibrium was confirmed by examining bulk-phase density, energy, and temperature. Based on this, the distribution of flavonoids within the system was investigated, as well as their effects on interfacial tension (Geng et al., 2023).

Laser confocal scanning microscopy observation

The LSM780 confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Oberkochen, Germany) was employed to excite the dyes Nile Blue A and Nile Red at wavelengths of 488 nm and 633 nm, respectively, for observing the microstructure of the emulsions (w = 2.0 %, 3.0 %, 4.0 %, φ = 60 %; φ = 50 %, 60 %, 70 %, w = 3 %). Before the observation, Hpd and MCT were stained with Nile Blue A and Nile Red (0.01 wt%; dissolved in 1,2-propanediol), respectively. Images were recorded with a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels and subsequently processed using the image analysis software ZEN 3.0 (Aditya et al., 2015).

Measurement of emulsion droplet size

The effects of Hpd concentration (w = 1 %, 2 %, 3 %, 4 %, φ = 60 %) and oil phase volume fraction (φ = 20 %, 40 %, 60 %, 70 %, w = 3 %) on the droplet size of emulsions were investigated separately. The emulsion diameter was measured at room temperature using a BT-9300H laser particle size distribution analyzer. Deionized water was used as the dispersant, and measurements commenced when the refractive index of the sample was between 15 % and 16 % (Song et al., 2022).

Evaluation of textural properties

The gel strength of Pickering emulsions constructed under different w and φ conditions was measured using a texture analyzer equipped with a P0.5 probe in GMIA Gelation mode. The probe speed was 1.0 mm/s, the testing distance was 4 mm, and the trigger force was 3.0 g (Kang et al., 2022).

Determination of rheological properties

The Pickering emulsion constructed by Hpd was measured using a HAAKE MARS Ⅲ rotational rheometer. The parallel-plate probe was P35TiL, and the plate spacing was approximately 1 mm. During the measurement, about 1 g of the emulsion was placed on the testing platform. After equilibrating at 25 °C for 60 s, amplitude sweep (f = 1 Hz, τ = 1–100 Pa), frequency sweep (τ = 5 Pa, f = 1–100 Hz) and shear rate sweep (γ = 0.1–10 s−1) were carried out (Froelich, 2025).

Statistical analysis

All obtained data represent the average of three determinations and were subjected to variance statistical analysis using IBM SPSS Statistics 20. Statistical differences were assessed using ANOVA and Duncan's test (n = 3, p < 0.05).

Results and discussion

Formation of Pickering emulsion gels

Determining the appropriate w and φ is crucial for preparing stable Pickering emulsions. A comparison of the Pickering capabilities of Hpt, Hpd, Neohpd, and Neohpddic is shown in Fig. 2. The upright bottles represented the state of the samples after 24 h of storage, while the inverted bottles showed the gel state after 2 h of inversion. Hpt failed to form a Pickering emulsion gel within the tested ranges of w and φ (Fig. 2A1 and 2A2). In emulsions with a fixed φ = 50 %, as the additive amount increased, the phase separation of emulsions stabilized by Hpd, Neohpd, and Neohpddic gradually decreased (Fig. 2B1, 2C1, and 2D1), indicating that increasing w significantly enhanced emulsification capability. At w = 2 %, the emulsifying ability of Hpd stabilized, with minimal phase separation observed. Upon inversion, the emulsion had already formed a gel, and the emulsified layer height was close to that at 4 %. In contrast, Neohpd and Neohpddic still exhibited noticeable phase separation at 4 % addition and failed to form a gel-like emulsion, suggesting that Hpd possessed superior emulsifying performance. Similarly, when investigating the effect of φ on emulsion formation, it was found that as φ increased from 20 % to 60 % at w = 2.0 % (Fig. 2B2, 2C2, and 2D2), phase separation in the prepared samples gradually decreased. At φ = 60 %, the emulsion stabilized by Hpd exhibited no phase separation and had formed an emulsion gel (Fig. 2B2). This is because higher φ values improved the emulsification performance of Hpd, Neohpd, and Neohpddic, consistent with the trend reported by Chevalier and Bolzinger (2013). However, when φ was further increased to 80 % and 90 %, all emulsions underwent demulsification (Fig. 2 2B2, 2C2, and 2D2), failing to form stable emulsions. This indicated that φ should not be excessively high. In summary, Hpd exhibited significantly higher emulsifying ability than Neohpd and Neohpddic. Appropriately increasing w and φ facilitated the formation of emulsion gels. Based on the results, Hpd, Neohpd, and Neohpddic all exhibited varying degrees of emulsifying properties, whereas Hpt was unable to form an emulsion due to the absence of sugar chains. This suggests that there may be a direct correlation between structure and particle emulsification properties.

Contact angle

The Pickering emulsifying ability of particles is closely related to their wettability. For particles to achieve sufficient interfacial adsorption efficiency, they must be partially wetted by both the continuous and dispersed phases while remaining insoluble in either phase. This enables spontaneous particle accumulation and self-assembly at the interface, forming a three-dimensional particle network (Colón-Quintana et al., 2023). A key parameter for evaluating the wettability of solid particles is the three-phase contact angle (θ), which reflects the wetting behavior of particles at the oil-water interface. Generally, when θ < 90°, particles exhibit strong hydrophilicity and are predominantly wetted by the aqueous phase. Due to the large contact area between particles and water, the interface curves toward the oil phase, favoring the formation of an oil-in-water (O/W) emulsion (Colón-Quintana et al., 2023). Conversely, when θ > 90°, particles are more lipophilic and preferentially migrate into the oil phase, causing the interface to curve toward the aqueous phase, leading to a water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion. When θ approaches 0° or 180°, emulsion formation is typically hindered. In this study, measurements revealed that the θ values for Hpt, Hpd, Neohpd, and Neohpddic at the MCT-water interface were 133.4° ± 0.2°, 51.35° ± 0.1°, 106.2° ± 0.1°, and 52.3° ± 1.2°, respectively (Fig. 3A). These results indicate that Hpt and Neohpd particles were more lipophilic, suggesting their potential to stabilize W/O emulsions, whereas Hpd and Neohpddic particles were more hydrophilic, favoring O/W emulsion formation. Notably, Neohpd's contact angle was closest to 90°, which, according to classical particle emulsification theory, should correlate with optimal emulsifying performance. However, this contradicts the findings in Fig. 2. A plausible explanation is that the contact angle-wettability theory is primarily based on idealized spherical SiO2 particles (Xiao et al., 2016), whereas edible particles derived from natural sources often exhibit irregular morphologies and heterogeneous surface properties. In this experiment, the differences in wettability among the four compounds were found to be closely associated with their molecular structures. Hpt, which lacks glycosidic groups, is dominated by its hydrophobic backbone, resulting in the largest contact angle and exhibiting pronounced lipophilicity. In contrast, Hpd possesses a highly flexible and strongly hydrophilic rhamnose group, which significantly reduces the contact angle, demonstrating strong hydrophilicity. Neohpd, despite containing a glycosidic group, exhibits a relatively rigid sugar moiety conformation. This rigidity diminishes its hydrophilic effect, yielding a contact angle close to 90° and imparting nearly balanced amphiphilicity. Similarly, the contact angle of Neohpddic is comparable to that of hesperidin, suggesting that its wettability is primarily governed by the rigid neohesperidin group. The ring-opening of the C-ring does not substantially alter its macroscopic hydrophilicity, as the flexibility of the hydrophobic backbone remains largely unchanged. These findings indicate that the presence or absence of sugar chains is likely the key structural factor regulating the wettability of these compounds. Thus, their actual emulsifying capacity and stability may require further experimental validation and cannot be reliably predicted by θ alone.

Interfacial tension

In conventional emulsion systems, the effectiveness of emulsifiers is often evaluated by their ability to reduce interfacial tension (Ravera et al., 2021). As shown in Fig. 3B, the interfacial tensions between saturated aqueous solutions of Hpt, Hpd, Neohpd, Neohpddic and MCT were 30.72 ± 0.36 mN/m, 30.63 ± 0.38 mN/m, 22.18 ± 0.63 mN/m, and 14.01 ± 0.42 mN/m, respectively. Compared with the interfacial tension between water and MCT (30.46 ± 0.39 mN/m), these results demonstrated that Neohpd and Neohpddic could significantly reduce interfacial tension, while Hpt and Hpd showed negligible reduction effects. The solubility of the four flavonoids was determined to be 0.0057 ± 0.0002 mg/mL, 0.0078 ± 0.0005 mg/mL, 0.1161 ± 0.0012 mg/L, and 0.0883 ± 0.0065 mg/L, respectively. For Hpd, despite its structural similarity to Neohpd and Neohpddic, its extremely low water solubility likely resulted in insufficient concentration in saturated aqueous solution to achieve measurable interfacial tension reduction. This evidence clearly indicated that assessing particle emulsifying capacity solely based on interfacial tension measurement was inadequate and potentially misleading. The evaluation should incorporate multiple characterization methods to obtain comprehensive understanding of emulsification performance.

Particle morphology

The surface roughness and shape of solid particles significantly influence interfacial behavior and emulsion stability. Polysaccharide- and protein-based emulsifying particles, along with modified silica particles commonly employed as inorganic emulsifiers, typically exhibit spherical structures. However, studies have demonstrated that rod-shaped particles exhibit superior emulsification efficiency compared to ellipsoidal particles (Li et al., 2018). Noon et al. (2020) postulated that the near-perfect crystalline rod-like morphology and high aspect ratio of quercetin particles contribute to their ability to stabilize Pickering emulsions. Similarly, Geng et al. (2021) reported that needle-shaped dihydromyricetin could effectively stabilize Pickering emulsion gels. SEM observation revealed distinct morphological differences among the particles (Fig. 4A). Hpt displayed a rough, irregular structure, whereas Hpd, Neohpd, and Neohpddic all exhibited well-defined rod-shaped morphologies with higher aspect ratios than spherical particles. Based on established theories, the morphology of these three particulate materials likely correlated with their capacity to stabilize Pickering emulsions.

Particle size

Particle size significantly impacts the adsorption kinetics at interfaces and the packing density, thereby influencing both the formation and stability of Pickering emulsions. Previous studies suggest that particles stabilizing micron-sized droplets should be at least one order of magnitude smaller than the emulsion droplets (da Laqua et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 4B, Hpt, Hpd, Neohpd, and Neohpddic all exhibited unimodal particle size distributions, with ranges of 4.49–39.47 μm, 0.96–7.02 μm, 4.35–60.02 μm, and 2.66–27.14 μm, respectively. Notably, Hpd demonstrated a substantially smaller particle size range compared to the other three samples. Research indicates that smaller particles exhibit faster adsorption kinetics at interfaces, enabling the formation of finer droplets and superior stability compared to larger particles (Doutsi et al., 2025). Xiao et al. (2016) proposed that smaller particles could form more densely packed interfacial layers, while Song et al. (2015) experimentally demonstrated that smaller starch particles (<10 μm) generated more stable emulsions with smaller droplet sizes than their larger counterparts (>10 μm). These findings collectively suggested that Hpd, with its smaller particle size, would exhibit enhanced emulsifying capacity and produce more stable emulsions-a conclusion consistent with the results presented in Section 3.1. This study demonstrated that the smallest particle size of Hpd was directly associated with its enhanced emulsifying performance. According to the Stokes-Einstein relation, smaller particles possess a higher diffusion coefficient in a suspension. Consequently, during emulsification, Hpd particles could swiftly migrate to the newly formed oil-water interface, adsorbing before droplet coalescence takes place to establish a densely packed protective layer. This rapid interfacial coverage was a crucial kinetic step in forming fine, stable emulsion droplets and ultimately constructing a three-dimensional gel network.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations can elucidate the dynamic behavior of oil-water interfaces in emulsion systems at the molecular scale, including the adsorption, orientation, and arrangement of surfactant molecules at the interface, as well as their interactions (e.g., hydrophobic effects, hydrogen bonding) with the oil and aqueous phases, thereby providing fundamental insights into the molecular basis of emulsion stability (Geng et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 5, analysis of the equilibrated process (100 ns) revealed that the constructed system rapidly achieved stable total energy, temperature, and density values within 30 ps of heating dynamics, indicating no significant perturbations during simulation. Post-equilibration density calculations (10 ns) systems yielded the following values: 958.6 kg/m3 (Hpt), 966.5 kg/m3 (Hpd), 965.9 kg/m3 (Neohpd), 966.2 kg/m3 (Neohpddic), and 951.6 kg/m3 (blank system). These density measurements, when compared with standard values for H2O and MCT, confirm appropriate insertion quantities of both components during system construction. Surfactants function by reducing interfacial tension, thereby decreasing interfacial adsorption energy. As presented in Section 3.3, we analyzed the interfacial tension of four flavonoids. While Hpt and Hpd showed limited effects in experimental measurements due to their low solubility, molecular simulations with equal molecular numbers forcibly inserted at the oil-water system revealed distinct behaviors (Table 1). The calculated interfacial tensions were 29.64 mN/m (Hpt) and 17.14 mN/m (Hpd), demonstrating Hpd's significant tension-reducing capability that contrasted with experimental observations. Notably, Hpt remained ineffective. Neohpd (21.75 mN/m) and Neohpddic (12.38 mN/m) both exhibited substantially lower values than the blank system (28.35 mN/m), corroborating well with actual experimental measurements. Fig. 6 illustrates the interfacial behavior analysis of these flavonoid molecules. All test flavonoids demonstrated migration capability toward the oil-water interface; however, Hpt exhibited distinct characteristics with significantly lower aggregation density at the interface compared to other flavonoids (Fig. 6A). Complementary hydrogen bond analysis revealed that Hpt formed substantially fewer hydrogen bonds than its counterparts (Fig. 6C), further corroborating its reduced intermolecular aggregation. The z-axis number density distribution analysis (Fig. 6B) showed similar centroid distributions for Hpd and Neohpd, while Neohpddic displayed bimodal peaks with a minor peak resembling those of Hpd and Neohpd. Notably, Hpt's centroid was preferentially located near the oil phase, exhibiting markedly different spatial distribution from the other three flavonoids, which likely explains its inability to effectively reduce interfacial tension. The MD simulations provided molecular-scale insight into the intrinsic interfacial tendency of Hpd. Although the simulations model individual molecules, the actual emulsifying particles were aggregates formed by the self-assembly of numerous molecules via intermolecular forces. The observed rapid migration of Hpd molecules to the interface and the formation of dense aggregates suggested that the macroscopic particles composed of Hpd inherently possessed strong interfacial affinity. Therefore, the migration and adsorption behavior of the particles were rooted in the intrinsic interfacial activity of their constituent molecules (Hpd). The formation of a robust molecular network at the interface via hydrogen bonding, as seen in the simulation, corresponded to the macroscopic observation of a stable interfacial particle layer and emulsion gel structure. In conclusion, the excellent emulsifying performance of Hpd could be attributed to a multi-scale process: its molecules have rapid interfacial migration and strong adsorption capabilities, which lead to the self-assembly of particles with appropriate wettability and small size. These particles, in turn, can efficiently diffuse and anchor at the interface, forming an interfacial film with high mechanical strength.

CLSM

To bridge the molecular-scale insights from MD simulations with the microstructure of the emulsion, CLSM was employed. CLSM is commonly employed to characterize the microstructure of emulsions. By utilizing fluorescent dyes to label solid particles and the oil phase, the distribution of laser-induced fluorescence can be used to determine emulsion types (Tao et al., 2021). Based on the nature of the continuous and dispersed phases, food emulsions can be classified into two fundamental types: water-in-oil (W/O) and oil-in-water (O/W). As shown in Fig. 7, the red fluorescence represented Nile Blue A-labeled Hpd particles, while the blue fluorescence indicated Nile Red-stained oil phase. The oil phase was clearly localized within the droplet interiors, with the particles forming densely packed layers at the droplet interfaces. This observation provided conclusive evidence that Hpd stabilized an O/W-type emulsion. Notably, the solid Hpd particles not only adsorbed at the oil droplet surfaces to function as emulsifiers, but also established a 3D network structure within the aqueous phase. This dual mechanism effectively prevented droplet coalescence and ensured the stability of the emulsion gel system. This remarkable microstructural integrity is interpreted as the direct macroscopic manifestation of the strong intermolecular interactions and efficient interfacial packing propensity of Hpd, as revealed by the MD simulations.

Droplet size

Droplet size, as a crucial microscopic characteristic of emulsions, significantly influences both emulsion stability and gel strength. The droplet size in Pickering emulsions is primarily depend on the oil phase volume fraction and the emulsifier concentration in the system. Xu et al. (2018) reported that droplet size could be readily modulated by varying ovalbumin concentration, while Yang et al. (2018) achieved smaller droplet sizes and enhanced gel hardness in high internal phase Pickering emulsions by adjusting the loading of acid-hydrolyzed starch nanocrystals. As evidenced by Table 2, Hpd-stabilized emulsions exhibited micron-scale droplet sizes, with a progressive decline in droplet diameter observed with increasing particle concentration, indicating a clear dose-dependent relationship. Conversely, at w = 3 %, increasing φ (from 20 % to 70 %) resulted in larger droplet diameters. This phenomenon can be attributed to the reduced aqueous phase volume at higher oil fractions, where the fixed amount of Hpd became insufficient for complete interfacial coverage, leading to droplet coalescence and subsequent size increase. Remarkably, this tighter droplet packing simultaneously enhanced the gel network strength, as corroborated by Fig. 7. Similar findings were reported by Geng et al. (2023) in proanthocyanidin particle-stabilized Pickering emulsion gels. In summary, increasing particle concentration reduces droplet size, while elevating oil phase fraction produced the opposite effect. These systematic variations demonstrate the delicate balance between particle availability and oil-water interfacial area in determining Pickering emulsion microstructure.

Gel strength

The strength of emulsion gels not only reflects their structure but also critically determines their processing characteristics and potential applications (Oppong et al., 2024). This study systematically evaluated the effects of w and φ on gel strength. As demonstrated in Fig. 8A, increasing w significantly enhanced the gel strength (p < 0.05). This improvement can be attributed to greater particle participation in interfacial film formation at higher concentrations, leading to progressively smaller droplet sizes. The resultant finer oil droplets facilitate tighter packing within the emulsion matrix, thereby substantially reinforcing the gel structure, which was consistent with the report of Song et al. (2022). Furthermore, elevating φ maximized particle-oil interactions, promoting denser droplet packing and consequently strengthening the gel network (Fig. 8B), which aligned with the report of Remanan and Zhu (2023) on succinylated quinoa starch-stabilized emulsions. These results collectively demonstrate that strategic increases in both w and φ can effectively enhance emulsion gel strength. The dual optimization of oil phase fraction and particle concentration represents a viable approach for tailoring the mechanical performance of Pickering emulsion gels for specific food applications.

Rheological properties

Rheological properties serve as fundamental indicators for characterizing food processing behavior and stability, playing a pivotal role in food manufacturing (Sözeri et al., 2024). In Pickering emulsions, the emulsifier particles can form a rigid layer exhibiting surface elasticity (Mason et al., 1995). The elastic storage modulus (G′) arises from interfacial energy storage through deformation of droplet interfaces under compression (Arditty et al., 2004). During rheological measurements, the mechanical response of emulsion gels reflects the microstructure of these rigid layers. In this study, the rheological properties of gels were evaluated through oscillatory stress sweeps, frequency sweeps, and shear rate sweeps. Strain sweep results (Fig. 9A) revealed that all samples initially maintained relatively constant G′ and G″ values with G'>G″, confirming their elastic gel-like behavior rather than viscous liquid characteristics (Dickinson, 2012). However, excessive shear stress caused significant structural breakdown, evidenced by rapid decreases in both moduli. Notably, increasing both w and φ enhanced the elastic modulus and storage modulus, indicating strengthened gel network structures-a finding consistent with the finding of Song et al. (2015) about OSA-modified rice starch. Frequency sweep measurements (0.1–100 Hz range, Fig. 9B) demonstrated G′ consistently exceeding G″, exhibiting typical Pickering emulsion rheological signatures with dominant elastic behavior. The gel-like properties of the emulsions were definitively characterized through frequency sweep measurements. A hallmark of a true gel is the dominance of the storage modulus (G′) over the loss modulus (G″) across a wide frequency range, coupled with minimal frequency dependence for both moduli. This behavior, clearly illustrated in Fig. 8B, distinguishes the system from a viscoelastic solution, which typically exhibits pronounced frequency dependence and a crossover point where G″ surpasses G′ at lower frequencies. The observed frequency-independent, solid-like response provided compelling evidence for the formation of a permanent elastic gel network stabilized by Hpd particles, rather than a transient structure governed by relaxation phenomena. Interestingly, both moduli showed initial increased during the early shear phase, differing slightly from stress sweep patterns. Shear rate dependence studies (Fig. 9C) revealed all emulsions displayed pseudoplastic shear-thinning behavior (Sang et al., 2018), with apparent viscosity initially increasing then decreasing at higher rates. Higher w or φ values yielded greater viscosities at equivalent shear rates. These results collectively demonstrate that optimizing w and φ parameters can enhance emulsion gel viscosity, effectively preventing droplet rupture and coalescence during processing.

Conclusions

Among the four citrus flavonoid compounds (Hpt, Hpd, Neohpd, and Neohpddic), Hpd demonstrated the most outstanding Pickering emulsifying performance. However, due to its relatively low water solubility, conventional contact angle and interfacial tension measurements failed to adequately explain its emulsifying mechanism. Further particle characterization revealed that Hpd particles exhibited a rod-like morphology with the smallest particle size, a characteristic that facilitated their efficient adsorption and stabilization at the interface. Molecular dynamics simulation further confirmed that Hpd could significantly reduce interfacial tension and rapidly migrate to the oil-water interface, forming molecular aggregates via hydrogen bonding. The study also found that Hpd could fabricate O/W Pickering emulsion gels when φ ≥ 50 % and w ≥ 2 %. With increasing w and φ values, the oil droplets adopted a more densely packed arrangement, leading to significantly enhanced mechanical properties of the emulsion gel and consequent improvement in system stability. The results not only provide theoretical foundations for the value-added utilization of citrus byproducts but also offer valuable references for developing food-grade polyphenol-based Pickering emulsion gels.
