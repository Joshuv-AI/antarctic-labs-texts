A Comprehensive Polish Medical Speech Dataset for Enhancing Automatic Medical Dictation
Authors: Andrzej Czyżewski, Sebastian Cygert, Karolina Marciniuk, Maciej Szczodrak, Arkadiusz Harasimiuk, Piotr Odya, Marina Galanina, Piotr Szczuko, Bożena Kostek, Beata Graff, Dariusz Szplit, Mariusz Budzisz, Krzysztof Narkiewicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPre-trained models have become widely adopted for their strong zero-shot performance, often minimizing the need for task-specific data. However, specialized domains like medical speech recognition still benefit from tailored datasets. We present ADMEDVOICE, a novel Polish medical speech dataset, collected using a high-quality text corpus and diverse recording conditions to reflect real-world scenarios. The dataset includes domain-specific vocabulary such as drug names and illnesses, with nearly 15 hours of audio from 28 speakers, including noisy environments. Additionally, we release two enhanced versions: one anonymized for privacy-sensitive use and another synthetic version created via text-to-speech, totaling over 83 hours and nearly 50,000 samples. Evaluating the Whisper model, we observe a 24.03 WER on our test set. Fine-tuning with human recordings reduces WER to 15.47, and incorporating anonymized and synthetic data further lowers it to 13.91. We open-source the dataset, fine-tuned model, and code on Kaggle to support continued research in medical speech recognition.Subject terms: Health policy, Health services

Introduction

The current generation of machine learning models, based on artificial neural networks, has recently achieved remarkable success in a wide range of domains, including image and speech recognition, as well as text generation. Among these, automatic speech recognition (ASR) has seen notable advances, particularly through the use of transformer-based models1, such as Whisper2, which employs an encoder-decoder transformer architecture with self-attention mechanisms to capture long-range dependencies in sequential data effectively. Although Whisper may occasionally introduce transcription errors 3, hence its limitations, it remains a practical and widely-used tool for converting spoken language into text. Importantly, It is a conditional text-generating model rather than a fully generative one, as its outputs are conditioned on the audio input it receives.

Despite the progress in ASR, a critical limitation of neural network models remains their reliance on training data that closely resembles test-time conditions. When deployed in new or challenging environments, these models often fail to generalize effectively4,5, raising serious concerns about their real-world reliability. One widely adopted strategy to mitigate this issue is transfer learning, where a pre-trained model is fine-tuned on a downstream task6. The success of this approach is highly dependent on the similarity between the source and target domains. For low-resource languages or specialized domains, such as medicine, it often requires data augmentation techniques or cross-lingual transfer learning from high-resource languages2,7,8.

Recent advancements in automatic speech recognition (ASR) have stimulated a growing interest in developing robust systems tailored for medical-domain applications, particularly under low-resource and acoustically challenging conditions. A number of recent studies have explored this problem space from complementary angles, ranging from hybrid architectures and multilingual modeling to real-time streaming capabilities and large-scale pre-training. The following papers exemplify these diverse strategies, highlighting the ongoing efforts to enhance ASR performance in medical settings, especially in scenarios involving telephony, multilingual interactions, and noisy clinical environments. The paper “Development of Hybrid ASR Systems for Low Resource Medical Domain Conversational Telephone Speech” by Lüscher et al. (2023)9, performed under the HYKIST project, focuses on building automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems for multilingual medical-domain telephone conversations, specifically for patient-physician interactions involving German, Arabic, Vietnamese, and Ukrainian speakers. A conversation involves three persons: the patient, the physician, and the interpreter. The study demonstrates that effective ASR systems for low-resource medical-domain telephone speech can be developed by combining targeted data collection, hybrid model architectures, and linguistic adaptations, such as dialect mapping. These approaches mitigate the impact of scarce in-domain data, achieving promising WER and CER results, particularly for challenging languages like Arabic (Arabic Conformer models reaching 17.2% Character Error Rate (CER) on development and 23.3% on test sets), and lay the groundwork for scalable multilingual ASR systems in critical medical contexts. It is worth noting that this study addresses the limitations of speaker diversity, varying acoustic conditions, and telephony bandwidth constraints by utilizing additional monolingual text data and dialect-specific datasets. The paper also highlights the potential of unsupervised domain adaptation and multilingual decoders to further improve performance, suggesting that even with limited data, iterative refinement and cross-lingual techniques can yield robust systems.

A paper by Edwards et al.10 regards a medical-domain automatic speech recognition (ASR) system trained on over 270 hours of medical speech and 30 million tokens of clinical text, achieving performance comparable to human transcriptionists. However, it is particularly interesting in the context of addressing acoustic challenges, such as background noise and the use of diverse recording devices. The system mitigates these issues through careful waveform normalization, including RMS scaling to 70 dB, clipping near-silent sections, and power normalization techniques, ensuring robust feature extraction. Moreover, medical speech involves rapid dictation, a lack of clear sentence boundaries, and complex domain-specific terminology, which creates another challenge to be addressed. This system efficiently achieves a lower WER and can handle multiple medical subspecialties. Overall, the system achieves near-human performance in medical ASR, which marks a significant advancement in ASR.

In cases where medical data are more readily available, as shown by Chiu et al. (2017)11, automatic speech recognition (ASR) for transcribing doctor-patient conversations may become more effective. In the study, a large dataset of 14,000 hours of clinical audio, comprising 90,000 single-channel recordings of doctor-patient conversations, was utilized. The study compares Connectionist Temporal Classification (CTC) and Listen, Attend, and Spell (LAS) models. The LAS model achieved a Word Error Rate (WER) of 15.6%, outperforming the CTC model’s WER of 20.1% on the test set. Moreover, LAS models were more robust to noisy transcripts and did not require an external language model. To capture critical medical phrases (e.g., symptoms, lab results), improved data alignment techniques were employed; however, challenges remain, including casual conversational errors and issues with audio quality.

When it comes to real-time ASR systems, another issue that may arise is non-monotonic alignments in standard RNNNT (Recurrent Neural Network Transducer) models, leading to undesirable behavior in streaming ASR, such as outputting multiple labels at a single time step. The paper by Tripathi et al.12 introduces a monotonic version of the Recurrent Neural Network Transducer (RNNNT) for automatic speech recognition (ASR), enforcing strictly monotonic alignments. This is done by using a modified loss function that sums probabilities only for monotonic paths in the forward-backward algorithm. Such an approach ensures smooth streaming recognition with minimal impact on WER, particularly effective for real-time ASR applications. Another paper that addresses the limited data in the medical telephony domain is a study by Vieting et al. (2023)13, which explores the use of large pre-trained models for automatic speech recognition (ASR) in a low-resource, medical-domain conversational telephony speech corpus in Vietnamese and German. The study utilized the publicly available XLSR-53 model, pre-trained on 50,000 hours of multilingual speech, as an initialization for custom pre-training. Multilingual pre-training on a combined Arabic, German, and Vietnamese dataset (VGA) achieved significant WER reductions of 14% for Vietnamese and 11% for German relative to monolingual baselines, demonstrating that multilingual data can compensate for limited target language resources by providing richer speech representations. Overall, this research indicates that large pre-trained models, such as XLSR-53, can be efficiently adapted for low-resource ASR in the medical telephony domain through unsupervised pre-training, model size reduction, and targeted fine-tuning.

Despite significant progress in the exploration of medical systems, many areas remain underdeveloped. In particular, automatic speech recognition (ASR) systems hold considerable promise for supporting clinicians in the creation of high-quality electronic health records. This could include generating structured descriptions of medical histories, automatically transcribing doctor-patient conversations, and even supporting decision-making by suggesting treatments. ASR could also be used in this area to improve the daily work of doctors by, for example, assisting them in creating high-quality electronic health documentation, e.g., by automatically filling in disease charts in interactive mode during medical history and prescribing treatment as required. However, implementing such solutions in clinical environments presents a host of unique challenges: recordings are often made in noisy settings, the language used frequently contains technical vocabulary, including medication names, disease terms, and other highly specialized expressions. Additionally, the complexity and variability of medical documentation styles across institutions and individual practitioners make standardization and precision difficult to achieve.

These challenges are especially acute in the Polish medical domain, where both linguistic structure and domain-specific content introduce additional complications. Medical texts, such as hospital discharge documents, employ highly specialized terminology that deviates significantly from general Polish language usage. Terms can appear as single words (e.g., astma) or multiword expressions (e.g., przewlekła obturacyjna choroba płuc), and are often context-dependent, influenced by ward-specific jargon or specialty conventions. A major issue is the heavy and inconsistent use of acronyms, which are prevalent throughout Polish clinical documentation. These acronyms can be universal (e.g., RTG for X-ray, godz. for hour) or domain-specific (e.g., CRP for C-reactive protein), with meanings that may vary across medical specialties such as surgery or pediatrics. Their pronunciation is also variable: some are spoken as words, others are spelled out letter by letter, and their written forms may appear as abbreviations or full expansions. This variability introduces ambiguity that is challenging for both human readers and natural language processing (NLP) systems. While exact error rates from acronym misinterpretation are difficult to quantify, the reliance on contextual clues for disambiguation suggests that such errors are frequent in automated systems14,15.

Moreover, the translation of Polish medical terms into other languages is often hampered by the lack of controlled vocabulary equivalents, such as those found in MeSH (Medical Subject Headings) (https://www.nlm.nih.gov/mesh/meshhome.html). Many Polish clinical terms are not covered by the Polish MeSH, complicating interoperability and cross-lingual NLP applications. Additional linguistic issues also arise, including polysemy (one term with multiple meanings depending on context), synonymy (multiple terms for the same concept), and inflectional variation due to the Polish’s complex morphological structure. These factors complicate core NLP tasks such as term extraction, especially when dealing with improperly constructed noun phrases or filtering variants of the same term. Altogether, these challenges reflect a broader issue: Polish clinical terminologies are underdeveloped in comparison to their English counterparts. Building robust Polish-language medical NLP tools often requires the manual or semi-automated development of domain-specific lexicons.

Addressing these issues requires not only technical advancements but also the collection and preparation of real-world clinical recordings that reflect day-to-day usage across medical subdomains. Only through such efforts can NLP tools, especially ASR-based solutions, be effectively adapted for use in the Polish medical context.

Nevertheless, there are only a few publicly available medical speech corpora. PriMock5716 is a set of mock conversations between patients and doctors (i.e., consultations with audio recordings, manual transcriptions, clinician notes, reference notes, and corresponding real consultation notes). It addresses the critical need for standardized benchmarks in clinical NLP research, bypassing privacy constraints and enabling progress in automated transcription and summarization tasks. Recordings were conducted in English with American and British accents. The comprehensive assessment comprises a total of 57 individual consultations, each spanning a duration of 8 hours, 38 minutes, and 6 seconds.

The AfriSpeech-200 dataset17, as detailed in the paper by Olatunji et al. (2023), is a manually curated, open-access dataset designed to enhance automatic speech recognition (ASR) for African-accented English in both clinical and general domains. The data were recorded by means of a crowd-sourcing online portal, it contains 200.7 hours of speech from 2,463 unique speakers across 120 African accents from 13 countries. It includes 67,577 audio clips (39,960 clinical, 27,617 general domain), with 173.4 hours in the training set, 8.74 hours in the development set, and 18.77 hours in the test set and covers clinical (e.g., medical terms, diseases, medications) and general domain content, with 30% of the corpus derived from templated sentences augmented with African names, cities, and medical terms. It is worth noting that the dataset utilizes synthetic or curated content.

An example of a recording database for the Burmese language is myMediCon18.The collection under consideration comprises sentences extracted from a select medical publication, totaling 14,592. A subsequent analysis revealed that less than 7% of the vocabulary was unique, although it remains unclear if this figure encompasses medical terminology from the ICD-9 dictionary, for instance. Recordings were made using professional audio equipment and personal devices, including smartphones and other mobile devices. The corpus under consideration comprises 11 speakers and contains a total of nearly 94 hours of recordings, ranging from 1.5 to 35 seconds in duration from 14,592 medical sentences, comprising 239,099 words and 15,431 unique words. To avoid ethical and privacy issues, text sourced from the clinical assessment handbook, translated into Burmese by three university students, covering doctor-patient consultations, diseases, medicines, and treatment methodologies. It is not yet publicly available.

MultiMed19, introduced by Le-Duc et al. (2024), is a dataset for multilingual medical ASR, providing large, publicly available resources in terms of duration and diversity. It covers five languages: English, Vietnamese, French, German, and Mandarin Chinese, and contains 150 hours of audio recordings. They vary across 16 accents, six roles (e.g., lecturer, doctor, patient, host, podcaster, broadcaster), and 10 different conditions, ensuring varied acoustic environments. The data are sourced from real-world medical conversations published by professional medical channels on YouTube, and segments potentially revealing speaker identities were anonymized. Additionally, the paper serves as a valuable source of information on the availability of recently released medical speech datasets. An available corpus for Vietnamese, VietMed20, a part of the MultiMed19, contains three subsets, i.e., 16 hours of tagged medical speech, 1000 hours of untagged medical speech, and 12000 hours of untagged colloquial speech. The data were collected from YouTube by crawling the audio content, with a focus on medical topics. This means that there is no control over the content (which may include misleading or incorrect medical information), and the recording conditions may be very different from those observed in a real-world scenario.

The BioClinical ModernBERT21, introduced by Sounack et al. (2024), is a comprehensive corpus that supports long-context processing (up to 8,192 tokens), designed for pre-training a transformer-based encoder tailored for biomedical and clinical Natural Language Processing (NLP) tasks. In total, it contains 53.08 billion tokens, making this dataset the largest biomedical and clinical corpus used for an encoder. The data are sourced from PubMed abstracts and PMC full-text articles, as well as 20 clinical datasets, from 30 institutions, domains, and regions. It is worth noting that BioClinical ModernBERT is a long-context encoder specifically designed for clinical and biomedical text. Models (base and large), training checkpoints, and code are publicly available on Hugging Face and GitHub.

Another frequently cited collection of recordings is MEDISCO. MEDISCO22 is an English-Indonesian collection of recordings of 360 phrases (covering 731 strictly medical terms). The corpus was read by 13 speakers (5 female, 6 male), who recorded about 10 hours. The text database was created from medical portals and regular newspaper articles. Unfortunately, it was not possible to locate an actual available copy of this dataset.

Mooney23 (https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/paultimothymooney/medical-speech-transcription-and-intent), another corpus for English, contains 8.5 hours of recordings describing disease symptoms from the patient’s point of view, however, these data lack the required audio and labeling quality.

The most related to our work is the dataset of simulated patient-physician medical interviews focusing on respiratory cases24, which comprises 272 recordings totaling over 55 hours. Similar to ours, they focus on obtaining high-quality data through real medical transcriptions. In our work, we go a step further and record data also in various real-world conditions (such as treatment rooms that were affected by external noise) and for many medical categories.

As a result, there is a need to develop high-quality datasets for ASR systems in the medical domain that will be recorded in real-world conditions. To address these challenges, we focus on the development of a dedicated dataset for medical speech recognition in the Polish language, which is a low-resource language in the context of ASR. The corpus was designed to cover various medical scenarios across multiple specialties, including radiology, cardiology, and hospital emergency departments, with specialized vocabulary (e.g., drug names, procedure descriptions, and recommendations) and diverse acoustic conditions. To ensure robustness, recordings were made in both controlled environments (e.g., quiet offices) and challenging conditions (e.g., noisy operating rooms and overlapping speech).

To address privacy concerns and explore the potential of synthetic data, we developed two additional versions of the above natural voice dataset. The first is the anonymized version of the above dataset, using the SALT tool25, which removes personally identifiable information. The second version is a synthetic speech corpus generated using a modern deep learning-based speech synthesis tool, SpeechGen26. These additional datasets enable us to assess the potential of utilizing anonymized or synthetic datasets for training ASR models, including scenarios where original recordings cannot be used due to privacy regulations.

To validate the utility of the collected data, we perform a study by fine-tuning the pre-trained Whisper model2. While the pre-trained Whisper achieved Word Error Rate (WER) of 24.03 on the test set, fine-tuning the model with human recordings allowed for reduction of the WER to 15.47 and further, incorporating anonymized and synthetic versions during training permitted the WER 13.91.

Methods

Consent to share data

All speakers who participated in the recordings signed an informed consent form, allowing their participation in voice recordings and the publication of both natural and anonymized voice recordings. Moreover, a procedure was implemented in which medical texts were anonymized and prepared for reading in the form of single sentences, which were then organized into randomized reading sets. The absence of widely accessible databases of medical speech recordings in Polish signifies a significant impediment to the advancement of Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) systems. The availability of these collections is, therefore, imperative for promoting the continued development of ASR technologies. This consent explicitly grants permission for their recordings to be used in creating and improving these tools while ensuring the process respects the privacy and rights of all individuals involved. This work is a part of the broader ADMEDVOICE project, which the Bioethical Commission for Scientific Research has favorably evaluated at the Gdańsk Medical University (Resolution no. KB/508/2023, dated September 15, 2023, updated July 2025).

Text corpus preparation

The record acquisition stage was preceded by creating a text corpus. For this purpose, medical data were obtained from the Gdańsk University Clinical Center. The following six medical categories were used to define the vocabulary for the recordings: cardiology, oncology, head and neck radiology, the resuscitation protocol, recommendations, and test referrals.

The texts consisted of actual descriptions of the studied patients, recorded during medical consultations and based on examination results. This approach ensured optimal coverage of the medical terminology dictionary but required multi-stage data processing. The first step involved manually removing sensitive data that could identify patients, ensuring accuracy. Next, the cleaned descriptions were divided into smaller units, i.e., phrases, ensuring optimal record file lengths. To optimize the recording, it was assumed that reading phrases should not take more than 30 seconds. The next step was text normalization, which, in addition to basic correction of whitespace characters or correct conversion, was carried out separately for data prepared for reading by speakers and separately for synthetic speech.

While constructing the text dataset, we utilized both the ICD-9 dictionary and our own custom dictionary of terms and abbreviations, which were derived from actual medical documents. In the resulting database, 12.3% of the words are strictly medical terms, while the remaining entries describe changes in performed activities.

Medical descriptions created during visits frequently included commonly used abbreviations, such as ‘wym.’ (meaning dimensions) and acronyms for disease entities, such as OBS (Obturacyjny Bezdech Senny - Obstructive Sleep Apnea). Additionally, typographical errors often occur due to time constraints. When read by humans, expansions and omissions of errors naturally occur, a phenomenon linked to the idiosyncrasies of human reading (as noted by Rayner et al.,27, where whole words are processed, and changes in word order do not significantly hinder comprehension). However, the specificity of these abbreviations required adjustments for optimal processing by text to speech models28–30. In ’Synthetic data section generation’ we give more details on how the text corpus was adjusted to be processed by speech synthesis tools.

Another challenge arises from the inconsistent use of expansions and abbreviations in pronunciation. Some medical practitioners use abbreviations in speech, such as ‘In the CD,’ while others consistently expand them when speaking with patients, as in ‘In the Color Doppler option.’ Consequently, the text database contains multiple variants, ensuring comprehensive documentation of different designations for a specific disease entity. These variations include full names in Polish and English, as well as complete Latin and Polish abbreviations. Examples are given in Table 1.

During the recordings, it was noted that fully expanding metric unit abbreviations led to erroneous reading or stammering. For instance, writing 10 × 10 mm instead of 10 by 10 millimeters was more efficient. It is important to note that the meanings of abbreviations often varied according to the medical category31. To address this, recordings sessions were conducted within medical categories, and the first phrase to appear contained full abbreviation expansions. Recorders were instructed to read the abbreviation if it was present in the notation for the remaining phrases.

The largest number of phrases referred to oncology (666), while the fewest phrases were related to emergency procedures (253). Emergency medicine often relies on action-oriented language focused on immediate intervention, which prioritizes speed and clarity over details, resulting in a narrower range of phrases. Audio registration

Natural voice recordings

The recordings were carried out taking into account several use scenarios - high quality recordings and recordings in the target places of use, i.e. doctors’ offices or treatment rooms. The recordings were carried out from June 2023 through December 2024 using various settings to ensure a comprehensive data. Recording locations included Gdańsk University Clinical Centre (UCC) treatment rooms (Fig. 1a), Gdańsk University of Technology (GUT) recording studios (Fig. 1b) and various environments of medical professionals uploaded via a deployed web service.

Examples of recording environments used in the study. (a) A typical doctor’s office representing real-world clinical setting. (b) The controlled acoustic environment of the Gdańsk University of Technology recording studio.

The first phase of the recordings took place in the studio rooms of Gdańsk University of Technology. They were recorded using a sampling rate of 48kHz and a bit depth of 24-bit. These rooms had a reverberation time (RT60) ranging from 0.04 to 0.18 seconds and a speech transmission index (STI) above 0.9, indicating excellent speech intelligibility. Room parameters were measured indirectly according to ISO 3382-2:2010 (measurement of room acoustic parameters) and IEC 60268-16 (objective rating of speech intelligibility by speech transmission index). The recordings were captured using professional equipment, including a Zoom F8 recorder, Zoom H4nPro, and Zoom UAC-8 audio interface, along with several medium- and high-end studio microphones, such as the Sennheiser MKH 416, Neumann TLM 103, Behringer B-5, Sennheiser ME 2, and Shure SM58. At least two microphones were used simultaneously during each recording session. Detailed metadata, including recording equipment configurations, is provided in the documentation accompanying the dataset.

The next phase of recordings took place at the treatment rooms at Gdańsk University Clinical Centre (UCC). Medical rooms typically have longer reverberation times, ranging from 0.41 to 0.54 seconds, with an average Speech Transmission Index (STI) of 0.75, indicating a quality level between good and excellent. The recording environment was affected by external noise, such as hallway activity and internal interference from medical equipment, including monitors and ventilators. Additionally, random interferences occurred from the outside environment and the medical apparatus inside the room. The recordings were carried out synchronously in several offices, ensuring consistent conditions. The previously described audio equipment was used along with a Zoom F2-BL recorder, which captures audio with 32-bit float quantization. Additionally, three ambisonic recording solutions were utilized: Rode NT-SF-1, Zylia ZM1, and Zoom H3. For this part of the work, the recordings were converted to mono, optimized for sound coming from the speaker’s direction. In all cases, audio was recorded using a Lavalier-type tie microphone (Sennheiser ME 2 or Zoom LMF-2). Multi-track recording with different types and settings of microphones allows the effectiveness of different solutions to be tested, and will ultimately allow the development of recommendations for ASR systems for hospitals.

To support recordings effort, a special portal was developed to facilitate the preparation and management of additional recordings, complementing those made in controlled conditions. This portal enabled remote recordings, allowing recruited medical personnel to access pre-prepared medical phrases, record speech, and listen to their recordings (Fig. 2). We have included a subset of anonymized recordings collected through this portal in our corpus. The portal is available online at https://multimed.org/admedvoice (the tool operation needs familiarity with the Polish language).

Anonymization

To address privacy concerns and explore the potential of using synthetic data for improving recognition capabilities, we have prepared anonymized version of the recordings. This process involved modifying the original audio files to remove identifiable speaker characteristics while preserving speech naturalness.

We tested the following anonymization algorithms: Adobe Audition CS633, Lightweight Speaker Anonymization34, SA-Toolkit35, and SALT25. After initial experiments, their effectiveness was evaluated using listening tests and the prediction algorithm36, which provides objective metrics and is used within the community37,38. NISQA is a deep learning-based framework that assesses speech quality and separately evaluates its naturalness in the synthetic voices. It was developed using a corpus of 14,000 MOS-rated speech recordings. Since the model was trained on English recordings, some disparities were observed in subjective ratings for Polish speech. However, we confirmed its usefulness for our project. Based on these evaluations, we selected the SALT library’s algorithms due to their ability to preserve high speech quality, intelligibility, and naturalness. Unlike the SA-toolkit system, which introduced an English accent, SALT preserved Polish speech characteristics.

The anonymization process utilized the WavLM-Large model of the SALT system. This model consists of six layers with parameter vectors of length 1024. Preliminary tests confirmed that using the full model resulted in superior output quality. In the first stage of anonymization, a set of reference speakers—referred to as a reference pool—was prepared. 41 speakers (19 women and 22 men) were selected, each contributing between 14 and 147 recordings, with an average of 69 per speaker. The extracted features of these speakers were then used for anonymization, shaping the voice timbre of the anonymized utterances. For each anonymized file, a unique set of parameters was chosen, incorporating features from four randomly selected reference speakers. The weight assigned to each reference speaker was also randomized, determining their influence on the final voice timbre. This approach ensured a greater variety of anonymized recordings.

The SALT system requires monophonic files with 16-bit quantization and a sampling rate of 16 kHz. If the sound files were recorded with other parameters, automatic conversion took place. However, recordings utilizing 32-float quantization required prior conversion in external tools. For files with a sampling rate of 48 kHz, SALT restored the original sampling rate after anonymization.

Synthetic data generation

Finally, we have prepared another corpus using text-to-speech tools. We have used the SpeechGen system26, which is characterized by an excellent ability to generate natural and fluent speech. The SpeechGen speech synthesizer offers 19 voices for the Polish language with various configuration options. Key parameters include speech speed, voice pitch, emotional coloring (options: good, angry, neutral) and bitrate. For our experiment, we used the following settings: default speech speed, neutral emotional coloring, and 48 kHz sample rate. Synthetic voice audio samples were obtained using sentences from selected medical categories.

It is important to note that there exist some challenges in the text corpus which affects synthesis process. The sentences contain specific medical terms, often with elements of other languages, such as Latin. For example, the abbreviation “LV” was initially interpreted by the synthesizer as the Roman numeral for “fifteen”, whereas in a medical context, it should be pronounced as individual letters ("L-V”). In addition, the texts contain abbreviations that specialists pronounce differently, not just as straightforward spellings. Further, we have observed that the SpeechGen synthesizer struggled with correctly reading digits in Polish due to incorrect numeral inflection. The numerals were read as ordinal numbers instead of as cardinal numbers. For example, “32” should be correctly read as “thirty-two,” but not “thirty-second” as synthesizer algorithm read. To address this, a multi-stage preprocessing pipeline was implemented. Sentences without numerical content or abbreviations were passed directly to SpeechGen. For sentences containing digits, these were converted into fully spelled-out numbers, ensuring consistency with the inflection used in the rest of the sentence. Regarding abbreviations, tags were inserted into text to force unambiguous pronunciation by ASR algorithms. Moreover, abbreviations were tagged using Speech Synthesis Markup Language (SSML) to enforce correct pronunciation. Sample sentences that include abbreviations before (1) and after (2) text preprocessing with insertion of SSML “say-as” tag are presented below.

1. “Piersi kontroluje w WCO w Gdańsku" (in English “She examines her breasts at WCO in Gdańsk”)

2. “Piersi kontroluje w <say-as interpret-as=""characters""> WCO </say-as> w Gdańsku”

Audio file quality and annotation

We have implemented an audio quality control protocol to ensure the removal of low-quality samples from the dataset. As previously mentioned, the audio database was constructed on several recording tools, including web-based systems. Depending on the data source, various quality control operations were performed to create a uniform corpus.

The quality control process involved two key stages. First, controllers manually assessed the correctness of the speakers’ reading of the displayed content. The second step was to evaluate the quality of the recordings using a modified Mean Opinion Score (MOS) scale and calculate its value with the NISQA model36 (the same we have used for assessing anonymized recordings). The subjective grading scale, ranging from 5 to 0, is a modification of the typical MOS scale, with an additional score of 0 indicating a mismatch between the text being read and the actual recording. This usually occurred due to a single word being misread, meaning the database could also be used in future research to correct transcriptions and make them more robust to speaker disfluencies. Figure 3 shows the distribution of recording quality across different recording locations. The most considerable variability in quality was observed in recordings from the online service, reflecting the challenges of remote data collection in uncontrolled environments. In contrast, the highest quality recordings were obtained in the recording studios, which feature controlled acoustic conditions.

Distribution of recording quality in the shared corpus (“unknown” - data coming from the online system).

Recordings made in professional studio and office environments under supervision were processed further. The recordings were manually trimmed to align with the provided text, and typical corrections for dynamic range and equalization (EQ) were applied to the voice recordings. Subsequently, the files underwent true peak RMS normalization, which reduced the volume by -6 dB. Recordings made via the online system did not undergo additional processing, as they exhibited distinct volume fluctuations. These audio files frequently contained acoustic interferences and digital distortions, such as auditory clicks and glitches.

Typically, recordings are made in doctors’ offices, often with a Mean Opinion Score (MOS) below 3.5. The use of speech synthesis during the recording acquisition process has been shown to enhance the overall quality of the available dataset. This approach allows for the collection of recordings free from acoustic distortions such as background noise or room reverberation, effectively serving as a form of data augmentation. However, as with recordings produced through anonymization, synthesized speech may exhibit typical digital artifacts or sound unnatural. At present, there is no robust metric capable of reliably comparing the quality of recordings from natural and synthetic sources.

Technical Validation

Finally, we present the validation results, which include training the model on the three datasets that were previously described. The experiment was run on a single NVIDIA A100 GPU using the PLGrid supercomputing infrastructure of Poland’s leading academic high-performance computing (HPC) center.39,40.

The datasets used for training and evaluation consisted of natural, synthetic, and anonymized voices. Preprocessing of the data, specific to the training pipeline, included two steps. First, following technical limitations of the Whisper model2, we have removed recordings whose duration was over 30 seconds. In the second stage of preprocessing, to improve data reading performance during the training, all audio files were resampled to 16 kHz. Table 4 presents the datasets for fine-tuning the Whisper-medium model. The datasets were split for training and testing such that each speaker belonged only to one of those sets. A validation set was used to select a model for evaluation on test data. On average, unique phrases were observed 4.6 times across the test set.

To validate the usefulness of the created data, we have performed four fine-tunings, using 1) natural recordings, 2) anonymized human voices, 3) synthetic voices, and 4) a combination of all of the data. As an ASR model, we used the Whisper-medium model, which contains 769M parameters. We have trained the model for a maximum of 10,000 steps using a batch size of 64 and a learning rate equal to 1e-5. The checkpoint corresponding to the lowest validation Word Error Rate (WER) was selected for testing purposes. Each training runs for up to 48 hours. Fig. 7 presents an example training run showing that model loss stabilizes after around 8,000 iterations. Additionally, we conducted an experiment in which the training dataset was expanded with Polish texts from the CommonVoice41 and Fleurs42 datasets.

Validation loss during the training on natural voices corpus. The model loss converges after around 8k iterations.

Trained models were evaluated on our test sets, i.e., synthetic, anonymized human voices. The results were presented in Table 6. Fine-tuning the Whisper-medium model resulted in a substantial reduction in Word Error Rate (WER) on natural speech, decreasing from 24% (95% CI (Confidence Interval): [22.3%, 26%]) to 9.5% (95% CI: [8.3%, 11.1%]). Confidence intervals were estimated using 1,000 bootstrap iterations. The non-overlapping confidence intervals indicate a statistically significant improvement, confirming that fine-tuning considerably enhanced speech recognition accuracy. Based on the results we obtained, we proved that our dataset helped reduce the WER of medical speech transcription during fine-tuning. Moreover, we substantiated that further experiments using our dataset can further improve the performance of voice transcription. The Whisper pre-trained model performs significantly worse across all subsets than the fine-tuned models, indicating the benefit of fine-tuning. We can observe that using the anonymized dataset works very well, even better than training on recorded voices. This is likely due to the more significant number of samples in this dataset, which confirms the effectiveness of anonymization as a data augmentation strategy.

Usage Notes

The ADMEDVOICE dataset provides a collection of voice recordings of individuals reading phrases from medical categories. Accompanying metadata are provided in a CSV file, offering detailed contextual information for each recording. Demo Kaggle notebook in R is provided as a starting point for data exploration and analysis. This script allows users to evaluate audio quality using the mos_pred and grade attributes. Natural voice recording equipment and conditions can be distinguished with rec_type and rec_place attributes.

The script prepared in Python provides demonstration use our model for speech to text transcription scenario. Our script was developed using PyTorch 2.5 package, transformers 4.48 and datasets from huggingface collection.

In case of fine-tuning of other model, preprocessing of the audio data can include resampling or selection of particular recording type or duration.

For practical guidance and hands-on demonstration, the Python script is contained in the notebook. The demo includes setup of fine-tuned model, loading data and audio file, and comparison of transcription and textual metadata of recording. The R script is given in the notebook. This file provides a practical illustration of how to work with the dataset, serving as a starting point for further exploration and analysis.
