Genome-wide variation analysis of two Salvia hispanica L. genotypes and implication for associations with metabolic and adaptive traits
Authors: Clara R. Azzam, Mokhtar Said Rizk, Ramadan A. Arafa, Khaled Adly Khaled, Reda M. Gaafar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdvances in next-generation sequencing have accelerated genome-wide exploration of genetic diversity in underutilized oilseed crops. Salvia hispanica L. (chia), a high-nutrient pseudocereal rich in omega-3 fatty acids, is increasingly valued for its health benefits and commercial potential, yet it remains poorly characterized at the genomic level. Understanding the scale and nature of genomic variation is essential for improving complex traits such as oil yield, stress tolerance, and seed quality.MethodsTwo contrasting chia genotypes, Black-chia (CACH-B) and White- chia (CACH-W), were resequenced using the Bio-Resequencing Toolkit (BRT) pipeline. High-coverage sequencing, with a mapping rate exceeding 99% and an average depth of approximately 28×, facilitated the detection and annotation of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), insertions and deletions (InDels), copy-number variations (CNVs), and structural variants (SVs). The functional classification of variant impacts enabled the identification of genes potentially linked to metabolic and adaptive traits.ResultsA total of 1.97 million SNPs, 401,493 InDels, 836 CNVs, and 15,288 SVs were identified across the chia genome. Notably, approximately 53% of exonic SNPs were non-synonymous (dN/dS ≈ 1.28), predominantly affecting lipid metabolism, transcriptional regulation, and stress response pathways, potentially altering key agronomic traits. In addition, CNV hotspots were concentrated in chromosomes 3 and 6, overlapping MYB, WRKY, and bZIP transcription factor loci, may potentially be involved in stress tolerance and yield. Furthermore, structural rearrangements, including inversions and duplications within the FAD2, FAD3, and CYP450 gene clusters, were potentially associated with seed pigmentation and omega-3 biosynthesis, pointing to their potential breeding relevance. Observed heterozygosity (Hₒ ≈ 0.71) and nucleotide diversity (π ≈ 7 × 10−3) indicated moderate to high allelic richness. In addition, the low FST value (0.038) indicates substantial genomic similarity between the two genotypes.ConclusionThis study presents the first comprehensive map integrating SNPs, CNVs, and SVs in S. hispanica L. The results reveal a structurally dynamic genome characterized by substantial sequence and structural variation, providing valuable insights into genomic diversity and potential adaptive mechanisms in chia. The coexistence of high SNP diversity and abundant structural variation underpins chia's nutritional specialization and environmental resilience. These results deliver a foundational genomic resource for marker-assisted breeding, genome-wide association studies, and the development of climate-resilient chia cultivars.

Introduction

Salvia hispanica L. (chia) is an annual species in the Lamiaceae family, native to Mexico and Guatemala. It has gained considerable attention for its nutrient-dense seeds, which are rich in dietary fiber, protein, omega-3 fatty acids, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), vitamins, minerals, antioxidants, and other bioactive compounds. In addition, Chia seeds also contain a variety of phenolic and flavonoid compounds that demonstrate strong antioxidant and protective biological activities. Therefore, chia is recognized as one of the most important nutrient-dense orphan crops, offering substantial nutritional and functional benefits1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. Due to this comprehensive nutritional profile, chia is classified as a functional food that supports cholesterol regulation, digestive health, and cardiovascular function7. In Egypt, recently developed two purified genotypes, Black chia (CACH-B) and White chia (CACH-W), derived from the national variety “Misr 1” and stabilized over three seasons. These genotypes serve as valuable genetic resources for future physiological and genomic research8.

Chia is recognized for its considerable nutritional and economic value, primarily due to its high α-linolenic acid content, which accounts for approximately 60% of total lipids, as well as its 25% protein, 30–34% dietary fiber, and notable concentrations of antioxidants such as chlorogenic and caffeic acids9. These attributes position chia as a promising pseudocereal for research on lipid metabolism and the development of functional foods10. The cultivation of chia has expanded into regions such as South America, Africa, and Europe, driven by rising demand for plant-based omega-3 sources11. Chia was domesticated around 5000 years ago in Mesoamerica, alongside other staple crops such as maize and amaranth12. Traits such as seed yield, oil content, and stress tolerance are likely important targets for breeding and selection in chia, potentially resulting in partial genetic bottlenecks. A genetic bottleneck is a reduction in the number of individuals for at least one generation, resulting in a loss of genetic diversity and changes in allele frequencies. This pattern mirrors observations in other crops, where nucleotide diversity was reduced by 40–60% relative to wild ancestors13. However, the outcrossing reproductive strategy of chia and its partial self-incompatibility likely preserved substantial heterozygosity, defined as the presence of different alleles at gene loci14. Black and white seeded varieties are the most widely cultivated, yet their genome-wide genetic differentiation remains insufficiently characterized. Clarifying whether these color variants are determined by a limited number of major loci or reflect broader genomic divergence is essential for both evolutionary research and breeding initiatives.

Despite the global expansion of chia cultivation, progress in genomic improvement remains limited. Most breeding programs still rely on open-pollinated landraces with poorly characterized pedigrees, which restricts selection efficiency. The development of comprehensive genomic resources is therefore essential for improving oil quality, accelerating genetic gain, and enhancing environmental resilience. Unlike established oilseed crops such as sesame and flax, chia breeding is in its early stages and primarily employs phenotypic selection for yield, oil composition, and seed color, with minimal use of molecular tools. Consequently, the integration of genomics is anticipated to facilitate marker-assisted selection, enhance parent identification, and enable genomic prediction15. In response to this need, collaborative initiatives in Latin America, Africa, and Europe have begun implementing genome-assisted approaches. However, the success of these efforts depends on access to robust variant catalogs and standardized technological platforms. Recent genomic investigations have significantly expanded resources for S. hispanica L. For example, researchers generated a chromosome-scale genome assembly and analyzed 20 chia genotypes, revealing population structure, extensive genomic diversity, and evidence of interspecific introgression within Salvia species16.

Genetic variation underlies both adaptation and plant breeding. Single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), which are single-base changes in the DNA sequence, constitute the most common type of genetic variation. SNPs are distributed every few hundred base pairs and provide critical insights into genetic diversity, evolutionary dynamics, and functional differentiation sesame17. Other genetic variants, including insertions and deletions (InDels), as well as larger structural changes such as inversions, duplications, translocations, and copy-number variations (CNVs), also play significant roles in genome plasticity and phenotypic diversity18, 19. In chia, comprehensive identification of these polymorphisms is crucial for associating genomic variation with key agronomic traits, including oil yield, fatty acid composition, and drought tolerance. This integrated approach supports both marker-assisted selection and genomic selection strategies20.

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) has revolutionized genomic research by enabling high-resolution and cost-effective identification of genetic variants21. Illumina paired-end sequencing facilitates accurate detection of SNPs and InDels, while computational modules enable the resolution of larger structural variants (SVs) and copy-number variants (CNVs. The Bio-Resequencing Toolkit (BRT) provides an integrated workflow that combines quality filtering (SOAPnuke), read mapping (BWA-MEM), variant calling (GATK), CNV identification, and SV detection. These processes are coordinated through YAML configuration files to enhance usability22.

Genome duplication and rearrangement events frequently occur within the Lamiaceae family and contribute to the diversification of metabolic pathways. For instance, Ocimum basilicum and Mentha species possess duplicated terpene synthase gene clusters that are responsible for their distinctive essential oil profiles23, 24. Comparable structural rearrangements in chia may have influenced the evolution of its unique lipid metabolism and antioxidant properties. Comparative genomics across the Lamiaceae enables the identification of conserved orthologous markers, thereby facilitating cross-species analyses and supporting more precise functional annotation.

Unconventional crops such as chia are gaining attention for their ability to thrive in low-input and marginal environments, making them promising for climate change adaptation and sustainable agriculture6. Chia demonstrated a notable capacity to thrive in semi-arid and marginal environments, underscoring its potential as a climate-resilient crop. This adaptability is frequently attributed to genetic polymorphisms in stress-responsive genes, such as dehydrins, heat shock proteins, and abscisic acid (ABA)-related regulators. CNVs and SNPs in these genes can influence gene expression and confer adaptive advantages. The integration of genomic, phenotypic, and environmental data facilitates the identification of genotype-environment interactions (G × E), which are essential for resilience under climate variability25. Therefore, chia is an important emerging oilseed crop and a valuable model for studying the genomic mechanisms of environmental adaptation and stress resilience.

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) represent a robust method for associating genetic polymorphisms with complex quantitative traits in modern breeding26. However, conventional SNP-based GWAS often do not account for the effects of large structural variants. To address this, recent models, including Farm CPU and BLINK, incorporate kinship corrections and multi-locus testing, thereby improving detection accuracy27. Furthermore, integrating SNP, CNV, and SV datasets enhances the potential for trait-linked variation and enables the identification of high-impact alleles potentially related to composition and stress adaptation28, 29. Consequently, a comprehensive catalog of SNPs, CNVs, and SVs is crucial for advancing functional genomics research in chia.

Although recent studies have produced chromosome-scale genome assemblies and population-scale resequencing datasets for S. hispanica L., comprehensive genome-wide analyses integrating SNPs, CNVs, structural variants, and functional annotations across contrasting genotypes are still lacking. In this study, the BRT pipeline was applied to two distinct chia genotypes, CACH-B and CACH-W. A genome-wide inventory of SNPs, InDels, CNVs, and SVs was generated, genomic diversity and heterozygosity were assessed, and genes related to lipid metabolism and stress adaptation were functionally annotated. This integrated analysis provides a high-resolution variant map for two purified Egyptian S. hispanica L. genotypes and offers valuable insights into genomic variation associated with lipid metabolism and stress adaptation. The resulting resource will facilitate future genome-wide association studies, marker-assisted selection (MAS), and the breeding of high-oil, climate-resilient chia cultivars, thereby promoting sustainable agriculture and food security.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

Two genotypes of cultivated S. hispanica L. Black chia (CACH-B) and White chia (CACH-W) were analyzed. Seeds were sourced from the Bio-Resequencing Toolkit (BRT) reference repository (project ID F25A430000221_PLAezywR). Both genotypes are representative of widely cultivated commercial seed-color types. DNA was extracted from young leaf tissue using a modified CTAB protocol optimized for mucilage-rich samples30. DNA concentration and purity were assessed with NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and Qubit 3.0 fluorometer. High-molecular-weight DNA (≥ 50 ng μl−1) was selected for library construction.

Library construction and sequencing

Genomic libraries with an average insert size of approximately 350 base pairs were prepared using the Illumina TruSeq DNA PCR-Free kit according to the manufacturer's protocol (Macrogen, Korea). Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with paired-end 150-base reads, yielding approximately 95 gigabases of raw data, corresponding to an average genome coverage of approximately 28× per genotype. Base calling and demultiplexing were performed using Illumina bcl2fastq v2.2031. Quality summaries and per-cycle error plots were archived to ensure reproducibility.

Read-quality control

Raw sequencing reads were filtered using SOAPnuke v2.1.5 to remove adapter-contaminated reads, reads containing more than 5% ambiguous (N) bases, and reads in which over 40% of bases had a Phred score below 20. On average, 97.4% of reads passed quality control. Post-filtering statistics were validated with Fast QC v0.1232. Per-base quality scores exceeded Q30 for more than 91% of all positions. Filtered reads were archived in compressed FASTQ format with MD5 checksums.

Assembly with reference genome

Sequencing reads were aligned to the S. hispanica L. reference genome assembly (BRT_ref_v1.0, ∼ 395 Mb), consistent with the reported genome size33, using BWA-MEM v0.7.1720 with parameters -M and -t 16. The resulting alignments were converted to BAM format and coordinate-sorted with SAMtools v1.17. PCR duplicates were identified and removed using Picard v2.27.5. Alignment statistics from Qualimap v2.3 indicated that at least 99% of reads mapped, and 93% of the genome was covered at a minimum depth of 10×.

Variant calling: SNPs and InDels

Variant detection was performed according to the GATK v4.5 Best Practices workflow34. Per-sample GVCFs were generated with HaplotypeCaller using the -emit-ref-confidence option. Joint genotyping was subsequently conducted with GenotypeGVCFs. Variant filtration was applied using the following thresholds: QUAL >30, DP ≥ 10, MQ ≥ 40, FS < 60, and QD > 2.0. Following filtration, VCF files were merged using bcftools merge and subsequently indexed for downstream analysis. Transition/transversion ratios (Ts/Tv) and allele frequencies were calculated using vcftools v0.1.17. The selected quality-control parameters were implemented to ensure reliable variant detection and to minimize sequencing and alignment artifacts prior to comparative genomic analyses.

Annotation and functional classification

The annotation process utilized the BRT-Annotator v1.0 module, which integrates SnpEff v5.135 and a curated S. hispanica L. gene GFF3 database. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted using variant-affected genes as the query set and the full annotated S. hispanica genome as the background. Statistical enrichment was assessed using hypergeometric testing and the false discovery rate (FDR) corrected with the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure36, with significance thresholds set at FDR < 0.05. Variants were classified as intergenic, intronic, regulatory, synonymous, or nonsynonymous. Predicted impacts were categorized as “high,” “moderate,” “low,” or “modifier.” Annotated VCF files were converted to tabular format for visualization. Gene functional annotation used established reference-based pipelines and publicly available genomic databases to assign putative functions to predicted genes based on defined similarity and confidence thresholds.

Copy-number variation (CNV) detection

CNVs were identified using BRT-CNV v1.0, which integrates read-depth normalization (1 kb bins) with circular binary segmentation37. Segments exhibiting an absolute log₂ ratio ≥ 0.4 and p < 0.01 (FDR-corrected) were classified as gains (log₂ > 0.4) or losses (log₂ < −0.4). Events overlapping ≥1 gene were assigned gene IDs and annotated using the S. hispanica L. genome database. Cross-sample comparisons were performed to distinguish shared CNVs from those specific to individual genotypes.

Structural variant (SV) detection

Structural variants (SVs), including deletions (DEL), insertions (INS), tandem duplications (DUP Tandem), inversions (INV), intra-chromosomal translocations (ITX), and inter-chromosomal translocations (CTX), were identified using BRT-SV v1.0. The BRT-SV module integrates split-read and discordant-pair evidence, applying thresholds of at least 5 supporting reads and a mapping quality of at least 30. Putative SVs were computationally detected using split-read and discordant-pair evidence, with stringent filtering criteria. SV coordinates were standardized in BEDPE format and annotated for overlapping genes using bed tools intersect38.

Diversity metrics and heterozygosity

Observed heterozygosity (Hₒ) was computed using the following equation:H0=number of heterozygous sitestotal non−missing genotyped sites

Per-chromosome observed heterozygosity (Hₒ) and missingness (refers to the proportion of samples or loci where a genotype cannot be determined) were calculated using vcftools –het option. Nucleotide diversity (π) was estimated in sliding windows of 100 kb using vcftools –window-pi option. Pairwise fixation indices (F_ST) were calculated to quantify genetic differentiation between genotypes.

Visualization and generation of figures

Variant summaries were visualized using Python 3.11, along with the pandas and matplotlib libraries. All figures utilize standardized color palettes and an A4-width resolution at 300 dpi.

Computational environment and reproducibility

Analyses were performed on a Linux server (Ubuntu 22.04, 32 CPUs, 128 GB RAM). All software versions, parameters, and configuration files are documented in brt.yml. Intermediate files and scripts were archived following Findable, Accessible, Interoperable and Reusable (FAIR) data guidelines39. The entire workflow can be reproduced by re-executing brt run config/brt.yml in the Bio-Resequencing Toolkit (BRT) environment.

Statistical analyses

All analyses in this study used genome-derived variant data, including SNPs and other variant metrics. Morphological and phenotypic traits were not assessed. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize variant-count distributions and genomic variation patterns. Because only a single genome sequence was analyzed for each genotype, no inferential statistical tests requiring biological or technical replication were performed. All analyses were therefore limited to descriptive comparisons between genotypes. Data processing and visualization were conducted in R v4.3.2 using the tidyverse and ggpubr packages40.

Ethical and data-availability statements

Plant material originated from authorized germplasm repositories, and no endangered or wild populations were utilized. All raw sequencing reads and variant files are available in the BRT repository under genotype F25A430000221_PLAezywR.

A total of 1, 971, 707 SNPs 401, 493 InDels, 836 CNVs and 15, 288 structural variants (SVs) were identified (Table 1). Among the SNPs, 91.9% were shared between the two genotypes, while the genotype-specific polymorphisms reflected substantial allelic diversity within commercial chia germplasm (Table 1). Quality assessment of raw reads demonstrated consistently high sequencing quality across all base positions, with Phred scores exceeding Q30 for the majority of reads (Fig. 2). SNPs distribution and functional annotation

The average SNP density was approximately 5.1 per kb, with a transition-to-transversion (Ts/Tv) ratio of about 1.70. Regional classification revealed that 62.0% of SNPs were intergenic, 21.0% were intronic, 8.0% were exonic, and the remaining 9.0% were splice-site or untranslated region (UTR)-related (Table 2). Among exonic SNPs, 53% were nonsynonymous, resulting in an estimated dN/dS ratio of approximately 1.28. Because this value was derived from a pairwise comparison between two closely related genotypes, it should be interpreted cautiously, as limited synonymous substitutions may inflate the apparent non-synonymous substitution rate. Structural variants (SVs)

A total of 15,288 SVs were identified across both genotypes (CACH-B and CACH-W) (Table 1). The genotype-level comparison based on the non-redundant SV call set showed a higher number of SVs in White-chia (CACH-W; 9178) than in Black-chia (CACH-B; 6110) (Table 1). The chromosome-wise distribution presented in Table 5 corresponds to SV event counts detected in the CACH-B genome only. Structural variant types included deletions (43.3%), insertions (27.8%), duplications (11.1%), inversions (7.1%), and translocations (10.8%). Chromosome-level distributions indicated SV enrichment on chromosomes 3–5, with chromosome 3 exhibiting the highest number of detected SVs (1441 events), followed by chromosomes 4 (1351 events) and 5 (1276 events) (Table 5). Notably, an inversion on chromosome 5 spans a cytochrome P450 gene cluster potentially associated with seed-coat pigmentation variation, while duplications in FAD3-associated regions may contribute to differences in omega-3 fatty acid content. It is important to note that the total SV count in Table 5 (9194) represents chromosome-wise SV event counts across variant categories detected in the CACH-B genome. Genome-wide diversity indices

The observed heterozygosity (Hₒ) was 0.709 for Black-chia (CACH-B) and 0.742 for White- chia (CACH-W), with nucleotide diversity (π) measured at 6.9 × 10−3 and 7.1 × 10−3, respectively (Table 6). Additionally, the fixation index F_ST between the two genotypes was 0.038, indicating low differentiation. The inbreeding coefficient (Fᵢ = 0.10–0.12) together with relatively high observed heterozygosity (Hₒ) suggests a deviation from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium rather than true inbreeding, potentially reflecting population structure or admixture effects commonly referred to as the Wahlund effect. Tajima's D values ranged from 0.28 to 0.31 in the two analyzed genotypes. These slightly positive values indicate modest deviations from the neutral expectation; however, given that the analysis was based on only two genotypes rather than a population-level sample, the biological interpretation of Tajima's D should be treated with caution. Therefore, no definitive conclusions regarding demographic history or selection patterns can be drawn from these estimates. Together, these metrics place chia within the moderately diverse range characteristic of semi-domesticated oilseeds. To further explore genomic features, a Circos plot was generated for the Black-chia (CACH-B) genotype (Fig. 4). Discussion

Genomic variation, SNP diversity, InDel polymorphism, and copy-number variation in S. hispanica L.

A comprehensive genomic analysis of S. hispanica L. provides evidence for extensive sequence polymorphism and structural heterogeneity, indicating that both point mutations and large-scale genome rearrangements contribute to its genetic diversity. The co-occurrence of approximately two million single-nucleotide polymorphisms SNPs and more than 15,000 structural variants (SVs) (Table 1) underscores the combined influence of nucleotide-level and chromosomal-scale variation. The high proportion of shared SNPs (91.9%) suggests a relatively recent common ancestry or extensive retention of ancestral polymorphisms between cultivated genotypes, whereas genotype-specific variants represent valuable reservoirs for trait differentiation and adaptive evolution. Our findings are consistent with the genomic diversity and population differentiation16 among cultivated and wild chia genotypes. The observed SNP density (∼5 SNP kb-1) and mean dN/dS ratio (∼1.28) suggest that, while most coding regions remain conserved, a substantial proportion of genetic variation may have functional significance (Table 2). This trend is consistent with findings in other partially domesticated oilseeds, such as Sesamum indicum, where moderate genetic diversity persists despite selective breeding17. Similarly, in sesame, enrichment of fatty-acid biosynthesis pathways has been associated with variation in oil-related traits, highlighting the importance of these metabolic networks in oilseed crop improvement41. Therefore, the enrichment of SNPs in lipid metabolism genes in S. hispanica L. supports the hypothesis that both environmental factors and human-mediated selection have contributed to its adaptive evolution. Insertion-deletion (InDel) polymorphisms contribute substantially to the genomic variability of chia. Although they constitute a smaller fraction of the dataset (4.6%, Table 3), frameshift-inducing InDels can have significant effects on gene function. The frequent occurrence of these InDels in genes related to lipid biosynthesis and stress response pathways underscores their relevance to adaptation and crop improvement. Similar patterns have been observed in flax, where small InDels modulate enzyme activities and phenotypic traits19. Copy-number variation (CNV) increases the complexity of the chia genome. The predominance of deletions over duplications (approximately 3:1; Table 4) indicates that dosage sensitivity may constrain gene amplification. In contrast, selective duplications involving regulatory gene families such as MYB, WRKY, and LTP are likely to affect quantitative trait variation rather than binary gene expression. Importantly, CNV hotspots on chromosomes 3 and 6 overlap with oil-quality quantitative trait loci (QTLs) identified in related oilseed crops, designating these regions as valuable targets for breeding and metabolic enhancement initiatives.

Comprehensive structural variation analysis (Table 5) shows that the S. hispanica L. genome retains significant structural flexibility. This characteristic, often reduced in intensively bred crops, enables adaptation through changes in gene content, dosage, and expression. The increased load of structural variants (SVs) in White-chia (CACH-W) may reflect greater structural genomic diversity, potentially contributing to phenotypic variation and adaptive potential. SVs at functional loci, such as P450 gene clusters and FAD3 duplications, likely affect key phenotypic traits, including stress response and fatty acid metabolism. This is consistent with reports of inversion-maintained haplotypes in sunflower42. Resequencing studies also show extensive allelic variation in stress-responsive genes43. Together, these findings highlight the role of SVs in shaping adaptive genomic architecture by modulating traits. Because structural variant detection in the present study was based on short-read paired-end sequencing, some complex rearrangements, large insertions, and duplication architectures may not be completely resolved. Therefore, the reported SVs dataset should be interpreted as a high-confidence computational prediction framework. Future studies integrating long-read sequencing technologies and experimental validation approaches, such as PCR-based confirmation, will be necessary to accurately validate breakpoint structures and complex SV events.

Genetic diversity analyses (Table 6, Table 8) show that chia germplasm retains broad allelic variation and low differentiation, which reflects genomic similarity between the two studied genotypes. The substantial sequence and structural variation observed in the analyzed S. hispanica genotypes indicates the retention of considerable genomic diversity despite their shared breeding background. Previous RAPD-based assessments also reported only a moderate reduction in genetic diversity among cultivated lines44. The preserved genetic variation provides a strong foundation for future genetic improvement and the development of elite cultivars with better agronomic performance and stress resilience. The present findings are also consistent with 45, who reported considerable SNP-based genetic diversity between cultivated and wild chia genotypes, indicating that S. hispanica L. retains substantial allelic variation across different genetic backgrounds. This preserved diversity may contribute to environmental adaptation and provide valuable genomic resources for future breeding and improvement programs. Functional enrichment analysis (Table 9) indicates that chia must simultaneously produce high levels of polyunsaturated fatty acids while maintaining tolerance to environmental stress. Key candidate genes, including FAD2, FAD3, and DGAT1 (Table 10), contribute to both oil biosynthesis efficiency and abiotic stress tolerance46. Regulatory elements such as bZIP45 and CYP450-C5 further support this coordination, as integrated omics evidence indicates their transcriptional co-regulation during seed filling47. Transcriptomic studies have also identified strong lipid-pathway gene activity in chia seeds48. Metabolomic analyses indicate that genetic variants influence the accumulation of antioxidant compounds potentially related to lipid metabolism49, and environmental factors modulate lipid composition through genotype-environment interactions50. The convergence of oil metabolism and stress-response pathways supports a pleiotropic model in which shared genetic networks govern both seed quality and resilience.

Recent genomic investigations in chia revealed extensive genetic and functional diversity associated with its nutritional and medicinal value. Several candidate genes involved in omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acid biosynthesis, seed mucilage formation, stress-response pathways, and secondary metabolite production were identified, in addition to numerous bioactive peptides with important antioxidant and antimicrobial activities. Moreover, the detection of nearly 3 million SNPs, 15,380 SSR markers, and widespread alternative splicing events highlights the potential of chia genomic resources for future functional genomics studies and marker-assisted breeding programs aimed at improving stress tolerance and seed quality51. The present findings are further52, who applied an integrated multi-omics framework combining genomic, transcriptomic, and metabolomic analyses to reveal the unique metabolic organization of chia seeds. Their study demonstrated coordinated regulation between lipid biosynthesis, antioxidant metabolism, and seed storage pathways, emphasizing the close relationship between seed nutritional quality and stress-adaptive mechanisms in S. hispanica L.

An integrative examination of the variant landscape shows distinct patterns. SNP and InDel densities correlate strongly with recombination-rich regions of the genome. In contrast, CNVs and SVs cluster predominantly in transposable element (TE)-rich, low-recombination zones. This pattern suggests a two-tier mutational architecture. Fine-scale sequence variants refine enzyme function and quantitative traits. Large structural variants reorganize gene networks or maintain favorable haplotypes under selection. This dual-layered genomic organization seems particularly advantageous for crop adaptation in diverse ecological niches. It exemplifies chia's evolutionary capacity to balance oil productivity with environmental resilience.

Genomic applications, comparative insights, and evolutionary adaptation in S. hispanica L.

The high-resolution genomic dataset of S. hispanica has a dense marker distribution (∼1 SNP per 200 bp) and low genetic divergence between the two genotypes (Fig. 3). This dataset provides a robust foundation for GWAS and genomic prediction. Fine-scale marker coverage enhances the precision of linkage mapping and trait association, especially for complex traits like oil content, seed size, and stress tolerance. Adding CNV and SV annotations into GWAS frameworks will further increase the ability to detect structural alleles with large phenotypic effects. Advanced statistical models such as FarmCPU and BLINK use mixed linear modeling and multi-locus testing27. These models can fully exploit these data, improving genomic selection and predictive breeding for oilseed improvement. Comparative analysis across oilseeds shows that chia maintains nucleotide diversity similar to flax, and sesame15, 53. However, chia has a much higher burden of structural variation. This elevated SV abundance may reflect greater structural genomic variability, potentially contributing to genomic flexibility and phenotypic diversification. Within Salvia, recent chromosome-scale assemblies33 highlight similar transposable element (TE)-mediated rearrangements and gene-family expansions. These changes relate to the diversification of secondary metabolites. Thus, the analyzed chia genotypes exhibit substantial genomic variation while maintaining overall genome stability, highlighting their potential as valuable genetic resources for future breeding and crop improvement programs. This makes chia an ideal system for both evolutionary genomics and applied breeding.

Chia has adapted to high-altitude Mesoamerican environments due to its flexible genome. High genetic diversity, significant structural variation, and the presence of ancient gene variants indicate that adaptation has occurred through the utilization of standing genetic variation and large-scale genomic changes, similar to mechanisms observed in other drought-tolerant species. The identification of key genes involved in oil biosynthesis and stress response suggests that the evolution of seed oil production and stress tolerance is interconnected, enabling the plant to store energy and persist in fluctuating environments. Although these findings are robust, the current study is limited to only two genotypes, which restricts insights into pairwise comparative genomic analysis between the two genotypes' diversity and the distribution of rare alleles. Although only two chia genotypes were analyzed in the present study, these genotypes represent purified contrasting lines developed from the Egyptian cultivar “Misr 1” through a breeding program aimed at stabilizing black- and white-seeded phenotypes. Therefore, the current work was designed as a comparative genome-wide analysis rather than a pairwise comparative genomic analysis between the two genotypes studied. Therefore, the current study was designed as a genome-wide comparative analysis aimed at characterizing pairwise genomic variation between the two studied genotypes. Future investigations involving larger and geographically diverse germplasm collections will be required to comprehensively assess population structure and evolutionary diversity in S. hispanica L. Future research should employ long-read sequencing and pan-genome approaches to resolve structural variant breakpoints and capture comprehensive pangenomic variation.

The integration of phenotypic, transcriptomic, and metabolomic data51 will be essential for associating genetic variants with key agronomic traits. Integrative insights into genome diversity, structure, and adaptation in S. hispanica L.

The comprehensive variant landscape across all genomic datasets (Table 1, Table 2, Table 3, Table 4, Table 5, Table 6, Table 7, Table 8, Table 9, Table 10) portrays a genome optimized for both stability and flexibility. Abundant SNP diversity refines functional efficiency. Structural variants reorganize gene networks to accommodate environmental and metabolic demands. Low differentiation reflects genomic similarity between the two studied genotypes. Functional enrichment highlights traits potentially related to lipid metabolism and abiotic stress tolerance. Collectively, these findings establish S. hispanica L. as a promising genomic model for breeding nutraceutical and climate-resilient oilseed crops.

Extensive variant data reveal that chia maintains an exceptionally balanced genome. It is highly polymorphic yet functionally constrained. The coexistence of nearly 2 million SNPs and more than 15,000 structural variants (Table 1, Table 2, Table 3, Table 4, Table 5) supports a dual evolutionary regime. Small-scale mutations generate gradual allelic shifts. Large chromosomal rearrangements remodel gene networks. Comparable hierarchical genomic plasticity has been reported in Brassica oleracea54 and Helianthus ecotypes42, where multi-tiered variation drives adaptive diversification. A similar multilayered architecture exists in Brassica juncea55. In chia, this flexible genomic structure likely evolved under selective pressures favoring metabolic adaptability and stress tolerance. This flexibility enables the species to colonize semi-arid and highland ecosystems across Mesoamerica.

Chromosome-specific SNP density patterns (Table 7; Fig. 1) emphasize the role of recombination in shaping genome evolution. Gene-rich chromosomal arms show higher polymorphism rates. Centromeric regions exhibit reduced variability due to recombination suppression. This uneven distribution of diversity influences Linkage Disequilibrium (LD) and determines the resolution of association mapping. The relatively uniform diversity across most chromosomes indicates a balanced distribution of genetic variation across the genome, without evidence of pronounced chromosome-wide reductions in diversity within the analyzed genotypes. This is consistent with the mild selection model44. These recombination dynamics parallel those in flax and sesame15, 53. This indicates that conserved recombination hotspots play a pivotal role in oilseed genome evolution. The functional significance of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and insertions or deletions (InDels) is shown by the proportion of nonsynonymous substitutions (Table 2). This indicates either relaxed selective constraint or directional selection for metabolic optimization. Single amino acid substitutions in key desaturase enzymes, including FAD2 and FAD3, can alter substrate specificity. As a result, they modify omega-6 to omega-3 fatty acid ratios46. In contrast, frameshift-inducing InDels (Table 3) are likely to be deleterious and rare because of strong purifying selection. The interplay between frequent neutral variants and rare, high-impact mutations facilitates gradual diversification while maintaining genome integrity. This balance between common neutral variants and rare high-impact mutations may facilitate genetic diversification while preserving genome integrity15. Comparative genomics within Salvia supports SNP-driven diversification trends across species26. Systems biology analyses associate SNP variation in desaturase gene families with modulation of lipid biosynthesis56, also demonstrating extensive synteny and genotype-specific genomic variation among available chia genome assemblies16.

Copy-number variations (CNVs) and structural variants (SVs) (Table 4, Table 5) represent significant sources of structural and regulatory innovation. CNV-driven duplications involving MYB, WRKY, and bZIP genes may facilitate transcriptional dosage adjustment during abiotic stress responses. Structural variants affect CYP450 and pigment-biosynthesis gene clusters. These cause observable seed-coat color polymorphisms between white- chia (CACH-W) and black- chia (CACH-B) genotypes. Similar rearrangements are responsible for color and oil-content polymorphisms in sunflower42. Duplications of FAD3-B may enhance omega-3 desaturase activity. This potentially explains the increased α-linolenic acid concentration observed in White- chia (CACH-W)33. These dosage-dependent SV effects illustrate direct structural regulation of metabolic phenotypes. Long-read genomic analyses in related Salvia species may reflect that large inversions and translocations contribute to adaptive diversification57. Comparative transcriptomics between chia color morphs provides evidence for CNV-associated differences in pigmentation and oil-biosynthesis gene clusters45. Genome-wide CNV mapping has also identified novel gene families involved in oil-biosynthesis regulation47.

Genome-wide diversity metrics and adaptive demographic analyses demonstrate chia's evolutionary resilience. Moderate heterozygosity and high nucleotide diversity (Table 6), the relatively high heterozygosity together with low genetic differentiation (F_ST = 0.038; Table 8) observed between the two analyzed genotypes, indicates close genomic similarity while maintaining detectable within-genotype allelic variation in the analyzed genotypes52. In contrast to self-pollinated crops such as soybean, the analyzed chia genotypes retained substantial sequence and structural variation, reflecting considerable genomic diversity within the evaluated germplasm, thereby maintaining a rich allelic pool. This demographic stability confers significant advantages for climate adaptation and breeding by facilitating genetic recombination and the integration of favorable alleles without reproductive isolation. Global germplasm analyses also reveal extensive nutrient-associated allelic variation across chia genotypes58, highlighting its potential as a model for nutraceutical enhancement and sustainable crop development.

Functional enrichment, adaptive genome structure, and breeding applications

Functional enrichment analysis (Table 9; Fig. 4) associates genomic variation with core physiological processes, demonstrating significant overrepresentation of genes involved in lipid metabolism and stress response. This pattern suggests co-selection for both energy storage and environmental tolerance. The accumulation of unsaturated fatty acids enhances chia's nutritional value and increases membrane fluidity during desiccation stress, providing dual adaptive benefits. Key loci such as FAD2, FAD3, and DGAT1, therefore, contribute to both agronomic performance and stress resilience46. These results indicate that chia's metabolic specialization likely evolved through correlated selection on biochemical and ecological traits, rather than exclusively through human-driven selection for oil content. The integration of structural and sequence-based variation demonstrates that genome organization is fundamental to adaptation. The co-localization of SNP hotspots with CNV and SV regions (Table 4, Table 5) supports a model in which transposable element activity drives both local mutation accumulation and large-scale genomic rearrangements. These variant clusters serve as evolutionary incubators, producing novel gene combinations that enable rapid adaptation. Structural variants are increasingly recognized as primary drivers of metabolic and phenotypic evolution in oilseed crops59. Comparable patterns of alignment between structural and sequence variation have been observed in Brassica and Salvia splendens33, 54. In chia, these genomic clusters include genes involved in lipid metabolism, stress tolerance, and secondary metabolite biosynthesis, all of which are critical for survival in nutrient-poor and water-limited environments. These results align with broader evidence indicating that SV-driven regulatory changes are a major source of phenotypic plasticity in crops60.

From a breeding perspective, the dense SNP distribution (approximately 1 SNP per 200 b p) provides a high-resolution framework for GWAS with reduced false-positive rates. Due to the rapid decay of linkage disequilibrium in chia, a single genome-wide panel of approximately 100,000 high-quality SNPs is sufficient for comprehensive quantitative potential trait-associated variation.

Incorporating CNV and SV data (Fig. 2, Fig. 3) enhances the detection of non-coding or dosage-sensitive variants that are often missed by SNP-only approaches. Building on this, the resulting comprehensive variant catalog includes diagnostic markers for oil-quality genes such as FAD2, FAD3, and DGAT1, as well as stress-regulatory genes including WRKY and bZIP. Notably, recent GWAS have identified both SNPs and CNVs near these loci that influence fatty-acid composition61. Furthermore, genomic prediction models integrating SNP and CNV datasets have demonstrated high accuracy for predicting oil yield in chia33. In addition, GWAS analyses have identified quantitative trait loci (QTLs) controlling seed yield and size62, further supporting the predictive relevance of these results. By integrating genomic prediction with marker-assisted selection, breeding cycles can be substantially shortened and phenotyping costs reduced27. Finally, structural-variant markers also serve as robust molecular tools for germplasm authentication, thereby supporting traceability and certification in nutraceutical crop markets.

Comparative genomics, evolutionary significance, and practical implications

Comparative genomic analysis shows that S. hispanica L. has greater genetic diversity and a higher SV load than other oilseed crops, including flax and sesame15, 53. This pronounced genome plasticity mirrors that of wild Salvia species in heterogeneous environments33, indicating that structural variation could be a conserved adaptive mechanism across the genus. Substantial CNV/SV abundance is accompanied by low differentiation, which reflects genomic similarity between the two studied genotypes while preserving substantial allelic and structural variation across the genome. This genomic flexibility suggests strong potential for trait diversification without compromising overall genome integrity. The observed genomic landscape is characterized by gradual allelic and structural variation that may contribute to trait diversity and environmental adaptability. However, further genomic analyses across a broader range of diverse germplasm are needed to evaluate the evolutionary significance of these patterns63. However, because the present study was limited to two cultivated genotypes and did not include wild genotypes, this interpretation should be considered preliminary and requires further validation using broader comparative genomic datasets.

From a genomic perspective, the variation observed in S. hispanica L. indicates notable genomic variability among genotypes, with multiple small-effect variants collectively contributing to trait diversity rather than single major mutations. The observed moderate FST values and relatively high heterozygosity suggest the presence of substantial standing genetic variation, which may contribute to tolerance under variable environmental conditions. The retention of structural and allelic diversity may enable adaptation to drought, temperature fluctuations, and nutrient-poor soils. These traits are increasingly relevant under global climate change51. Therefore, maintaining genomic diversity may provide an adaptive advantage beyond being a neutral evolutionary feature. This pattern is consistent with observations in other oilseed crops under environmental stress64.

Although these findings are robust, our study was limited to only two genotypes. Future research should expand sampling to include landraces, wild relatives, and genetically diverse materials from different origins. This broader approach will help elucidate global patterns of genetic diversity and enable the detection of rare alleles. Integrating long-read sequencing with Hi-C scaffolding will improve SV breakpoint mapping and enable us to identify novel insertions not present in current assemblies, as demonstrated in other oilseeds33. Recent advances in CNV-calling pipelines now deliver greater accuracy and benchmarking for complex crop genomes65. Additionally, combining GWAS with transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses will allow us to directly associate genotype and phenotype. This integration will help us validate the functional predictions presented in Table 9, Table 10. Multi-omics strategies have already revealed complex trait architectures in flax and Brassica54.

The extensive polymorphism identified provides substantial value for conservation and breeding applications. SNPs and CNVs are effective for germplasm characterization and diversity monitoring, supporting genetic resource management. In this context, preserving wild Salvia relatives is essential for maintaining allelic richness and adaptive potential66. From an agronomic perspective, the low differentiation, reflecting genomic similarity between the two studied chia genotypes, enables strategic crossing, recombining beneficial alleles while preserving diversity. Altogether, these characteristics position chia as a promising model for implementing genomic selection in emerging crops aimed at sustainability and nutrition. Although the present study is limited to two Salvia hispanica L. genotypes derived from a single breeding background, it provides a high-resolution comparative genomic framework for assessing sequence and structural variation. The observed SNP, CNV, and SV landscapes reflect substantial intra-genotypic diversity, but should be interpreted strictly as pairwise genomic differences rather than pairwise comparative genomic analysis between the two genotypes. Future studies that include geographically diverse germplasm will be necessary to better characterize global patterns of genetic variation and the distribution of rare alleles. Conclusion

This study presents an integrative genomic framework for Salvia hispanica L. The genome exhibits high plasticity and moderate heterozygosity, shaped by single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and structural variants (CNVs and SVs). Nearly 2 million SNPs enable precise mapping of metabolic and regulatory loci, particularly those involved in lipid biosynthesis, stress response, and secondary metabolism. Structural variants overlap with key transcription factor families and lipid-transfer genes, highlighting the impact of gene dosage on phenotypic diversity related to oil content and environmental resilience. The genomic variation observed indicates limited divergence between the two studied genotypes, reflecting high genomic similarity while maintaining substantial allelic diversity within each genotype. A more comprehensive genomic analysis incorporating a larger number of genotypes and broader germplasm representation will be required to further characterize genetic diversity and genome-wide variation in Salvia hispanica L.

Functional enrichment analyses link genomic variation to lipid accumulation and stress tolerance, demonstrating a strong connection between nutritional and adaptive traits. The dense SNP map and annotated structural variants provide a valuable resource for genomic-assisted breeding. This dataset supports GWAS and genomic prediction for key agronomic traits, such as oil yield, seed size, and drought tolerance. Haplotype diversity enables efficient marker-assisted and genomic selection. The genome's dual-layered structure, combining fine-scale SNP variation with large structural rearrangements, promotes metabolic specialization and environmental adaptability, positioning chia as a climate-resilient crop. These findings extend recent genomic studies of chia, particularly comparative genomic analyses and investigations of genetic diversity patterns. Future research should focus on pan-genome assembly, inclusion of wild relatives and diverse landraces, and integration of genomic, transcriptomic, and metabolomic data to clarify the relationship between genetic variation and phenotypic traits. Salvia hispanica L. remains a genetically rich, adaptable, and promising model for improving minor oilseed crops.
