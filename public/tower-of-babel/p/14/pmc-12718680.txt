Optimizing outcomes for the patients with extensive-stage small-cell lung cancer
Authors: Peng-Hui Wang, Szu-Ting Yang, Kuan-Chong Chao
Published: 2025

Lung cancer compared with non-small cell lung cancer (SCLC) is a very aggressive subtype of lung cancers, compared to other non-SCLC,1–3 partly because of its biologically highly invasive characteristics accompanied with rapid disease evolution, early metastases, and prompt dissemination, and partly because of absence of effective screening methods, resulting in late diagnosis, contributing to over two-thirds of SCLC presenting as extensive-stage SCLC (ES-SCLC) with very dismally the worst outcomes (a stagnant 5-year survival plateau at 7%),4 even though SCLC represents a relatively rare type of lung cancers, accounting for only 10% to 15% of all lung cancers.4 Unlike treatment for non-SCLC patients, therapeutic strategies based on a doublet chemotherapy regimen (etoposide-platinum [EP] regimen), with acceptable responses (60%-80%, including a 43% complete response rate); however the duration of response (DOR) is very short, and almost all ES-SCLC cases progress due to the development of resistance, limiting the long-term clinical benefits, resulting in the historical survival benchmark hovering between 8 and 10 months.4 Recent JCMA data in resected NSCLC also highlights the need for stratified prognostic approaches.5 All indicate that the therapy for ES-SCLC is suboptimal, and clinical needs are still unmet. Recently, with advanced technology and better understanding of genomic and molecular biology of cancers,6–11 many biomarker-guided agonist therapies have been continuously developed, and many of them have been well investigated for their safety and efficacy by many prospective, randomized clinical trials (RCTs) as well as reviewed by clinical practice and real-world experience.12–15 To reach the maximal benefit-to-risk ratio, many obstacles should be overcome. For example, oncologic safety (the maximal therapeutic efficacy) is always the priority before the conduction of any changes in place of the established standard of care (SOC) therapy.16 Additionally, therapy-related adverse events (AEs) are always kept in mind, particularly for those patients who obtained only marginal benefits of therapeutic efficacy but should pay many treatment-related AEs or therapy-related life-threatening situations, which are relatively difficult to manage. Therefore, any recommendation of using the aforementioned type of treatment strategy is always in doubt and of concern.17

Based on the aforementioned concept, we are very happy to introduce Dr. Liao’s study, published in the August 2025 issue of the Journal of Chinese Medical Association (JCMA), to report their results focusing on the use of the new treatment strategy in the management of patients with highly lethal diseases—ES-SCLC.18 Dr. Liao retrospectively enrolled 232 women with ES-SCLC undergoing the first-line (1L) chemotherapy + immunotherapy (immunochemotherapy, immune checkpoint inhibitors [ICIs] as a backbone of immunotherapy) (n = 33) or conventional SOC chemotherapy (etoposide + platinum [EP] regimen) alone (n = 199) to investigate the oncological outcome between the two groups.18 There was no significant difference in progression-free survival (PFS) between the two groups (median PFS: 5.0 vs 4.8 months); however, the overall survival (OS) was statistically significantly different between the immunochemotherapy and SOC chemotherapy groups, with a median OS of 13.9 months compared to that of 8.5 months (p = 0.003).18 The authors also investigated what prognostic factors were associated with PFS and OS. They found baseline liver metastasis may be the most critical factor, which is not only associated with PFS (hazard ratio [HR]: 11.23; 95% CI, 2.95-42.68; p < 0.001) but also associated with OS (HR: 3.5; 95% CI, 1.18-10.50; p = 0.024).18 Additionally, ES-SCLC patients treated with immunochemotherapy, having received consolidative thoracic radiotherapy (cTRT), achieved better PFS (HR: 0.32; 95% CI, 0.10-0.97; p = 0.044), although the benefits of OS were not statistically significant. Furthermore, grade ≥3 hematological AEs (HR: 4.96; 95% CI: 1.04-23.64; p = 0.044), and a neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio [NLR] of ≥4 (HR: 3.66; 95% CI, 1.19-11.22; p = 0.023) were significant predictors of OS.18 The independent factors contributing to grade ≥3 hematological AEs included older age (≥65 years [odds ratio (OR): 18.31; 95% CI, 1.61-208.12) and baseline liver metastasis (OR: 15.18; 95% CI, 1.22-189.39).18 Finally, because there are many ICIs available in the market, the authors further performed the subgroup analysis according to the different brand names of ICIs, including atezolizumab and durvalumab. They found that the median PFS and OS were 5 and 15.8 months in the atezolizumab group compared to 4.9 months and 12.3 months in the durvalumab group, respectively.18

The results of the current study by Dr. Liao supported that the ES-SCLC patients received immunochemotherapy as the 1L therapy may have better therapeutic outcomes compared to those receiving the SOC chemotherapy alone. Therefore, the authors concluded that adding ICIs to 1L chemotherapy provides survival benefits in ES-SCLC patients, while close monitoring for AEs is required.18 We congratulate the authors’ success to publish their work and offer the potential role of adding ICIs into conventional chemotherapy in the management of women with ES-SCLC. Their work is highly valuable because their report offered hope for these highly lethal diseases (ES-SCLC). We take advantage of discussing some updated advances in this field, according to the findings from Dr. Liao’s study.

The advent of ICIs, particularly those targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 (programmed cell death protein-1/programmed death ligand 1) axis, has radically transformed the treatment landscape for various cancers, and of course, ES-SCLC is included.4,12,13,18–21 Many RCTs, including the phase 3 IMpowere133,19,22 and clinical practice and real-world experience (like Dr. Liao’s study)18 have tested the outcome of ES-SCLC patients treated with immunochemotherapy. So far, unlike to the best revolutionary efficacy of immunochemotherapy on non-SCLC,19,22 its effects for treating SCLC have been less exciting or dramatically impressive, although treatment options seemed to have a trend to show the significant improvement of either PFS or OS, which further result in a small proportion (10%) who can be alive more than 5 years while maintaining manageable AEs, leading to approval of immunochemotherapy combining EP regimen and either atezolizumab or durvalumab as first-line treatment for ES-SCLC by FDA and EMA.19 To support the small but clinically significant improvement in OS, Dr. Liao’s study also showed similar results.18 Additionally, Dr. Liao found that both atezolizumab (15.8 months) and durvalumab (12.3 months) improved OS.18

Although the authors attempted to investigate the independent prognostic factors to predict the therapeutic outcomes of ES-SCLC treated with immunochemotherapy, the authors found that the presence of liver metastases, grade ≥3 hematological AEs, the elderly population, and NLR of ≥4 are associated with worse outcomes of these ES-SCLC treated with immunochemotherapy. However, none can act as predictive biomarkers to identify those who will derive clinical benefit from this immunochemotherapy strategy. Currently, unlike many cancers with very useful and valuable biomarkers to identify the proportion of patients who will benefit from the definitive biomarker-guided agonist treatment, such as mismatch repair deficiency (MMRd) in endometrial cancer and PD-L1 immunohistochemical scoring (CPS) in cervical cancer,12,13,20 no specific biomarker has been approved for using immunochemotherapy for treating ES-SCLC patients.22 All the biomarkers tested to date have failed to predict any significant benefit from ICIs in the management of ES-SCLC patients.22 Therefore, it is still a long way to clarify the real benefits of an integration of ICIs into the conventional EP chemotherapy, not only for offering the evidence to demonstrate the enhancing survival benefits compared to EP alone but also for showing the better compliance (fewer or similar or little increase of AEs et al, but they are manageable) without compromising the therapeutic effects, suggesting that “optimizing outcomes” for the ES-SCLC patients are still unsatisfied. Therefore, we are still looking forward to pursuing new strategies and/or new combinations that potentially have enhanced benefits and safety profiles. All may need further discussion, although the authors failed to do so.

For example, the role of anti-angiogenesis agents, such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitor, tyrosine kinase inhibitors, as well as pan-target angiogenesis inhibitors-recombinant human endostatin,19 may be overlooked by authors; however, these agents have become more and more important because they have been worked as one of the essential components (backbone) for treating many kinds of cancers in the long-time period, particularly for using VEGF inhibitors in the management of those patients with believed incurable status, such as persistent, advanced, recurrent and metastatic status (PARM status). As shown above, of course, the anti-angiogenesis agent-related AEs should not be neglected.9,13,14 Based on the recent report by Liu et al,19 conducting a frequentist network meta-analysis and systematic review to navigate 1L therapies for ES-SCLC patients, the data showed the combination of PD-L1 inhibitor, VEGF inhibitor and EP regimen took the most survival benefits (HR: 0.61, 95% CI, 0.47-0.79) compared to EP regimen alone and/or other agents integrating into EP regimen, such as CDK4/6 (cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6) inhibitor (HR: 1.22, 95% CI, 0.78-1.91), CXCR4 (C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4) inhibitor (HR: 1.51, 95% CI, 0.90-2.53), CTLA-4 (cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4) inhibitor (HR: 0.94, 95% CI, 0.81-1.09), PD-1 inhibitor (HR: 0.73, 95% CI, 0.66-0.80), PD-L1 inhibitor (HR: 0.73, 95% CI, 0.65-0.82), combination of PD-L1 and CTLA-4 inhibitor (HR: 0.81, 95% CI, 0.67-0.97), and VEGF inhibitors (HR: 0.86, 95% CI, 0.67-1.12).17 All indicate that the use of triplet therapy by ICI, EP, and bevacizumab for treating ES-SCLC patients may be worthy of further investigations.

Finally, the role of radiotherapy (cTRT) may be overlooked by Dr. Liao’s study, although the authors have mentioned it as an independent prognostic factor for improving PFS in ES-SCLC patients treated with immunochemotherapy. However, the OS benefit did not have a statistically significant difference. Additionally, patients treated with immunochemotherapy have a larger proportion to undergo cTRT compared to those treated with chemotherapy alone (60.6% vs 27.1%, p = 0.241).18 As shown before,10 statistical meanings cannot be directly transformed into clinical value, and vice versa, no statistical significance does not always mean nothing in clinical practice. Therefore, the benefits of immunochemotherapy may also be enhanced by cTRT, although multivariate analysis has been used to test its statistical significance. We emphasize that any interpretation of the data from the publications should always be in concern. To minimize the occurrence of misunderstandings or inadequate interpretations, all results from the clinical studies must not contradict logical reasoning process. We encourage the audience to eagerly look forward to continuing reading, re-thinking, and real practicing to validate the publications everywhere. The JCMA is worthy of reading and believing, because it has already offered this aim. At the end, because the current issue addressing immunochemotherapy for treating cancer patients with an incurable state is very important, and the space for comments is limited at this time, we announce that further comments will be coming soon.
