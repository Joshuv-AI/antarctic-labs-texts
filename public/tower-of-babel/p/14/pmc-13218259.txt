Diet-dependent chemical fingerprinting in Tenebrio molitor larvae reared on tuna by-products
Authors: Nam-Gyun Kim, Gyeyeong Choi, Tae-Hee Kim, Soeon Ahn, Seung-Hee Moon, Soo-Jin Heo, Nalae Kang, Jun-Ho Heo, Junpyo Kwon, Seong-Yeong Heo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiet composition significantly influences the chemical quality of edible insect ingredients. This study compared Tenebrio molitor larvae reared on wheat bran (WB) and tuna by-products (TB) using proximate analysis and metabolomics. On a dry matter basis, crude protein was similar at about 45 g per 100 g, whereas TB showed higher lipid and lower carbohydrate contents. Fatty acid methyl ester analysis revealed higher oleic and linoleic acids and a shift toward C18 unsaturated fatty acids in TB. Total hydrolyzable amino acids and essential amino acid proportion were conserved, although proline was higher in TB. Untargeted mass spectrometry of ethanol extracts identified 26 diet responsive metabolites, with WB enriched in polar metabolites and TB enriched in lipid derived components. TB extracts showed stronger antioxidant activity and no apparent cytotoxicity. Overall, tuna by-products remodeled lipid chemistry and metabolomic signatures while maintaining protein adequacy, supporting lipid enriched insect ingredients.Keywords: Tenebrio molitor, Tuna by-products, Lipid composition, Untargeted metabolomics, Antioxidant activity

Introduction

The global fish-processing industry generates large quantities of solid by-products such as heads, viscera, bones, skin and trimmings, which remain not yet fully valorized across most value chains (Ozogul et al., 2021; Roy et al., 2023). As a substantial portion of the global catch is processed before reaching consumers, these operations routinely generate considerable volumes of by-products, although the amounts depend on the species and processing intensity (Kaushik et al., 2024). Fish by-products are rich in nutrients, containing collagen, gelatin, bioactive peptides, omega-3-rich lipids, and mineral fractions, with clear potential for high-value applications (Jimenez-Champi, Romero-Orejon, Muñoz, & Ramos-Escudero, 2024; Venugopal, 2021). Yet variability in raw material quality, short shelf life, and logistical constraints increase handling and processing costs and limit their industrial uptake (Xia et al., 2024). Developing methods to convert fish by-products into stable, higher-value ingredients enhances the utilization of processing residues and the recovery of nutritional and functional compounds for food applications (Kaveh, Darmian, Hashemi, & Abedi, 2025).

Teneb.rio molitor (T. molitor) larvae (mealworms) are increasingly used as edible and feed ingredients because of their favorable techno-functional and nutritional properties. They can be produced predictably under standardized rearing conditions with controlled temperature, humidity, and stocking densities (Errico et al., 2022; Zhang, Wang, Liang, Guo, & Wu, 2025). Mealworm powder and oil have also been evaluated as partial substitutes for fishmeal and vegetable oils and as protein supplements in poultry and pet food formulations (Jiang et al., 2024). Recent studies have used their diet selection to tailor the attributes of mealworm-derived ingredients by partially replacing cereal-based feeds with plant residues such as wheat bran, potato peels, and brewery spent grains (Boukid, Riudavets, Del Arco, & Castellari, 2021; Kotsou, Chatzimitakos, Athanasiadis, Bozinou, & Lalas, 2024). These plant-based residue substrates can maintain larval growth within practical inclusion levels while enriching monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA) and polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) content and shifting specific lipid species, along with coordinated changes in nucleosides and small organic acid levels. In contrast, fishery by-products are characterized by high protein content and complex lipid and mineral fractions that differ from those in cereal-based feeds (Romero-Lorente et al., 2022). Therefore, the bioconversion of these by-products by T. molitor has the potential to produce larval ingredients with distinct lipid compositions and micronutrient profiles that are better aligned with high-value food ingredients and other marine-linked food applications. Establishing reproducible chemical fingerprints for such diet-shaped ingredients can support quality control, authenticity and traceability, and rational formulation of insect-based food ingredients.

Channeling hard-to-manage fishery by-products for mealworm bioconversion provides a practical way to convert perishable, heterogeneous streams into a standardized biomass intermediate while retaining their nutritional value (Cooney et al., 2023). This approach can provide a dual benefit by facilitating the management of fishery by-products and converting specific marine-derived nutrients into value-preserving, land-based insect biomass. At the compositional level, the rearing substrate can alter larval lipid classes. It can change the balance between MUFA and PUFA, reshape the profiles of neutral lipids and lysophospholipids, and shift the pools of low-molecular-weight metabolites (Ferri, Spano, Dell'Anno, Mannina, & Rossi, 2024; Mao, Li, Meng, Jin, & Han, 2024). However, most previous studies on T. molitor have focused on growth performance and proximate composition, and the systematic use of fishery by-product substrates remains limited compared with cereal-based ones. Consequently, relatively little is known about how larval nutritional properties respond to diet and how such changes affect downstream applications (Mannaa, Mansour, Park, Lee, & Seo, 2024; Montalbán, Martínez-Miró, Schiavone, Madrid, & Hernández, 2023).

Moving from coarse nutritional metrics to rational ingredient design requires strategically designed, metabolomics-informed studies that deliberately use feed composition to shape larval lipid and metabolite profiles and to route specific marine-derived nutrients into insect biomass. In this context, establishing robust chemical signatures across liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) and gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) platforms and reporting them with transparent levels of identification confidence would yield marker panels for quality control and traceability, reduce safety risks by linking diets to chemical outcomes, and identify practical levers to tailor mealworm fractions for existing edible-insect markets (García-Pérez, Becchi, Zhang, Rocchetti, & Lucini, 2024; Rakusanova & Cajka, 2024). Closing this evidence gap is significant beyond basic biology, as it supports scalable valorization pathways for marine by-products by enabling bio-conversion-based nutrient programming through feed stream design and aligns with circular bioeconomy goals of keeping materials in use and designing out waste (Rossi et al., 2025).

In this study, we conducted an untargeted, cross-platform metabolomic comparison of T. molitor larvae reared on a wheat bran and a protein- and fatty- rich tuna by-product. We tested whether changing the substrate produced reproducible shifts in nutritional properties, including neutral lipids, lysophospholipids, fatty acid distributions, and selected small molecules. For clarity, we reported our features satisfying predefined thresholds for multivariate importance, minimum fold change, and statistical significance. LC-MS data in positive and negative ion modes were cross-checked against Progenesis QI, UNIFI, and GC–MS libraries, with explicit identification confidence levels. We then interpreted these patterns alongside proximate, amino acid, and fatty-acid baselines. In parallel, we measured antioxidant activity and cytotoxicity of larval ethanolic extracts to assess whether diet-driven chemical shifts translated into functional differences relevant to food and feed applications. Overall, this work defines a compact set of diet-responsive markers and a reproducible workflow linking rearing substrate to ingredient chemistry and bioactivity, supporting quality-oriented development and monitoring of insect-derived food ingredients.

Materials and methods

T. molitor larvae sources and feedstocks

T. molitor larvae were obtained from KOINSECT (Jeonbuk, Republic of Korea). Larvae were reared at 25 °C and 60% relative humidity in plastic boxes (35 × 55 × 10 cm). Each box contained approximately 600 g of larvae (∼8000–12,000 individuals per container) with 2.4 kg of wheat bran or 300 g of tuna by-products. The larvae were then washed with tap water in an ultrasonic bath and dried in a microwave dryer to a final moisture content below 5%. The dried larvae were milled to a fine powder, which was used directly for proximate, amino acid, and fatty acid analyses or for preparing 80% ethanol (EtOH) extracts. T. molitor larvae reared on wheat bran were designated as the WB group, whereas those reared on tuna by-products were designated as the TB group. All subsequent results are expressed on a dry matter (DM) basis unless otherwise stated.

Preparation of T. molitor larvae extract

Oven-dried T. molitor larvae were ground to a powder. For each sample, 500 mg of powder (n = 6; WB, TB) was suspended in ice-cold 80% (v/v) EtOH at a solvent-to-solid ratio of 20 mL per g in amber tubes on ice. Suspensions were sonicated in an ice-water bath for 30 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 30 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were filtered through 0.22 μm PTFE syringe filters, and then concentrated under reduced pressure at 20–25 °C on a rotary evaporator. The dried residues were redissolved in 1.0 mL of 80% (v/v) EtOH to obtain extracts equivalent to 500 mg dry larvae per mL. Extracts were sealed and stored at −80 °C until use.

Proximate and nutritional composition analysis

Moisture, ash, crude protein, crude fat, and fatty acid composition were analyzed at the Technology Innovation Center for Life Science, Jeju National University (Jeju, Korea). Fatty acids were converted to fatty acid methyl esters (FAME) and analyzed using gas chromatography with a flame ionization detector (GC–FID). Individual fatty acids were identified by retention times relative to commercial FAME standards and reported as percent of fatty acids. Moisture was determined by oven-drying to constant mass, and ash by incineration at 550 °C in a muffle furnace. For amino acid analysis, samples were hydrolyzed in 6 N HCl and analyzed with an amino acid analyzer (L-8800, Hitachi High-Tech, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with an ion-exchange resin column. Qualitative and quantitative determinations were performed using external calibration with a multicomponent amino acid standard mixture.

LC-MS analysis of T. molitor larval extracts

A LC-MS system was used to identify components in the 80% EtOH extracts of T. molitor larvae. The extracts were analyzed using a BEH C18 chromatography column (100 mm × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.7 μm particle size, Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) and a Waters ACQUITY UPLC system (Waters Corporation) equipped with a binary pump, auto-sampler, and UV detector. UV–Vis absorption spectra were recorded at 100–400 nm during the UPLC analysis. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in water) and solvent B (acetonitrile). The gradient program was as follows: 0 min, 100% A; 30 min, 0% A. Mass detection was performed using a SYNAPT XS Q-TOF (Waters) mass spectrometer equipped with a traveling-wave ion mobility. Data were collected in positive and negative electrospray ionization modes with the following settings: source temperature 120 °C; desolvation temperature 250 °C; capillary voltage 2.0 kV; cone gas flow 50 L/h; and desolvation gas (N₂) flow rate, 600 L/h. Spectra were acquired over a mass range of m/z 100–1300 in MSE mode.

LC-MS feature processing and multivariate analysis

Raw LC-MS data acquired with MassLynx v4.2 (Waters) was imported into Progenesis QI (Nonlinear Dynamics, Waters) for retention time alignment, peak detection, deconvolution, adduct/isotope grouping, and feature quantification. Positive and negative ion modes were processed separately. Prior to statistical analysis, features with the lowest 20% interquartile range were removed. Missing values were imputed as half the minimum nonzero intensity per feature, and intensities were TIC-normalized, log10-transformed, and Pareto-scaled. The resulting feature matrices were analyzed with the MetaboAnalyst v.6.0 and MetaboAnalystR (Chong & Xia, 2018) to generate PCA and PLS-DA score plots, volcano plots for pairwise comparisons, and hierarchical-clustering and correlation heatmaps.

Compound annotation and candidate consolidation

Putative identities were assigned in Progenesis QI by querying the KEGG, PubChem, and LipidMaps. Candidates with Match Score ≥35 were retained, and synthetic compounds were excluded. In parallel, features were matched in UNIFI to a metabolic profiling library for positive and negative ion modes (7308 entries each). Only software-flagged “Good Match” hits were accepted, defined as mass error ≤ 2 ppm, response ≥5000, isotope match intensity RMS ≤ 20%, and isotope match m/z RMS ≤ 15 ppm. When GC–MS (EI) data were available, spectra were searched against the Wiley and NIST commercial spectral libraries. Only matches with a similarity index (SI) ≥ 90% were retained, and annotations were further supported by consistency with retention behavior. Metabolite identification was performed based on accurate mass, MS/MS fragment ions, and isotopic distribution, following the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) guidelines, and the identified metabolites were classified into MSI Level 2 (putatively annotated compounds). For biological interpretation (WB and TB), statistical thresholds of VIP ≥ 1.0, fold change ≥2, and q < 0.05 were applied. The q-values were obtained using false discovery rate (FDR) correction based on the Benjamini–Hochberg method. The final candidate list consisted of differential features satisfying these statistical cutoffs and, at least, one of the library-matching rules above.

Antioxidant activity

Radical scavenging activities of WB and TB extracts were measured using 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) and 2,2′-azinobis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS) assay. Ascorbic acid was used as a reference standard and evaluated under the same experimental conditions. For DPPH, DPPH and the WB/TB extracts were dissolved in methanol. 100 μL of the sample and 100 μL of DPPH solution were added to a 96-well plate, incubated for 30 min at 25 °C in the dark, and read at 517 nm. For ABTS, the radical cation was generated by mixing 7 mM ABTS with 2.45 mM ammonium persulfate and allowing the mixture to stand in the dark at room temperature for 12–16 h. The ABTS solution was diluted to an absorbance of 0.70. Subsequently, 150 μL of diluted ABTS solution and 50 μL of sample were combined; after 10 min at room temperature, absorbance was measured at 734 nm.

Cell viability

RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere. For cell viability, cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 1.5 × 105 cells/mL and allowed to attach for 24 h. MTT was added to a final concentration of 0.2 mg/mL and incubated for 3 h. Supernatants were removed, and formazan crystals were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and absorbance was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments Inc., Winooski, VT, USA).

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Significant statistical difference was assessed using one-way ANOVA, followed by Duncan's test. Significance is indicated at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Diet-dependent changes in WB and TB larval appearance

Under standardized short-term feeding conditions, both WB and TB groups exhibited normal larvae with no visible deformities or discoloration related to handling or processing. As both groups were reared, ultrasonically washed, and dried using identical procedures, any visible differences likely reflect diet composition rather than post-harvest treatment. Accordingly, gross appearance as an initial qualitative indicator of substrate dependent effects (Evison, Gallagher, Thompson, Siva-Jothy, & Armitage, 2017). The larvae reared on WB and TB were showed visually discernible differences (Fig. 1A and B).

Visual comparison of T. molitor larvae reared on two diets, (A) wheat-bran and (B) by-product.

Randomly selected larvae (n = 30 per group) were analyzed to compare whole-body size and pigmentation. Relative to WB, the TB groups were larger (110.28 ± 6.30%) and exhibited stronger dark pigmentation (133.28 ± 5.05%) with more matte surface texture (Fig. S1A and S1B). These differences are consistent with diet-driven changes in tissue composition reported in mealworms and other holometabolous insects, where increased dietary lipid and protein availability promotes somatic lipid deposition and alters cuticular pigmentation (Lee, Simpson, & Wilson, 2008).

Compositional characteristics of WB and TB larvae

Proximate composition

Proximate analysis composition was analyzed to assess the major nutritional components of a sample, including crude protein, carbohydrate, fat, moisture, ash, fiber, etc., to provide an overall composition profile. One aim of this analysis was to test whether replacing a cereal-based substrate with tuna by-product diet changed larval nutritional value, focusing on protein yield and energy density, which are key criteria for edible-insect ingredients. The second aim was to provide a compositional baseline for interpreting detailed fatty acid and metabolomic data.

The proximate composition of WB and TB is summarized in Table 1. Crude protein was similar between groups. WB contained 45.58 ± 0.47 g/100 g DM and TB contained 45.06 ± 0.30 g/100 g DM, indicating maintained nitrogen assimilation despite the substrate change. In contrast, macronutrient partitioning differed among the diets. Carbohydrate content was higher in WB (15.20 ± 0.40 g/100 g DM) than in TB (11.72 ± 0.53 g/100 g DM), whereas lipid content was higher in TB (32.52 ± 0.30 g/100 g DM) than in WB (26.92 ± 0.80 g/100 g DM). TB also had lower moisture and ash contents (8.06 ± 0.06 and 2.64 ± 0.01 g/100 g DM) compared to WB (8.90 ± 0.11 and 3.40 ± 0.04 g/100 g DM, respectively).

These results indicate that the protein- and lipid-rich tuna by-product substrate preferentially enhanced lipid accumulation without compromising protein content. Such diet-dependent shifts are consistent with previous reports demonstrating that lipid fractions of T. molitor are highly sensitive to dietary energy and fatty acid inputs, while crude protein content exhibits limited variation across different rearing substrates. (Kröncke & Benning, 2023; Morales et al., 2025). Similar patterns, with relatively stable crude protein levels and more diet-sensitive lipid fractions, have been reported for T. molitor reared on various food by-products (Jajić et al., 2022). Consistent with these research, the tuna by-product diet increased larval energy density mainly through enhanced lipid deposition, while protein content remained essentially unchanged. Consequently, the TB profile is advantageous for food-oriented ingredient systems, as it increases extractable lipid yield without compromising protein, supporting the valorization of nutrient-rich residues through mealworm bioconversion (Rumbos et al., 2022).

Amino acid composition

To complement the proximate analysis, we measured larval amino acid composition to test whether replacing wheat bran with a tuna by-products substrate changed protein quality and quantity. Total amino acids were similar between the diets, WB (467.05 mg g−1 DM) and TB (453.57 mg g−1 DM) (Table S1), in line with the similar crude protein contents. Essential amino acids (EAA), His, Ile, Leu, Lys, Met, Phe, Thr and Val, with Trp not measured, comprised ∼39.0% in WB and ∼ 38.3% in TB, indicating preserved protein quality despite the change in substrate. Several other residues were modestly lower, by ∼0.3–3 mg g−1. The sum of the branched-chain amino acids Val, isoleucine and Leu decreased from 94.8 to approximately 90.0 mg g−1 in TB, whereas the combined amount of Tyr and Phe increased slightly from 50.9 to 51.6 mg g−1.

In the case of proline, the largest absolute difference, increasing by 5.28 mg g−1 (from 8.3 to 9.7%) was observed. As proline-rich proteins are characteristic components of insect structural tissues, including cuticle associated chitin protein complexes and other extracellular matrices (Ma et al., 2024), higher proline content may indicate diet-dependent shifts in these structural protein pools in TB. However, the overall proportion of essential amino acids remained unchanged despite the higher proline level. Thus, the tuna by-product diet preserves protein quality while allowing only modest shifts in non-essential amino acid composition (Montalbán et al., 2023; Thakur et al., 2025).

Fatty acid profiles

Feeding tuna by-products produced larvae with a dramatically higher content of unsaturated fatty acids than WB (Table 2). Total unsaturated fatty acids increased from 13.8 g/100 g DM in WB to 25.4 g/100 g DM in TB (+84.1%), driven by oleic acid (from 9.2 to 15.5 g/100 g DM) and linoleic acid (from 4.0 to 8.8 g/100 g DM). MUFA increased from 9.8 to 16.3 g/100 g DM and the ratio of unsaturated to saturated fatty acids improved from 2.9 to 3.4 despite only a modest rise in saturated fatty acids mainly due to palmitic acid (16:0) content. These results are consistent with greater deposition of oleic- and linoleic-acid-related lipid species in TB-fed larvae, indicating that substrate choice can substantially shift larval lipid profiles without an apparent reduction in crude protein. Therefore, these fatty acid data highlight both the potential for mealworms to divert surplus dietary energy into lipid storage and the importance of substrate choice, which can tune larval lipid chemistry without sacrificing protein, thereby enabling targeted oil remodeling to valorize marine by-products and achieve specific lipid profiles for food and feed (Morales et al., 2025).

LC-MS based metabolomic profiling and curated panel analysis

Global LC-MS metabolomic separation of WB and TB groups

To obtain a global overview of how the two diets shape the larval metabolome, we first examined whether WB and TB occupied distinct regions in multivariate space when all detected LC-MS features were considered together. In positive-ion mode, the PCA score plot (Fig. 2A) showed a clear separation between the WB and TB groups, with biological replicates tightly clustered within each diet, while the negative-ion PCA (Fig. 2B) displayed a similar pattern. As dietary factors were the dominant drivers of the separation in the score space, yielding stable, well-resolved clusters demonstrate a strong diet-dependent effect.

PLS-DA score plots in the positive and negative modes (Fig. 2C and D) reproduced the PCA separation. This supports the interpretation that the two feeding regimes produced reproducible and well-defined metabolic profiles rather than overlapping clouds. Volcano plots for positive and negative ion modes (Fig. 2E and F) highlighted numerous diet-responsive features as well. The multivariate LC-MS results showed that the two diets generated distinct metabolic fingerprints, consistent with a coordinated, diet-driven reorganization of the larval metabolome rather than changes in only a few analytes. These global patterns provide a system-level view of how metabolite distributions respond to substrates but do not indicate which individual molecules drive separation. To address this, we applied more stringent library searches and high-quality MS/MS spectral matching to identify specific diet-responsive features that contributed most strongly to observed group differences.

Cross-platform identification and construction of a curated metabolite panel

To corroborate the LC-MS findings and obtain more reliable assignments for major fatty acid-derived lipids, we analyzed the EtOH extracts using GC–MS. GC–MS of fatty acid-derived volatiles and semi-volatiles can be matched to well-curated EI libraries with relatively high confidence; therefore, these data provide an independent check of the dominant lipid classes resolved by FAME and LC-MS workflows (Vinaixa et al., 2016). Where methyl ester signals were detected, they were interpreted cautiously in light of sample preparation and potential analytical artefacts rather than assumed to represent native esterified forms.

In TB larvae, GC–MS profiles were dominated by cis-unsaturated C18 species, with oleic and linoleic acid derivatives representing the most abundant signals, whereas saturated C16–C18 fatty acids were present at lower relative levels. By contrast, WB larvae showed a broader distribution that included higher proportions of trans-C18 isomers, branched-chain saturated acids, and short-chain fatty acids (Tables S2 and S3, and Fig. S2). These GC–MS patterns were consistent with the FAME results in indicating higher cis-C18 unsaturated signals in TB, whereas WB were relatively richer in trans-C18 isomers, branched-chain saturated acids, and shorter-chain fatty acids. Together, these two platforms support the conclusion that the tuna by-product diet shifts the larval lipid profile toward oleic and linoleic acid derived species (Lim et al., 2018).

To complement the feature-centric Progenesis QI workflow and obtain an independent line of evidence for compound identities, we processed the LC-MS data in UNIFI using a curated metabolic profiling library. This software–library combination is widely used in targeted and untargeted metabolomics because it links accurate mass, isotope patterns and fragmentation behavior to predefined entries, and therefore provides a structured way to screen for plausible small-molecule and lipid candidates under defined matching criteria (Bittremieux, Wang, & Dorrestein, 2022).

Applying the UNIFI “Good Match” filters yielded 36 putative features that spanned both early polar metabolites and later-eluting lipid-derived species (Table S4). At short retention times between 0.76 and 4.6 min, the assignments included amino acids and small carbohydrates such as L-arginine, L-tyrosine (in both ion modes), D-tryptophan, sucrose, and 3-hydroxy-ε-methylglutarate. Most library matches appeared after approximately 17 min and were dominated by neutral and polar lipid signals. These late-eluting features include free fatty acids, such as palmitate (16:0), stearate (18:0), linoleic acid (18:2), an 18:3 octadecatrienoic acid isomer, an 18:1 octadecenoic acid isomer, monoacylglycerols, and related esters, such as 2-monoolein and propylene glycol monoleate. Several lysophosphatidylethanolamines (lyso-PEs) have been identified. LPE 16:0 was observed in the negative-ion mode, and LPE 18:0 and 1–18:1-lyso-PE were detected as matched positive- and negative-ion pairs at the same retention times.

The dataset contains multiple fatty amides, including tetradecanamide, hexadecanamide, octadecanamide, and erucamide, as well as hydrophobic components such as the diterpenoid sclareol and hydrocarbon 6-phenyldodecane. The mass errors were within ±2 ppm. In addition, species such as tyrosine and lyso-PEs were detected in more than one adducts form or ion mode, providing orthogonal support for these tentative assignments. From a broader perspective, the UNIFI-based panel reinforces the picture that emerges from FAME and GC–MS data. The recurrent detection of palmitate, stearate, and C18-unsaturated species, together with monoacylglycerols, lyso-PEs, and fatty amides, is consistent with an insect lipidome in which diet-responsive neutral lipids, membrane phospholipids, and signaling-related lipids are tightly interconnected (Gowda, Sasaki, Hasegawa, Chiba, & Hui, 2022). Using this staged approach, feature detection and multivariate statistics, followed by semi-targeted library screening and targeted structural validation, aligned with the current best practices in untargeted LC-MS metabolomics and helped support the reliability of the putative annotations, although these assignments remain tentative without validation using authentic standards (Schrimpe-Rutledge, Codreanu, Sherrod, & McLean, 2016).

To derive a compact and biologically interpretable marker set, we combined information from three sources: statistical filtering in Progenesis QI, library-based annotation in UNIFI, and GC–MS confirmation of fatty acid-derived species. Features were first selected in Progenesis QI using thresholds for the VIP score, fold change, and q-value. These candidates were then cross-checked against in-software libraries and the UNIFI metabolic profiling library, and finally compared with GC–MS identifications where EI spectra were available.

This stepwise integration produced a curated panel of 26 tentatively annotated metabolites with consistent dietary responses and chemically plausible structures (Fig. 3 and Table S5). Of these, nineteen compounds were elevated in TB and seven in WB. TB-enriched metabolites were dominated by lipid-derived species. These include free C16 and C18 fatty acids, such as myristic, palmitic, palmitoleic, stearic, and linoleic acids; several oxidized or conjugated C18 species; furan fatty acids; monoacylglycerols 1-monolinolein and 1-oleoyl-rac-glycerol; lyso-phosphatidylethanolamines (1–18:1-lyso-PE, LPE 16:0, and LPE 18:0); and fatty acid amides, such as octadecanamide. TB also contained higher levels of alpha-linolenic acid, linolenyl alcohol, and the nucleoside-related metabolites adenosine and hypoxanthine, which were consistent with increased activity in neutral and polar lipid pools and nucleotide metabolism.

By contrast, features more abundant in WB were more polar and carbohydrate-linked. This subset included di-linoleoyl galactosylglycerol, oligosaccharides (gentianose and maltotetraose), long-chain alkyl ferulate octacosyl ferulate, nucleoside uridine, the glycation product fructoselysine, and tryptophan. This pattern aligns with the proximate data, where WB larvae contained more carbohydrates, and with the cross-platform lipid results showing that feeding on tuna by-products yielded a more C18-unsaturated, storage-lipid-rich profile. Moreover, the overall structure of this panel is consistent with previous studies on edible insects: dietary manipulation of T. molitor and related species often produces coordinated changes in triacylglycerols, monoacylglycerols, free fatty acids and phospholipids, reflecting tight coupling between storage lipids and membrane remodeling under different substrate conditions (Siddiqui, Zeiri, & Asif Shah, 2025). Accordingly, the enrichment of C18:1-, C18:2- and C18:3-related signals in TB fits a lipidome that follows the fatty acid supply of the tuna by-products feed while preserving the protein fraction, as indicated by our proximate and amino acid data (Palupi et al., 2024; Perez-Santaescolastica, De Pril, Van de Voorde, & Fraeye, 2023). Conversely, WB-biased metabolites, including galactolipids, oligosaccharides, and phenolic ester conjugates, resemble plant- or cereal-derived signatures that can be partially retained or transformed in the insect, a pattern also reported for mealworms reared on fiber- and starch-rich by-products (Adamaki-Sotiraki, Choupi, Vrontaki, Rumbos, & Athanassiou, 2024; Vrontaki, Adamaki-Sotiraki, Rumbos, Anastasiadis, & Athanassiou, 2024).

From an application standpoint, the ability of a relatively small set of annotated metabolites to distinguish between the two diets suggests that this panel could be developed as a practical marker set for quality control and feed traceability in residue-to-insect bioconversion chains. Nevertheless, key metabolites should first be confirmed by targeted MS/MS and, where possible, by comparison with authentic standards (Pang et al., 2024).

Panel-based targeted multivariate analysis and model evaluation

Using a prespecified panel of 26 LC-MS features, we observed well-separated clusters for the two diets. PCA separated WB and TB with a tight within-group spread, and PLS-DA trained on the same panel yielded similarly clean separation (Fig. 4A and B). At the class-level, the donut chart confirmed that this panel is dominated by fatty acids and related lipid species, with smaller contributions from carbohydrates, nucleosides/bases, and indole/tryptophan derivatives (Fig. 4C). Consistently, heatmaps showed a clear block pattern indicating that TB replicates were consistently higher in lipid-derived signals, including representative C18 fatty acids, monoacylglycerols, and lyso-phospholipids, whereas WB replicates were characterized by higher levels of polar features such as uridine and sugar-like compounds (Fig. 4D and E).

In addition, the VIP plot highlighted the same key discriminators, with high VIP scores for both TB-enriched C18 lipid species and WB-enriched nucleoside and carbohydrate-related metabolites (Fig. 4F). Metabolite-set enrichment analysis further emphasized lipid-centered shifts, particularly in fatty acids, linoleic-acid derivatives, and monoacylglycerols, together with a smaller set of carbohydrate-related changes (Fig. 4G). As the analysis relied on a small curated panel, these results should be viewed as a diagnostic summary rather than a full untargeted landscape. The panel aligned with earlier compositional data: TB larvae had higher bulk lipids and a shift toward C18 unsaturated in the FAME analysis, and the LC-MS signals that dominated the TB side were congruent with that pattern. In contrast, WB-enriched uridine and sugar class signals were consistent with higher carbohydrate partitioning in proximate analyses and may reflect differences in nucleotide and carbohydrate flux. Consequently, the panel provides a practical diet-response fingerprint that can be used for quality control and monitoring of the residue-to-insect bioconversion workflows.

Although individual annotations remain tentative without targeted MS/MS confirmation, the convergence across analytical platforms supports the robustness of this diet-response fingerprint. From an application perspective, such a compact metabolite panel could be further refined into practical markers for process monitoring and quality control in residue-to-insect bioconversion workflows, enabling feed-driven modulation of mealworm ingredient composition for food and feed applications.

Antioxidant activity of WB and TB larvae

To determine whether dietary treatment translated into differences in functional antioxidant capacity, we evaluated DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activity of 80% EtOH extracts prepared from WB and TB at 0, 12.5, 25, and 50 mg DM per mL (Fig. 5). Both extracts showed dose-dependent quenching, but at 25 and 50 mg DM per mL the TB extracts consistently left fewer radicals than WB, indicating enhanced antioxidant activity. This trend was further supported by IC50 values, with TB extracts showing lower values than WB in both assays. In the DPPH assay, the IC50 value of TB was 29.53 mg DM per mL, compared to 67.89 mg DM per mL for WB. Similarly, in the ABTS assay, TB exhibited an IC50 value of 34.68 mg DM per mL, whereas WB showed a higher value of 90.26 mg DM per mL, confirming the relatively higher radical scavenging efficiency of TB extracts. Ascorbic acid, used as a reference standard, showed markedly lower IC50 values, with 10.84 μg/mL in the DPPH assay and 7.65 μg/mL in the ABTS assay. This difference reflects the inherent distinction between crude extracts and pure antioxidant compounds. Thus, short-term TB feeding enhanced the antioxidant capacity of larval extracts at equivalent dry-matter doses. The observed differences are consistent with diet-associated changes in extractable metabolites identified by untargeted metabolomic analysis, together with broader compositional shifts. These findings support the use of fishery by-products as functional feed inputs capable of modulating both nutritional composition and antioxidant properties of mealworm-derived ingredients.

Cytotoxicity of WB and TB

To evaluate the potential for food applications, we assessed cell viability of WB and TB extracts using RAW264.7 murine macrophage cells as a representative mammalian model for preliminary cytotoxicity screening, as these cells are highly sensitive to xenobiotic-induced cellular responses (Ki, Baek, Hawkes, Kim, & Yeon Hwang, 2023; Lee et al., 2024). Cell viability remained high across the tested extract concentrations (0.3125–5 mg DM equivalent per mL) for both WB and TB groups (Fig. 6). In TB, cell viability remained around the baseline level across the tested concentration range, indicating no apparent cytotoxicity. In contrast, WB exhibited a modest decrease in viability at higher doses, suggesting a slight cytotoxicity effect only under concentrated exposure conditions. In WB, the modest reduction in viability at higher concentrations may reflect a mild, dose-dependent cellular stress response rather than overt cytotoxicity. These results indicate a favorable biosafety profile for mealworm extracts, particularly those derived from tuna by-product feeding, supporting their potential for broader utilization as nutritionally relevant insect-derived ingredients.

Conclusion

Feeding T. molitor larvae with tuna by-products produced biomass with higher lipid and fatty acid contents, without compromising crude protein levels and an essential amino acid balance comparable to the wheat bran group. Amino acid profiles showed only modest diet-dependent shifts, with consistently higher proline levels in TB, indicating preserved protein quality despite changes in macronutrient partitioning. Untargeted LC–MS, supported by GC–MS and library-based annotation, yielded a compact set of diet-discriminant metabolites dominated by C18 lipid-derived species in TB, whereas WB was characterized by a smaller set of more polar, carbohydrate-related features, providing a practical chemical fingerprint for ingredient characterization and monitoring. EtOH extracts from TB showed stronger antioxidant activity and no apparent cytotoxicity compared with WB extracts across the tested concentration range, suggesting diet-associated bioactivity-associated differences. Collectively, the present findings demonstrate that tuna by-products can serve as an effective alternative rearing substrate for producing mealworm ingredients with distinct lipid chemistry and reproducible metabolomic signatures, while preserving protein quality, thereby supporting their potential integration into food-oriented insect ingredient systems.
