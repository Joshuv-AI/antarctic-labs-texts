Time to Resolution of Diabetic Ketoacidosis and Its Predictors among Adult Diabetic Patients in Public Hospitals of Northeast, Ethiopia
Authors: Ayele Bayih, Zenebe Abebe, Awraris Hailu, Yihenew Sewale Bizu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite the discovery and development of diagnostic tests, medications, and technologies, diabetic ketoacidosis remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality among patients with diabetes. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the time to resolution of diabetic ketoacidosis and its predictors among adult diabetic patients in public hospitals of the North Shoa Zone, Northeast Ethiopia. A retrospective follow-up study was conducted among 340 patients with complete medical records from February 1, 2021, to February 1, 2024. Data were analyzed using STATA version 16. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and the log-rank test were used to assess differences in survival among groups. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals, and model fitness was assessed using the Cox–Snell residual test. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed to identify predictors of time to resolution of diabetic ketoacidosis among adult diabetic patients. Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable model, and predictors were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval. The median time to resolution of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) among the 340 participants was 14 h (95% CI: 13–16). Age groups 45–54 years (AHR = 0.47; 95% CI: 0.28–0.79) and ≥ 55 years (AHR = 0.24; 95% CI: 0.12–0.45), moderate DKA (AHR = 0.23; 95% CI: 0.16–0.32), severe DKA (AHR = 0.10; 95% CI: 0.06–0.17), and initial random blood sugar > 500 mg/dL (AHR = 0.49; 95% CI: 0.36–0.65) were significantly associated with a slower time to DKA resolution. In contrast, having type 2 diabetes mellitus (AHR = 4.20; 95% CI: 2.42–7.34), a duration of diabetes mellitus > 5 years (AHR = 6.65; 95% CI: 2.50–17.76), and not being newly diagnosed (AHR = 6.45; 95% CI: 2.42–17.18) were significantly associated with a faster time to DKA resolution. The median time to resolution of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) was 14 h. Age greater than 45 years, moderate to severe diabetic ketoacidosis, high initial blood glucose levels, and newly diagnosed diabetes were associated with slower resolution. In contrast, type 2 diabetes, duration of diabetes mellitus greater than 5 years, and not being newly diagnosed were associated with faster resolution. Consequently, early risk stratification and targeted management may improve DKA outcomes.

Introduction

Background

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by hyperglycemia resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both. Diabetes mellitus has reached epidemic proportions worldwide, affecting an estimated 537 million adults in 2021, and is projected to rise to 643 million by 2030 and 783 million by 2045 if current trends continue1. Chronic hyperglycemia predisposes patients to both macrovascular and microvascular complications, but it also places individuals at high risk for several life‑threatening acute metabolic complications, one of the most serious being diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)2.

Diabetic ketoacidosis is a complex metabolic state characterized by hyperglycemia, ketosis, and metabolic acidosis, resulting from an absolute or relative insulin deficiency combined with increased counter‑regulatory hormones such as glucagon, cortisol, catecholamines, and growth hormone3. Globally, DKA remains a major cause of hospitalization and mortality among people with diabetes, with higher incidence and poorer outcomes in resource‑limited settings4. Although treatable with appropriate medical care, DKA places significant strain on healthcare systems worldwide. The overall prevalence of DKA varies widely, influenced by healthcare access, diabetes awareness, and socioeconomic factors. In high‑income countries, structured diabetes education and emergency care protocols have reduced mortality rates to < 1% in many centers5. However, in low‑ and middle‑income countries, mortality rates remain higher, sometimes exceeding 10%, due to delayed presentation, limited diagnostic tools, and inadequate treatment resources6. Moreover, hospitalizations for DKA have been increasing globally, driven by rising prevalence of diabetes, suboptimal adherence to insulin therapy, and increased life expectancy of individuals with diabetes7. Diabetic ketoacidosis remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among diabetic patients8–10. The mortality rate ranges from 2 to 5% in developed countries, whereas in developing countries, the rates ranges from 6–24%11.

Time to resolution (TTR) refers to the duration from initiation of therapy to when metabolic abnormalities (hyperglycemia, ketosis, and acidemia) normalize, indicating recovery from the acute episode10. The DKA resolution time is not only a marker of the effectiveness of clinical management but also corresponds with hospital length of stay (LOS), resource utilization, and costs of care12. International guidelines recommend resolution criteria such as normalization of the anion gap, serum bicarbonate ≥ 18 meq/L, and pH > 7.3013. However, the actual time and parameters taken to achieve resolution varies considerably across settings and patient populations14. In developed nations such as Thailand, Australia, and Saudi Arabia, the median times for DKA resolution were 8.5 h, 11 h, and 15 h, respectively15–17. Conversely, in African countries such as South Africa and Kenya, the median times to resolution for DKA were 21 h and 59 h, respectively18,19. In Ethiopia, a study revealed that the median resolution time from DKA was 24 h20.

A study in Qatar reported that lower sodium and potassium levels and prolonged duration of DKA were associated with slower resolution time of DKA21. The studies conducted in different countries reported that older age, severity of DKA, initial blood glucose level, duration of DM, type of diabetes, and presence of other health conditions were a predictors of resolution time of DKA16,20,22–25.

Diabetes care in Ethiopia faces substantial challenges including limited access to insulin and diabetes supplies, shortage of trained healthcare professionals, and barriers to timely emergency care26. A study in Ethiopia hospitals have highlighted the high burden of DKA with common precipitating factors of infections and poor treatment adherence, and significant morbidity and mortality22. A study conducted in Ethiopia found that the median recovery time of DKA was 42 h, with blood glucose level, duration of diabetes, and severity of DKA significantly associated with prolonged recovery27. The studies in Ethiopia have reported the time to resolution of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA); however, most are limited to single institutions, restricting generalizability and often overlooking clinically important factors such as diabetes type. Understanding the time to DKA resolution and its predictors is crucial for improving patient outcomes, guiding evidence-based clinical practice, supporting clinical decision-making, optimizing resource utilization, and informing future research. Therefore, this study aimed to estimate the time to DKA resolution and identify its predictors among diabetic patients admitted to public hospitals in the North Shoa Zone, Northeast Ethiopia.

Methods and materials

Study area and period

The study was conducted in public hospitals in the North Shoa Zone, Northeast Ethiopia approximately 130 km north of Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia. According to data from the North Shoa Zone Health Bureau, the zone has 11 hospitals: one tertiary hospital (Debre Berhan Comprehensive Specialized Hospital), three general hospitals (Enat, Mahil Meda, and Hakim Gizaw), and seven primary hospitals (Debre Sina, Showa Robit, Ataye, Deneba, Molale Meda, and Arerti). All hospitals provide essential services for diabetes mellitus (DM) and its complications. Currently, more than 1,000 DM patients are receiving follow-up care at study settings.

Study design and period

A retrospective follow-up study was conducted from February 1, 2021, to February 1, 2024, among adults admitted with diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), with data collected from May 3 to 29, 2024.

Population

Source population

All adult DKA patients admitted and received treatment at public hospitals in the North Shoa Zone, Northeast Ethiopia.

Study population

Adult DKA patients admitted to and receiving treatment at selected public hospitals in the North Shoa Zone from February 1, 2021, to February 1, 2024.

Sample population

Adult patients with diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) who were admitted to and treated at public hospitals in the North Shoa Zone between February 1, 2021, and February 1, 2024, were included in the study.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

The study included individuals aged 18 years and older who were diagnosed with diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) at public hospitals in the North Shoa Zone between February 1, 2021, and February 1, 2024.

Exclusion criteria

Adult patients were excluded if their medical records were incomplete or unavailable, if they were diagnosed with DKA during pregnancy or if they were initially admitted for other underlying health conditions and subsequently developed DKA.

Sampling technique and procedure

A stratified multistage sampling technique was employed. One (01) tertiary hospital, two (02) general hospitals, and three (03) primary hospitals were selected, with general and primary hospitals chosen by lottery. All individuals diagnosed with diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and treated at the selected hospitals were identified from the emergency outpatient department (EOPD) registration books. Patients were listed using their unique medical record numbers (MRN) starting from February 1, 2021, for each hospital. The sample size was then proportionally allocated to the selected hospitals, and patient charts were selected using a computer-generated simple random sampling technique. Over the three-year period, a total of 734 DKA patients were treated in the EOPD (Fig. 1).

Variable of the study

Dependent variable

Time to resolution of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).

Independent variables

Patient-related predictors of DKA outcomes include age, sex, and residence. Clinical factors comprise initial blood glucose, DKA severity, precipitating factors, duration of DKA symptoms, type and duration of diabetes, and frequency of follow-up. Comorbidities such as acute or chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, and infections may also influence outcomes, and treatment-related predictors include potassium supplementation, management of precipitating factors, and complications arising during DKA treatment, such as hypoglycemia or hypokalemia.

Operational definition

Event is a resolution of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), occurring when a patient achieved documented clinical improvement with blood glucose < 200 mg/dL and negative urine ketones during the follow-up period.

Censored is a patient who did not achieve DKA resolution during follow-up due to referral, discharge against medical advice, death, or end of the study period.

Diabetic ketoacidosis is an acute metabolic complication of diabetes mellitus characterized by hyperglycemia with blood glucose ≥ 250 mg/dl, the presence of ketonuria, and compatible clinical features, as documented in the medical record28,29.

Resolution of diabetic ketoacidosis is confirmed when, both blood glucose levels < 200 mg/dl and negative urine ketones (confirmed repeatedly in 1–2 h) are met30,31. In this study, resolution of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) was defined as achieving both glycemic control and cessation of ketogenesis, ensuring full normalization of the acute metabolic derangements rather than a transient improvement. While traditional guidelines define DKA resolution by normalization of serum bicarbonate, venous pH, anion-gap closure, and blood glucose < 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L), the core pathophysiology focuses on cessation of ketone production and correction of acidemia rather than glucose alone. Because ketone clearance typically lags behind glucose correction, relying solely on glucose may overestimate recovery. In resource-limited settings without direct measurement of β-hydroxybutyrate, qualitative urine ketone testing every 1–2 h was used alongside sustained blood glucose < 200 mg/dL to confirm resolution of ketosis. This approach aligns with physiological principles, ensuring both acid–base restoration and ketone elimination, and reduces the risk of premature transition from acute care before complete DKA resolution.

Incomplete data defined as a patient medical records missing one or more essential variables required to determine time to DKA resolution or its predictors. These included absence of documented time of initiation of DKA treatment, time of documented DKA resolution, or key laboratory parameters used to confirm resolution.

Infection prior to diabetes ketoacidosis define as the presence of a documented clinical diagnosis of infection of pneumonia, urinary tract infection, sepsis, or other infections recorded in the medical chart before or at the time of admission for diabetic ketoacidosis, supported by clinical findings, laboratory results, or initiation of antimicrobial therapy.

Data collection tool and procedure

A structured data extraction tool was prepared by considering study variables such as sociodemographic, biochemical, clinical, and treatment predictors, as well as treatment outcomes, based on the literature with similar objectives to this study and using the DKA follow-up sheet form16–18,20. Data were obtained from patients’ charts, with the time from the first vascular fluid administration considered the starting point and the endpoint was either DKA resolution or censoring. Data collection was conducted by nurses under the supervision of the principal investigator. Extracted information was systematically entered and managed using Kobo Toolbox, allowing structured digital recording, cleaning, and secures storage, while maintaining the original paper-based records as the primary source.

Measurement

Resolution of diabetes ketoacidosis was defined as documented clinical improvement accompanied by blood glucose < 200 mg/dl and negative urine ketones. Time to resolution was calculated as the interval (in hours) from initiation of DKA treatment to documented resolution or it calculated from the first vascular filling to treat the DKA in the emergency room or intensive care unit to diabetes ketoacidosis resolution32. This definition aligns with common clinical practice in settings where arterial or venous pH, bicarbonate, and anion gap measurements are not routinely available. Although complete ketone negativity is not mandated in all international guidelines, urine ketone clearance remains a practical and widely used marker of recovery in low-resource settings.

Prolonged resolution of diabetes ketoacidosis was operationally defined as failure to achieve DKA resolution within 24 h after initiation of treatment33–36.

Quality assurance

A properly designed and structured checklist was used for data collection. Prior to the actual data collection, 5% (18 medical charts) of the sample size was pretested at Debre Berhan Comprehensive Specialized Hospital to ensure the availability and clarity of required variables in patient records. One day of training was provided to four data collectors and supervisors. Throughout the data collection period, the principal investigator and supervisors closely monitored the process, reviewed data for completeness, consistency, clarity, and accuracy, and made necessary corrections. The principal investigator also performed careful data entry and thorough data cleaning before the start of analysis.

Data processing and analysis

Data were collected using Kobo Toolbox version 2021.2.4. The data were coded, cleaned, and checked for completeness and consistency before being exported to STATA version 16 for analysis. Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of patients were summarized in tables. Continuous variables were described using medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The Kaplan–Meier survival method was used to estimate time to DKA resolution, and differences in resolution time across categorical variables were compared using the log-rank test. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with a maximum value of 1.82. A Cox proportional hazards regression model was employed to identify predictors of time to DKA resolution. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals, with a global test value of 0.1027, indicating no violation. Model fitness was assessed using Cox–Snell residuals and demonstrated an adequate fit (Fig. 2). Variables with a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariable analysis were included in the multivariable Cox regression model, and statistical significance was declared at p ≤ 0.05 with 95% confidence intervals. The outcome variable was dichotomized as censored (0) or DKA resolution1.

Clinical characteristics and biochemical profiles of study participants

Among 340 participants, the majority of participants, 273 (80.3%) had type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM), and 234 (68.8%) had established diabetes. The most common clinical presentation of DKA patients was the classic triad of polyuria, polydipsia, and polyphagia, reported in 95.6% of cases. The median duration of DKA symptoms before treatment was 24 h (IQR: 12–48 h). Most patients presented with mild DKA 233 (68.5%). Previous DKA episodes were reported in 186 (56.2%) of participants, and infection proceeding of DKA episode was identified in 95 (28%) cases. Comorbidities were present in 71 (20.9%) patients with hypertension being the most common (30, 42.3%). The most frequent complication related to DKA or its treatment was hypokalemia, occurring in 89.4% of patients (Table 3).

Time to resolution from diabetic ketoacidosis

Among the 340 study participants, 315 (92.65%) adults achieved resolution of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), while the remaining 25 (7.35%) were censored. Participants were observed for a minimum of 4 h and a maximum of 64 h, yielding a total of 2,943.5 person-hours at risk. The overall recovery rate from DKA was 5.77 per 100 person-hours (95% CI: 0.05–0.07), with a median time to resolution of 14 h (95% CI: 13–16).The median resolution time varied according to patient characteristics, biochemical profiles, and DKA severity. Patients aged 18–24 years had a median DKA resolution time of 10 h (IQR: 8–12), compared with 11 h (IQR: 9–13) for those aged 25–34 years and 11 h (IQR: 7.5–20) for those aged 35–44 years. In contrast, markedly longer median recovery times were observed among older patients, reaching 22 h (IQR: 17–28) in the 45–54 age group and 24 h (IQR: 19–30) among those aged ≥ 55 years. Patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus exhibited a longer median time to resolution of 16 h (95% CI: 14–17), whereas those with type 2 diabetes mellitus recovered more rapidly, with a median time of 9 h (95% CI: 7–10). Stratification by DKA severity further demonstrated a stepwise increase in recovery time, with median durations of 12 h (95% CI: 10–13) for mild DKA, 22 h (95% CI: 20–27) for moderate DKA, and 44 h (95% CI: 40–46) for severe DKA (Table 4).

Survival estimates of resolution time of DKA

By 14 h, 50% of patients with diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) had achieved resolution. By 20 h, approximately 70% had recovered, and by 60 h, over 90% had resolved their episode. Notably, 20–25% of patients required more than 24 h for complete resolution. Overall, the majority of DKA cases responded to treatment within the first 24 h of initiation (Fig. 3).

Comparison of resolution status

Patients with severe DKA had a slower resolution with a median time of 44 h (95% CI: 40–46), compared with 22 h (95% CI: 20–27) of moderate DKA and 12 h (95% CI: 10–13) of mild DKA (Fig. 4). A Type 2 diabetes patients had a faster resolution time than type 1 diabetes patients with a median time of 9 h (95% CI: 7–10) versus 16 h (95% CI: 14–17) (Fig. 5). Additionally, patients with initial blood glucose levels > 500 mg/dl had slower resolution times with a median of 21 h (95% CI: 20–25), compared with 12 h (95% CI: 10–12) among those with blood glucose ≤ 500 mg/dl (Fig. 6).

Predictors of time resolution from diabetic ketoacidosis

Variables including age, sex, residence, type of diabetes, duration of diabetes mellitus, initial blood glucose level, severity of DKA, previous DKA episodes, and treatment center showed a p-value < 0.25 in the bivariable analysis and were included in the multivariable analysis. In the multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression model, severity of DKA, type of diabetes mellitus, duration of diabetes mellitus, initial blood glucose level, and age were statistically significant predictors with resolution time of DKA at p-value ≤ 0.05.

Patients aged 45–54 years had a slower rate of DKA resolution time compared to those aged 15–24 years (AHR = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.28–0.79). Patients aged ≥ 55 years had a slower rate of DKA resolution time than as compared to aged 15–24 years (AHR = 0.24, 95% CI: 0.12–0.45). Patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus had a 4.2-fold faster rate of resolution time of DKA compared to patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus (AHR = 4.20, 95% CI: 2.42–7.34).

Patients with severe DKA had a slower rate of resolution time compared with those with mild DKA (AHR = 0.10, 95% CI: 0.059–0.17). Likewise, patients with moderate DKA had a slower resolution time than patients with mild DKA (AHR = 0.23, 95% CI: 0.16–0.32). Patients with random blood sugar (RBS) > 500 mg/dl experienced a slower rate resolution time compared with those with RBS ≤ 500 mg/dl (AHR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.36–0.65).

Moreover, participants with diabetes duration > 5 years had a 6.65 times faster DKA resolution time compared with those with duration ≤ 5 years (AHR = 6.65, 95% CI: 2.50–17.76). Finally, patients who were not newly diagnosed with diabetes had a 6.45 times faster resolution time compared with newly diagnosed patients (AHR = 6.45, 95% CI: 2.42–17.18) (Table 6).

Discussions

The median time of DKA resolution was 14 h (95% CI: 13–16).This finding is consistent with a study conducted in Saudi Arabia 15 h12 and Egypt 15 h37. The possible justification might be that the use of comparable, guideline-based management protocols involving early intravenous fluid resuscitation, continuous insulin infusion, and frequent monitoring of blood glucose and electrolytes, which facilitate rapid correction of metabolic abnormalities. In addition, the studies involved predominantly adult patient populations with likely comparable severity of DKA at presentation, particularly with respect to pH, serum bicarbonate, and anion gap levels key determinants of recovery time. Furthermore, management in hospital settings with adequate laboratory support and trained healthcare professionals may have enabled timely treatment adjustments and close monitoring, contributing to similar DKA resolution times across these settings. However, the finding is shorter than studies conducted in Debre Markos 24 h10–36, Qatar 18 h. (10.5–29)21, Addis Ababa 27 h.(16–38)4, Kenya 30.0 h. (95% CI: 24.0, 48.0)38, Saudi Arabia 23.7 h. (16.2–36.9)39 and Ethiopia 42 h27. The possible discrepancy might be differences in the severity of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) at presentation may explain the observed discrepancies, as more severe acidosis or the presence of comorbidities can prolong recovery in some studies. In contrast, earlier hospital presentation and prompt initiation of fluid resuscitation and insulin therapy along with better healthcare resources, intensive monitoring, and adherence to standardized management protocols likely contributed to the faster resolution observed in this study. Furthermore, variations in the definitions of DKA resolution, as well as differences in patient characteristics such as age, precipitating factors, and baseline glycemic control, may also account for the differences in recovery times. However, the finding is slower than studies conducted in Osaka 11 and 10 h40, and Australia 11 h. (6.5–16.5)17, Thailand 8.5 h15. The possible reason might be a difference in population characteristics, baseline health status and methodological variations, including study design, measurement techniques, and timing definitions, may also contribute to slower results. Additionally, environmental and lifestyle factors, such as diet, physical activity, and regional stressors, could affect physiological or behavioral responses, variations in healthcare access, monitoring, and intervention practices may further account for these differences, and smaller sample sizes or greater variability in the study population may lead to longer average times compared with larger or more homogeneous cohorts. A possible explanation for the discrepancy could be differences in the treatment protocols and socioeconomic status of the patients. A study that insulin pump therapy leads to better outcomes than injections dose33 and could be low socioeconomic status, since low educational levels are linked to a higher risk of severe diabetic ketoacidosis41.

Patients aged 45–54 years had slower DKA resolution times compared with those aged 15–24 years. This finding is supported by a study conducted in Kenya38,Egypt37, and Saudi Arabia12,39. This possible reason could be age-related physiological changes, such as reduced renal and hepatic function, slower metabolic adaptation, which can delay recovery. Older adults also tend to have more comorbid conditions, including cardiovascular disease or chronic kidney disease, which complicates fluid and insulin management. Additionally, decreased insulin sensitivity, delayed recognition of symptoms, and potential effects of polypharmacy may further prolonged metabolic stabilization in this age group.

Participant aged ≥ 55 years had slower resolution times compared with those aged 15–24 years. This findings is supported by a studies conducted in Kenya38,Egypt37, and Saudi Arabia12,39. The possible reason might be that older adults tend to have more comorbid conditions, including a cardiovascular disease which complicates fluid and insulin management.

Patients who had > 500 mg/dl random blood sugar were slower resolutions as compared to those who had ≤ 500 mg/dl. This finding is supported by studies conducted in Ethiopia20, Saudi Arabia16,and Addis Ababa4. The possible reason might be that an extremely high blood glucose concentration can suppress the body’s insulin secretion and impair glucose removal by down regulating glucose transporter systems, potentially prolonging the resolution time. Additionally, elevated blood glucose levels may exacerbate acidosis and dehydration, leading to electrolyte imbalances such as potassium and sodium imbalances, which in turn worsen hyperglycemia and complicate DKA management. Moreover, patients with very high blood glucose concentrations may experience more severe complications, including electrolyte imbalances and organ dysfunction, further contributing to prolonged resolution time.

Patients who were no newly diagnosed had a slower resolution times compared with newly diagnosed. This finding is supported by studies performed in Israel42 and France32. The possible reason may be explained by lack of prior metabolic adaptation and insulin therapy in newly diagnosed patients, which can result in more severe hyperglycemia and delayed biochemical stabilization. In contrast, patients with an established diagnosis often have some degree of ongoing glycemic management, better awareness of disease symptoms, and earlier recognition of complications, which can be facilitate faster resolution. Additionally, newly diagnosed patients may present with more profound insulin deficiency or dehydration, further prolonging recovery time.

Patient who had DM duration > 5 years were a faster resolution time as compared to DM duration ≤ 5 years. This finding is supported by studies performed in Israel42 and France32, Ethiopia20, and Kenya38. This possible reason may be explained that prior metabolic adaptation and insulin therapy, which can result in less hyperglycemia and delayed biochemical stabilization and patients with an established diagnosis often have some degree of ongoing glycemic management, better awareness of disease symptoms, and earlier recognition of complications, all of which can facilitate faster resolution.

Patients with moderate and severe DKA had slower resolution times compared with those with mild DKA. This finding is supported by studies conducted in Ethiopia20, Kenya38, Saudi Arabia39, Addis Ababa4. The possible reason could be that disease pathophysiology, patients with severe DKA had more electrolyte abnormalities than patients with mild and moderate forms of the disease, higher admission potassium levels and PH are independent predictors of a slower time to resolution from DKA17.Additionally, there might be due to greater dehydration, severe electrolyte imbalances, and higher insulin requirements that prolong metabolic stabilization.

Patient who was type 2 DM were 4.2 times faster resolution times as compared to type1 DM. This finding is in line with a study conducted in Thailand43. The possible justification might be variations in pathophysiology and disease management between the two types. Type 2 diabetes is typically characterized by insulin resistance rather than absolute insulin deficiency, and patients often retain some endogenous insulin production, which may facilitate faster metabolic recovery. Additionally, type 2 patients are generally older and may have a shorter duration of hyperglycemia at presentation, allowing for more rapid response to interventions. In contrast, type 1 diabetes involves absolute insulin deficiency, requiring careful titration and longer stabilization periods. Another reason might be differences in comorbidities, treatment adherence, and baseline glycemic control may also contribute to the observed faster resolution in type 2 diabetes. However, these findings contradict with studies conducted in the UK44 and France32. The observed discrepancy may be attributed to variations in the severity of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) across study populations. In the studies used for comparison, patients with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) appeared to present with less severe forms of DKA compared to those with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), which contrasts with the findings of the present study. This difference could be related to variations in patient characteristics, timing of diagnosis, access to healthcare services, or differences in clinical management across settings. Additionally, heterogeneity in study design and diagnostic criteria for DKA severity may have contributed to the observed variation.

Conclusions

Half of the patients recovered within 14 h of treatment initiation. Prolonged resolution was significantly associated with moderate to severe DKA, initial blood glucose > 500 mg/dL, and newly diagnosed diabetes, whereas type 2 diabetes predicted faster recovery. Therefore, patients should monitor glucose levels, adhere to treatment, and seek prompt care. Clinicians should prioritize high-risk groups using evidence-based protocols, while health authorities should ensure adequate resources, prevention programs, and ongoing training and researchers should explore additional predictors to improve DKA outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Using secondary data, we were unable to assess certain predictors of time to DKA resolution, including admission pH, potassium levels, and adherence to treatment protocols. The analysis focused on the number of DKA episodes rather than individual patients, so those with multiple episodes may have been included more than once, potentially affecting results due to varying clinical characteristics and treatment responses. Data on baseline or chronic medications, including SGLT-2 inhibitors, were unavailable, limiting adjustment for potential confounders. Furthermore, missing physiological data—such as pH, bicarbonate, anion gap, creatinine, and lactate prevented adjustment for these variables, despite guidelines recommending pH > 7.3, bicarbonate ≥ 18 mmol/L, and anion gap closure as standard criteria for DKA resolution.
