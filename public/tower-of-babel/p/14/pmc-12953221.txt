Microscopic mechanism of anyon superconductivity emerging from fractional Chern insulators
Authors: Fabian Pichler, Clemens Kuhlenkamp, Michael Knap, Ashvin Vishwanath
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryFractional quantum Hall (FQH) states and superconductors typically require contrasting conditions, yet recent experiments have observed them in the same device. A natural explanation is that mobile anyons give rise to superconductivity. However, realizing this requires an unusual energy hierarchy that binds minimally charged anyons, a scenario that requires an additional mechanism in a repulsive system. Here, we show that such an energy hierarchy arises naturally in fractional Chern insulators (FCIs) at fillings ν = 2/(4p∓1) when they are driven toward a quantum phase transition into a “semion crystal”—a charge density wave (CDW) with semion topological order. Near the transition, Cooper-pair correlations are enhanced, so that a charge-2e superconductor appears with doping. Using tensor network simulations of a repulsive Hubbard-Hofstadter model at ν = 2/3, we demonstrate a transition from an FCI to a robust semion crystal, identifying the semion crystal as a viable competing phase together with conventional CDW and FQH states. Our framework unifies recent approaches to anyon superconductivity, reconciles it with strong repulsion, and provides guidance for flat-band moiré materials such as twisted MoTe2.

Introduction

Anyons, quasiparticles with fractional statistics, and often with fractional charge, are the emergent low-energy excitations of strongly correlated systems with topological order.1,2 The first experimental realization of anyons occurred in fractional quantum Hall (FQH) states, discovered in fractionally filled Landau levels.3 However, charged anyons in the Landau levels of conventional FQH states lack kinetic energy, due to the strong magnetic field. This enables the formation of incompressible FQH plateaus, but restricts dynamics and prohibits the emergence of competing phases, such as superconductors.4 Yet, a dispersion can be imprinted on anyons when a periodic potential is applied to a Landau level5,6 or when the fractional state results from a Chern band.7,8,9,10,11 It was realized early on that a gas of charged anyons with a finite dispersion may form a superconductor.12,13,14,15,16,17,18,19,20 Recent experiments with two-dimensional semiconductor heterostructures have indeed observed both fractional Chern insulators (FCIs) stabilized by magnetic fields21,22 and fractional quantum anomalous Hall (FQAH) states at zero fields.23,24,25,26,27 FQAH states are thus realized on a lattice and are often stable down to zero magnetic field. These properties endow the anyons with finite kinetic energy, opening up the possibility for the formation of competing states, not available in continuum Landau levels.6,28,29,30,31 Several recent developments in moiré materials,32 including the observation of superconductivity in MoTe2,33 the same material in which FQAH states have been realized,23,24,25,26 has rekindled interest in connections between superconductivity and anyons.30,31,34

A key ingredient in determining whether a liquid of charged anyons actually leads to a superconductor hinges on the choice of anyon. Recent theoretical studies of doping anyons point to the following simple diagnostic, at least for abelian anyons. Fuse the anyon repeatedly until a local excitation (a finite group of electrons) appears. If the first local excitation is bosonic (e.g., a Cooper pair), a doped gas is expected to superconduct; if instead the first local excitation is fermionic (e.g., the electron), superconductivity is pre-empted by other phases. Indeed, the charge-e semion of Laughlin’s original proposal12,13 and in Divic et al.,34 as well as the30 charge-2e/3 anyon of the Laughlin ν = 1/3 state satisfy this condition by fusing with themselves to directly give a charge-2e boson; a finite density of these excitations is an “anyon superconductor,” in contrast to doping the charge-e/3 anyon of the Laughlin state. However, the latter are generally believed to be energetically cheaper given the predominantly repulsive interactions and their minimal charge, and are more commonly realized as dopants in experiments. Hence, the central challenge in engineering superconductivity out of FCIs is energetics: one must arrange for the right anyon to be the cheapest charge carrier and the one activated on doping. Consequently, in the previous example, we must ensure that the energy cost of a 2e/3 anyon is lower than that of two well-separated e/3 anyons. In this paper, we provide a general mechanism for realizing this energetic hierarchy.

Simply put, our mechanism invokes a third phase, in addition to the FCI and superconductor. This phase is an insulator but with semion topological order, which, for general filling, breaks translation symmetry—hence, we call it a semion crystal (SX). We will argue that if the FCI is in proximity to the SX, more precisely if it is near a Quantum Critical Point (QCP) in that phase, then one is guaranteed to have the correct anyon energetics to favor superconductivity. Such a topological criticality mechanism for superconductivity has recently been discussed for integer Chern states adjacent to chiral spin liquids (CSLs),34 which provides a modern perspective on anyon superconductivity of charge-e semions.12,13 The semion crystal phase is quite natural in the FCI context. It has previously come up to explain insulators with vanishing Chern response observed in MoTe2.35 The appearance of a charge density wave (CDW) with semion topological order seems surprising at first sight; the following viewpoint may help clarify why it emerges here. In the same triangular lattice Hubbard model with π/2 flux per plaquette, at unit filling per site, a CSL is well established.36 For the case considered in this work, at 2/3 filling, the system instead breaks translation symmetry, forming a new triangular lattice with a three times larger unit cell, such that each elementary plaquette now encloses 3π/2 = -π/2 (mod2π) flux. The effective lattice again favors a CSL, but with reversed chirality, thus rationalizing both the emergence of the SX and the predicted opposite chirality of the topological order.

While not essential, these discussions are most transparent when the FQH states arise from electrons with a two-component spin index, which may correspond to physical spin or an approximate pseudo-spin, such as a layer or valley index.36,37 In the following, we refer to this additional flavor simply as spin. At the critical point of the topological transition, the charge gap collapses, while the spin sector remains gapped throughout both the FCI phase and the semion crystal reached by strengthening repulsive interactions. This quantum criticality thereby enforces a hierarchy in which the lowest-energy charge carriers are spin singlets. Near criticality, therefore, anyons with even numerator charge cost less energy, guaranteeing that the cheapest anyons fuse to a spinless boson (e.g., a Cooper pair) rather than to a spinful electron. Concrete realization and main results are as follows: we consider realizing spin-singlet Halperin FQH states in flat Chern bands. The Halperin states are labeled by the integers (mmn)38 where for electrons m is an odd integer. Further, we focus on spin-singlet states that arise in the special case of m = n ± 1, and n∈2Z.39,40,41 We discuss these states at general fillings of the Chern band ν=22n±1 with a parton-vortex approach, within which we can describe the FCI, the semion crystal, the superconductor, and the transitions between them.

Focusing on ν = 2/3, we demonstrate a robust FCI using density-matrix renormalization group simulations of a purely repulsive microscopic model. We further demonstrate that increasing Hubbard repulsion drives the FCI into the semion crystal—a CDW of electrons supporting an emergent CSL. The fact that the tuning of a simple repulsive interaction creates this exotic topological crystal shows that it can form easily despite its complexity and that it is likely to be a relevant competing phase to the FCI. Moreover, the Cooper pair-field correlations peak in the vicinity of the transition, signaling incipient superconductivity.

We further discuss how the mechanism generalizes to fillings ν = 2/(2n±1). Although ν = 2/3 enjoys special simplifications, the same logic of the energetic hierarchy applies: spin singlet, bosonic charge-2e anyons emerge as the preferred low-energy carriers near the corresponding topological transitions. The transition between the FCI and SX can be re-expressed as a plateau transition of Cooper pairs from a hierarchy state νCooper = 1/(∓2−n−1) to νCooper = ∓1/2, providing an intuitive bosonic description of all three phases—FCI, semion crystal, and superconductor—and clarifying their interrelations. Interestingly, the relative sign of the charge Hall conductance in the FCI and the sense of propagation of the chiral edge mode in the semion crystal alternates with the ±1 choice in the fractions above. Doping both the FCI and the semion crystal near the transition favors the formation of superconductors, which in turn inherit the alternating chirality of the semion crystal. At these more general fillings, we find that a generic pathway to charge-2e superconductivity without residual topological order exists—provided the superconductor spontaneously breaks translation symmetry. Translationally invariant charge-2e superconductors can also appear upon doping FCIs, yet within our analysis, these phases retain an additional topological sector and should be identified as SC∗ states, with ν = 2/3 being the exception.

Our analysis, therefore, unifies several recent proposals for anyon superconductivity from studies of lattice models to theories for superconductivity proximate to FQAH states in twisted MoTe2. Previous studies of the Hubbard-Hofstadter platform focused on the integer filling ν = 2 (corresponding to m = 1, n = 0), where topological criticality, CSLs, and superconductivity were identified.34,36,42,43 Here we venture into genuinely fractional fillings, where FQH states emerge, and investigate how the topological criticality mechanism manifests in this regime.

Our work is structured as follows: we provide a field-theoretic analysis of the transition between singlet FQH states and a topological CDW, based on a parton description, and elaborate on the mechanism for anyon superconductivity in the vicinity of the critical point. Using the analytical insights, we then develop a microscopic Hubbard-Hofstadter model and numerically study its phase diagram at ν = 2/3 using tensor network techniques. Our results are consistent with a continuous topological transition between a bilayer FQH phase and the semion crystal. Crucially, we also numerically confirm that across the transition, the spin gap remains open while the charge gap closes. We discuss experimental implications, including potential consequences for recent experimental observations in twisted MoTe2, provide an outlook, and discuss the general insights into anyon superconductivity obtained from our formalism (see discussion section).

Results

Phases and phase transitions at fixed filling: FCI and semion crystal

In this section, we show that favorable conditions for anyon superconductivity emerge in isolated Chern bands that host a pseudo-spin flavor, such as real spin, a layer, or valley index. A microscopic mechanism for forming anyon superconductivity is then provided when strong repulsion drives these fractionally filled states near competing topological CDW states. Based on parton constructions, we develop a field-theoretic description of such a transition from an FCI to the semion crystal (describing a CDW state atop of which a CSL forms). This generalizes previous results on the topological transition between an integer quantum Hall (IQH) state and a CSL34,36,42,43 to fractional fillings. While we focus on Halperin states in the following, our arguments can be generalized to the Jain sequence and SU(N) singlet states (see Note S9).

FCIs

At a total filling of ν = ν↑+ν↓ = 2/(m+n), interactions can stabilize Halperin FQH states, labeled by the integers (mmn),38 where m is odd for fermionic states. The low-energy properties of these states are described by topological quantum field theories with the following effective Lagrangian(Equation 1)LFCI=−14π∑I,JKIJαI∧dαJ+12π∑IqIcαI∧dA+12π∑IqIsαI∧dAs,where αI∧dαJ = ϵμνλαI,μ∂ναJ,λ, and the K-matrix, charge vector qc, and spin vector qs are defined as(Equation 2)K=(mnnm),qc=(1,1)T,qs=(1,−1)T.

Furthermore, A and As represent the background electromagnetic fields that keep track of charge and spin, respectively. As is a background gauge field that couples to spin Sz, which we introduce to keep track of the spin degree of freedom. For pseudo-spins which are encoded as a layer degree of freedom, As may well be physical and would, for instance, dictate the counterflow response. For fermions, m must be an odd integer. Among the fermionic Halperin states (mmn), the sequence with m = n ± 1, at filling ν=22n±1 where n is an even integer, is of particular interest here. These states enjoy full spin rotation invariance, and furthermore, the (112) state with filling ν = 2/3 has the same topological order as the ν = 2/3 Jain state, which is commonly observed in experiments of spin-polarized systems,3,23,24,25,26 while the ν = 2/5 Halperin state has the same topological order as the commonly observed ν = 2/5 Jain state. Although we construct our theory for Halperin states for which our arguments become particularly transparent, and the presence of spin symmetry clarifies some of our arguments, the concepts we illustrate are applicable much more broadly, as we also discuss in the section experimental implications.

Quasiparticles of states in the sequence m = n ± 1 are labeled by an integer vector l=(l↑,l↓)T, which denotes the couplings of the quasi-particle current to the emergent gauge fields. The charges and self-statistics of an anyon l are given by:(Equation 3)Ql=−eqcTK−1l=−el↑+l↓2n±1Szl=qsTK−1l=±(l↑−l↓)θl=πlK−1l=π2n±1[l2(1±n)∓2nl↑l↓].

We find that the l↑=(1,0)T and l↓=(0,1)T anyons have electric charge −e/(2n±1). These anyons with the smallest electric charge carry the same spin Sz = ±1/2 quantum numbers as the original electrons, while l2=(1,1)T anyons carry vanishing spin Sz = 0 and have twice the charge Q = −2e/(2n±1). Thus, the electron is decomposed into anyons which do not carry a fractionalized spin and the original action of an electronic SU(2) spin symmetry is preserved on the level of the anyons. This is a special property of the (n ± 1,n ± 1,n) states consistent with their spin-singlet nature.39,44

Parton construction

Singlet FQH states can be described using fermionic spinons in IQH states at level Cfσ=±1 and bosonic chargons in a 1/n Laughlin state. To describe a continuous transition to a semion crystal, the chargons undergo a crystallization transition, forming a bosonic CDW. The spinons remain in the same state across the transition, implying that the spin gap stays finite.

Since the spin remains unfractionalized in the Halperin states, they can be conveniently described using a parton description, where we decompose the electron operator eσ into a fermionic spinon fσ, which carries its spin, and a chargon b′(x).(Equation 4)eσ(x)=fσ(x)b′(x).

Written in this way, b′(x) is a hard-core boson, which is appropriate for our setting, as we are working at fractional filling of the electrons. The decomposition of the electron into spinon and chargon leads to a gauge constraint(Equation 5)ne(x)≡∑σeσ†eσ(x)=∑σfσ†fσ(x)=b′†b′(x),which is enforced by fσ(x) and b′(x) carrying opposite gauge charge (see Equation 4).

To illustrate how the partons encode the FQH states, it is convenient to consider a lattice system in a background magnetic field giving rise to Φ = Φ0/2 external flux per unit cell of an underlying lattice, where Φ0 is the flux quantum. While it is convenient to consider a finite background flux, it is straightforward to adjust the construction to the pure FQAH setting without background magnetic field Bz = 0. To find (n ± 1,n ± 1,n)-type quantum Hall states, the total filling of the Chern bands must be ν = 2/(2n±1), which implies that the electron density is ne = νΦ/Φ0 = 1/(2n±1) per unit-cell. Due to the constraint, spinons are at a density of nf↑=nf↓=ne/2, and we assume that both spinons fill Chern bands with Cfσ=±1, such that they are in an IQH state with total Chern number Cf = ±2. The parton construction Equation 4 can be understood as a form of flux attachment, such that the flux seen by each parton partially cancels the external flux. From the Streda formula for the IQH state of the spinons, we require that the internal gauge flux seen by them is Φf=Φ0(nf↑+nf↓)/Cf. Consequently, the remaining flux Φb′, seen by the chargons is(Equation 6)Φb′=Φ−Φf=n2n±1Φ0.

Now, since the chargons are at a density:(Equation 7)nb′=ne=12n±1,they can naturally form a bosonic Laughlin state at filling(Equation 8)νb′=nb′/Φb′=1/n.

To obtain a commensurate flux for all fillings, the spinon Chern number Cf = ±2 had to alternate in sign. We remark that in the trivial case, n = 0 at a density ne = 1, all the flux is absorbed by the spinons (see Equation 6), and the chargons see zero net flux. This case was analyzed previously in Divic et al. and Kuhlenkamp et al.,34,36 and we focus here on the non-vanishing n.

Semion crystal

A competing class of gapped ground states at fractional fillings is CDWs, stabilized by minimizing the long-range Coulomb repulsion. One option to undergo a crystallization transition is to simultaneously close the charge and spin gap of the (n ± 1,n ± 1,n) FQH state, resulting in a trivial crystal, most likely via a first-order transition.

More interestingly, a crystal can also form by only closing the charge gap, activating only the bosonic chargons. Such crystallization transitions of bosonic degrees of freedom can be continuous.35,45,46 This competing crystalline order, which we refer to as semion crystal in the following, the transition into it, and the proximate phases upon doping are the focus of our work. The properties of this state are particularly transparent in our parton construction. There, we decompose the electron into the spinon and hardcore chargon eσ(x) = fσ(x)b′(x), where the bosonic parton b′(x) is at fractional filling of the underlying lattice with density nb < 1. Long-range interactions can then lead to a crystallization transition at which the boson gap closes, while the spin gap, and therefore the gap of the fermionic partons fσ, remains open. Once the b′(x) form a crystal, these degrees of freedom are trivially gapped and can be integrated out. To leading order, they only leave behind a fluctuating gauge field a, and, after coupling to the external spin gauge field, we have:(Equation 13)LSX[a,As]=∓14π(α↑∧dα↑+α↓∧dα↓)+12πa∧d(α↑+α↓)+12πAs∧d(α↑−α↓),which, after integrating out a, further simplifies the field theory of the crystal to(Equation 14)LSX=∓24πα∧dα+22πAs∧dα,where α↑ = −α↓ = α. We see that the crystal is an electrical insulator with vanishing Chern number (no dependence of A), and describes a CSL with semion topological order, which we refer to as a semion crystal. The semion crystal can be described from two complementary perspectives. At long wavelengths, the pictures coincide, but their relative utility depends on how deep one is in the ordered phase. Focusing on the bosonic operators b′(x) that crystallize, we note that they carry unit electric charge and acquire semionic statistics through their coupling to the emergent gauge field. The phase can therefore be viewed as a CDW of spinless semions. Moving further into the crystalline phase, the charge gap grows while the super-exchange processes that generate spin dynamics are progressively suppressed. In this limit, the most transparent description is of a CSL formed from the spins of the frozen electronic CDW.

The alternating quantum spin Hall response seen in Equation 14 is inherited from the spin modes of the Halperin states. This is discussed in more detail in the section microscopic lattice model.

Interpretation of the FCI to semion crystal transition as bosonic Cooper pair transition

Here we point out that transitions like (1¯1¯2)↔semioncrystal can be interpreted as purely bosonic plateau transitions of Cooper pairs. This immediately suggests that there are Cooper pairs near the transition. A similar argument was made for the IQH (with σxy = 2e2/h) to CSL transition by Divic et al.34 Here, we show that a related picture emerges for all the fractional electronic states we consider in this paper as well.

To illustrate this, consider the transition from the (1¯1¯2) FQH state with {σxy,σxys}={2/3,−2} to the semion crystal with {σxy,σxys}={0,−2}. We can trivialize the spin response by stacking a νtot = 2 IQH state on top of it, composed of spin up and spin down electrons, each in a Chern band with unit Chern number. Since these are IQH layers with no bulk anyons, their only effect is to change the edge physics, and hence bulk properties of the original system are unchanged upon stacking. After incorporating the IQH state, this gives {σxy,σxys}={8/3,0} for the FQH and {σxy,σxys}={2,0} for the semion crystal. Note, the spin response has been trivialized, which allows for a description of the transition purely in terms of spinless degrees of freedom (see Note S8 for more details). It is nothing but a plateau transition between a Laughlin state of spin singlet Cooper pairs with σxyCooper=12(2e)2h=2e2h and its hierarchy state σxyCooper=(12+16)(2e)2h=23(2e)2h=83e2h.

The nature of this plateau transition can be understood as follows. Consider a parent state built of the Laughlin state of Cooper pairs with an excess filling of νexcessCooper=1/6 of semions. Two options then become available. First, the excess density of semions could crystallize, breaking translation symmetry and leaving behind semion topological order. Or, they could form their own FQH state, which is the basis of the hierarchy construction.

This argument can be generalized to all other abelian spin singlet states at filling ν = 2/(2n±1), which have {σxy,σxys}={2/(2n±1),±2}. The relevant hierarchy states of Cooper pairs appear at filling νCooper=∓12+14n±2. Again, we must incorporate the spin singlet IQH state, with alternating sign of the Hall conductance, to cancel the spin response. Doing this gives {σxy,σxys}={∓2,0} for the semion crystal and {σxy,σxys}={22n±1∓2,0} for the FQH state. Again, this is nothing but a plateau transition between a Laughlin state of spin-singlet Cooper pairs (Hall conductance σxyCooper=∓12(2e)2h), and a hierarchy state with Hall conductance, σxyCooper=(1∓2−1/n)(2e)2h, where we recall that n is even.

Effective theory of the critical point

In the previous sections, we gave a heuristic description of the transition between the FQH and semion crystal phases, first in terms of the bosons b′ transitioning between a bosonic Laughlin and a crystalline insulating phase, and later as a plateau transition between bosonic hierarchy states of Cooper pairs. We now derive the effective theory of the critical point in the former picture using the parton construction. There, the bosons, as described in Equation 4, are both at a finite density and see a background flux. To obtain a convenient, effective theory, we would like instead to work with bosonic variables that effectively are at zero density, represented by a rotor field. Note, simply going over to the vortex description of b′ is not sufficient in this case since it also experiences a flux, and hence the vortices are at finite density. Instead, we will introduce an extended parton construction, which will help us define this new bosonic field. We illustrate this for the simple case of n = 2 corresponding to ν = 2/3 and 2/5, where the extended construction is:(Equation 15)eσ(x)=fσg1g2b(x).

The vortex theory can have an emergent Lorentz symmetry, as the condensation transition is not density-changing. As we have seen for a fractional boson density nb = ne = q/p, the vortices see Φv = 2πq/p flux per unit cell, where p=(2n±1) is equal to the number of valleys. We have q = 1 for π flux per unit cell and q = 2 for FCIs without background flux. Consequently, in the low-energy description, there are p vortex fields φ˜=(φ˜1,…,φ˜p), which are related under the magnetic translation symmetries46 by the transformation T1:φ˜j→φ˜j−1 and T2:φ˜j→ωvjφ˜j, where the phase factor is ωv=ei2πΦv. Expressing Equation 19 in terms of the low-energy vortex fields and simplifying the Chern-Simons part by integrating out a, leads to the effective theory describing the FCI and SX phases, and their transition:(Equation 20)LQCP=∑j=1p{|Dα↑(1)+α↓(1)φ˜j|2−s|φj˜|2}−Vint(φ˜j)−n4π(α↑+α↓)∧d(α↑+α↓)∓14π(α↑∧dα↑+α↓∧dα↓)+12πA∧d(α↑+α↓)+12πAs∧d(α↑−α↓).

We note that the effective low-energy theory is just bosonic QED3 + Chern-Simons, with p flavors of bosons, coupled to a gauge field with a Chern-Simons action. Since the background flux of α↑,↓ is already taken into account by the flavor index, vortices couple only to fluctuations of the gauge fields α↑,↓(1)=α↑,↓−⟨α↑,↓⟩. We can access the two phases by varying the boson mass s, while Vint represents interactions between vortex fields that are constrained by the magnetic symmetry.

Let us now confirm that the vortex theory correctly reproduces the two insulating phases. When s > 0 and all the vortex fields φ˜ are gapped, we obtain the effective action:(Equation 21)LFCI=−n4π(α↑+α↓)∧d(α↑+α↓)∓14π(α↑∧dα↑+α↓∧dα↓)+12πA∧d(α↑+α↓)+12πAs∧d(α↑−α↓),which is nothing but the action for the FQH states, i.e., Equation 1, with(Equation 22)K=(n±1nnn±1).

The transition to the crystal is described by the condensation of the vortex field φ˜, when s < 0, which leads to a Meissner effect for the internal gauge field, setting α↑ = −α↓ = α and reducing to Equation 14. In this way, the coupling to the external gauge field A is lost, signaling the formation of an insulator while retaining semion topological order and quantized spin transport. In the process of vortex condensation, we need to pick out one combination of vortex fields to condense, which breaks translation symmetry. Thus, in this limit, our theory describes a semion crystal. Note, we obtain essentially the same topological order for all values of p in the SX phase, except for the ± dependence in Equation 14 on whether p = 2n±1, in contrast to the topological order of the FQH state that depends strongly on p.

The critical point is described by the theory obtained on setting s = 0, which at this level of approximation describes a continuous transition. Both the change in topological order and translation symmetry breaking occur simultaneously on crossing the critical point. This occurs because the p=(2n±1) flavors of vortices that undergo the condensation transition carry nontrivial statistics (θv = πν) that are related to the filling ν=22n±1 of the FQH phase. Note, in the limit n→∞, the statistics are trivialized, and one approaches the usual complex scalar field transition with p components.

We emphasize that the transition from spin-singlet Halperin states to the semion crystal phase is driven by the crystallization of the chargons (dual to the vortex fields described above), while the spin sector remains unchanged. This implies that the spin gap remains open across the transition, energetically favoring doping of spin-singlet excitations. Close to the critical point, the vortex field is soft and its current couples with vector l=(1,1)T to α↑,α↓, resulting in vortices carrying charge(Equation 23)Qφ˜=−2e2n±1.

Charge fluctuations at the critical point are then governed by vortices, or equivalently by spinless anyons. Microscopic details encoded in the vortex interactions Vint(φ˜) determine the pattern of vortex field condensation φ˜i, allowing for the formation of different types of CDWs.46 The special case of the bilayer IQH (110) to CSL transition, as discussed in Divic et al.,34 Kuhlenkamp et al.,36 Kuhlenkamp,42 and Divic et al.43, can be understood from setting n = 0 within the broader framework developed here. There too, superconductivity emerges on doping the topological critical point,34 although all Hall conductances are integral. Here, we will see that, surprisingly, an analogous outcome is realized even when the insulators exhibit fractional Hall conductance.

Finite doping: Superconductivity

The existence of a spin gap at the quantum critical point, while the charge gap softens, provides the foundation for anyon superconductivity and rationalizes the equivalent plateau transition of Cooper pairs. On the semion crystal side of the transition, the energetic separation of spinful and spinless excitations provides a natural mechanism for superconductivity upon doping the Kalmeyer-Laughlin state,48 which dates back to seminal work by Laughlin12,13,14,15,16,49 and was recently revisited using modern techniques.34,50 Approaching the QCP from the FCI side, we find an equally natural route to superconductivity: electric charge enters the system in the form of vortices φ˜, which are soft excitations close to criticality. These vortices naturally fuse to a charge-2e boson (which is the Cooper pair) as the cheapest local excitation, thereby providing a mechanism for anyon superconductivity. We show that when doped to a finite density, vortices themselves form quantum Hall states, which demonstrates in detail how superconductors without residual topological order appear in proximity to the quantum critical point. These conventional superconductors can emerge naturally from both the FCI and the SX phase and (with the notable exception of ν = 2/3) are found to be distinct from more exotic superconductors that have been recently proposed.30

For simplicity, we focus on doping of the (1¯1¯2) and (332) FQH state at fillings ν = 2/3 and ν = 2/5, respectively. In Note S1, we generalize our arguments to general (n ± 1,n ± 1,n) states and show that there is always a pathway to simple charge-2e superconductivity that breaks translation symmetry.

Doping chargon-vortices into the fractional Chern insulator

When describing the critical point in the previous section, we assumed that the bosonic parton b′(x) sees a finite flux and forms a bosonic FQH state. In the dual vortex description, this finite flux seen by b′(x) is equivalent to starting from a finite density of vortices. When studying finite doping around ν = 2/3 and 2/5 arising from (1¯1¯2) and (332) states with n = 2, we work in the extended parton picture discussed in the section effective theory of the critical point, where eσ = fσg1g2b. If the extra fermionic partons g1,2 form IQH states, the boson will experience zero flux. This is the case when the flux Φb/Φ0 = 2/(4 ± 1) (see Equation 6 for n = 2) is equally distributed among the fermionic partons g1,2, such that both are at integer filling νg1,2=1. The couplings of gauge fields to partons and the respective fillings are outlined in Table 1. The boson b now forms a superfluid in the FQH phase and is gapped in the semion crystal. Note that one may identify g1g2b as the single boson b′ used to describe the FQH state in the subsection FCI. This construction has the advantage that the vortices of b are now at zero density in the FQH state, making it easier to study finite doping compared to the decomposition in Equation 4. The vortices for this boson see finite background flux, which can be resolved by introducing the low-energy valley degrees of freedom for the vortices. Now, doping can be easily studied since both fluxes and densities are proportional to the doping.

After moving to the dual vortex description of Equation 16, the Lagrangian of the system reads(Equation 24)L=Lfσ[a1]+Lg1[a2−a1]+Lg2[A−a2+a]+Lφ˜[β]−12πa∧dβ,where the action for the fermionic partons is given in Equation 17, and the last two terms refer to the vortex fields φ˜. We now analyze this theory for specific cases.

Doping the ν = 2/3 state

Consider doping holes so that the density of up and down electrons per unit cell is ne↑=ne↓=13−δ2. Due to our parton construction Equation 15, this implies a change of all the parton densities. To keep the fermionic partons gapped, we assume that the flux per unit cell of a1,2 and a adjusts upon doping:(Equation 25)12π∇×a1=−13+δ212π∇×a2=13−δ212π∇×a=−3δ2,which keeps all fermionic partons gapped in integer levels for general δ. Since da/2π→0 as δ→0, the bosons b see excess flux only when the system is doped, ensuring that the density of vortices of the b field is also proportional to δ. The key question is the filling of vortices, i.e., the ratio of their density to the flux they see. This is νvorticestotal=3/2 and will be derived below, but let us give a simple physical argument for it first. The flux seen by the vortices is just the added charge, hence −δ. Now, for the density of vortices, we are working in the low-energy space where the only degrees of freedom are vortices that carry electric charge 2e/3. Thus, if we add δ of charge, this implies a vortex density of (−δ)/(−2/3δ) = 3δ/2. The ratio of this vortex density to the flux gives us νvorticestotal=3/2. We will see that there are natural bosonic quantum Hall states that occur at this filling, which will lead overall to a superconductor. Equations of motion provide the following constraints:(Equation 26)da1+da2=0da−da2=2πnb2da2−da1=da,which are satisfied for nb = 2/3-δ, as fluxes are only counted modulo 1. This way, the state formed by the partons fσ,g1,g2 remains unchanged. We can describe the change of flux in the vortex picture. Assuming all the extra charge enters as vortices, they are at the following density:(Equation 27)nφ˜=12π∇×a=−32δ,which can be seen either from the fact that vortices have charge 2/3 or from the equations of motion derived from δLδβ=0, relating nφ˜ with da/2π in the same way. The first term −1/3 gives us three valleys for the vortices into which we can dope. The second term is the flux that must be accommodated by forming a bosonic quantum Hall state. Note, the vortices are at filling: νvorticestotal=3/2. There are two natural choices for vortices at this filling. At νvorticestotal=3/2, they could form a Jain state and spontaneously pick a single valley(Equation 29)νJain=pp+1;p=−3,or, the vortices can form a valley singlet, which is described by:(Equation 30)Kv,singlet=(011101110),qv=(111),leading to the following action for the vortices(Equation 31)Lφ˜[β]=−14πKv,singletIJγI∧dγJ+12πβ∧d∑I=13γI.

Furthermore, the sign of the quantized spin response tells us that this is a “d-id” superconductor, at least at the level of edge modes, with c- = −2, i.e., two chiral edge modes propagating opposite to the direction of the charge Hall conductance, consistent with Shi and Senthil.30

Finally, let us discuss translation symmetry. This depends on the valley structure of the doped vortices. Forming a singlet of the SU(3) valley symmetry automatically preserves the translational symmetry of the system, since the latter is a subgroup of the former. However, spontaneously choosing a valley and subsequently forming a p = −3 Jain state breaks translational symmetry, leading to an SC + CDW phase. Both are possible options that can be realized, depending on the specifics of the microscopic interaction.

Doping the ν = 2/5 state

To describe the (332) state at ν = 2/5, we only need to switch the flux experienced by the spinons, such that they each reside in an IQH state at level +1. Keeping all the partons in their respective levels corresponds to the following flux assignment:(Equation 35)12π∇×a1=15−δ212π∇×a2=35−3δ212π∇×a=−5δ2,which now implies that the vortices see a flux dβ/2π=−35−δ and are at density nφ˜=−5δ/2. At the total filling νvorticestotal=5/2=2+1/2, the vortices can pick a valley in which they form a bosonic IQH state with a 1/2 Laughlin state on top. This vortex state is described by(Equation 36)Lφ˜[β]=−14πKv,2+12IJγI∧dγJ+12πβ∧d∑I=13γIKv,2+12=(010100002),qv=(111).

When combined with LFQH[a], this produces a charge-2e superconductor without residual topological order. As in the case of 2/3 filling, the superconductor inherits the spin edge modes from the spin sector (or equivalently c- = +2), which are now propagating in the same direction as the charge modes of the FQH state. This pattern persists for all singlet FCIs: conventional charge-2e superconductivity can form if it also breaks translations. Note S1 explains this construction in detail and also presents new families of SC∗ states that may arise from the doped FCIs. These translation symmetry-breaking superconductors differ from those proposed in Shi and Senthil30 at related fillings.

Equivalent picture using fermionic partons

Our parton construction in the previous Equation 15 was chosen to be able to conveniently describe the FQH and semion crystal phases at different fillings, as well as the superconductors that emerge on doping them. Here, we outline how some of the same results at particular fillings can be recovered using just the fermionic partons and dispensing with the boson. This specific parton ansatz was used in Shi and Senthil30 to analyze the doped 2/3 fractional Chern insulator; here we adapt it to our setting and supply an independent derivation highlighting how a charge-2e superconductor emerges upon doping, generalizing the approach to generic spin singlet FQH states at filling ν = 2/(2n±1); see Note S1.

Before delving into finite doping, let us briefly specialize to ν = 2/3 and discuss how all three phases, FQH, semion crystal, and superconductor, can be accessed at fixed density within the fermionic parton theory eσ(x) = fσg1g2. While energetics may make a superconductor at the same filling unlikely, an effective theory will help us build intuition for the parton description. Indeed, starting from the parton Chern number assignments (-2,1,1) for the (fσ,g1,g2) partons, it is seen that the semion crystal is accessed with the assignments (-2,1,0) while the superconductor with (-2,1,-2). Thereby only the topology of the last parton changes, which can be modeled as sign changes of the Dirac fermion mass, and effectively describes the topology-changing transitions. Moreover, the magnetic translation symmetry resulting from the fact that g2 sees emergent gauge flux of 1/3, implies three valleys related by translations. This gives rise to three species of low-energy fermions Ψi, which are related to one another by magnetic translations. Ultimately, we can write down the following effective theory:(Equation 37)LDirac=∑i=13Ψ¯iDa+AΨi+miΨ¯iΨi−{18π(a+A)∧d(a+A)+CSg}+24πa∧da,where Da=γ·(∂+ia) is the covariant derivative contracted with the Dirac gamma matrices, and the last term arises by noticing that Chern assignments of the first two partons are always (fσ,g1)=(−2,1), implying that they are in the bosonic IQH phase with Hall conductance 2.51 Finally, the terms in curly brackets (including the gravitational Chern-Simons term CSg, which account for chiral edge modes in units of c- = 1/2), are introduced to properly offset the topological action and are at a fractional level since we have an odd number of Dirac fermions. This analysis parallels that of Lee et al.,28 which studied composite fermion Dirac transitions between FCIs. If all the mass terms are equal and positive, translations are preserved, and we obtain the FQH LFQH=14π(a+A)∧d(a+A)+CSg+24πa∧da, which has Hall conductance 2/3 and no edge central charge.30 On the other hand, if all mass terms are equal and negative, again we have translation symmetry, but now the action is LSC=−24π(a+A)∧d(a+A)−4CSg+24πa∧da, which is readily seen to be a 2e-superconductor with c- = −2. Finally, we consider the semion crystal. The Chern number assignment requires that we make one mass term negative and the other two positive. This choice, however, breaks translation symmetry, and if it occurs spontaneously, it corresponds to crystallization. Unlike the critical bosonic QED3 theory (Equation 20), where topology change and translation symmetry breaking occur together, in these variables, that scenario is less natural. The effective theory then is, LSX=24πa∧da. It is worth noting two points. First, at the fixed density transition from FQH to superconductor, we will have enhanced correlations at the wave-vectors connecting the three valleys, resulting in an “octet” of CDW correlations corresponding to (traceless part of) the fermion bilinears ⟨Ψ¯iΨj⟩.28 This is despite the fact that at this transition, the actual CDW phase is not in the picture. Second, the 2/3 insulator is special in having such a remarkably simple description of all three phases, and even a fourth one. For the latter, two Dirac masses are negative and one positive, and the system enters an IQH insulator that breaks translation and exhibits σxy = −2. This simplicity follows from the previously discussed mapping that relates all these phases to quantum Hall states of Cooper pairs. Furthermore, all of them correspond to Jain states for the Cooper pairs, and hence can be accessed by tuning the Chern number of the associated composite fermions. Indeed, the Ψ are the composite fermions obtained after attaching flux to the Cooper pairs. Now, let us turn to the finite doping transition, where these Chern assignments will be obtained by adding charge.

Density-tuned transitions from fermionic partons

For simplicity, we consider particle doping δ > 0. To form a superconductor upon doping, the Chern number of one of the auxiliary partons, say g2, must change from 1 to −2. To keep both the spinons fσ, and the other chargon g1 at fixed integer levels (−2,1) requires the emergent gauge fields to adjust such that g2 experiences a magnetic flux of nΦg2=Φg2/Φ0=−13−δ2, while being at a density of ng2=23+δ. In the presence of a lattice, the total Chern number of g2 is constrained by a Diophantine equation52,53,54:(Equation 38)Cg2·nΦg2−ng2=Z.

For Equation 38 to be satisfied, terms involving the doping δ must cancel while also recovering Cg2=1 in the limit δ→0. Thus, we find Cg2=−2 as a consistent solution in the doped case (see Note S4 for more details), which signals the formation of charge-2e superconductivity. This illustrates why the underlying lattice potential is crucial for establishing superconductivity upon doping the FCI. For the FCI at 2/3 filling, this establishes a relation to previous work,30 which we obtain in an independent approach. Performing a continuum expansion and taking into account that, due to the flux seen by g1,2, there are three equivalent minima Ki, we find(Equation 39)HK=∑i=1312m∗(k−Ki)2+⋯.

Upon doping, the finite spin gap close to the critical point ensures that the spinons remain in their gapped IQH state. Consequently, it is energetically favorable to dope spin-singlet anyons which carry charge 2/3 and correspond to g1,2. When changing the electron density by δ, we need to adjust the densities of all partons fσ,g1,g2. To ensure that the spinons remain in a gapped level −2 IQH state, we must therefore change the flux they experience. Specifically, their flux changes by −δ/2 according to the Streda formula. Since the partons g1,2 are coupled to the spinons through the gauge fields a1,2,(Equation 40)L=Lfσ[a1]+Lg1[a2−a1]+Lg2[−a2+A],a change in the flux of the spinons leads to a change in the flux of the partons g1 and g2. As discussed, the flux of the spinons must change by(Equation 41)12π∇×a1=−13−δ2,such that they remain in an IQH state. We show that choosing a change in flux for the gauge field(Equation 42)12π∇×a2=13+δ2,results in the same superconductor as in the previous section. Note that the flux assignment Equation 42 is consistent with no change in the external flux of A. The flux Φg2 seen by g2 changes by ΔΦg2/Φ0=−δ/2, adjusting the Hamiltonian(Equation 43)HK=∑i=1312m∗(k−Ki−aδ)2+⋯,where aδ is a gauge field generating flux ΔΦg2. Doping the semion crystal

Close to the critical point, the semion crystal is described by the following action:(Equation 47)LSX=Lφ[−α+A]±24πα∧dα+22πAs∧dα,where we have explicitly reinstated a charge −e bosonic field φ, corresponding to the chargon in the parton decomposition eσ = fσb′, which is in a translation symmetry-breaking Mott phase but close to its critical point. Doping this CSL works similarly to previous proposals,34,50 with the difference of translational symmetry breaking in the semion crystal we discuss. In a regular Kalmeyer-Laughlin spin liquid, the charge gap is much larger than the spin gap Δc≫Δs, which implies that the lowest lying anyons carry spin 1/2. In stark contrast, the lowest energy excitations of the semion crystal close to the QCP are spinless, charge Q = −e semions described by φ(x). Since Δc/Δs→0, it is natural to think of these anyons as fractionalized Cooper pairs. A similar interpretation is possible on the FCI side of the transition, as shown by the previously discussed mapping to a transition of bosonic Cooper pairs.

Upon hole doping, the density of doped chargons is −δ. To keep the spinons gapped, we must adjust the flux 12π∇×α∼∓δ/2, leading to a filling νb = ±2. Consequently, the b bosons form a bosonic IQH state, which, when integrated out, precisely cancels the topological order in Equation 47, arising from the spinons. We expect that the translational symmetry breaking is preserved upon doping, which leads to a charge-2e superconductor without residual topological order in the presence of a CDW (see Figure 1B).

Forming any bosonic state with σxyb=−2 will give rise to a superconducting state according to the Ioffe-Larkin rule. Which state is chosen then influences the residual topological order. If the bosons were to form a more exotic νb = −2 state, such as a Laughlin state of paired semions, the resulting state is an SC∗ as it retains background semion topological order.

Properties of the anyon superconductor

At 2/3 filling, both options we discussed are topologically “d-id” superconductors. Owing to the fact that the neutral spin modes on the boundary are counter-propagating and remain unmodified close to the critical point, the superconducting state retains a spin response −24πAs∧dAs, which is opposite to the electronic Hall effect. Due to the nature of the FQH parent states at general fillings ν = 2/(2l + 1), the sign of the spin-quantum Hall effect (and therefore also the thermal Hall response) alternates as σxys=2·(−1)l. Interestingly, while the spin-quantum Hall response is inherited from the parent states, the chiral central charge changes, i.e., from c- = 0 in the 2/3 FQH state to c- = −2 in the superconductor.

Due to the finite spin gap, the decay of superconducting correlations is constrained by a finite spin correlation length ξs:(Equation 48)⟨Δ(r)⟩=⟨eσ(0)eσ¯(r)⟩∼exp(−|r|ξs).

As we are describing an insulator-to-insulator transition, this behavior is expected. Remarkably, since our doping procedure preserves the spin gap, we expect that it also sets the size of Cooper pairs for weak doping. This implies Cooper pairs are inherently tightly bound close to the transition, while the topology is consistent with a chiral d+id superconductor in the BCS limit. The tight binding of the Cooper pairs implies that the critical temperature Tc is set by the stiffness ρs of the superconductor, rather than the pairing scale. Starting from each parent state, the stiffness is expected to grow with the doping level and suppressed by the anyon mass m∗ such that ρs∼δ,m∗−1; see Chen et al.20 for a derivation. Which type of superconducting state is selected upon doping, i.e., whether it breaks translations or not, is ultimately a matter of microscopics. Furthermore, we note that charge-2e superconductivity with opposing thermal Hall response can be obtained by considering Kohn-Luttinger mechanisms in the complementary weakly interacting limit where the parent state is a chiral metal.57,58

Microscopic lattice model

Guided by these general insights, we aim to design a microscopic model that hosts a quantum phase transition from a Halperin FQH state to a semion crystal. To this end, we consider a triangular lattice Hubbard-Hofstadter model of pseudo spins that couple uniformly to a perpendicular magnetic field and form Chern bands. The choice of the triangular lattice is motivated by the fact that the resulting CDW states frustrate spin interactions and, moreover, are naturally realized in moiré lattices of semiconductor heterostructures. By tuning inter- and intralayer interactions, distinct competing phases may be stabilized, such as the Halperin FQH states, CDW states, and semion crystals. We represent the many-body wave function of the system using matrix product states (MPS) on an infinite cylinder geometry. Using the TeNPy library,59 we perform infinite density matrix renormalization group (iDMRG) calculations to variationally optimize the MPS. There exists extensive numerical evidence for a continuous transition in the simple n = 0 case at a total filling of ν = 2.34,36,43 Here, we focus on a filling of ν = 2/3, corresponding to an electron density of ne = νΦ/Φ0 = 1/3 per unit cell. At this filling, a transition from the (1¯1¯2) bilayer FQH state to a semion crystal can be observed. For a small nearest-neighbor interaction V and a finite on-site interaction U, the (1¯1¯2) FQH state is stabilized. Increasing V favors the formation of a charge-ordered CDW state; see Figure 4B for an overview of the phase diagram. The spin order of the CDW depends on the interaction U. For large U, the dominant spin coupling is the anti-ferromagnetic Heisenberg coupling JH∼O(t′2/U,t4/(V2U)), leading to a spin-ordered 120° phase. Here, the spin exchanges arise on top of the CDW. For example, the “direct exchange” term is realized either by next-nearest neighbor hopping processes or by fourth-order nearest neighbor hopping processes. For smaller U, the presence of a chiral coupling Jχ∼O(t′3/U2,t′t4/(V2U2)), which is suppressed by an additional factor of t′/U relative to JH, favors the formation of a CSL on top of the CDW, i.e., the semion crystal.

While the transition between the (1¯1¯2) FQH state and the 120° Néel state appears to be first order, we provide evidence for a continuous transition from the (1¯1¯2) FQH state to the semion crystal, with the spin correlation length remaining finite across the transition.

First, we establish that the Halperin (1¯1¯2) state is stabilized in the bilayer Hubbard-Hofstadter model (Equation 49) on the triangular lattice. In contrast to the (110) state discussed in Divic et al. and Kuhlenkamp et al.,34,36 the (1¯1¯2) state has a non-trivial topological order and has fascinating properties in its own right. As discussed in the subsection FCIs, it can be understood in a parton language as spinons filling Chern bands with C = −1 and chargons in a bosonic 1/2 Laughlin state. We uniquely identify the (1¯1¯2) state from its entanglement spectrum and by performing adiabatic flux insertion into the cylinder.

Increasing the nearest-neighbor interactions triggers a phase transition and tunes the system into a different insulator, which we will argue is nothing but the semion crystal. While the charge response of this phase is trivial, spin-pumping reveals a quantized spin-Hall response, which is identical to the spin-Hall response of the (1¯1¯2) state (see Figure 6B). For general semion crystals formed at filling ν = 2/(2n±1), the quantum spin Hall response is alternating Qs = ±1. This can be understood in this limit as lattice translations are broken, and the unit cell is enlarged. Within the enlarged unit cell, the electron and spinon density is integer (ne = 1), while the flux per unit cell of the crystal is ±Φ0/2 modulo 2π. Thus, the spinon density in the crystal is now large enough to absorb all of the external flux by forming an IQH state at level ±2, while the chargons are in a Mott state with respect to the enlarged unit cell. As a concrete example, consider the 2/3 FCI, which transitions into the semion crystal, shown in the top left of the phase diagram of Figure 4B. While triangles of the lattice enclose π/2 flux, the larger triangles defined by the charge-ordered state have three times the area and enclose a flux of 3π/2≡−π/2, which reverses the sign of the spin Hall conductance compared to the CSL at integer filling.

Having established and characterized the Halperin (1¯1¯2) and semion crystal states, we now study the topological quantum phase transition between them by increasing the nearest-neighbor interaction strength V, while keeping the on-site interaction U = 4t fixed.

First, we study the spontaneous breaking of discrete lattice translations as nearest-neighbor interactions are increased. We define the order parameter of the crystal as the static structure factor at the ordering wave vector K=(0,4π/3a)T(Equation 57)ΔCDW=⟨ρ(−K)ρ(K)⟩−⟨ρ(K)⟩2,with the density operator(Equation 58)ρ(k)=1N∑q,σcσq†cσq+k.

We find the order parameter ΔCDW to grow quickly near the critical point, consistent with a continuous transition (Figure 7A). We remark that, depending on the cylinder geometry, the FQH state may already weakly break translations due to a finite cylinder circumference. This is, for instance, the case for the here considered Ly = 4. Different geometries are discussed in Note S6. Next, we study the spin and charge correlation lengths when tuning across the critical point. We find the density correlation length, corresponding to the charge sector (Q,Sz)=(0,0), to diverge at the critical point as a function of the bond dimension. In contrast, the spin correlation length, (Q,Sz)=(0,ℏ), remains finite and quickly converges with bond dimension (see Figure 7B). This suggests a finite spin gap across the transition, consistent with the continuous transition discussed in the previous sections. Furthermore, the ratio between the charge (Q,Sz)=(2,0) and charge (Q,Sz)=(1,ℏ/2) correlation length peaks at the critical point, growing with bond dimension and are, in particular, larger than the trivial expectation of 1/2, which arises when the charge-2 correlations would only be inherited from charge-1 correlations (Figure 7C). This behavior is expected when charge-2 excitations become energetically favorable compared to spinful charge-1 excitations. We emphasize that the large spin gap in both the FCI and the semion crystal protects these phases against perturbations. Accordingly, we expect them to be stable beyond the SU(2) symmetric model (Equation 49) considered here. All together, these tensor network results provide strong evidence that our model realizes a continuous transition from a (1¯1¯2) FQH state to a semion crystal, with the spin gap remaining open and with enhancement of Cooper pair correlations across the transition. We leave the examination of the finite doping state to future numerical work. We note that even in the strict limit of spinless fermions, the system could undergo the same FCI to semion crystal transition, enabling an identical anyon-superconductivity mechanism in its vicinity, just as in the spinful case. The relevant energy scale near the transition is set by a finite charge Q = 1e gap. Finding a microscopic model that stabilizes such spinless semion crystal and perhaps realizes a continuous transition from a 2/3 FCI phase remains an exciting open challenge.

Discussion

Experimental implications

Let us now discuss the experimental outlook of our work.

Realizing the spinful Hubbard-Hofstadter model with flat bands

The microscopic model proposed in the section microscopic lattice model can be engineered in moiré materials by applying strong magnetic fields to a moiré lattice and utilizing layer quantum numbers, which are immune to Zeeman splitting, as pseudospin.36,37 Specifically, in homo-bilayers of transition metal dichalcogenides, twisted at a small angle after a 60° rotation (H-stacking) of one of the layers, spin-valley locking suppresses tunneling between the layers. The electron therefore carries two independent quantum numbers: spin (which is locked to valley) and layer. A magnetic field then polarizes the electron spin,36 leaving a layer pseudospin that is immune to Zeeman splitting—providing exactly the ingredients of the two-component Hubbard-Hofstadter model.36,37 Such models were found to realize the integer Chern insulator to CSL transition.34,36,42 Further, realizing a robust 2/3 FQH state requires flat bands. In our model, this is obtained by appropriate nearest-neighbor hoppings, a feature that will need to be tuned experimentally by knobs that control the electronic dispersion.

Twisted MoTe2

The most striking experimental parallel is the observation of superconductivity in twisted MoTe2,33 in close proximity to the 2/3 fractional quantum anomalous Hall insulator.23,25 Hole doping the top valence band of twisted MoTe2 results in spontaneous valley polarization, and electrons occupy a single Chern band that hosts integer (filling ν = 1) and fractional (ν = 2/3,3/5, …) quantum anomalous Hall states. Between the ν = 1 and ν = 2/3 states, the system passes through an anomalous Hall metal and then a superconductor, while gate-tuned displacement fields stabilize additional valley-polarized insulators with zero Hall conductance.

Therefore, from a coarse-grained point of view, both our setting and MoTe2 host the fractional 2/3 state, a proximate superconductor on doping, and nearby insulators without Hall conductance. However, some important differences between these systems should be noted, even at the level of symmetries. We have assumed spin rotation symmetry, while the valley-polarized MoTe2 has strongly broken spin rotation symmetry due to spin valley locking. Can the two be related? The first observation is that since our model has a spin gap, weak breaking of spin-rotation symmetry leaves the phases intact. For instance, the topological order of the CSL is stable when spin rotation symmetry is broken,36 despite the fact that spin transport is not quantized any longer. Next, we note that for the strong spin-rotation symmetry breaking relevant to tMoTe2, our mechanism provides a general guideline: if the 2/3 FCI is in proximity to a specific CDW—the semion crystal—then the charge 2e/3 anyon becomes energetically cheaper than a pair of separated e/3 quasiparticles. Doping such an FCI should favor superconductivity. Repulsive interactions are indeed expected to drive FCIs toward a CDW order; the key is to realize the correct type of CDW (see Note S9). A promising strategy is to search the phase diagram of MoTe2 for insulating states that continue to break time reversal symmetry but exhibit a vanishing Hall response, which can be a potential candidate for this exotic CDW, and examine if we have a continuous (or weakly first order) transition into the 2/3 FCI. Indeed, it has already been speculated that precisely such exotic CDWs could arise in MoTe2.35 Our framework links that putative CDW directly to enhanced Cooper pairing in the neighboring FCI, providing a concrete route to accounting for superconductivity.

As an alternative scenario, superconductivity in this system could instead evolve from the intervening chiral metal.33,66 To distinguish these scenarios, it will be helpful to have more information on the energy ordering of quasiparticles. A simple (but indirect and imperfect) way to do this is via the observation (or absence) of daughter FCI plateaus, as the hierarchical sequence changes depending on which anyon is lowest energy. If the minimal charge 1/3 anyon is the cheapest, then one obtains the usual Haldane-Halperin hierarchy,67 and several of these are observed for ν < 2/3. This is consistent with that being the lowest charge excitation on the electron doping (smaller ν) side. However, on the opposite side, incompressible states at fillings predicted by the 2/3 anyon hierarchy, such as ν = 4/5 would provide strong evidence for 2/3 anyons to be cheaper for hole doping (see Note S2), regardless of the mechanism. This energy ordering would be consistent with the observation of superconductivity for ν > 2/3. Other potential signatures of the energy ordering of anyons could come from spectroscopic probes68,69,70,71,72,73 or by measuring the electric charge via shot-noise measurements74,75 or via single electron transitor measurements.76,77

In the MoTe2 experiments, a re-entrant integer QAH plateau (RIQAH) was reported33 between the 2/3 and 3/5 FQAH states. Although additional confirmation is warranted—particularly since edge-equilibration effects could influence the measurement—taking the observation at face value, this state could potentially arise from the usual plateau transition driven by 1/3 anyons. In our formalism, a different possibility is suggested. Stacking a Chern-1 QAH layer on top of the 2/5 to semion-crystal transition also reproduces such a feature (see Note S3). The stacking converts the 2/5 into a 3/5 FQAH state, while the CDW, after stacking, reproduces the Hall conductance of the RIQAH. Note that, however, the presence of a background topological order in this scenario implies that this phase is more appropriately designated RIQAH∗.

Other candidate platforms

Multilayer rhombohedral graphene displays a zero-field FQAH series,27,32 and the microscopic Hamiltonian preserves full spin rotation symmetry. Although superconductivity with broken time reversal symmetry has been observed in this system, it has so far appeared only in the moiréless regime, where the fractional states have not yet been seen. Future theoretical studies retaining the spin degree of freedom will be needed to clarify their role, which may allow the mechanism outlined here to operate near the ν = 2/3 state. Experiments that search for the possible emergence of superconductivity near the 2/3 fractional state of multilayer rhombohedral graphene would be of great interest. Furthermore, high-field FCIs in graphene-hBN platforms21 provide another arena where continuous FCI-CDW transitions have been predicted.28,78

Outlook

Superconductivity that emerges from doping conventional insulators requires that electrons first bind into Cooper pairs, and those pairs then acquire long-range phase coherence. In FCIs, the microscopic degrees of freedom are anyons rather than electrons. For anyons, the analog of the first step is to establish the right energy hierarchy for anyon charges. A finite density liquid of them then leads to phase coherence and superconductivity. In this work, we have established how FCIs at the brink of a topological phase transition can supply the necessary energetic hierarchy that leads to anyon superconductivity. We have demonstrated that such transitions can be reached in microscopic electronic models with predominantly repulsive interactions.

In fact, the topological criticality mechanism previously discussed for integer Chern states leads precisely to binding of electrons, as long as one is on the Chern insulator side of the transition.34 Our FQH extends this mechanism to anyons of the FCI. Because the mechanism relies only on repulsive interactions and does not invoke time-reversal symmetry, it provides a realistic route to superconductivity in strongly correlated Chern systems.

One of the biggest surprises of our work is the numerical discovery of the semion crystal in simple Hubbard models at fractional filling. One may view this phase as a period-three electron CDW on which the residual spins form a CSL, akin to the cluster-Mott physics proposed for 1T-TaS2.79 Finding a stable semion crystal in such a minimal model highlights it as a viable state, even competing with conventional crystals and FQH states. Elucidating the properties and stability of such semion crystals at other fillings or in the absence of a moiré potential (i.e., the formation of a Wigner crystal supporting a CSL) is an interesting direction.

In future work, it will be essential to understand the effect of strong SU(2) symmetry breaking. In this context, an important step will be to study generalizations of our setting to higher Chern bands, which can smoothly connect to the SU(2) limit discussed in this work.80,81,82

Our analysis focused on Abelian quasiparticles, but FCIs supporting non-Abelian statistics exhibit even richer pairing possibilities.83 Determining how topological criticality operates in that context is an exciting theoretical challenge.

Recent calculations suggest that even in continuum Landau levels, the minimal-charge quasiparticles may form bound states.84,85 However, such binding is believed not to occur for 1/3 and 2/3 fillings86 for Coulomb interactions. Investigating analogous binding phenomena in FCIs could further broaden the scope of anyon superconductivity. However, a recent numerical study of quasiparticle gaps in twisted MoTe2 did not find evidence for binding of the e/3 quasiparticles.87 The energetic hierarchy of anyons in MoTe225,25 or rhombohedral graphene,27,32 and hence the question of relevance of anyon superconductivity, remains an open question. A variety of experimental signatures could shed light on this problem, from the existence of exotic hierarchy states (see Note S2), thermal Hall responses, or spectroscopic probes of anyon gaps.71,72

In summary, we have identified a new CDW, the semion crystal, that appears in a family of electronic models hosting FCIs. We point out that a generic, interaction-driven route to anyon superconductivity exists, that leverages proximity of the FCI to the transition into the semion-crystal, which opens multiple avenues for advancing our understanding of unconventional superconductivity in topological flat bands.
