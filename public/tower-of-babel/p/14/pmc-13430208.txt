Introduction

Invasive candidiasis remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality among hospitalized and immunocompromised patients. Although Candida albicans remains a leading etiologic agent, non-albicans Candida species now account for more than half of bloodstream infections in many healthcare settings (Antinori et al., 2016; Bays et al., 2024; De Cássia Orlandi Sardi et al., 2018; De Gaetano et al., 2024; Ekenoğlu Merdan and Zıkşahna, 2026). Among these emerging pathogens, Candida auris (also known as Candidozyma auris) has garnered global attention due to its rapid international spread, persistence in healthcare settings, and frequent multidrug resistance. Clinical isolates commonly exhibit reduced susceptibility or resistance to azoles and polyenes, and, in some cases, echinocandins, limiting therapeutic options and contributing to mortality rates approaching 40% in invasive infections (Chowdhary et al., 2018; Cortegiani et al., 2018; Lockhart et al., 2017).

Successful colonization and infection by C. auris require the coordinated regulation of numerous stress-response and homeostatic pathways that enable adaptation to antifungal exposure, host-imposed stresses, and nutrient limitation. Membrane transporters play critical roles in these processes by controlling the movement of metabolites, ions, and xenobiotics across cellular membranes. Among these, members of the ATP-binding cassette (ABC) and major facilitator superfamily (MFS) transporter families are best known for their contributions to antifungal resistance through the active extrusion of toxic compounds (Costa et al., 2014; Ernst et al., 2010). Increasing evidence from fungal pathogens indicates that these transporters also contribute to broader physiological processes, including cellular homeostasis, metabolic adaptation, biofilm development, host interaction, and virulence (Allert et al., 2022a; Antinori et al., 2016; Costa et al., 2014; Du et al., 2020; Shah et al., 2014).

Genomic analyses have revealed that C. auris encodes a large and diverse repertoire of transporter genes, including expanded ABC and MFS transporter families (Chatterjee et al., 2015; Muñoz et al., 2018; Rossato and Colombo, 2018; Sharma et al., 2016). Many of these transporters are transcriptionally responsive to environmental stress and antifungal exposure, suggesting important roles in adaptation to changing conditions encountered within the host and healthcare environments (Allert et al., 2022b; Du et al., 2020). Despite their abundance and potential importance, the physiological functions of most individual MFS transporters in C. auris remain poorly characterized.

Within the MFS, the Qdr subfamily has attracted attention because of its diverse biological functions. Initially characterized in Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Qdr-family transporters contribute to multidrug resistance and solute transport, with Qdr3 displaying a relatively uniform distribution across the plasma membrane (Dos Santos et al., 2014; Shah et al., 2014). Subsequent studies in fungal pathogens have linked Qdr-family proteins to nutrient acquisition, maintenance of membrane potential, stress tolerance, biofilm-associated phenotypes, and virulence-related processes (Dos Santos et al., 2014; Ma et al., 2022; Qadri et al., 2022; Shahi et al., 2020). These observations suggest that Qdr transporters may function as broader regulators of cellular homeostasis rather than solely as determinants of drug resistance. However, whether Qdr-family proteins perform similar roles in C. auris remains unknown.

Here, we characterize the consequences of qdr3 deletion in C. auris strain B8441 using an integrated phenotypic, ultrastructural, transcriptomic, proteomic, and virulence-based approach. Our findings provide new insights into Qdr3′s contributions to cellular homeostasis, mitochondrial remodeling, biofilm architecture, and pathogenicity in this emerging fungal pathogen.

Results

Deletion of qdr3 does not alter basal growth or antifungal susceptibility in C. auris

To assess the contribution of Qdr3 to cellular fitness, we analyzed three independently generated C. auris Δqdr3 knockout mutant strains. Growth-curve analyses demonstrated that all mutants exhibited kinetics comparable to those of the wild-type B8441 under nutrient-rich conditions, indicating that qdr3 is not required for growth under the conditions tested at 30 °C (Figure S1). Likewise, antifungal susceptibility testing revealed no significant differences in minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values between the wild-type and Δqdr3 mutants across all compounds tested (Table S1).

qdr3 deletion induces intracellular remodeling and alters mitochondrial properties

Scanning electron microscopy revealed no discernible differences in cell shape or surface morphology between the wild-type and Δqdr3 cells (Figure S2). In contrast, transmission electron microscopy identified marked intracellular alterations in the mutant strain. Δqdr3 cells exhibited significantly higher Rhodamine 123 fluorescence than the wild-type strain, as confirmed by quantitative analysis of the median fluorescence intensity (MFI) (p < 0.05; Fig. 2B), consistent with mitochondrial-associated alterations. qdr3 deletion alters cell wall composition

To determine whether loss of qdr3 affects cell wall architecture, we quantified major cell wall components by flow cytometry and fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 3). Calcofluor white staining revealed a modest reduction in chitin-associated fluorescence in the Δqdr3 strains; although this difference was not statistically significant (Fig. 3A). In contrast, Concanavalin A staining revealed increased mannan exposure in one of the three independent Δqdr3 mutants compared with the wild type. β-glucan staining showed a trend toward reduced fluorescence in the mutant strains; however, this effect did not reach statistical significance. qdr3 deletion alters biofilm architecture and promotes extracellular matrix accumulation

Biofilm formation was assessed at 37 °C using XTT reduction, crystal violet staining, and colony-forming unit (CFU) quantification. Across all three assays, the Δqdr3 mutant exhibited significantly lower biofilm-associated measurements than the wild-type strain B8441 (Fig. 4). Specifically, the mutant exhibited reduced metabolic activity in the XTT assay, reduced biomass as measured by crystal violet staining, and fewer recoverable viable cells after biofilm growth. To further examine biofilm structure, mature biofilms were analyzed by scanning electron microscopy (Fig. 5). Consistent with quantitative assays, Δqdr3 biofilms contained fewer densely packed cellular aggregates than those formed by the wild type. Wild-type B8441 produced compact multicellular communities containing aggregated yeast cells and pseudohyphal elements, with relatively limited extracellular matrix. In contrast, Δqdr3 biofilms were less cohesive, exhibited more dispersed cells, and accumulated more extracellular matrix. These architectural differences resulted in a flatter, more irregular biofilm surface topography.Fig. 5Scanning electron microscopy of biofilms of the qdr3 mutant under control conditions. Collectively, these findings support a role for Qdr3 in biofilm development and structural organization in C. auris. Loss of qdr3 reduces biofilm biomass and cellular aggregation while promoting extracellular matrix accumulation, indicating that Qdr3 influences multiple aspects of biofilm architecture.

qdr3 deletion enhances virulence in the Galleria mellonella infection model

To determine whether Qdr3 influences pathogenicity, Galleria mellonella larvae were infected with an equivalent inoculum of wild-type B8441 or a representative Δqdr3 mutant strain. Larvae infected with the Δqdr3 mutant exhibited significantly reduced survival and increased mortality relative to those infected with the wild type (Fig. 6). This virulence phenotype was reproducible across independent biological replicates. . These findings demonstrate that deletion of qdr3 enhances virulence in the Galleria mellonella infection model, revealing a complex relationship between cellular homeostasis and pathogenicity in C. auris.Fig. 6Survival of Galleria mellonella larvae following infection with wild-type Candida auris B8441 or a Δqdr3 mutant. Discussion

This study provides functional characterization of the Qdr3 transporter in C. auris and demonstrates that qdr3 deletion is associated with coordinated changes in intracellular organization, biofilm architecture, mitochondrial-associated pathways, and virulence. Although Qdr3 and other QDR-family transporters have been implicated in drug tolerance and membrane-associated processes in S. cerevisiae and C. albicans (Costa et al., 2014; Dos Santos et al., 2014; Ernst et al., 2010), their roles in C. auris remain largely unexplored.

The absence of differences in antifungal susceptibility between the Δqdr3 mutant and the wild-type strains suggests that Qdr3 is not a major determinant of azole efflux under the conditions tested. This contrasts with broader efflux-associated functions attributed to related transporters in other fungi and highlights potential species-specific functional diversification of MFS transporters in C. auris (Allert et al., 2022b; Du et al., 2020; Muñoz et al., 2018).

Ultrastructural and functional analyses revealed marked intracellular changes in the Δqdr3 mutant, including an increased abundance of mitochondrial profiles and altered cytoplasmic organization. These findings were supported by transcriptomic and proteomic enrichment of pathways associated with mitochondrial translation, protein import, and respiratory metabolism, indicating coordinated changes in mitochondrial-associated processes following qdr3 deletion.

Exploratory ultrastructural analysis further showed that this phenotype was reproducible across three independently generated Δqdr3 mutants, all of which exhibited a higher abundance of mitochondrial profiles than the wild-type strain while preserving normal cristae architecture. Although TEM analysis of ultrathin sections does not permit determination of the absolute number of mitochondria, the consistent increase in mitochondrial profile abundance across independent mutants supports an association between qdr3 deletion and mitochondrial remodeling. These ultrastructural observations are also consistent with the increased Rhodamine 123 fluorescence detected in the mutant, suggesting altered mitochondrial activity in the absence of Qdr3. The preservation of cristae architecture indicates that the observed phenotype was not accompanied by evident mitochondrial structural damage.

Biofilm phenotypes further demonstrate the broad impact of qdr3 deletion. Similar alterations in biofilm-related phenotypes have been described for QDR-family transporters in C. albicans (Shah et al., 2014), C. auris Δqdr3 strains showed reduced biofilm biomass, metabolic activity, and viable cell recovery. In addition, structural analyses revealed decreased cellular aggregation and increased extracellular matrix deposition. In addition, structural analyses revealed decreased cellular aggregation and increased extracellular matrix deposition. Together, these observations support a role in maintaining biofilm architecture and multicellular organization in C. auris.

The Galleria mellonella model has been widely adopted for investigating fungal virulence, including C. auris, because it reliably discriminates differences in pathogenic potential while avoiding many of the ethical and logistical constraints associated with mammalian models (Brennan et al., 2002; Garcia-Bustos et al., 2021; Kavanagh and Fallon, 2010; Kavanagh and Sheehan, 2018; Tsai et al., 2016). A finding was the increased virulence of the Δqdr3 mutant in the G. mellonella infection model. This phenotype differs from observations reported for several fungal pathogens in which disruption of transporter genes is associated with reduced virulence (Shah et al., 2014). Although the mechanisms underlying this phenotype remain unclear, several non-mutually exclusive explanations may be considered. Alterations in cell wall composition and reduced expression of surface-associated genes may increase exposure of immunogenic cell-wall components, potentially enhance host immune activation, and contribute to host damage. This is consistent with recent reports in C. auris showing that altered glucan exposure can increase virulence through heightened immune stimulation (Selisana et al., 2024). Alternatively, the coordinated mitochondrial remodeling observed in the Δqdr3 mutant may reflect broader metabolic adaptations that could influence host-pathogen interactions. This possibility, however, remains to be experimentally investigated. Finally, the extensive redundancy of MFS and ABC transporters in C. auris may buffer loss of Qdr3 in ways that reconfigure cellular physiology rather than simply reducing fitness (Dos Santos et al., 2014; Muñoz et al., 2018).

Taken together, these findings support Qdr3 as a multifunctional regulator of C. auris physiology. Rather than functioning solely as a drug efflux transporter, Qdr3 contributes to mitochondrial homeostasis, cell-surface architecture, and host interaction. While several phenotypes, such as altered biofilm structure and changes in cell-surface gene expression, reflect conserved roles of QDR-family transporters, others – including enhanced virulence and mitochondrial remodeling suggest that some Qdr3-associated phenotypes may represent species-specific features of C. auris. Future studies aimed at identifying Qdr3 substrates and defining the metabolic consequences of its loss will be important to further clarify how membrane transport contributes to cellular homeostasis and pathogenicity in C. auris.

Materials and methods

Strains and culture conditions

The experiments were conducted with the C. auris strain B8441, obtained from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and qdr3 mutants were generated in three independent knockout strains (Δqdr3.1, Δqdr3.2, Δqdr3.3) using the CRISPR Add-Tag method (Ennis et al., 2021). The primers used in this study are listed in the Supplemental Table 2. The C. auris pre-inoculum was cultured on Sabouraud agar (20 g/L dextrose, 10 g/L peptone, 18 g/L agar) for 24 h at 30 °C.

Susceptibility testing

MIC testing was performed in three biological replicates according to the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST), EDef 7.2 reference method for yeasts (Arendrup et al., 2012). Briefly, the cells of C. auris B8441, Δqdr3 mutants, and C. parapsilosis ATCC22019, starting from their pre-inoculum, were inoculated into 96-well plates containing 100 µL of RPMI 2X medium supplemented with 2% glucose and buffered to pH 7.0 with 165 mM MOPS, and antifungal agents at concentrations ranging from 0.03 to 125 µg/mL for caspofungin, 0.03 to 16 µg/mL for posaconazole, and 0.125 to 64 µg/mL for fluconazole (Diflucan). A total of 2.5 × 104 cells in 100 μL of water were added to each well, reaching a final volume of 200 µL. Plate were incubated at 35 °C for 24 h, and the fungal growth was evaluated by measuring optical density at 530 nm using Agilent BioTek Synergy H1 reader. MIC was defined as the lowest antifungal concentration that inhibited ≥90% of fungal growth.

Growth curve assays

In 96-well plates, 200 µL of a 2.5 × 105 cells/mL aliquot of C. auris B8441 or Δqdr3 mutants diluted in Sabouraud medium were added. Plates were incubated in a microplate reader for 48 h at 30 °C and 37 °C, with OD530 measurements taken every hour after quick shaking (Agilent BioTek Synergy H1 reader). The data were analyzed with One-way and Two-Way ANOVA tests, comparing each mutant with its wild-type strain under the control condition, both with p ≤ 0.05. Three biological replicates of the assay were performed, each containing three technical replicates. The mean values of each biological replicate were used for the graph and statistical tests.

Rhodamine123 mitochondrial activity assays

To test the mitochondrial activity of the wild type versus knockout strains, cells from both strains were incubated overnight at 30 °C and 200 rpm in Sabouraud medium. Two aliquots of 108 cells for each strain were separated, washed with PBS buffer, and pelleted. One aliquot was treated with Rhodamine 123 at 10 µg/mL for 1 h at 30 °C and 200 rpm. It was then washed with PBS buffer, and the cells were resuspended in 200 µL of culture medium. 100 µL were acquired on a FacsAria II flow cytometer under excitation conditions of 485–505 nm and emission conditions of 525–535 nm. To control mitochondrial membrane potential interruption, the other 100 µL was treated with carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenylhydrazone protonophore (CCCP) at 1 mM for 30 min. The sample was then analyzed on the cytometer. The unstained aliquot was also analyzed under the same conditions, forming the unstained control.

Calcofluor white and concanavalin A staining

To measure the chitin content in the cells, the cells were counted and normalized to a concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL in ultrapure water. The cells were stained with 15 µM of Calcofluor white (CFW) or Concanavalin A (ConA) for 1 hour at room temperature. The cells were washed twice with sterile three times PBS for 5 min. For microscopy, 8-well slides were coated with poly-l-lysine and cells were adhered until dry. Then, the slide was washed with ultrapure water, sealed with ProLong Gold and observed with STELLARIS Confocal Microscope Platform (Leica Microsystems). The same cells were then infused into the Cytoflex LX Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter), with fluorescence excitation intensity at 405 nm and emission at 475 nm for CFW and Alexa488 channel (530/30 bandpass filter) for ConA, 20,000 events were considered. To assess the significance of the data, the One-way ANOVA test was used, comparing each mutant with its wild-type strain under the control condition, both with p ≤ 0.05.

Dectin-1 staining

To assess the differences in β-glucan deposition between wild-type C. auris and the qdr3 mutants, planktonic cells were harvested, washed with PBS and treated with dPBS-BSA 2% for 1 hour at room temperature. Then, cells were washed three times with dPBS. 5µg/mL dectin-1 antibody (Abcam) in dPBS-BSA 2% was added and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. Cells were washed two times with dPBS and incubated with goat anti-mouse Alexa 546 antibody (Thermo Fischer Scientific) (1:500 v/v) for 1 hour in the dark. Cells were washed two times with dPBS, added to a microscopy slide as described above and observed with STELLARIS Confocal Microscope Platform (Leica Microsystems). The same cells were then infused into the Cytoflex LX Flow Cytometer (Beckman Coulter), with fluorescence excitation intensity at 561 nm and emission at 572 nm, and 20,000 events were considered. To assess the significance of the data, the One-way ANOVA test was used, comparing each mutant with its wild-type strain under the control condition, both with p ≤ 0.05.

Biofilm assays

To measure biofilm formation, inocula were adjusted to 1 × 107 cells per mL in RPMI medium, and 200 μL were pipetted into the wells of a 96-well plate. The cells were incubated in a CO2 incubator at 37 °C for 72 h, with a medium change at 48 h. For the XTT reduction assay, the wells were washed three times with sterile 1X PBS, and 180 μL of PBS with 20 μL of XTT at 1.722 mg/mL and 1 μL of 2 mM menadione were added. The plate was incubated for 3 h in a CO2 incubator at 37 °C. 50 µL of the supernatant were transferred to another plate containing 150 µL of PBS, and the absorbance at 490 nm was measured. For the crystal violet assay, the wells were initially washed as in the XTT reduction assay. 100 μL of methanol were added at room temperature for 15 min. The methanol was removed, and the plate was kept in an incubator at 35 °C for 30 min to dry. 200 μL of crystal violet was added for 5 min at room temperature. The plate was washed three times with PBS to remove any residual dye, and 100 μL of 33% acetic acid was added. 50 µL of the supernatant were transferred to another plate containing 150 µL of PBS, and the absorbance at 570 nm was measured. For colony formation units (CFU) count, cells were washed as described above, resuspended in PBS, counted, diluted to 1 × 107 cells/mL in 1 mL and diluted up to 1:1000,000. Then, 5 µL were spotted on a Sabouraud plate and incubated overnight. The colonies grown were counted. Five biological replicates of the assays were performed, with five technical replicates each. In each technical replicate, outliers were excluded using the Grubb's test with a critical G at 95% confidence. To assess the significance of the data, the One-way ANOVA test was used, comparing each mutant with its wild-type strain under the control condition, both with p ≤ 0.05.

For the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) biofilm assay, the inoculum was applied to plastic pieces placed inside 24-well plates, containing 1 mL of the cell solution per well. The plate was incubated for 72 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. After growth, the samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in sodium cacodylate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.4) for 2 h at room temperature. Cells were washed three times with buffer containing sodium cacodylate 0.1 M at pH 7.2, magnesium chloride 2 mM, and sucrose 0.2 M in dH2O. The sample was then dehydrated in ethanol (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%) for 10 min each. Samples were dried to the critical point (Leica EM CPD300) by immersion in ethanol and 10 exchanges with liquid CO2. Then, they were coated with a layer of gold and analyzed using a JEOL JSM-6010 Plus/LA SEM operating at 10 keV.

Galleria mellonella infection assay

Larvae were fed with wax and pollen and kept at 25 °C until they reached 0.2–0.25 g. 24 h before infection, larvae were transferred to 37 °C. Prior to infection, the pro-leg region of 10 larvae per condition was cleaned with 70% ethanol. Suspensions of wild-type C. auris and mutants standardized to 1 × 106 cells were injected using 10 µL syringes (Hamilton, USA), and the larvae were incubated in a CO2 incubator at 37 °C. The experiment was monitored for 10 days, and the criterion for death was lack of movement. For the control condition, PBS was used instead of the fungal suspension. To assess the significance of the data, the Mantel-Cox (Log-rank) test was used. Two independent experiments were conducted.

Scanning electron microscopy of C. auris cells

The cells were collected by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 3 min and washed three times with PBS buffer. Then, they were suspended in 1 mL of fixative, composed of 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, pH 7.2, for 1 hour at room temperature. Subsequently, they were washed three times with post-fixation solution (0.1 M cacodylate buffer, 0.2 M sucrose, and 2 mM MgCl2 – magnesium chloride). After fixation, the cells were adhered to round coverslips coated with poly-l-lysine for 30 min. The excess was quickly removed, and dehydration was performed gradually by adding: 30, 50, 70, 95 and 100% ethanol, for 10 min each. After dehydration, ethanol was exchanged with carbon dioxide (CO2) 10 times. The coverslips were metalized with gold and visualized in a scanning electron microscope (Jeol JSM-6010 Plus-LA) at 5 kV with a working distance (wd) of 8. One biological replicate of each strain was performed.

Ultrastructure transmission electron microscopy

The samples cultivated in liquid medium were washed with 0.1 M phosphate buffer and centrifuged to discard excess fluid. The fixative solution (2% glutaraldehyde, 4% paraformaldehyde, 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) was added and incubated for two hours at room temperature. The tube was centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. The sample was suspended in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer and incubated for 10 min on ice. The tube was centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. This process was repeated two more times. Post-fixation solution (1% potassium permanganate in ultrapure water) was added, and the cells were incubated in this solution for 1 hour. The cells were centrifuged, and most of the supernatant was discarded, leaving about 200 µL of solution. A small amount of ultrapure water was added gently to dilute the permanganate solution. The sample was suspended in ultrapure water and incubated for 5 min, then centrifuged, and the supernatant discarded. This procedure was repeated two more times. 0.15% tannic acid in 0.1 M sodium phosphate buffer, pH 7.4, was added and incubated for 1 min, centrifuged, and the supernatant discarded. The sample was suspended in ultrapure water and incubated for 5 min, centrifuged, and the supernatant discarded. This procedure was repeated two more times. 2% uranyl acetate solution was added and incubated for 1 hour on ice, protected from light. It was centrifuged, and the supernatant discarded. The sample was suspended in ultrapure water and incubated for 5 min, then centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. This process was repeated two more times. The sample was dehydrated with increasing concentrations of ethanol (50%, 70%, 90%, and 100%), incubating on ice for 10 min in each solution. The 100% ethanol incubation was performed three times. A 2:1 solution of ethanol (100%): low-viscosity epoxy resin without catalyst was added, and the sample was incubated in this solution for 1 hour at room temperature in a vertical carousel shaker. Afterward, it was centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. A 1:1 solution of ethanol: resin without catalyst was added and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature in a vertical carousel shaker. The tube was centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. A 2:1 solution of ethanol: resin without a catalyst was added and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature in a vertical carousel shaker. Afterward, the tube was centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. Pure resin without a catalyst was added and incubated for 16 h at room temperature in a vertical carousel shaker. Afterward, the tube was centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. Resin with catalyst was added and incubated for 4 h at room temperature in a vertical carousel shaker. Afterward, the tube was centrifuged, and the supernatant was discarded. For sample embedding, the samples were distributed at the end of molds for subsequent polymerization. The remaining volume of the mold was filled with fresh pure resin. The samples were incubated at 60 °C for 48–72 h. Around 70 nm cuts were made using the ultramicrotome and collected on grids for TEM. A drop of lead citrate was placed on a surface covered with Parafilm®, and the grid was placed on the drop so that the cuts were in direct contact with the solution and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. The grids were washed with 18.2 MΩ.cm H2O by immersing the grid 30 times in a beaker with water (100 mL). This procedure was repeated 3 times. The grid was dried gently and stored in a clean, dry place for TEM analysis using a JEOL 1400 Plus transmission electron microscope (Jeol) operating at 100 kV. One biological replicate of the experiment was performed.

Exploratory ultrastructural assessment

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) micrographs were evaluated across all strains using standardized criteria in ImageJ. A total of 10 images from the wild-type strain and the three independently generated Δqdr3 mutants were examined. Only mitochondrial profiles exhibiting a characteristic double membrane and/or clearly discernible cristae were considered. Cells containing a substantial portion of the cytoplasm were preferentially selected to minimize bias associated with partial ultrathin sections. Mitochondrial profile abundance, cytoplasmic organization, organelle density, preservation of cristae architecture, and the presence of degenerative mitochondrial features were assessed using identical evaluation criteria for all strains. Because the analysis was based on two-dimensional ultrathin sections, the results are reported as mitochondrial profile abundance rather than absolute mitochondrial number.

Transcriptomic analysis

Experiments were performed with three biological replicates and one technical replicate. From a liquid inoculum, the cells were counted, and the necessary amount was separated to achieve 6.25×10⁶ cells/mL in a 250 mL Erlenmeyer flask containing 50 mL of Sabouraud liquid medium. The flasks were incubated in an orbital shaker for 6 h at 30 °C and 200 rpm. The content of each flask was pelleted and suspended in 1 mL of PBS. The PBS was removed, and the cells were frozen in dry ice. For RNA extraction, the frozen cell pellet was macerated in three cycles of liquid nitrogen and suspended in 1 mL of Trizol, gently mixed by inversion until dissolved, and kept in an ice bath. The tube was incubated for 5 min, followed by the addition of 200 µL of chloroform, and the mixture was shaken by inversion. The content was centrifuged in a refrigerated centrifuge (4 °C) for 5 min at maximum speed. The aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube, and 10% v/v of 3 M ammonium acetate and 3 vol of cold 100% ethanol were added. The mixture was incubated on dry ice for 1 hour and centrifuged at maximum speed for 30 min. Afterward, the pellet was washed twice for 15 min each with 1 mL of 100% and 70% ethanol, respectively. The RNA pellet was dried in a speedvac without heat and resuspended in 50 µL of RNAse-free water. Quantification of the samples was performed using the Qubit Fluorometric Quantitation reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc), followed by RNA HS assays to determine concentration and RNA IQ assays to check fragment quality. For library construction, the TruSeq RNA Library Prep Kit v2 (Illumina) was used for constructing the cDNA libraries of the cells, according to the manufacturer's protocol. The final library was quantified by Qubit Fluorometric Quantitation (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc) and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Agilent RNA 6000 Pico Kit). Sample sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform at the Fiocruz-PR Precision Public Health Center (CSPP). For sequence analysis, mapping, and differential expression calculation, CLC Genomics Workbench™ v20.0 (Qiagen) software was used, with the corresponding C. auris genome for the B8441 strain (GCA_002759435.2 V2). The parameters used for the alignments were: cost per nucleotide substitution (2), insertion cost (3), deletion cost (3), read length (0.8), and read similarity (0.8). Only reads that were mapped once were considered in the analysis. The statistical test applied was Differential Gene Expression (DGE) using the RNA-seq package with CLC Genomics Workbench™ v20.0 (Qiagen). Expression values for the transcripts were recorded in TPM (Transcripts per Million), and the TMM (Trimmed Mean of M-values) was used as the normalization method. The criteria for selecting differentially expressed transcripts were fold change ≥ 2 and adjusted p-value for FDR (False Discovery Rate) ≤ 0.05. The sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under BioProject accession number PRJNA1476715.

Proteomic analysis

Cell suspensions of both B8441 (WT) and Δqdr3 strains in the log growth phase corresponding to approximately 50 µL were washed with PBS, frozen, and lyophilized overnight at 0.04 mBar and −50 °C. The lyophilized pellets were broken up with 10 cycles of 1-minute vortexing (with 0.1 mm zirconia beads) and 1 min on ice. 100 µL of 8 M urea was added to the tubes, and the vortexing and ice cycles were repeated five more times. The samples were centrifuged at 18,000 rpm and 4 °C for 15 min, and the flow-through were collected. The protein concentration was determined with the specific Qubit Fluorometric Quantitation assay, according to the manufacturer's instructions. 100 µg of proteins were transferred to a new tube, and the concentration was adjusted to a final volume of 100 µL with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate. Dithiothreitol (DTT) was added to a final concentration of 10 mM and the samples were vortexed and heated at 56 °C for 30 min, then cooled to room temperature. Iodoacetamide was added to a final concentration of 15 mM, vortexed, and incubated in the dark for 25 min at room temperature. DTT was added again to a final concentration of 5 mM, vortexed, and incubated in the dark for 15 min. The protein mixture was diluted with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate until the urea concentration was below 2 M. The pH was measured and adjusted to between 7 and 8. For digestion, trypsin (0.5 µg/mL, Thermo Fisher) was added at a 1:50 (w/w) ratio and incubated overnight at 37 °C. To stop the reaction, TFA was added to a final concentration of 0.2%, and the pH was lowered below 2. The protein concentration was measured with the appropriate Qubit kit, following the manufacturer's instructions. The sample was centrifuged at 18,000 rpm and 4 °C for 15 min, and the flow-through was collected. The peptides were purified with StageTip-C18. 100 µL of LC-MS grade methanol were added to the StageTip to condition it, and it was centrifuged at 1000 x g for 2 min. The methanol was discarded. Two cycles of adding 100 μL of Solution A (water; 0.1% formic acid) and centrifuging at 1000 x g for 4 min were performed. Next, 200 μL of the sample were added to the StageTip and centrifuged at 1000 x g for 5 min. For elution of the peptides, the StageTip was washed twice with 200 μL of Solution A and centrifuged at 1000 x g for 6 min. The StageTip was then attached to an adapter and placed into a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube. The samples were eluted by passing 20 μL of Solution E (0.1% formic acid, 40% ACN) through the StageTip twice, and the StageTip attached to the tube was centrifuged at 1000 x g for 1 min for each 20 μL. The mass spectrometry data were analyzed using MaxQuant software v.2.6.6.0. Peptide identification was performed by searching for the C. auris B8441 strain sequences from the FungiDB database (https://fungidb.org/fungidb/app). Search parameters included variable modifications for acetylation of the N-terminal of the protein and oxidation of methionine. The data generated by MaxQuant were analyzed using Perseus software (v.2.1.3.0), where they were filtered for fold change greater than 1.5 based on the comparison groups. The peptide list was filtered using a t-test with FDR ≤ 0.05, resulting in a new list of peptides that were either enriched or depleted for each mutant compared to the wild-type.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 11 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical tests applied to each experiment are described in the corresponding figure legends when appropriate. Growth curve data were analyzed using one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. Biofilm and fluorescence assays were analyzed using one-way ANOVA. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo software (version 10; BD Biosciences), and median fluorescence intensity (MFI) values were exported for statistical analysis. Comparisons between B8441 and Δqdr3, and between Δqdr3 and Δqdr3 treated with carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP), were performed using paired two-tailed Student’s t-tests, whereas comparisons between B8441 and B8441 treated with CCCP were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Survival curves were analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the Mantel–Cox (log-rank) test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.
