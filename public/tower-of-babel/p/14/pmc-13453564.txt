Philadelphia-negative myeloproliferative neoplasms and acquired von Willebrand syndrome: only pertinent at diagnosis?
Authors: Fanny Lemarié, Jérémy Picard, Hubert Galinat, Simon Jaouen, Brigitte Pan-Petesch, Christophe Nicol, Laura Herbreteau, Lanig Civi, Laëtitia Rio, Wayne-Corentin Lambert, Vincent Olivier, Éric Lippert, Jean Christophe Ianotto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhiladelphia-negative myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) are associated with both thrombotic and bleeding complications. Acquired von Willebrand syndrome (AVWS) is particularly reported in essential thrombocythemia (ET), and its role in bleeding risk remains uncertain.ObjectivesThis study characterized patients with AVWS at the time of MPN diagnosis and evaluated the association with clinical events.MethodsIn our single-center cohort study, 178 patients diagnosed with polycythemia vera (PV), ET, or prefibrotic myelofibrosis (pre-PMF) were included. AVWS was assessed at diagnosis and was defined by a von Willebrand factor:glycoprotein IbM/von Willebrand factor:Ag ratio of <0.65.ResultsPrevalence of AVWS was 38% in the whole cohort (46.3% in PV, 33% in ET, and 36.4% in pre-PMF cases). Patients with AVWS showed a more proliferative profile with elevated platelets, leukocytes, hematocrit, and lactate dehydrogenase levels. The incidence of AVWS increased with the number of elevated blood cell counts, particularly in patients with 3 myeloid lineages implications (OR, 6.14; 95% CI, 2.22-18.9; P < .001). In multivariable models, female sex (OR, 2.52; 95% CI, 1.15-5.78; P = .02), thrombocytosis of >700 × 109/L (OR, 10; 95% CI, 4.48-24.3; P < .001) and polycythemia (OR, 3.75; 95% CI, 1.54-9.61; P = .004) remained independently associated with AVWS. History of bleeding was significantly higher in patients with AVWS (17.65% vs 5.5%; P = .018). Hemorrhage-free survival and thrombosis-free survival were comparable between the 2 groups (P = .45 and P = .61, respectively). All except 1 patient corrected their ratio during follow-up.ConclusionAVWS affects over one-third of newly diagnosed patients with MPN, particularly women or those with thrombocytosis or polycythemia. These results suggest AVWS is not solely driven by extreme thrombocytosis but also by overall myeloproliferative burden.

Introduction

Philadelphia-negative myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) are chronic diseases, including essential thrombocythemia (ET), polycythemia vera (PV), primary myelofibrosis (PMF), and prefibrotic myelofibrosis (pre-PMF). They are characterized by medullar proliferation of myeloid cells and specific mutations [1]. Their natural history is characterized by thrombotic and bleeding events [2,3]. Recent reviews on bleeding in patients with MPN have reported an incidence of bleeding and major bleeding (MB) of 4.6 and 0.79% patients/y in ET and 6.5 and 1.05% patients/y in PV, respectively [4,5]. In ET, hemorrhagic events are associated with a higher rate of mortality than thrombosis, highlighting the need to identify bleeding risk factors [6].

One hypothesis generally put forward to explain a higher bleeding risk in MPN is the presence of acquired von Willebrand syndrome (AVWS) [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. This rare and heterogeneous disorder is characterized by new onset of hemorrhagic symptoms, in the absence of a personal or family history of bleeding diathesis. Von Willebrand factor (VWF) defects in MPN have been explained by either glycoprotein (GP)Ib-mediated adsorption of high-molecular-weight multimers (HMWMs) to the surface of activated platelets or ADAMTS-13–mediated increased proteolysis. This phenomenon is observed in the presence of thrombocytosis or enhanced shear stress [[11], [12], [13]]. Since HMWMs carry the most significant hemostatic effect, their loss results in a reduction of VWF activity (VWF:Ac). Consequently, the ratio of VWF:Ac to VWF antigen (VWF:Ag) decreases, similar to type 2A von Willebrand disease [14]. Historically, the gold standard for assessing VWF:Ac has been the evaluation of ristocetin cofactor activity (VWF:RCo). Recently, innovative automated functional techniques based on the binding of VWF to recombinant GPIb have emerged, including VWF:GPIbM, which is more accurate and provides ristocetin-independent evaluation [15].

In the scientific literature, the reported incidence of AVWS in MPN ranges from 10% to 55% [[16], [17], [18], [19], [20]], although it is likely underreported and variably identified. While the role of AVWS as an independent bleeding risk factor remains controversial, some authors advocate for routine screening in patients with ET and PV, particularly prior to surgical procedures [21,22]. Additionally, concerns regarding the potential association between extreme thrombocytosis and AVWS often lead clinicians to withhold low-dose aspirin in such cases [23,24]. However, bleeding incidence among patients with AVWS varies considerably across studies, with estimates ranging from 0% to 40% [[16], [17], [18], [19],25].

The inconsistencies in these findings may be attributed to several factors: population heterogeneity, variability in VWF assay techniques and threshold values, discrepancies in bleeding event documentation, and differences in the timing of AVWS diagnosis within the MPN course—sometimes occurring in patients already receiving cytoreductive therapy. This study aimed to characterize the clinical and biological profiles of patients diagnosed with AVWS at the time of Philadelphia-negative MPN diagnosis, using modern biological tools such as the GPIbM assay, and to evaluate the association with subsequent clinical events.

Materials and Methods

Design and setting

This ambispective, observational, single-center cohort study was conducted at Brest University Hospital, France (NCT02897297-2015) from May 2015 to May 2024. The OBENE (Observatoire BrEstois des NEoplasies myéloprolifératives) registry includes patients diagnosed with Philadelphia-negative MPNs. Individuals either initially diagnosed or referred for follow-up (FU) at the center were enrolled. All patient provided informed consent according to local ethics requirements.

Diagnoses were made in accordance with consecutive World Health Organization classifications at the time of diagnosis: bone marrow biopsies for ET and pre-PMF cases (before cytoreduction), and isotopic red cell mass assessment for patients with JAK2-mutated ET if their hematocrit was 42% or higher to rule out masked PV.

Clinical and biological data were recorded ambispectively: retrospectively for a thrombotic and hemorrhagic history at any time and prospectively for new events during FU, until death, or last news. We did not collect data on blood groups, since patients with blood group O tend to have lower levels of VWF. Thromboprophylaxis was adapted to cases. Cytoreduction was conducted according to the most recent recommendations, using 1 of the following treatments: phlebotomies, hydroxyurea, pegylated-interferon, anagrelide, pipobroman, or ruxolitinib. Treatment response for cytoreduction was evaluated at 6 ± 2 months and 12 ± 2 months according to international response criteria [26]. Data collection is done routinely for patients followed in our center. Patients with unclassifiable MPN, PMF, or VWF abnormalities identified after MPN diagnosis while under cytoreduction were excluded from this study.

AVWS definition

Patients were prospectively and routinely assessed for quantitative and qualitative abnormalities of plasma VWF at the time of diagnosis, prior to the initiation of cytoreductive therapy. VWF:Ag (Liatest VWF:Ag; Diagnostica STAGO) and VWF:GPIbM (Innovance VWF Ac; Siemens Healthineers) were quantified using turbidimetric assays, following the manufacturer’s recommendations. The VWF:GPIbM is based on the spontaneous binding of VWF to a gain-of-function mutant GPIb fragment and does not require ristocetin. The quantification of VWF multimers was not routinely available for this study.

AVWS was defined by a VWF:GPIbM/VWF:Ag value of <0.65, in the absence of a personal or family history of bleeding disorder. Therefore, the patients were separated into 2 groups: those with AVWS (AVWS group) and those without (no-AVWS group).

Definition of hemorrhagic event

Only MBs or clinically relevant nonmajor bleedings (CRNMB) were recorded. In accordance with the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis (ISTH), MB is defined as fatal bleeding and/or symptomatic bleeding in critical areas or organs, including intracranial, intraocular, intraspinal, retroperitoneal, intra-articular, pericardial, or intramuscular bleeding with compartment syndrome. Additionally, it encompasses bleeding that results in a decline in hemoglobin levels of ≥2 g/dL, or leading to transfusion of ≥2 units of whole blood or red blood cells [27].

CRNMB were considered if they did not meet the MB criteria but met at least 1 of the following criteria: required medical intervention by a health care professional, unscheduled hospitalization, increased level of care, or a face-to-face evaluation [28]. All other hemorrhages were excluded from this study.

Definition of thrombotic event

Arterial thromboses encompassed cerebrovascular accident, transient ischemic attack, acute myocardial infarction, and peripheral arterial thrombotic event. Venous thromboses included cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, and intra-abdominal venous thrombosis. All other types of thrombosis were excluded from this study.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize patient characteristics. Continuous variables were expressed as medians with IQRs and categorical variables as numbers and percentages. Comparisons of continuous variables were performed using the Mann–Whitney U-test, while categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared or Fisher exact test, as appropriate.

Univariable logistic regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with AVWS at diagnosis. Unadjusted odds ratios (ORs) with their 95% CIs were reported. To control for confounding, multivariable logistic regression models were built including variables associated with AVWS in univariable analyses at a P value of < .20. Adjusted ORs with 95% CIs were reported. Multivariable analyses were restricted to complete cases.

To assess the impact of cellular proliferation and multilineage involvement on AVWS, polycythemia was defined as hemoglobin of >16.5 g/dL in men or >16.0 g/dL in women or hematocrit of >49% in men and >48% in women. Thrombocytosis was defined as a platelet count of >450 × 109/L and leukocytosis as a white blood cell (WBC) count >10 × 109/L [1].

Because platelet count showed a nonlinear association with AVWS risk, this relationship was explored using a receiver-operating characteristic curve analysis with Youden index. A threshold of 700 × 109/L was used to dichotomize platelet count for multivariable regression analyses.

Hemorrhage-free survival and thrombosis-free survival were defined as the interval between MPN diagnosis and the occurrence of the first hemorrhage or first thrombosis, respectively. Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Patients without an event were censored at the date of last FU, including those lost to FU. Statistical analyses were conducted using XLSTAT (XLSTAT 2025; Lumivero) for nonparametric and categorical tests, and R software (R Studio 2024.12.0) for logistic regression and survival analyses.

AVWS identification at diagnosis

In the whole cohort, AVWS was identified in 68 (38.2%) patients at the time of MPN diagnosis, including 33 (48.6%) patients with ET, 31 (45.6%) with PV, and 4 (5.9%) with pre-PMF. Patients with AVWS mainly had a JAK2 mutation (n = 56, 82.4% vs 76, 69.1% in the no-AVWS group; P = .05) (Table 1). At diagnosis, patients with AVWS exhibited a median VWF:Ag of 105.5% (IQR, 87%-144.5%), VWF:GPIbM of 56.5% (IQR, 44.7%-70.5%), and VWF:GPIbM/VWF:Ag ratio of 0.54 (IQR, 0.48-0.59), vs 110% (IQR, 96.5%-138.2%), 91% (IQR, 73%-114%), and 0.79 (IQR, 0.73-0.89) in the no-AVWS group, respectively. A statistically significant difference was observed for VWF:GPIbM levels (P < .0001), whereas no significant difference was found for VWF:Ag (P = .39). No patient had a VWF:GPIbM level of <30%, while 24 (35.3%) patients had levels between 30% and 50%. FVIII:C level was significantly lower in the AVWS group (104% [IQR, 85.5%-132.5%] vs 127% [IQR, 104%-155%]; P = .001) (Figure 1). Platelet count was significantly higher in the AVWS group (758 × 109/L [IQR, 630-959 × 109/L] vs 598 × 109/L [IQR, 514-691 × 109/L]; P < .0001), as well as hematocrit (46.7% [IQR, 43.2%-51.9%] vs 43.5% [IQR, 41%-48.1%]), WBC (10.1 × 109/L [IQR, 7.9-12.1 × 109/L] vs 8.8 × 109/L [IQR, 7.7-10.5 × 109/L]; P = .02), absolute neutrophil count (7.1 × 109/L [IQR, 5.7-9 × 109/L] vs 6 × 109/L [IQR, 4.6-7.4 × 109/L]; P = .006), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level (422 IU/L [IQR, 299-520 IU/L] vs 329 IU/L [IQR, 245.2-466.2 IU/L]; P = .001) (Table 1). Patients with AVWS were predominantly characterized by platelet counts of >700 × 109/L, whereas extreme thrombocytosis (≥1000 × 109/L) was generally uncommon except in ET, where it was observed in about one-quarter of cases. In contrast, patients without AVWS more frequently exhibited moderate thrombocytosis between 450 and 700 × 109/L (P < .0001 for the whole cohort, P = .004 for PV, and P = .06 for ET) (Supplementary Figure 1).Figure 1Hemostatic profile at diagnosis of myeloproliferative neoplasms.

Within ET patients, 33 individuals (33%) were diagnosed with AVWS, predominantly with JAK2 mutation (70% vs 54%; P = .024). These patients had higher platelet count (761 × 109/L [IQR, 692-988 × 109/L] vs 619 × 109/L [IQR, 535-702 × 109/L]; P < .0001), higher hematocrit (43.4% [IQR, 42.2%-45.4%] vs 42.3% [IQR, 40.3%-44.2%]; P = .04), and higher LDH levels (393.5 IU/L [IQR, 270-514 IU/L] vs 303 IU/L [IQR, 236-407 IU/L]; P = .02). Hemostasis analyses showed similar trends to those observed in the overall cohort. Patients diagnosed with AVWS had a median VWF:Ag of 109% (IQR, 94%-143%) vs 110% (IQR, 95%-136%; P = .95) and VWF:GPIbM of 60% (IQR, 51%-70%) vs 90% (IQR, 72%-116%; P < .001). The median VWF:GPIbM/VWF:Ag ratio was 0.57 (IQR, 0.5-0.61) vs 0.81 (IQR, 0.75-0.91 in the no-AVWS group) (Supplementary Table 1).

In patients with PV, AVWS was detected in 31 (46.3%) of the 67 patients. These patients exhibited the same proliferative profile with elevated WBC (11.7 × 109/L [IQR, 9.7-12.9 × 109/L] vs 9.3 × 109/L [IQR, 8.3-10.8 × 109/L]; P = .006), neutrophil count (8.5 × 109/L [IQR 7-10 × 109/L] vs 6.6 × 109/L [IQR, 5.6-7.7 × 109/L]; P = .005), and platelet count (699 × 109/L [IQR, 578-926 × 109/L] vs 513 × 109/L [IQR, 407-647 × 109/L]; P = .0002). Allele burden of the JAK2V617F was similar in both groups (38% [IQR, 26.5%-49%] vs 38.5% [IQR, 23%-53.5%]; P = .87). Median VWF:Ag was 100% (IQR, 84.5%-150.5%), and median VWF:GPIbM was 53% (IQR, 43.5%-66%) in patients with AVWS, compared with 112% (IQR, 98.7%-137.5%; P = .06) and 93.5% (IQR, 75.5%-111%; P < .0001), respectively, in those without AVWS (Supplementary Table 2). The odds ratio for developing AVWS in patients with PV compared with patients with ET was 1.74 (95% CI, 0.88-3.46; P = .10).

In the whole cohort, a history of bleeding was documented in 18 patients (10.1%) including 12 (17.65%) patients in the AVWS group vs 6 (5.5%) in the no-AVWS group (P = .018). In the AVWS group, 66% of a hemorrhagic history involved patients with PV (25% for ET; P = .05) (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 3).

Within the 2-year prediagnosis period, a greater number of patients in the no-AVWS group reported thrombotic events (n = 19, 17.3%) compared with AVWS group (n = 5, 7.35%; P = .06) (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 3).

Hemorrhagic and thrombotic events postdiagnosis and relation to AVWS presence

The median FU was 39.6 months (range, 9.1-117.1 months). Patients with AVWS were more likely to receive cytoreductive therapy, particularly hydroxyurea, compared with those without AVWS (P = .01). No significant differences were observed in cytoreduction-free or antithrombotic treatment between groups (Table 1).

During this period, 11 (6.2%) patients experienced new MB or CRNMB events (n = 4/68 [5.9%] in the AVWS group vs n = 9/110, [8.2%] in the no-AVWS group). The most frequent location of hemorrhages was digestive tract (Supplementary Table 3). Among the 93 patients who underwent bone marrow biopsy procedures, including 26 with AVWS, no MB or CRNMB was documented. Median time to first hemorrhagic event was 11.3 months. Hemorrhage-free survival was comparable between the 2 groups (P = .45) (Figure 2A), even after adjustment for cytoreductive treatment status (P = .56) or the presence of antithrombotic therapy (P = .75) (Figure 2B,C). Among the 11 patients who experienced bleeding complications, 8 were receiving cytoreductive treatment at the time of the event. Three patients had persistent cytoses: 1 treated patient with platelets of <450 × 109/L, 1 treated patient with platelets of 970 × 109/L, and 1 untreated patient with platelets of 1400 × 109/L. One patient had polycythemia with hematocrit of >54%. The remaining patients had normalized their hemograms. At the time of bleeding, 50% of patients were receiving anticoagulant therapy (direct oral anticoagulants or low-molecular-weight heparin), compared with 18.4% of patients without bleeding at 1 year (P = .019). When considering direct oral anticoagulants alone, there was no significant difference between patients with bleeding and those without bleeding at 1 year, although only 3 patients with bleeding had received a direct oral anticoagulant. Hemorrhage-free survival was similar according to VWF:Ac level (<50% vs ≥50%; P = .4) and driver mutation status (JAK2, CALR, MPL, or triple negative; P = .46) (Supplementary Figures 3 and 4). Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive characterization of the clinical and biological features associated with AVWS in patients with Philadelphia-negative MPN. Among 178 patients diagnosed with PV, ET, or pre-PMF, AVWS was identified in 38%, an incidence consistent with previous reports, and in all types of MPN. AVWS was predominantly observed in patients with PV (46.3%), followed by ET (33%). These findings contrast with earlier studies, which have reported a higher AVWS incidence in ET (13%-55%) relative to PV (12%-17%). The identification of AVWS in pre-PMF has been rarely described.

Traditionally, AVWS in MPN has been attributed to extreme thrombocytosis, particularly when platelet counts exceed 1000 × 109/L [17,19,29,30]. However, our data challenge this paradigm by demonstrating that AVWS is associated with a more broadly proliferative phenotype, characterized by elevated platelet and hematocrit. Our analysis showed a stepwise increase in AVWS risk with the number of elevated blood cell counts, with the highest risk observed in patients exhibiting trilineage proliferation. This finding suggests a cumulative effect of myeloproliferation on VWF impairment. The modest coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.18) between platelet count and the VWF:GPIbM/Ag ratio indicates that factors beyond platelet count contribute substantially to VWF dysfunction in this population. Multivariable analysis identified both thrombocytosis of >700 × 109/L and polycythemia as independent risk factors for AVWS, while male sex appeared to be protective. Unlike previous reports, no association was found with younger age and JAK2 mutation status or leukocytosis [16,17,19,31].

While extreme thrombocytosis is a well-recognized driver of AVWS, these data suggest that polycythemia may also contribute. Elevated hematocrit alters blood rheology and promotes platelet margination toward the vessel wall, increasing platelet–VWF interactions and facilitating consumption of HMWM [32,33]. Concurrently, increased shear stress may induce VWF unfolding, exposing its A2 domain to ADAMTS-13–mediated cleavage [34]. While shear stress alone modestly increases VWF proteolysis, its combination with platelet binding significantly amplifies cleavage efficiency—likely due to the mechanical forces transmitted through VWF–platelet complex [34]. JAK2V617F-driven erythroid signaling may still upregulate membrane proteins such as calreticulin, potentially destabilizing the VWF–FVIII complex [35]. Supporting this mechanism, murine models expressing JAK2V617F in both hematopoietic and endothelial compartments exhibit selective loss of HMWM [36].

We identified that presence of AVWS at diagnosis was associated with a higher rate of hemorrhagic history (P = .018) and a close to significance reduction of thrombotic events (P = .06). On the contrary, postdiagnosis hemorrhagic and thrombotic event rates were comparable between groups, even after adjustment for cytoreductive and antithrombotic therapy. The median time to first bleeding was 11.3 months. Notably, Song et al. [19] reported that hemostatic abnormalities in AVWS may normalize within weeks following initiation of cytoreductive therapy. For the first time, we reanalyzed these patients and found that AVWS disappeared is all patients during FU, despite persistent thrombocytosis or under aspirin only. The persistence of bleeding despite resolution of AVWS confirms that other factors contribute to bleeding risk in MPN. Of note, no bleeding complications occurred during or after bone marrow biopsies, including among patient with AVWS, despite the procedures being performed prior to the initiation of cytoreductive therapy and in the absence of hemostatic prophylaxis. While this observation does not warrant definitive recommendations, it supports that AVWS should not systematically delay diagnostic bone marrow evaluation due to bleeding concerns. Furthermore, our cohort experienced fewer bleeding events than typically reported in the literature, supporting the notion that appropriate and timely management can effectively reduce hemorrhagic risk in this population. This relatively mild bleeding in MPN-associated AVWS contrasts with the more severe hemorrhagic risk in lymphoproliferative disorders, which can lead clinicians to overestimate bleeding in MPNs. In fact, current evidence, including ISTH registry data, shows that AVWS associated with lymphoproliferative disorders differs from MPN-related AVWS in both phenotype and mechanism. Lymphoid disorders represent the largest etiologic subgroup (≈48%) and exhibit higher bleeding rates (≈87%) [37]. Treatment of the underlying malignancy can improve AVWS in both groups, but normalization occurs more slowly in lymphoid disorders (4-6 months) compared with MPNs [38]. Direct comparisons remain limited, particularly due to the lack of prospective studies in lymphoid disease using modern hemostatic assays such as GPIbM.

This study has several strengths, including systematic AVWS screening at diagnosis, which is a major advantage, as many studies have assessed AVWS retrospectively, sometimes years after the diagnosis of MPN [16,17]. VWF:GPIbM test was selected for its accuracy, sensitivity—particularly in cases of HMWM deficiency—and is now recommended for the diagnosis of von Willebrand disease [14,15,25,39]. The threshold of 0.65 for VWF:GPIbM/VWF:Ag ratio was chosen to minimize the risk of false positives, which could lead to overdiagnosing AVWS [40,41]. Regarding the classification of bleeding, only relevant bleedings were recorded. MB and CRNMB definitions are widely accepted for phase III anticoagulant drug studies and relatively objective, limiting the analysis of intervariability in the severity of bleeding [42,43].

Nevertheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. The monocentric design and moderate sample size may limit the generalizability of our findings. The presence of a personal history of bleeding may have influenced the search for AVWS at diagnosis, leading to an overestimation of its incidence and a higher incidence of hemorrhagic history. Postdiagnosis events should be interpreted cautiously for several reasons: 1) AVWS status may have change through the correction of biological abnormalities after cytoreduction as documented by Song et al. [18,19,44]. No FU of VWF assessment was performed in this study. 2) Levels of VWF:GPIbM and VWF:Ag were relatively preserved within the cohort, with no patient exhibiting a VWF:GPIbM level of <30%. 3) The loss of HMWM was not investigated by gel electrophoresis, although this is the reference technique and some studies have shown a weak correlation between VWF:Ac and multimer analysis.

Taken together, these findings support the notion that AVWS in MPNs is likely multifactorial, involving not only thrombocytosis but also other disease-related mechanisms that remain to be fully elucidated. As highlighted by Venkat et al. [45], the contribution of AVWS to bleeding risk is not straightforward, suggesting that bleeding events themselves are also multifactorial and that their specific risk factors—beyond VWF defects—warrant further investigation. In this context, the investigation of other hemostatic abnormalities, such as impaired VWF–collagen interaction or altered platelet function assessed by in vivo aggregometry techniques, including the multiple electrode aggregometry, may represent promising avenues for future research [40,[46], [47], [48]].

In conclusion, 38% of patients with Philadelphia-negative MPN exhibit AVWS at diagnosis, particularly among those with thrombocytosis, polycythemia, and female sex. Notably, the incidence of biological AVWS was higher in PV than in ET, challenging the traditional association of AVWS exclusively with extreme thrombocytosis. These findings highlight the role of overall myeloproliferative burden—beyond platelet count alone—in the pathophysiology of AVWS. Presence of AVWS was only associated with previous vascular events. His systematic disappearance after correct treatment, diminish its predictive value for subsequent bleeding (whatever the cytoreductive and antithrombotic status). Nobody knows whether AVWS is transient or not. Prospective studies with systematic VWF monitoring are required to better understand the clinical impact of AVWS. Ongoing studies on young patients are expected to clarify its relevance in cytoreduction-naive individuals.
