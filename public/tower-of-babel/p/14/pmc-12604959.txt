AI enhanced strong-field terahertz spectral detection and imaging
Authors: Mingxuan Zhang, Shaojie Liu, Jiahui Li, Zehao Yang, Zejun Ren, Deyin Kong, Yida Wu, Lin Du, Peidi Yang, Xinxiong Chen, Songbo Cui, Caihua Wan, Xiaojun Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryTerahertz (THz) waves demonstrate advantages including broad absolute bandwidth, low photon energy, and superior penetration capability, showing significant potential in nondestructive testing and spectroscopic imaging applications. However, current techniques face challenges including unknown material refractive indices, pulse signal aliasing, and time-consuming detection processes, which hinder the advancement and commercialization of THz-based nondestructive testing. This work presents a nondestructive testing system employing an enhanced 4-inch spintronic strong-field THz emitter, integrated with neural-network-assisted thickness prediction and contour detection. The system achieves micrometer-scale accuracy for ultrathin materials and rapid defect identification in large-scale samples. Experimental results demonstrate 99.8% measurement accuracy within ±8 μm for micrometer-scale samples, with submillimeter-level depth resolution for defect characterization and contour imaging. This technology demonstrates scalability and high flexibility, laying the foundation for thickness measurement of multilayer composite coatings and internal defect detection in complex materials, showing broad prospects in surface coating processing, component maintenance, and cultural heritage preservation.

Introduction

THz waves (0.1–10 THz), occupying the electromagnetic spectrum between microwaves and infrared radiation, uniquely combine the strong penetration capability of microwaves with the high-resolution characteristics of infrared radiation. Compared to conventional inspection technologies, THz imaging demonstrates distinct advantages in detecting metal defects,1,2 coatings,3 and packaging materials,4 with penetration depths reaching centimeter-scale and spatial resolution achieving submillimeter-level precision, enabling high-resolution characterization of material internal structures.5 Relative to microwave detection, the shorter wavelength of THz technology significantly enhances interfacial defect identification capabilities. When compared with infrared inspection, THz waves can penetrate infrared-shielding media such as composite materials whereas eliminating thermal radiation effects and high-energy impacts on test objects. Current THz wave imaging applications span security screening,6 cultural heritage preservation,7 paint thickness measurement,8,9 internal structure evaluation of composite materials,10 and fundamental scientific research in condensed matter nanoscale imaging.11

The new-generation technological revolution and industrial upgrading have driven rapid advancements in vehicle manufacturing and aerospace engineering, creating growing demands for nondestructive thickness measurement of non-metallic materials such as paints and thermal barrier coatings and surface defect inspection in metallic components. Due to its penetration capability through many non-polar materials and high spatial resolution, THz spectroscopic analysis and spectral imaging have emerged as established methodologies for thickness evaluation and nondestructive testing of composite materials. The time-of-flight (ToF) method, a conventional approach for through-thickness measurement and computed tomography (CT) imaging, demonstrates operational simplicity and intuitive interpretation.12,13 However, this technique requires precise knowledge of the refractive index of the material to ensure measurement accuracy. Furthermore, thickness determination via ToF typically relies on the time delay between front and back surface reflections, yet encounters reflection peak aliasing in ultrathin samples, which complicates direct calculation of interfacial features. Recent studies have demonstrated that artificial intelligence (AI) enhanced approaches employing iterative training with thickness measurement signal datasets enable effective identification of aliased reflection time differences.14,15,16 Current CNN-based convolutional neural networks achieve 20-micrometer-level thickness measurement, though enhancing training efficacy, stability, and cross-scenario generalizability remains crucial.14,15,17,18

Beyond thickness measurement, defect imaging constitutes a critical component of nondestructive evaluation.19,20 While raster scanning serves as a conventional approach for defect characterization, providing detailed spatial information through step-by-step scanning, it becomes a time-consuming process for large-scale specimens. Therefore, developing technologies capable of accurate micrometer-scale thickness quantification and efficient defect imaging in large-area samples is imperative. This necessitates simultaneous improvements in system flexibility and testing efficiency for practical industrial implementations.21,22

This work presents an AI-enhanced strong-field THz spectroscopic analysis and imaging technique that achieves micrometer-scale thickness measurement and efficient large-area defect contour detection. The system leverages a one-dimensional photonic crystal enhanced spintronic strong-field THz emitter, demonstrating critical performance metrics including a 58 dB dynamic range, 0.367 ps full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) pulse duration, and a broadband spectrum approximately 5.13 THz.23,24,25 These combined characteristics enable superior capabilities for both ultrathin material characterization and high-resolution imaging applications. The proposed methodology not only determines the THz-frequency refractive indices of homogeneous materials but also implements a Conformer network architecture integrating CNNs with Transformer modules.26 This AI framework achieves silicon wafer thickness measurement spanning 1–1000 μm with 99.8% accuracy within ±8 μm tolerance through systematic training validation. Additionally, the technology combines rapid preliminary defect screening for large samples with AI-assisted defect contour imaging. It effectively detects submillimeter-scale metal defects and images millimeter-scale voids or cracks, exhibiting high system flexibility and testing efficiency. This advancement establishes a foundation for AI-assisted THz thickness measurement of multilayer composite coatings and internal defect detection in complex materials, showing application potential in paint processing, security inspection, and biomedical fields.

Experimental setup and details

Figure 1 illustrates the AI-enhanced spintronic strong-field THz spectroscopic detection imaging setup and its corresponding time-domain pulses and frequency spectrum. Figure 1A details the experimental configuration of the detection system. To generate strong-field THz radiation, a Ti:sapphire laser amplifier is employed to produce femtosecond laser pulses with 35 fs pulse duration, 1 kHz repetition rate, 4 mJ peak energy, and 800 nm central wavelength. These pulses pump a 4-inch-diameter spintronic THz emitter (STE) with a one-dimensional photonic crystal structure, generating a 4-inch strong-field THz beam via the inverse spin Hall effect (ISHE). The THz pulses interact with the test target through the nondestructive detection module, after which they are captured by the electro-optic sampling (EOS) detection unit. A lock-in amplifier and computer process the signals, which are visualized as THz waveforms using custom-developed software for subsequent data analysis. Figures 1B and 1C respectively demonstrate the transmission-mode and reflection-mode detection functionalities of the system. In transmission-mode detection, the sample is positioned in the optical path where THz waves are normally incident from the emitter onto the specimen, with planar mirrors redirecting the transmitted THz radiation to the detector. For reflection-mode detection, the sample directly reflects THz waves. The emitted beam impinges on the specimen at a 15° incident angle and is subsequently reflected to the detector at a matching 15° angle by either the sample or its substrate. Both detection modes enable raster scanning through two-dimensional translation of a perforated metal plate within the optical path. Considering material-specific requirements: transmission-mode detection is suitable for nonconductive composite materials and low-density substances, whereas reflection-mode detection applies to coated materials on metallic substrates. This dual-mode design simultaneously satisfies diverse material characterization needs, ensuring broad application compatibility.

Figures 1D and 1E display the system-generated THz reference signal and its frequency spectrum, respectively. The THz time-domain pulse exhibits an FWHM of 0.367 ps and a spectral bandwidth of approximately 5.13 THz, with the dynamic range peaking at 58 dB around 0.88 THz. This signal corresponds to the emission from the enhanced spintronic strong-field THz emitter without test sample, serving as the incident signal for the nondestructive detection module in Figure 1A. For thickness measurement, when the temporal separation between front and back surface reflections exceeds the pulse FWHM, the two echo pulses remain non-aliased, enabling direct thickness calculation from their temporal spacing. Consequently, narrower time-domain pulses permit measurement of thinner specimens. With the 0.367 ps-FWHM THz pulse, the minimum detectable thickness for silicon wafers (refractive index ∼3.4) is approximately 16.1 μm, demonstrating potential for direct thickness measurement on ultrathin samples. Regarding defect imaging, the broad spectral bandwidth and high dynamic range of the THz signal enhance spectroscopic imaging resolution and quality, enabling more sensitive and accurate detection. Notably, when using a thinner electro-optic crystal for EOS, the time-domain pulse width reduces to ∼0.2 ps with an effective spectral bandwidth reaching 10 THz. This indicates opportunities for technical optimization, suggesting potential for micrometer/submicrometer-scale sample detection.23

The ToF method serves as a universal approach for thickness measurement, with its governing formula expressed as:(Equation 1)d=cΔt2ncosθtIn Equation 1, c denotes the speed of light in free space, Δt represents the temporal separation between front and back surface pulse peaks, n is the refractive index of the material under test, and θt corresponds to the THz refraction angle at the interface. Following Snell’s law, the refraction angle θt is calculated as:(Equation 2)θt=sin−1n0sinθinIn Equation 2, n0 denotes the refractive index of air (n0 = 1), and θi represents the incidence angle of the electromagnetic wave at the interface. The THz wave in the nondestructive detection module of this system exhibits a 15° incidence angle at the front surface of the sample.

Equations 1 and 2 indicate that the ToF thickness measurement requires prior knowledge of the refractive index of the material. For materials with unknown refractive indices, this technology enables refractive index calculation via reflection-mode measurements.27 As shown in Figure 2B, the THz wave undergoes reflection at the sample interface: R0 corresponds to the front-surface reflection, R1 to the back-surface reflection, and R2 to the secondary back-surface reflection. Figure 2C displays the reflection-mode measurement results for a high-resistivity silicon wafer with a nominal thickness of 515 μm. Three distinct reflection peaks corresponding to the previously mentioned reflections R0, R1, and R2 are clearly observed in the signal. By substituting their extracted electric field strengths into Equations 8, 9, and 10, the calculated THz-frequency refractive index of the silicon wafer is approximately 3.4644. This value aligns closely with the established reference value of 3.418 for high-resistivity silicon in the THz regime,28,29,30 validating the efficacy of this technique in estimating unknown material properties. It is noteworthy that the proposed refractive index extraction method is applicable under near-normal or small-angle THz incidence, whereas larger incidence angles may introduce deviations from conventional reference values.

By substituting the extracted refractive index obtained through the aforementioned method into Equations 1 and 2, the thickness of the test sample can be calculated based on the temporal separation between reflection peaks in each echo signal. Figure 2D presents thickness measurement results for multiple high-resistivity silicon wafers, demonstrating that all measured values fall within the nominal thickness specifications and their tolerance ranges. Specifically, the error bars in the figure are derived from the error range of the thickness of this batch of silicon wafers. This conclusively validates the capability and effectiveness of the proposed technology for accurate thickness measurement of ultrathin samples.

Theoretical calculations demonstrate that THz echoes from the front and back surfaces of test samples exhibit no aliasing when the sample thickness exceeds 16.1 μm. However, given the opposite polarities of these reflection pulses, their superposition at small temporal separations may cause partial signal cancellation in overlapping regions, complicating peak discrimination. Consequently, the practical minimum resolvable thickness becomes twice the theoretical value (≥32.2 μm) to ensure reliable pulse identification. To achieve micrometer-scale thickness measurement for ultrathin samples, establishing precise one-to-one correspondence between aliased reflection patterns and sample thickness becomes essential. Deep learning methods effectively address this requirement by resolving aliased signals whereas providing auxiliary thickness verification for non-aliased, relatively thicker specimens.

A dataset was established based on experimentally measured emission signals from the enhanced spintronic strong-field THz emitter. The dataset comprises training, validation, and test sets containing simulated silicon wafer reflection signals with thicknesses spanning 1–1000 μm at 1 μm increments (1000 groups total). Reflection and transmission coefficients are calculated using Equations 6 and 7, enabling determination of echo amplitudes and time delays for front/back surface reflections at specified thicknesses. Superposition of these calculated echoes yields the simulated reflection signal. The training set incorporates 8000 signals (8 experimental emission signals with 1000 thicknesses), while the validation and test sets each contain 1000 signals (1 experimental signal with 1000 thicknesses). It should be noted that the primary task of our neural network model is to determine sample thickness by analyzing the temporal waveform of the signal and identifying the peak positions of the two echo pulses. Although the simulated dataset shown in Figure 3 was generated using a measured signal segment of approximately 8 ps combined with zero-padding, the positions of the main pulses in each simulated signal remain well preserved and clearly identifiable. While post-pulse oscillations are indeed present at the physical level, their amplitude is significantly weaker and does not materially affect the extraction of the main pulse positions or the accuracy of thickness calculation. Nevertheless, a longer reference signal could improve the physical realism of the simulated waveforms, which is particularly important for multilayer or complex structures where secondary reflections may carry additional information. In future studies focused on multilayer analysis or full-waveform inversion for thickness prediction and sample characterization, we will employ more complete reference signals in our investigations.

As illustrated in Figure 4A, the deep learning framework employs a Conformer network model for training. The Conformer architecture represents a hybrid model that integrates CNNs with Transformer modules. This combination preserves the powerful local feature extraction capability inherent in CNNs while leveraging the Transformer’s advantages in global context modeling, thereby enabling a more comprehensive understanding of input patterns. The network architecture comprises conventional CNN blocks, positional encoding-enhanced Transformer layers, feature coupling units (FCUs), and fully connected layers. The multilayer perceptron structure within FCUs facilitates effective integration of features from both CNN and transformer branches through nonlinear space transformation, thereby enhancing the feature representation capacity of the model. Residual connections are incorporated to improve training efficiency and stability, enabling more effective learning of deep feature representations. Layer normalization mitigates internal covariate shift, accelerates network convergence, and enhances model generalization. The proposed model exhibits extensibility for thickness measurement of other materials. Although the dataset is generated based on the refractive index of silicon wafers, the network fundamentally learns and extracts one-dimensional data features from input reflected echo signals. Notably, the output is determined by the temporal difference Δt between pulse peaks, remaining independent of material type and refractive index n. By substituting the refractive index of an unknown material into Equations 1 and 2, the scaling factor between its thickness and silicon wafer thickness can be calculated. The measured thickness of the new material is then obtained by multiplying each network output by this coefficient. Rapid defect localization and AI-assisted contour extraction

Raster scanning is widely adopted for defect detection due to its precise localization capability. However, this method encounters significant efficiency limitations when inspecting decimeter-scale or larger specimens, primarily manifested as prolonged scanning duration and reduced throughput. This work proposes a complete process for rapid defect localization and AI-assisted contour extraction of decimeter-scale large-sized samples. Firstly, relying on the one-dimensional photonic crystal-enhanced spin strong-field THz source in this technology, which can directly emit THz radiation with a diameter of four inches, the surface features of the sample are extracted, and the damaged areas on the sample are quickly located. After determining the range of the damaged area, raster scanning is performed on it to extract the preliminary contour features in the signal. Then, the data are smoothed to remove the pixelation of the image, and finally, an AI model is used for processing and output to realize detailed detection of the defect contour. The previous process can shorten the time required for detecting decimeter-scale large-sized samples and improve detection efficiency through two main steps: defect localization and contour scanning extraction. In addition, the previous process can be split into individual steps according to actual detection needs, which also reflects the testing flexibility of this technology. The feasibility of this technology will be explained and verified from the two steps of rapid detection of defects in large-sized samples and AI-assisted defect contour detection below, respectively.

Our technique employs a one-dimensional photonic crystal-enhanced spintronic strong-field THz emitter capable of generating collimated 4-inch-diameter beams. This configuration enables large-area scanning within equivalent time frames, significantly improving defect screening efficiency for macroscopic samples. Experimental validation was conducted using two 120 mm × 120 mm aluminum plates: one with a flat surface and another containing a 60 mm-diameter circular depression (1 mm depth), inspected through the reflection-mode detection module illustrated in Figure 1C. As demonstrated in Figure 5, the reference plate exhibits a single reflection peak corresponding to the surface echo (Figure 5A), whereas the defective specimen reveals two temporally resolved peaks (Figure 5B) representing reflections from the surface and defect interface. The sub-picosecond temporal pulse width of our THz emission ensures clear temporal separation between these echoes, demonstrating millimeter-to-submillimeter defect depth detection capability. Given that a single measurement with the 4-inch-diameter THz beam covers 16 times the area of the 1-inch beam, this method achieves approximately 16-fold higher detection efficiency for decimeter-scale and larger samples compared to small-spot detection techniques. For imaging validation, a 50 mm × 20 mm metal plate featuring a hollowed “THz” letter pattern (Figure 6B) was scanned using the transmission-mode nondestructive testing module depicted in Figure 1B. With a scanning step size of 2 mm, the peak-to-peak amplitude of THz signals was utilized for imaging reconstruction, as presented in Figure 6C. Given that the optical path length remains constant during detection, the temporal position of the THz pulse on the time axis shows minimal variation. Consequently, signal acquisition at each sampling point requires scanning only the temporal vicinity of the THz pulse. This approach significantly reduces the acquisition time for fine sample scanning, thereby enhancing detection efficiency. The limited sampling density resulted in discernible pixelated artifacts forming the letter pattern. To enhance contour extraction, the bicubic interpolation method defined in Equation 12 was applied to the raw image data, yielding the refined reconstruction shown in Figure 6D.(Equation 12)I(x,y)=∑i=03∑j=03aijxiyjIn Equation 12, the coefficients aij incorporate function values and their first-order, second-order, and mixed partial derivatives at 16 neighboring pixels surrounding the interpolation point, where x and y denote relative positional offsets between the interpolation point and adjacent known pixels.

Comparison between Figures 6C and 6D reveals that bicubic interpolation effectively achieves image smoothing and mitigates mosaic artifacts caused by insufficient pixel density. Relative to nearest-neighbor and bilinear interpolation methods, bicubic interpolation generates smoother images with reduced jagged edges while preserving finer details and mitigating block-like artifacts. Additionally, a Gaussian filter was applied to suppress environmental noise, thereby enhancing the prominence of structural contours.

It should be noted that the unique challenge in THz imaging lies in its characteristically low signal-to-noise-ratio (SNR), uneven contrast, and often fragmented features—conditions under which traditional algorithms struggle. The importance of our AI-assisted approach is in performing it reliably and autonomously on THz scans where conventional methods fail without extensive manual parameter tuning and post-processing. We processed the scanned image from Figure 6D using three classical methods and our network, and the results are shown in Section S4 of the supplemental information. The results clearly demonstrate that classical methods produce fragmented contours or significant noise. In addition, the AI model we applied delivers a clean, continuous, and accurate contour extraction without any manual intervention, directly from the raw interpolated data. The AI does not make the scanning faster; it makes the post-scan analysis radically more efficient and robust, which is a major bottleneck in translating THz imaging from the lab to the factory floor.

In conclusion, this work presents an AI-assisted THz spectroscopic analysis and imaging technique, employing a nondestructive detection system based on spintronic strong-field THz emitter capable of both reflection-mode and transmission-mode operation. Leveraging the broadband spectrum and sub-picosecond temporal pulse width of the emitted THz waves, we achieve refractive index extraction and thickness measurement for micrometer-scale specimens. A THz reference signal dataset was established alongside a Conformer-based neural network, enabling 99.8% accurate thickness prediction within ±8 μm error range for overlapping signals from ultrathin samples. The 4-inch collimated beam from our spintronic THz emitter facilitates rapid defect screening in decimeter-scale specimens, with demonstrated detection sensitivity reaching millimeter-to-submillimeter depth resolution. Furthermore, the short wavelength and high dynamic range characteristics permit detailed defect scanning and spectroscopic imaging, complemented by neural network-based edge extraction for precise contour mapping.

While these advancements demonstrate significant capabilities, several areas merit further optimization. The optical configuration could be enhanced through implementation of thinner electro-optic crystals for THz detection, potentially broadening spectral bandwidth and narrowing temporal pulses without compromising field intensity, thereby enabling thickness measurement of sub-micrometer layers. Higher-energy laser pump source could be employed, which will allow us to reduce the aperture size for higher spatial resolution while maintaining the necessary SNR, fully unlocking the potential of this configuration for high-resolution imaging. The dataset architecture and network parameters also hold potential for iterative refinement to accelerate training convergence and improve prediction fidelity.

In conclusion, this work effectively addresses critical challenges including detection of unknown material refractive indices, ultrathin material thickness measurement under pulse signal aliasing, and proposes a complete process for rapid localization and AI-assisted contour extraction of decimeter-scale large-sized samples, thereby enabling versatile nondestructive testing and spectroscopic imaging capabilities. The proposed technique demonstrates scalability across diverse materials and offers high flexibility through adaptable detection modalities, ensuring operational efficiency. This lays a solid foundation for thickness measurement of micrometer-scale multilayer composite coatings, internal defect detection in complex materials, and advanced spectroscopic imaging, with broad application prospects in surface coating processing, component maintenance, and cultural heritage preservation.

Limitations of the study

In this study, the setup presents potential for optimization. Employing thinner electro-optic crystals could enhance spectral bandwidth and narrow the temporal pulse width of the detected THz waves, enabling thickness measurement of thinner samples, provided sufficient THz signal field intensity is maintained.

Detailed structure and emission mechanism of the STE

The 4-inch diameter spintronic thin film is fabricated on a 500-μm thick Si substrate. The main structure of the thin film consists of a W(2 nm)/Co20Fe60B20(2 nm)/Pt(2 nm) trilayer heterostructure, where Co20Fe60B20 serves as the ferromagnetic (FM) layer, and both W and Pt serve as heavy metal (HM) layers with opposite spin Hall angles. The FM layer is magnetized to saturation by an external magnetic field. When femtosecond laser pulses are absorbed by the thin film, spin-up and spin-down electrons are excited above the Fermi level, generating a longitudinal spin current due to their different densities and mobilities. At the interface between the FM and HM layers, the spin current is converted into a transverse in-plane charge current via the inverse spin Hall effect (ISHE). Owing to the total thickness of the heterostructure being only 6 nm, the radiated THz transients can be amplified and effectively augmented through coherent superposition.

To enhance the utilization efficiency of the sample for the pump laser pulse, a one-dimensional photonic crystal structure composed of 40 periods of alternating layers of 136-nm thick SiO2 and 92-nm thick HfO2, with a total thickness of ∼9 μm, was formed between the Si substrate and the heavy metal W. This multilayer film structure coherently enhances the THz wave, increasing the field strength of the THz radiation and thereby improving the dynamic range of the radar system. The dynamic range was calculated as the ratio of the peak signal amplitude to the root-mean-square (RMS) noise floor, expressed in decibels (dB). The 4-inch STE is fabricated using highly robust coating technology and mature magnetron sputtering methods.

Under femtosecond laser pump, THz radiation is generated from the one-dimensional photonic crystal structure enhanced STE. A 1-inch femtosecond laser beam, after passing through an expanding system of a concave lens and a convex lens, becomes a 4-inch femtosecond laser beam incident on a 4-inch STE to produce THz pulses.
