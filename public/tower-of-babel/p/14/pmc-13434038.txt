Bifurcation analysis in a piecewise-smooth dynamical system modeling the effects of biological and ethological controls on the coffee berry borer
Authors: Carlos Andrés Trujillo-Salazar, Gerard Olivar-Tost, Deissy Milena Sotelo-Castelblanco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe coffee berry borer (CBB), Hypothenemus hampei, is the most damaging pest in coffee agroecosystems worldwide, requiring effective and sustainable control strategies. In this work, we develop and analyze a piecewise-smooth dynamical system that models the interaction between biological control and threshold-activated ethological control. The model incorporates a discontinuous switching mechanism based on pest density, leading to non-smooth dynamics analyzed within the Filippov framework. We characterize pseudo-equilibria, tangent points, and sliding dynamics, and identify both codimension-one and codimension-two bifurcations that organize the system’s qualitative behavior in parameter space. Our results reveal that the interplay between the activation threshold and trapping intensity determines transitions between regimes of pest persistence, suppression, and low-density control. In particular, early activation of control combined with sufficient capture rates leads to regimes where pest populations are effectively contained or driven toward extinction within the model framework. This reveals novel dynamical regimes not captured by smooth models. These findings provide a rigorous dynamical foundation for threshold-based pest management strategies and highlight the relevance of discontinuity-induced phenomena in ecological systems.Keywords: Coffee berry borer, Nonhyperbolic equilibrium point, Saddle-node bifurcation, Piecewise-smooth dynamical systems, Boundary equilibrium bifurcation, Codimension two bifurcationSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Ecology, Ecology, Mathematics and computing, Zoology

Introduction

Coffee is one of the main sources of agricultural income in many regions worldwide, particularly in Latin American and Asian countries, where its production not only contributes significantly to local economies but also shapes the cultural and social landscape of rural communities1. In Colombia, in particular, coffee serves as a key driver of the national economy, providing employment to millions of people2. However, coffee cultivation is constantly threatened by various pests, with the Coffee Berry Borer, Hypothenemus hampei (Ferrari, 1867) (Coleoptera: Curculionidae: Scolytidae), hereafter referred to as CBB, being the most widespread and devastating1,3,4.

The CBB is a pest that has severely impacted the global coffee industry, as this small beetle bores into the coffee fruit, feeding on its seed and causing significant production losses. Controlling this pest has been a persistent challenge for coffee growers, who face substantial economic losses when they fail to keep CBB populations below acceptable economic damage thresholds, which are generally set at less than 5% of the affected harvest1.

In an effort to address this issue, various control strategies have been explored, including chemical control, which involves the use of insecticides to eliminate adult CBBs that are outside the coffee bean and attempting to penetrate other beans1,5. However, chemical control is becoming increasingly less common due to its negative environmental impact, high toxicity, effects on human health, and the growing market demand for pesticide-free food products. This has driven the search for environmentally friendly pest control alternatives, such as biological and ethological control6,7.

Biological control, or biocontrol, is a pest management strategy that employs living organisms such as parasitoids, predators, and entomopathogens to reduce harmful species populations to tolerable levels1,8,9. On the other hand, ethological control, in the specific case of the CBB, is based on manipulating the natural behaviors of the pest, particularly those of flying females searching for new coffee beans to infest. This approach relies on traps, often handcrafted, as an effective method for capturing them. These traps use alcoholic attractants that mimic the characteristic odors of the insect’s natural environment, contributing to a sustainable reduction in its population6,7,10. In fact6, states that mass trapping is one of the most promising alternatives for controlling the CBB.

The use of ants as biological control agents deserves special attention, as they have been shown to be effective in reducing CBB populations by acting as natural predators in agroecological systems. This strategy is particularly attractive in terms of sustainability, as it enables environmentally responsible pest management with reduced reliance on insecticides, thereby promoting ecosystem conservation11. However12, highlights that in certain cases, biological control alone is insufficient, necessitating the implementation of complementary strategies, such as ethological control. Recent studies have explored the effects of harvesting strategies, additional food sources, and control interventions on pest–natural enemy dynamics, highlighting the complexity of agro-ecosystem interactions13–15.

However, the success of these practices depends on various factors, including climatic conditions, landscape diversity, crop characteristics, labor availability, economic resources, and farmers’ knowledge. In the current context, characterized by shifting climate patterns, fluctuations in coffee prices, and a growing demand for sustainable products, farmers face the challenge of making well-informed decisions regarding CBB management9.

Mathematical models are also essential tools for designing effective control strategies to mitigate the impact of the CBB on coffee plantations. Their utility lies in their ability to serve as substitutes for direct scientific experiments. In population ecology, mathematical models are fundamental for developing theoretical foundations that explain the factors affecting population size16. As noted in17, the formulation and study of models that describe the dispersal of the CBB and its temporal growth rate are essential for its effective management.

Numerous studies in the literature address the mathematical modeling of CBB population dynamics. However, when focusing specifically on studies that employ systems of ordinary differential equations to describe the interaction of the CBB with some form of biocontrol, a few key works can be identified. These include models incorporating the entomopathogenic fungus Beauveria bassiana18,19; parasitoid wasps such as Cephalonomia stephanoderis, Prorops nasuta, and Phymasthichus coffea20; and predatory ants5,12.

In this context, the study of piecewise-smooth dynamical systems modeling predator-prey interactions with discontinuities is particularly relevant. These models allow for the incorporation of control strategies that induce abrupt changes in system dynamics when specific population thresholds are exceeded. For example, in21,22, predator-prey systems have been explored in which the prey corresponds to a pest subject to predation by a natural enemy while also being affected by chemical control measures. Thus, and in line with sustainability principles, this study examines a mathematical model based on a piecewise-smooth dynamical system that describes the population dynamics of the CBB when subjected to predation by an unspecified species of ants, while also being affected by ethological control strategies. This approach enables the description of the complex interaction between different control strategies and their effects on the evolution of CBB populations, yielding results of interest in population ecology and providing valuable insights for the coffee industry. Additionally, from a mathematical perspective, the model exhibits rich dynamical behaviors characterized by codimension-one and codimension-two bifurcations, whose analysis is carried out through a rigorous bifurcation diagram.

Scientific literature has documented numerous studies on bifurcation analysis in piecewise-smooth dynamical systems modeling predator-prey dynamics. Some of these studies do not consider any form of control, such as23,24, while others incorporate control measures in a generalized manner, as in22. Additionally, there are investigations addressing chemical control measures, such as21,25–27. In this context, the present study also conducts a bifurcation analysis in a piecewise-smooth dynamical system modeling predator-prey dynamics but stands out due to three key elements. First, the agroecological context is specific, as the prey and predators correspond to the CBB and an unspecified species of ants, respectively. Second, in accordance with sustainability principles, the control mechanism considered is ethological. Finally, special emphasis is placed on characterizing codimension-two bifurcation points in the analysis, which emerge from the intersection of bifurcation curves in the parameter space. This space is defined in the first quadrant of a coordinate plane, where the axes correspond to two key model parameters: the percentage of the CBB carrying capacity that must be exceeded to activate ethological control and the average daily capture rate of CBB individuals. The objective of this study is to demonstrate, through the modeling of piecewise-smooth dynamical systems (used to capture abrupt changes in dynamics) the benefits of the combined implementation of biological and ethological control strategies on the CBB. Additionally, the mathematical results, particularly codimension-one and codimension-two bifurcations, are interpreted in the context of population ecology, providing a deeper understanding of the effects of integrated control on pest dynamics.

Despite the extensive literature on predator–prey models and pest control strategies, the role of threshold-based interventions within a piecewise-smooth dynamical framework remains insufficiently explored. In particular, the interaction between biological control and decision-driven ethological control introduces discontinuities that fundamentally alter system dynamics. In this work, we address this gap by formulating and analyzing a piecewise-smooth model that captures such threshold-driven mechanisms. Our analysis focuses on the emergence of discontinuity-induced phenomena, including sliding motion and codimension-one and codimension-two bifurcations, and their implications for pest management. To the best of our knowledge, the interaction between threshold-based ethological control and piecewise-smooth dynamics has not been systematically analyzed in this context

Paper organization. In Section 2, the mathematical model is formulated as a piecewise-smooth dynamical system, specifically a system of ordinary differential equations, describing the interaction between the CBB and predatory ants, along with the implementation of ethological control. The stability of the equilibrium points of the associated smooth systems is analyzed, and a Filippov analysis is conducted to characterize the key points in the sliding zone, particularly pseudo-equilibria, tangent points, and boundary equilibria. Finally, in Section 4, the biological implications of the results are discussed, as well as potential applications of the model in integrated pest management strategies for the CBB. Possible model extensions and modifications that could lead to future research directions are also proposed.

Model formulation and analysis

In this section, we develop a mathematical model describing the population dynamics of the coffee berry borer (CBB) under the combined effects of biological control, exerted by natural enemies, and ethological control, implemented through trapping mechanisms.

The modeling approach is constructed progressively. We begin with a baseline predator–prey system that captures the interaction between the pest and its natural enemies. Then, we incorporate an additional control mechanism based on trapping, whose activation depends on a threshold condition. This leads naturally to a piecewise-smooth dynamical system, in which the governing equations change depending on the state of the system.

From an ecological and management perspective, such switching behavior reflects decision-making protocols in pest control: intervention is typically activated only when pest density exceeds a predefined threshold. Therefore, the mathematical structure of the model captures not only biological interactions but also management strategies, providing a more realistic description of pest dynamics in agro-ecosystems.

The resulting system exhibits non-smooth dynamics, including sliding motion along the switching surface and the emergence of discontinuity-induced bifurcations, which constitute the central analytical contribution of this work. These features are not present in classical smooth models and play a key role in shaping the qualitative behavior of the system.

We now proceed with the construction and analysis of the model.

Model with capture

One of the main conclusions mentioned in5 regarding the analysis of system (1) is that when the maximum per capita consumption rate of the predator, α, is low, specifically , the action of ants alone is insufficient to control the pest. This suggests the need to incorporate an additional control strategy to complement biological control by ants, such as ethological control.

Given this, the CBB is now considered to be affected not only by predation but also by the implementation of an ethological control strategy based on handcrafted traps with alcoholic attractants, as extensively documented in7,32. The parameter h represents an average daily capture rate of CBB individuals and is assumed to be constant. This assumption provides a simplified description of the trapping process, capturing its aggregate effect under stable operational conditions. In practice, however, capture efficiency may depend on factors such as pest density, trap density, and environmental variability. Incorporating such dependencies would lead to a more complex model and is left as a possible extension for future work. It is assumed that ethological control is applied if the CBB population exceeds a certain percentage .The parameter represents the proportion of the pest carrying capacity that must be exceeded to activate ethological control. From an ecological and management perspective, this parameter can be interpreted as a decision threshold, analogous to action thresholds used in integrated pest management. In practice, θ may be determined based on field monitoring data, empirical guidelines, or economic criteria defining acceptable pest levels. Thus ethological control is applied when the condition B>θ q is met. The introduction of this additional control mechanism modifies the structure of the system and gives rise to new equilibrium configurations. Piecewise-smooth dynamical systems

The introduction of a threshold-based switching mechanism constitutes the key modeling innovation of this work. The piecewise-smooth formulation arises from the incorporation of a threshold-based control mechanism into the population dynamics. In this framework, the activation of ethological control is governed by a decision rule based on pest density, leading to a discontinuity in the vector field. Such threshold-driven formulations are consistent with approaches used in ecological modeling to represent management interventions that are applied only when population levels exceed predefined limits. In particular, the threshold B = θ q can be interpreted as an action threshold in integrated pest management, reflecting practical decision-making criteria derived from monitoring and control protocols.

A piecewise-smooth dynamical system is now constructed from systems (1) and (3). To this end, it is assumed that the population dynamics between the CBB B and the ants R is governed by system (1) when B<θ q, and by system (3) when B>θ q. From an ecological and management perspective, the switching condition B = θ q represents a threshold-based decision rule for the implementation of ethological control. When the pest population remains below this threshold, only biological control is active. Once the threshold is exceeded, additional control measures are deployed, leading to a change in the system dynamics.

Bifurcation analysis and numerical results

In this section, we analyze the global bifurcation structure of the system in the (θ ,h) parameter space, where θ represents the activation threshold for ethological control and h denotes the capture rate. The interaction between these two parameters determines the qualitative behavior of the system and reveals the mechanisms through which control strategies influence pest dynamics.

From a mathematical perspective, the model exhibits a rich bifurcation structure induced by the piecewise-smooth nature of the system. In particular, the switching mechanism gives rise to discontinuity-induced bifurcations, including both codimension-one and codimension-two phenomena, which organize the transitions between qualitatively different dynamical regimes.

From an ecological and management viewpoint, this bifurcation structure provides a systematic framework to understand how variations in intervention intensity (h) and activation thresholds (θ) affect pest suppression, persistence, or extinction. In this sense, the parameter space can be interpreted as a decision landscape, where different regions correspond to distinct management outcomes.

We begin by identifying the bifurcation curves that partition the parameter space and define the different dynamical regimes of the system.

Discussion and conclusions

This section provides an integrated interpretation of the results, linking the bifurcation structure of the model with the ecological behavior of the system and the implications for pest management strategies. We emphasize how the different dynamical regimes identified in the (θ ,h) parameter space translate into distinct population outcomes.

Population dynamics and ecological interpretation

The bifurcation structure in the (θ ,h) parameter space reveals a wide variety of dynamical regimes, each associated with a specific ecological scenario. These regimes can be understood in terms of the interaction between pest growth, biological control, and threshold-based intervention.

In regions where sliding motion occurs, the system evolves along the switching surface, meaning that the pest population remains close to the intervention threshold. Ecologically, this corresponds to a regime of sustained control, where trapping and natural enemies act together to regulate the pest density. This situation reflects an effective management strategy in which the pest population is neither eradicated nor allowed to grow freely, but is instead maintained at controlled levels.

In contrast, in regions where sliding motion is absent, the system typically evolves toward equilibria determined by the underlying subsystems. Depending on the parameter values, this may lead to persistence of the pest population, indicating that control is either insufficient or activated too late. These regimes highlight the limitations of relying solely on biological control or on poorly calibrated trapping strategies.

Another important feature is the role of boundary equilibria, which arise from the interaction between the switching mechanism and the system dynamics. These equilibria represent critical transitions between regimes and may correspond to situations in which control measures are just sufficient to alter the qualitative behavior of the system. Their presence indicates that small parameter variations can shift the system from effective control to pest persistence.

The analysis also shows that the position of the switching surface relative to the equilibria of the subsystems is crucial. As θ increases, the activation of control is delayed, which may cause relevant equilibria to become virtual and thus ecologically unobservable. This reflects situations in which intervention occurs too late to prevent pest proliferation.

Overall, the interplay between the activation threshold θ and the capture rate h determines whether the system evolves toward suppression, persistence, or regulated coexistence. This highlights the importance of coordinating the timing and intensity of control strategies in order to achieve effective pest management.

Synthesis and implications

The results of this study show that the incorporation of threshold-based decision mechanisms into population models leads to qualitatively new behaviors that cannot be captured by classical smooth systems. The piecewise-smooth structure of the model gives rise to discontinuity-induced phenomena, including sliding motion and higher-order bifurcations, which organize the global dynamics.

In particular, the identification of codimension-two bifurcation points reveals critical parameter configurations at which different dynamical regimes intersect. These points mark transitions where small changes in control parameters may lead to significant shifts in system behavior, underscoring the sensitivity of pest management outcomes to intervention design.

From an applied perspective, the model provides a conceptual framework for understanding how control strategies interact and how their effectiveness depends on both timing and intensity. Rather than increasing control effort alone, the results indicate that appropriate calibration of the activation threshold and trapping rate is essential.

More broadly, this work highlights the relevance of non-smooth dynamical systems in modeling ecological processes involving decision-based interventions. Future research may extend this framework by incorporating additional complexities such as state-dependent control, spatial effects, or stochastic variability, as well as by integrating empirical data for model validation.
