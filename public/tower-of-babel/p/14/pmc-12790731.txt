Crosstalk via ICAM-1 enhances supportive phenotype of stellate cells and drives hepatocyte proliferation in iPSC-derived hepatic organoids
Authors: Tomohiro Mochida, Masato Miyoshi, Sei Kakinuma, Taro Shimizu, Jun Tsuchiya, Keiya Watakabe, Kento Inada, Shun Kaneko, Fukiko Kawai-Kitahata, Miyako Murakawa, Sayuri Nitta, Mina Nakagawa, Mamoru Watanabe, Yasuhiro Asahina, Ryuichi Okamoto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe interaction between hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and hepatocytes contributes to HSC activation and liver regeneration; however, the mechanisms in humans remain unclear, particularly the significance of their direct contact and the role of cell adhesion molecules. In this study, we established a novel contact co-culture organoids using induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived hepatic stellate-like cells (iPS-HSCs) and hepatocyte-like cells (iPS-Heps), termed iPSC-derived hepatocyte-stellate cell surrounding organoids (iHSOs). The iHSOs exhibit a unique morphology with iPS-HSCs surrounding central iPS-Heps. The iHSO enabled the identification of ICAM-1-interleukin-1β (IL-1β)-mediated iPS-Hep proliferation supported by iPS-HSCs, which displayed a quiescent and cytokine-rich phenotype, whereas this proliferative support was not observed in primary liver cell-based co-culture organoids. Furthermore, iHSOs treated with acetaminophen allowed for the modeling of HSC activation induced by hepatocyte injury, demonstrating their application potential. Our study presents a valuable platform for studying the HSC behavior and complex interactions between HSCs and hepatocytes in humans.

Introduction

Chronic liver disease primarily results from persistent liver damage associated with factors such as viral hepatitis (hepatitis B and C), alcohol-associated liver disease, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, and immune-mediated liver disease (De Siervi et al., 2023). This damage initiates a cascade of responses leading to liver fibrosis, characterized by the excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) during tissue repair. Continued ECM deposition progress to cirrhosis, an irreversible stage associated with severe complications and liver failure. The global prevalence of cirrhosis is rising, contributing to approximately 2.4% of worldwide deaths (Huang et al., 2023). Currently, liver transplantation remains the only definitive treatment for advanced cirrhosis, highlighting the urgent need for effective antifibrotic therapies to halt or reverse fibrosis (Tapper and Parikh, 2023).

The primary source of ECM in liver fibrosis is activated hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), a type of non-parenchymal liver cell. In the healthy liver, HSCs remain quiescent, storing vitamin A within their lipid droplets (Akkız et al., 2024). However, upon stimulation, such as by damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and inflammasomes released from injured hepatocytes, or growth factors such as transforming growth factor β and platelet-derived growth factor from liver sinusoidal endothelial cells (LSECs) and macrophages, HSCs become activated and undergo transdifferentiation into myofibroblasts, which actively produce ECM (Akkız et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2021). This transformation of HSCs plays a critical role in driving the progression of liver fibrosis. Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) have revealed that HSCs exhibit a variety of phenotypes that extend past the classical quiescent and activated classifications, including subpopulations rich in chemokines and inflammatory cytokines, highlighting the complexity of HSC behavior in fibrosis (Cheng et al., 2023; Filliol et al., 2022; He et al., 2023; Merens et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2017).

HSCs interact closely with hepatocytes, influencing liver homeostasis and repair processes. While HSCs can promote hepatocyte proliferation and liver regeneration, hepatocytes may play a role in HSC activation and apoptosis, suggesting a bidirectional relationship (Barry et al., 2020; Kitto and Henderson, 2021; Oakley et al., 2003). HSCs and hepatocytes communicate through both indirect signals and direct contact (Sato et al., 2003; Urushima et al., 2021; Wake, 2006); however, the molecular mechanisms and functional significance of these interactions—particularly those mediated by cell-adhesion molecules—remain to be elucidated. Most knowledge of HSC-hepatocyte interactions comes from animal models, such as partial hepatectomy and chemically induced liver injury models in mice, which have provided invaluable insights (Balabaud, 2004). However, these models have limitations in replicating human liver physiology because of species difference, and there is a need for a system that can be validated using human cells.

Our group and others have reported the human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived cells including hepatic stellate-like cells (iPS-HSCs) can be used as a promising tool for the study of liver diseases (Coll et al., 2018; Koui et al., 2017; Miyoshi et al., 2019; Lai et al., 2022). The stem cell-derived HSCs exhibit key HSC functions, such as vitamin A storage and the capacity for activation and ECM production upon stimulation. These HSCs are promising alternative resource to primary HSCs, which must be isolated from human donor liver. However, studies on iPS-HSCs have largely focused on the traditional quiescent and activated states, leaving it unclear whether these cells can recapitulate the diversity of HSC phenotypes observed in vivo. Thus, there is a need to investigate whether human iPS-HSCs can capture the full range of HSC populations.

In this study, to reproduce physiological liver conditions, we developed a direct-contact, co-culture 3D organoid model using human iPS-HSCs and human iPS-derived hepatocyte-like cells (iPS-Heps), which we named human iPSC-derived hepatocyte-stellate cell surrounding organoids (iHSOs). Our data revealed the unique cytokine and chemokine-rich phenotype of iPS-HSCs within iHSOs as compared with 3D monocultures of iPS-HSCs and facilitation hepatocyte proliferation by cell-to-cell interaction. This approach provides a valuable platform for studying the HSC behavior and complex interactions between HSCs and hepatocytes in human, with implications for antifibrotic drug development.

Upon passaging and culturing iPS-HSCs in a 2D monolayer (Figure 1D), we observed a significant decrease in NGFR expression, a marker of quiescent HSCs. Expression of additional quiescence-associated genes, including CYGB and lecithin retinol acyltransferase (LRAT), trended downward (Figure 1E). The expression and production of alpha-smooth muscle actin (ACTA2) and collagen 1A1 (COL1A1) (HSC activation markers) were increased in 2D iPS-HSCs at day 10 after passage (Figures 1E and 1F). These findings indicated that continued 2D culture of iPS-HSCs induces spontaneous in vitro activation and a loss of quiescent HSC characteristics.

To prevent spontaneous in vitro activation of iPS-HSC, we utilized a 3D culture system, allowing iPS-HSCs to form spheroids (Figure 1D). In the iPS-HSC spheroids, NGFR expression was maintained, and the expression of CYGB and LRAT increased (Figure 1E). Although COL1A1 expression remained stable, ACTA2 expression decreased (Figure 1E). In 2D culture, immunostaining and their quantification revealed significant increase of α-SMA and type I collagen (COL1) (Figure 1F). In contrast to 2D culture, both α-SMA and COL1 production in 3D spheroids did not exhibit a time-dependent increase (Figure 1G). Vitamin A-positive cells were also increased in 3D spheroids compared to 2D culture (Figure 1H). Thus, culturing iPS-HSCs in a 3D environment partially induces some aspects of quiescence while avoiding activation phenotype by in vitro activation.

Establishment of new organoids using both iPS-HSCs and iPSC-derived hepatocyte-like cells

To suppress the activated characteristics in 3D culture, we developed a new 3D co-culture system of iPS-HSCs in contact with iPS-Heps (Figure S1A). Human iPS-Heps were differentiated from iPSCs as previously described (Ang et al., 2018) and were confirmed to highly express hepatocyte markers (hepatocyte nuclear factor 4 alpha [HNF4A], a-fetoprotein [AFP], albumin [ALB], and cytochrome P450 family 3 subfamily A member 4 [CYP3A4]) and exhibited characteristics of ALB-positive hepatocytes (Figures S1B and S1C). Using iPSCs labeled with different fluorescent markers (GFP and tdTomato), we induced iPS-HSCs and iPS-Heps and co-cultured them in a 3D system (Figure 2A). After 3 days in spheroid formation medium, organoid formation was observed, and upon switching to co-culture medium (without growth factors), the organoids continued to grow (Figures 2B and 2C). Immunostaining confirmed that HNF4α-positive iPS-Heps were located internally, while DESMIN-positive iPS-HSCs, surrounded the iPS-Hep sphere (Figure 2D). iHSOs consistently exhibited this morphology of iPS-HSC surrounding the outer layer, even when different iPS cell lines were used, demonstrating the clear reproducibility of the organoid culture system (Figures 2D, S1D, and S1E). Co-culture of primary human hepatocytes (PHHs) and primary HSCs yielded organoids with a similar structure (Figure S1F), supporting the physiological relevance of the iHSO system. Electron microscopic analysis revealed that stellate cells surrounded the surface of the spherical organoid and had multiple fine processes that made contact with internal hepatocytes and HSCs (Figure 2E). Hepatocytes contained mitochondria and glycogen granules and exhibited microvilli, which contact with HSCs (Figure 2E). Vitamin A-storing iPS-HSCs were observed on the outer layer after iHSOs were treated with retinol, and electron microscopy revealed significant lipid droplet accumulation in the cytoplasm of iPS-HSCs within iHSOs (Figure 2F). These findings indicate that iHSOs are a culture system where stellate cells, capable of storing vitamin A, interact with hepatocytes.

iPS-HSCs promote the proliferation of iPS-Heps within iHSOs

Upon investigating the effects of Hep-HSC co-culture within the iHSO system, quantification of iPS-Hep area via fluorescent labeling (Figure 3A) revealed no significant size difference between iHSOs and iPS-Hep spheroids at early stages. However, by day 7, iHSOs exhibited a significantly larger hepatocyte region—both in diameter and area—compared to mono-organoids composed solely of iPS-Heps (Figure 3B). We further evaluated iHSOs generated by various combination of iPSC-lines, where the hepatocyte area was quantified via fluorescent labeling. Consistently, iHSOs demonstrated a larger hepatocyte area than Hep-spheroids, confirming robust reproducibility of the system (Figures 3C and 3D). When the number of HSCs was reduced to one-tenth, the hepatocyte area became markedly smaller. These findings suggest that hepatocyte proliferation depends on a threshold level of HSCs, rather than showing a linear relationship with HSC number (Figures S1G and S1H). To analyze the iPS-Hep proliferation within iHSOs and mono-spheroids, EdU incorporation was assessed. The EdU assay demonstrated that EdU-positive cells in the iPS-Heps within iHSOs was significantly increased compared with those in iPS-Hep mono-spheroids (Figure 3E). Induction of quiescent and early activation states in iPS-HSCs by hepatocyte-stellate cell interaction

This design allowed us to distinguish the effects of 3D culture from those of cell-cell interactions within iHSOs. Principal-component analysis (PCA) clearly showed that the samples clustered into three groups: iPSCs, iPS-Heps, and iPS-HSCs (Figure 4B). Transcriptomic profiles of iPS-HSCs significantly varied with culture context, whereas iPS-Heps displayed only modest shifts. qPCR analysis revealed that co-culture did not promote further maturation of iPS-Heps; rather, hepatic lineage markers were modestly down-regulated (Figure S2C). These observations align with the highly proliferative—and thus regenerative—state of iPS-Heps within iHSOs.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) demonstrated that iPS-HSCs within iHSOs were significantly enriched for canonical HSC signatures (Figure 4C). To explore more detailed transcriptional changes, we analyzed HSC subpopulations recently identified by scRNA-seq analyses. According to Yang et al. (2017), iHSO-derived iPS-HSCs expressed markers of quiescent and stage-1 activated states, whereas expression of stage-2 and stage-3 markers remained low (Figure S2D). Similarly, gene set variation analysis (GSVA) indicated that pathways enriched in qHSC and stage-1 aHSCs were more strongly enriched in iPS-HSCs within iHSOs (Figure S2D). Using the cyHSC and myHSC signatures defined by Filliol et al. (2022), representative cyHSC genes were upregulated and myHSC genes were suppressed in iHSO-derived HSCs (Figure S2E). GSEA statistically confirmed significant enrichment of cyHSC signature, contrast to no enrichment of myHSC signature (Figure 4C). Finally, applying the quiescent, initiatory, and activated gene sets of Merens et al. (2025), iHSO-derived iPS-HSCs predominantly aligned with the initiatory subtype while retaining a residual quiescent signature (Figures 4C and S2F).

Validation by qPCR analysis demonstrated that the qHSC marker genes NGFR, LRAT, CYGB, growth factor (hepatocyte growth factor [HGF]), and chemokines (C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 [CXCL1] and C-C motif chemokine ligand 2 [CCL2]) were upregulated in HSCs within iHSOs. In contrast, no significant changes were detected in the expression of ACTA2 and tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases 1 (TIMP1), while COL1A1 exhibited a slight decrease in expression (Figures 4D and S3A). In 3D co-culture with primary human HSCs (pHSCs) and iPS-Heps, pHSCs also showed upregulation of NGFR, LRAT, CYGB, HGF, and CCL2, although the response of activated-HSC signature genes diverged from that observed in iPS-HSCs (Figure S3B). FACS analysis revealed a significant increase in NGFR-positive cells among iPS-HSCs within iHSOs, suggesting enhanced quiescence of iPS-HSCs within iHSOs (Figure 4E). In summary, iPS-HSCs within iHSOs exhibited the transcriptional profiles characteristic of quiescence and initial activation states.

Unique characteristics of iPS-HSCs within iHSOs

In addition to our bulk RNA-seq data, we integrated RNA-seq data obtained from public databases, including data from pluripotent stem cell-derived HSCs, PHHs, LSEC, and primary HSCs. PCA revealed that the stem cell-derived HSCs, including our iPS-HSCs, constituted a distinct cluster, clearly separate from others (Figure 4F). Although different HSC differentiation protocols caused some variation in gene expression profiles, these variations were minor compared to the differences between stem cell-derived HSCs and primary HSCs (Figure S3C).

GSVA on our iPS-HSC samples, stem cell-derived HSCs, and primary HSC samples revealed distinctive features of our iPS-HSCs within iHSOs. Analysis of multiple gene sets and signatures associated with HSCs revealed that only our iPS-HSCs within iHSOs showed enrichment in quiescent HSC-related signatures and cyHSC signatures (Figure 4G). In contrast, many stem cell-derived HSCs showed low enrichment scores for quiescent HSC-related signatures, as well as limited enrichment for signatures associated with activated HSCs.

Lineage signatures along the stellate-cell developmental trajectory further confirmed that iPS-HSCs within iHSOs exhibit a stronger stellate cell identity compared to a mesothelial identity. These findings suggest incomplete differentiation of stem-cell-derived HSCs and highlighted the need for refined differentiation protocols (Figure S3D). These results demonstrate that our iHSO-induced HSCs has unique characteristics among stem cell-derived HSCs, resembling HSCs with non-activated phenotype; quiescent and cytokine/chemokine rich state.

ICAM-1 in HSCs mediates cellular interactions within the iHSOs

In the iHSO system, iPS-Heps and iPS-HSCs communicate via both direct contact-dependent and indirect contact-independent mechanisms. To assess the indirect effects, we added iHSO-conditioned medium to iPS-HSC mono-spheroids (Figure S4A); however, this treatment did not reproduce the quiescent and early activation HSC phenotypes observed in iHSOs (Figure S4B). These findings suggest that direct hepatocyte-stellate cell contact is essential for driving the phenotypic changes in iPS-HSCs.

Therefore, we examined cell-adhesion molecules to clarify their contribution to hepatocyte-stellate interactions in iHSOs. The role of ICAM-1 in iHSOs was investigated with both small-molecule inhibition and shRNA-mediated knockdown. Treatment with the ICAM-1 inhibitor A205804 led to a dose-dependent reduction in ICAM-1 expression, with near complete suppression at 40 μM (Figures 5D and S4E). The hepatocyte component of iHSOs was decreased by the ICAM-1 inhibition in a concentration-dependent manner. At 40 μM, both diameter and area of iPS-Heps were significantly decreased compared to the control (Figure 5E). The second inhibitor, A286982, also impaired hepatocyte growth within iHSOs (Figure S4F). Consistently, shRNA-mediated silencing efficiently decreased ICAM-1 expression in iPS-HSCs within iHSOs (Figures 5F and S4G) and produced iHSOs with smaller hepatocyte components, recapitulating the phenotype observed with the ICAM-1 inhibitors (Figure 5G). Collectively, these results indicate that ICAM-1 plays a hepatocyte-supportive role in the Hep-HSC interaction within iHSOs.

Gene expression analysis showed that APAP exposure led to a significant decrease in quiescent markers, including NGFR, LRAT, and CYGB (Figure 7F). Conversely, APAP treatment significantly upregulated ACTA2 and TIMP1, while COL1A1 remained unchanged. Immunostaining further confirmed a slight increase in α-SMA and COL1 production, and a marked upregulation of type IV collagen (COL4) deposition (Figures 7G, 7H, and S6A). Discussion

In this study, we established a co-culture model termed iHSOs, composed of human iPS-HSCs and iPS-Heps. Within iHSOs, iPS-Heps displayed increased proliferation, while iPS-HSCs showed increased expression of genes associated with quiescent and early-activated HSCs. This HSC expression profile aligns with the characteristics of “cyHSCs” or “initiatory HSCs,” a recently identified HSC subgroup that differs from classical collagen-producing activated stellate cells (Filliol et al., 2022; Merens et al., 2025). Although these subtypes have been primarily characterized in murine liver injury models, their relevance to human HSCs remains to be fully validated. Among them, cyHSCs have been reported to exert hepatocyte-supportive effects. Furthermore, HSCs support hepatocyte regeneration and control their zonation via Rspo3 (Sugimoto et al., 2025). These hepato-supportive observations are consistent with the enhanced hepatocyte proliferation observed in iHSOs through Hep-HSC interactions. This study provides the first evidence that iPS-HSCs in iHSOs adopt a cyHSC/initiatory phenotype, distinct from earlier stem-cell-derived HSC models, and recapitulates the phenotypic diversity of HSC in vivo. Although the mechanisms underlying Hep-HSC interactions that induce quiescent or initiatory HSC states in iHSOs remain unclear, as do the stability of these states and their hepatocyte-proliferative effects during long-term culture, our model successfully reflects the hepatic regenerative phase in vivo. These results underscore the physiological relevance of iHSOs and their potential utility in studying liver regeneration and disease mechanisms.

Cell-cell interaction within iHSOs depending on ICAM-1-IL-1β axis promoted iPS-Hep proliferation (Figures 5 and 6). Co-culture systems involving HSCs and hepatocytes including cell lines, primary cells, and iPS-derived cells have been reported (Coll et al., 2018; Mannaerts et al., 2020; Pingitore et al., 2019). Compared to these systems, our iHSO system is characterized by a distinct structure with a central hepatocyte core surrounded by HSCs. Replicating in vivo features such as mixed cellular architecture and hepatocyte zonation remains a challenge in organoid development. On the other hand, the structural arrangement enables a direct contact between two cell types across the entire sphere and a morphological assessment, such as quantifying the hepatocyte region. Consistent with this design, we observed enhanced hepatocyte proliferation in iHSOs compared to iPS-Hep mono-spheroids, indicating that Hep-HSC interactions play a supportive role in hepatocyte proliferation. Furthermore, the iHSO culture system successfully modeled APAP-induced liver injury (Figure 7). Thus, the iHSO culture system represented a new organoid platform with direct cell-to-cell interactions, which partly mimics the physiological microenvironments of HSCs in human. Our data also demonstrated that the iHSO culture system provides a new tool for investigating not only human HSC activation but also mechanisms of cell-cell crosstalk between hepatocytes and HSCs.

In our study, we established a novel contact co-culture system featuring cell-to-cell interactions between HSCs and hepatocytes. However, several limitations should be noted. First, both cell types are derived from iPSCs. iPSC-derived differentiated cells resemble fetal cells and are immature as compared with primary cells. As shown in Figure 4, although iPS-HSCs within iHSOs have enriched HSC-related gene signatures compared to other pluripotent stem cell-derived HSCs, they still exhibit expression profiles different from those of primary HSCs (pHSCs). Using primary human hepatocytes (PHHs) and pHSCs, we confirmed that organoids resembling iHSOs could form under the same conditions (Figure S1F), supporting the system’s robustness. However, iPS-derived HSCs failed to promote PHH expansion (Figures S7A and S7B), differ from the increased proliferation of iPS-Heps in iHSOs. This observation may suggest a potential developmental mismatch between the fetal-like iPS-HSCs and the adult PHHs, implying the importance of developmental stage synchronization for effective cell-cell interactions. Moreover, while pHSCs co-cultured with iPS-Heps exhibited quiescent and early-activation phenotypes, they also upregulated classical activation markers (Figure S3B). This may be attributed both to the difference in maturation status between iPS-HSCs and pHSCs, and to the characteristics of the pHSCs used (HHSteCs), which had been expanded in vitro after isolation and may have partially acquired an activated phenotype. These findings highlight key differences between iPSC-derived and adult-cell-based organoids, underscoring the unique characteristics of iHSOs. Second, iHSOs are composed of only these two cell types, because we aimed to build a model for analyze interactions between HSCs and hepatocytes. The absence of other liver nonparenchymal cells, such as Kupffer cells and LSECs, means that the conditions do not fully replicate the in vivo environment. For genes such as ICAM-1, which are expressed in multiple liver cell types, caution is needed when interpreting iHSO data. For example, ICAM-1 expression increases in hepatocytes during liver injury in vivo, but remains unchanged in APAP-treated iHSOs (Figures S7C and S7D). Third, external factors present in the iHSO culture medium may confound the analysis of mechanistically relevant pathways, such as the Rspo3 function of HSCs in iHSOs. Further optimization of the culture conditions is needed (Sugimoto et al., 2025). Despite these limitations in cellular diversity and maturation, the iHSO platform allows dual genetic manipulation of HSCs and hepatocytes, offering a synthetic model for liver disease research. This approach may facilitate the discovery of therapeutic targets for various liver conditions.

In conclusion, our study developed a novel contact co-culture model, iHSOs, which enables the analysis of interactions between HSCs and hepatocytes. We demonstrated that iPS-Hep proliferation in iHSOs is promoted by ICAM-1-IL-1β stimulation derived from iPS-HSCs, suggesting that ICAM-1 is a key adhesion molecule in HSC-hepatocyte interactions. The iHSO model could be applicable to development of antifibrotic and liver regenerative therapies that target HSC-hepatocyte interactions.

Methods

Human iPSC lines

Human iPS cell lines (RIKEN2F [Fujioka et al., 2010], 2F-GFP [Takahashi et al., 2024], PB001 [Masaki et al., 2015], and ChiPSC18) were cultured and maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere, following the protocol for the Cellartis DEF-CS 500 Culture System (Takara Bio). We regularly tested all iPSCs to ensure their pluripotency by FACS using SSEA-4 and TRA1-60 and they were free from infection, including mycoplasma contamination. We confirmed that differentiation potential into hepatocyte-like cells and hepatic stellate-like cells HSCs did not vary among 2F-GFP, PB001, and ChiPSC18. A detailed information of iPSC lines is described in the supplemental information.

Differentiation of 2D human iPS-HSCs

iPSCs were differentiated into HSCs according to the protocol described by Miyoshi et al. (2019). After differentiation, iPS-HSCs were induced and subsequently passaged onto either Matrigel-coated plates or transferred to EZSPHERE SP microplate 24-well (IWAKI Co., Ltd. [AGC], 4820-900SP) for spheroid culture. Both 2D monolayer and spheroid cultures were maintained in spheroid formation medium (SM) and co-culture medium (CM). A detailed information of differentiation methods, SM, and CM is described in the supplementary information.

Differentiation of 2D human iPS-Heps

iPSCs were differentiated into hepatocytes using the previously reported protocol with some modification (Ang et al., 2018). iPSCs were seeded at a density of approximately 0.5×104 cells/cm2 in a 12-well plate coated with Matrigel, and differentiation was initiated the following day. Successful induction of iPSC-derived hepatocyte-like cells (iPS-Heps) was achieved by day 18. A detailed information of differentiation methods was described in supplementary information.

Human iHSO culture

Well-differentiated iPS-Heps and iPS-HSCs were dissociated by TrypLE Select treatment (Thermo Fischer Scientific, 12563-011) after washing with PBS. iHSOs were prepared by mixing iPS-Heps and iPS-HSCs at a density of 4.7 × 104 cells/well each and seeding them in an EZSPHERE SP microplate 24-well. Mono-culture spheroids were created by seeding iPS-Heps or iPS-HSCs alone at a density of 4.7 × 104 cell/well. During day 1–3 of co-culture, both iHSOs and mono-culture spheroids were cultured in (SM).

Differentiated cells were required to exhibit hepatocyte-like morphology with minimal heterogeneity. From days 4–10, the cells were cultured in CM. Half of the medium was replaced on days 4 and 7, and the medium change was done carefully to prevent organoid dissociation. The appearance of cystic organoids following spheroid formation was considered indicative of suboptimal hepatocyte differentiation, and such samples were excluded from downstream analyses. A detailed information is described in the supplementary information.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software (GraphPad). Standard error of the mean (SEM) and statistical significance were calculated using Student’s two-tailed t test or Mann-Whitney U test for comparisons between two groups, and one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test for comparisons among three or more groups. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Supplementary discussion, supplementary figures, their figure legends, and other information of materials and methods are shown in the supplementary information.
