Novel antibacterial peptide HfAMP against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Fei Lin, Weiwei Jin, Longbing Yang, Chunren Tian, Jin Zhang, Zhenglong Jiao, Jian Peng, Guo Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe urgent development of novel antibacterial agents to combat antibiotic-resistant Staphylococcus aureus is imperative. Antimicrobial peptides are considered promising alternatives to traditional antibiotics for addressing this pressing issue. This study investigates the antibacterial activities and potential mechanisms of the peptide HfAMP against methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA).MethodsThe antibacterial activity of HfAMP against MRSA was evaluated using the broth microdilution method, growth curve, and time-kill assay. Its potential mechanism of action was investigated through propidium iodide, laurdan, DiSC3(5), 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate, and ATP quantification assays. To assess the safety and stability of HfAMP, cytotoxicity, hemolysis, and antibacterial activity were examined under different conditions (i.e., temperatures, ions, serum, pH, and enzymes). Furthermore, a mouse skin infection model was employed to evaluate the in vivo efficacy of HfAMP.ResultsThe results demonstrate that HfAMP exhibits bacteriostatic and bactericidal activity against MRSA, with a minimum inhibitory concentration of 4 µg/mL. It remains stable under a range of pH, temperatures, and serum, although it is susceptible to degradation by pepsin. Mechanistic investigations show that HfAMP compromises bacterial membranes by interacting with membrane components and disrupting the proton motive force, leading to metabolic disturbances. In a mouse model of MRSA-induced skin infection, HfAMP significantly reduced bacterial burden and inflammation in the affected skin tissues.ConclusionCollectively, these findings indicate that HfAMP possesses potent antibacterial activity against MRSA by compromising bacterial viability and inhibiting virulence, highlighting its potential as a promising candidate for the treatment of MRSA skin-infection.Keywords: Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus, Antimicrobial peptide, Membrane mechanism, In vivo skin infection

Background

Staphylococcus aureus is a significant pathogen responsible for infectious deaths worldwide; these infections are associated with increased morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs [1]. S. aureus infections have the potential to escalate into a global epidemic, and there has been a concerning rise in the spread of methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) infections in both hospital and community settings [2, 3]. Furthermore, MRSA is listed among the priority drug-resistant bacteria, which urgently necessitates the discovery and development of novel antibiotic treatments [4]. According to a recent study, it is estimated that in 2019, S. aureus, including both MRSA and other resistant strains, resulted in approximately 10,600 deaths and incurred costs exceeding $1.7 billion annually in the United States [5]. Globally, it is responsible for around 1.1 million deaths [6].

Worryingly, the widespread resistance of S. aureus to traditional antibacterial agents, such as methicillin, vancomycin, aminoglycosides, and β-lactam antibiotics, has been observed. Over the past few decades, the prevalence of MRSA infections has been a significant threat to human health [7]. Vancomycin is considered a first-line treatment for MRSA [8]. Unfortunately, in 1997, heterogeneous vancomycin resistance was reported for the first time, and in 1998, vancomycin-resistant S. aureus (VRSA) was identified among clinical isolates [9]. Both MRSA and VRSA have been classified by the World Health Organization as globally prioritized drug-resistant bacteria. Consequently, there are limited therapeutic options available for the treatment of MRSA infections [10]. In this context, antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) have been extensively studied as potential alternative therapies for the prevention and treatment of MRSA infections due to their unique antibacterial mechanisms.

AMPs have indeed garnered significant attention as promising candidates for the development of novel antibacterial agents. They effectively combat various pathogenic factors and exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, including efficacy against Gram-positive bacteria, Gram-negative bacteria, fungi, and viruses. Many AMPs also demonstrate lethal effects on multidrug-resistant bacteria [11–15]. Due to their broad spectrum of antimicrobial activity, effectiveness against resistant bacteria, low likelihood of inducing bacterial resistance, and abundant sources, AMPs have garnered increasing attention and favor. In our previous study, we constructed a peptide prediction model and screened for an antimicrobial peptide from this model [16]. Through sequence comparison, the peptide was found to be 100% homologous to Haemophilus felis and was named HfAMP. Furthermore, in the preliminary antimicrobial spectrum screening assay, we found that HfAMP exhibited excellent antibacterial activity against MRSA. However, the in vitro antibacterial mechanism and in vivo efficacy of HfAMP against MRSA remain unclear. Therefore, further in-depth investigation is urgently needed.

In this study, we evaluated the in vivo therapeutic potential of HfAMP using a mouse model of skin infection induced by MRSA. Additionally, we elucidated the in vitro mechanism of HfAMP through experimental validation. Our findings demonstrated that HfAMP exerts its bactericidal effects by inducing membrane disruption and disrupting bacterial metabolic homeostasis. Importantly, HfAMP was shown to be highly effective in treating MRSA-induced skin infections.

Materials and methods

HfAMP synthesis

HfAMP (MKKIFRVIFKKILKRLIV) and fluorescein isothiocyanate labeled HfAMP (FITC-HfAMP) were synthesized using solid-phase methods at GL Biochem (Shanghai, China). The molecular weight was measured by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MS, LCMS-2020, SHIMADZU, Japan), and its purity (> 95%) was analyzed via reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC, P1600, AUNO, Beijing). Charge was examined using a zeta potential analyzer (NanoBrook 90Plus PALS, Brookhaven, USA).

Antibacterial activity tests

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of HfAMP was determined using the broth microdilution method [17]. The tested strains included Acinetobacter baumannii ATCC 19606, Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853, Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700603, S. aureus ATCC 25923, and MRSA GU-1. All bacteria strains were aerobically grown on Mueller-Hinton Broth (MHB, Hopebio, Qingdao) or Luria-Bertani (LB, Hopebio, Qingdao) agar plates at 37 ℃ without CO2 and controlled humidity. Overnight cultures were washed and resuspended in MHB. Subsequently, bacteria suspensions were adjusted using a nephelometer (WGZ-800, Xinrui, Shanghai) to a concentration of 1 × 106 CFU/mL. Then, 100 µL of bacterial suspension were inoculated with HfAMP (0–64 µg/mL) at 37 °C for 16–18 h. To determine the minimal bactericidal concentration (MBC), 10 µL aliquots from each well were plated on LB agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.

Growth Inhibition and time-kill kinetics of HfAMP

Bacteria (1 × 106 CFU/mL) were incubated with HfAMP (0–8 µg/mL) at 37 °C, and the OD630 value was measured at 0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, and 24 h using a microplate reader (BioTek SYNERGY-H, Agilent, USA). For time-kill kinetics, samples were collected at 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 12 h, serially diluted, and plated on LB agar. After 24 h of incubation, colonies were counted.

Physiological ions, serum, pH, temperature, and enzyme tolerance assays

The tolerance of HfAMP was assessed by measuring the MIC under physiological conditions, which included salt ions (150 mM NaCl, 4.5 mM KCl, 2 mM CaCl2, and 1 mM MgSO4), serum (5%, 10%, and 20% fetal bovine serum), pH values (1, 4, 7, and 10), temperature conditions (37 °C, 70 °C, and 100 °C), and enzymes (trypsin and pepsin) [18]. HfAMP was exposed to temperatures of 37 °C, 70 °C, and 100 °C for 1 h. It was also tested at pH values of 1, 4, 7, and 10 for 1 h. Additionally, HfAMP was treated with trypsin (1 mg/mL) and pepsin (1 mg/mL) for 1 h, and inactivated enzymes were treated at 60 °C for 30 min. Following these treatments, the MIC of the samples was determined using the microbroth dilution method.

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy

The secondary structure changes of HfAMP were evaluated in H2O, 30 mM SDS, and 50% TFE by using CD spectroscopy. The HfAMP solutions (50 µM) were scanned over the wavelength range of 190 to 250 nm at 25 °C using a MOS-405/AF-CD spectropolarimeter (Bio-logic, France).

Antibiofilm activity tests

The effect of HfAMP on biofilm by S. aureus ATCC 25923 and MRSA GU-1 was evaluated as previously described [19, 20]. Overnight cultures were resuspended in trypticase soy broth (TSB, Solarbio, Beijing). First, the effect of HfAMP on static biofilm attachment was examined. The bacteria (1 × 108 CFU/mL) and HfAMP were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Second, to assess the inhibitory effect of biofilm formation, the bacteria and HfAMP were co-incubated for 24 h. Finally, to evaluate the biofilm disruption effect of HfAMP, the bacteria were first incubated for 24 h, and the HfAMP was added, and incubation continued for an additional 24 h. Bacterial membrane interaction and viability assessment

First, the live/dead ratio of S. aureus ATCC 25923 was evaluated by using a viability/cytotoxicity assay kit [21]. Bacteria (1.0 × 108 CFU/mL) were incubated with HfAMP at 37 °C for 1 h and then dual-stained with SYTO 9 (5 µM, Thermofisher, USA) and propidium iodide (PI, 10 µM, Sigma, Germany) for 15 min. The live/dead ratios were quantified using laser confocal microscopy (Olympus, Japan). Next, morphological damage was assessed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Bacteria and HfAMP (8 µg/mL) were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The bacteria were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde, dehydrated, dried, and gold-sputtered before imaging with SEM (SU8100, Hitachi, Japan). The bacteria were fixed with 1% osmium acid, embedded in EMBed 812, sectioned, stained with 2% uranyl acetate, and observed by TEM (HT7800, Hitachi, Japan). Additionally, the localization and binding of HfAMP to bacteria were examined using FITC-labeled peptides [22, 23]. Bacteria were cultured with FITC-labeled HfAMP (4 µg/mL) at 37 °C for 5 minutes, followed by staining with FM 4–64 (Coolabe, Beijing) and 4’, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Solarbio, Beijing) for 10 min, and observed by laser confocal microscopy (Olympus, Japan).

Effect of phospholipids on the antibacterial activity of HfAMP

The effect of different types of phospholipids on the activity of HfAMP was assessed using a checkerboard microdilution assay [18, 24]. The phosphatidylcholine (PC, Aladdin, Shanghai), phosphatidylglycerol (PG, Aladdin, Shanghai), phosphatidylethanolamine (PE, Aladdin, Shanghai), cardiolipin (CL, Aladdin, Shanghai), and HfAMP were co-incubated with bacteria for 16–18 h. The MIC of HfAMP in the presence of different phospholipids was recorded.

Effect of HfAMP on bacteria membranes

The impact of HfAMP on the cell membranes of S. aureus ATCC 25923 and MRSA GU-1 was evaluated using fluorescence intensity assays. Inner membrane permeability was assessed using the fluorescent dye PI (5 µM). Bacteria (1 × 108 CFU/mL) were incubated with HfAMP (0–16 µg/mL) at 37 °C for 1 h. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek SYNERGY-H, Agilent, USA). Subsequently, membrane fluidity was evaluated using the fluorescent dye Laurdan (10 Μm, Sigma, USA). Generalized polarization (GP) was calculated using the formula: GP = (I435 - I490)/(I435 + I490). Membrane potential was assessed using the fluorescent probe DiSC3(5) (0.5 µM, Sigma, USA). Additionally, the proton motive force of HfAMP-treated bacteria was measured using BCECF-AM (1 µM, UElandy, Suzhou).

Effect of HfAMP on bacterial energy metabolism

To investigate the effects of HfAMP on bacterial energy metabolism, S. aureus ATCC 25923 and MRSA GU-1 were treated with HfAMP (0–16 µg/mL) for 1 h. Intracellular ATP levels were quantified using an Enhanced ATP Assay Kit (UElandy, Suzhou). Bacterial electron transport was assessed using iodonitrotetrazolium chloride (INT, 1 mM; Aladdin, Shanghai), with the OD490 being measured [25]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were determined using 2’,7’-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA, 10 µM, Yuanye, Shanghai) as the kit instructions [21]. The antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC, 6 mM, Beyotime, Shanghai) was used as a control. The impact of HfAMP on bacterial respiration was assessed using the dye resazurin (Solarbio, Beijing) [24]. Bacteria were incubated with 0.1 µg/mL resazurin, and fluorescence changes were continuously monitored for 1 h.

Hemolysis of human red blood cells (hRBCs)

The hemolytic rate of HfAMP was examined using hRBCs in line with a preceding study [26]. The hRBCs was provided by healthy volunteers, and the use of healthy human blood was approved by the IRB of Guizhou Medical University. One hundred microliters of various concentrations of HfAMP (0–128 µg/mL) and 4% hRBCs were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. While phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4, Solarbio, Beijing) was used as the negative control, and 1% Triton X-100 (Solarbio, Beijing) as a positive control for 100% hemolysis. Following the incubation period, the samples were centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min, and the OD570 absorbance value was measured by microplate reader (BioTek SYNERGY-H, Agilent, USA).

Mammalian cell cytotoxicity assays

Hep G2 cells (ATCC number: HB-8065) and A549 cells (ATCC number: CCL-1) were utilized to assess the cytotoxicity of HfAMP on mammalian cells [20]. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA) at 37 °C in 5% CO2. One hundred microliters of cell suspension (2 × 105 cells/mL) were incubated with HfAMP (0–128 µg/mL) at 37 °C for 24 h. Following, 10 µL of CCK8 solution was added, and incubation was continued for 1 h. Absorbance values were measured at OD450 (BioTek SYNERGY-H, Agilent, USA), and the percentage of cell survival was calculated.

In vivo efficiency

Female BALB/C mice (6–8 weeks, 18–20 g) were purchased from HFK Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All animal studies were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Guizhou Medical University (Approval No: 2302013). A total of 25 mice were divided into 5 biologically independent animals per group [27]. The dorsal skin of the mice was depilated, and 100 µL of MRSA GU-1 (1 × 108 CFU/mL) was injected subcutaneously into the right and left buttock regions to induce abscess formation. HfAMP (2.5 or 5 mg/kg), vancomycin (2.5 mg/kg), and 0.9% NaCl were administered via subcutaneous injection (100 µL per mice) near the abscesses 2 h after infection and continued daily for 7 days. Twenty-four hours after the final dose, the mice were exposed to isoflurane anesthesia in a gas-tight chamber and stayed in the chamber and were then sacrificed were by cervical dislocation. Infected skin tissues were collected, homogenized, plated on LB agar plates for CFU enumeration (37 °C, 24 h), and processed for H&E staining to assess histopathological changes. The severity of the skin inflammatory response was evaluated using a semi-quantitative scoring system [28].

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was repeated three times, and the data are shown as mean ± SD. The software GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 (San Diego, CA, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Multi-group analyses were performed using a one-way ANOVA.

Results

Antibacterial activity and stability

The chemical structure, three-dimensional structure, α-helix wheel diagrams, and predicted physiological properties of HfAMP exhibited cationic, hydrophobic, and α-helical characteristics, as illustrated in Fig. 1. CD spectroscopy results showed negative bands at 222 and 208 nm and a positive band at 193 nm in water, 30 mM SDS, and 50% TFE (Fig. 1E), indicating that HfAMP has an α-helical structure. In addition, the zeta potential of HfAMP is 2.08 ± 0.13 mV. These findings suggest that HfAMP can interact with the bacterial membrane through electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions.

The antibacterial efficacy of HfAMP was determined by its MIC against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. HfAMP exhibited antibacterial activity against the test bacterial strains, with MICs ranging from 8 to 32 µg/mL (Table 1). In further studies involving the Gram-positive bacteria S. aureus ATCC 25923 and MRSA GU-1, HfAMP demonstrated excellent antibacterial activity, with MICs and MBCs of 4 and 8 µg/mL, respectively. At sub-MIC levels (2 µg/mL), HfAMP inhibited the growth of S. aureus ATCC 25923 and MRSA GU-1 (Fig. 2A-B). In addition, HfAMP can significantly lead to bacteria death in a time- and concentration-dependent manner; 8 µg/mL of HfAMP completely eliminated S. aureus ATCC 25923 within 0.25 h and MRSA GU-1 within 0.5 h (Fig. 2C-D). However, the antibacterial activity of HfAMP was not as potent as that of vancomycin; 1 µg/mL of vancomycin completely eliminated S. aureus ATCC 25923 and MRSA GU-1 at 8 h.

Antimicrobial resistance in S. aureus is often exacerbated by biofilm formation [29]. The biofilm life cycle comprises four distinct phases: attachment, development, maturation, and dispersal [30]. In this study, we evaluated the efficacy of HfAMP against biofilms formed by S. aureus ATCC 25923 and MRSA GU-1 using XTT methods. The results indicated that 16 µg/mL of HfAMP inhibited the attachment of S. aureus ATCC 25923 and MRSA GU-1 to the surface (Fig. 2E-F), 64 µg/mL of HfAMP effectively inhibited biofilm formation (Fig. 2G-H), and 128 µg/mL of HfAMP destroyed the mature biofilm (Fig. 2I-J).

Functions of membranes and energy metabolism

AMPs most commonly act on the membrane, leading to cell lysis due to their net positive charge. Therefore, we evaluated the functional effects of HfAMP on bacterial membranes. We found the fluorescence intensity of PI increased with increasing concentrations of HfAMP (Fig. 3A-B), indicating that Hf-AM caused a dose-dependent increase in inner membrane permeability. In contrast, the fluorescence intensity of PI decreased at 16 µg/mL due to the complete lysis of bacteria. Furthermore, we also found that the membrane fluidity of S. aureus ATCC 25923 and MRSA GU-1 changed when treated with various concentrations of HfAMP (Fig. 3C-D). Additionally, HfAMP not only affects the membrane but also binds to membrane components. Our results demonstrate that the antibacterial activity of HfAMP against S. aureus ATCC 25923 and MRSA GU-1 was significantly reduced in the presence of PG and CL, indicating that HfAMP was able to specifically bind to PG/CL on the inner membrane of bacteria (Fig. 3E-F). This binding disrupts the structural integrity of the cell membranes, thereby affecting normal cellular function.

Moreover, live-dead analysis revealed that HfAMP induced membrane damage, leading to the death of S. aureus ATCC 25923 (Fig. 3G). In addition, the FITC-labeled HfAMP was found to colocalize with FM 4–64 (cell membrane) in S. aureus ATCC 25923, indicating immediate binding to the bacteria membrane (Fig. 3J). Furthermore, the morphological structure of the cells in the untreated group was intact and full. However, after treatment with HfAMP at 8 µg/mL, the bacteria membranes displayed irregular atrophy and rupture, which was accompanied by membrane rupture and lysis, with some cells appearing fragmented into isolated bacterial fragments (Fig. 3H-I). These observations further confirm the direct interaction of HfAMP with bacterial membranes.

Next, we further evaluated the functional effects of HfAMP on the membrane function. The results showed that the fluorescence intensity of DisC3(5) significantly increased in S. aureus ATCC 25923 and MRSA GU-1 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4A-B), suggesting that HfAMP disrupts S. aureus ATCC 25923 and MRSA GU-1 Δψ and leads to membrane potential depolarization. Moreover, we found that HfAMP slightly decreased the fluorescence intensity of BCECF-AM in S. aureus ATCC 25923 and MRSA GU-1, indicating that HfAMP downregulated the ΔpH and disrupted the transmembrane proton gradient (Fig. 4C-D). This decrease in fluorescence intensity may be related to the rapid fragmentation of the bacteria by the HfAMP. In addition, HfAMP treatment significantly decreased the degree of INT reduction in S. aureus ATCC 25923 and MRSA GU-1 (Fig. 4E-F) and inhibited the electron transport chain (ETC).

In addition, changes in membrane potential and ETC lead to changes in bacterial ATP production [18]. We found an increase in intracellular ATP in S. aureus ATCC 25923 and MRSA GU-1 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4G-H). Furthermore, we observed significantly increased ROS (Fig. 4I-J) and respiration level (Fig. 4K-L) of the S. aureus ATCC 25923 and MRSA GU-1, which correspondingly aggravated membrane damage to further paralyze bacterial homeostasis. The increase in ROS levels in S. aureus ATCC 25923 and MRSA GU-1 treated with HfAMP was attenuated by NAC (Fig. 4I-J). Taken together, our results suggest that HfAMP triggers membrane damage, dissipates PMF, and perturbs bacterial metabolism, thereby resulting in the accumulation of toxic products and bacterial death.

Biocompatibility

In biomedical applications, the biosafety of AMPs is a significant concern. We found HfAMP displays a high hemolysis rate; when exposed to 8 µg/mL, the hemolysis rate was 7.65% (Fig. 5A), which was significantly higher than the internationally accepted standard of 5% [33]. At 128 µg/mL, the hemolysis rate increased sharply to 96.52%. As shown in Fig. 5B, the survival rates of A549 and HepG2 cells were 20.61% and 7.96%, respectively, when treated with 64 µg/mL of HfAMP, which were clearly below the 70% threshold [34, 35].

In vivo antibacterial activity

Considering that HfAMP has hemolytic and cytotoxic activity, we established a mouse model of MRSA GU-1 skin infection to evaluate the in vivo antibacterial activity of HfAMP. The bacterial counts in the control group and vancomycin-treated group were approximately 1.17 × 108 CFU/g and 2.90 × 105 CFU/g, respectively (Fig. 5C). The bacterial counts were about 1.53 × 107 CFU/g at a low dose (2.5 mg/kg/d) and 3.47 × 106 CFU/g at a high dose (5 mg/kg/d) (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5C). These results indicate that HfAMP treatment significantly reduced the bacterial load in skin tissues and that its antibacterial activity improved with increasing dosage. In addition to reducing bacterial load, HfAMP treatment also inhibited the development of inflammation at the infection site. Compared with the untreated mice, the skin tissue of the vehicle group infected with MRSA GU-1 showed a large number of neutrophils (Fig. 5D). In contrast, HfAMP-treated mice exhibited reduced neutrophil infiltration, with the therapeutic effect being more pronounced at 5 mg/kg/day than at 2.5 mg/kg/day. Similarly, vancomycin treatment also reduced neutrophil levels. Additionally, the biopsy of healthy skin served as a control. The inflammatory scores for the vehicle group, HfAMP group (5 mg/kg and 2.5 mg/kg), and vancomycin group were 3, 2, 2, and 1, respectively. These findings suggest that HfAMP has promising in vivo antimicrobial activity.

Discussion

The use of antibiotics has saved millions of lives in recent decades; however, the prolonged and excessive use of these medications has led to a rapid increase in bacterial resistance. Currently, antibiotic resistance causes the deaths of hundreds of thousands of individuals annually [6]. This situation underscores the urgent need for the development of new antibiotics to combat resistant bacterial strains. AMPs are small molecules involved in the innate immune response across all living organisms. They have attracted significant attention due to their diverse biological functions and broad-spectrum activity against a wide range of pathogens [36]. In the current study, we report a novel antibacterial peptide, HfAMP, which exhibits high efficacy against MRSA. HfAMP effectively inhibits the growth of both S. aureus ATCC 25923 and the clinical isolate MRSA GU-1, exhibiting rapid bactericidal activity. Moreover, HfAMP shows tolerance to high concentrations of serum, but it is sensitive to pepsin and highly toxic to mammalian cells in vitro and has high hemolytic activity against red blood cells. Mechanistic studies reveal that HfAMP targets bacterial membranes, inducing membrane damage, decreasing membrane potential, and depolarization. Most importantly, HfAMP was effective in animal models of MRSA skin infections, indicating its potential as a promising candidate for further development as a novel antibiotic.

Biofilms are significant contributors to chronic and recurrent infections, yet they are often overlooked in the screening of new antibiotics [37, 38]. Consequently, inhibiting and disrupting biofilms presents a crucial challenge for the development of effective antimicrobials. Previous studies reported that the antimicrobial peptide LI14 could inhibit biofilm formation and eliminate mature biofilms [24]. In this study, we found that HfAMP effectively inhibits bacterial attachment to surfaces, prevents biofilm formation, and disrupts mature biofilms in a concentration-dependent manner.

AMPs offer significant advantages over conventional antibiotics, including potent broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity, high stability, and a reduced risk of antimicrobial resistance development [39]. Although AMPs have the potential to be utilized as effective antimicrobial drugs, their activity can be influenced by various factors, including chemical, biological, physical, and physiological conditions. For example, the antibacterial activity of the human peptide LL-37 decreases with increasing salt concentration and binding to human serum [40, 41]. In contrast, our results indicate that the antibacterial activity of HfAMP is unaffected by varying concentrations of salts, serum, pH, and temperature; however, it does decrease in the presence of pepsin.

The primary mechanism by which AMPs exert their antibacterial effects involves disruption of the biophysical integrity of bacterial membranes, accompanied by a series of physiological changes. These include increased membrane permeability and fluidity, reduced membrane potential, disrupted PMF, bacterial metabolic disorders, and leakage of cytoplasmic contents [36, 42]. For example, NP-6 has been shown to affect bacterial membrane integrity and increase membrane permeability [43]. Bacterial membranes are considered attractive primary targets for the development of novel antimicrobial drugs, as they are critical for bacterial growth and survival [39]. In our study, we found that HfAMP can lead to the disruption of bacterial membranes by increasing membrane permeability and membrane fluidity in MRSA, leading to the leakage of intracellular contents. This suggests that HfAMP interacts with various membrane components of the bacteria. Furthermore, additional experiments revealed that exogenous PG and CL significantly reduced the antibacterial activity of HfAMP, with MIC values increasing 8-fold and 4-fold, respectively. This suggests that HfAMP targets PG and CL within the inner membrane, ultimately inducing membrane damage. This finding is consistent with a study by Shi et al. [24], which showed that the MIC of the antimicrobial peptide LI14 against MRSA T114 was increased 4-fold and 2-fold in the presence of exogenous PG/CL. Additionally, the broad-spectrum adjuvant SLAP-S25 exhibited synergistic activity by targeting the PG in the membrane [44]. These results suggest that HfAMP specifically binds to the components of inner bacterial membranes, leading to cell membrane damage. Meanwhile, PMF dissipation plays a crucial role in the lethality of AMPs by impairing essential bacterial functions and accelerating bacterial death [39, 45]. In our study, HfAMP dissipated the ΔΨ component of PMF. Furthermore, HfAMP resulted in metabolic perturbations, increased ATP levels, and led to the accumulation of toxic products. These findings emphasize that bacterial PMF may serve as a potential target for the development of novel antimicrobial agents.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we report that HfAMP is an excellent membrane-targeted antibiotic candidate for combating infections elicited by MRSA due to its broad-spectrum antibacterial activity, anti-biofilm properties, and efficacy in animal models of infection. Furthermore, HfAMP targets bacterial membrane components and induces membrane damage, leading to PMF disruption and metabolic perturbation, ultimately leading to bacterial death.
