Galectin-9-driven immune evasion constrains radiotherapy-induced systemic antitumor immunity
Authors: Jiaming Song, Boning Liu, Rodney Cheng-En Hsieh, Hsiao-Fan Chen, Riyao Yang, Jinjian Liu, Zhiguo Liu, Shasha Shi, Qihui Chen, Delong Liu, Shunjie Xiong, Mien-Chie Hung, Linlin Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile radiotherapy (RT) is effective for local tumor control, it rarely induces the regression of non-irradiated metastases, a phenomenon known as the abscopal effect. The mechanisms constraining this systemic immune response remain poorly understood. This study investigates galectin-9 (Gal-9), a ligand for the TIM-3 (T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3) immune checkpoint, as a mediator of immune escape that limits RT systemic efficacy and evaluates combinatorial RT/Gal-9 blockade in preclinical models.BackgroundMethodsMultiplatform analysis (RNA sequencing, immunoblot, flow cytometry, ELISA, immunohistochemistry) characterized RT-induced Gal-9 regulation in human lung/colorectal cancer cell lines, murine tumors/serums, and paired patient tumors. Local and abscopal therapeutic efficacy was evaluated in homologous (CT26/CT26, LLC/LLC) and heterologous (CT26/4T1) two-tumor mouse models. Immune profiling of tumor microenvironment, tumor-draining lymph nodes (tdLNs), and splenic compartments was comprehensively assessed by flow cytometry. Mechanistic studies employed STING (Stimulator of Interferon Genes) inhibition (H151), CD8+ T-cell depletion (anti-CD8α), macrophage/monocyte depletion (PLX-3397), interferon-I (IFN-I) blockade (anti-IFNAR1), and lymphocyte egress inhibition (FTY720).ResultsRT upregulated Gal-9 predominantly within host myeloid compartments (dendritic cells, macrophages, monocytes, neutrophils) versus tumor cells, in both irradiated tumors and abscopal tumors. Mechanistically, RT-activated STING-IFN-I axis locally induced Gal-9+ myeloid cells that subsequently disseminated systemically. Clinically, elevated post-RT Gal-9 in patient biopsies correlated with poor therapeutic outcomes. Notably, combining Gal-9 blockade with RT elicited potent abscopal responses in homologous two-tumor mouse models. Furthermore, anti-Gal-9 markedly enhanced radio-immunotherapy efficacy in the poorly immunogenic Lewis lung carcinoma. Immunologically, Gal-9 blockade synergized with RT to activate the myeloid and T cell compartments, enhancing dendritic cell accumulation in tdLNs and boosting CD8+ T cell infiltration in abscopal tumors. Depletion of CD8+ T cells/monocytes or blocking lymphocyte egress from lymph nodes abrogated the abscopal efficacy, underscoring their essential roles.ConclusionsOur findings establish RT-induced Gal-9 as a novel dual myeloid/T-cell immune checkpoint restricting abscopal responses. Gal-9 blockade represents a promising strategy to potentiate radiotherapy against metastatic disease, defining a therapeutic paradigm distinct from conventional checkpoint inhibitors.

Introduction

Radiotherapy (RT) has been widely used as a standard treatment modality for local tumor control, yet it occasionally induces regression of metastatic tumors outside irradiated lesions, a rare phenomenon termed the abscopal effect.1 2 Enhancing this effect could significantly improve metastatic disease management. Beyond direct cytotoxicity, RT triggers immunomodulatory responses that may drive abscopal responses.3 4 It induces immunogenic cell death, releasing tumor antigens to activate the cGAS (cyclic GMP-AMP synthase)/STING/interferon-I (IFN-I) axis and prime T cells,57 while also promoting immunosuppressive mechanisms like PD-L1 (Programmed Death-Ligand 1) upregulation.810 RT thus acts as a double-edged sword in the tumor microenvironment (TME).11 Despite preclinical synergy between RT and anti-PD-1 (Programmed Cell Death Protein 1)/PD-L1 therapies,8 12 13 clinical responses remain modest in metastatic settings,14 15 with abscopal effects persisting as rare events.16 These limitations highlight unresolved immune evasion pathways, particularly glyco-immune mechanisms that may constrain systemic antitumor immunity.

Galectin-9 (Gal-9), a β-galactoside-binding lectin with two carbohydrate-recognition domains (CRDs), has emerged as a key immune checkpoint.1723 Through its CRDs, Gal-9 binds specific glycoproteins, such as TIM-3 and 4–1BB(CD137), to cluster immunoregulatory receptors and regulate immune signaling pathways.2429 We and others have revealed its expression in antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and inducible upregulation in tumor cells by inflammatory cytokines, particularly type I/II interferons.18 Clinically, although elevated Gal-9 typically associates with immunosuppression and poor prognosis across malignancies30 31, certain contexts demonstrate paradoxical correlations with favorable outcomes,32 33 likely reflecting enhanced immune cell infiltration. Our preclinical studies demonstrate that Gal-9 inhibition synergizes with agents such as GITR (glucocorticoid-induced tumor necrosis factor receptor-related protein) agonists, ATM (ataxia-telangiectasia mutated) inhibitors and EGFR (Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor)-tyrosine kinase inhibitors to elicit potent antitumor activity.17 18 34 The anti-Gal-9 mAb (monoclonal antibody) LYT-200 shows promise in Phase I/II trials (NCT04666688) in metastatic solid tumors, both as monotherapy and combined with chemotherapy or anti-PD-1 agents.35 Despite recent advances, critical knowledge gaps remain regarding how cancer therapies regulate Gal-9 induction and how Gal-9 blockade reprograms glyco-immune networks in the TME.

In this study, we identify RT-driven Gal-9 upregulation as a central barrier to abscopal responses. Gal-9 blockade synergizes with RT to overcome myeloid/T-cell checkpoints, activating systemic antitumor immunity. These findings establish Gal-9 as a dual immune checkpoint and promising target for enhancing RT efficacy in metastatic disease.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and reagents

All cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, Virginia, USA), independently validated by short tandem repeat DNA fingerprinting, and tested negative for mycoplasma infection. H596, H460, Calu-1, HCT116, H1975, PC9, HCC827, CT26, LLC and 4T1 were cultured in RPMI 1640 (Roswell Park Memorial Institute medium 1640) or DMEM (Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium) (media, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Anti-Gal-9 antibody was purchased from BioRad (Hercules, California, USA). Anti-p-STAT1 (Tyr701), STAT1, p-STING, p-TBK1 and Actin antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA). IgG isotype control, anti-mouse Gal-9, anti-mouse PD-1, and anti-mouse CD8 antibodies were purchased from Bio X Cell (Lebanon, New Hampshire, USA). Anti-IFN-α receptor 1 antibodies were purchased from Selleck (Houston, Texas, USA). FTY-720, PLX-3397, and H151 were purchased from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, New Jersey, USA). Anti-mouse fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies including APC/Cy7-CD45, AF700-CD3, FITC-CD4, PerCP/Cy5.5-CD8, PE-C7-Granzyme B, PerCP/Cy5.5-Gal-9, PE-Gal-9, APC-CD103, BV605-CD11C, FITC-CD11b, PE-F4/80, PE-Cy7-CD86, PE-CF594-MHC II, AF700-Ly6G, BV421-Ly-6C, BV605-CD44, PE-CF594-CD62L, BV510-PD-1, BV421-TCF1 antibodies were purchased from BioLegend (San Diego, California, USA).

Purification of recombinant Gal-9 proteins

GST (Glutathione S-Transferase)-fused N-CRD or C-CRD mouse Gal-9 were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21 using pGEX-N-CRD or pGEX-C-CRD constructs, respectively, as described previously.18 Proteins were purified by glutathione-affinity chromatography with glutathione-conjugated Sepharose beads (GenScript) following the manufacturer’s protocols.

ELISA

Nunc Maxisorp ELISA plates (BioLegend) were coated overnight at 4°C with 2 µg/mL mouse Gal-9 GST-tagged domain proteins in PBS (Phosphate-Buffered Saline). After washing, plates were blocked with bovine serum albumin and incubated with Gal-9 antibodies (Clone: RG9-1), followed by horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-mouse IgG antibodies (Sigma-Aldrich). Following additional washes, plates were incubated with 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 20 min. Reactions were terminated with H₂SO₄, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm (reference 570 nm) using a BioTek Synergy Neo2 plate reader.

Antibody protection against Gal-9-induced T-cell death

Splenic T cells were isolated from C57BL/6 mice and activated with anti-CD3 (1 µg/mL) + anti-CD28 (1 µg/mL) for 72 hours to induce TIM-3 expression prior to Gal-9 exposure. This activation renders T cells susceptible to Gal-9-induced cell death via TIM-3 ligation. Murine T cells were incubated overnight with 2 µg/mL Gal-9±2 µg/mL anti-Gal-9 antibodies. Cells were stained with FITC-anti-CD4 and PE-anti-CD8 antibodies. Viable CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were quantified using counting beads as described previously.20 Briefly, 1×10⁴ BD FACS AccuDrop Beads (BD Biosciences) were added per sample, with 3000 bead events acquired during flow cytometry. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software.

Immunoblot analysis

Immunoblot analysis was performed as described previously.36 Briefly, proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE (Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA). The PVDF membranes were first blocked in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.2% Tween 20 and 5% fat-free dry milk, and then incubated with primary antibodies at 4℃ overnight, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 hour. After washes, specific protein bands were visualized using a chemiluminescence detection reagent (Millipore, Billerica, Massachusetts, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Pierce Biotechnology, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA)

Immunohistochemical staining of patient with rectal cancer tumor tissues

Paraformaldehyde-fixed tumor tissues were stained using anti-human Gal-9 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology). The slides were scanned at 20× magnification and quantitative analysis of Gal-9 was conducted using StrataQuest software. The data was shown as a percentage of Gal-9 positive area.

Cell isolation and flow cytometric analysis

For flow cytometric analysis, bilateral tumors, lymph nodes and spleens were collected from mice on day 13 or day 16 after tumor inoculation. Cells from spleens or lymph nodes were isolated by grinding the tissues through 70 µm strainers. For TILs isolation, the collected tumor tissues were cut into small pieces and incubated in collagenase IV (3 mg/mL, Solarbio) and DNase I (50 ug/mL, Solarbio) for 20–30 min at 37°C. The dissociated cells were then passed through a 70 µm cell strainer (BD) and washed with staining buffer (PBS supplemented with 1% BSA and 0.1% NaN3). The cells were re-suspended in staining buffer and stained with Zombie Violet Fixable Viability Kit (BioLegend), blocked with FcR Blocking Reagent (Miltenyi), and stained with indicated fluorescence-labeled surface antibodies in staining buffer for 20–30 min on ice in the dark. For subsequent intracellular staining, cells were first permeabilized using a Fixation and Permeabilization Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and then stained with appropriate antibodies. The stained cells were detected by flow cytometry (BD Fortessa). Data were analyzed using the Cytobank platform.37

Generation of mouse BMDCs and BMDMs

Bone marrow cells were harvested from the femurs and tibias of 7–10 week-BALB/c mice. Bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were then differentiated for 7–8 days using RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS (Fetal Bovine Serum), mouse granulocyte macrophage colony-stimulating factor (20 ng/mL, Sinobiological), and interleukin-4 (10 ng/mL, Sinobiological). Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were differentiated for 7–8 days in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and macrophage colony-stimulating factor (10 ng/mL, Sinobiological). The culture medium was replenished on days 3 and 6.

Animal models

6–8 weeks old male C57BL/6J mice and female BALB/c mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology and housed in laminar flow cabinets under specific pathogen-free conditions. Sex was not considered as a biological variable. No statistical methods were used to predefine sample size. Monitoring of mice was done with blinding. To test whether combination treatment could exert abscopal effects, homologous two-tumor models were established. Briefly, a total of 1×106 CT26 or Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) cells were first injected subcutaneously into the left flank of the BALB/c (CT26/CT26 model) or C57BL/6J mice (LLC/LLC model), and then 2 days later 5×105 cells were inoculated into the contralateral flank to serve as abscopal tumors. To evaluate antigen specificity of the abscopal effect in our system, heterologous two-tumor models were established by injecting CT26 (1×106) and 4T1 (5×105) in the contralateral flanks of BALB/c mice (CT26/4T1 model). After tumors were palpable, mice were randomized into treatment cohorts as indicated. Treatment initiation required both tumors to reach 40–50 mm³. Local RT was delivered directly to the left flank tumor using image-guided irradiator. For anti-Gal-9 treatment experiments, 100 µg of the anti-Gal-9 antibody was injected intraperitoneally every 3 days for a total of three or four times. For anti-PD-1 treatment experiments, 100 µg of the anti-PD-1 antibody was injected intraperitoneally every 3 days for a total of four times. For CD8+T cell depletion or IFNAR1 blocking experiments, anti-CD8 antibodies (200 µg/dose) or anti-IFNAR1 antibodies (50 µg/dose) were injected intraperitoneally 2 or 3 days prior to IR and repeated every 3 days until experiment endpoint. The efficiency of CD8+ T cell depletion was determined by flow cytometric analysis of spleen and bilateral tumors. For macrophage/monocyte depletion, PLX-3397 was administered orally at 50 mg/ kg 2 days prior to IR and repeated every day until the experiment endpoint. To block lymphocyte egress from lymph nodes, FTY720 (15 µg/dose) was injected intraperitoneally 3 days prior to IR and repeated every day until the experiment endpoint. Tumor size and body weight were measured every 2–3 days. Bilateral tumor volumes were calculated using the formula: length×width2/2. No data was excluded. Mice were sacrificed when tumors reached 1500 mm3 in size to comply with animal ethical guidelines (Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee protocols).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the GraphPad Prism software. All data are presented as mean±SD or SEM. For comparisons between two independent groups, unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied. Comparisons across multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), while longitudinal tumor growth measurements were assessed using two-way ANOVA. P values<0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

Results

RT induces Gal-9 upregulation in both tumor cells and myeloid cells

To investigate RT-induced immune evasion mechanisms, we analyzed two clinical cohorts (GSE233517, n=11; GSE165612, n=6) comprising paired pre-RT and post-RT biopsies from patients with colorectal, prostate, and ovarian cancer. Integrated analysis of both cohorts revealed Gal-9 as the most consistently upregulated immunosuppressive checkpoint following RT (5/11 in GSE233517; 2/6 in GSE165612), while other checkpoints, such as PDCD1 (Programmed Cell Death Protein 1), TIGIT (T cell Immunoreceptor with IG and ITIM domains), CD244, CD276, showed limited induction (figure 1A, online supplemental figure S1A).38 Complementary murine studies confirmed that fractionated RT (8 Gy×3) induced Gal-9, but not other checkpoints, in TSA mammary carcinomas (GSE83915) (online supplemental figure S1B). Single-cell RNA sequencing of irradiated MC38 colon adenocarcinomas demonstrated significant Gal-9 upregulation (2 Gy, 16 Gy, 20 Gy) predominantly in dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages (Macs) (GSE262699, GSE206387) (figure 1B),39 40 with no comparable induction of other checkpoints (online supplemental figures S1C and S2A,B). Dose-dependent Gal-9 induction (2 Gy, 8 Gy, 20 Gy) was observed in monocytes from irradiated B78 melanoma models (GSE234159) (online supplemental figure S2C). Transcriptional analysis of MC38 myeloid subsets (GSE197767)10 further confirmed RT-enhanced Gal-9 (online supplemental figure S3A). These data collectively suggest Gal-9 as a uniquely conserved RT-responsive immune checkpoint across human malignancies and murine systems.

To validate these findings and assess systemic effects, we employed a homologous two-tumor model (CT26/CT26) in BALB/c mice, irradiating the left-flank tumor (5 Gy) with the right-flank serving as an abscopal tumor (figure 1C). Flow cytometry (online supplemental figure S3B) revealed significantly elevated Gal-9 expression (evidenced by median fluorescence intensity) in CD45+ immune cells (online supplemental figure S3C) and myeloid populations, including DCs (CD11C+), Macs (F4/80+CD11b+), monocytes (CD11b+Ly6G-Ly6Chi) and neutrophils (CD11b+Ly6G+Ly6C+), in both irradiated and abscopal tumors, with more pronounced upregulation in major histocompatibility complex (MHC) IIhi myeloid cells (figure 1D). Gal-9 upregulation was also observed in tumor-draining lymph node (tdLN) DCs but not in T cells (online supplemental figure S3D,E). While tumor cells (CD45−) exhibited marginal upregulation compared with myeloid cells in vivo (figure 1E), in vitro studies confirmed irradiation (IR)-induced Gal-9 in human lung/colorectal cancer cell lines (4, 8, 10 Gy) (figure 1F and online supplemental figure S3F). Validation in an A20 lymphoma model confirmed fractionated RT (4 Gy×2) induced Gal-9 in irradiated/abscopal tumors and tdLNs (GSE281695) (figure 1G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that IR elicits robust Gal-9 upregulation predominantly in host myeloid cells and, to a lesser extent, in tumor cells, at irradiated sites, abscopal tumors, and tdLNs.

To evaluate the clinical relevance, we assessed Gal-9 expression by immunohistochemistry in paired tumor samples from 28 patients with rectal cancer receiving neoadjuvant RT (5 Gy×5). Patient clinical characteristics and demographics are detailed in tables1 2. This revealed significant post-RT Gal-9 elevation versus baseline (figure 1H, left and middle panels). Notably, non-responders (Mandard tumor regression grade 4 to 5) exhibited higher post-RT Gal-9 expression (>50% positive tumor area; figure 1H, right panel) than responders, supporting Gal-9 as a key mediator of RT-associated immune evasion.

STING-IFN-I axis mediates RT-induced Gal-9 expression

Building on our prior evidence that IFN-Is induce Gal-9 in tumor cells,18 we extended this regulatory axis to myeloid cells, demonstrating robust IFN-I-mediated Gal-9 upregulation in BMDCs and BMDMs (online supplemental figure S4A). Given RT’s established role in the activation of STING-IFN-I signaling,5 we tested whether this pathway mediates Gal-9 upregulation. Indeed, IFN-I blockade with anti-IFNAR-1 antibodies in CT26-bearing mice abrogated IR-induced Gal-9 upregulation in both irradiated and abscopal tumors, concomitant with reduced STAT1 (Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 1) phosphorylation (figure 2A,B).41 This establishes IFN-I dependence for Gal-9 induction. Complementary in vitro study revealed STING activation in irradiated human lung cancer lines (online supplemental figure S4B), with pharmacological STING inhibition (H-151, 5 µM) significantly suppressing TBK1 (TANK-binding kinase 1) phosphorylation, reducing cellular Gal-9 (western blot; figure 2C), and decreasing soluble Gal-9 secretion (ELISA; figure 2D). Collectively, these results establish the STING-IFN-I pathway as the dominant mechanism for IR-induced Gal-9 expression.

Locally induced Gal-9+ myeloid cells disseminated systemically

While the STING/IFN-I axis explains local Gal-9 induction, a paradox emerges: IFNβ increases exclusively in irradiated tumors without elevation in abscopal tumors or serum.42 We found that serum from irradiated mice exhibited Gal-9 elevation without IFNβ increase (post-IR, days 3 and day 6) (figure 2E and online supplemental figure S4C). Clinical analysis revealed significant Gal-9 induction in circulating monocytes and DCs in patients with non-small cell lung cancer post-RT (GSE190905, figure 2F),43 prompting our hypothesis that locally induced Gal-9+ myeloid cells undergo systemic trafficking. Supporting this, transcriptomic profiling demonstrated upregulated cell chemotaxis pathways in both irradiated and abscopal tumors (GSE281695, figure 2G). Critically, flow cytometric analysis confirmed enhanced infiltration of Gal-9+ DCs and Macs in both tumor sites (figure 2H). These results collectively suggest that locally induced Gal-9+ myeloid cells migrate from irradiated sites to systemic sites, including distant tumors, independent of IFN-I diffusion.

The combination of IR with Gal-9 blockade elicits robust abscopal effects in preclinical tumor models

Based on our observation of systemic Gal-9 upregulation following RT, we hypothesized that inhibiting Gal-9 would enhance therapeutic outcomes. We validated a Gal-9-neutralizing antibody (Clone: RG9-1), which was confirmed to specifically target the N-CRD domain and potently inhibit Gal-9-mediated TIM-3+T cell death (online supplemental figure S5A–C). To evaluate the efficacy of combining anti-Gal-9 therapy with RT, bilateral CT26 tumor-bearing mice (CT26/CT26 model) received 5 Gy RT to left-flank tumors (dose reduced from 15 Gy due to complete eradication at higher doses; online supplemental figure S5D,E), with right-flank tumors serving as abscopal controls (figure 3A). Compared with untreated controls, IR monotherapy (5 Gy) showed a moderate local tumor suppression, while combination with anti-Gal-9 significantly enhanced local tumor control without weight loss (figure 3B left panel, figure 3C upper panels, online supplemental figure S5F,G). Notably, IR alone had negligible effects on abscopal tumors versus untreated controls, whereas co-treatment with anti-Gal-9 markedly inhibited abscopal tumor growth and prolonged survival (figure 3B right panel, figure 3C lower panels, figure 3D). These findings demonstrate that anti-Gal-9 synergizes with IR to enhance both local and abscopal tumor control.

To further validate these findings, we evaluated the combinational therapy in LLC, a poorly immunogenic syngeneic model that is refractory to current immune checkpoint blockade therapies (figure 3E).44 Mice bearing bilateral LLC tumors (LLC/LLC model) received 10 Gy RT to left-flank tumors, with right-flank tumors as abscopal counterparts. Neither anti-PD-1 monotherapy, RT, nor their combination elicited significant responses compared with the untreated controls. Notably, adding anti-Gal-9 to RT/anti-PD-1 therapy dramatically enhanced efficacy in both irradiated and abscopal tumors (figures 3F and online supplemental figure S6A,B), while maintaining treatment safety (online supplemental figure S6C). These results establish Gal-9 inhibition as a critical synergist that overcomes radioimmunotherapy resistance in immunologically cold tumors by countering RT-induced immune evasion.

Anti-Gal-9 synergizes with IR to activate myeloid and T cells in abscopal tumors

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying enhanced efficacy of IR/anti-Gal-9 therapy, we profiled tumor-infiltrating leukocytes in bilateral CT26 tumors at two critical post-IR intervals: day 3 post-IR (day 13 post-inoculation) and day 6 post-IR (day 16 post-inoculation) (figure 4A; gating strategy in online supplemental figure S7A). Minimal T cell alterations occurred at day 3 post-IR (online supplemental figure S7B,C), but significant T cell infiltration and activation was observed by day 6 post-IR. This indicates that recovery from IR-induced lymphodepletion enables subsequent functional immune responses that drive tumor regression. While IR monotherapy enhanced CD45+ and CD8+ T-cell infiltration in irradiated tumors (online supplemental figure S7D; figure 4B upper panels), it failed to do so in distant sites, likely due to Gal-9-mediated systemic immunosuppression; combining IR with anti-Gal9 reversed this immunosuppression, enabling robust infiltration into distant tumors, supporting a potent abscopal effect (figure 4B lower panels, figure 4C). Furthermore, while irradiated tumors showed minimal induction of cytotoxic, memory and stem-like markers (figure 4D,E upper panels; figure 4F), abscopal tumors showed substantial increases in granzyme B+ (GZMB+) CD8+ T cells, elevated trends in GZMB+ CD4+ T cells, and significant expansion of effector memory (CD103−CD44ʰⁱCD62Lˡᵒ), tissue-resident memory (CD103+CD44ʰⁱCD62Lˡᵒ), and progenitor exhausted (TCF1+PD-1+) T cell subsets, compared with monotherapy (figure 4D,F lower panels, figure 4G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that anti-Gal-9 synergizes with IR to enhance T cell infiltration, activation, and memory differentiation, with a preferential effect in abscopal tumors.

IR/anti-Gal-9 promotes DC accumulation and T cell expansion in abscopal tumor-draining lymph nodes

To determine whether the combination of IR and anti-Gal-9 therapy elicits systemic immune activation in the periphery, we performed immune profiling of bilateral tdLNs in CT26 tumor-bearing mice. While combination therapy induced only moderate increases in CD45+ cells, CD8+ T cells, and CD4+ T cells in ipsilateral tdLNs (draining irradiated tumors) (online supplemental figures S10A–C left panel), it substantially elevated these populations in contralateral tdLNs (draining abscopal tumors) compared with IR alone (figure 6A,B; online supplemental figure S10C right panel). Modest T cell expansion occurred in the spleen (online supplemental figure S10D). Notably, while DC accumulation in ipsilateral tdLNs was modestly enhanced (online supplemental figure S10E), more pronounced increases were observed in contralateral tdLNs (figure 6C). Furthermore, CD86+ subset frequencies among CD45+ cells, reflecting costimulatory molecule expression on antigen-presenting cells, were significantly higher in contralateral than in ipsilateral tdLNs (figure 6D vs online supplemental figure S10F). Consistent with this, contralateral tdLNs showed enrichment of CD86+ DCs, CD103+ cDC1s, CD8+ cDC1s, and cDC2s (figure 6E vs online supplemental figure S10G). These findings collectively demonstrate that the IR/anti-Gal-9 drives robust DC recruitment and T cell expansion preferentially in distal nodes draining abscopal tumors, aligning with systemic antitumor response.

Systemic efficacy requires coordinated innate and adaptive immunity

Given the significant increase in tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells following combination therapy, we assessed their functional contribution. Depletion of CD8+ T cells using anti-CD8 antibodies (figure 7A; confirmed by flow cytometry in online supplemental figure S11A) prior to treatment initiation completely abrogated the therapeutic efficacy of IR/anti-Gal-9 in both irradiated and abscopal tumors (figure 7B–D), establishing CD8+ T cells as essential effectors for local and systemic tumor control.

To determine whether CD8+ T cell-mediated abscopal effects require antigen sharing, we established a heterologous two-tumor model (CT26/4T1, figure 7E). In this model, mice bearing CT26 colon carcinomas (irradiated) and antigenically distinct 4T1 mammary carcinomas (non-irradiated) were used to assess the specificity of the systemic immune response. While combination therapy maintained local control of irradiated CT26 tumors, it completely failed to induce regression of 4T1 abscopal tumors in the heterologous model (figure 7F and online supplemental figure S11B,C). The 4T1 growth kinetics were statistically indistinguishable from untreated controls, in stark contrast to robust abscopal responses observed in homologous CT26 models (figure 3A–D). These results demonstrate that systemic anti-tumor immunity requires shared antigen recognition, confirming an antigen-specific mechanism.46

We next evaluate whether T cell trafficking from lymphoid organs contributes to therapeutic efficacy (figure 7G). FTY720-mediated blockade of lymphocyte egress from lymph nodes did not significantly impair control of irradiated tumors but completely abrogated the abscopal response (figure 7H). These results demonstrate that local tumor clearance is mediated by resident effector T cells, whereas systemic abscopal control requires de novo migration of antigen-specific CD8+ T cells from lymphoid reservoirs.

In view of concurrent myeloid activation during combination therapy, we examined its functional contribution. Macrophage/monocyte depletion via CSF1R inhibitor PLX-3397 significantly restored growth of both irradiated and abscopal tumors in the LLC model (online supplemental figure S12A–C), confirming these myeloid subsets as critical mediators of local and systemic control. Based on the essential role of IFN-I signaling in myeloid activation45 and our observed enhancement of IFN-I pathway activity during combination therapy, we assessed its contribution using anti-IFNAR-1 blocking antibodies (figure 7I). IFN-I blockade significantly compromised treatment efficacy in both irradiated and abscopal tumors (figure 7J and online supplemental figure S12D), confirming its pivotal role in antitumor immunity.

Collectively, these data demonstrate that optimal abscopal control requires coordinated antigen-specific CD8+ T cell effector functions, IFN-I-licensed myeloid support, and lymphoid reservoir-derived T cell trafficking, establishing innate-adaptive crosstalk as the mechanistic basis for systemic tumor regression.

Discussion

Immune evasion represents a fundamental hallmark of cancer that drives therapy resistance.47 Our study unveils a critical immune escape mechanism mediated by the glycan-binding checkpoint Gal-9 that underlies RT resistance and constrains abscopal effect. While RT-induced STING/IFN-I signaling promotes initial antitumor immunity, it also upregulates Gal-9, establishing chronic IFN-I as a dual-edged driver that ultimately contributes to immunosuppression and therapy failure.45 Pharmacological confirmation of STING-dependent Gal-9 induction in vitro provides mechanistic validation, which will be further confirmed in genetic knockout models. Notably, while prior work documented IR-induced Gal-9 in tumor cells,48 we reveal a previously unrecognized dimension: potent RT-driven Gal-9 induction in myeloid populations (dendritic cells, macrophages, monocytes, neutrophils). This systemic response across immune lineages suggests myeloid-derived Gal-9 may be the dominant driver of post-IR immunosuppression. Our data suggest that locally induced Gal-9+ myeloid cells subsequently disseminate systemically to circulation and distant sites. This trafficking mechanism explains distant immune suppression without requiring IFN-I diffusion. The elevation in serum Gal-9 may serve as a potential biomarker for post-RT immunosuppression. Future studies will map the migration routes by directly tracking Gal-9+myeloid cell migration from irradiated to abscopal sites and distant lymphoid organs (tdLN, spleen). Critically, strong post-RT Gal-9 induction in paired rectal cancer patient biopsies correlated with poor therapeutic response, confirming its clinical relevance. Collectively, these findings establish RT-induced Gal-9 as a central mediator of adaptive immune resistance.49

Using two syngeneic immunocompetent models, we demonstrate that Gal-9 inhibition synergizes with RT to enhance local and systemic efficacy in bilateral tumor-bearing mice. The triple combination of RT/anti-Gal-9/anti-PD-1 achieves superior tumor control in the immunologically cold LLC models that are refractory to conventional checkpoint inhibition. This work advances beyond prior reports of RT/anti-PD-1/anti-TIM-3 in glioma and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma models50 51 through two key contributions: (1) mechanistically linking Gal-9 upregulation to abscopal failure, and (2) validating the concept in immunotherapy-resistant tumors, thereby extending actionable therapeutic paradigms. Critically, our single-dose irradiation regimen reveals clinically translatable opportunities for optimizing fractionation protocols, treatment sequencing refinement, and biomarker-guided patient selection based on Gal-9 expression. While cohort size precluded sex-specific response analysis, a limitation for future therapeutic optimization, our findings provide robust preclinical evidence supporting Gal-9 as an actionable therapeutic target across malignancies. These results establish a compelling rationale for clinical evaluation of Gal-9 inhibitors combined with standard RT in checkpoint-refractory metastasis.

Comprehensive immune profiling reveals that IR/anti-Gal-9 fundamentally reprograms CD8+ T cells toward memory and stem-like phenotypes with enhanced functionality. CD8+ T-cell depletion abolished therapeutic efficacy, confirming their role as central effectors. Intriguingly, combination therapy expanded CD8+ T cells in contralateral tdLNs, potentially through increased APC trafficking or T cell migration.52 FTY720-mediated blockade established that abscopal effects require de novo T cell trafficking from lymphoid reservoirs,53 while local tumor control relies on resident T cells. The absence of abscopal responses in heterologous tumor models (CT26/4T1; figure 7E,F) conforms to the antigen-specific nature of the abscopal effect, a key constraint given clinical tumor heterogeneity. While metastases with low antigenic overlap may therefore be refractory, expanded memory T-cell pools suggest potential control of delayed antigen-matched metastases. Future studies employing tetramer-based tracking will be essential to precisely resolve the kinetics of tumor antigen-specific T cell differentiation and their role in systemic immunity.

Beyond T cells, combination therapy drives myeloid cell (DCs, Macs, monocytes, neutrophils) reprogramming toward immunostimulatory states characterized by enhanced antigen presentation and elevated IFN-I-pSTAT1 signaling, a central pathway in myeloid activation.45 This identifies Gal-9 as a novel myeloid checkpoint that modulates IFN-I-driven immunity. Critically, these reprogrammed myeloid cells are indispensable for therapeutic efficacy: macrophage/monocyte depletion via CSF1R inhibition (PLX-3397) restored growth of both irradiated and abscopal tumors. Anti-IFNAR-1 blockade similarly impaired efficacy, underscoring its critical role in mediating the antitumor response. While TIM-3-mediated suppression of cGAS-STING in DCs potentially explains Gal-9’s regulation of IFN-I immunity,54 55 TIM-3-deficient models remain essential for definitive validation. Potential contributions from alternative Gal-9 receptors (eg, Dectin-1, CD44, PD-1) also require further dissection. Together, our findings demonstrate that RT/anti-Gal-9 efficacy relies on coordinated remodeling and crosstalk between innate and adaptive immunity to achieve sustained abscopal tumor control.

In summary, we identify Gal-9 as a master dual immune checkpoint governing coordinated immunosuppression across myeloid and T cell compartments, a previously unrecognized mechanism limiting RT’s abscopal effects. These findings establish Gal-9 blockade as a potent strategy to overcome RT resistance, through fundamental reprogramming of innate and adaptive immunity, providing the mechanistic foundation for clinical translation in metastatic cancers with urgent unmet needs.
