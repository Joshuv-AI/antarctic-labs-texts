Longitudinal analysis of inspiratory support requirements in obesity hypoventilation syndrome: is NIV de-escalation warranted?
Authors: Vivien Moro, Zsuzsanna Takacs, Szabolcs Baglyas, Eszter Podmaniczky, Laura Takacs, Andras Lorx, Luca Valko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOptimal long-term management and inspiratory support requirements remain debated in obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS).ObjectivesThis prospective, observational study assessed inspiratory support requirements in OHS patients initiating noninvasive ventilation (NIV) after acute hypercapnic respiratory failure, using a volume-assured mode targeting normocapnia.MethodsVentilator data and clinical parameters were collected at baseline, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months and 12 months after initiation of NIV. Inspiratory support requirements were assessed using driving pressure and apparent respiratory system compliance and compared over time using the Wilcoxon test and Friedman analysis of variance. Groups with persistent and minimal inspiratory support needs at 1 year were compared using Mann-Whitney, χ2 and Cochran Q tests.ResultsOf 40 enrolled patients, 39 completed the study. Therapeutic adherence (37 (94.9%)) and efficacy (35 (89.7%)) were high. Inspiratory support requirements decreased over the study period (12.1 (9.6–14.9) to 9.9 (6.3–12.2) cmH₂O, p<0.001), though 84.6% of patients still required >5 cmH₂O driving pressure. Initial driving pressure, higher minute ventilation and higher apparent respiratory system compliance were associated with reduced support requirements after 1 year, but no baseline parameter reliably predicted minimal support need. Reduced ventilatory support at 6 months demonstrated the strongest correlation with maintaining lower driving pressure at 12 months.ConclusionsInspiratory support needs reduce over 12 months in OHS patients acutely initiated on long-term NIV, but few patients showed only minimal inspiratory support requirements to maintain normocapnia. Reliable reduction was apparent after 6 months of treatment, suggesting that respiratory muscle recovery is slower than previously assumed. Further studies are needed to verify whether an initial ‘hit hard’ approach with sequential de-escalation of ventilatory support results in optimal outcome.Keywords: Obesity Hypoventilation Syndrome, Non invasive ventilation, Respiratory Muscles, Compliance, Sleep apnoea

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This prospective cohort study found that inspiratory support needs decrease significantly over 12 months with goal-directed, volume-assured NIV. Reduced support at 6 months strongly predicts minimal needs at 1 year; however, baseline clinical parameters do not reliably predict diminishing requirements. The decrease occurs independently of body mass index, respiratory rate and time and minute ventilation, indicating improved muscle function.

Introduction

The global rise in obesity has been paralleled by an increasing incidence of obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS), a significant contributor to chronic respiratory failure associated with high morbidity and mortality.1 2 OHS is defined by the triad of obesity (body mass index (BMI) ≥30 kg/m²), daytime hypercapnia (PaCO₂ ≥45 mm Hg) and sleep-disordered breathing, in the absence of other causes of hypoventilation.3

The pathophysiology of OHS is complex and not yet fully understood. Multiple mechanisms contribute to the development of daytime hypercapnia, including obesity-related mechanical load impairing respiratory mechanics, a blunted central respiratory drive and compromised airway patency, especially during sleep.4 5

Despite the importance of early diagnosis and intervention, most patients are diagnosed only after an episode of acute hypercapnic respiratory failure (AHRF).6,8 Long-term positive airway pressure (PAP) therapy significantly improves survival and is the cornerstone of treatment; however, the optimal PAP modality remains a subject of debate.9

Although continuous PAP (CPAP) therapy may improve symptoms and reduce hypercapnia in some patients, certain clinical situations warrant more advanced respiratory support with non-invasive ventilation (NIV) using bilevel PAP. Current guidelines recommend maintaining NIV in patients who remain ventilator-dependent after an episode of AHRF, whereas CPAP initiation is advised for stable patients.2 10

While CPAP can maintain airway patency and restore reduced FRC, substantial inspiratory support might be required to counteract the ongoing weight-related overload and the associated chronic fatigue of the inspiratory muscles.6 11 There are currently limited data on which patients require substantial inspiratory support and whether this requirement may decrease over time with effective therapy induced muscle unloading.

In this study, we aimed to analyse trends in inspiratory support requirements in OHS patients initiating goal-directed NIV following AHRF. Long-term NIV was delivered via a volume-assured automated ventilation mode, targeting normocapnia. Changes in driving pressures during the first year of long-term ventilation were studied.

Materials and methods

Aims and design

We conducted a prospective, observational cohort study in OHS patients who initiated optimised, goal-directed NIV aimed at normocapnia following admission for AHRF. The primary endpoint was the change in ventilator parameters reflecting inspiratory support requirements at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months and 12 months post initiation, compared with baseline. Secondary endpoints included changes in clinical parameters and therapeutic efficacy (normocapnia and therapy adherence defined as percentage of days used and ventilator hours/day). The study was approved by the Semmelweis University Regional Research Ethics Committee (SE TUKEB 251/2017), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Participants

We enrolled patients who initiated long-term NIV at the Semmelweis University Home Mechanical Ventilation Programme (Budapest, Hungary) due to OHS following hospitalisation for AHRF between 2018 and 2025. Inclusion criteria were adults (≥18 years) diagnosed with OHS per the European Respiratory Society Task Force Report.12 Exclusion criteria included elective NIV initiation, significant spirometry evidence of obstruction (forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV₁) <70% predicted if FEV₁/forced vital capacity (FVC) <70%) and neuromuscular, thoracic or metabolic conditions explaining hypercapnia.

NIV protocol

All patients initiated NIV during AHRF. NIV was continued and optimised during the index hospitalisation using polysomnography (PSG) with arterial PaCO₂ or transcutaneous CO₂ (tcCO₂) monitoring, as part of a goal-directed strategy to normalise daytime PaCO₂ and PaO₂. Treatment objectives were consistent with current German guidelines.13 NIV was delivered in pressure-controlled, volume-targeted mode via full-face or total-face masks using Philips Respironics A40 (Philips Respironics, Murrysville, Pennsylvania, USA) or Löwenstein Medical prisma Vent40 (Löwenstein Medical, Bad Ems, Germany) ventilators. Ventilator settings were titrated during PSG from baseline settings deemed clinically necessary during initial stabilisation of the AHRF, but otherwise in accordance with current clinical guidance.14 15 Expiratory positive airway pressure (EPAP) was adjusted in steps of at least 1 cmH2O with intervals no shorter than 5 min to reduce obstructive events (target apnoea-hypopnoea index (AHI) <5–10/hour). Targeted tidal volume (determined by inspiratory positive airway pressure (IPAP) range) was set at 8 mL/kg and adjusted up to 10 mL/kg to maintain PaCO₂/tcCO₂ levels within 35–45 mm Hg. In cases of persistent hypercapnia, the backup respiratory rate was increased to enhance minute ventilation (MV). Inspiratory time/breathing cycle ratio (target≈35%) was adjusted for comfort and synchrony. Supplemental oxygen was administered if needed (PaO₂ <60 mm Hg, despite adequate ventilation).

Follow-up and data collection

Assessments were conducted at baseline, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months and 12 months and included equipment and therapy target checks, ventilator waveform analysis and clinical monitoring. Therapy was modified as clinically indicated to achieve AHI, PaCO2 and PaO2 targets. IPAP adjustments were made (through IPAP range) if VT values were outside of target range or hypocapnia was present. EPAP reduction was performed in cases of severe adverse reactions (aerophagia, low compliance because of EPAP intolerance according to subjective symptoms). In cases where AHI levels were outside target range (>10/h) and overwhelmingly caused by airway obstruction as evidenced by ventilator downloads, blind EPAP adjustment was allowed (+2 cmH2O) initially, and repeat PSG was advised if target was not reached by the next follow-up. At each time point, demographics (age, gender, BMI, comorbidities), clinical outcomes (pulmonary function tests (PFT), arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis, Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS), Severe Respiratory Insufficiency Questionnaire (SRI)), ventilator parameters (days used, daily usage, IPAP, EPAP, respiratory rate, tidal volume, AHI, supplemental oxygen use) and 6 min walk distance (6MWD) were collected.16 17

PFTs were performed using the Piston PinkFlow metre (Piston, Budapest, Hungary). ABG samples were obtained at least 15 min after discontinuation of ventilation and/or oxygen therapy.

Inspiratory support requirement was assessed by driving pressure (DP) levels. To account for individual differences in tidal volume, inspiratory time and MV, derived parameters were calculated, including apparent respiratory system compliance (aCrs), calculated as the ratio of tidal volume to driving pressure; inspiratory time-normalised aCrs (aCrs1) and respiratory rate-normalised aCrs (aCrs2). Persistent significant inspiratory support requirement was defined as DP >5 cmH2O.

Statistical analysis

Parameter distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables are presented as number (percentages), and continuous variables as median (IQR). Subgroups (DP ≤5 cmH2O vs >5 cmH2O at 12 months) were compared using Mann-Whitney, χ2 or Cohran Q tests. For paired comparisons, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test or Friedman analysis of variance by ranks were used.

The diagnostic performance of physiological parameters in identifying patients without persistent inspiratory support requirement was evaluated by receiver operating curve (ROC) analysis, with optimal thresholds determined via Youden’s index.

Correlations between improvement in clinical parameters were assessed using Spearman’s rank-order correlation. Missing data were excluded in pairwise fashion. All tests were two-tailed, statistical significance was set at p value < 0.05. Data analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics V.30.0 (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this study. The research was based on clinical data and methodological considerations, with limited external participation. Outcome measures and recruitment protocols adhered to standard clinical guidelines.

Results

Study participants

100 OHS patients were screened for eligibility. Of these, 40 individuals were enrolled, and 39 were included in the final analysis (see online supplemental figure A). One patient died during the study period, resulting in an observed overall mortality rate of 2.5%. No patients were lost to follow-up because of treatment discontinuation.

Missing data were <7% for all recorded parameters. Baseline characteristics for the entire cohort are detailed in table 1. Patients were predominantly male, with marked obesity, in their fifth or sixth decade of life. Lung function tests mirrored restrictive ventilatory patterns, arterial bicarbonate levels indicated severe chronic hypoventilation and baseline AHI values were in the lower range. ESS reflected moderate daytime sleepiness (see in table 1 and online supplemental table A).

Changes in parameters reflecting inspiratory support requirement

EPAP decreased in four patients and increased in seven patients throughout the study period, and repeat PSG was necessary in one patient as per the follow-up parameters. Longitudinal analysis of ventilator parameters showed that EPAP requirement (based on AHI and patient comfort) remained stable (12 (9.9–14)–12 (11.3–14) cmH2O; p=1.000) over the follow-up period, however IPAP need decreased from 24.9 (21.3–27.9) to 22.7 (19.2–26) cmH2O, p=0.011 corresponding to a DP decline from 12.1 (9.6–14.9) to 9.9 (6.3–12.2) cmH2O, p<0.001. DP demonstrated a significant reduction over all time points (p=0.002). The most pronounced decreases were observed at 3 months, 6 months and 12 months compared with baseline (all p<0.01). Despite these improvements, 84.6% of patients continued to require substantial inspiratory support (DP >5 H2Ocm) at 12 months of treatment.

Correlation between clinical and mechanical improvement

When analysing longitudinal changes, we found that all parameters reflecting lung mechanics (DP, aCrs, aCrs1, aCrs2) showed significant correlations with changes in relevant PFT parameters, the physical function scale of SRI, but not with any ABG parameters, 6MWD or ESS values (see online supplemental table B and online supplemental figure B).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated longitudinal changes in inspiratory support requirements in OHS patients starting goal-directed long-term NIV following an episode of AHRF. We found that during 12 months of tailored ventilation using a volume-assured automated mode, inspiratory support requirements decreased significantly; however, the majority of patients continued to require relevant (>5 H2Ocm) inspiratory support at the end of the study. Goal-directed NIV was associated with high adherence and therapeutic efficacy in our cohort, with significant improvements observed in clinical outcomes, including ABG and PFT values, SRI, ESS scores and 6MWD.

The optimal treatment strategy for OHS remains debated. The rationale for providing inspiratory support is grounded in the well-documented obesity-related mechanical overload on the respiratory system, which contributes to chronic respiratory muscle fatigue.18 Excess adipose tissue impairs diaphragmatic excursion and limits chest wall expansion, resulting in elevated intra-abdominal and pleural pressures. These pathophysiological changes result in a restrictive spirometry pattern, characterised by reduced FVC and FEV1 with a preserved FEV1/FVC ratio.19 20

Despite this, current guidelines recommend CPAP as first-line therapy for stable OHS patients, while NIV is generally reserved for patients with low AHI, persistent hypoxemia despite CPAP or those requiring ventilatory support post-AHRF.10 This is based on randomised trials showing no significant advantage of NIV over CPAP in stable OHS patients.21,23 CPAP acts by increasing lung volumes through the prevention of small airway closure, thereby improving ventilation/perfusion (V’/Q’) mismatch and restoring functional residual capacity (FRC).24 25 In contrast, NIV enhances MV, allows fatigued respiratory muscles to rest and offers better V’/Q’ matching through additional inspiratory support.26 27 In fact, NIV has been shown to yield more pronounced improvements in PaCO₂, FEV₁ and 6MWD results, along with reductions in pulmonary hypertension.21 23 28

Importantly, most trials comparing CPAP and NIV in OHS excluded patients with probable (low AHI) or definite inspiratory support needs (eg, desaturation despite optimised CPAP), representing up to 20–25% of screened patients.22 23 Furthermore, these studies did not employ tailored NIV or assess the efficiency of inspiratory support.21,23 Notably, EPAP levels were consistently lower in NIV arms than CPAP arms by 3–4 cmH2O, and insufficient pressure support was often evident, as reflected by persistent hypoventilation or desaturation in up to 22% of patients.21,23

The clinical improvements observed in our cohort are considerable compared with the results achieved in the mentioned trials. For example, in the Howard study, 39.1% of patients remained hypercapnic after 1 month of therapy, whereas only 13.2% of our patients had PaCO₂ >45 mm Hg at that timepoint.21 This suggests that individualised NIV may lead to improved long-term outcomes in OHS.

Currently, no reliable methods exist to identify patients who require sustained respiratory support. Repeated advanced respiratory mechanical assessment, such as transpulmonary and transdiaphragmatic pressure measurements, help differentiate reversible fatigue from irreversible respiratory muscle impairment, though these methods are cumbersome.1129,31 Monitoring inspiratory support trends during long-term volume-assured ventilation may offer a less invasive alternative. Since MV and BMI remained stable throughout the study, the significant decrease in DP is most likely produced by the increased contribution of the respiratory muscle of patients, but might also be influenced by an improvement in respiratory system compliance. Decreased DP could theoretically be caused by decreased tidal volume, increased frequency or increased inspiratory time; however, parameters which corrected for these values (aCrs, aCrs1, aCrs2) also showed significant decrease throughout the study. This strengthens the assumption that inspiratory support needs decrease over time, irrespective of respiratory patterns.

Evidence regarding the long-term benefit and safety of transitioning from NIV to CPAP remains limited.32 33 Some studies suggest that continuation of NIV following AHRF at intensive care unit discharge is essential, but de-escalation of PAP therapy may be feasible within 2–3 months.34 This is based on the hypothesis that respiratory muscle dysfunction may be reversible with adequate unloading; however, the timeline for recovery is currently not well-understood. Our findings support this theory, showing that respiratory function improves with muscle unloading and that personalised NIV may reverse the need for ongoing inspiratory support in selected patients. Notably, patients who required reduced ventilatory support at 6 months demonstrated the strongest correlation with maintaining lower DP at 12 months. This suggests that respiratory muscle recovery may take longer than previously assumed, and CPAP trials after 2–3 months may be premature in some cases where later de-escalation might still be an option.

When comparing baseline characteristics across subgroups based on respiratory support requirement after 12 months, we found that lower DP, higher MV, greater aCrs and better sleep quality were associated with reduced support needs. Spirometry parameters (FVC, FEV₁, PEF, PIF) were also less impaired in this group, suggesting that persistent support requirements may reflect more advanced disease and irreversible muscle impairment rather than transient fatigue.

These findings highlight the potential value of clinical assessments focused on respiratory muscle function to distinguish OHS subtypes and guide individualised therapy. It is however important to note that none of the initial parameters reliably predicted minimal ventilatory support need after 1 year.

Our study has several limitations. The small sample size limited statistical power to identify predictors of inspiratory support requirements and potential confounders. Ventilator parameter requirements were based on target clinical parameters, rather than repeated PSG. Furthermore, respiratory muscle function and workload were not directly assessed but inferred from surrogate parameters. Although, to our knowledge, this is the first exploratory study in OHS to examine changes in inspiratory support needs, further research is warranted to validate our findings, directly measure muscle function and demonstrate that tailored inspiratory support improves clinical outcomes.

In conclusion, inspiratory support requirements decrease over time with goal-directed long-term NIV in OHS patients following AHRF but remain substantial in most patients. These findings highlight the importance of efficient respiratory muscle unloading and support a strategy of ‘hitting hard initially’ with sequential de-escalation from NIV to CPAP after 6 months if relevant ventilatory support requirement reduction is observed. Further trials should address whether this goal-directed approach and direct assessment of respiratory muscle function may help identify clinically relevant OHS subtypes, facilitate optimal PAP modality selection and improve long-term outcomes.
