Suspected microscopic ureteral injury detected by time-dependent CT attenuation elevation in ascites: A novel diagnostic insight from ultra-late dynamic imaging
Authors: Toshimitsu Tohya, Shizuki Daiho, Nana Maeda, Munekage Yamaguchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo report a case of suspected ureteral injury following gynecologic surgery, in which a temporal increase in computed tomography (CT) attenuation values of ascitic fluid in the pouch of Douglas observed during the ultra-late phase of dynamic CT imaging aided in diagnosis.CaseA woman in her 40s, gravida 0, para 0, with a history of total laparoscopic hysterectomy, bilateral oophorectomy, and left ovarian cystectomy performed two years earlier at another institution for uterine fibroids, presented to our department with severe lower abdominal pain. Imaging with CT and MRI revealed a 10 cm pelvic mass and hemoperitoneum, prompting emergency laparoscopic surgery. Intraoperatively, a ruptured right ovarian endometriotic cyst was identified, and a right oophorectomy was performed. On postoperative day five, transvaginal ultrasonography demonstrated an accumulation of ascites in the pouch of Douglas. Dynamic contrast-enhanced CT revealed a progressive increase in CT attenuation values of this fluid during the ultra-late phase, suggestive of a microscopic ureteral injury. Although retrograde pyeloureterography showed no definitive injury, the suspicion of subclinical ureteral damage led to the prophylactic placement of a right ureteral stent. Subsequently, the ascitic fluid resolved spontaneously. The stent was removed after 1.5 months, and the patient remained asymptomatic.DiscussionA progressive increase in CT attenuation of Douglas pouch ascites in the ultra-late phase of dynamic imaging may indicate subtle ureteral injury. Ureteral stenting can be considered a feasible intervention in such cases.

Introduction

Ureteral injuries are a recognized complication of pelvic surgery and exhibit considerable etiological heterogeneity.1, 2, 3 Pathological mechanisms include complete or partial transections, ligature-induced obstructions, kinking, shear forces, and thermal damage from energy devices.4 These injuries are often insidious and may escape intraoperative detection. Therefore, gynecologic surgeons must maintain a high index of suspicion and conduct thorough assessments when ureteral injury is possible. Herein, we report a case where dynamic contrast-enhanced CT revealed a delayed increase in ascitic fluid attenuation in the pouch of Douglas, leading to the suspicion of a microscopic ureteral injury. Placement of a ureteral stent resulted in a favorable clinical outcome.

Case presentation

A woman in her 40s (gravida 0, para 0) had previously undergone a total laparoscopic hysterectomy, bilateral oophorectomy, and left ovarian endometriotic cystectomy for uterine fibroids. She presented to our department with acute lower abdominal pain. Laboratory findings included a WBC count of 16,990/μL and a CRP of 0.88 mg/dL. CA125 was 13.0 U/mL (<35). Transvaginal ultrasonography and abdominal CT identified intra-abdominal hemorrhage extending into the upper abdomen and a hemorrhagic right ovarian cyst. MRI confirmed the presence of massive hemoperitoneum, raising suspicion of endometriotic cyst rupture (Fig. 1A). Laparoscopic exploration revealed approximately 700 mL of intraperitoneal hemorrhage. A ruptured right ovarian cyst with active bleeding was observed; thus, right oophorectomy was performed with preservation of the left ovary, considering potential recurrence and malignancy. The pelvic peritoneum adjacent to the right ovarian artery was incised to ensure the ureter's location, and the ovarian artery was coagulated and transected on the ovarian side (Fig. 1B). Postoperatively, drainage transitioned from hemorrhagic to serous. On postoperative day four, drainage decreased and the drain was removed. However, transvaginal ultrasound on postoperative day five identified anechoic fluid in the pouch of Douglas (Fig. 2). Since the right ovarian vessels running near the right ureter were transected during surgery, the fluid collection in the pouch of Douglas was considered possibly due to urine leakage associated with ureteral injury. To determine whether ureteral injury was present, a dynamic CT scan was performed. Although no overt signs of ureteral injury were apparent, dynamic CT demonstrated a gradual rise in attenuation of the Douglas pouch ascites—from 14.46 HU initially to 46.67 HU at 80 minutes post-contrast (Fig. 3)—indicating a possible leak of contrast medium, suggestive of microscopic ureteral injury or venous microbleeding. Retrograde pyeloureterography was performed but showed no gross ureteral damage (Fig. 4). Serum creatinine remained within normal limits (Creatinine: 0.53 mg/dL). Nevertheless, ureteral wall thinning or subtle injury could not be excluded, prompting prophylactic placement of a right ureteral stent. Only the right ovarian vessels were manipulated during the surgery, and since no procedures were performed on the left side of the pelvis, a stent was placed solely in the right ureter. The ascitic fluid resolved spontaneously within days following stenting (Fig. 5). Approximately six weeks later, the stent was removed. A follow-up dynamic CT scan showed no abnormalities, and the patient remained asymptomatic. Discussion

A U.S.-based review article reported that the incidence of iatrogenic ureteral injuries during surgery ranges from 0.5 % to 10 %, with gynecologic procedures accounting for the majority (52–82 %) of cases.1, 2, 3 Among these, hysterectomy (54 %), colorectal surgery (14 %), and ovarian tumor resections (8 %) were predominant causes.1 Ureteral trauma in gynecology is of particular concern due to its potentially significant patient and healthcare burden.

In this case, although overt ureteral injury was not confirmed, the dynamic CT finding of a time-dependent increase in ascitic attenuation values provided a unique diagnostic clue. Literature review revealed no prior reports of ureteral injury diagnosis based on this imaging feature in the field of gynecology, though similar patterns have been reported in intracerebral hemorrhage.5,6

Dynamic CT (including delayed imaging) is the initial modality of choice for detecting ureteral injury, while definitive diagnosis typically requires retrograde pyeloureterography.7 In the present case, although no injury was observed intraoperatively or on imaging, a subtle contrast leak was suggested by a 3.2-fold increase in CT values over 80 minutes. This could represent the early stage of a microscopic injury or microvascular bleeding. Collaborative decision-making among gynecology, urology, and radiology teams led to the prophylactic insertion of a ureteral stent, with resolution of the suspected leakage and favorable patient outcome.

Given the physical and psychological ramifications of ureteral injuries, early intervention—such as ureteral stenting—should be considered even when only subtle signs are present.8 To our knowledge, this is the first report in gynecologic literature suggesting that ultra-late phase dynamic CT imaging and progressive increases in Douglas pouch attenuation may aid in the diagnosis of microscopic ureteral injury. There may be some indeterminate cases of ureteral injuries.6

In conclusion, this case underscores the diagnostic utility of temporal increases in CT attenuation in ascitic fluid during ultra-late dynamic CT imaging in detecting potential microscopic ureteral injuries. Ureteral stenting may serve as a prudent management strategy when such injuries are suspected despite inconclusive conventional diagnostic modalities.
