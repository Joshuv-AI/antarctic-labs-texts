Blood steroid hormone profile and clinical outcomes following switching from metyrapone to osilodrostat in patients with Cushing disease
Authors: Kei Yokozeki, Hiraku Kameda, Aika Miya, Shigeki Jin, Kotaro Matoba, Akinobu Nakamura, Tatsuya Atsumi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Steroidogenesis inhibitors such as metyrapone and osilodrostat, target 11β-hydroxylase to inhibit cortisol synthesis, are used in inoperable or recurrent Cushing disease. While osilodrostat has been reported to be more effective at lower doses than metyrapone, there are only a few reports describing the difference between osilodrostat and metyrapone in clinical practice. In this study, we evaluated the changes in steroid hormone profiles and clinical outcomes in seven Cushing disease patients switched from metyrapone to osilodrostat after incomplete remission post-transsphenoidal sinus surgery. Three of the seven patients were using trilostane, which was discontinued at the same time as metyrapone. Steroid hormone concentrations, including cortisol, progesterone (Prog), pregnenolone (Preg), deoxycorticosterone, corticosterone, 17-hydroxyprogesterone (17Prog), 17-hydroxypregnenolone (17Preg), and 11-deoxycortisol, were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry. No adverse events occurred after switching to osilodrostat. Potassium and ACTH levels increased significantly, and dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate and testosterone levels decreased significantly. Cortisol levels did not change significantly, whereas the ratios reflecting CYP17A1 activity (17Preg/Preg and 17Prog/Prog) decreased significantly, suggesting superior inhibition of CYP17A1 with osilodrostat. Clinical improvements included reduced antihypertensive medication requirements, decreased masculinization, and resolved gastric discomfort. Different inhibition patterns of the steroid synthetic enzymes may explain the observed clinical outcomes between these drugs. These data suggest that in patients with Cushing disease receiving metyrapone, switching to osilodrostat may benefit cases with inadequate disease control and complications including hypertension, manifestation of masculinization, and gastric discomfort.

Introduction

Patients with Cushing disease have hypercortisolemia due to excessive ACTH production from corticotroph pituitary adenomas. If untreated, mortality rates are higher than those in the general population [1]; thus, accurate diagnosis and optimal treatment and management of complications are necessary to improve patient outcomes. The first-line treatment is surgical removal of the tumor, and medical therapy is used in cases where surgery is difficult for some reason, or in cases of residual or recurrent ACTH overproduction after surgery [2]. Drug treatment is classified into two main categories: the other targeting the suppression of ACTH production in the pituitary gland (pasireotide, cabergoline) and those targeting the suppression of cortisol production in the adrenal gland (metyrapone, osilodrostat, ketoconazole, and mitotane) [3].

Metyrapone and osilodrostat inhibit cortisol production by blocking 11β-hydroxylase (CYP11B1), the enzyme responsible for the final step in cortisol synthesis. Adverse events such as nausea, dizziness, hypokalemia, and adrenal insufficiency have been reported [4-8].

The mechanisms of action of metyrapone and osilodrostat are similar, but osilodrostat has been noted to be more effective at lower doses [6]. Although reports investigating testosterone, deoxycorticosterone (DOC), and 11-deoxycortisol (11-DOF) responses have accumulated for both metyrapone and osilodrostat [6, 9], there are only a few reports comparing steroid hormone profiles after the switch to osilodrostat from metyrapone and investigating changes in clinical findings [10]. Therefore, the present study was conducted to clarify the changes in steroid hormone profiles after switching from metyrapone to osilodrostat in a clinical setting.

Materials and Methods

Ethics approval

This study conformed to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare’s ethical guidelines on clinical research, and it was approved by the Hokkaido University Hospital Ethics Committee (clinical study reference number 024-0116).

Patients

This case study comprised seven patients diagnosed with Cushing disease who were not in remission after transsphenoidal sinus surgery. All subjects who were admitted between October 2021 and May 2024 and switched steroid hormone inhibitors from metyrapone to osilodrostat were enrolled.

Osilodrostat was started at 2 mg/day in accordance with its drug information following discontinuation of metyrapone. Three of the seven patients were using trilostane, and in these patients, trilostane was discontinued at the same time as metyrapone. The dose of osilodrostat was titrated up until the normalization of urine free cortisol.

Blood steroid hormone profile and enzyme activity

Blood samples and urinary cortisol were collected while on metyrapone just prior to switching to osilodrostat and during the period when hypercortisolism improved with osilodrostat.

All patients were supplemented with hydrocortisone to prevent adrenal insufficiency due to anticortisol treatment. Blood samples were taken in the early morning before hydrocortisone administration. ACTH levels and testosterone levels were measured by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Roche, Penzberg, Germany). Dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEA-S) levels were measured by chemiluminescent enzyme immunoassay (Beckman Coulter, Inc., Brea, CA, USA).

Blood steroid hormones (cortisol, aldosterone, pregnenolone [Preg], progesterone [Prog], DOC, corticosterone, 17-OH-pregnenolone [17Preg], 17-OH-progesterone [17Prog], and 11-DOF) were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry at the request of the Department of Forensic Medicine, Faculty of Medicine and Graduate School of Medicine, Hokkaido University. The analysis of blood steroid hormones was conducted using the method described by Jin et al. [11]. Sample preparation involved solid-phase extraction columns, and measurements were performed without derivatization.

The instrumentation used in this study was identical to that previously described [12]. The chromatographic separation was achieved using a Hypersil GOLD column (50 × 2.1 mm I.D., 5 μm; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) maintained at 35°C. The mobile phase, flowing at 0.2 mL/min, consisted of a linear gradient of 0.1% formic acid in water (mobile phase A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (mobile phase B). Mass spectrometric detection was performed using electrospray ionization in positive ion mode, employing multiple reaction monitoring. The detection parameters for each steroid were as follows: cortisol (m/z 363.0→121.1), aldosterone (m/z 361.0→343.1), Preg (m/z 299.0→281.2), Prog (m/z 315.0→97.2), DOC (m/z 331.0→97.2), corticosterone (m/z 347.0→121.1), 17Preg (m/z 315.0→297.3), 17Prog (m/z 331.0→97.2), and 11-DOF (m/z 347.0→109.2). The internal standard, 6α-methylprednisolone-d3, was monitored at m/z 378.2→360.3. Quantification was performed using the internal standard method.

The activity of steroidogenic enzymes was assessed by the downstream/upstream steroid ratio [13]. A lower ratio indicates lower enzyme activity.

Statistics

Wilcoxon’s signed rank test was performed using GraphPad Prism 10.3.0 (GraphPad Software, MA, USA) to analyze the data. Continuous data are expressed as medians (minimum value–maximum value). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Characteristics of patients

Five patients with Cushing disease and two with cyclic Cushing disease were analyzed. One case of cyclic Cushing disease was switched to osilodrostat during a period of excessive ACTH and cortisol secretion. The other case was switched during a period of stability.

The reason for switching steroid hormone inhibitors was either due to the side effects associated with metyrapone or to difficulty managing hypercortisolemia. The patients were administered a fixed dose of hydrocortisone throughout the course of switching steroid hormone inhibitors.

No remarkable adverse events were observed after the switch to osilodrostat. The changes in steroid hormone profiles and serum potassium levels before and after the drug change in each case are shown in Supplementary Tables 1–3.

Serum potassium levels were increased significantly after the switch to osilodrostat (median 3.7 quartile range [3.4–4.4] mEq/L in the metyrapone group vs. 4.2 [3.7–4.6] mEq/L in the osilodrostat group, p < 0.05). ACTH levels also increased significantly after the change in drugs (78.5 [25.8–122.8] pg/mL vs. 106 [34.8–160] pg/mL, p < 0.05). Urine free cortisol did not differ significantly. DHEA-S was significantly decreased (144 [84–382] μg/dL vs. 61 [31–90] μg/dL, p < 0.05), as was testosterone (89 [24.4–263] ng/dL vs. 19 [6.07–66] ng/dL, p < 0.05) (Fig. 1). Testosterone levels were also found to be significantly decreased when evaluated in women only.

There was no significant difference in cortisol levels before and after switching from metyrapone to osilodrostat. Preg did not change significantly before or after the drug change. Prog increased significantly after the drug change (13.35 [7.4–38.04] ng/mL in the metyrapone group vs. 15.2 [8.2–33.69] ng/mL in the osilodrostat group). Case 1 underwent hysterectomy with bilateral adnexectomy for uterine cancer. The other cases were also postmenopausal, so the relationship between Prog and the menstrual cycle was not considered to be significant in this study.

17Preg and 17Prog decreased significantly after switching to osilodrostat (27.1 [4.9–69.8] ng/mL vs. 9.6 [3.9–21.7] ng/mL, 2.6 [1.7–8.2] ng/mL vs. 1.47 [0.8–2.3] ng/mL, all p < 0.05). DOC, corticosterone, and aldosterone did not change significantly before or after the drug change (Fig. 1).

The Prog/Preg ratio (3β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 [HSD3B2] activity) increased significantly after switching to osilodrostat. The 17Prog/17Preg ratio (HSD3B2 activity) and DOC/Prog ratio (CYP21A2 activity) did not change significantly before or after the drug change, but the 17Preg/Preg ratio (CYP17A1 activity) and 17Prog/Prog ratio (CYP17A1 activity) decreased significantly after the drug change. The corticosterone/DOC and cortisol/11-DOF ratios (CYP11B1 activity) were not altered by changing drugs (Fig. 2).

Androgenic manifestations (alopecia and hirsutism in Case 1; beard growth and acne in Case 7) were improved after switching medication. In the two cases with masculinization, DHEA-S decreased from 263 μg/dL to 19 μg/dL in Case 1 and from 100 μg/dL to 5.2 μg/dL in Case 7 because of the drug change. Gastric discomfort in Cases 1 and 6 were also improved after the drug change. Clinical symptoms after switching to osilodrostat

With the exception of Case 3, each patient’s blood pressure remained below 130/80 mmHg while on metyrapone because of antihypertensive medication. After the switch to osilodrostat, systolic blood pressure was less than 100 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure was less than 60 mmHg in all cases except Case 4; therefore, the antihypertensive drug was reduced as necessary. In Case 3, blood pressure was 160/100 mmHg while on metyrapone, and the patient had difficulty managing her blood pressure. However, after the switch to osilodrostat, her blood pressure decreased to 99/65 mmHg, and the antihypertensive medication was also reduced.

Discussion

This study showed that switching from metyrapone to osilodrostat significantly reduced 17Preg, 17Prog, DHEA-S, and testosterone levels, as well as their metabolites. Furthermore, 11-DOF also showed a decreasing trend (p = 0.078), with improvements in clinical symptoms including masculinization signs and hypertension.

Although there have been several reports comparing the efficacy of metyrapone and osilodrostat [14, 15], this is the first report to examine both the changes in steroid hormone profiles and the detailed clinical consequences, focusing on cases after the switch to osilodrostat from metyrapone in real-world clinical practice.

Both metyrapone and osilodrostat inhibit CYP11B1, but hypertension, hypokalemia, and increased androgens are often a problem with the use of metyrapone because of elevations in the cortisol precursors DOC and 11-DOF as well as their upstream metabolites [2, 3]. Although hypertension, hypokalemia, and increased androgens have also been reported in patients receiving osilodrostat [3, 6, 16], the documented masculinizing symptoms were mild and did not lead to treatment discontinuation in any cases [16, 17]. Blood pressure has also been reported to be decreased compared with that with metyrapone [18]. In patients with Cushing disease suffering from the side effects of metyrapone, such as hypertension and masculinizing signs, a change to osilodrostat may be effective in improving symptoms.

In the present study, the lack of significant differences in serum cortisol or urinary free cortisol suggests that osilodrostat has at least the same level of cortisol suppression compared with metyrapone. Moreover, the 17Preg/Preg and 17Prog/Prog ratios decreased significantly after switching from metyrapone to osilodrostat, suggesting that osilodrostat may suppress CYP17A1 more effectively than metyrapone. The suppression of CYP17A1 by osilodrostat may have resulted in a significant decrease in 17Prog and 17Preg, and their metabolite 11-DOF showed a decreasing trend (p = 0.078) after the drug change. The improvement in clinical findings, including the increased serum potassium, decreased antihypertensive medication, and improved signs of masculinization, was attributed to the suppression of CYP17A1 by osilodrostat and the consequent decrease in 11-DOF. In addition, among the five cases (Cases 1, 3, 5, 6, and 7) in which antihypertensive medications were decreased, four cases (Cases 1, 3, 5, and 7) showed a decrease in serum cortisol and urinary free cortisol after switching to osilodrostat, although the difference was not significant. These results do not exclude the possibility that osilodrostat was more effective than metyrapone in suppressing CYP11B1. In four of seven cases (Cases 3, 4, 5, and 6), DOC also decreased before and after the drug change, which may have also contributed to the decrease in antihypertensive medication use and the increase in serum potassium levels. The improvement in masculinizing signs was also attributed to the suppression of CYP17A1 and the decrease in testosterone after the change from metyrapone to osilodrostat.

On the other hand, the action of trilostane inhibits HSD3B2, which is involved in the conversion of Preg to Prog and 17Preg to 17Prog, thereby suppressing cortisol production. Therefore, the use of trilostane may affect the steroid hormone profile. In the three patients who had been using trilostane in this study, when trilostane use was discontinued and switched to osilodrostat, the significant differences in Prog, 17Preg, 17Prog, 17Preg/Preg ratio and 17Prog/Prog before and after the drug switch disappeared (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2). In four patients who had not been using trilostane, the similar results were observed (Supplementary Figs. 3, 4). Since no differences in changes in steroid hormone profiles were observed between the groups regardless of trilostane use, it is presumed that trilostane administration did not influence the hormonal changes observed upon the drug switch in this study. However, due to the small number of cases, definitive conclusions cannot be drawn.

Furthermore, the inhibition of HSD3B2 also inhibits the conversion of DHEA to androstenedione. As a result, DHEA-S levels increase during trilostane administration and decrease with discontinuation. This condition suggests that the discontinuation of trilostane may contribute to the improvement in masculinization. However, in the present study, after switching to osilodrostat, not only DHEA-S but also testosterone levels decreased significantly, which may be because of the CYP17A1 suppression effect of osilodrostat. This response was also beneficial in improving masculinization.

In vitro experiments showed that osilodrostat may suppress CYP17A1 with less accumulation of 11-DOF and testosterone compared with that with metyrapone [19, 20]. Previous reports on steroid hormone profiles under metyrapone and osilodrostat in real patients have indicated the suppression of CYP17A1 by osilodrostat [10], supporting the results of the present study. Our study included only patients switched from metyrapone to osilodrostat, examined a wide variety of steroid hormone precursors, and reported detailed changes in clinical findings in addition to those noted in the previous studies.

After switching to osilodrostat, a significant increase in Prog levels was observed, leading to an elevated Prog/Preg ratio. This phenomenon may be attributable to the discontinuation of trilostane in three patients who had been concurrently treated with trilostane and metyrapone prior to the switch to osilodrostat.

Previous reports have shown that osilodrostat significantly suppressed cortisol compared with levels with metyrapone [14, 19], and a systematic review comparing the effects of osilodrostat and metyrapone concluded that the cortisol suppression was significantly stronger with osilodrostat [15]. A few cases in the present study showed cyclic cortisol fluctuations, resulting in excessive cortisol secretion while titrating osilodrostat, and hydrocortisone was also administered to prevent adrenal insufficiency while taking osilodrostat, which may explain the differences from previous reports. Furthermore, the half-life of metyrapone is 2 hours [21], whereas that of osilodrostat is 4 hours [7, 8], and the timing of the medication administration and blood sample collection may have influenced the difference in serum cortisol levels in the previous report. Further studies eliminating these effects on the difference in cortisol suppression between metyrapone and osilodrostat are awaited.

In this study, there were no adverse events associated with the change from metyrapone to osilodrostat. Although osilodrostat has been reported to have a higher incidence of adrenal insufficiency than that with metyrapone [10], this effect was prevented in all patients in the present study by switching drugs with hydrocortisone.

ACTH was significantly increased after the switch to osilodrostat, and previous reports showed a trend of increased ACTH with osilodrostat, although the difference was not significant [6, 10]. This may reflect negative feedback stemming from cortisol suppression.

This study has some limitations. First, it was based on a small number of cases (seven), obscuring the statistical differences. Second, this study included both well-managed and poorly managed cortisol levels prior to the change to osilodrostat, presumably affecting the clinical findings before the drug change. Third, Case 4 was treated with a small dose of osilodrostat for a shorter period of time than the other cases, and the limitation of this study is that the effect of the duration of osilodrostat administration was not adequately evaluated. Finally, the inability to clearly determine how trilostane use may have affected this analysis is one of the limitations of this study.

Conclusion

In the present study, metyrapone and osilodrostat have different patterns of steroidogenic enzyme inhibition and may have different clinical effects. In patients with problems such as hypertension, hypokalemia, and masculinizing signs while using metyrapone, a change to osilodrostat may be effective in improving symptoms (Graphical Abstract).
