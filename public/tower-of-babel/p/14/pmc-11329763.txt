Gypenoside inhibits gastric cancer proliferation by suppressing glycolysis via the Hippo pathway
Authors: Li Pan, Bingxue Lan, Shoumin Li, Yong Jin, Miaomiao Cui, Ying Xia, Sixi Wei, Hai Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastric cancer (GC) remains a global disease with a high mortality rate, the lack of effective treatments and the high toxicity of side effects are primary causes for its poor prognosis. Hence, urgent efforts are needed to find safe and effective therapeutic strategies. Gypenoside (Gyp) is a widely used natural product that regulates blood glucose to improve disease progression with few toxic side effects. Given the crucial role of abnormal glycometabolism in driving tumor malignancy, it is important to explore the association between Gyp and glycometabolism in GC and understand the mechanism of action by which Gyp influences glycometabolism. In this study, we demonstrated that Gyp suppresses GC proliferation and migration both in vitro and in vivo. We identified that Gyp suppresses the malignant progression of GC by inhibiting glycolysis using network pharmacology and metabolomics. Transcriptome analysis revealed that the Hippo pathway is a key regulator of glycolysis by Gyp in GC. Furthermore, Gyp induced upregulation of LATS1/2 proteins, leading to increased YAP phosphorylation and decreased TAZ protein expression. The YAP agonist XMU-MP-1 rescued the inhibitory effect of Gyp on GC proliferation by reversing glycolysis. These findings confirmed that Gyp inhibits GC proliferation by targeting glycolysis through the Hippo pathway. Our study examined the role of Gyp in the malignant progression of GC, explored its therapeutic prospects, elucidated a mechanism by which Gyp suppresses GC proliferation through interference with the glycolytic process, thus providing a potential novel therapeutic strategy for GC patients.Keywords: Gypenoside, Gastric cancer, Metabolism, Glycolysis, ProliferationSubject terms: Cancer, Drug discovery, Oncology

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a significant global health problem, accounting for 5.6% of newly diagnosed cancer cases and standing as the fourth major contributor to cancer-related deaths1. The current conventional treatments for GC include surgery, chemotherapy and radiotherapy. However, these approaches exhibit notable drawbacks, including high rates of recurrence and metastasis, diminished quality of survival, and heightened toxicity2. Consequently, there is an urgent need to explore and develop safer and more effective treatment strategies.

Metabolic reprogramming is a hallmark of cancer, with the Warburg effect serving as the classical foundation for the cancer metabolic reprogramming pathway3,4. This theory posits that tumor cells sustain malignant progression by fostering aerobic glycolysis achieved through alterations in a number of metabolic features5,6. Glucose is a vital energy source for cell growth, undergoing glycolysis to generate lactate. The accumulation of lactate creates a conducive acidic microenvironment that facilitates tumor progression. Oxygen glycolysis is the main way for cancer cells to obtain energy. Disrupting the glycolysis process in tumor cells could offer a novel and promising therapeutic approach for GC treatment7,8.

In recent years, accumulated studies have explored the potential of targeting metabolism to influence cancer progression9. Natural products are gaining recognition as a therapeutic modality to modulate metabolic reprogramming and serve as convenient natural remedies10,11. Some natural products have been proven to exhibit superior inhibitory effects on cancer progression and have shown promise in mitigating toxic side effects induced by radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or targeted therapies12. Furthermore, they exhibit better biocompatibility compared to conventional therapeutic drugs13–15. Gypenoside (Gyp) is the primary active ingredient in Gynostemma pentaphyllum, and it plays a crucial role in regulating glycometabolism disorders16. In animal studies Gyp has been shown to improve diabetic Alzheimer's disease by modulating brain glucose uptake17. Although the anti-tumor activity of Gyp has also been confirmed18,19, the molecular mechanisms by which Gyp regulates glycometabolism pathways still remain unknown.

Studies have revealed complex and tight connections between oncogenic signaling and abnormal metabolic patterns20. The Hippo pathway plays a crucial role in biological processes, and its aberrant activation is closely involved in cancer development. The core components of the Hippo pathway include the kinase cascade reaction, involving MST1/2, LATS1/2, YAP, and TAZ21. YAP/TAZ act as transcriptional coactivators, and their abnormal activation fosters the malignant proliferation of tumor cells22,23. It has been demonstrated that the Hippo pathway is closely related to glucose homeostasis, and aerobic glycolysis enhances YAP/TAZ transcriptional activity24,25. Furthermore, GLUT1, a member of the glucose transporter protein family, has been shown as a target of the Hippo pathway26. In this study, we utilized the HGC-27 and AGS cell lines for in vitro experiments and established a mouse xenograft tumor model in vivo. Through comprehensive analyses integrating network pharmacology, metabolomics, and transcriptomics, we investigated the relationship between Gyp and glycometabolism in GC, along with its underlying mechanism, aiming to provide a novel approach and reference for GC treatment.

Results

Gyp significantly hinders the proliferation of GC cells

To assess the inhibitory impact of Gyp on GC cells, we exposed HGC-27 and AGS cells to various concentrations (0, 50, 75, 100, 125, 150 μM) of Gyp and examined changes in cell viability after 48h. The results demonstrated a pronounced sensitivity of GC cells to Gyp, revealing a concentration-dependent reduction in cell viability with increasing drug concentration. The IC50 values for HGC-27 and AGS were 89.4 μM and 109.3 μM, respectively (Fig. 1A,B).

(C,D) The number of cell colonies for HGC-27 and AGS cells was determined after Gyp treatment through clone formation assay. (E,F) The expression of PCNA, Bax, and Bcl2 proteins in HGC-27 and AGS cells was examined following Gyp treatment using Western blot analysis. Data are presented as the mean ± SD of expression levels from three independent experiments (*p < 0.05, ***p < 0.001).

Additionally, clone formation assays indicated a noteworthy reduction in the number of cell colonies in both GC cell lines as Gyp concentrations increased (Fig. 1C,D). Meanwhile, the expression of proliferative and apoptotic proteins was assessed using Western blot analysis. The results indicated that Gyp downregulated PCNA and Bcl2 expression while upregulating Bax expression in GC cells (Fig. 1E,F). Collectively, these findings suggested that Gyp effectively prevents proliferation and induces apoptosis in GC cells.

Gyp effectively inhibits migration and invasion in GC cells

The wound healing assay demonstrated a notable attenuation in the migratory ability of HGC-27 and AGS cells, in the presence of Gyp (Fig. 2A,B). Meanwhile, transwell assays showed that Gyp treatment significantly inhibited the migration and invasion ability of GC cells (Fig. 2C,D). Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT) is pivotal in facilitating the metastasis of tumor cells27. EMT-related molecules were examined by Western blot analysis, revealing an upregulation in the expression of E-cadherin protein in HGC-27 and AGS cells, along with a decrease in N-cadherin and Vimentin protein levels after Gyp treatment (Fig. 2E,F). These findings indicated that Gyp effectively inhibits migration and invasion in GC cells.

Gyp effectively suppresses xenograft tumor growth in vivo

After successfully demonstrating the inhibitory effect of Gyp on GC cells in vitro, we proceeded to assess its impact using HGC-27 xenograft tumor model in vivo. Notably, compared with the control group, tumor growth was significantly decelerated in the Gyp-treated group (Fig. 3A,B), and tumor volume and weight were also decreased significantly (Fig. 3C,D). Throughout the experiment, no significant variance in body weight was observed between the two groups of mice (Fig. 3E). Furthermore, Ki67 staining of tumor tissue in the Gyp-treated group exhibited a substantial reduction as expected (Fig. 3F). H&E staining of the heart, liver, and kidneys showed no discernible damage (Fig. 3G), and the serum concentrations of ALT, AST, BUN, and CREA were within the normal range in both groups (Supplementary Table S3). These results collectively affirmed the robust anti-cancer effects of Gyp with minimal toxic side effects in vivo.

Network pharmacological analysis reveals targeted metabolism as a potential mechanism for inhibiting malignancy in GC by Gyp

In this section, we employed network pharmacology to unravel the potential mechanisms underlying the inhibition of malignancy in GC by Gyp. We initially investigated the correlation between the target genes of Gyp and those associated with GC. By leveraging the PharmMapper and UniProt databases, 171 targets of Gyp were identified. Simultaneously, 1850 GC-associated genes were screened from the OMIM, Drugbank, and Genecards databases. Intersected these genes using VENNY software, 56 common targets were identified (Fig. 4A). Based on these identified 56 common targets, we constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network (Fig. 4B) with the STRING database, and Metascape and David databases were employed for KEGG (Fig. 4C) and GO (Fig. 4D–F) enrichment analyses. Interestingly, our analyses suggested that the malignant progression process of GC is closely associated with metabolic abnormalities.

Gyp hinders the malignant progression of GC by inhibiting glycolysis

To better understand the abnormal metabolic pathways in GC, a metabolite analysis of HGC-27 cells was performed using UPLC-QTOF-MS. The total ion chromatogram (TIC) of the quality control (QC) samples was utilized to detect overlapping spectra, and the results showed that the response intensities and retention times of the peaks were basically overlapped, indicating the minimal variation caused by instrumental errors during the whole experiment (Fig. 5A). The PCA models obtained through sevenfold cross-validation exhibited high intra-group aggregation and significant separation between groups, indicating a reliable model with notable differences between groups (Fig. 5B). Subsequently, orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was employed to identify overall differences in metabolic profiles between groups and confirm distinctive metabolites (Fig. 5C). Enrichment analysis of KEGG pathways indicated that the inhibition of GC malignant progression by Gyp is closely related to glycometabolism (Fig. 5D). Supernatants from HGC-27 cells were collected for glucose and lactate assays following Gyp treatment. The results revealed an increase in glucose concentration (Fig. 5E) and a decrease in lactate concentration in the treatment group compared to the control group (Fig. 5F). While RNA-seq suggested the down-regulation of glycolysis-related genes after Gyp treatment (Fig. 5G). Furthermore, qPCR analysis revealed that the mRNA levels of glycolysis-related genes such as G6PD, PKM2, LDHA, GLUT1, and PFK were significantly reduced in HGC-27 cells after Gyp treatment. (Fig. 5H). The above findings suggested that Gyp could retard the malignant progression of GC by inhibiting glycolysis.

Gyp inhibits GC glycolysis via the Hippo pathway

To elucidate the mechanism of Gyp-induced inhibition of glycolysis in GC, we conducted RNA-seq analysis following Gyp treatment of HGC-27 cells. The results unveiled substantial alterations in gene expression (Fig. 6A), with 301 genes up-regulated and 193 genes down-regulated (Fig. 6B). The KEGG enrichment analysis found that down-regulated genes were significantly enriched in the Hippo pathway (Fig. 6C), and the GO enrichment analysis was shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. The proteins associated with this pathway were validated through Western blot analysis. The findings demonstrated that Gyp could elevate the expression of LATS1/2 and phosphorylated YAP, concurrently reducing TAZ protein expression in GC cells (Fig. 6D). Concurrently, we conducted rescue experiments using the YAP agonist XMU-MP-1. The CCK8 assay revealed that XMU-MP-1 reversed the inhibitory effect of Gyp on HGC-27 cells (Fig. 6E). Results from the clone formation assay indicated a significant reduction in the number of colonies in HGC-27 cells after Gyp treatment, and this reduction could be rescued in the presence of XMU-MP-1 (Fig. 6F). Additionally, assays for glucose and lactate levels in the cell supernatants were conducted to elucidate the effect of XMU-MP-1 on Gyp-induced inhibition of glycolysis in GC cells. The results demonstrated that XMU-MP-1 could reverse the inhibitory effect of Gyp on glycolysis in GC cells (Fig. 6G,H). These findings collectively indicated that Gyp inhibits GC glycolysis through the Hippo pathway.

Discussion

Finding safe and effective treatment options for GC remains a pressing issue. In addition to improving the course of the disease by regulating blood glucose28, Gyp has garnered significant attention in recent years for its demonstrated anti-tumor potential in lung, colorectal, and esophageal cancer cells19,29,30. Particularly, multiple mechanisms have been suggested to underpin the anticancer impacts of Gyp, including the induction of apoptosis and autophagy in tumor cells, as well as the inhibition of proliferation through targeting the AKT/GSK3β/β-catenin signaling pathway31,32. In view of previous studies, we demonstrated that Gyp inhibits GC glycolysis by modulating the Hippo pathway through in vivo and in vitro experiments. Our study provides a possible avenue for effective treatment of GC.

Cellular metabolism reprogramming is a critical feature of cancer, essential for tumor growth and migration, including within GC33,34. The malignant progression of GC is closely intertwined with glycometabolism, reprogramming of this process is considered a crucial mechanism that enhances the proliferation of GC cells35. As a crucial component of glycometabolism, glycolysis plays a central role in the overall metabolic network, representing the fundamental metabolic pathway through which tumor cells acquire energy36. The Warburg effect, discovered in the twentieth century, elucidates that altering the primary metabolism of glucose from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis is a key pathway for tumor cells to acquire energy37. The pharmacological effects of Gyp prompted us to explore its relationship with metabolism using network pharmacology. Our network pharmacological analysis highlighted a substantial correlation between Gyp and metabolic reprogramming in malignancy of GC, emphasizing the need for further exploration of the relationship between Gyp and metabolism in GC. Cancer-induced metabolic alterations enable the development of customized therapeutic strategies via metabolomics, leading to enhanced treatment outcomes in scientific research38. Therefore, we employed a non-targeted metabolomics approach to assess the metabolic profile of GC cells and the results indicated that Gyp alters glycometabolism in GC cells. Subsequently, we observed a reduction in glucose consumption, lactate production, and mRNA level of glycolysis-related genes in GC cells treated with Gyp. These data further suggested that Gyp inhibits the glycolytic process in GC cells.

Combining metabolomics and transcriptomics provides a unique opportunity to dissect alterations in the local tumor microenvironment, as well as to discover new tumor targets and pathways associated with drug resistance39,40. Normally, a series of enzymatic reactions of signaling pathways are intricately linked to biological processes, and the Hippo pathway plays a pivotal role in regulating various biological processes. The Hippo-YAP signaling pathway, primarily comprised of the Mst1/2 and Lats1/2 kinase cascade along with the transducers YAP and its paralog TAZ, represents a promising target for cancer prevention and treatment41,42. Upon activation of Mst1/2, Lats1/2 phosphorylates YAP, resulting in the inhibition of nuclear accumulation of YAP/TAZ43. In the context of cancer, dysregulation of Hippo pathway activity disrupts normal cell growth, resulting in abnormal cell differentiation and proliferation. Moreover, crosstalk between the Hippo signaling pathway and metabolic pathways can diminish the sensitivity of anticancer drugs44. Our more in-depth analysis using transcriptomics revealed that Gyp potentially suppresses GC proliferation and migration by modulating the Hippo pathway. Meanwhile, analysis via Western blot demonstrated that Gyp inhibits Hippo pathway signaling by upregulating the expression of LATS1/2 and phosphorylated YAP proteins, while concurrently downregulating the expression of TAZ proteins. The pivotal role of the Hippo pathway effector YAP in glucose metabolism has prompted extensive studies45,46. In a study by Li et al., it was found that the Hippo-YAP signalling pathway plays a key role in reprogramming glucose metabolism in breast cancer and influences the immune landscape of breast cancer47. Cox et al. showed that YAP is a new regulator influencing glucose metabolism, facilitating the synthesis of nucleotides essential for organ growth48. Furthermore, it was demonstrated that YAP was able to activate the transcription of genes involved in glucose metabolism, thereby accelerating glycolysis in tumor cells49,50. These findings emphasise the importance of YAP in regulating glucose homeostasis. The results of our study indicated that the YAP agonist XMU-MP-1 could rescue the inhibitory effect of Gyp on GC proliferation and glycolytic processes. We demonstrated Gyp inhibits the glycolysis of GC through the Hippo pathway. However, further research is required to investigate how Gyp regulates the expression of glycolytic-related genes G6PD, PKM2, LDHA, GLUT1, and PFK via the Hippo pathway. Meanwhile, future in vivo experiments should be refined to include the detection of mRNA and protein levels of the glycolysis-related genes mentioned above.

In conclusion, our study elucidated a mechanism by which Gyp suppresses GC proliferation through interference with the glycolytic process via the Hippo pathway, enhancing the potential for more effective intervention in the malignancy of GC. Gyp is expected to be a potential and effective drug for the treatment of GC.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Gypenoside (purity > 99%) was procured from Selleck (Houston, TX, USA), and the working solution was prepared by dissolving it in DMSO to achieve a 100 mM concentration. XMU-MP-1 was procured from MCE (Shanghai, China), and the working solution was prepared by dissolving it in DMSO to achieve a 10 mM concentration. Lactate assay kit was acquired from Zybio (Chongqing, China), while the Glucose assay kit was sourced from Maccura (Chengdu, China).

Cell culture

The human gastric cancer cell lines AGS and HGC-27 were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Biological Industries, KBH, IL) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco, NY, USA). The cultures were sustained in cell culture dishes within a controlled humidity environment with 5% CO2 at 37 ℃.

CCK8 assay

Adjust the density of HGC-27 and AGS cells to 3 × 103/well. Following a 48 h treatment with Gyp, 10 μL of CCK8 reagent (MCE, Shanghai, China) was added into each well. Subsequently, the absorbance of the samples at 450 nm was measured using an enzyme-labeled instrument (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) following a 2 h incubation.

Colony formation assay

HGC-27 and AGS cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 5 × 102 cells. After 48 h of incubation, Gyp was added to wells at concentrations of 75 μM and 100 μM, respectively. The medium with the respective Gyp concentration was refreshed every 3 days. Following a 12-day incubation period, cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and the number of clonal spots was quantified using Image J software.

Wound healing assay

Following 48 h of Gyp treatment, HGC-27 and AGS cells were plated in 6-well plates and incubated 12 h, then cell scratches were created using a 200 μL pipette tip. After replacing the 10% FBS medium with 1% FBS medium, incubation was continued and photographs were taken. The extent of the scratched area was analyzed using Image J software.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Gyp-treated cells were diluted to a cell density of approximately 5 × 104 cells per well. For the invasion assay, the upper chamber was exposed to Matrigel (Corning, NY, USA) and incubated at 37 ℃ for 12 h. The cells, suspended in 200 μL of serum-free medium, were introduced into the upper chamber, with an additional 650 μL of medium containing 20% FBS being added to the lower chamber. For the migration assay, following resuspension in 200 μL of serum-free medium, the cells were seeded into the upper chamber. Simultaneously, 650 μL of medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After 48 h of incubation, cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde and subjected to 0.1% crystal violet staining. Cells undergoing migration or invasion were subsequently observed and quantified using a microscope.

Quantitative reverse transcription PCR

GC cells treated with Gyp for 48 h were harvested, and total RNA was extracted from each cell group using the TRIzol method (Invitrogen, CA, USA). The extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScriptTM RT reagent Kit (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan). The reaction system was prepared according to the ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Relative expression of the target genes was calculated using the 2–△△Ct method with HPRT as the reference gene. The primer sequences for RT-qPCR are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Network pharmacology analysis

Gyp-related targets were sourced from the PharmMapper database (http://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/), while GC-related genes were acquired through OMIM (https://www.omim.org/), Drugbank (https://www.drugbank.ca/), and Genecards (https://www.genecards.org/) databases. Metascape (https://metascape.org/) and David (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/) databases were utilized to conduct GO and KEGG enrichment analysis. The data screening principle adhered to the standard of p value < 0.05.

RNA-seq

HGC-27 cells were exposed to 100 μM Gyp for 48 h. Cell lysis was carried out for total RNA extraction using Trizol reagent. The transcriptomic data was generated using Illumina Novaseq technology and further analyzed utilizing the DAVID database. The data screening principle adhered to the standard of p value < 0.05.

Metabolomics analysis

1 × 107 HGC-27 cells were harvested for non-targeted metabolomics analysis. Then the cells were gently washed with PBS at 37 °C. To eliminate proteins and extract the metabolites, cold methanol/acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) was added. The mixture was then moved to a fresh centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 14,000g for 5 min at 4 °C to obtain the supernatant. The obtained supernatants were taken for LC–MS analysis. Analysis was conducted on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.7 μm). The flow rate was set at 0.4 mL/min, with the column temperature maintained at 25 °C, and the autosampler temperature set to 5 °C. An injection volume of 2 μL was utilized, and the mass spectrometer functioned in both positive and negative ion modes.

Concentration measurement of lactate and glucose

8 × 105 HGC-27 cells were plated in 6-cm dishes. After 12 h, the experimental group received treatment with either Gyp or XMU-MP-1, while a DMSO control group was established. Following 48 h of incubation, the cell supernatants were collected, and the glucose and lactate concentrations in the supernatants were measured using a glucose assay kit (Maccura, Chengdu, China) and a lactate assay kit (Zybio, Chongqing, China) on a fully automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi, TKY, Japan). Concurrently, the total protein concentration of the samples was determined by the BCA method. The concentrations of glucose and lactate were normalized according to the total protein concentration of the samples to avoid intergroup variations.

Western blot analysis

Cells from each group treated with Gyp for 48 h were harvested and lysed using RIPA cell lysate (Solarbio, Beijing, China) and the total protein concentration in the supernatant was determined using a BCA kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China). The proteins were separated via SDS-PAGE and transferred onto a PVDF membrane (Millipore, MA, USA). Then the membrane was blocked with 5% skimmed milk at room temperature for 2 h. The primary antibody was incubated overnight at 4 °C, followed by the addition of the corresponding secondary antibody and a 2 h incubation. Finally, the droplet was supplemented with super-sensitive ECL luminescence reagent (MeilunBio, Dalian, China), and images were captured using a gel imaging system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). Details about the primary antibodies employed can be found in Supplementary Table S2.

Xenograft model

Six-week-old female immunodeficient BALB/c nude mice were acclimated for one week under controlled conditions (18–25 °C with a 12-h light/dark cycle), with ad libitum access to chow and water. After being randomly grouped according to their body weights, mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 1 × 107 HGC-27 cells on the right side (4/group). After 7 days of inoculation, the mice received a daily intraperitoneal injection of Gyp or vehicle control at a dosage of 30 mg/kg for 14 consecutive days. Subsequently, the mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation while under isoflurane inhalation anesthesia and the tissues including tumors, hearts, livers, kidneys, and blood were collected for subsequent experiments. Body weight and tumor volume of the mice were measured every two or three days. Alanine Transaminase (ALT), Aspartate Transferase (AST), Blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN), and Creatinine (CREA) were analyzed using a fully automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi, TKY, Japan). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, while strictly adhering to the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0, and received approval from the Animal Research Ethics Committee of Guizhou Medical University (Approval No. 2201515).

Immunohistochemical staining

The tissue sections were baked at 60 °C for 2 h, followed by deparaffinization and rehydration. After high-pressure antigen retrieval, the slides were treated with 3% H2O2 for 15 min, blocked with 10% BSA for 1 h, and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody. Subsequently, the corresponding secondary antibody was applied, followed by the addition of freshly prepared DAB color development solution and hematoxylin staining. Finally, the slides were dehydrated and sealed with neutral plastic.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software and SPSS 25.0 software. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Student’s t-test analyzed group comparisons, while one-way ANOVA assessed differences among multiple groups. p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
