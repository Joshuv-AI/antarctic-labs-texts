Whey Processed Oxymel induces apoptosis and S-phase arrest in MCF7 and MDA-MB-468 breast cancer cells
Authors: Akram Ashoori, Masoumeh Dolati, Tahereh Komeili Movahed, Hamed Afkhami, Majid Asghari, Farzaneh Tafvizi, Fatemeh Heidari, Maryam Tabibi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer remains a major global health challenge, highlighting the need for safer and more effective therapeutic agents. This study demonstrates that goat-derived Whey Processed Oxymel (WPO) induces apoptosis, oxidative stress modulation, and cell cycle arrest in both MCF7 and MDA-MB-468 triple-negative breast cancer cells (TNBC). MDA-MB-468 and MCF7 breast cancer cells were treated with WPO, and cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay. Apoptosis was measured via Annexin V/PI staining and flow cytometry. Caspase-3/7 activity, cell cycle distribution, and intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) and glutathione (GSH) levels were also evaluated. WPO treatment resulted in a significant dose- and time-dependent decrease in the viability of MDA-MB-468 and MCF7 cells. The Annexin V/PI assay confirmed that WPO induced marked apoptosis in both cancer cell lines. Caspase-3/7 activity was increased, and S-phase cell cycle arrest was observed in both cell lines, with minimal effect on normal HFF cells. WPO reduced GSH in both cell types and significantly increased ROS in MDA-MB-468 cells. These findings indicate that WPO exhibits selective anticancer activity against breast cancer cell lines under in vitro conditions, with minimal toxicity to normal fibroblasts. While promising, these results require validation in in vivo models to assess bioavailability, toxicity, and therapeutic efficacy.Keywords: Whey Processed Oxymel (WPO), MDA-MB-468 cells, MCF7 cells, Apoptosis, Oxidative stress, Cell cycleSubject terms: Cancer, Cell biology, Molecular biology, Oncology

Introduction

Cancer disease is a multi-step process during which normal cells turn into malignant and aggressive cells1. 2,041,910 new cancer diagnoses are expected, and 618,120 cancer-related deaths are projected to occur in the United States by 20252. Today breast cancer, as the most frequent cancer diagnosed worldwide, has surpassed lung cancer and the incidence of this disease has increased greatly3, 4, 5. The most common methods are surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy6. Patients receiving chemotherapy and radiotherapy often experience relatively severe adverse effects due to the limited selectivity of these modalities for malignant versus normal proliferating cells7. Therefore, despite recent advances in cancer treatment, most of these methods have side effects and long-term consequences, including nausea, vomiting, fatigue, loss of appetite, change in taste, hair loss, dry mouth, constipation, skin changes, damage to the oral mucosa8. Due to this, scientists are constantly looking for new and effective treatments with fewer damages9.

For over half a century, natural products have been thoroughly studied for anticancer properties due to their exceptional chemical variety10. The impact of dairy products on the prevention of many cancer forms and, in particular, its preventive effects have been demonstrated in vivo and in vitro as well as in the analysis of extensive cohort and case–control studies11, 12, 13, 14. One potential functional food that has been identified as having significant health benefits is whey, which is a combination of proteins derived from animal milk15. Whey proteins are soluble in water16 and possess valuable functional properties17.

Currently, the use of whey protein as a nutritional strategy in cancer treatment has been considered18. Interestingly, compared to other protein sources such as soy, whey protein has a higher concentration of essential amino acids19. Proteins that have different chemical, physical and functional properties, including immunoglobulins, lactoferrin, α-lactalbumin, beta-galactoglobulin, serum albumin and galactoperoxidase are among the most important proteins that make up whey. These components have shown antioxidant activities in various cancer models20. According to in vitro studies and the anti-oxidative stress properties of whey proteins, it has been proven that this compound can neutralize free radicals and thus protect biological molecules against oxidative damage21, 22. Glutathione is a key antioxidant in cells that plays an important role in neutralizing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and protecting cells from oxidative stress23. High levels of GSH have been reported in many cancer cells, which are resistant to ROS and common anticancer therapies24, 25, 26. Whey proteins are expected to increase ROS27 by reducing GSH and inducing oxidative stress in cancer cells. Therefore, whey proteins are expected to help to remove cancer cells due to their GSH-lowering properties28.

On the other hand, whey protein components have been shown to promote apoptosis in various cancer models. Apoptosis or programmed cell death is considered as one of the targeted therapies for cancer treatment29. Apoptosis proceeds through two main pathways: extrinsic (death receptor-mediated) and intrinsic (mitochondrial). The intrinsic pathway is tightly regulated by the BCL2 apoptosis regulator (BCL2) protein family, which includes both pro-apoptotic members (e.g., Bcl2-Associated X Protein (BAX), Bcl2 Antagonist/Killer (BAK), Bcl2 Interacting Mediator of cell Death (BIM)) and anti-apoptotic members (e.g., BCL2, B Cell lymphoma-extra large (BCL-XL), Myeloid Cell Leukemia-1 (MCL-1)). An increase in the BAX/BCL2 ratio promotes mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, cytochrome c release, and subsequent activation of caspase-9 (CASP9) and downstream effector caspases (e.g., caspase-3/7), ultimately leading to programmed cell death29.

Although many natural compounds activate cell death pathways with potentially improved selectivity compared to conventional chemotherapeutics, cancer cells can still develop resistance through diverse mechanisms, including ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter-mediated drug efflux, metabolic reprogramming, and evasion of apoptosis via BCL2 overexpression, P53 mutation, caspase inactivation, or Inhibitor of Apoptosis Proteins (IAP) upregulation. This underscores the necessity of mechanistic characterization for each natural agent to evaluate its true therapeutic potential and resistance liabilities30, 31.

In this research goat whey was used because, despite the similarity in composition of goat and cow milk, goat milk has been reported to contain less lactose and be better digestive tolerance32. In addition, goat whey is less allergenic33. In the past, studies have been conducted on the effects of whey proteins on cancers such as colorectal34, liver35, and leukemia36.

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, the effect of goat Whey Processed Oxymel (WPO) on MDA-MB-468 and MCF7 breast cancer cell lines has not been investigated so far; Therefore, this study was designed to evaluate the hypothesis that goat-derived Whey Processed Oxymel (WPO) induces apoptosis, modulates oxidative stress, and arrests the cell cycle in MCF7 and MDA-MB-468 breast cancer cells while sparing non-cancerous cells.

Materials and methods

Preparation of sweet sekanjabin and whey

WPO was prepared from fresh alpine goat milk using a standardized oxymel-based protocol. Briefly, skimmed milk (pH 6.7) was acidified with sekanjabin (vinegar-sugar syrup, pH 4.2–4.5), and the supernatant (whey) was filtered, lyophilized, and stored at –20°C. Full biochemical profiling of the 100 gr WPO powder including: Total protein: 6.45 gr, Fat: 2.67 gr, Lactose: 84.20 gr, Moisture: 4.50 gr, Ash: 5.9 gr (Iran National Standard: 6959 and 5877 numbers), and Absence of microbial/fungal contamination (ISO 21,528–1/2). Then, specific amounts of powder were weighed and diluted in deionized water to prepare liquid whey solutions of desired concentrations. The resulting solution was passed through an Amicon filter with a molecular weight of 3 kDa to completely separate casein and fat. All steps were performed under sterile conditions.

Cell culture

The MCF7 (IBRC C10082), HFF, and MDA-MB-468 (IBRC C10095) cell lines were obtained from the Iranian Biological Resource Center (Tehran, Iran). MCF7 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, UK), MDA-MB-468 in DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, UK), and HFF cells in DMEM High Glucose medium (Gibco, UK). Then 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Sigma, USA) were added to mediums. The cells were hold in an incubator with 5% CO2 and 95% relative humidity at 37°C. Cell morphology and confluence were observed daily using an inverted microscope. After reaching approximately 80% confluency and being washed with PBS, the cells were detached using a 0.25% Trypsin–EDTA solution (Gibco, UK). After centrifuging the separated cells, a hemocytometer and Trypan Blue staining (Merck, Germany) were used to assess their viability and count. Normal HFF fibroblasts were included as a non-cancerous control to assess the selectivity of WPO’s cytotoxic effects, which is critical for evaluating potential therapeutic applications where selective targeting of cancer cells is essential.

MTT assay

In each 96-well plate, 200 µL of culture medium containing 5,000 cancer cells were seeded in each well and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and 95% relative humidity. Different doses of WPO (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 10, 14, 16, 18, 20, and 24 mg/mL) and Tamoxifen (0, 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5, and 15 μg/mL) were considered for cell treatment, after which the cells were incubated under similar conditions for 24, 48, and 72 h. 20 µg of 5mg/ml of MTT (3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) (Sigma Aldrich, Germany) was added in the dark to form formazan crystals and incubated for 4 h. 200 μl of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added after emptying the wells. An ELISA reader (BioTek, Synergy/HTX, Germany) was used to detect absorbance at 570 nm after the plates had been shaken in the dark for 30 min. HFF cells were employed as a healthy control, and untreated MCF7 and MDA-MB-468 cells were utilized as negative controls. The inhibitory concentration (IC50) of various whey liquid dilutions that can stop 50% of cancer cells from growing was determined by the formula. Three replications of each trial were conducted.

Percentage of cell viability (%) = (mean absorbance of treated cells/mean absorbance of control cells) × 10037, 38.

Annexin V-FITC/PI staining and flow cytometry for apoptosis detection

The dual staining technique using Annexin V-FITC and Propidium Iodide (PI) was used in combination with flow cytometry to evaluate the presence of apoptosis. This method makes it possible to distinguish between necrotic, early apoptotic, late apoptotic, and living cells. After being seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in a 6-well plate, cells (MCF7, MDA-MB-468, and HFF) were treated for 48 h at 37 °C with IC50 doses of WPO and Tamoxifen (TMX). Negative controls were cells that had not been treated. Following treatment, the medium was gathered, and trypsinization was used to extract the adhered cells. After washing and resuspending in 90 μL of binding buffer, the cells were incubated for 10 min with 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC solution and then with 5 μL of PI in the dark at room temperature. The resulting suspension was transferred to a flow cytometer tube and analyzed by flow cytometry (Attune™ NxT Flow Cytometer, Thermo Fisher Scientific, the USA). The cells were divided into four quadrants for data evaluation: lower left (Annexin V −/PI −) had living cells, lower right (Annexin V +/PI −) had early apoptotic cells, upper right (Annexin V +/PI +) had late apoptotic cells, and upper left (Annexin V −/PI +) had necrotic cells39.

Acridine orange and propidium iodide staining

Fluorescent dyes called Acridine Orange (AO) and Propidium Iodide (PI) are used to measure necrosis, apoptosis, and cell viability. When stimulated by blue light (λ = 500 nm), AO readily passes through live cell membranes, binds to DNA, and emits green fluorescence. By contrast, PI can only enter dead or membrane-compromised cells, and when it binds to DNA, it emits red fluorescence (λ = 617 nm). Because AO displays live cells in green and PI highlights necrotic cells in red, the combination of these dyes enables the differentiation of live, apoptotic, and necrotic cells. Because of membrane blebbing and chromatin fragmentation, apoptotic cells have increased orange fluorescence. At a final concentration of 50 μg/mL each, AO and PI were produced for fluorescence staining and viability evaluation. To obtain the working concentration, equal quantities of each dye were combined with stock solutions containing 100 μg/mL. To examine cells by fluorescence microscope, 10 μL of the staining solution was added to 10 μL of cell suspension. The mixture was then carefully pipetted.

Evaluation of Caspase-3/7 activities

Using a colorimetric ELISA kit (Catalog No. ab193736, Abcam), the activity of Caspase-3/7 activity was assessed by the manufacturer’s instructions. A chromogen-labeled substrate is cleaved by active caspases in this method, generating a colored product proportionate to the caspase activity that is evaluated with an ELISA reader (BioTek, Synergy/HTX, Germany) at an absorbance of 405 nm. WPO at its IC50 concentration was added to 96-well plates seeded with 7,000 MCF7 or MDA-MB-468 cancer cells per well. The cells were then incubated for 48 h at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 5% CO2. After incubation, the medium containing trypsinized cells was centrifuged. 500 μL of Caspase Lysis Buffer was used to lyse the cells, which were then incubated for 20 min at 4 °C before being centrifuged for 15 min at 4 °C at 12,000 rpm. For additional examination, the supernatants were gathered and kept at −80°C. DTT stock (1 M in deionized water) was diluted to 10 mM in caspase lysis buffer immediately prior to use to reconstitute the Positive Control. As directed by the kit, the standard solution was diluted with 1μL of a working solution comprising 0.5 μL DTT, 50 μL Caspase-3 Buffer, and 5 μL Caspase Substrate was mixed with 50 μL of the sample, standard, or Positive Control. Cell cycle distribution analysis

Cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/ml in a 6-well plate and treated for 48 h. Finally, suspended cells were collected and adherent cells were added after trypsinization. Samples were centrifuged for 5–10 min and suspended in 500 μl of cold PBS (2–8°C). Then, 1 ml of cold 70% ethanol was added to the samples and, after vortexing, they were placed in the refrigerator for 2 h. To prevent cell aggregation, the samples were gently shaken. After the experiment was completed, the samples were centrifuged and washed twice with PBS. Cells were incubated for 30 min at room temperature in PBS containing PI (20 μg/ml), Triton X-100 (0.1%), and RNase A (10 μg/ml). Finally, the suspension was transferred to a flow cytometry tube and analyzed by flow cytometry (Attune™ NxT Flow Cytometer).

Reactive oxygen species detection assay

In order to examine the level of intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), MCF7 and MDA-MB-468 cell lines were cultured at 5,000 cells per well. After that, the cells were treated with IC50 WPO and incubated at 37 °C, 5% CO2 for 48 h. Three wells were considered as positive control (H₂O₂), three wells as negative control (untreated cells), and three wells for blank (untreated cells without dye to examine background autofluorescence). After treatment and removal of the cell culture medium, 100 μl of assay buffer was added to each well. Then the buffer was quickly removed and 100 μl of DCF staining buffer was added to all wells except the blank wells. The plate was covered and incubated for 60 min at 37 °C away from light. Then, 100 μl of stimulus solution (R3) was added to the positive control wells and incubation was performed again for 20 min in the dark.

Next, the DCF staining solution was removed from the wells and test buffer was added and removed twice consecutively for washing. Finally, the fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, Synergy/HTX, Germany) at an excitation wavelength of 480 to 500 nm and an emission wavelength of 510 to 550 nm. The fluorescence value of each well was analyzed after subtracting the background fluorescence (blank).

Glutathione (GSH) detection in whey samples

The antioxidant levels of the samples in this experiment were assessed by measuring glutathione (GSH) using a colorimetric method. Since GSH is a very important antioxidant and contains free thiol groups, its levels can be considered an indicator of the antioxidant status of tissues or biological samples. As a coenzyme of the enzyme glutathione peroxidase (GPX), GSH plays a vital role in neutralizing oxidative free radicals. The method involves the reaction of free thiol groups in GSH with DNTB (Ellman’s reagent), resulting in a yellow-colored complex that is measured at 405 nm. Cells were cultured in a 96-well plate, treated with IC50 WPO, and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 48 h. Controls included three wells for positive control, untreated control, and blank (non-stained cells for autofluorescence). After culturing the Kiazist glutathione assay kit (Iran) was used to determine GSH levels. The results were analyzed according to the kit instructions using cell lysates prepared in accordance with the kit’s procedure. Absorbance was recorded at 405 nm using an ELISA reader.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis and curve fitting were carried out using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0.2). Three separate experiments’ results were presented as mean ± standard deviation. The significance of differences between groups was assessed using a Two-way and One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001). Cell cycle distribution was analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.6.2, BD Life Sciences).

Results

Cytotoxic assessment of WPO: killing breast cancer cells while preserving HFF cells

The cytotoxic effects of WPO on MDA-MB-468, MCF7 (Fig. 1A) and HFF (Fig. 1B) cell lines were evaluated using the MTT assay at different doses (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 10, 14, 16, 18, 20, 24 mg/mL) and time intervals (24, 48 and 72 h). Consistent with the results for both MDA-MB-468 and MCF7 cells, a dose- and time-dependent decrease in cell viability was observed. However, no significant difference was observed between the two cancer cells with increasing concentrations and time at 48 h. On the other hand, HFF cells (Fig. 1B) showed minimal cytotoxicity at all concentrations tested, indicating that WPO selectively targets cancer cells.

Apoptosis induction by WPO and TMX through Annexin V/PI assay in MCF7 with minimal side effects on normal HFF cells

Flow cytometric analysis of Annexin V/propidium iodide (PI) staining was used to evaluate the effects of WPO and TMX on apoptosis and necrosis in normal HFF fibroblast cells and MCF7 breast cancer cells. Using this method, the viable, apoptotic, and necrotic cell populations can be analyzed quantitatively and statistically. As shown in Fig. 2A, MCF7 cells showed a significantly higher apoptosis rate than the control group after 48 h of treatment with IC50 concentration of WPO and TMX. While Annexin V −/PI − cells were considered viable, Annexin V +/PI − cells were classified as early apoptotic, Annexin V +/PI + cells as late apoptotic, and Annexin V −/PI + cells as necrotic. This classification enables precise identification of different stages of cell death.

Treatment of MCF7 cells with IC50 WPO significantly increased early and late apoptosis compared to the control group. The experiments were performed in triplicate.

Notably, the proportion of early apoptotic cells increased from 0.42 ± 0.59% in the control group to 13.70 ± 1.55% after treatment with IC50 WPO, while late apoptosis increased from 0.45 ± 0.26% to 28.00 ± 0.28%. Similarly, treatment with IC50 TMX resulted in 13.70 ± 1.41% early apoptosis and 32.66 ± 1.89% late apoptosis, indicating a greater induction of late apoptosis than WPO. Furthermore, all groups showed low levels of necrosis, which could indicate that cell death occurs mainly through apoptosis rather than necrosis. Interestingly, HFF cells showed little change in apoptosis after exposure to IC50 WPO compared to IC50 TMX-treated cells. The results related to early and late apoptosis were 5.72 ± 0.67% and 0.29 ± 0.07%, respectively, in HFF cells treated with IC50WPO, while the corresponding values in the control group were 0.48 ± 0.46% and 0.15 ± 0.21%. The findings indicated that the WPO did show a few apoptotic effects on healthy cells.

The treatment of HFF cells with IC50 concentration of TMX for MCF7 cells (IC50 = 10.36 µg/mL) induced a higher apoptosis rate (early apoptosis: 13.15 ± 1.20%; late apoptosis: 11.25 ± 1.06%), but it should be noted that these values remained significantly lower than those observed in MCF7 cells treated with TMX (13.70 ± 1.41% early and 32.66 ± 1.89% late apoptosis). Interestingly, when the IC50 concentration of WPO for MCF7 cells (IC50 = 20.34 mg/mL) was applied to HFF cells, it resulted in only minimal apoptosis (early: 5.73 ± 0.67%; late: 0.29 ± 0.07%; total: 6.02%).

According to Fig. 2, it was observed that in the IC50 concentration of WPO in MCF7 and HFF cells, the survival percentage was 55.6 and 93.2%, respectively. In the case of TMX, the survival percentage was 52.14 and 72.08%, respectively. Therefore, the IC50 effect of WPO on the survival of HFF cells was 93.2% and death was 6.8%, while the IC50 effect of TMX on the survival of HFF cells was 72.08% and death was 27.92%. Our WPO kills 21.12% less HFF cells. These results suggest that WPO induces considerably less apoptosis in normal cells than TMX, highlighting its selective cytotoxicity and potential safety as a therapeutic agent.

In all selectivity experiments, normal HFF cells were exposed to WPO concentrations corresponding to the IC₅₀ values of cancer cells (20.34 mg/mL for MCF7; 18.11 mg/mL for MDA-MB-468), not their own higher IC₅₀, thereby modeling a clinically relevant scenario where healthy tissues are exposed to tumor-inhibitory drug levels.

Enhanced apoptosis induction by WPO in MCF7 and MDA-MB-468 cells through caspase 3/7 activation

After treatment with IC50 WPO, apoptosis induction in MCF7 and MDA-MB-468 cells was evaluated by measuring the enzymatic activity of caspases 3/7, as shown in Fig. 5. When compared to the control group, the IC50 WPO-treated group’s relative caspase 3/7 activity in MCF7 cells was a slight increase, which could be due to the lack of expression of caspase 3 in MCF7 cells (p < 0.05)42. Also, IC50 WPO treatment increased caspase 3/7 activity in MDA-MB-468 cells (Fig. 5B), showing a statistically significant increase (p < 0.01) from the control.

WPO modulates apoptotic gene expression in breast cancer cells

Real-time qPCR analysis revealed that WPO treatment significantly upregulated pro-apoptotic genes BAX and CASP9, and downregulated anti-apoptotic BCL2 in both MCF7 and MDA-MB-468 cells (Fig. 6). The BAX/BCL2 ratio increased by (3.04)-fold in MCF7 and (2.32)-fold in MDA-MB-468 (p < 0.0001), indicating mitochondrial pathway activation. While our qPCR data show increased caspase-9 mRNA expression, this does not necessarily indicate activation of the protein.

WPO induces cell cycle arrest in MDA-MB-468 and MCF7 cells

MDA-MB-468, MCF7, and HFF cells were subjected to flow cytometric examination to assess the impact of WPO therapy on cell cycle progression. Following IC50 WPO therapy, the cell cycle distribution of cancer cells changed significantly, but normal HFF cells barely changed. IC50 WPO treatment significantly reduced the G0/G1 phase (p < 0.0001) and increased the S phase population (p < 0.0001) in MDA-MB-468 cells compared to the control group, indicating S phase arrest. Similarly, IC50 WPO treatment in MCF7 cells showed a similar pattern of cell cycle arrest with a increase in the percentage of cells in the S phase (p < 0.0001) and a decrease in the G0/G1 phase (p < 0.0001). On the other hand, the G0/G1, S, and G2/M phases of HFF cells did not change significantly after WPO treatment, indicating that normal cells were less affected by WPO exposure. The above findings indicated that WPO has no side effects on healthy cells while it causes cell cycle arrest in cancer cells, especially in the S phase (Fig. 7).

Discussion

The findings of this study demonstrate the anticancer effects of WPO and its capacity to induce apoptosis, affect oxidative stress, and cell cycle arrest in breast cancer cells while sparing normal cells. With a dose- and time-dependent reduction in cell viability, the MTT assay results show that WPO exhibits considerable cytotoxicity against breast cancer cell lines. WPO exhibited cytotoxic effects against the MDA-MB-468 TNBC cell line, a model known for its intrinsic therapeutic resistance. This observation aligns with prior reports on the sensitivity of TNBC cells to certain whey-derived components43. Importantly, the comparatively lower cytotoxicity of WPO toward HFF fibroblasts suggests a degree of selectivity of WPO for malignant cells, a feature also reported for other whey-derived factors such as lactoferrin44, 45. That said, these findings do not establish WPO as a therapeutic agent, but rather support its further investigation as a candidate modulator of cancer cell death under controlled experimental conditions. The observed time-dependent changes in IC₅₀ values may reflect dynamic interactions between WPO bioactive peptides and cellular stress responses46, 47; however, the underlying mechanisms remain to be elucidated.

With a negligible necrotic effect, WPO treatment boosted apoptosis in both MCF7 and MDA-MB-468 cell lines as compared to the control group. According to these results, WPO mostly causes planned cell death as opposed to non-specific cytotoxicity. Although both WPO and TMX were able to induce apoptosis in cancer cells, it should be noted that the effective concentration (IC50) of the two drugs differed significantly. Our findings, confirmed by the MTT assay, demonstrate the selective ability of WPO to induce programmed cell death of cancer cells with minimal toxicity to healthy cells. These findings are broadly consistent with prior reports on the pro-apoptotic properties of whey-derived preparations48. However, given the preliminary nature of our in vitro data, any suggestion of clinical utility such as potential use as an adjuvant or combination agent in cancer therapy, requires rigorous validation in preclinical models.

The observed upregulation of BAX and CASP9, coupled with downregulation of BCL2, provides direct molecular evidence for the activation of the intrinsic (mitochondrial) apoptotic pathway in both MCF7 and MDA-MB-468 cells following WPO treatment. The BCL2 family serves as a critical checkpoint in this process. Anti-apoptotic members like BCL2 preserve mitochondrial integrity, whereas pro-apoptotic members like BAX induce mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization, leading to cytochrome c release and apoptosome formation. This is further supported by the significant increase in the BAX/BCL2 ratio-a well-established indicator of mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and apoptotic commitment49, 50, 51.

Future studies should assess cleaved caspase-9 levels to confirm the functional activation of this apoptotic pathway in response to WPO treatment.

Our findings are consistent with prior reports demonstrating that whey-derived bioactive peptides can modulate the BCL2 family balance in cancer cells. For instance, lactoferrin and α-lactalbumin, key components of goat whey, have been shown to upregulate BAX, downregulate BCL2, and induce apoptosis in breast and colon cancer models, while sparing normal cells48, 52, 53. The observed broad-spectrum activity of WPO across both MCF7 and MDA-MB-468 subtypes further suggests a mechanism independent of hormone receptor status, potentially mediated by these conserved whey bioactives54, 55.

In the present study, when HFF cells were exposed to the IC₅₀ concentration of TMX determined for cancer cells, the apoptotic response was significantly higher compared to when these cells were exposed to the IC50 concentration of WPO determined for cancer cells. This suggests that, under the experimental conditions of this in vitro study, WPO exhibits a more favorable selectivity index than TMX.

While this study primarily focused on the effects of WPO as a single agent, the potential synergistic effects of WPO in combination with standard chemotherapeutic agents like TMX represent an important direction for future research. Given the different mechanisms of action between WPO and TMX, combination therapy might enhance efficacy while reducing side effects, particularly in hormone-responsive breast cancer subtypes.

It should be emphasized that this comparison is not intended to position WPO as a therapeutic substitute for TMX, but rather to illustrate its relative cytotoxic selectivity in a cellular model. Due to the well-documented off-target effects of TMX, including endometrial hyperplasia, polyps, and increased risk of uterine malignancies56, 57, 58, highlight the continued need for agents with improved safety profiles, particularly in long-term or adjuvant settings. WPO, as a natural whey-derived preparation, warrants further investigation in this context. In keeping with the findings of Shih-Hsuan Cheng et al. the limited necrosis seen in all groups lends more credence to the theory that WPO predominantly causes apoptosis rather than unchecked cell death59. Furthermore, it has been known that α-lactalbumin, a significant protein in milk, can cause tumor cells to undergo apoptosis while leaving healthy cells unharmed60.

Increased orange/red fluorescence and morphological characteristics including nuclear condensation and membrane blebbing are further indications of WPO’s apoptotic effects in MCF7 and MDA-MB-468 cells, according to the results of acridine orange staining. These results are consistent with other studies on naturally occurring bioactive substances that have anticancer effects, especially those that come from dairy proteins61, 62.

Moreover, the present study demonstrates the strong pro-apoptotic effect of WPO, demonstrating its great ability to activate critical caspases and successfully cause apoptosis in MCF7 and MDA-MB-468 breast cancer cells, as seen by the considerable increase in caspase 3/7 activity in these tumor cell lines. This is consistent with earlier findings showing that whey substances can activate caspase-dependent pathways to improve apoptotic signaling in cancer cells63. By the way, MCF7 cells are caspase-3-deficient due to a 47-bp deletion in exon 3 of the CASP3 gene; thus, the observed caspase-3/7 activity likely reflects caspase-7 activation, consistent with prior reports42, 64.

As observed, WPO treatment had no effect on normal HFF fibroblasts in S phase, while inducing S phase arrest in MDA-MB-468 and MCF7 cells. It has been documented that natural substances can stop DNA replication and encourage apoptotic cell death by stopping cancer cells in the S phase65. This specific cytostatic action is in line with the last research on peptides derived from dairy products, which have shown their capacity to alter the progression of the cell cycle in cancer cells while protecting healthy cells66, 67.

In this study, it was found that WPO treatment significantly increased the levels of ROS in MDA-MB-468 breast cancer cells (p < 0.05), while no significant change was observed in MCF7 cells. ROS-based therapy is a therapeutic strategy to kill cancer cells by enhancing intracellular ROS levels. Although ROS-based therapy has been applied in clinical treatment, there are still obstacles that limit its therapeutic efficacy. One of the main obstacles is the removal of ROS by the cellular antioxidant system, especially GSH. It has been reported that the reduction of GSH levels in tumor cells leads to increased ROS levels and oxidative damage68. In this study, WPO significantly reduced intracellular glutathione (GSH) levels in both MCF7 and MDA-MB-468 cell lines, but there was no significant increase in ROS levels.

Glutathione is one of the most important cellular antioxidants that play a key role in maintaining the oxidation–reduction balance and neutralizing ROS69. Reduced GSH levels can lead to increased oxidative damage and ultimately cell death70. From a therapeutic perspective, reducing GSH levels in cancer cells is an important strategy to increase the effectiveness of oxidative stress-based therapies71. Since reducing intracellular GSH levels is beneficial for many cancer treatment regimens, researchers have proposed various promising strategies to achieve GSH depletion. Studies such as Marini et al. and Elda Valenti et al. have also shown that GSH depletion leads to increased ROS accumulation and sensitization of cancer cells to chemotherapy72, 73.

Despite the significant decrease in glutathione in MCF7 cells, no significant increase in ROS levels was observed. The same finding was found in the study by Shih-Hsuan Cheng et al., where compared with the control group, GSH levels were significantly lower after administration of WPC alone or in combination with rapamycin. However, WPC alone had no effect on ROS levels in MDA-MB-231 cells and when administered as part of a combination treatment, it had no effect on the increase in ROS concentration induced by rapamycin27. According to the findings of Boyi Niu et al., compounds in WPO may prevent the increase in ROS through alternative pathways. In some natural compounds, it has also been observed that despite the decrease in GSH, the presence of active peptides or polyphenols prevents the accumulation of ROS. On the other hand, cancer cells themselves respond to oxidative stress by activating antioxidant pathways and thereby adapt themselves (redox homeostasis). Therefore, the intracellular redox state is in a dynamic balance as a result of the controlled production and removal of ROS74. Determining the exact mechanisms of this requires further research.

Despite the significant GSH depletion observed in both MCF7 and MDA-MB-468 cells, a marked increase in ROS was detected only in MDA-MB-468. This discrepancy may reflect the distinct redox buffering capacities of these subtypes: MCF7 cells are known to maintain robust antioxidant defenses including catalase, SOD, and thioredoxin even under GSH deficiency75, 76, whereas MDA-MB-468 cells, as basal-like TNBC models, exhibit inherently high basal ROS and impaired NRF2/KEAP1 signaling, rendering them exquisitely vulnerable to GSH loss77, 78.

The findings of this study demonstrate that WPO exerts significant anticancer effects on MCF7 and MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells in vitro, through the induction of apoptosis, oxidative stress, and cell cycle arrest. These results suggest that WPO may have potential as an adjunctive agent in breast cancer therapy. However, given that this is an in vitro study, the clinical relevance of these effects remains to be determined through further preclinical and clinical investigations.

Conclusion

In summary, WPO demonstrates in vitro selective cytotoxicity against breast cancer cells via apoptosis and oxidative stress. However, its clinical potential remains contingent upon in vivo validation, and deeper mechanistic characterization—including bioavailability, pharmacokinetics, and interaction with standard-of-care therapies.

Limitation and recommendation

Despite the promising results of this study in demonstrating the anticancer effects of WPO on breast cancer cells, several limitations should be acknowledged:

First, the experiments were conducted in vitro using established cell line models, which do not fully recapitulate the complexity of the tumor microenvironment, stromal interactions, immune surveillance, or systemic pharmacokinetics present in vivo. Consequently, the translational relevance of these findings to clinical settings remains to be validated.

Second, while HFF cells served as a non-cancerous control, they are dermal fibroblasts and do not represent the tissue of origin for breast cancer. Future studies should incorporate non-transformed mammary epithelial cell lines (e.g., MCF-10A) to better model breast tissue-specific selectivity and physiological relevance.

Third, MCF-7 cells harbor a well-documented genetic deficiency in caspase-3 due to a 47-bp deletion in exon 3 of the CASP3 gene. As such, the observed caspase-3/7 activity likely reflects caspase-7 activation, and these findings may not be generalizable to caspase-3-competent tumors.

Fourth, although we assessed mRNA expression of key apoptotic regulators (BAX, BCL2, CASP9) by real-time qPCR, protein-level validation (e.g., by Western blot) was not performed. Future studies should include proteomic confirmation to solidify the proposed molecular mechanisms.

Fifth, the investigation was limited to two molecular subtypes of breast cancer MCF7 and triple-negative (MDA-MB-468). The effects of WPO on HER2⁺ models or other cancer types remain unexplored, limiting the generalizability of the results.

Finally, the dosing regimen (concentration and duration) was based on preliminary cytotoxicity screens. Optimal therapeutic dosing, long-term safety, bioavailability, and in vivo efficacy have not been established. Preclinical animal studies are essential to address these gaps before any consideration of clinical application.
