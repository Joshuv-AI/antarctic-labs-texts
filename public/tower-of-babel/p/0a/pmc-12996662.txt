Longitudinal data analysis of lung aeration and gas exchange trajectories in bronchopulmonary dysplasia: the devil is in the details—authors’ reply
Authors: Daniele De Luca, Luca Bonadies, Eugenio Baraldi, Luca Vedovelli, Almudena Alonso-Ojembarrena, all authors
Published: 2026

We read the correspondence by Dr Oulego with great interest.1 He raises concerns regarding aspects of our data analysis and the presentation of certain graphical elements, suggesting that these may be open to misinterpretation by readers. We are grateful to Dr Oulego for his careful review and engagement with our work. We welcome the opportunity to clarify these points and to further explain the rationale behind our analytical and presentation choices.

First, Dr Oulego is concerned about the timescale used in our analyses.2 In response to this concern, during the revision process, we re-performed the analysis using post-menstrual age as exclusive time scale (i.e. without combining gestational and post-menstrual age). The results of the new models were essentially identical to those presented in the main manuscript. These results are presented in Supplementary Figures 6, 7 and 8 in the online appendix, and demonstrate that Dr Oulego's concern is unfounded. The linear representation of time, even when expressed as postmenstrual age, was also criticised. In fact, Dr Oulego assumes that there is no linearity in lung maturation and evolution of bronchopulmonary dysplasia: this could be true but, at the same time, there is no statistical reason or previous data to prefer a non-linear model and therefore this only remains a working hypothesis. When Dr Oulego refers to a ‘true maturational pattern’, it should be noted that there are no other means of capturing lung maturation in NICU-admitted infants. Maturation may indeed differ between babies with the same postmenstrual age, but there is currently no practical way of investigating this at the bedside (e.g. CT and MRI scans are not an option for obvious reasons).

Dr Oulego also criticised the figures reporting the main and supplementary results. He stated that it was correct to use 95% confidence intervals around the estimated group means, but that these do not refer to individual observations. He is right: this graphical representation is technically and statistically correct, and we would add, necessary. We are confident that the average reader understands what confidence intervals mean, as this they are described in the figure legends. Furthermore, it is evident that we are not performing any predictive analysis in our work. There is no ROC analysis, and no predictive metrics are given. STARD guidelines were neither cited nor followed, and the work is not classified as a diagnostic accuracy study. Quite on the contrary, the purpose of our study was to describe lung aeration and gas exchange during the early phase of moderate-to-severe broncho-pulmonary dysplasia (BPD) development, as detailed several times in the manuscript. Similarly, functional data analysis did not aim to replace a formal diagnostic accuracy analysis, and its limitations are clearly described in the discussion: ‘The functional data analysis might have been limited by sparse temporal sampling (e.g. only three postnatal time points), which could have affected the precision of continuous trajectory estimation’.

In conclusion, it is important to recognise the pragmatic nature of a study embedded in routine NICU care, which may differ significantly from that of a paediatric intensive care unit. For this reason, it is unlikely that neonatologists would be misled. In fact, our timeline includes important milestones such as 34- and 36-week postmenstrual age, which are widely used to manage therapies (e.g. continuous positive airway pressure and caffeine or diuretics) commonly used in extreme preterm babies. This guarantees a wide application of our results. While cross-disciplinarity is certainly useful, it is important to highlight the specificity of neonatology, as recently reaffirmed, as evaluating projects and data using a different lens has brought several catastrophic consequences to the development of our specialty.3 Our work provides the description of pathophysiological phenotype of patients that might be treated early in life with new anti-BPD drugs.4, 5, 6 As such, our results should be read with the benefit of clinical experience.
