Evaluation of tattoo reliability in breast cancer re-irradiation☆
Authors: Grace Lee, Michael Velec, Hedi Mohseni, Tara Rosewall, Yat Tsang, Jennifer Croke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTattoos help guide field placement in breast re-irradiation. This study evaluates the stability of medial tattoos in patients with prior breast radiotherapy (RT) to determine their reliability as surface markers.Materials and methodsWe retrospectively identified patients who had breast/chest wall re-irradiation between January 2022 and December 2023 (RT2) and prior breast RT (RT1) at our institution. Planning CTs for both RT courses were registered using rigid image registration. The medial tattoo, sternum, and internal perforating branches of the internal thoracic vessels were contoured on both CT1 and CT2, and POIs were placed at the respective centers to assess shifts and 3D distances reported as target registration errors (TRE).ResultsEighteen patients were included, average sternum 3D TRE was 0.8 mm(SD0.8), within CT voxel thickness. The average medial tattoo 3D TRE was 9.2 mm(SD5.4). Significantly greater 3D TRE was observed in patients whose arm positions varied between scans (Same: 4.7 mm vs Different: 10.9 mm, p = 0.002). The largest 3D TRE was 22.1 mm, observed in a patient who had a mastectomy before RT2. The average vessels 3D TRE was 4.1 mm (SD2.3) and impacted by arm position (Different = 4.4 mm vs Same = 2.9 mm, p = 0.009).ConclusionRelying solely on previous medial tattoos as indicators of the previous RT field border can be inaccurate due to arm positioning and surgical procedure changes that impact surface anatomy over time. Reproducing the patient’s original setup and arm positioning is essential to reducing registration errors in breast re-irradiation. If varying arm positions are unavoidable, internal thoracic perforator vessels may provide more robust registration.Keywords: Breast radiation therapy, Tattoo stability, Re-irradiation, Image registration, Tattoo marks, Skin marks

Introduction

The use of traditional tattoos for daily patient setup and positioning for radiotherapy (RT) treatment is still common practice in many cancer centers. In clinical breast setup, daily patient positioning uses the tattoos for laser alignment [1] and may incorporate treatment field reference points from their respective tattoos to ensure the visual light field projections cover the clinical breast volume. However, prior studies have shown permanent tattoos result in psychosocial impact on patients and may act as reminder of their cancer diagnosis [2,3]. In the recent decade, the provision of semi-permanent, non-permanent, or non-visible marks are shown to be viable options for patient setup and can help reduce patients’ psychological and cosmetic concerns associated with permanent tattoos [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. Moreover, there is increasing development of advanced technologies like surface guided RT (SGRT), which provide accurate and efficient RT setup without the use of tattoos [1,[10], [11], [12]].

Yet another reason tattoos are thought to be important is in the event of re-irradiation, where tattoo marks can serve as permanent references of prior field borders [13]. Re-irradiation, as defined by the ESTRO-EORTC consensus paper [14], is a new course of RT in a patient who has previously received radiation treatment to a region that overlaps with a previously treated volume or treating an area where the cumulative dose raises concerns about toxicity. Re-irradiation practices in breast cancer are becoming increasingly common. According to the 2023 ESTRO-EORTC survey on re-irradiation patterns of care, breast/chest wall re-irradiation is the 5th most treated site as reported by physicians [15]. Andratschke et al. [16] emphasized the challenges in re-irradiation lie in the complexity of balancing tumour control with the risk of severe normal tissue toxicity from the cumulative RT doses.

In the era of pre-CT planning, when a patient had prior breast RT, the medial alignment tattoo (herein referred to as medial tattoo), typically placed on the anterior chest midline between the breasts, was used as a surrogate marker referencing the prior treatment field. Any new RT fields will be placed with a margin away from this tattoo to avoid potential treatment overlap. While this practice originated in the era of simulator-based planning, the reliance on these tattoos as references for previous RT fields continues to prevail even with the adoption of 3D based planning. However, the reliability of permanent tattoos as stable reference marks has not been well established.

This study aims to assess the reproducibility of the medial tattoo in patients previously treated with breast RT, who received a subsequent course of breast re-irradiation. In this study, breast re-irradiation refers to any subsequent radiotherapy course to the ipsilateral or contralateral breast for a new primary or recurrent breast cancer. Specifically, we evaluate the reliability of the medial tattoo between the patient’s prior and re-irradiation planning CTs and determine any factors that influence the stability of this permanent tattoo.

Materials and methods

We retrospectively identified patients treated with breast/chest wall re-irradiation from January 2022−December 2023, who had prior (ipsilateral or contralateral) breast RT at our institution. The project was reviewed and provided an exemption from the REB with formal Quality Improvement Review Committee approval at our institution (QIRC 23-0667). Our institutional breast RT scanning protocol remained relatively the same during this period and all CT scans were acquired with a 2 mm slice thickness. Patients were positioned supine on a breast board and immobilized with arm support having the ipsilateral arm raised and the contralateral arm raised or by their side. Patients were CT scanned during free-breathing (FB) and/or deep inspiration breath-hold (DiBH) using an active breath-hold device where heart sparing was required. Patients were planned for whole breast RT using a two-field tangential field-in-field IMRT. In patients requiring nodal RT, supraclavicular +/- axillary regions were treated using 3D conformal parallel opposing fields. Partial breast RT involved the use of three to five coplanar and/or non-coplanar fields, optimized using field-in-field IMRT. The Pinnacle3 (Philips Radiation Oncology Systems, Milpitas, CA) treatment planning system was use prior to 2018, after which our institution switched to RayStation (RaySearch Laboratories, Sweden).

Our institutional breast RT protocol required four tattoo placements: Medial (midline; over the sternum), Lateral (1.5 cm posterior to breast − align with medial tattoo), Superior (1.5 cm above breast), and Inferior (1.5 cm from infra-mammary fold – align with superior tattoo). Radio-opaque markers, commonly referred to as BBs, were placed on the tattoos, so they were easily identifiable on the CT scans. During CT scans for breast re-irradiation, patients’ tattoos from their prior breast/chest wall RT were located and reused for patient alignment when possible. For patients requiring a new course of contralateral breast RT, the previous medial tattoo was BB’ed and used as the midline tattoo to prevent introducing a new tattoo in the same body region, thereby avoiding the potential for incorrect tattoo use during setup [17].

For this study, the previous RT treatment CT (CT1) and current CT (CT2) for re-irradiation were anonymized and imported into RayStation. The entire sternum, manubriosternal joint, and the manubrium were contoured on both CTs. The ribs were not contoured or used in the rigid match as they undergo an upward and outward movement during respiration, while the manubrium remains relatively fixed [18]. For each patient, a local rigid image registration was performed between CT1 and CT2 using an automated contour-surface alignment of the sternum. The goal of rigid registration of the anterior bony sternum (Fig. 1) was to act as a stable surrogate for the midline breast/chest wall area where potential re-irradiation overlap occurs. In patients who had both FB and DiBH scans, rigid registration was performed between FB-to-FB scans or DiBH-to-DiBH scans to minimize the effects of breathing motion. The accuracy of rigid image registration was evaluated using point of interest (POI) anatomic landmarks visible on both CT1 and CT2. The medial tattoo BBs were contoured, and POIs were automatically placed at the centroid of each volume. Additionally, POIs were identified at the internal thoracic perforator vessels as surrogates for matching on both CT scans. These vessels were selected due to their consistent presence at each of the upper 5 or 6 intercostal spaces bilaterally, originating from the right and left internal thoracic vessels at the sternum [19]. The POIs were placed at the locations where the internal thoracic vessels penetrate the intercostal muscles to form perforating branches [20,21] (Fig. 2). In females, the perforator branches are relatively large from the 2nd through 4th intercostal spaces, where they enter the breasts [21]. These perforator vessels are frequently imaged and assessed for their important role as recipient vessels in breast reconstruction [19,22,23].

The superior-inferior, right-left, anterior-posterior shifts and 3D distances were calculated for all POIs (sternum, medial tattoo, vessels) using rigid image registration between reference and target images and reported as target registration error (TRE). Their absolute values were used to determine the mean 3D TRE, which is the Euclidean distance, calculated as the root mean square between the pairs of corresponding points [24]. Treatment factors such as time from prior RT to re-irradiation (RT1-RT2), arm positioning at CT2 compared to CT1, and surgery type were determined. The TRE mean and standard deviation were summarized for all patients. Individual patient means were compared with treatment factors using the two-tailed Student’s t-test, and relationships between variables were tested using Pearson's correlation test. Statistical significance was considered when the p-value was <0.05.

Results

A total of 18 patients were included; median age was 67 years (range: 44–85). The median time between RT1 -RT2 was 7.2 years (range: 3 months −13.5 years). At the time of breast re-irradiation, 61 % of patients were treated using DiBH, while 39 % were treated using FB. Rigid image registration was performed using matched breathing states (i.e., FB-FB or DiBH-DiBH). As DiBH was not commonly used during the era of patients’ initial RT, the majority of image registrations (94 %) were conducted using FB scans. Rigid image registration was first performed on the sternum. The mean sternum TRE was 0.8 mm (SD0.8), which was within CT voxel thickness. The mean medial tattoo 3D TRE was 9.2 mm (SD 5.4). The superior-inferior direction showed the largest TRE at 6.6 mm (SD 5), while right-left and anterior-posterior directions were 3.7 mm (SD 4.3 mm) and 3.1 mm (SD 2.3), respectively (Table 2). The highest observed 3D TRE in the cohort was an individual absolute value of 22.1 mm, measured in a single patient following mastectomy. This value reflected large TRE components in the superior-inferior (15.9 mm) and right-left (15.3 mm) directions, while the anterior-posterior TRE was much smaller at 0.4 mm. There was no correlation observed between the medial tattoo 3D TRE and the time between RT1-RT2 (Pearson’s r = 0.419) or patient age (Pearson’s r = -0.118).

On average, three vessel POIs (SD: 1.2) were identified per patient. The mean vessels 3D TRE was 4.1 mm (SD 2.3), the superior-inferior direction at 2.5 mm (SD 2), right-left at 2.3 mm (SD 1.9), and anterior-posterior at 1.3 mm (SD 1.1) (Table 2). Unlike the medial tattoo TRE, which had a large superior-inferior shift, the vessels had similar superior-inferior and right-left TREs of ≤2.5 mm, and even smaller (<1.5 mm) anterior-posterior shift. In all patients, the 3D TRE was consistently lower for vessels compared to the medial tattoo (Fig. 3). The post-mastectomy patient similarly had a higher-than-average 3D TRE of 7.6 mm, mostly contributed by the right-left direction TRE of 6.6 mm, but a smaller shift in the superior-inferior (2.4 mm) and anterior-posterior (1.1 mm) directions. There was no correlation observed between the vessels 3D TRE and the time between RT1-RT2 (Pearson’s r = 0.196) or patient age (Pearson’s r = -0.083).

To assess the impact of arm positioning, patients whose arm positions were the same during prior RT (both arms up, n = 5) were analyzed separately from those with different arm positions (contralateral arm by their side, n = 13). The mean medial tattoo 3D TRE was significantly higher in patients who had different arm positions (10.9 mm) vs the same arm position (4.7 mm), p = 0.002 (Fig. 4). The largest TRE was in the superior-inferior direction (8.2 mm) for patients with different arm positions and was reduced by three-folds to 2.4 mm in patients with the same positioning, p = 0.002. While there was also a reduction in the right-left and anterior-posterior TREs with arm positioning, the differences were not statistically significant (Table 3). The mean vessels 3D TRE was significantly different in patients with different arm positions (4.4 mm) vs the same arm positions (2.9 mm), p = 0.009. The superior-inferior TRE was not significantly impacted by differing arm positions (2.6 mm vs 2.3 mm, p = 0.479), while arm positions were twice as large in different vs same positions on the right-left (2.6 mm vs. 1.3 mm, p = 0.004) and anterior-posterior (1.5 mm vs. 0.7 mm, p = 0.001), respectively (Table 3).

Discussion

In this work, the location of the medial tattoo in patients who had prior breast RT was evaluated and found to be an unreliable surface mark for planning subsequent breast re-irradiation. On average, the medial tattoo showed a 3D TRE of 9.2 mm between previous and subsequent CTs, with even greater discrepancies when arm positioning differed between scans. This highlights a limitation of permanent tattoos is their placement on the external skin, which is inherently mobile. The skin’s laxity is influenced by age and can be pulled or distorted during surgery [25]. Given the mobility of the skin and its susceptibility to changes over time, it will similarly alter the position of the tattoo.

In the past, when CT planning was not common practice, the patient’s permanent tattoo was the only visible mark available for daily treatment setup and served as a post-treatment indicator of prior field borders in patients requiring re-irradiation [1]. While the use of tattoos has been historically effective, the widespread adoption of 3D-CT planning tools and image registration has rendered permanent tattoos increasingly obsolete. The medial tattoo is only a surface reference point to the medial center of the field border (representing the 50 % isodose) and does not fully represent the entire prior treatment field. Modern image registration strategies allow for co-registration of the initial CT with re-irradiation CT [26], as well as registration of previous doses and/or isodose lines for dose evaluation and assessment of potential overlapping area. Indeed, according to a 2023 ESTRO survey of international practices on re-irradiation, 67 % of respondents co-register initial CTs for re-irradiation planning, 41 % overlay physical dose distribution, and 29 % transfer isodose lines as part of re-irradiation planning practice [15]. These practice patterns indicate an ongoing shift toward image-based planning strategies rather than tattoo-based methods.

Furthermore, the traditional view that permanent tattoos are required for patient alignment and setup is being challenged by the emergence of SGRT. With ongoing development in this technology, SGRT has been shown to be comparable or even superior in setup accuracy and efficiency compared to tattoo-based methods [1,[10], [11], [12]]. In a study comparing SGRT with alignment based on skin mark (or tattoos), Kost et al. found SGRT reduced setup errors in all breast/chest wall tangential and regional nodal RT [11]. Other studies have shown SGRT to be effective in accelerated partial breast irradiation [27] and regional nodal RT [10], with similar accuracy and shortened treatment setup times. As the breast is a movable, external structure, SGRT is beneficial in that it accounts for the breast topology and skin surface rather than being limited to relying on setup points as defined by the tattoos [1]. While SGRT may not be widely available in all centers, transitioning away from permanent tattoos to semi-or non-permanent options is also feasible and is regarded as beneficial to the patient. Previous literature has shown that permanent marks are most commonly refused by breast cancer patients [13]. Reasons for their dislike or refusal of permanent tattoos include aversion to a permanent reminder, fear of needles, dislike of tattoos, religious reasons, cosmesis concerns, impact on clothing choices, lack of choice, disempowerment, and the reminder of a cancer diagnosis [13,[28], [29], [30]]. The negative emotional impact of these permanent marks persists even after the patient’s RT treatment [2,3,29,31]. Semi-permanent, non-permanent, and non-visible setup marks represent clinically viable alternatives to permanent tattoos and may help reduce the psychological impact and cosmetic concerns experienced by patients [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]. Modern radiation therapy treatments should continue to push beyond the reliance on permanent tattoos to prioritize patients’ choice in non-invasive, non-permanent, or mark-free options.

Finally, our findings have clinical implications in the re-irradiation setting, where replicating prior treatment setup and immobilization setting is essential. Currently, there is increasing evidence supporting the use of partial breast techniques in the re-irradiation setting [[32], [33], [34], [35]], showing acceptable local control and toxicity, though longer term follow-up is required. While efforts are underway to standardize re-irradiation practices [14,15,17,36,37], the planning practice for breast re-irradiation varies from institution to institution. However, care must be taken in the re-irradiation planning process to account for prior radiation exposure to the same anatomical site. The re-irradiation guidance document by The Royal College of Radiologists suggests reproducing the previous RT setup, including the immobilization device and patient positioning, to allow more accurate registration of previous and current planning CTs [17]. The ESTRO-ACROP guideline for positioning and immobilization recommended both arms up as a more stable position compared to the ipsilateral arm up [38]. Our study showed that patients scanned with both arms up on both CTs had a significant reduction in the medial tattoo 3D TRE. When the arm position varied between the two CTs, the arm position primarily impacted the skin surface in the cranial-caudal position. We therefore propose that when patients return for re-irradiation, the original planning setup including arm positioning, board angle, and arm setting should be replicated as closely as possible. This will achieve more optimal alignment and reduce image registration uncertainty. Additionally, our study found the average 3D TRE of internal vessels was less than half that of the medial tattoo, suggesting that these vessels may serve as more stable and reliable surrogates for image registration, especially in cases where arm positioning varies between scans.

We acknowledge the study's limitations. First, it is a small, single-institution, retrospective study, which may affect the generalizability of the results. Second, one challenge of breast re-irradiation is the inability to calculate the TRE of the breast tissue as rigid image registration is unable to account for changes in the breast shape, size, or position. Rigid image registration was used on the bony sternum as the medial tattoo is located within this region, and it is a stable, unambiguous landmark for re-irradiation. In the 2023 ESTRO-EORTC study investigating re-irradiation patterns of care, rigid image registration was the most common method for fusing images for breast re-irradiation target delineation, reported by 70 % of radiation and clinical oncologists [15]. It is beyond the scope of this project to evaluate how the deformation of the breast changes with the bony sternum. Prior studies have examined the use of deformable image registration to resolve breast deformation [39,40] for tumour bed target delineation. Xie et al. [41] explored the use of different B-splines deformable image registration methods to resolve breast deformations following lumpectomy and found that added point metric and masking technique reduced TRE and improved soft tissue match between pre-operative diagnostic CT and post-operative planning CT. However, deformable registration algorithms and their validation for re-irradiation remain a topic of open discussion [42] that is beyond the scope of this paper. Lastly, this study focused only on anatomical changes in tattoo marks and did not examine the dosimetry of current or previous RT plans. Future studies to assess dosimetry profiles could allow further understanding of the interplay between external tattoo changes and dose in breast re-irradiation.

Conclusion

In patients who had prior breast RT, the medial tattoo is not a reliable surface reference for subsequent breast re-irradiation planning. Relying solely on previous medial tattoos as indicators of the previous field border can be inaccurate due to arm positioning and surgical procedure changes that alter surface anatomy over time. Breast irradiation should replicate prior patient positioning and board settings and apply modern registration techniques for accurate evaluation of previous radiation dose. Future exploration of deformable registration algorithms may enhance the accuracy of mapping prior RT dose in patients requiring breast re-irradiation.
