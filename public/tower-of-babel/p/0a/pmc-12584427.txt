Intra-host diversity of SARS-CoV-2 in children with long-term and short-term excretion in nasopharyngeal samples
Authors: Haifa Khemiri, Alessio Lorusso, Kaouther Ayouni, Nicolas Radomski, Monia Ben Khaled, Iolanda Mangone, Adriano Di Pasquale, Cesare Cammà, Ilhem Ben Fraj, Najla Mekki, Khawla Meftah, Oussama Souiai, Imen Ben Mustapha, Semia Rekaya, Henda Touzi, Amel Sadraoui, Zina Meddeb, Nahed Hogga, Hanen Smaoui, Mohamed-Ridha Barbouche, Monia Ouederni, Mariem Gdoura, Henda Triki, Sondes Haddad-Boubaker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe intra-host diversity of SARS-CoV-2 in pediatric populations remains insufficiently studied. To address this significant knowledge gap, the present study investigates the intra-host variability of SARS-CoV-2 in both immunocompetent and immunodeficient children, including those with severe cases. The research encompasses both short-term and long-term infections, aiming to provide a comprehensive understanding of viral dynamics in these vulnerable groups.Materials and methodsThis study analyzed 47 pediatric COVID-19 patients: 37 with short-term infections and 10 with long-term infections including 3 immunodeficient cases. Viral RNA was extracted and detected using RT-qPCR, followed by whole-genome sequencing (WGS) using the Illumina COVIDSeq assay. Bioinformatics analysis was performed with the nextflow/viralrecon pipeline. The number of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and the frequency of Major (M-SNPs) and minor (m-SNPs) missense SNPs were analyzed using the ggplot2 R package and GraphPad Prism software, respectively.ResultsA total of 96 SARS-CoV-2 sequences from 45 patients (35 short-term and 10 long-term shedders), were classified into four variants: Alpha, B.1.160, Delta, and Omicron. SNPs were common across SARS-CoV-2 variants (88.21%-100%), with frequent cytosine-to-thymidine (C > T) substitutions (44.05%-52.85%). Missense mutations were also prominent (57.52% to 80.32%). Variability in SNPs numbers was observed over time in Delta and Omicron variants, particularly in severe and immunodeficient cases. Missense m-SNPs exhibited variation in long-term shedders, especially among severe immunocompetent and immunodeficient patients.ConclusionOur findings revealed significant intra-host variability in SARS-CoV-2 among severe and immunodeficient pediatric patients. These results underscore the critical importance of continuous surveillance of viral variants within pediatric populations to enhance our understanding of their impact on disease progression and clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11759-9.Keywords: Intra-host diversity, Pediatrics, Immunodeficient, Immunocompetent, M-SNPs, M-SNPs, Missense SNP

Introduction

Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) emerged in December 2019 and was rapidly spread, causing global health and socio-economic damages [1]. It was declared as a pandemic in 2020 [2]. SARS-CoV-2 is an enveloped single stranded positive RNA virus, belonging to the genus of Betacoronavirus and subgenus of Sarbecovirus [3]. Its genome size is nearly 30 kb and consists of fourteen open reading frames (ORF) [4]. They encode sixteen non-structural proteins (NSP 1–16), nine accessory proteins and four structural proteins (i.e. the spike (S), the envelope (E), the membrane (M), and the nucleocapsid (N) protein) [4, 5]. As other RNA viruses, SARS-CoV-2 exhibits a high mutation rate, estimated at 1.12 × 10−3 mutations per site per year [6]. The accumulation of these mutations has resulted in the emergence of more potent variants that outcompeted the original strain and achieved globally dominant [7]. Furthermore, the important mutation rate allows SARS-CoV-2 to exhibit significant genetic diversity, giving rise to multiple viral strains within an individual host (intra-host), forming quasi-species [8, 9]. As noted by Domingo et al. (2012), the evolution of these quasi-species is decisively influenced by the high mutation rates during viral replication, as well as the occurrence of recombination events [10]. The intra-host diversity was estimated by measuring the number of intra-host single nucleotide variants (iSNVs) or the number of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) [11, 12]. In the case of SARS-CoV-2 intra-host study, the cutoff value of allele frequency ≥ 2–5% was considered [13–20]. The study of intra-host diversity is relevant for the comprehension of SARS-CoV-2 evolution dynamic. It can impact the development of antiviral agents, vaccines and improved diagnostic tests as well as the public health interventions.

The majority of SARS-CoV-2 intra-host studies focused on the general population, including mainly adult individuals [14–20]. They reported the presence of either different viral quasi-species or recombination within long-term shedders for more than 10 days as well as in immunodeficient (ID) patients [4, 20–25]. Such patients exhibited a significant intra-host diversity [21, 26–29]. The ID population experiences the most prolonged shedding periods up to three months [30–32].

However, the intra-host diversity investigation in pediatric patients was limited. To the best of our knowledge, only one recent study has specifically described the intra-host genetic diversity of SARS-CoV-2 Omicron variants in children [16]. Our whole genome sequencing (WGS)-based study aims to investigate the intra-host variability of SARS-CoV-2 in immunocompetent and immunodeficient children during long-term infections compared to those with short-term infections. We analyzed nucleotide mutations and missense amino acid changes to highlight the genetic diversity within hosts and investigated the evolutionary dynamics of SARS-CoV-2.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Bio-Medical Ethics Committee of Pasteur Institute of Tunis, Tunisia (2020/14/I/LR16IPT/V1) and the local Medical Ethics Committee of Bechir Hamza Children’s Hospital of Tunis, Tunisia (12/2021). It was performed under ethical standards according to the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Informed and written consent was obtained from the parents or legal tutors of children. All samples were investigated after de-identification to ensure patient anonymity.

Patients and investigated samples

The study involved 47 pediatric patients, aged from 13 days to 18 years, according to the WHO age classification, including nine cases hospitalized at Bechir Hamza Children’s Hospital of Tunis (Table 1) [33]. It included mild, moderate and severe forms of COVID-19 as defined by WHO guidelines [34]. The mild form was characterized by common symptoms such as fever, cough, and sore throat, without breathing difficulties or abnormal chest imaging. The moderate form presented similar symptoms with signs of lower respiratory involvement and SpO₂ ≥ 90%. The severe form was associated with viral pneumonia and breathing difficulties [34].

Among these patients, 37 patients were classified as short-term shedders (≤ 10 days) and 10 as long-term shedders (>10 days) [35, 36]. All the short shedder patients were immunocompetent. The long-term shedder group comprised seven immunocompetent patients and three with primary immunodeficiencies (PID) including Ataxia telangiectasia, Agammaglobulinemia and Hyper IgM syndrome. A total of 104 positive nasopharyngeal samples were collected, weekly from February 2021 to February 2022, until SARS-CoV-2 test results became negative. All cases had at least two positive samples. Their diagnosis was performed in the laboratory of clinical virology at Pasteur institute of Tunis.

SARS-CoV-2 RNA detection by Real-time RT-qPCR

Viral RNA was extracted from 140 µl of nasopharyngeal samples using the QIAamp Viral RNA mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), according to the manufacture instruction. The presence of SARS-CoV-2 RNA was detected with RT-qPCR using either HKU protocol as previously described [37] or commercial kits such as NeoPlex™ (GENEMATRIX, Korea), Allplex (SEEGENE, Séoul, Corée du Sud), EurobioPlex FlyCoSyn™ (EUROBIO SCIENTIFIC, Paris), Bio-Speedy®, (BIOEKSEN, Istanbul) and IP2-IP4 (Institut Pasteur, Paris) [38], according to the manufacturer recommendations.

SARS-CoV‐2 WGS

WGS was carried out, for samples with a cycle threshold (Ct) cutoff value of less than 33, at the Istituto Zooprofilattico Sperimentale dell’ Abruzzo e del Molise (Teramo, Italy) (IZSAM). The viral loads (ct values) in the nasopharyngeal swabs used for WGS at Days1, 7, 14, 21 and 28 were presented in Additional file 1: Table S1. The WGS was conducted using the Illumina COVIDSeq assay, as described in previous studies [36, 39–42]. The extracted RNA was reverse-transcribed into single-stranded cDNA, which was then amplified using CPP1 and CPP2 primers. The library preparation and purification were performed with Illumina Tune Beads (Illumina Technology, USA). Amplification product quantification was carried out using the Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA), and quality assessment was done with an Agilent Technologies 2100 Bioanalyser and a high-sensitivity DNA chip, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting libraries were pooled, denatured, diluted to 1.4 pM, and sequenced on the NextSeq 500/550 and 1000 platforms (Illumina, Inc., USA), producing 150 bp paired-end reads.

Analysis of WGS data

Raw sequences obtained from WGS were analyzed using the nextflow/viralrecon pipeline (version 1.0.0), as previously described [43]. In Brief, quality control and adapter trimming were performed with FastQC (version 0.11.9) and fastp (version 0.23.4), respectively. The host reads were removed using Kraken 2 (version 2.1.2). The reads were then aligned to the Wuhan-Hu-1 reference genome (NC_045512/MN908947.3) using Bowtie2 (version 2.5.3) and SAMtools (version 1.15.1). Variant calling and consensus sequence generation were conducted using BCFTools (version 1.15.1) and iVar tools (version 1.4.2). Subsequently, variant annotation was performed with SnpSift (version 4.3) and SnpEff (version 5.0e). Quality control and visualization of raw reads, alignment, assembly, and variant calling results were conducted with MultiQC (version 1.20), which provides a single report for multiple samples. The identification of SARS-CoV-2 lineage and sub-lineage was performed using Pangolin (version 4.3) via the dedicated web application (https://pangolin.cog-uk.io/). The obtained sequences were submitted to the GISAID database (https://www.gisaid.org) [44]. Corresponding Accession numbers are described in Additional file 2: Table S 2.

Visualization of intra-host diversity of SARS-CoV-2 variants

Intra-host diversity was assessed by detecting missense mutations in serial samples from each patient across each SARS-CoV-2 variant. The analysis involved identification and quantification of the missense mutations over time within each patient. Results were visualized using ggplot2, a data visualization package of the R programming language (version 4.2.1). The alternative frequency (AF) of each missense SNPs was calculated for each patient over time and data was presented in heatmaps using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1.244). Missense SNPs with a frequency of less than 90%, were considered as minor SNPs (m-SNPs), while those with a frequency of 90% or greater were considered major SNPs (M-SNPs) [13–16, 19].

Statistical analysis

A one-sample t-test was conducted using SPSS software to determine whether there is a significant difference in the number of SNPs between the first and last collected samples for each patient. Statistical significance was evaluated using a confidence level.

Results

Epidemiological and clinical findings

This study included 47 patients: 18 males and 29 females. Of these, 44 were immunocompetent, and 3 had PID. The average age was 11.7 years (Table 1). They were classified into short-term shedders (n = 37) and long-term shedders (n = 10) by considering ten days as a threshold value [35, 36]. The short-term shedder group comprised symptomatic patients with mild COVID-19 (n = 28), moderate forms (n = 1), and severe forms (n = 6), as well as asymptomatic individuals (n = 3). The long-term shedder group comprised symptomatic patients with mild COVID-19 (n = 6) and severe forms (n = 4). Primary immunodeficient patients were part of the long-term shedder group, presenting mild (n = 2) and severe (n = 1) forms of COVID-19. Regarding therapy, anti-SARS-CoV-2 treatments were administered to three immunocompromised patients and one hospitalized patient among the long-term shedder group, as detailed in Additional File 3: Table S3.

WGS outcomes

Genomes with median depth of coverage >10X and breadth of coverage >90% were considered for downstream analyses, as previously documented [45, 46]. Sequences with low coverage of less than 10% were excluded. Detailed metrics including coverage (%), median depth (X), and other parameters obtained after data analysis for each sample, across all collection time points and SARS-CoV-2 variant groups, are presented in Additional File 4: Tables S4–S7. Investigated genomes were obtained from 45 patients: 35 short-term shedders and 10 long-term shedders. A total of 95 sequences were recovered and classified into four variants: Alpha (n = 20), B.1.160 (n = 4), Delta (AY.122 sub-variant) (n = 40), and Omicron (BA.1 sub-variant) (n = 31), as detailed in Additional file 4: Tables S4-S7. In the short-term shedder group, patients were infected with four variants: Alpha (n = 9), B.1.160 (n = 2), Delta (n = 12) and Omicron (n = 12). In the long-term shedder group, patients were infected with three variants: Alpha (n = 1), Delta (n = 7) and Omicron (n = 2). The immunodeficient patients were infected with the Omicron (n = 2) and Delta (n = 1) variants. Various types of mutations were identified. SNPs were abundant across all SARS-CoV-2 sequences (88.21%−100% of sequence in each variant) (Fig. 1) (Additional file 5: Figure S1). Nucleotide deletions were present in low rates in Alpha (11.52%), Delta (6.47%), and Omicron (10.06%) variants, and insertions were extremely rare (0%−0.93%) (Fig. 1).

Rates of different types of mutation in each SARS-CoV-2 variant: Alpha A, B.1.160 B, Delta C and Omicron D

SNPs can lead to changes in the encoded amino acid, known as non-synonymous or missense SNPs, or preserve the same amino acid, known as synonymous SNPs. Most SNPs observed were missense, with an average of 22.65 (78.24%) in Alpha, 16.25 (57.52%) in B.1.160, 27.7 (70.08%) in Delta, and 37.38 (80.32%) in Omicron variants (Fig. 2).

Distribution of missense and synonymous SNPs across Alpha, B.1.160, Delta, and Omicron variants

The most frequent SNP changes across all variants was the substitution of cytosine to thymidine (C > T) (44.05%−52.85%) (Fig.3A-D, Additional file 5: Figure S2). This was followed by the cytosine to adenine (C > A) substitution in the B.1.160 (25.70%), and Delta (29.34%) variants (Fig. 3B, C). In the Alpha and the Omicron variant, the next most common substitution was the adenine to guanine (A > G), occurring at frequencies of 20.88% and 18.65%, respectively (Fig. 3A, D).

Intra-host diversity analysis of SARS-CoV-2 variants

Intra-host diversity was assessed by analyzing the evolution of SNPs in successive samples collected for each patient across different SARS-CoV-2 variants in both groups of interest (i.e. short-term and long-term shedders).

Variation of SNPs numbers over time

For the short-term shedder group, only 2 samples were obtained by patients. The number of SNPs in all patients varied as follows: 27–39 at day1 (32 of average) and 27–37 at day7 (31.4 of average) in the Alpha, 27–31 at day1 (29 of average) and 26–33 at day7 (29.5 of average) in the B.1.160, 35–51 at day1 (43.9 of average) and 39–58 at day7 (45 of average) in the Delta and 37–56 at day1 (50.9 of average) and 36–55 at day7 (48.5 of average) in the Omicron variants (Fig. 4).

Variability in SNPs across SARS-CoV-2 variants in the short-term shedder group

Considering each patient separately, for the Alpha and B.1.160 variants, the number of SNPs were almost similar in both samples obtained from each patient (Figure 4, Additional file 5: Figure S3). In contrast, for patients infected by Delta and Omicron variants, weak variability of the SNP numbers of was observed in the Delta variants of the patients P26 and P29 (6 to 7 SNP differences, with an average of 6.5) and the Omicron variants of the patients P34, P36, P40, P41 and P43 (7 to 20 SNP differences, with an average of 11.6) (Figure5).

Frequency and distribution of minor (m-SNPs) and major (M-SNPs) missense SNPs

In the short-term shedder group, M-SNPs were predominant on both day 1 and day 7 across all variants (Alpha, B.1.160, Delta, and Omicron). Notably, only the B.1.160 genomes showed a significant frequency of m-SNPs (average of 27.8%) on day 7, precisely detected in a hospitalized child (P13) who showed 55.6% of m-SNPs (Fig. 8A- Fig. 9B).M-SNPs were distributed along the genome of SARS-CoV-2 variants, while m-SNPs were predominantly located in the orf1ab gene for the Alpha, B.1.160, and Delta variants, and in both the orf1ab and S genes for the Omicron variant (Fig. 9). In the long-term shedder group, M-SNPs remained predominant in the Alpha, Delta, and Omicron variants over time. However, the Alpha variant displayed a considerable frequency of m-SNPs on day 1, precisely detected in a hospitalized child who showed 23.1% of m-SNPs (Fig. 8B-Figure 9E). M-SNPs were distributed along the genome of SARS-CoV-2 variants, while m-SNPs were mainly located in the N gene for the Alpha variant, in the orf1ab gene for Delta, and in both the orf1ab and S genes for the Omicron variant (Fig. 9).

Discussion

The investigation of intra-host diversity may provide valuable insights and contribute to a deeper comprehension of disease dynamics that can impact treatments strategies as well as efforts to prevent disease spread and implement public health measures for SARS-CoV-2 as other respiratory viruses [23, 47]. It has been primarily studied in the general population, including mainly adult patients, demonstrating significant viral quasi-species and recombination strains within long-term shedders and immunodeficient patients [14–20]. However, to date, only one study has examined the intra-host diversity of SARS-CoV-2 in children [16]. In our study, we focused on intra-host variation in 47 pediatric patients with COVID-19, including both primary immunodeficient and immunocompetent children.

Our findings revealed various mutations in all SARS-CoV-2 genomes. Substitutions and particularly missense SNPs, which affect gene expression, were the most prevalent ones with 88.21% to 100% and 57.52% to 80.32% rates respectively. Similarly, Jary A et al.. (2020) reported predominance of substitutions (93.1%) with 50% of missense SNPs [25]. In our series, the substitution of cytosine to thymidine (C >T) was the most observed in all SARS-COV-2 variants, followed by the substitution of cytosine to adenine (C >A) for the Alpha, B.1.160, and Delta variants, as well as cytosine to adenine (C >A) and adenine to guanine (A >G) for the Omicron variant. Other studies reported that the change of C >T was the most prevalent [13, 20, 48]. Additionally, other common SNPs such as A >G, G >A, T >C, and G >T were reported [13, 20, 48]. These mutations contribute to the significant genetic diversity and gene expression of SARS-CoV-2 that may lead to the emergence of new strains [7, 25].

For a deeper understanding of SARS-CoV-2 virus-host interactions, the evolution of SNP numbers along the SARS-CoV-2 infection of each patient, as well as the frequency of missense Major and minor SNPs were investigated in both groups (short-term and long-term shedders). Our findings revealed some variability of the SNP numbers of the Delta and Omicron genomes comparing short and long shedder groups, particularly for severe cases and immunodeficient patients. Furthermore, slight arise in minor missense SNPs was also detected in immunodeficient patients infected with the Delta and Omicron variants. Both observations suggest that intra-host diversity is likely pronounced in pediatric long-term shedders, particularly severe and immunodeficient ones that may harbor multiple lineages, co-replicating among the host. Similarly, other authors reported an increasing number of within-host variants during prolonged infection in adults [14, 21, 24, 48]. In addition, Karamitros et al.. (2020) pointed the occurrence of recombination events among lineages infecting long-term shedder adult patients [23]. The variability of SNPs in primary immunodeficient patients likely reflects the impact of compromised immune systems on virus replication. One hypothesis is that immune deficiency may create an environment favorable for prolonged viral replication, thereby increasing opportunities for the genesis of multiple mutations. This process fosters the emergence of diverse viral variants within the host. Through continuous adaptation, viral quasi-species populations of closely related but genetically distinct variants can arise. Interestingly, prolonged fecal shedding of SARS-CoV-2 has been previously documented in PID patients, underscoring the potential risk of within-host variant generation and highlighting the importance of comprehensive monitoring across nasal, throat, and stool samples [31]. Similarly, prolonged viral excretion and the risk of within-host mutations of vaccine-derived Polioviruses (PV) and Enteroviruses (EV) have been reported in PID patients with primary antibody or combined immunodeficiencies following exposure to the oral polio vaccine [12, 49, 50]. Thus, Poliovirus Surveillance among these PID Patients has been recommended by the WHO Global Polio Eradication Initiative (GPEI). Regarding moderate pediatric infection, without prolonged excretion period, Liu et al. (2024), showed no significant intra-host diversity when infected by different Omicron variants [16]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of intra-host variability among pediatric immunodeficient and immunocompetent severe cases. The study of intra-host diversity, especially in the case of prolonged excretion, from different age groups, may contribute to a better understanding of the virus’s adaptability and persistence [13, 20, 51, 52].

On the other hand, the distribution of m-SNPs across the genome showed main location among coding regions: orf1ab, S and N regions. This pattern aligns with observations by Liu et al. (2024) and Armero et al. (2021), who reported intra-host single nucleotide variants (iSNVs), or m-SNPs, mainly in S and orf1a regions [16, 24]. This distribution likely reflects their initial emergence as sporadic mutations at distinct genomic sites followed by their fixation that confer an adaptive advantage to the virus [16, 53]. Thus, diversity can significantly influence the natural progression of the infection, the viral phenotype, and the effectiveness of diagnostic assays [23, 47].

Overall, it is worthy to note that, although 47 pediatric patients were followed in this study, only a limited number of immunodeficient and severe cases were represented. Therefore, a larger cohort of these particularly vulnerable patients should be investigated to validate and extend our findings. Additionally, we emphasize that the investigation of whole-genome sequences and SNP calling may rely on the pipeline, tools, and specific parameters used, which may influence the results.

Conclusion

In summary, the intra-host diversity of SARS-CoV-2 is notably characterized by variability in SNP numbers and frequency of missense m-SNPs, particularly in severe and immunodeficient pediatric patients infected with Delta and Omicron variants. The m-SNPs were mainly located in coding regions (orf1ab, S and N regions) that may confer an adaptive advantage to the virus. Our findings enhance the understanding of SARS-CoV-2 diversification in pediatric hosts that may also impact the sensitivity of diagnostic tools as well as efficiency of therapeutics measures. Continuous monitoring of viral evolution using advanced genetic tools is crucial for tailoring medical treatment, especially, in pediatric population which constitute a sensitive and vulnerable group.
