Identification and Evolution of Salt‐Tolerant Genes in Weedy Rice Through High‐Throughput Resequencing
Authors: Bing Zou, Bing Han, Jianchang Sun, Mingmao Sun, Xiaoding Ma, Li Chen, Di Cui, Jing Ma, Xiaohong Guo, Longzhi Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWeedy rice, a wild relative of cultivated rice, is highly stress‐resistant and proliferates in paddy fields. In this study, 353 weedy rice accessions were analyzed to identify salt‐tolerance genes using population evolution analysis, phenotypic screening, genome‐wide association studies (GWAS), transcriptome analysis, haplotype characterization, gene knockout experiments, and Na+ and K+ ion flux assays. Population structure analysis classified the accessions into six distinct groups. Three salt‐tolerant accessions—HW131, HW136, and HW119—were identified based on leaf rolling degree (LRD), leaf withering degree (LWD), chlorophyll content (ChlC), and nitrogen content (NC) traits. GWAS and transcriptome data pinpointed LOC_Os06g39270 and LOC_Os06g11860 as candidate salt‐tolerance genes. Haplotype analysis and qPCR confirmed two major haplotypes: AHap2 and BHap1. A 2‐bp deletion (TC) at position 818 bp in LOC_Os06g11860 was associated with severe salt sensitivity (phenotypic grade 7), whereas the wild‐type exhibited strong tolerance (grade1). Knockout mutants exhibited significantly increased Na+ and K+ flux across mesophyll cell membranes compared to wild‐type plants, validating LOC_Os06g11860 (OsERFH1) as a crucial salt‐tolerance gene. This study provides novel genetic insights into salt‐stress adaptation in weedy rice, paving the way for breeding enhanced salt‐tolerant varieties.

Introduction

Soil salinity is the second most critical abiotic stress affecting global crop production after drought (Saleh et al. 2025). Saline‐alkali soils represent a vast yet underutilized agricultural resource. The total area of saline and alkali land in China is about 99.13 million ha, and approximately 500 million mu of saline‐alkali land is suitable for growing grain crops, predominantly distributed across 23 provinces, including the northeast, north, northwest, and coastal regions (Liu and Wang 2021; Zhu et al. 2018). Developing salt‐tolerant rice varieties is essential for effectively using these lands, ensuring increased grain production and food security.

Research on salt‐tolerant rice in China began in the 1950s, leading to the development of landraces that perform under salinity levels below 0.3% (Sun et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2019). However, the developed varieties possess only weak salt‐tolerance and still suffer from yield losses under salt stress, which limits their practical utility. International research on salt‐tolerant rice has spanned over 80 years, but only a few major salt‐tolerance genes have been identified, with underlying mechanisms remaining unclear. Early breeding efforts relied on conventional selection, yielding varieties with poor adaptability to moderate‐to‐high salinity soils. Some salt‐tolerant rice accessions, such as Pokkali and its derivatives (e.g., FL478, Nona Bokra, Xuecaiqing, Huangjingnuo, and Changmaogu), have been widely studied. Among them, Pokkali is a traditional cultivar known for its full‐cycle salt‐tolerance (Qin et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2021).

The discovery and functional analyses of salt‐tolerant genes have provided important clues for understanding plant salt‐tolerance mechanisms. By March 2021, approximately 185 salt‐tolerance‐related genes had been cloned. Zeng et al. (2021) identified nine QTL loci associated with germination rate and germination index under salt stress using a BC1F1 population. Shi et al. (2017) detected 22 QTL significantly correlated with salt tolerance by analyzing 478 rice germplasms. Ren et al. (2005) isolated the SKC1 gene, which regulates K+/Na+ homeostasis under salt stress through map‐based cloning. Campbell et al. (2017) conducted the genome‐wide association study (GWAS) on 390 rice germplasms and identified a locus RNC4 related to root Na+ content. However, so far, only Saltol and HST1 successfully applied in improving salt tolerance (Yu et al. 2023; Wei et al. 2024; Deng et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2024; Yuan et al. 2024). These limitations highlight the urgent need for novel salt‐tolerant resources and genes.

To fill the gap of novel salt‐tolerant resources and genes, it is crucial to identify unique genetic resources through phenotypic evaluation and functional analysis. Leaf salt damage and seedling survival under salinity stress serve as comprehensive indicators of salt tolerance. Common parameters for salt‐tolerance evaluation include leaf rolling degree (LRD), leaf withering degree (LWD), chlorophyll content (ChlC), proline content (Pro), catalase (CAT) activity, and glutathione (GSH) levels. Lakra et al. (2015) used ChlC to assess salt tolerance in transgenic tobacco, while Han et al. (2020a, 2020b) and Han et al. (2022) evaluated crop resistance to cold, drought, and salinity using LWD. Plants counteract salt stress through multiple molecular mechanisms, including ion homeostasis, osmotic stress regulation, cytoskeletal remodeling, hormone signaling, and cell wall modifications (Bo et al. 2020; Padmavathi et al. 2024; Zahra et al. 2023). Salt stress initiates a signaling cascade that activates transcription factors (TFs) and regulates downstream stress‐responsive genes (Kruasuwan et al. 2023). Teng et al. (2022) employed CRISPR/Cas9 to generate mutations in the BEARI TF, further elucidating salt‐stress response pathways.

Combining multiple omics approaches is a reliable strategy for identifying salt‐tolerant genes. GWAS, transcriptomics, and proteomics enable rapid and effective gene discovery and facilitate investigations into the molecular mechanisms underlying salt tolerance in seedlings. Weedy rice, a wild relative of cultivated rice, thrives in paddy fields and surrounding areas, exhibiting strong tolerance to salt, drought, cold, and waterlogging (Zhang et al. 2022). Some researchers regard weedy rice as a “gold mine” of untapped germplasm resources within cultivated rice fields (Wang et al. 2021).

Potassium (K+) plays a critical role in plant responses to abiotic stresses and is an essential inorganic cation involved in enzymatic function, stomatal regulation, vacuolar pressure maintenance, and cell elongation (Yu et al. 2023; Dreyer and Uozumi 2011). Early in plant evolution, K+ deficiency and salinity pressure drove the development of adaptive mechanisms to enhance stress tolerance through K+ regulation (Rodríguez‐Navarro and Rubio 2006; Zhao et al. 2019). Studies have shown that salt tolerance is closely linked to K+ homeostasis (Mak et al. 2014; Shabala and Pottosin 2014; Zhang et al. 2019). K+ channels and transporters play pivotal roles in maintaining K+ balance under stress conditions (Véry and Sentenac 2003; Chérel et al. 2014). In apple roots, positive K+/Na+ influx through transporters enhances K+/Na+ uptake, contributing to drought resistance (Qi et al. 2018). These findings suggest that monitoring K+ homeostasis provides valuable insights into salt‐tolerance mechanisms.

In this study, a total of 353 rice accessions, including 315 weedy rice lines, 11 genetic materials from F10 RIL population which was constructed by hybridization of Jigeng88 and WR16, and 27 salt‐tolerant varieties such as Pokkali and Nona Bokra, were used to investigate the evolution and selection of salt tolerance. Genetic analysis classified weedy rice accessions and characterized their genetic backgrounds to elucidate tolerance mechanisms. GWAS and transcriptome analyses were employed to identify salt‐tolerant genes or loci in the genome. LOC_Os06g11860 was selected for gene knockout, and phenotypic evaluation of knockout lines confirmed its role in salt tolerance. Na+ and K+ flux assays were conducted to further validate its function and explore the physiological mechanisms underlying salt tolerance in weedy rice.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Sequencing

A total of 353 rice accessions were used to study the salt tolerance, consisting of 315 weedy rice (WR) accessions, 11 genetic materials are from F10 RIL population constructed by hybridization of Jigeng88 and WR16, a weedy rice, and 27 salt‐tolerant materials containing Pokkali, Nona bokra, and Kenyu88, and so on. Among the 315 weedy rice, 36 materials are from Neimenggu region, 52 materials are from Heilongjiang province, 106 from Liaoning province, 79 from Jilin province, 27 from Jiangsu province, and 15 from Ningxia region. Among the 27 salt‐tolerant materials, 16 materials are from Liaoning province, 2 from Sri Lanka, 2 from Jilin province, 2 from Jiangsu province, 1 from Ningxia region, 1 from Guangdong province, 1 from Beijing, 1 from Hebei province, and 1 from Japan. Sequences from another 76 rice accessions were obtained from a published study (Han et al. 2022), and 277 samples were sequenced for this study (Tables S1 and S13).

Sequencing libraries were generated by Truseq Nano DNA HT Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina USA). Agilent2100 Bioanalyzer was used to analyze the size distribution and quantified using real‐time polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Illumina Hiseq PE150 platform was used to genomes sequenced. High‐quality paired‐end reads with bases were ≥ Q20 and ≥ Q30 were generated and mapped to the reference genome (ensemblplants_oryza_sativa _japonica_group_ irgsp _1_0_ gca _001433935_1) using BWA (Burrows‐Wheeler Aligner, Version: 0.7.8). Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) calling was performed using a Bayesian approach in the package SAMtools. Remaining high‐quality SNPs (coverage depth ≥ 5, maf ≥ 0.05, miss ≤ 0.1). The package ANNOVAR (Version: 2013‐05‐20) was used to annotate SNPs (Wang et al. 2010). Based on the resequencing of 353 rice accessions, the average sequence depth is 13.08 X and coverage rate is 96.25%, with a total of 14.18 G bp clean reads; mapped reads is 13.95 G bp, and the average mapping rate is 98.45% (Table S1). A total of 6,218,543 SNPs in total were identified on the dp6‐miss0.1‐maf 0.05 filtering condition, 638,178 categories were gain, including 1639 exonic synonymous; 1308 exonic nonsynonymous; 6673 intronic; 2993 upstream and 3434 downstream; 1338 UTR3; 476 UTR5; 35 UTR5 and UTR3; 11 exonic stop gains and 4, 8 exonic stop loss mutations; 8 splicing; 18 upstream/downstream; and 620,241 Intergenic (Table S2).

Phylogenetic Tree, PCA, Population Structure Analysis

ADMIXTURE (v1.3.0) (Alexander et al. 2009) was used to analyze the population structure. PLINK (Purcell et al. 2007) and Phylip (v3.697) were used to calculate genetic distance and construct phylogenetic trees. Kinship calculation and principal component analysis (PCA) were performed by GCTA (v1.26.0) software (Yang et al. 2011).

Salt Stress and Evaluation of Salt‐Tolerant Traits

Based on the aforementioned NJ analysis, according to cluster result and genetic distance, a lot of materials clustered in the same layer and have a close genetic relationship. To avoid duplication of materials, 222 representative materials were selected and conducted a core germplasm population for the following GWAS analysis and consisted of 34 WR accessions from Heilongjiang, 64 WR accessions from Jilin province, 88 WR accessions from Liaoning province, and 36 WR accessions from Neimonggu (Table S3).

All accessions were sown in the seedling nursery of the Beijing Experimental Base in 2023. Salt‐tolerance samples KY88 was used as test control (Table S3). Fifty seeds from each sample were selected and disinfected with 1% sodium hypochlorite for 15 min. The seeds were then rinsed three times with distilled water and placed in an artificial incubator at 32°C without light for 2 days to promote germination. When the seeds were exposed to the bud tips, 12 seeds with the same germination were selected from each material and planted on a 96‐well plate, placed in a basin containing distilled water, and incubated in an artificial incubator (14 h of light at 28°C, 10 h of darkness at 24°C, and 50% relative humidity). It was first cultured in distilled water for a duration of 2 days before being transferred to Yoshida nutrient solution (pH 5.5–5.8) for further growth, with the nutrient solution being refreshed every 2 days. The treatment group was subjected to salt stress in Yoshida nutrient solution containing 0.9% NaCl for a period of 7 days, while the control group was maintained in Yoshida nutrient solution devoid of NaCl for the same duration. The experiment was repeated twice.

The morphological indexes that included leaf ChlC, NC, LRD, and LWD on the third day of salt stress treatment were investigated in both the treatment and control groups. Change amplitude was determined using the formula: (control group value − treatment group value)/control group value. On the seventh day of salt stress treatment, the LWD of the treatment group was measured. Both LRD and LWD were evaluated according to the criteria of grades 1, 3, 5, 7, and 9, as detailed in Tables S4 and S5 (Han et al. 2006).

GWAS Analysis and Transcriptome Sequencing

Genome‐wide mean linkage disequilibrium (LD) was calculated using the PopLDdecay (Zhang et al. 2019) software. LDBlockShow (v1.40) (Dong et al. 2021) was utilized to generate part of the Manhattan map and LD heat map. According to the identification of salt tolerance, the extremely salt‐tolerance variety HW131 and salt‐sensitive materials HW157 was selected. These samples were planted and subjected to salt stress treatment using the same methods as before. On the third day of treatment, leaves were collected, and three replicates were set up. Leaf RNA was extracted using the TRIzol method (TRIzol, Invitrogen). Quality control was performed using an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer. For library construction, preliminary quantification was done using a Qubit2.0 Fluorometer, and the library insert size was detected with the Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer. The effective concentration of the library was accurately quantified using qRT‐PCR. All libraries were sequenced on the Illumina platform of Beijing Novogene Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). In the raw data obtained from sequencing, reads were filtered by removing those with adapters, reads containing N, and low‐quality reads (Qphred < = 5) ensuring that more than 50% of the read length was retained (Yan et al. 2013).

Bioinformatics Analysis

HISAT2 was chosen as the comparison tool, and the paired‐end clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 v2.0.5 (Mortazavi et al. 2008). New gene prediction was conducted with StringTie (1.3.3b) (Pertea et al. 2015). FeatureCounts (1.5.0‐p3) (Liao et al. 2014) was utilized to quantify the reads mapped to each gene. For differential expression analysis between the two comparative groups, DESeq2 software (1.20.0) (Anders and Huber 2010) was employed. The resulting p‐values were adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate. Genes with an adjusted p‐value < =0.05 were identified as differentially expressed through DESeq2.

Haplotype Analysis

Haplotype evolution analysis was conducted in 343 rice populations from Han et al. (2022) using PopART software. Sequence files in the original FASTA format were imported into DnaSP and grouped according to landrace varieties, cultivated varieties, weedy rice, and wild rice to obtain haplotype and frequency distribution data. The data were then formatted using Notepad++ and entered into the “Data and Traits” module on the template, saving it as a Nexus file required by PopART. This file was loaded into PopART, and the “TCS Network” option was selected from the “Network” menu, generating a haplotype network diagram (Leigh and Bryant 2015).

Real‐Time Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR)

Leaf RNA was extracted using the TRIzol method (Invitrogen), and its concentration and purity were assessed with an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer. Fluorescence primers were designed with Primer5, using Ubiquitin (UBI) as the reference gene. After screening, two pairs of fluorescent quantitative primers were selected. The first strand of cDNA was synthesized using Suzhou Yuheng UEIris RT mix with DNase‐All‐in‐One (R2020), with rice cDNA serving as the template. The fluorescence quantitative transcription level was measured with an ABI Steponeplus 96‐well fluorescence quantitative PCR system. The reagents included StarLighter SYBR Green qPCR Mix (Universal). The PCR reaction mixture consisted of 1.0 μL of template (cDNA), 0.5 μL of Primer F (10 μM), 0.5 μL of Primer R (10 μM), 0.4 μL of ROX, 10.0 μL of 2 × StarLighter SYBR Green qPCR Mix, and 7.6 μL of ddH2O, totaling to 20 μL. The amplification protocol was as follows: 95°C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 10 s, and 60°C for 30 s.

T2 Mutants and Phenotyping Identification

The gRNA target sequences were designed, the Oligo dimer was prepared and constructed into the CRISPR/Cas vector, and Agrobacterium containing the target gene vector was cultured in suspension. The mature zhonghua11 rice seeds were selected, the glume was peeled off, and the callus was cultured. A sufficient callus was selected and placed in a sterile triangular flask, an appropriate amount of Agrobacterium suspension was added, and the callus was left to infect for 20 min at room temperature. The callus was put on sterile filter paper to absorb the excess bacterial liquid, transferred to a solid co‐medium covered with a layer of sterile filter paper, and cultured in darkness at 26°C for 3 days. One month later, a bright yellow callus with a diameter of 1–2 mm had grown, and the seedlings had differentiated after another 10 days. The seedlings grew to about 2–3 cm, and when there were obvious roots, the seedlings were transferred to the rooting medium to let the seedlings grow to the T0 generation mutants. Identified T2 generation seeds were planted in the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (CAAS) Experimental Station, Beijing.

Na+ and K + Flux Assays Using the NMT Method

Na+ and K+ fluxes were measured in mesophyll cells using the NMT method (NMT‐YG‐100, Younger USA LLC, XuYue, Beijing) as described previously (Li et al. 2023; Zhou, Li, et al. 2022; Zhou, Zong, et al. 2022). All accessions were subjected to salt stress at 3‐day stage. The leaves were collected and immobilized in measuring buffer for 30 min of equilibration. Then the steady‐state fluxes in rice leaves were continuously recorded. During this period, all accessions were always kept under stable salt conditions. Na+ and K+ fluxes were examined, and all results were converted into net flux using JCal V3.3 (youngerusa.com or xbi.org).

Results

Population Structure Analysis of Weedy Rice

To investigate the genetic relationships among rice accessions, we performed a neighbor‐joining (NJ) clustering analysis on 353 accessions (Figure 1b). The accessions were initially grouped into six populations. Weedy rice from Northeast China (Liaoning, Jilin, and Heilongjiang provinces) formed three distinct subgroups, with the Neimonggu weedy rice clustering closely with accessions from Heilongjiang. Weedy rice from Ningxia formed a separate cluster, while Jiangsu weedy rice, classified as XI‐1A accessions (Han et al. 2022), clustered with the salt‐tolerant varieties Pokkali and Nona Bokra. This suggests that widely recognized salt‐tolerant materials belong to the XI‐1A group. Other salt‐tolerant accessions clustered separately at the tip of the phylogenetic tree.

Population structure analysis of 353 weedy rice accessions. (a) Optimal K value determination for population structure. (b) NJ cluster analysis. (c) PCA of rice accessions. (d) Population structure analysis. HLJ, Heilongjiang; JL, Jilin; JS, Jiangsu; LN, Liaoning; NMG, Neimonggu; NX, Ningxia; STM, salt‐tolerance materials.

Population structure analysis revealed that when K = 6, the cross‐validation (CV) error reached an inflection point at 0.2468 (Figure 1a), indicating that the 353 accessions could be divided into six populations. At K = 2, weedy rice from Liaoning was the first to diverge, followed by accessions from Jilin and Heilongjiang. The Neimonggu accessions clustered with those from Heilongjiang, suggesting close genetic relatedness. At K = 5, Pokkali and Nona Bokra separated from the main cluster and grouped with Jiangsu weedy rice, while other salt‐tolerant accessions, including Kenyu88, clustered with Northeast weedy rice (Figure 1d). These findings are consistent with the NJ clustering results.

PCA identified three major components: PC1 explained 51.62% of the total variance, PC2 accounted for 11.03%, and PC3 contributed 8.35%, with a cumulative variance of 71.00% (Figure 1c). This high variance explanation indicates that the principal components effectively capture the population's genetic diversity, supporting the robustness of the analysis.

Phenotypic Identification of Salt‐Tolerance Traits

Salt tolerance was assessed in 222 core weedy rice accessions by applying 0.9% NaCl stress at the seedling stage. On the third day of treatment, LRD was evaluated following the criteria in Table S4 (Figure 2a). The average LRD across two replicates showed a normal distribution, with 35 accessions classified as grade 1–3, 78 as grade 3–5 (including KY88), 69 as grade 5–7, 32 as grade 7–9, and 8 as grade 9 (Figure 2c and Table S4).

Simultaneously, leaf ChlC and NC were measured in both salt‐stressed and control plants. The calculated changes in chlorophyll (ChlC) and NC followed a normal distribution (Figure 2e,f).

GWAS Analysis of Salt‐Tolerance Traits

LD analysis was conducted on 222 weedy rice accessions, with the LD decay curve shown in Figure 3a. The LD decay distance was determined based on the threshold at which R2 decreased to half of its maximum value (R 2 = 0.44). When R 2 reached 0.22, the LD decay distance for the weedy rice population was estimated at 100 kb (Figure 3a).

GWAS analysis and candidate genes associated with salt tolerance. (a) LD decay distance of the weedy rice population. (b) Manhattan plots for LWD, LRD, ChlC, and NC traits. (c) LD block plot of selected salt‐tolerance candidate genes (all plots are detailed in Table S8b).

A GWAS was performed for four salt‐tolerance traits. Manhattan plots revealed that 43 loci were associated with the ChlC trait. In comparison, 27 loci were identified for the NC trait when the logarithm of odds (LOD) score exceeded four across all chromosomes. Similarly, 49 loci were linked to the LWD trait, and 23 loci were associated with the LRD trait under the same LOD threshold (Figure 3b, Table S6).

Further analysis of these loci identified 21 significant candidates, of which four were significantly associated with the LWD trait (p < 0.05): loci at 984,737 bp (LOD = 4.55) and 4,863,772 bp (LOD = 4.37) on chromosome 1, 17,678,558 bp (LOD = 4.48) on chromosome 12, and 12,665,633 bp (LOD = 4.80) on chromosome 9. Additionally, four loci were significantly associated with ChlC: 42,238,822 bp (LOD = 4.57) on chromosome 1 and three loci—23,165,707 bp (LOD = 4.04), 23,709,191 bp (LOD = 4.33), and 23,972,404 bp (LOD = 4.37)—on chromosome 6. Three loci were significantly linked to NC: 6,335,209 bp (LOD = 4.06) and 11,607,360 bp (LOD = 4.09) on chromosome 6 and 14,992,587 bp (LOD = 4.41) on chromosome 8. For LRD, five significant loci were identified: 8,058,056 bp (LOD = 4.65) on chromosome 9 and four loci—3,617,152 bp (LOD = 4.61), 5,797,789 bp (LOD = 4.13), 23,377,374 bp (LOD = 4.24), and 28,087,697 bp (LOD = 4.57)—on chromosome 11 (Table S7). Moreover, five co‐localized SNPs between ChlC and NC traits were identified at 23,974,800 bp, 23,990,177 bp, 23,999,448 bp, 24,000,280 bp, and 4,015,367 bp, all located on chromosome 6 (Table S7).

Using the 100‐kb LD decay distance (Figure 3a), candidate gene identification was conducted within ±100 kb of the 21 significant loci. A total of 26 candidate genes harboring nonsynonymous mutations were identified, encoding proteins related to salt tolerance, including kinase proteins, retrotransposon proteins, cytochrome P450, ethylene‐responsive TFs, and receptor‐like kinases. Co‐localization analysis revealed that one gene, LOC_Os06g40220, encoding OsFBX199, an F‐box domain‐containing protein, was associated with both ChlC and NC traits (Table S8a, S8b).

LD block analysis within the 100 kb region surrounding these candidate genes indicated that significant SNP loci were located within high‐linkage blocks and that genes within these LD blocks are potentially associated with salt tolerance (Figure 3c and Table S8b).

Haplotype Analysis of Salt‐Tolerance Candidate Genes

Haplotype analysis of 26 salt‐tolerance candidate genes identified several key haplotypes associated with salt stress resilience. The gene LOC_Os01g02680 harbors the salt‐tolerance haplotype CAAG, characterized by a GT‐to‐AG variation, with a population frequency of 49.2%. LOC_Os01g02690 carries the CG haplotype, featuring a G‐to‐C variation, with a frequency of 53.5%. LOC_Os01g02750 exhibits the salt‐tolerance haplotype CAGTTGG, with T‐to‐C and C‐to‐T variations, occurring in 53.8% of the population. The LOC_Os01g02760 gene possesses the ATAAT haplotype, resulting from a CTTC‐to‐TAAT variation, with a frequency of 30.15%. Additionally, LOC_Os01g02800 harbors the GC haplotype with an A‐to‐G variation but occurs at a low frequency of 0.33%. The LOC_Os01g02920 gene features the TGCTACG haplotype without variation, with a high prevalence of 90.99%. LOC_Os01g09610 has the A haplotype (C‐to‐A variation) at 13.23% frequency. The LOC_Os09g20970 gene exhibits the CCGTTAT haplotype (G‐to‐T variation) in 42.13% of the population. LOC_Os12g29660 contains the CC haplotype with no variation, with a frequency of 75.96% (Table S9).

The LOC_Os01g72730 gene displays the T haplotype with no variation at 91.46%, while LOC_Os06g39070 harbors the GA haplotype with no variation, reaching 96.09%. LOC_Os06g39780 carries the G haplotype (T‐to‐G variation) at 91.62% frequency. The LOC_Os06g40220 gene features the CGCAT haplotype, exhibiting TC‐to‐CG and C‐to‐T variations at 47.44% frequency. Similarly, LOC_Os06g39270 harbors the GCG haplotype (C‐to‐G and C‐to‐T variations) at 87.08% frequency (Table S9).

Further analysis identified LOC_Os06g11860 with the CC haplotype (no variation, 83.40%), LOC_Os06g20354 with the T haplotype (no variation, 92.03%), LOC_Os08g24760 with the ATA haplotype (CCG‐to‐ATA variation, 54.02%), LOC_Os09g13740 with the ACA haplotype (no variation, 74.88%), LOC_Os11g07060 with the AG haplotype (A‐to‐G variation, 66.99%), and LOC_Os11g07120 with the GC haplotype (A‐to‐G variation, 73.20%). LOC_Os11g07140 harbors the AGTT haplotype (AGG‐to‐GTT variation) with a frequency of 76.92%, while LOC_Os11g07160 features the AATTTATCTGCTAAG haplotype (GCCA‐to‐AATT and T‐to‐C variations) at 76.80% (Table S9).

Moreover, LOC_Os11g07230 carries the TGCCAAGTATAACC haplotype with no variation at 87.79%. LOC_Os11g10740 exhibits the CGC haplotype (no variation, 61.06%). LOC_Os11g39370 presents two haplotypes, CTAC or CAAT (ACG‐to‐CTA or ACG‐to‐CAA variations), at 17.74% frequency. LOC_Os11g46860 harbors the CT haplotype (no variation, 33.33%). Phenotypic analysis revealed that individuals carrying favorable haplotypes for all 26 genes consistently exhibited a salt‐tolerance grade of 1 in HW119, HW13, and HW1361. To date, three salt‐tolerant materials have been validated through excellent haplotype analysis (Table S9).

Transcriptome Analysis of Salt‐Tolerance Haplotypes

Based on haplotype analysis, the highly salt‐tolerant genotype HW131 and the salt‐sensitive genotype HW157 were selected for transcriptome analysis. Differential expression analysis identified 5409 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in HW131 under salt stress (THW131) compared to the control (CHW131), with 2648 upregulated and 2761 downregulated DEGs (Table S10, Figure 4a). In HW157 under salt stress (THW157), 11,777 DEGs were identified compared to the control (CHW157), with 6725 upregulated and 5052 downregulated genes (Table S11, Figure 4b).

(b) DEGs between CHW157 (control) and THW157 (treatment) in weak salt‐tolerant samples. (c) Relative expression levels of LOC_Os06g11860 in CHW131, THW131, CHW157, and THW157. (d) Relative expression levels of LOC_Os06g39270 in CHW131, THW131, CHW157, and THW157. (e) Superior haplotype of LOC_Os06g11860. (f) Superior haplotype of LOC_Os06g39270.

Comparative analysis integrating GWAS and transcriptomic data (Zhu et al. 2022) from HW131 and HW157 identified LOC_Os06g39270, LOC_Os06g11860, and LOC_Os06g20354 as candidate genes (Table S12). As shown in Figure 4c,d, LOC_Os06g11860 and LOC_Os06g39270 were upregulated by 3.24‐fold and 7.21‐fold, respectively, in THW131, while their expression was downregulated by 0.58‐fold and 0.86‐fold, respectively, in THW157. However, LOC_Os06g20354 was upregulated in both THW131 and THW157, leading to its exclusion from further analysis. Consequently, LOC_Os06g39270 and LOC_Os06g11860 were confirmed as key salt‐tolerance genes for further investigation.

To further validate these findings, haplotype analysis was performed on LOC_Os06g11860 and LOC_Os06g39270. For LOC_Os06g11860, two haplotypes were identified: AHap1 and AHap2. The AHap2 haplotype (CC) showed a significant association with NC traits (p = 0.014) and was present in 186 out of 222 samples (83.80%). Notably, all salt‐tolerant materials (HW131, HW136, and HW257) carried AHap2. In contrast, AHap1 (CT) was detected in 29 samples (13.06%) and featured a C‐to‐T variation at 6,305,654 bp, resulting in a Gly‐to‐Asp amino acid substitution. Seven samples (3.14%) were excluded due to missing data. AHap2 was identified as the superior haplotype for further validation (Figure 4e and Table S9).

For LOC_Os06g39270, two haplotypes, BHap1 and BHap2, were identified. BHap1 (GCG) was significantly associated with ChC traits (p = 0.04) and was present in 182 out of 222 samples (81.98%), with all salt‐tolerant materials (HW131, HW136, and HW257) carrying this haplotype. BHap2 (CCG) was detected in 21 samples (9.45%), while 19 samples (8.55%) were excluded due to missing data (Figure 4f and Table S9). BHap1 was confirmed as the superior haplotype for further verification (Figure 4f).

Gene Function Validation

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed on HW131, HW136, and HW257, which carry the AHap2 haplotype, and HW157, HW148, and HW067, which carry the AHap1 haplotype. As shown in Figure 5a, LOC_Os06g11860 expression was significantly upregulated in AHap2 carriers, whereas expression was slightly downregulated in AHap1 carriers. This pattern was consistent with transcriptome results, confirming AHap2 as the superior haplotype. LOC_Os06g11860 was designated as OsERFH1 (Figure 5a).

Functional validation of OsERFH1. (a) qPCR validation of the superior OsERFH1 haplotype. (b) Gene structure of AHap1 and AHap2. (c) Gene knockout site verification. (d, e) Phenotypic assessment of salt tolerance in OsERFH1 mutants. (f) Na+/K+ flux assay confirming gene function. ED, gene‐edited line; WT, wild‐type.

To validate the function of OsERFH1, gene editing was performed. In the T2 generation, two base pairs (TC) at positions 818–819 bp were deleted in the gene‐edited (ED) mutants. Salt tolerance was assessed at 0.9% NaCl, where the LWD was 7 in ED lines and 1 in wild‐type (WT) plants, indicating a significant reduction in salt tolerance following OsERFH1 knockout (Figure 5b–e).

Further functional validation was conducted via ion flux assays. In WT mesophyll cells, Na+ influx fluctuated around 250 pmol·cm−2·s−1. In contrast, in ED lines, Na+ influx increased to ~1500 pmol·cm−2·s−1, suggesting that the loss of OsERFH1 function elevated cytoplasmic Na+ accumulation. Similarly, K+ influx remained near 0 pmol·cm−2·s−1 in WT but reached ~750 pmol·cm−2·s−1 in ED lines, further supporting that OsERFH1 loss altered ion homeostasis (Figure 5f).

Evolutionary Analysis of Salt‐Tolerant Materials and Haplotypes

A total of 37 rice accessions with known tolerance to salt, drought, and cold were selected for cluster analysis (Table S13). The results revealed that stress‐tolerant weedy rice clustered in the inner branches, followed by recognized salt‐tolerant cultivars. Materials from Liaoning province, including Yangeng rice 22, Liaoyan 241, and Yangeng rice 237, formed a distinct cluster, suggesting that weedy rice predates other salt‐tolerant varieties such as Pokkali (HW230) and Nona Bokra (HW236). Additionally, drought‐tolerant (S705, Han et al. 2022), cold‐tolerant (HW240, Han et al. 2020b), and salt‐tolerant (HW261, previously identified) varieties clustered with newly identified salt‐tolerant lines HW119, HW131, and HW136, reinforcing their classification as salt‐tolerant genotypes (Figure 6a).

Evolutionary analysis of salt‐tolerant haplotypes. (a) Phylogenetic clustering of 37 rice accessions. (b) Evolutionary relationships of haplotypes in weedy and salt‐tolerant rice. (c) Haplotype distribution in a 343‐rice population dataset (Han et al. 2022). IR, improved rice; LR, landrace rice; OR, Oryza rufipogon ; STM, salt‐tolerant materials; WR: weedy rice.

Haplotype analysis (Figure 6b) indicated that AHap2 was shared among certain weedy rice and salt‐tolerant accessions and was located in an internal clade, whereas AHap1 was unique to some weedy rice and appeared at the clade's tip, suggesting that AHap1 possibly evolved later than AHap2. Notably, Nona Bokra harbors the AHap2 haplotype, reinforcing its evolutionary proximity to salt‐tolerant materials.

To further examine AHap2 distribution among landrace, improved, weedy, and wild rice populations, haplotype analysis was conducted using a dataset of 343 rice accessions (Han et al. 2022). As shown in Figure 6c, AHap2 was found across all categories, residing in the inner branches of the evolutionary network, whereas AHap1 was restricted to outer branches, supporting its more recent emergence. AHap3 was absent in the 222 core weedy rice germplasm analyzed and was therefore excluded from further investigation.

Discussion

WR ( O. sativa f. spontanea) is a weedy rice species known for its extreme stress tolerance and ability to thrive in cultivated paddy fields (Han et al. 2020a, 2020b, 2022). Over long‐term natural selection and evolution, WR has adapted to diverse environmental pressures, including salt, drought, and cold stress, giving rise to new stress‐tolerant rice varieties (Han et al. 2020a, 2020b). WR accessions from different regions have been subjected to distinct environmental conditions, leading to diverse genetic backgrounds and ecotypic differentiation.

Identification of Salt‐Tolerant Materials and Genes

In this study, 353 WR accessions from three saline‐alkali regions were analyzed to identify salt‐tolerant materials adapted to local soil conditions. WR from the Northeast Soda Saline‐Alkali Zone (Heilongjiang, Jilin, Liaoning, and Neinonggu) was first distinguished from the broader population, followed by WR from the Yellow River Saline‐Alkali Zone (Ningxia) and the Coastal Saline‐Alkali Zone (Jiangsu). The final clustering separated salt‐tolerant materials (STM) from Liaoning, Jilin, and Jiangsu, suggesting that these STM evolved later than WR. Notably, Pokkali and Nona Bokra recognized salt‐tolerant cultivars from Sri Lanka, clustered with WR from the Coastal Saline‐Alkali Zone, indicating their evolutionary proximity (Figure 1). This distribution aligns with the findings of Zhu et al. (2022), which identified saline‐alkali lands across 23 Chinese provinces, including those examined in this study. Thus, our research focuses on WR from Northeast China as a genetic resource for breeding salt‐tolerant rice.

Salt tolerance in WR was evaluated based on LRD, LWD, ChlC, and NC under 0.9% salt stress. Salt‐tolerant materials HW136, HW131, HW256, and HW119 were identified, whereas HW105, HW065, HW157, and HW222 exhibited salt sensitivity (Figure 2a–f). Cheng et al. (2024) and Yuan et al. (2024) used LWD to assess salt tolerance under similar conditions, while Zhu et al. (2022) measured ChlC after salt treatment to identify salt‐tolerant rice. Our findings, consistent with these studies, confirm that 0.9% salt concentration is suitable for WR salt‐tolerance assessment. In contrast, cultivated rice is typically evaluated under 0.5% salt stress, further highlighting WR's superior salt tolerance, as corroborated by the clustering analysis in Figure 6a.

GWAS identified 26 salt‐tolerance‐related genes associated with LWD, LRD, ChlC, and NC across chromosomes 1, 6, 8, 9, 11, and 12 (Tables S8a and S8b). To refine candidate gene selection, haplotype analysis, haplotype ratios, salt‐tolerance classification, and variation loci relative to the reference genome were examined. The results showed that HW131, HW136, and HW119 carried nearly all excellent haplotypes among the 26 identified genes (Table S9), supporting their strong salt tolerance. To further validate candidate genes, transcriptome analysis was performed on salt‐tolerant WR (HW131) and salt‐sensitive WR (HW157), revealing that LOC_Os06g11860 and LOC_Os06g39270 were significantly differentially expressed (Table S10, 11). These genes were upregulated in salt‐tolerant WR (HW131) and downregulated in salt‐sensitive WR (HW157), indicating their potential roles in salt‐tolerance regulation. Functional annotation identified LOC_Os06g11860 as an ethylene‐responsive TF similar to a DRE‐binding protein, while LOC_Os06g39270 belongs to the UDP‐glucuronosyl/UDP‐glucosyltransferase family. Both genes are located on chromosome 6. Notably, Deng et al. (2022) identified the salt‐tolerance gene RST1 on chromosome 6 at 28,586,445–28,590,358 bp, but it does not overlap with the loci identified in this study. Luo et al. (2024) indicated the gene OsERF120 could bind to the DRE/CRT element of the OsMST6 promoter and activate the expression of OsMST6 to positively regulate chilling tolerance. This suggests that LOC_Os06g11860 (OsERFH1) in weedy rice may represent an important salt‐tolerance gene, warranting further investigation.

Identification and Evolution of Salt‐Tolerance Haplotype

Salt‐tolerant WR materials clustered within the inner branch alongside drought‐resistant (S705; Han et al. 2020b, 2022) and cold‐tolerant (HW240; Han et al. 2020a) WR accessions (Figure 6a), supporting the reliability of the salt‐tolerant materials identified in this study. This also provides indirect evidence that salt, drought, and cold tolerance share overlapping genetic mechanisms. These findings reinforce the notion that WR serves as a hidden germplasm reservoir in cultivated rice fields and is invaluable for discovering stress‐resistance genes. Notably, Pokkali, Nona Bokra, and Tesanai clustered in the secondary branch (Figure 6a), suggesting that these widely recognized salt‐tolerant cultivars evolved later than WR materials but earlier than cultivated rice varieties such as Yangeng22, Liaoyan241, and Yangeng237. This evolutionary positioning highlights WR's ancestral status in salt tolerance, underscoring its significance for identifying novel salt‐tolerance genes. Additionally, Oryza rufipogon accessions harbor the AHap2 haplotype (Figure 6c), providing further evidence that AHap2 contributed to the domestication of WR from cultivated rice (Han et al. 2022).

Prospects for the Breeding and Utilization of Salt‐Tolerant Genes

Investigating salt‐tolerant genes in WR has substantial scientific and practical value, particularly in mitigating soil salinization, enhancing crop stress resilience, and securing global food production. By leveraging WR's salt‐tolerance mechanisms for crop improvement, it is possible to develop salt‐tolerant rice varieties that address soil degradation while promoting ecological restoration and sustainable agricultural development. Future advancements in genomic technologies will further unlock the potential of WR‐derived salt‐tolerant genes. For instance, drought‐resistant S705 (Han et al. 2022) has been successfully hybridized with Jigeng88 to develop new drought‐resistant germplasm. Similarly, salt‐tolerant HW119 from Yanbian Prefecture, Jilin, has been crossed with Longdao18 to produce salt‐tolerant germplasm. While the application of WR salt‐tolerant genes is a long‐term and challenging endeavor, WR's unique evolutionary trajectory offers immense value for breeding programs aimed at improving rice resilience to environmental stresses.
