Exploring dynamic changes in nutritional profile, flavor dissipation, and antioxidant activity during the cooking process of marinade
Authors: Shi Nie, Qingfeng Ding, Hui Hong, Ruixi Liu, Mingli Tian, Zongcai Tu, Chunming Tan, Lu Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMarinating is a traditional cooking medium primarily used in preparing braised dishes, with its flavor profile largely determined by the characteristics and processing properties of the spices involved. This study investigated the effects of cooking time on the quality and flavor of a marinade prepared with a specific spice formulation. The results indicated that free amino acids and fatty acids reached relatively high levels at 40 min of cooking. Among the 246 volatile compounds identified by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), 29 key compounds were significantly associated with flavor substances. The content of most flavor compounds showed a decreasing trend, and flavor dissipation was particularly severe during the 60–80 min cooking period. The dominant aroma characteristics of the marinade during the 40–60 min cooking period were balsamic and spicy aroma. Correlation analysis of 29 key flavor compounds with sensory attributes, free amino acids, and fatty acids showed that most compounds significantly influenced aftertaste and woody notes (P < 0.05), and exhibited a significant positive correlation with most amino acids and fatty acids (|r| > 0.7, P < 0.05). Furthermore, the marinade maintained good antioxidant activity throughout the preparation process. These findings provide a theoretical basis for understanding the impact of stewing time on the flavor formation mechanism in marinade.Keywords: Spices, Marinade, Flavor dissipation, Brining time, Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS)

Introduction

Marinating, as the essence of Chinese culinary flavor, embodies the profound charm of traditional culinary culture through its unique flavor profile (Xu et al., 2020). It is crafted by simmering a medley of aromatic spices, seasonings, and water (or stock), yielding a sophisticated flavor system that harmoniously balances rich aroma with profound taste depth (Wang et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2024, Wang et al., 2024). The development of the distinctive flavor of marinated foods from two primary sources: first, the flavor compounds released by spices during the cooking process (Das et al., 2023), second, the thermally induced reactions that occur during cooking (Huang et al., 2021). These extracted flavor compounds interact with volatile components, collectively shaping the unique flavor of the marinated products (Unal et al., 2023).

In recent years, research on the flavor, edible quality, and safety of marinated foods has garnered significant attention (Qi et al., 2022; Yao et al., 2021). Studies indicated that the primary volatile compounds in marinating largely originate from spice components, a finding supported by multiple researchers (Duan et al., 2020; Qin et al., 2020). However, the formation of marinated food flavor is not solely dependent on spices. During the heating and cooking process, amino acids and sugars undergo Maillard reactions, leading to the decomposition or synthesis of new flavor compounds, thereby altering the flavor characteristics of the marinated food. Additionally, thermal processing affects the concentration of key odor compounds, directly influencing the overall aroma of the marinated food. Specifically, insufficient cooking time may result in inadequate aroma release, producing a bland flavor, while excessive cooking time may extract bitter compounds or even generate off-flavors (Yu et al., 2020). Furthermore, continuous heating causes the migration and diffusion of nutritional and flavor components as chemical bonds break and reform. Similar studies have confirmed the critical role of heating time. For instance, Fu et al. (2023) investigated the effects of stewing time on the release of flavor and nutrients in morel soup, found that 40 min of stewing yielded higher levels of volatile flavor compounds, free amino acids, and antioxidant activity compared to other durations. Gao et al. (2025) studied soy sauce and observed that prolonged heating reduced the concentrations of amino acids and nucleotides to varying degrees, altering the corresponding odor-active compounds and thus affecting the intensity of its aroma. Nevertheless, current research has predominantly concentrated on final product characterization, while systematic investigations into the temporal evolution of flavor compounds during marinating processes and their correlation with bioactive components remain notably scarce.

Therefore, this study focused on the cooking process of marinades, systematically analyzing the dynamic changes and volatilization characteristics of flavor compounds at different cooking times to elucidate the mechanism by which cooking time affects the flavor characteristics and quality of marinades. By measuring the antioxidant activity, free amino acids, and free fatty acids in the marinade, and identifying volatile compounds using headspace solid-phase microextraction gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC-MS) a correlation network analysis revealed the intrinsic associations between key flavor compounds and sensory quality, as well as free amino acids and free fatty acids. This study systematically elucidates the regulatory patterns of flavor and quality during the cooking process, aiming to provide theoretical support for optimizing process parameters in the standardized production of marinades, thereby improving flavor stability and quality controllability.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials and reagents

Five fresh silver carp, each weighing (2.5 ± 0.5) kg, were procured from a local aquatic products market in Nanchang, China. They were carefully arranged in water - filled plastic bags infused with oxygen. Subsequently, they were conveyed to the laboratory in a live condition within the span of 1.0 h. These fish were humanely sacrificed by a physical blow to the head. Subsequently, they were gutted, the central spine of each fish was removed, and the remaining fish body was cut into pieces measuring 5.0 cm × 5.0 cm. The pieces were then rinsed under running tap water. All experimental procedures had received official clearance by the Committee on Laboratory Animal Ethics and Welfare of Jiangxi Normal University (approval number: 20231018-A3). Spices were purchased from Chongqing (Sichuan, China), 37 fatty acid standards from Sigma (Deisenhofen, Germany), 19 alkanoic acid standards (98 %, GC purity), gallic acid and quercetin were purchased from Shanghai yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), sulfosalicylic acid from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China), chloroform, methanol, hexane, and dichloromethane were purchased from Macklin (Shanghai, China), o-dichlorotoluene from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (GC purity, Shanghai, China), dichloromethane from Tianjin Damao Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (GC purity, Tianjin, China).

Preparation of marinade

200 g of silver carp bones were sliced into small fragments, and 20 g of peanut oil (Shandong Luhua Group Co., Ltd., Shandong, China) was added. The bones were fried until brown on both sides, then green onion, ginger and boiling water were added, and the mixture was cooked under high pressure for 40 min before the fish soup was filtered. The fish soup was then poured into a pressure cooker (YTD-6B Ceramic Soup Pot, Nepal Home Appliances Manufacturing Co.) and boiled after adding soy sauce (45 g), yellow wine (60 g) (Haitian Condiment Food Co., Ltd., Foshan, China), sugar color (20 g), salt (10 g) and spices (Table S1), with the cooking time set to 20, 40, 60 and 80 min respectively. The volume of the marinade remained unchanged after cooking. Marinade samples were collected at 20 min, 40 min, 60 min and 80 min.

Sensory evaluation

The panelists were trained, and a sensory evaluation panel was formed by recruiting 15 healthy volunteers (7 males and 8 females, aged between 20 and 30 years) from the laboratory. Participants were required to have prior experience in sensory evaluation, be non-smokers, have no history of rhinitis, and confirm no allergies to common spices used in the marinade, as well as no taste or olfactory disorders. The panelists underwent aroma recognition training and were subsequently screened based on their ability to correctly describe and analyze different aroma compounds presented randomly. Finally, the tests were conducted in the dedicated sensory evaluation laboratory of Jiangxi Normal University, with the room temperature maintained at 25 ± 1 °C, the panelists were provided with marinade samples and asked to record the aroma attributes to determine the final aroma profile of the marinade, which included spicy, herbal, five spices, aftertaste, and woody. A 10-point scale indicated intensity: 1–3 (low), 4–6 (medium), 7–10 (high).

Prior to evaluation, the panelists received targeted training for the five attributes-spicy, herbal, five spices, aftertaste, and woody-using pure reference substances to anchor each flavor: spicy was calibrated using capsaicin aqueous solution and diluted Sichuan pepper oil, herbal with menthol aqueous solution, five spices with a hot water extract of commercially available standard five-spice powder, woody with cinnamaldehyde aqueous solution, and aftertaste with licorice solution. Training was conducted three times per week, each session lasting 1 h. The training began with theoretical explanations to clarify the origin of each attribute (e.g., woody derived from the woody aroma of cinnamon and star anise), its sensory characteristics, and potential confusions with other attributes (e.g., five spices as a composite aroma versus the singular freshness of herbal). Panelists then participated in practical tasting sessions, recorded flavor descriptions, and collectively calibrated their perceptions.

The sample presentation followed a standardized procedure: 30 mL of each marinade sample was dispensed into a glass cup. Non-panel staff assigned 3-digit random numbers for sample coding and determined the presentation order using a Latin square design (to avoid the order effect). Only one sample was presented to each panelist at a time, with an interval of more than 5 min between consecutive samples. During the intervals, panelists rinsed their mouths with 37 °C warm water containing 0.1 % sodium bicarbonate to clean the oral cavity of residual flavors.

A double-blind design was implemented throughout the test: panelists were only informed of the 5 evaluation attributes and the scoring criteria, and had no knowledge of the sample processing information. There was no contact between panelists and the staff responsible for sample preparation, coding, and aliquoting. After the test, designated personnel decoded the samples and matched the "sample code - sample information - scoring results" to ensure the elimination of subjective bias and truly reflect the flavor differences of marinade samples prepared with different processes.

The sensory evaluation was carried out in full compliance with relevant laws, rules, ethical and moral principles. The privacy of all participants was strictly safeguarded. All participants provided their informed and clear consent prior to their involvement. The experiment had obtained approval from the Committee on Experimental Ethics and Welfare of Jiangxi Normal University (approval No: 20230820-005).

Quantification of total phenol content and total flavonoid content

The total phenol and total flavonoid contents were determined with reference to Kusumawardani and Luangsakul (2024), with slight modifications. The total amount of phenols in the marinade was determined by Folin-ciocalteu method. 0.2 mL of marinade sample was mixed with 0.1 mL of Folin-ciocalteu reagent and allowed to react at 25 °C for 5 min, then 0.3 mL of 20 % (w/v) Na2CO3 and 1.0 mL of distilled water was added, and reacted for 25 min away from light. The sample was centrifuged for 5 min at 25 °C and 8000 g, take the supernatant and measure its absorbance at 765 nm with an enzyme labeler (Synergy H1, BioTek Instruments Inc., USA) to measure its absorbance at 765 nm. A gallic acid (0–200 μg/mL) standard curve was plotted for total phenol content calculation, with results in μg GAE/g E.

The aluminum nitrate colorimetric method determined marinade total flavonoid content. Combine 0.5 mL marinade sample with 0.5 mL 2 % AlCl3 solution, mix well and then react at 25 °C for 10 min, centrifuge at 25 °C, 8000 g for 5 min, and measure the absorbance at 430 nm. A quercetin (0–50 μg/mL) standard curve calculated total flavonoid content with results in μg QuE/g E.

Determination of free amino acids (FAA)

We extracted and analyzed FAA following Liu et al. (2022) with modifications. Briefly, 3.0 mL of soup sample was centrifuged at 4 °C, 4000 g for 5 min (Shanghai Lu Xiangyi Centrifuge Instrument Co., Shanghai, China), take 2.0 mL supernatant accurately, add 2.0 mL 4 % sulfosalicylic acid, mix well and stand for 15 min. Centrifuge at 4 °C, 1000 g for 10 min. Filter the supernatant through a 0.22 μm organic membrane and transfer it to the injection bottle. The specific analytical conditions were referred to Nie et al. (2025).

Determination of free fatty acids

The experimental methodology is referred to Nie et al. (2025). Briefly, 1.0 mL of sample was mixed with 100 μL of internal standard nineteen alkanoic acid (C19:0, 10 mg/mL) and 2.0 mL of chloroform-methanol (2:1, V/V), vortexed well and leave it to stand for 1 h. Then centrifuged at 4000 g for 5 min and collected the lower lipid layer. Finally, the organic reagent was evaporated with nitrogen to obtain the concentrated lipids. Then 500 μL of 4 % NaOH-methanol solution was added, vortexed for 30 s, and 6 mL of n-hexane was added, the mixture was incubated in a 60 °C water bath for 30 min. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm organic membrane and stored in a gas phase injection vial for analysis.

Statistical analysis

The data analysis for antioxidant activity, total phenolics, total flavonoids, and free amino acids was performed with SPSS v.22.0. Histograms were plotted with the help of Origin Pro v.2021. TBtools v.1.082 was used to generate hierarchical clustered heat maps. Volatile flavor compound aromas were categorized according to chemical book (www.chemicalbook.com). All experiments were repeated three times.

Results and discussion

Effects of cooking time on the nutrient composition of marinade

Free amino acids

Spices contain flavor-related compounds such as amino acids and free fatty acids, which can enhance the taste complexity of food (Che et al., 2024). The changes in free amino acids in the marinade were illustrated in Fig. 1A. A total of 14 free amino acids were detected, most of which increased in content after 40 min of cooking, and the total content reaching the maximum (35.16 mg/100g) (Fig. 1B). This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact that during the heating process, the high temperature disrupts the cellular structure of cooking ingredients, causing the free amino acids originally present in cells to dissolve directly into the marinade (Duan et al., 2020). Meanwhile, bound amino acids are released into a free state due to the cleavage of chemical bonds (e.g., thermal decomposition or enzymatic hydrolysis), which collectively contributes to the increase in total free amino acid content (Duan et al., 2020). During the 40–80 min of boiling the free amino acids might undergo thermal degradation due to the continued high temperatures, resulting in a decrease in their content.

Changes in free amino acids and free fatty acids during the cooking process of marinade. (A) Heat map of free amino acids; (B) Total content of free amino acids; (C) Content of umami amino acids; (D) Content of bitter amino acids; (E) Content of sweet amino acids; (F) Content of odorless amino acids; (G) Heat map of free fatty acids.

Based on differences in their characteristic functional groups and spatial structures, these amino acids can be divided into umami, sweet, bitter, and odorless amino acids. As shown in Fig. 1C–F, umami amino acids are most abundant in the marinade, followed by bitter and sweet amino acids. Specifically, the contents of umami amino acids (Asp, Glu) and sweet amino acids (Thr, Ser, Gly, Ala) were significantly higher at 20 min and 40 min of cooking than at 60 min and 80 min. With prolonged cooking time, the content of bitter amino acids in the marinade gradually decreased with increasing cooking time. Notably, bitter amino acids do not always exert a negative impact on flavor. For instance, Lioe et al. (2004) found that phenylalanine and tyrosine can enhance the umami taste of soy sauce. Within the complex system of marinating, bitter amino acids can interact with umami amino acids, sweet amino acids, and other flavor compounds, thereby enhancing the richness and complexity of the overall flavor (Wu et al., 2024).

Free fatty acids

Fig. 1G illustrated the effects of different cooking times on the free fatty acids in the marinade. A total of nine free fatty acids were identified, including six saturated fatty acids, two polyunsaturated fatty acids, and one monounsaturated fatty acid. Interestingly, the types and contents of fatty acids change with increasing cooking time. Heptadecanoic acid (C17:0) and linolenic acid (C18:3n3) were not detected after 40 min of cooking. This is primarily due to chemical transformations at high-temperature: polyunsaturated fatty acids (e.g., linolenic acid) disappear through oxidation and decomposition into volatile small molecules, while saturated fatty acids (e.g., C17:0), though more stable, may still degrade via hydrolysis or oxidation reactions during prolonged heating (Cui et al., 2017). The total free fatty acid content increased from 9.318 g/100 g–10.914 g/100 g during cooking for 20–40 min, attributed to the continuous release of lipids from the spices into the marinade. Additionally, prolonged heat treatment induces lipid oxidation and decomposition into volatile flavor compounds such as aldehydes, alcohols, and ketones (Sequeira-Muñoz et al., 2006). These results indicate that both free amino acids and free fatty acids are closely associated with the flavor formation of the marinade: they may serve as flavor precursors, providing a rich foundation for the overall flavor profile, and they may also contribute to the enhancement of overall flavor balance by modulating taste and aroma intensity.

HS-SPME-GC-MC analysis

A total of 246 volatile flavor compounds were identified from the four groups of samples, which could be classified into 9 categories based on their characteristics, including aldehydes, alcohols, ketones, esters, terpenes, aromatic compounds, nitrogen-containing compounds, hydrocarbons, and others. Among them, aromatic compounds were the most abundant (53 species), followed by terpenes (33), aldehydes (31), and alcohols (29) (Fig. 2). As shown in Fig. 3A, the samples cooked for 20 min, 40 min, 60 min, and 80 min contained 46, 20, 25, and 27 unique flavor compounds, respectively, with 56 common flavor compounds shared among all samples. These common compounds are mainly derived from spices, while the formation of unique compounds may be related to process parameters such as cooking time. This not only reflects the flavor differences of the marinade at different cooking stages but also serves as an important marker for distinguishing the cooking stages of the marinade. Fig. 3B presented the relative content of volatile compounds across different cooking durations. Aromatic compounds consistently dominated, followed by aldehydes, terpenes, and alcohols. Notably, most flavor compounds exhibited a declining trend with prolonged cooking, suggesting that extended cooking time significantly affects flavor retention in the marinade.

Aromatic compounds are key components of the marinade's flavor, dominating in both variety and content among all flavor substances (Fig. 2A). These compounds, primarily derived from natural components in spices, are dissolved and released into the marinade under high-temperature conditions during cooking (Wang et al., 2023). Studies have shown that the dissipation of aromatic compounds exhibits a clear time dependence: as cooking time extends from 20 min to 40 min, the flavor dissipation rate significantly increases from 39 % to 76 % (Fig. 3C), with representative compounds such as eugenol, methyleugenol, and 5-methoxysafrole showing particularly prominent dissipation. This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the presence of low-boiling-point phenolic compounds in spices. Additionally, during the initial cooking phase, water-soluble components (such as amino acids and small-molecule aldehydes) rapidly dissolve, while low-boiling-point volatile compounds are also released in large quantities along with steam (Wang et al., 2023). This dual effect leads to a continuous decrease in the content of aromatic compounds in the marinade as cooking time progresses. Notably, terpenoids, another core contributor to the rich flavor of the marinade (Fig. 2B), are also mainly derived from spices (Feng et al., 2022). Unlike aromatic compounds, the dissipation of terpenoids shows distinct stage-specific characteristics: the flavor dissipation rate reaches 55 % in the early cooking stage (20–40 min) (Fig. 3C), after which the growth slows significantly, increasing only to 61 % and 62 % at 60 min and 80 min of cooking, respectively (Fig. 3C). This data indicates that the dissipation of terpenoids basically reaches a dynamic equilibrium after 60 min of cooking.

Aldehydes exhibited significant volatility during the cooking process (Fig. 2C), with their content sharply decreasing from an initial 2073.99 μg/kg to 375.94 μg/kg (Fig. 3B), and the flavor dissipation rate increasing from 12 % to 82 % (Fig. 3C). Despite the reduction in content, specific aldehyde compounds play a crucial role in shaping the characteristic flavor of the marinade. For example, 3-Methoxy-benzaldehyde and 4-methoxy-benzaldehyde are the dominant compounds in the marinade after 20 min of cooking, both possessing a hawthorn-like odor. 5-Methyl-2-furaldehyde and 4-isopropylbenzaldehyde, which increase significantly during the 40 min and 60 min cooking stages, contribute pleasant aromas. 4-Methoxy-benzaldehyde maintains a high content throughout the cooking process of the marinade, primarily derived from spices such as star anise and cinnamon, and exhibits green and anise-like aromas (Xing et al., 2025). In addition, a total of 21 ketone compounds were detected in the marinade (Fig. 2D), with their total content continuously decreasing as cooking time extends, and the flavor dissipation rate rising from 38 % to 71 % (Fig. 3C). Esters showed a similar variation trend to ketones (Fig. 2E), with both their types and contents significantly reducing during the 60–80 min cooking period. Among them, benzalacetic acid ethyl ester and alpha-terpinyl acetate are the esters with relatively high contents in the marinade. Benzalacetic acid ethyl ester, mainly derived from cinnamon, has sweet, floral, and cinnamon-like odors. Alpha-terpinyl acetate, widely present in plant essential oils, exhibits fresh floral, fruity, and woody aromas (Turan and Coskun, 2024).

Volatile alcohols are mainly derived from lipid oxidation or Maillard reaction derivatives. During the 20–60 min cooking of the marinade, the content of alcohol compounds continuously decreased (Fig. 2F), and the flavor dissipation rate also increased from 47 % to 65 % (Fig. 3C). Notably, when the marination time was extended to 60–80 min, the content of alcohol compounds tended to stabilize. Linalool has a rich floral aroma and is widely derived from various natural spices, which can bring a pleasant aroma to the marinade. Citronellol is widely used as a flavoring agent in foods and beverages, with citrus, fresh and floral notes (Elsharif and Buettner, 2017). Eucalyptol, the main component of eucalyptus oil, is widely present in natural aromatic oils. It has a camphor-like aroma and possesses bactericidal and preservative effects. However, eucalyptol is volatile when heated (Salehi et al., 2019), so it was only detected in the marinade cooked for 20 min. Studies have shown that 3-phenylpropanol has a herbal odor and can be converted from methyl cinnamate (Courel et al., 2019). Therefore, during the cooking process of the marinade, the content of (E)-ethyl cinnamate gradually decreased, while the content of 3-phenylpropanol first increased and then decreased.

Characterization of volatile flavor compounds

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on GC-MS data to elucidate the temporal changes in volatile flavor compounds during cooking (Fig. 3D). The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) collectively explained 77.13 % of the total variance, demonstrating effective discrimination among samples. Further, vital volatile compounds during the cooking process of marinating were screened using Variable Importance in Projection (VIP). Among the four sample groups, 15 volatile organic compounds (VOCs) with VIP >1 were identified, including eugenol, caryophyllene, and anethole (Fig. 3E). These primarily consist of 7 aromatic compounds, 4 aldehydes, 2 esters, and 2 compounds of other classes, indicating that they can serve as key biomarkers to determine the discriminative power in the PLS-DA model. Notably, eugenol contributed clove-like, spicy, and woody notes to the marinating, enhancing both flavor complexity and functional properties through its antioxidant and antibacterial activities (Qiang et al., 2025). Anethole imparted characteristic anise and licorice sweetness while improving flavor persistence (Wang et al., 2024). These compounds exhibited synergistic effects with cinnamaldehyde and other components, collectively forming the distinctive complex spicy profile characteristic of braised products.

Odor Activity Value (OAV), calculated as the ratio of compound concentration to its odor threshold, was employed to identify key aroma compounds (OAV >1). A total of 29 such compounds were detected across all samples (Table 1), predominantly comprising aldehydes, alcohols, esters, and aromatic compounds. It is evident that at 20 min of cooking, (E,E)-2,4-heptadienal, cinnamyl alcohol, eugenol, and 5-isopropyl-2-methylphenol exhibit relatively high OAV values, presenting fatty, apricot-like, and herbal aromas. This indicates that the aroma characteristics of the marinade at 20 min of cooking are fatty, balsamic, and spicy. At 40 min of cooking, cinnamyl alcohol, eugenol, and 5-isopropyl-2-methylphenol have higher OAV values, with floral, clove, and vanilla aromas, suggesting that the aroma profile of the marinade at this stage is balsamic and spicy. The aroma characteristics of the marinade at 60 min of cooking are similar to those at 40 min. Combined with the aroma descriptions, the aroma features of the marinade at 80 min of cooking are floral and spicy.

Sensory profile evolution

The radar chart (Fig. 3F) illustrated the sensory scores of the cooking marinades at different cooking times. At 20 min of cooking, scores for aftertaste and woody flavor were relatively low; this is because the formation of woody flavor compounds typically requires prolonged simmering (Wang et al., 2025). Furthermore, the primary flavor compounds formed are volatile compounds, which have a short residual time in the mouth, resulting in a lower aftertaste score. The herbal flavor was weaker at 40 and 60 min of cooking. This is attributed to the loss of some volatile herbal components (e.g., low-boiling-point terpenes) through steam evaporation (Siddiqui et al., 2024), which reduces the content of free-state herbal flavor compounds and thus weakens the perceived taste.

Correlation analysis between the 29 key flavor compounds and the 5 sensory attributes revealed that all compounds except benzaldehyde, 3-phenyl-2-propenal, and cinnamylcetate significantly (P < 0.05) influenced the overall sensory characteristics of the marinade (Fig. 4). Mantel-test analysis further indicated that most key flavor compounds exerted a certain effect on aftertaste and woody notes (P < 0.05 and R > 0.6), with benzalacetic acid ethyl ester, eucalyptol, and citronellol being particularly prominent. Eucalyptol exhibits a refreshing woody aroma reminiscent of eucalyptus, accompanied by a slight minty coolness and a subtle herbal undertone (Luca et al., 2023). These flavor compounds not only impart a fresh, herbal aroma individually but also synergize with other flavor substances to enhance the overall complexity and harmony of the flavor profile (Ayu et al., 2024).

Effect of cooking time on antioxidant activity and total phenol and total flavonoid contents of marinade

The total flavonoid content in the marinade exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease as cooking time extended (Fig. 5A). It reached a maximum of 62.11 μg QuE/g E after 60 min of cooking. This trend may be attributed to the hydrolysis of flavonoid glycosides under prolonged high-temperature conditions, which initially increased the content of flavonoid aglycones but subsequently led to the degradation of these compounds, resulting in an overall reduction in total flavonoid content (Gao et al., 2025). In contrast, the total phenolic content in the marinade remained relatively stable throughout the brining process, maintaining a range of 320–350 μg GAE/g E (Fig. 5B). The overall phenolic content remained relatively constant throughout the marinating process. This stability can be attributed to its nature as a macro-level indicator, where the continuous leaching of phenolic acids from the spices was balanced by the degradation products of flavonoids (Del Burgo-Gutiérrez et al., 2023). These phenolic compounds can undergo complex chemical reactions, such as the Maillard reaction with other marinade components (e.g., amino acids and sugars), generating a series of flavor-active substances, including pyrazines and furans (Han et al., 2024). These reactions not only enrich the flavor profile of the marinade but also impart a unique, rich, and robust flavor characteristic.

Analysis of antioxidant properties and total phenolic and flavonoid compounds in marinades with different cooking times. (A) Total flavonoid content; (B) Total phenolic content; (C) ABTS+• scavenging ability; and (D) DPPH• scavenging ability.

The results of ABTS+• and DPPH• scavenging capacities can directly reflect the antioxidant activity of the marinade, thereby indirectly indicating its oxidation level. Throughout the marinating process, the ABTS+• and DPPH• scavenging rates of the marinade remained at high levels, consistently exceeding 88 % and 95 %, respectively (Fig. 5C–D), demonstrating its strong antioxidant capacity. This property effectively inhibits the occurrence of oxidation reactions, reduces the generation of oxidation products, and consequently protects key components in the marinade, such as lipids and proteins, from oxidative degradation, ensuring the stability of the marinade's quality (Li et al., 2021; Singh and Yadav, 2022).

Correlation analysis of key flavor compounds with FAA and FFA

Fatty acids and amino acids are critical precursor substances for flavor compound formation. During the cooking process, these precursors undergo a series of complex chemical reactions, including degradation and thermal oxidation, to produce various volatile flavor compounds. In order to explore the regulatory mechanisms by which amino acids and fatty acids influence flavor compound formation during cooking process, Spearman correlation coefficients were used to analyze the relationships between 29 key flavor compounds, 14 amino acids, and 9 fatty acids, with a correlation network model established (Fig. 6, Fig. 7).

Correlation analysis between 29 key flavor compounds and 9 fatty acids (A) and 14 free amino acids (B).

Diagram of the formation mechanism of key flavor compounds during the cooking process of marinade.

The results showed that fatty acids exhibited a significant positive correlation with most flavor compounds. For instance, C17:0, C18:0, and C18:3n3 showed strong positive correlations with 3-phenyl-propenal and anethole (r > 0.7, P < 0.05). This can be attributed to thermal oxidation and degradation of fatty acids during marinating, which produce aldehydes, alcohols, and ketones (Sequeira-Muñoz et al., 2006). Additionally, extended cooking time further accelerates fatty acid degradation while disrupting the cellular structure of spices, releasing more flavor compounds originally sequestered within cells (Duan et al., 2020). This coregulatory mechanism ensures that fatty acids metabolic dynamics are closely linked to changes in flavor compound levels. Notably, 2,4-decadienal displayed a significant negative correlation with C14:0, C18:0, C20:0, C18:1n9t, and C18:2n6c (r < −0.7, P < 0.05). This is attributed to the thermal oxidative degradation of fatty acids, which generates 2,4-decadienal through a series of reactions (Sun et al., 2021). Linoleic acid autoxidation produces two kinds of conjugated hydroperoxides. Among them, the cleavage of 9-alkoxy radicals generate 2,4-decadienal and 3-nonenal, and 2,4-decadienal imparts a fatty odor to the marinating (Zhang et al., 2022). Studies have shown that its content increases with higher heating temperatures and longer durations (Boskou et al., 2006), consistent with its accumulation during marinating. Under thermal reaction conditions, 2,4-decadienal is prone to oxidative degradation, generating additional aldehydes such as benzaldehyde, acrolein, and 2-butenal (Boskou et al., 2006). Thus, the benzaldehyde content in the marinating remained relatively stable throughout the cooking period between 40 and 80 min. Cinnamaldehyde in the marinade, primarily derived from spices like cinnamon, but can also be formed via benzaldehyde conversion. Decanal, another major product of lipid oxidation with an orange peel or buttery aroma, undergoes thermal oxidative cleavage to generate smaller molecules such as 2-decenal, nonanoic acid, and hexanal (Wang et al., 2024).

Amino acids also exhibited a significant positive correlation with most flavor compounds. For instance, Gly, Ala, and Cys are positively correlated with benzalacetic acid ethyl ester and phenethyl alcohol (r > 0.7, P < 0.05). Certain lipid oxidation products can degrade amino acids into corresponding Strecker aldehydes and α-keto acids via the Streker mechanism. For example, in the marinating, 2,4-decadienal can react with phenylalanine to form phenethyl aldehyde (Hidalgo and Zamora, 2007), an aromatic compound with hyacinth and green leaf aromas. In addition to flavor compounds produced by the thermal oxidation and degradation of amino acids and fatty acids, the marinade also contains a large number of characteristic aromatic terpenoids, such as β-pinene and linalool, which possess pine and lily-of-the-valley aromas, respectively, and are major flavor components in spices. β-Myrcene is commonly derived from spices and can be converted from linalool or by high-temperature pyrolysis of β-pinene; it is an important raw material for the synthesis of terpenoid spices (Behr and Johnen, 2009). Geraniol, with a fresh, grassy aroma, can be further converted from myrcene or citronellol. Citronellol is a monoterpenoid compound; when it undergoes dehydrogenation or oxidation, it forms citronellal, which has a more elegant rose-like fragrance than geraniol (Jiang et al., 2023). In summary, cooking time is a critical factor influencing the flavor formation of marinating. This finding not only provides theoretical support for the standardized production of marinating but also offers important references for practical process optimization. However, this study only infers the impact of flavor compounds on sensory attributes (such as aftertaste and woody) through correlation analysis, lacking actual sensory verification experiments to confirm their specific contribution. In the subsequent research, we will quantitatively verify the actual influence of the 29 key compounds on attributes like herbal and floral notes through recombinant models.

Conclusions

This study investigated the dynamic changes in nutritional quality and flavor during the cooking process of marinade. Results showed that the marinade maintained good antioxidant activity throughout the entire cooking process. At 40 min of cooking, the marinade demonstrated superior texture and nutritional quality. Flavor dissipation was significantly severe when the marinade was cooked for 60–80 min. Furthermore, most key flavor compounds significantly influenced the overall sensory profile of the marinade, particularly its aftertaste and woody notes. Correlation analysis revealed that FAA and FFA were significantly positively correlated with the majority of these key flavor compounds. This study clarifies the influence of cooking time on the flavor substances and quality of marinade, providing a basis for optimizing the cooking process parameters of marinade and possessing practical guiding significance for improving the flavor stability and quality controllability of marinade. Under the specific spice formulation used in this study, a stewing time of 40 min was identified as a potential optimum for achieving flavor balance. However, it must be noted that different spice combinations, due to variations in their chemical composition, may exhibit distinct dissolution kinetics and interactions of flavor compounds, potentially leading to different optimal stewing times. The potential mechanism revealed by this study is that stewing time modulates overall flavor by influencing the dynamic changes of key flavor precursors such as free amino acids and fatty acids, which provides an analytical framework and methodological reference for optimizing stewing processes in a broader context.
