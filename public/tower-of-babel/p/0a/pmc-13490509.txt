Isolation and characterization of Lactobacillus plantarum LP2301 from bovine milk: acid and bile tolerance, antibacterial activity, and genomic safety evaluation
Authors: Hongyan Fang, Qian Li, Mengzhu Song, Yuzhu Sun, Yufei Wang, Ning Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe long-term and non-standard use of antibiotics poses a significant threat to food safety and human health. Probiotics are a promising alternative to antibiotics, effectively combating bacterial infections while reducing the risks associated with antibiotic resistance. Gram staining confirmed LP2301 as a Gram-positive bacterium, and species identification as Lactobacillus plantarum was established by 16S rRNA gene sequencing and whole-genome sequencing. In this study, LP2301 was isolated from the milk of a healthy dairy cow, which exhibits characteristics of acid (pH = 2.5) and 0.3% bile salt tolerance and the ability to secrete effective substances that inhibit the growth of pathogenic bacteria. Through whole-genome sequencing, we identified plantaricin biosynthetic gene clusters (including plnE and plnF) and the absence of hemolytic ability in LP2301. Combined experimental and genomic evidence suggests that the antimicrobial activity is attributable to bacteriocins, particularly plantaricin-related peptides, and Proteinase K treatment experiment confirmed the presence of proteinaceous bacteriocin-like substances contributing to antimicrobial activity. However, the specific effective components require further experimental verification. These findings suggest that LP2301 has potential as a natural antimicrobial agent and a candidate for further evaluation as an antibiotic alternative, pending in vivo validation.Subject terms: Biotechnology, Microbiology

Introduction

Antibiotic overuse and misuse pose a significant threat to global health through multiple interconnected mechanisms, with antimicrobial resistance (AMR) representing the most critical challenge, which promotes the emergence and proliferation of resistant bacterial strains via mechanisms such as enzymatic degradation of drugs (e.g., β-lactamases), target site modifications, efflux pumps, and horizontal gene transfer (HGT) of resistance determinants1–4. These pathogens can transfer to humans through contaminated food products, direct contact, or environmental dissemination, amplifying the public health burden5,6. Beyond driving resistance, antibiotics profoundly disrupt the gut microbiome, leading to dysbiosis characterized by reduced microbial diversity, depletion of beneficial commensals, and overgrowth of opportunistic pathogens such as Clostridioides difficile and Enterobacteriaceae. This disruption compromises colonization resistance, increasing host susceptibility to infections and facilitating the expansion of antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) within the gut resistome4,7–11. Without urgent global collaboration, AMR could cause 10 million annual deaths by 2050, underscoring the imperative for integrated, cross-sectoral responses to preserve antibiotic efficacy and safeguard public health12,13.

The application of probiotics, particularly Lactobacillus plantarum, as alternatives to antibiotics represents a promising strategy to combat bacterial infections while mitigating the risks associated with antibiotic resistance14–16. This approach leverages multiple mechanisms of action inherent to probiotics, offering a multifaceted alternative to traditional antimicrobial therapies. L. plantarum exhibits potent antimicrobial activity through the synthesis of various compounds, including bacteriocins (e.g., plantaricin), organic acids (lactic acid, acetic acid), hydrogen peroxide, and biosurfactants15,17,18. These substances directly inhibit the growth and viability of a broad spectrum of pathogenic bacteria, such as Salmonella spp., Escherichia coli O157:H7, Staphylococcus aureus, Listeria monocytogenes, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Bacteriocins disrupt bacterial cell membranes and metabolic processes, while biosurfactants demonstrate significant antibacterial and antibiofilm properties, effectively reducing biofilm formation and viability of pathogens19–23. The cell-free supernatants (CFS) of L. plantarum cultures retain potent antimicrobial activity, suggesting the potential for topical or therapeutic applications beyond direct probiotic administration21,24.

Beyond direct antimicrobial effects, L. plantarum enhances host defense mechanisms. It strengthens intestinal barrier function by upregulating the expression of tight junction proteins (e.g., E-cadherin) and mucins (e.g., MUC1), thereby reducing pathogen translocation across the gut epithelium25,26. Furthermore, L. plantarum demonstrates anti-oxidative properties, contributing to overall cellular health25. L. plantarum competitively excludes pathogens by colonizing the gastrointestinal tract, adhering to intestinal epithelial cells, and producing an environment unfavorable for pathogenic growth through pH reduction and antimicrobial compound secretion. This competitive exclusion disrupts the colonization and proliferation of pathogens like Salmonella, ETEC K88, and Eimeria spp., reducing infection severity and diarrheal symptoms in animal models20,22,26,27. Oral administration of L. plantarum can modulate the gut microbiota composition, influencing the abundance of indigenous Lactobacilli and potentially promoting a more resilient microbial community28. L. plantarum functions as a multifaceted alternative to antibiotics through its ability to produce direct antimicrobial agents, enhance host barrier and immune functions, modulate the microbiome, and competitively exclude pathogens29–31.

In this research, a strain of L. plantarum, LP2301, was successfully isolated and identified from the milk of healthy cows. Subsequently, its whole-genome sequencing, physiological and biochemical characteristics, growth curve, Gram staining, and bacteriostatic circle were examined. Notably, LP2301 is capable of efficiently secreting substances that can inhibit Escherichia coli, and it can withstand bile salts and acidic conditions. The findings of this study indicate that LP2301 exhibits promising probiotic properties and warrants further investigation as a potential antibiotic alternative and oral modulator of intestinal microbiota.

Results

Identification result of LP2301

The BLAST result of the 16S rRNA gene sequence (Fig. S1A) showed 99.02% identity with Lactobacillus plantarum strain TMPC 2M625 (Accession: OM757923.1) and 98.60% identity with L. plantarum TMPC 31B14 (Accession: OL423291.1). The de novo sequencing result of the pie chart of homologous species classification in the NR (non-redundant protein sequence) database is shown in Fig. S1B.

Morphological and growth characteristics of LP2301

To understand the growth status of LP2301, its growth characteristics were studied by MRS plate observation, Gram staining, and growth curve. The results revealed that LP2301 presented as milky white colonies on MRS plates, with large colonies and smooth edges (Fig. 1A). In Fig. 1B, Gram staining confirmed that it was a Gram-positive bacterium. its growth curve was also measured, and the result indicated that LP2301 entered the exponential phase after 4 h, reached the stationary phase at approximately 12 h (OD600 = 1.1476), and maintained this plateau until 36 h (OD600 = 1.2646). After 36 h, OD600 decreased slightly to 1.2458 at 48 h.

The bile salt tolerance and acid resistance of LP2301

A bile salt tolerance assay of LP2301 was conducted. The results showed that although there was a significant reduction in growth compared to normal medium, LP2301 could still survive in large numbers in a 0.3% bile salt environment (Fig. 2A, B). After being cultured in a 0.3% bile salt solution for 4 h, the survival rate was 91%, which remained at a high level and did not show a significant decrease. This is completely consistent with expectations. Importantly, the colony count returned to the initial level in 2 h. This indicates that the strain population exhibits robust resilience and recovery capabilities, enabling it to surmount transient pressure-induced damage.

To further verify the viability of LP2301, an acid tolerance experiment was conducted. As shown in Fig. 3A, LP2301 can grow normally in an environment with pH=2.5. Except for the significant difference in the first hour compared to the control, there were no significant differences in the number of colonies at 0 h, 0.5 h, and 2 h (Fig. 3B). Compared with the control group, after being treated with pH 2.5 for 1 h, the survival rate of LP2301 was 95%. This indicates that LP2301 can tolerate an acidic environment with pH = 2.5. The strain did not experience significant mortality upon exposure to strong acid, and its survival rate remained extremely high even after being exposed to gastric acid for 0.5 hours. The colony count at 2 h showed no difference from that at 0 h, indicating not just survival but potentially also the activation of repair mechanisms or even the resumption of growth after adapting to the acidic environment. This is crucial for probiotics to cope with the complex and dynamic environment within the stomach.

The antibacterial ability of LP2301

To verify the antibacterial properties of LP2301 as a probiotic, antimicrobial activity assay and antibacterial zone tests were executed which results were shown in Figs. 4 and S1. As shown in Fig. 4A, the number of E. coli on the LB plates with LP2301 supernatant added at 0 h, 2 h, 4 h, and 8 h was significantly lower than that on the LB plates without LP2301 supernatant added. Through statistical analysis, it can also be found that, except for the 4-hour time point, there were significant statistical differences in the number of E. coli between the two groups at the other three time points. Subsequently, the experimental results of the antibacterial zone also demonstrate that the supernatant of LP2301 exhibits a notable antibacterial effect (Fig. 4C). During the growth process of LP2301, it secretes certain metabolites with antibacterial activity. In Fig. S1C, the results indicated that the supernatant of LP2301 exhibited inhibitory effects against Staphylococcus aureus, Salmonella typhimurium, and Bacillus cereus. The reduction in activity after Proteinase K indicates the presence of proteinaceous bacteriocin-like substances (Fig. S1D).

The whole-genome characteristics of LP2301

To examine the genetic characteristics of LP2301 and its ability to secrete effective antimicrobial substances, whole-genome sequencing was performed. The whole genome size is 3,174,340 bp. The GC content of the LP2301 gene is 44.57%. A contig number is 66, and N50 is 139,470 bp. The genome coverage statistics are presented in Table S2. The number of predicted protein-coding genes is 2981. Figure 5A and 5B present the gene enrichment analysis conducted using gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG). An analysis of the pathogen-host interactions of LP2301 was conducted. By using BLAST to align gene protein sequences with the PHI-base database (Pathogen Host Interactions Database), the results are shown in Table 2 (Top 20). The virulence factors obtained from sequencing LP2301 were also annotated, as shown in Table 3 (Top 20). Genes with sequence similarity to those involved in pathogen-host interactions (PHI-base) and virulence factors (VFDB) were identified. These include conserved housekeeping genes (e.g., clpC, clpE, and phoP) that are commonly found in lactic acid bacteria and do not confer pathogenic potential. In this annotation result, no typical enterotoxin genes were found in LP2301. Meanwhile, genes such as lap, clpC, prsA2, gtcA, and efaA, which are related to adhesion, were detected in LP2301. This is because probiotics need to adhere to intestinal epithelial cells in order to colonize, competitively exclude pathogens, and modulate immunity. Although we have identified some genes related to hemolysis (pident 30–60%, high e-value), these homologous genes may encode completely different functions in L. plantarum, such as membrane transporters (ABC transporters), stress response proteins, enzymes involved in cell wall synthesis, 3-ketoacyl-ACP reductase CylG, and biofilm formation. To confirm the absence of hemolysis in LP2301, we also tested its hemolytic properties (Fig. S1E). The results indicated that LP2301 did not form a hemolysis halo on the blood agar plate. Signal peptide-containing proteins were predicted using SignalP. LP2301 secretes proteins with signal peptides (Table 4).

Through in-depth genome mining and functional annotation analysis of transporters from second-generation sequencing, a putative plantaricin biosynthetic gene cluster was found in LP2301, comprising structural genes such as plnE and plnF, along with genes encoding immunity proteins and an ABC transporter system (Table S1). Discussion

LP2301 was successfully isolated from bovine milk with combined acid (pH 2.5) and bile salt (0.3%) tolerance and with multi-antibacterial activity against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. Compared to L. plantarum LPH10, which exhibited tolerance in simulated gastric acid (pH 3.0)32, LP2301 exhibits superior acid survival at pH 2.5. Although LP2301 exhibited a generally versatile carbohydrate fermentation profile, it did not ferment fructose under the tested conditions, highlighting strain-specific metabolic diversity. The strain’s acid production and sugar fermentation profile suggest potential for food fermentation applications, but direct experimental validation is required. This adaptability stems from its versatile metabolic pathways, which enable efficient carbohydrate utilization and lactic acid production, driving pH reduction and preservation while enhancing product characteristics33–36.

L. plantarum exhibits remarkable acid and bile salt tolerance, which is crucial for its survival and functionality in the gastrointestinal tract. Several studies provide comprehensive insights into the mechanisms underlying this tolerance and its implications for probiotic applications. L. plantarum strains demonstrate significant resistance to acidic environments, as evidenced by their ability to maintain high survival rates in simulated gastric acid conditions (pH 3.0)37. This acid tolerance is further enhanced when these strains are cultured in specific growth media, such as terebinth milk, where L. plantarum exhibited higher survival at pH 2.5 compared to standard MRS broth38. The acid resistance mechanisms appear to be strain-specific and may involve complex cellular adaptations. Exposure to low pH triggers a multifaceted response in L. plantarum. Acid tolerance involves the induction of general stress response proteins (e.g., chaperones GroEL, DnaK) to prevent protein denaturation39,40. Cells maintain intracellular pH homeostasis through proton extrusion via F0F1-ATP synthase and increased amino acid decarboxylation (e.g., lysine biosynthesis), consuming intracellular protons39,41. Acid adaptation also remodels cell membrane composition by increasing saturated and cyclopropane fatty acids, enhancing membrane integrity and reducing permeability to protons41. The high survival rate at pH 2.5 (95% after 1 h) suggests that LP2301 has the ability of acid tolerance, which is a desirable property for probiotics intended for oral administration, although in vivo validation is required.

Regarding bile salt tolerance, comparative proteomic analyses have identified key proteins involved in bile salt response and adaptation42. These include glutathione reductases that protect against oxidative injury, cyclopropane-fatty-acyl-phospholipid synthase, maintaining cell envelope integrity, bile salt hydrolase (BSH), ABC transporters, and F0F1-ATP synthase participating in active removal of bile-related stress factors. Notably, BSH activity plays a crucial role in bile salt tolerance and has been extensively studied across various L. plantarum strains43–45. Furthermore, several studies have demonstrated that food-associated L. plantarum strains possess inherent bile salt tolerance, allowing them to survive gastrointestinal transit and potentially confer health benefits46,47. This characteristic is not limited to gut-associated microbes but extends to food-origin strains, indicating an evolutionarily conserved trait among Lactobacillus species47. This tolerance is particularly important for their probiotic functionality, including anti-inflammatory properties, cholesterol-lowering effects, and modulation of gut microbiota37,48,49.

L. plantarum supernatants exhibit antimicrobial activity against multiple clinically relevant pathogens, including Gram-negative (E. coli and Salmonella) and Gram-positive (S. aureus) bacteria, mediated by a complex mixture of bioactive compounds whose efficacy is influenced by cultivation conditions and strain specificity. The antimicrobial mechanism of substances secreted in the supernatant of L. plantarum involves a complex interplay of multiple compounds, primarily bacteriocins, organic acids (lactic, acetic, propionic, phenyllactic acid)18,21,50, antifungal compounds (3-phenyllactic acid, benzeneacetic acid derivatives, hydroxy fatty acids)18,51,52, and bacteriocins or antimicrobial peptides17,53, which collectively disrupt pathogen viability through membrane damage, interference with cellular processes, and inhibition of biofilm formation. In this study, we speculate that the antimicrobial substance secreted by LP2301 is a bacteriocin.

Bacteriocins, such as plantaricin variants, are ribosomally synthesized peptides that exhibit activity against various Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens. These peptides target bacterial membranes, increasing permeability and causing leakage of intracellular components like ATP and K⁺, ultimately leading to cell death. For instance, plantaricin produced by L. plantarum strains destabilizes the membrane integrity of pathogens like Salmonella and E. coli, as evidenced by electron microscopy showing morphological damage19,54. Organic acids, including lactic, acetic, and phenyllactic acids, contribute significantly to antimicrobial activity by lowering environmental pH and uncoupling membrane potential. The proton gradient dissipation disrupts proton motive force (PMF), reducing ATP synthesis and compromising cellular energy metabolism. For example, L-PLA from L. plantarum ZJ316 rapidly depletes ATP in Salmonella cells and increases extracellular conductivity, indicating membrane disruption50,55. Collectively, these mechanisms underscore the potential of L. plantarum supernatant as a natural antimicrobial agent in food preservation and biomedical applications, leveraging its multifaceted approach to pathogen inhibition21,56.

Although LP2301 carries genes annotated in the VFDB and PHI-base databases, these should not be interpreted as evidence of pathogenicity. Many of these genes encode conserved housekeeping functions, such as ATP-dependent proteases, ABC transporters, and two-component regulatory systems, which are common in lactic acid bacteria and may contribute to stress tolerance, niche adaptation, or interference with pathogen virulence. L. plantarum naturally carries multiple intrinsic resistance genes (such as macB, lmrD, and msbA), which are often located on the chromosome, are not transferable, and do not compromise the safety of probiotics. Many resistance genes encode efflux pumps, which may simultaneously participate in stress tolerance, bile salt resistance, intestinal colonization, and other probiotic functions. LP2301 has the core physical characteristics of excellent probiotics, which include excellent gastrointestinal viability. It can not only resist the strong killing effect of gastric acid, but also grow vigorously under bile pressure. LP2301 can secrete stable and bioactive metabolites and effectively inhibit the growth of various pathogenic bacteria in vitro. Limitations of this study include the lack of in vivo efficacy and safety data. Future work should include animal models to evaluate protective effects against enteric infections, comprehensive safety toxicology, and purification of the active antimicrobial peptides.

Methods

Isolation and culture of L. plantarum

L. plantarum 2301 (LP2301) was aseptically isolated from a single healthy lactating dairy cow's milk at a dairy cow farm (Daqing, China). In brief, healthy lactating cows were restrained, and their udder and teats were disinfected. The first three milk samples were discarded. The milk was then squeezed into sterile 50 mL tubes, sealed, and transported on ice to the laboratory within 3 hours for further processing. The milk was shaken well, and 100 μL of milk was added to 900 μL of sterile physiological saline, followed by thorough mixing. The original milk solution and the diluted solution were evenly spread onto MRS agar plates, which were then inverted and incubated in a 37 °C incubator for 12–24 h. Subsequently, single colonies were incubated in a 37 °C incubator for 12–24 h for purification. The purified single colonies were then cultured in MRS broth for 12–24 h until the broth became turbid. Bacterial DNA was extracted, and universal bacterial 16S rRNA gene primers 27F (5’-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3’) and 1492R (5’-GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3’) were used for PCR amplification. The samples were sent to Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for identification. All L. plantarum cultures were incubated statically at 37 °C in an anaerobic jar with an anaerobic culture bag (Mitsubishi Gas Chemical Company) to maintain anaerobic conditions.

Biosecurity statement

All clinical isolates were treated strictly in accordance with the Regulations on Biological Safety of Pathogen Microbiology Laboratory by the State Council of the People’s Republic of China (000014349/2004-00195). All necessary safety operations were implemented to avoid pathogen transmission and infection.

Gram staining

LP2301 was cultured on MRS nutrient agar (AOBOX, Beijing, China) and incubated upside down overnight at 37 °C. According to the method previously introduced57, LP2301 was stained according to the instructions provided with the Gram Staining Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China).

Biochemical identification of LP2301

The biochemical characterization of L. plantarum LP2301 was conducted according to the instructions of Micro-biochemical identification tubes for bacteria (HopeBio, Qingdao, Shandong, China). The specific indicators measured include oxidase, catalase, fructose, galactose, xylose, D-arabinose, D-melibiose, bile broth, esculin, cellobiose, maltose, mannitol, salicin, sorbitol, sucrose, raffinose, inulin, and lactose.

LP2301 De novo sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Bacteria DNA Kit (Tiangen, Beijing). The whole genome DNA was randomly fragmented to an average size of 200-400 bp. The selected fragments were through end-repair, 3’ adenylated, adapter-ligation, and PCR amplification. After purification with the magnetic beads, the library was qualified by the Qubit 4.0 fluorometer, and the length of the library was assessed by the 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. The qualified libraries were sequenced on the MGI DNBSEQ-T7 platform at Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). After sequencing, raw reads were filtered via Fastp (v 0.23.0) by removing adapters and low-quality reads, then clean reads were obtained. A genome survey was performed using Jellyfish (v 2.3.0) for k-mer counting and GenomeScope2 for genome size and heterozygosity estimation based on clean reads. Genome assembly was done using SPAdes (v 3.5.0), and the Gapfiller (v 1.11) was used for filling gaps. Functional gene annotation was performed through homology-based searches using DIAMOND (v2.0.8) against multiple specialized databases, including NR (general functions), KEGG (metabolic pathways), VFDB (virulence factors), CARD (antibiotic resistance), CAZy (carbohydrate-active enzymes), PHI-base (host-pathogen interactions), ARGs (antimicrobial resistance genes), TCDB (transporters), and BacMet (biocide and metal resistance). Additionally, Gene Ontology (GO) terms and COG categories were assigned via orthology mapping with eggNOG-mapper (v2.1.4). Protein feature prediction was further conducted to identify transmembrane helices (TMHMM v2.0), signal peptides (SignalP v6.0), lipoproteins (LipoP v1.0), and subcellular localization (ProtCamp v9.0) for comprehensive functional characterization.

Growth curve of LP2301

To better understand the growth characteristics of LP2301, its growth curve was detected by a turbidimeter. A total of 1 mL of MRS broth was added to a sterile turbidimetric tube to zero the turbidity meter, and then the bacterial culture was dripped into the tube until its turbidity reached 0.5 MacFarland Criteria (MCF). After that, 198 μL of MRS broth was supplied to each well of a 96-well plate, and then added 2 μL of bacterial culture with a turbidity of 0.5 MCF was added to each well. The 96-well plate was placed in a microplate reader at 37 °C, and the OD600 was measured once every hour for 48 consecutive hours. This experiment was performed with at least three independent technical replicates. Data are presented as mean ± SEM.

Acid resistance rest of LP2301

The LP2301 bacterial culture was centrifuged at 8000 × g for 5 min. Subsequently, it was resuspended using 2 mL of sterile physiological saline. Serial dilution (10-fold) was then carried out, and 10 μL of the appropriate dilution was transferred to a cell-counting plate to enumerate the number of viable bacteria. Based on this enumeration, the concentration of the original bacterial culture was calculated. According to the determined bacterial culture concentration, an appropriate volume of the bacterial culture was added to 0.1 mol/L HCl-MRS broth medium (pH = 2.5) to regulate the final concentration of the LP2301 bacterial culture to 107 units per milliliter (CFU/mL). After anaerobic incubation at 37 °C for 2 h, samples of the LP2301 bacterial culture were rapidly withdrawn at 0, 0.5, 1, and 2 h. These samples were centrifuged at 8000 × g for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended with an appropriate volume of sterile physiological saline. Serial dilution was performed again, and specific serial dilution concentrations were selected for colony counting. Based on the observation of the number of colonies on plates at various dilutions in the preliminary experiment, dilutions of 10−3 and 10−4 were employed in this experiment. Ten micro-liters of the bacterial suspension at each dilution was evenly spread on MRS plates. A control group was established. After anaerobic incubation at 37 °C for 48–72 h, the number of colonies on the plates at each time point was counted. The concentration of the original bacterial culture was calculated using the formula: concentration of original bacterial culture (CFU/mL) = actual number of colonies (CFU)/sampling volume (mL) × dilution factor. All experiments were performed with at least three independent technical replicates. Data are presented as mean ± SEM.

Bile salt tolerance test of LP2301

The LP2301 bacterial culture was centrifuged at 8000 × g for 5 min. Subsequently, it was resuspended with 2 mL of sterile physiological saline, subjected to serial dilution (10-fold). Then, 10 μL of the appropriate dilution was transferred to a cell-counting plate to enumerate the number of viable bacteria. Based on the obtained results, the concentration of the original bacterial culture was calculated. According to the determined bacterial culture concentration, 10 μL of the bacterial culture was added to MRS broth medium with a porcine bile salt concentration of 0.3%, ensuring that the final concentration of the LP2301 bacterial culture was adjusted to 107 CFU/mL. After anaerobic incubation at 37 °C for 4 h, the LP2301 bacterial culture was rapidly sampled at 0, 2, and 4 h. The sampled solutions were centrifuged at 8000 × g for 5 min, the supernatants were discarded, and the pellets were resuspended with an appropriate volume of sterile physiological saline. Serial dilution was then carried out, and the serially diluted solutions were used for colony counting. Based on the observation of the colony numbers on plates at various dilutions in the preliminary experiment, dilutions of 10−3 and 10−4 were selected for this experiment. For each dilution, 10 μL of the bacterial suspension was evenly spread on MRS plates. A control group was also established. After anaerobic incubation at 37 °C for 48-72 h, the colony numbers on the plates at each time point were counted. The concentration of the original bacterial culture was calculated according to the colony numbers using the formula: Original bacterial culture concentration (CFU/mL) = actual number of colonies (CFU)/sampling volume (mL) × dilution factor. All experiments were performed with at least three independent technical replicates. Data are presented as mean ± SEM.

Antimicrobial activity assay of LP2301

The sensitivity of E. coli to LP2301 supernatant was assessed as previously described58, which is a co-incubation assay measuring reduction in pathogen CFU at fixed time points (0, 2, 4, and 8 h). LP2301 was cultured in MRS broth at 37 °C for 24 h under anaerobic conditions. The culture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm sterile filter (Millipore) to remove bacterial cells. For protease treatment, the supernatant was incubated with 1 mg/mL proteinase K (Sigma) at 37 °C for 2 h, followed by heat inactivation of the enzyme. Subsequently, 50 μL of the supernatant was extracted and uniformly spread onto three sterile LB agar plates. The plates were placed on the clean bench for 15 minutes to enable the bacterial culture to permeate into the culture medium. The E. coli solution was subjected to gradient dilution, and 50 μL of the diluted bacterial culture was extracted for inoculation onto plates pre-inoculated with LP2301 supernatant. Simultaneously, 50 μL of the E. coli suspension was taken for inoculation onto fresh LB plates, which would function as the control group. After standing for 15 minutes, all the plates were inverted and incubated at a 37 °C incubator for 24 h before counting. Gentamicin (GM) and ampicillin (AMP) served as positive controls. All experiments were performed with at least three independent technical replicates. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. The antibacterial zone test of the supernatant of LP2301 against E. coli, S. aureus, S. typhimurium, and B. cereus was conducted. The supernatant of LP2301 after Protease K treatment was also tested against E. coli and S. aureus.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was replicated at least three times, yielding comparable results. All data were analyzed by GraphPad Prism version 10. The data met the assumptions of normal distribution and homogeneity of variance (verified by the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene test, P > 0.05), thus two-way ANOVA was selected, with treatment and time as fixed effects. If the overall analysis showed significant differences (P < 0.05), the Sidak test was used for post-hoc multiple comparisons to determine the significant differences between different treatment groups at specific time points. The significance levels were set as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001.
