Chemical signaling in reaction networks generates corresponding mechanical impulses
Authors: Oleg E Shklyaev, Anna C Balazs, Keisha Walters
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemical reaction networks (CRNs) in the body are directed pathways that transmit reagents to reactive sites and trigger chemical processes, which ultimately instigate the appropriate physical activity. Typically, models for CRNs do not describe the coupling among chemistry, hydrodynamics and fluid–structure interactions that inherently arise in fluids. Herein, we develop a model that describes the above interrelated physicochemical behavior and show that chemical transport in a CRN spontaneously gives rise to transduction of chemistry into mechanical work, to form a complementary chemo-mechanical network (CMN). To simulate CRNs, we use the repressilator model, a reaction pathway involving biomimetic feedback loops. The encompassing material system is formed from an ordered array of enzyme-coated beads that are interlinked to form a flexible network. Coupling of chemistry and hydrodynamics occurs through the solutal buoyancy mechanism where variations in chemical concentration drive the fluid motion that deforms the flexible network of beads. Consequently, this system displays chemo-mechanical transduction as chemical signals in the CRN are converted to mechanical action. Using this model, we design materials systems encompassing CRNs that spontaneously generate CMNs, which perform the mechanical work of transporting particles or morphing the structure of the elastic network. The propagation of chemical signals along CMN that lead to mechanical actions mimic a nervous system, which transmits signals that instruct a responsive musculature.Keywords: chemical reaction networks, synchronization of mobile oscillators, chemo-mechanical transduction, biomimetic behavior, synthetic biology

Chemical reaction networks (CRNs) that convey chemical signals to instigate specific physical activity can mimic the functioning of nervous systems in living organisms that coordinate sensory information with mechanical action. The development of theoretical frameworks that integrate the reaction dynamics in CRNs with physical activity in synthetic materials could provide insights into fundamental physicochemical events operative in living systems. Constructing such models, however, remains a significant challenge. Using theory and simulation, we couple the dynamic features of CRNs to mechanical action, which leads to the formation of a chemo-mechanical network (CMN). Devising models that capture these simultaneously occurring events could facilitate the development of synthetic materials that display an appropriate, purposeful mechanical response to physical and chemical changes in the environment.

Introduction

The functioning of living organisms is enabled by the combined cooperative actions of multiple systems, including the musculoskeletal (1), digestive (2), and nervous systems (3). The nervous system acts to coordinate sensory information with mechanical action by transmitting signals throughout the body. Chemical reaction networks (CRNs) are involved in conveying these signals to appropriate locations to instigate specific physical activity (4). Typically, models for CRNs describe the evolution of large number of reactants and therefore ignore spatial variations of chemical concentrations and reaction rates within the system (5–7). It is, however, well known that the CRNs can produce distinct spatiotemporal patterns (e.g. Turing patterns), which arise due to spontaneously occurring spatial variations in chemical concentrations and distribution of reaction rates (8, 9). The development of theoretical frameworks that integrates spatial variations and reaction dynamics in the CRNs with the instigation of physical activity could provide insights into fundamental physicochemical events operative in nervous systems. Constructing such models, however, remains a significant challenge. As a step toward overcoming this challenge, we use theory and simulation to integrate dynamic features of chemical signaling and the resultant mechanical action, and thereby reveal the formation of a chemo-mechanical network (CMN) (10–14). The CMN responds to both spatial and temporal input, being dependent on the geometric layout and/or chemical changes in the CRN. The model derived herein allows us to specify not only interconnections between reagents in the CRN but also describe regions of high reaction rates, which we identify as the spatial location of the reaction. We show how controlling the spatial distribution of the catalytic sites and associated reaction rates allows us to program the dynamic behavior of the entire CRN. As illustrative examples, we show the system's ability to transmit specific chemical signals that lead to specific functions, i.e. trigger mechanical transport of particles along specified directions, and controllably generate elastic deformations in networks. The findings allow us to design multi-functional materials that produce specific actions in response to variations of the local chemical environment (15, 16).

We model reaction sites as mobile, microscale beads that are coated with enzymes and immersed in chemical solution. We assemble these beads into an interconnected elastic mesh. Chemistry and mechanics are coupled through the solutal buoyancy mechanism (17). Chemical reactions catalyzed by the beads produce spatiotemporal gradients in chemical concentrations and fluid density, which generate the solutal buoyancy forces and drive fluid motion (18, 19). The flow deforms the elastic network and brings beads into the vicinity of new neighbors where the position of one reactive site can affect the spatiotemporal behavior of a neighboring site (20, 21). The position of the catalytic beads in the spatial arrangement of the CMN determines the sequence of chemical reactions in the network and spatially separates local reaction steps in the CRN. The moving solution transports dissolved chemicals between spatially separated reaction sites (catalytic beads) and enhances chemical exchange (20, 21).

We design bio-inspired materials encompassing basic elements of CMNs: (1) fundamental feedback loops that form the extensive CRNs (22); (2) the elastic networks connecting the catalytic sites; (3) buoyancy forces (17); and (4) the global geometry of the network (e.g. a linear string or circular ring). The model essentially introduces additional geometric degrees of freedom into the CRN, allowing us to specify the sequence of different communicating beads along the network and modifications in the chemical exchange between the beads due to the convective flows. By producing a given action in response to changes in the environment, these materials can mimic the adaptability of living systems. The elastic network could be physically realized as a composite material, which combines solid particles interwoven into a flexible polymeric fabric (23–25). Such materials should, on one hand, be sufficiently porous to allow access of the ambient chemical solution to the enzyme-coated particle surfaces and, on the other hand, sufficiently flexible to fix relative positions of particles but allow their elastic displacements from equilibrium positions.

As noted above, CRN are formed from the interconnection of fundamental feedback loops (22). To demonstrate interplay of events (1)–(4) above, we take the fundamental feedback loop in the system to be the repressilator (26). For a system of three enzymes, bound to the surface of beads of types A, B and C, the repressilator involves a negative feedback loop where chemical production at A suppresses production at bead B, which suppresses chemical production at bead C and finally C suppresses production at bead A. We consider the triplet formed by the sequence of A, B, and C beads as a basic unit and form networks by periodic repetition of this triplet along open or closed rings. We focus primarily on a closed ring topology of repeated bead triplets because these networks support chemical waves and provide simple models that illustrate salient features of the chemo-mechanical coupling.

The generated buoyancy forces form mobile vortices of fluid that follow the propagating chemical waves and deform the elastic network. The relocation of beads by the fluid drag modifies the local chemical environment and thereby can inform the system of its spatial configuration. Moreover, these chemo-mechanical interactions enable the ring-like CMN, to “instruct” particles to follow the chemical signals propagating along a particular configuration of the network. It is this inherent feedback among chemistry, fluid flow and network elasticity that forms the basis of the CMN.

Systems encompassing some components similar to (1)–(4) in our system have been previously discussed in the literature. Assemblies of oscillating units have experimentally been fabricated as collections of immobile nanolitre droplets (27, 28) filled with reactants driving the self-oscillating Belousv–Zhabatinsky (BZ) reaction. Inside these fixed geometries, collections of droplets operate as oscillators that synchronize their mutual behavior and produce a collective response (29). Assemblies of communicating droplets can be used to fabricate analog and computing devices (28). To enable CRNs to control mechanical actions, mechanisms that transduce chemical energy to mechanical form should be involved, as in the case of catalytic patches undergoing the BZ reaction localized around catalytic patches caused swelling and deswelling transitions inside the gel network (30, 31). Depending on the position of the patches, the pulsating units displayed in-phase, out-of-phase synchronizations and even were able to propagate waves of swelling/deswelling gel regions, which could produce mechanical works (30).

It is noteworthy that repressilator networks were used to study effects of CRN spatial topology on the chemical dynamics in synthetic biology to design biomimetic systems that perform cellular functions. Hexagonal lattice arrangements of repressing genes were modeled to understand regulatory communications between the neighboring cells of biological tissues (32). Chemical signals promoted by the genes assembled in the ring-like lattices that described features of biological networks operating in a noisy environment (33). For catalyst-coated, mobile microparticles the coupling of the repressilator scheme and the solutal buoyancy mechanism enabled CRN to exhibit other life-like features such as the formation of dynamical colonies (34, 35) and biological quorum sensing (36, 37).

The manuscript is organized as follows. In the “Theoretical model” section, we formulate equations that control the chain-like CMN in a solution of appropriate reactants. To understand mechanisms underlying the propagation of chemical waves along the network of catalytic beads, we employ a one-dimensional (1D) approximation that permits analytical solutions of the linearized equations. We perform the linear stability analysis that indicates the existence of two types of chemical behavior: stationary distributions of reagents and chemical oscillations. The latter are realized in the form of chemical waves traveling along the networks. The dynamic behavior of the system is controlled by the reaction rates and geometry of the network. We show that the number of catalytic beads in the periodic network uniquely determines the length and speed of the chemical waves propagating along the CMN. Moreover, the system displays an optimal wave length, which we later use in the models of higher dimensionality as a basic chemical signal transmitted along the networks and generating mechanical actions.

We then analyze the behavior of the catalytic rings in three dimensions (3Ds) and demonstrate representative examples of chemical waves propagating along ring-like networks of different sizes. In our approach, we use the similarity of wave propagation along the 1D chain of beads and the 3D rings to analyze the behavior of the 3D systems.

Finally, we couple 3D reaction-diffusion equations to buoyancy forces to generate fluid motion and deform elastic structures. We illustrate that chemical waves traveling along CMNs produce related mechanical waves that propagate along the networks in the same direction with the same velocity. In these examples, the mechanical effects are produced and guided by the chemical waves. By analogy, in a synthetic material, such CMNs can act as nerve impulses, coordinating functions of a fabricated system.

Theoretical model

We consider the behavior of a network of N chemically active beads located at positions {ri(t)}={(xi,yi,zi):i=1…N} that are immersed in a solution-filled container (Fig. 1A). The beads are connected by elastic links that resist bending and stretching forces deforming the configuration of the network. We assume that all beads have the same radius R and a density ρC that is greater than the density of the chemical solution ρ0. The beads are coated with three enzymes that catalyze three steps of a CRN known as the repressilator (26, 38). The local cyclic transformations: A→NAB, B→NBC, or C→NCA, are catalyzed by NA red, NB blue, and NC green beads (N=NA+NB+NC). The reactants A, B, and C have concentrations CZ (Z=A,B,C) and inhibit mutual production with rates described by the Michaelis–Menten kinetics (38, 39)

The notations Z=A,B,C and Z−1=C,A,B indicate permutations of the chemical indexes to describe the cyclic connections among the reagents. KZ is the activation coefficient, PZm is the maximal promoter activity, and the Hill coefficient n=3 determines the steepness of the reaction rate. The cyclic inhibition of reactants in this CRN implies that the production of chemicals {Z}={A,B,C} at each bead strongly depends on the positions of other beads and the efficiency of chemical transport between the beads. Therefore, the behavior of CRNs in terms of spatiotemporal dynamics of chemicals {CZ(r,t)} is controlled by positions {ri(t)} of the catalytic beads linked into the elastic network.

The reagent concentrations CZ(r,t) modify the local density of solution Δρ=−ρ0∑ZβZCZ with respect to the density ρ0 of the initial chemical mixture. The solutal expansion coefficients βZ=−(1/ρ0)∂ρ/∂CZ specify changes in the density caused by reactants CZ. The related buoyancy force fB=gΔρ acts parallel to the gravity vector g.

Equations (1–5) control the behavior of the CMN via the following sequence of processes. The chemical reactions described by Eq. 4 are catalyzed around beads at positions {ri(t)}. The produced mixture of reactants {CZ} causes buoyancy forces that drive fluid flows, as expressed in Eqs. 2 and 3, and move submerged beads to a new configuration {ri(t′)} (see Eq. 5). Therefore, the bead configuration {ri(t)} of the CMN determines the dynamic regime of associated CRN {CZ(r,t)}, which in turn, affects positions {ri(t)} via drag forces exerted by the flow on the beads and hence sets up a feedback loop between the chemistry and hydrodynamics.

The dynamics of the system are characterized by the time scales of relevant physical processes. While changes in the distribution of chemicals occur on the time scale tD*=(r*)2/D controlled by the diffusion, the fluid motion is established on the time scale tν*=(r*)2/ν controlled by the viscous process. The typical ratio tν*/tD*=D/ν∼10−9/10−6=10−3 indicates that motion of the fluid develops much faster than the chemical distribution and that the slowest, chemical processes control the dynamics of the system. For this reason, we first consider the purely diffusive problem decoupled from the fluid motion (described by u(r,t)) and the network dynamics (described by {ri(t)}). Results and discussion

Chemical signal propagating along 1D network

The analysis of linear chains of beads is simplified when the problem is formulated as an infinite chain in one spatial dimension, x, shown in Fig. 1A. We assume, that catalytic beads of three types {Z}={A,B,C} are assembled into a 1D periodic array of ordered triplets localized at positions {xjA,xjB,xjC} within intervals a(j−1)≤x<aj enumerated by index j. Within each interval of length a, the beads are equidistantly separated by a/3 and have respective positions xjA=a(j−5/6), xjB=a(j−1/2), and xjC=a(j−1/6). Here, the catalytic sources of reactions are modeled as periodic Dirac delta functions with strength FZ(x,t).

The dynamics of concentrations {CZ(x,t)} depends on the reaction rates Π and is stationary (∂tCZ=0) below critical value Π<Πc and oscillatory above Π>Πc. The boundary Πc that separates the two regions can be found by performing the linear stability analysis of Eq. 7. We first find time-independent base state solutions {C0Z(x)} of Eq. 7, and then study the dynamics of small perturbations CZ(x,t)=C0Z(x)+eωtcZ(x) that exponentially grow or decay depending on the complex rate ω=ωr+iωi (i=−1). The condition ωr=0 determines the boundary Πc. The stability problem is similar to the problem of an electron propagating in a periodic 1D potential described by the Dirac delta functions. The solutions in terms of the Bloch functions (45, 46) is discussed in the Supplementary material.

The stability boundary Πc(a,k=0) that separates the time-independent base-state solutions {C0Z(x)} below the curve from the stationary chemical waves {CZ(x,t)} above is shown in Fig. 1D. The increase of the critical reaction rate Πc(a,k=0) with an increase in the lattice period a (and inter-bead distance) implies that networks with more closely arranged beads require smaller reaction rates to ignite chemical oscillations. Frequencies ωi(a,k=0) of the chemical oscillations that appear as a result of Hopf bifurcations along the curve Πc(a,k=0) are shown in Fig. 1E. The decrease of frequencies with the increasing a indicates that larger inter-bead separations a/3 cause a slower chemical exchange between the beads.

A chemical network with a fixed inter-bead distance of a/3 can support propagating chemical waves that have different lengths λ=na and may span multiple (n) elementary cells, each containing three beads of types A, B, and C. Critical reaction rates Πc(a=1,k) necessary to ignite the waves are shown in Fig. 1F as functions of the wave number k=2π/λ. Dots of different colors show critical values Πc(a=1,k) for chemical waves with lengths λ=na that span integer number of triplets (n=2,3,4,5,8,10,∞). The minimal rate Πc(a=1,k=(2π/5a)) is realized for the chemical wave that has length λ=5a and spans five periodic cells (n=5). Therefore, a sufficiently long 1D network that can support chemical waves with different lengths should favor waves with λ=5a. The frequencies ωi(a=1,k) of chemical waves increase with an increase in length λ=na and reach the maximal value for waves with infinite wave length k=0, as it is shown in Fig. 1G.

The stability analysis reveals the existence of two types of chemical dynamics: stationary distributions of reagents (Π<Πc) and chemical waves (Π>Πc) traveling along the network.

Nonlinear evolution in 1D system

For supercritical reaction rates Π>Πc, the evolution of chemical waves with different lengths λ=na is obtained by solving Eq. 7 on the interval 0<x<L with periodic boundary conditions CZ(t,0)=CZ(t,L). The chemical waves of lengths λ=na (n=1,2,…) propagate along the network in the direction opposite to x-axis (Fig. 1A).

For longer computational domains L=na (n≥2), chemical waves with multiple lengths are possible. Nevertheless, in the vicinity of the threshold Πc(kn) only waves with the minimal critical reaction rate Πc(kn) are realized. For example, in the domain L=2a waves with k=0 (n = 1) and k=π/a (n = 2) are both allowed, but only the solution with k=0 is realized because Πc(k=0)<Πc(k=π/a) as shown in Fig. 1F. Therefore, in both domains L=a and L=2a, chemical oscillations are realized in the form of standing waves with the time evolution shown in Fig. 2B.

Traveling waves propagate in the domains with L≥3a. Snapshot of chemical waves {CZ(t′,x)} and oscillations CA(t,x=(a/6)), CB(t,x=(a/2)), and CC(t,x=(5a/6)) at the bead locations are shown in the respective Fig. 2D and E for L=3a. Unlike the case of standing wave, the phase shifts between the chemical components CA, CB, and CC are now all different.

Instantaneous concentrations, CA(t′,x), of chemical waves that propagate in the domains with increasing lengths L=an and n = 4, 5, 6, 8, 10 are shown in Fig. 2F–J. Snapshots of concentration CA(t,x) that illustrate propagation of the chemical wave in the domain L=5a are presented in Fig. S1. When the computational domain becomes sufficiently long, waves with wavenumbers k1=2π/L and k2=4π/L are both possible, but the stable one has a lower critical value Πc(k). Therefore, values Πc(k2=(4π/10a))<Πc(k1=(2π/10a)) shown in Fig. 1F (with magenta and orange dots) imply that in the domain L=10a (Fig. 2J) the wave with k2=4π/L is stable and we observe two waves.

During the oscillatory process (see Fig. 2B and E), chemical concentrations at the catalytic bead A display variations between minimal and maximal values, which can serve as an amplitude AZ=maxCZ(t,x)−minCZ(t,x) of chemical oscillations. Amplitudes AZ are plotted in Fig. 2K with lines of different color to show waves of different lengths λn=na. Above the instability threshold, we fit amplitudes AZ(ΔΠ) as functions of distance from the respective bifurcation points, ΔΠ=Π−Πc(k=(2π/na)) for the first five numerical values ΔΠ and find approximately square root dependence, AZ(kn)∝ΔΠ1/2. The velocities of the waves strongly depend on their wavelengths λn and demonstrate weak dependence on the supercritical distance Π−Πc (Fig. 2L). The latter results imply that the velocity of propagating signal is determined by the chemical exchange between neighboring beads, which is controlled by the scale r*=D/γ.

The findings indicate that chemical waves of an optimal length propagating above the critical reaction rate Πc operate as signals transmitted along the network with a specific velocity. These signals, characterized by the spatial length, amplitude and velocity, can drive mechanical action in the CMN. The waves propagate in the direction of catalytic beads A←B←C, opposite to that in which chemicals produced by the beads suppress one another along the network, i.e. A→B→C (Fig. 1A).

Chemical signal propagating along 3D ring-like networks

The simplest 3D networks of catalytic beads constitute a linear chain with open ends (Fig. S2) or closed loops in a ring-like manner (Fig. 3A). The open chain-like networks need to be sufficiently long to generate chemical waves in the middle region, where effects of two ends are minimized (Fig. S2 and Video SV1). To neglect effects due to end sites, we focus on the 3D closed chains in the form of rings that support chemical waves and feature behavior similar to that of 1D periodic chains considered above. In other words, the ring structure should support the propagation of chemical waves with comparable wavelengths and speeds as seen in sufficiently long linear structures. Consequently, the above studies of 1D periodic chains provide intuition for the behavior in the ring and long chain structures.

The chemical dynamics of the 3D networks catalyzed by N beads is obtained by solving three coupled reaction-diffusion equations, Eq. 6, in a rectangular domain with the lateral and vertical dimensions given by 0≤x,y≤L and 0≤z≤H, respectively (Fig. 3B–D). Homogeneous concentrations CZ(t=0)=0.3 M for all reactants were used as the initial conditions. The chemical dynamics are not sensitive to the initial values, which affect the duration of the transient regime leading to the resulting steady oscillations. We assume D=0.6×10−9m2s−1, γ=0.6×0.1s−1, and P0m=3×10−7mols−1. Chemical signals produced by the 3D ring-like networks are determined by the configuration of the system, which is prescribed by the geometry of computational domain (L, H), coordinates of the immobile catalytic chain {ri}, and chemical activity of beads characterized by the relative reaction rate Π−Πc. In addition to the repeating triplets of beads, the total number of beads N introduces additional spatial periodicity in the ring and uniquely determines the critical value Πc.

Similar to the 1D periodic system, the 3D network produces a time-independent concentrations {CZ(r)} (Fig. 3B–D) below the critical reaction rate Πc. Above Πc, the network can produce oscillatory regimes CZ(t,r) that include standing waves (Figs. 3E–H, S3, S4, and S6) and waves traveling along the ring (Figs. 4, S5, S7, and S8).

Unlike the 1D case, beads in the ring can exchange chemical signals not only with beads along the network (tangent to the circumference), but also with beads located on the diametrically opposite sides of the ring. For example, bead one in Fig. 3A can communicate with beads five and six. These communications can be neglected as the radius of the ring becomes sufficiently larger than the scale r*=(D/γ)1/2 characterizing the distance over which chemicals {Z} are transported by diffusion.

Above the threshold Π>Πc, the smallest ring-like networks, with N=3 and N=6, produce chemical standing waves where concentrations CA(t,r) oscillate between minimal and maximal distributions, as shown in Fig. 3E and F (Fig. S3A and B). The oscillating chemical signals in the N=3 and N=6 cases are similar to the waves (k=0) realized in the 1D chain with the periodic conditions imposed over n=1 and n=2 triplets (see Fig. 2–C).

The behavior of larger networks is more complex; a system can encompass standing waves with different spatial periods. This scenario is realized for N=24 beads at different values of reaction rate Π, as shown in Fig. 3G and H (also Fig. S4D and E).

With increases in the number of beads N, the majority of ring-like networks can support at least one traveling chemical wave; the propagation of these waves is illustrated in snapshots of concentrations CA(t,r) for two sequential moments (Fig. 4). For example, networks that contain N=9 (Fig. 4A), N=15 (Fig. 4B), and N=27 (Fig. 4F) catalytic beads support one traveling wave. Networks shown in Fig. 4C, and Fig. 4E support two, and networks shown in Fig. 4G and H contain four waves (full sequence of snapshots is shown in Fig. S5). All these waves travel in the counterclockwise direction, as determined by the sequence A→B→C of cyclic suppressing interactions. The number of chemical waves increases with the number of beads, N, along the network, but also can be changed by varying Π.

To support chemical waves, the network of collinear beads does not need to be exactly circular and can be deformed. The closed-loop chains can have other configurations as long as the distance between beads is larger than r*=D/γ, a condition that ensures near-neighbor communication along the network. We expect that signals propagating along large, looped networks should be similar to waves that propagate along sufficiently long open chains, where effects of the edges can be neglected.

To illustrate ability of open chain-like networks to transmit chemical signals, we simulate the 3D dynamics produced by a chain of beads in the form of a spiral. The network is sufficiently long (N = 246) and has two open ends. The dynamics of concentration CA(r,t) shown in Fig. S9 (and Video SV5) illustrate propagation of chemical signals along the network. Unlike chemical dynamics in the closed rings, where regimes of propagating waves are established relatively quickly, waves produced by the spiral slowly evolve to stationary oscillations that span the entire network.

The theory of waves produced by the ring-like structures should be modified to cases involving long open chains. For example, a long chain may connect two rings containing different number of beads and therefore producing chemical oscillations of different frequencies.

Mechanical actions enabled by propagating chemical signals

In chemical mixtures, spontaneously appearing solutal buoyancy forces drive fluid flows, which naturally couple chemical {CZ} and mechanical {ri} degrees of freedom in the CMN. To simulate the mechanism of transduction, which moves the submerged objects in response to chemical waves, we solve Eqs. 2–5 in a rectangular box (0≤x,y≤L and 0≤z≤H). On all boundaries, we impose no-slip conditions for fluid velocity u=0 and absence of chemical fluxes ∂nCZ=0 normal to the walls. We use parameters D=0.6×10−9m2s−1, γ=0.6×0.1s−1, reaction rate Π=5P0m, and expansion coefficient βA=5β0, with P0m=3×10−7mols−1 and β0=4×10−6M−1.

Reactants {CZ(t,r)} change the local density of solution Δρ=−ρ0∑ZβZCZ in proportion to expansion coefficients βZ, which couple chemical composition {CZ} to fluid flow u(t,r) via buoyancy forces fB=gΔρ (Eq. 3). For simplicity, we assume βA≠0, βB=βC=0. Inclusion of additional expansion coefficients βZ≠0 (for Z=A,B,C) modifies the dynamics of network, but still produces oscillations of chemical concentrations CZ together with pulsations of fluid velocities around the catalytic beads (34). The generated flow drags catalytic beads to a new configuration {ri(t)}, which produces new distributions of chemicals {CZ(t,r)}. Elastic bonds between the beads resist the imposed deformations and work to restore initial, relaxed configuration {ri(t=0)} of the network. We place the ring {ri(t=0)} at the bottom in the center of domain. This position is unstable (47) and the network is dragged by the flow to one of the corners. To keep the ring at the center, we apply to beads (j) a potential U=α(rj(t)−rj(0))2 that mimics binding to the bottom wall via additional elastic bonds. Therefore, instantaneous configuration of the network is a result of competition between elastic forces generated by the flexible network (described by ks and kb), applied potential U(α) and drag forces imposed by the flow (set by βA).

The propagation of mechanical deformations along the circular network is presented in Fig. 5. Chemical waves (shown in magenta) travel in the counterclockwise direction along the ring and generate fluid flows shown in Fig. 5A with black arrows. The chemical CA(t,r) produced at the red beads reduces the local density (βA<0) of solution. As the fluid moves from higher to lower density, an upward component of flow is produced above the red beads (first panel in Fig. 5A). Regions with the upward flow follow the chemical waves propagating along the network and impose fluid drag on the beads of the network. The circulating, upward flow drags the nearby beads to elevated positions ri(t)={xi,yi,zi}. As this upward flow shifts after the chemical wave further along the ring, the local drag forces decrease and elastic forces imposed by the elastic bonds return the beads to the bottom wall.

The fluid vortices revolving along the network of beads can also entrain submerged tracer particles. Figure 6 illustrates how particles (shown in rainbow colors), which initially were randomly dispersed on the bottom, are swept by the chemically generated flow and follow propagating chemical waves shown in magenta (high values of CA(t,r)). The efficiency of transport depends on the coupling between the concentration CA(t,r) in the chemical wave and moving tracers. This coupling involves the expansion coefficient βA that relates the local reagent concentration to the buoyancy force f=−gρ0βACA, and the fluid drag of the tracers that depends on the structure of the fluid vortices and geometry of the particles. For larger βA, the submerged objects are more strongly bound to the propagating chemical wave that leads the motion. When the fluid velocities are not sufficiently strong to entrain the particles, they lag behind the chemical wave and the connection between the particles and chemical wave is lost. Conclusions

Local heterogeneities in chemical concentrations and reaction rates are not usually considered in models for CRNs. Herein, we developed a theoretical model that captures the spatial layout of the reactive sites and local differences in reaction rates along chemical pathways. To encode the spatial dimension into the chemical pathway, we used mobile catalytic beads assembled into an elastic network in the form of a linear string and a ring. In these networks, the chemical pathway encoded in the CRN is linked to positions of the catalytic beads and elastic properties of the inter-bead bonds. The spatial topology of the catalytic sites represents an additional parameter that can navigate between multiple dynamical regimes enabled by the CRN.

The chemical dynamics are further assumed to be coupled to solutal buoyancy forces that arise in response to density variations in the solution. The mechanism connects the chemical signals to fluid motion and to the elastic deformations of the submerged network of catalytic beads. These mechanical deformations effectively follow chemical waves enabled by local catalytic reactions and coupled to hydrodynamics. Analysis of the model reveals how the inter-bead chemical signaling affects flow in the surrounding fluid and, of equal importance, how the flow affects the positions of beads and resulting signaling processes.

To demonstrate possible functionalities of the CMN, such as mechanical waves and particle transport, we employed the repressilator model as an example of a CRN that drives the mechanical response of the system. Alternative choices include experimentally realized oscillatory reactions, such Belousov–Zhabotinsky (BZ), Bray–Liebhafsky, and Briggs–Rauscher reactions (48), which could also be employed to trigger mechanical actions. The latter reactions involve a large number of intermediate steps. Therefore, we chose the repressilator model, which includes only three chemical components.

In our model, we used parameters that enable chemical dynamics on the millimeter length scale and oscillatory periods on the order of tens of seconds. With these choses, we used the diffusivity coefficient D∼10−9m2s−1 (hydrogen peroxide) and decay rate γ∼10−1s−1 to obtain a characteristic length scale r*=(D/γ)1/2∼10−4m that controls chemical processes.

In a physical system, distance d* on the order of r* represents an effective radius around the network of beads beyond which chemical communications decay. In another words, this parameter controls the locality of the chemical processes and is essential for the discussed applications. For example, the locality implies that different networks that are separated by distances larger than d* can transmit waves in the same fluidic container independently without interference. Below the scale d*, the networks should synchronize their activity in the region of the contact. The independent networks that perform different functions in a single fluidic container can enable the creation of chemical circuits that operate like integrated electronic circuits realized on a single piece of semiconducting material.

We found that closed chains of catalytic beads could promote chemical oscillations similar to those observed in arrays of BZ droplets (28). Furthermore, this oscillatory behavior can produce controllable shape changes in the string of collinear beads, analogous to the shape changes in gels undergoing the BZ reaction (31, 49, 50). Notably, the micron-sized BZ droplets in the oscillating array (28) suggest that CRN can be compartmentalized into micron-sized containers, and act as the beads described herein. We also illustrated the potential functionality of the CMN by showing that chemical waves controllably “instruct” dispersed particles to move and follow propagating signals along a particular configuration of the network. Other functionalities of CMNs are evident in nature. For example, Escherichia coli bacteria harness oscillations in concentrations of MinC, MinD, and MinE proteins in a chemical network to control the mechanical processes involved in cell division (51).

The chemical network of catalytic beads can also operate as artificial “nervous systems” that transmit signals to specified parts of a manmade material to instigate mechanical work. In analogy to biological systems, the combined CMN mimics the following functionality: (1) a “metabolism” as it transduces chemical energy into mechanical action; (2) a “vasculature” as it transports energy, chemical signals and matter in a fluidic environment; and (3) a “musculoskeletal” system as the buoyancy-driven flows instigate controllable mechanical deformations that yield the appropriate physical actions. The ability to perform all the functions could facilitate the fabrication of biomimetic materials capable of autonomous, self-directed behavior.
