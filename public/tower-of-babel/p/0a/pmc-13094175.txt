Associated factors and predictive nomogram of long COVID: a cross-sectional study in China
Authors: Zhijin Zhang, Yi Xue, Leyi Gao, Lujia Guan, Haifan Zhang, Haomiao Ma, Xuyan Li, Jieqiong Li, Huqin Yang, Zhaohui Tong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLong COVID (coronavirus disease) poses a substantial challenge to individual and global public health. In the context of China’s Omicron wave, key aspects such as its long-impact on non-hospitalized adults, the role of reinfection and other pathogenic infections during recovery require updated evidence. Therefore, the aim of this study is to delineate factors associated with long COVID and create a robust predictive model for primary-care to identify individuals at high risk of long COVID, utilizing data collected during the Omicron wave.MethodsThis study employed an online survey to assess SARS-CoV-2 infection status and track long COVID symptoms 9 to 15 months post-initial infection in 2099 participants. Associated factors of long COVID were identified through univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses. The long COVID risk prediction model was visualized using a nomogram, and its performance was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC) and a calibration curve.ResultsAn analysis of 1,408 valid responses revealed that 35.9% of participants reported persistent long COVID symptoms 9 to 15 months after their initial SARS-CoV-2 infection. During the rehabilitation period, SARS-CoV-2 re-infection and other respiratory infections were reported in 32.4% and 34.3% of cases, respectively. The most prevalent long COVID symptoms were as follows: fatigue (16.2%), cough (9.0%), decreased activity tolerance (7.3%), shortness of breath (6.0%), expectoration (5.8%), and forgetfulness (5.7%). Multivariable analysis identified the factors independently associated with long COVID: female gender, absence of comorbidities, frequency of SARS-CoV-2 infections, history of other respiratory infections, and the presence of seven acute symptoms. Using logistic regression analysis, we developed a nomogram for long COVID prediction, which achieved an AUC of 0.731. Additional subgroup analysis revealed that participants with either SARS-CoV-2 reinfection or other pathogenic respiratory infections were more likely to report both a higher number of long COVID symptoms and increased symptom severity.ConclusionsIn summary, to mitigate the risk of long COVID, it is critical to prevent SARS-CoV-2 reinfection and other respiratory infections during the post-infection period. We developed a user-friendly nomogram model with satisfactory predictive performance to evaluate the risk of long COVID among COVID-19 patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04221-2.

Background

Long coronavirus disease (COVID), a type of post-acute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection (PASC), is a chronic, debilitating condition that persists in at least 10% of individuals following a COVID-19 infection [1–3]. This multisystemic syndrome is characterized by symptoms that can last for months or even years, imposing a substantial burden on both individual quality of life and broader socioeconomic systems [4, 5]. In China, rigorous genomic and population-immunity surveillance has documented the rapid succession from BA.5/BF.7 to XBB sublineages and widespread exposure, creating a real-world context in which community-based long-term outcomes after Omicron warrant systematic study [6, 7].

Previous studies have made some attempts at developing predictive models for long COVID [8–11], including nomograms that help clinicians identify at-risk individuals. However, most existing models are based on hospitalized cohorts and rely heavily on laboratory biomarkers [9, 10]. While these models provide valuable insights, they often lack applicability in primary care settings for non-hospitalized patients. It is important to note that a significant number of individuals infected with newer variants, such as BA.5, BF.7, and XBB, did not require hospital care during the waves of Omicron subvariants [12]. Thus, we aim to build a novel predictive model leveraging readily obtainable demographic and acute-phase information to enhance real-world applicability at scale.

Moreover, the emergence of SARS-CoV-2 reinfections and mixed infections during recovery raises new challenges in understanding long COVID. Emerging analyses yield divergent findings on the effect of reinfection. A large EHR-based cohort reported a lower incidence of new long COVID diagnoses after reinfection than after initial infection [13], whereas pediatric RECOVER-EHR data in the Omicron era linked reinfection to higher long COVID risk among children and adolescents [14]. Beyond SARS-CoV-2 itself, post-pandemic shifts in respiratory pathogen ecology—including influenza and Mycoplasma pneumoniae—have been observed, with “immunity debt” proposed as a partial explanation [15, 16]; however, robust evidence connecting these other pathogenic infections during recovery to long COVID risk is lacking. Comparative cohort analyses of inpatients show that COVID-19 confers a broader and more durable burden of post-viral sequelae than seasonal influenza; nevertheless, influenza is also linked to persistent pulmonary complications, reinforcing the need to assess multisystem outcomes and concurrent exposures in contemporary settings [17].

This study addresses these gaps by focusing on community (non-hospitalized) adults in China during the Omicron period. Our objectives are twofold: (1) to identify correlative factors for long COVID with specific attention to SARS-CoV-2 reinfection and other pathogenic infections during recovery; and (2) to build a visual, user-friendly nomogram that enables primary-care clinicians to make early identification of individuals at elevated risk.

Methods

Study participants and ethical declaration

This study was conducted in China from November 5, 2023, to February 28, 2024. Participants were recruited via two channels: (1) social media promotions; and (2) offline contacts of the research team at Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital, which included individuals undergoing physical examinations, outpatients and inpatients. The inclusion criteria were as follows: age 18 years or older; able and willing to sign an online informed consent and finish the questionnaire; confirmed as COVID-19 via either positive SARS-CoV-2 nucleic acid/antigen tests, or a clear history of close contact with confirmed cases plus self-reported COVID-19 symptoms; 9 to 15 months after their first COVID-19 infection; and more than one month since the most recent COVID-19 or other respiratory tract infection. Participation was entirely voluntary. Prior to initiating the survey, all participants were fully informed of their rights via an online consent procedure. A total of 2,099 questionnaires were collected. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Board of Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital (project approval number: 2023-ke-355-1) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Questionnaire design and outcomes

The questionnaire was rigorously developed and distributed via the online platform (https://www.yunedc.com/). Participants accessed the questionnaire by either scanning a provided QR code or clicking the designated link, enabling them to review and complete the survey.

The survey consisted of two parts: a self-developed section and validated published scales. The self-developed section was specifically created for this study to collect core study-specific information, encompassing the following content: (1) sociodemographic information; (2) self-reported pre-existing comorbidities (such as respiratory diseases, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, diabetes mellitus, chronic renal failure, etc.); (3) vaccination status; and (4) details regarding acute SARS-CoV-2 infections (including infection frequency, dates of infection, symptomatology, and associated treatments). Participants who had experienced multiple infections were prompted to provide detailed information regarding their first and second infection, while only the dates of subsequent infections were recorded; (5) other pathogenic respiratory infections (including infection frequency, dates of infection, and whether confirmed by pathogen testing or physician diagnosis); (6) the status of rehabilitation training; and (7) the current impacts of long COVID (including self-reported data on symptom presence and severity, specialized scale-based assessments of symptom burden and overall health status, as well as impacts on work and study). The questions pertaining to long COVID were adapted from the Global COVID-19 Clinical Platform Case Report Form for Post COVID condition (Post COVID-19 CRF) [18] and the Symptom Questionnaire of the RECOMER Alliance [19], encompassing 48 distinct symptoms across 12 systems. The severity of each long COVID symptom was self-reported using a four-point scale: asymptomatic (0 points), mild (1 point), moderate (2 points), and severe (3 points). Long COVID was defined in accordance with the World Health Organization (WHO) clinical case definition [20]. It is characterized by symptoms that begin or persist three months post-infection, with a minimum duration of two months that cannot be attributed to other causes. This latter aspect was assessed based on participants’ subjective attribution of symptoms to COVID-19. Other pathogenic respiratory infections were defined as those confirmed by pathogen identification or physician diagnosis; records based only on symptom-based only were excluded from the final analysis. The English version of this self-developed section is available as Supplementary Material.

Furthermore, we employed a specialized scale to evaluate the overall relative health status and adverse health outcomes of the participants. The assessment of overall health status included instruments such as the EuroQol Five Dimensions Questionnaire (EQ-5D) [21], the EuroQol visual analogue scale (EQ-VAS) [22], the Activity of Daily Living Scale (ADL) [23], and the WHO Disability Assessment Schedule (DAS) [24]. In contrast, the evaluation of adverse health outcomes encompassed various measures, including fatigue (assessed using the Fatigue assessment scale (FAS)) [25], dyspnoea (measured by the modified Medical Research Council dyspnoea scale (mMRC)) [26], cough (evaluated through the Cough Symptom Score (CSS)) [27], forgetfulness (assessed with the Cognitive Failure Questionnaire (CFQ)) [28], depression/anxiety (measured by the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HAD)) [29], headache (evaluated using the headache impact test-6 (HIT-6)) [30] and chest pain (assessed through the Seattle Angina Questionnaire (SAQ)) [31].

Quality control of questionnaires

In order to enhance the reliability of our data, we implemented a rigorous methodology for the exclusion of invalid questionnaires. This process included the removal of duplicate entries, which were identified through a combination of participant identification numbers, IP addresses, gender, and age. Furthermore, we excluded any incomplete questionnaires, as well as those with completion times that were either less than 20 s or exceeded 12 h. Additionally, questionnaires containing implausible dates of infection, such as instances of reinfection occurring prior to the initial infection, were also eliminated. As a result of these stringent criteria, a total of 238 questionnaires were discarded.

Statistical analysis

For continuous variables that exhibited a normal distribution, data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. In contrast, nonparametric continuous variables were described using the median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were summarized through counts and percentages. Comparisons of continuous variables between two groups were conducted using t-tests when the data were normally distributed; otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U-test was utilized. Categorical variables were analyzed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, particularly in cases where the sample size was small.

Initially, the subjects were categorized into two groups: those with long COVID and those who had recovered, in order to conduct a differential analysis. To identify indicators independently associated with long COVID, we first performed univariable logistic regression. Candidate variables for the multivariable model included those with a univariable P < 0.05, as well as additional indicators considered clinically relevant based on prior evidence. All candidate variables were then entered into a multivariable logistic regression model using the enter method. Additionally, collinearity diagnostics were conducted for all variables included in the multivariable model to assess the presence of multicollinearity, with tolerance values and variance inflation factors (VIF) used as the key evaluation indices. The results were presented as adjusted odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and P values. Furthermore, to enhance the applicability of the research outcomes for clinical practitioners, a nomogram was developed using the “rms” package in R software (version 3.6.0). A calibration curve was generated to assess the correlation between the event rate predicted by the nomogram and the actual observed outcomes. The bootstrap self-sampling method (with the number of self-sampling times B = 1000), was employed for internal validation to mitigate overfitting bias. The prediction accuracy of the nomogram was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index) and receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Additionally, we investigated the impact of subgroups based on the frequency of COVID-19 infections (single versus multiple infections) and the history of other respiratory infections (presence or absence) on the incidence and severity of various Long COVID symptoms. A P-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY), R software (version 3.6.0), and MedMalc software.

Result

Characteristics of the study population

Following a rigorous participant selection process and stringent quality control measures for the questionnaires, a total of 1,408 valid questionnaires were ultimately included for analysis. The participant cohort primarily consisted of individuals undergoing physical examinations at Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital (n = 866, 61.5%), along with participants recruited via social media platforms (n = 305, 21.6%). Additionally, the sample included 121 outpatients (8.6%) and 116 inpatients (8.2%) from Beijing Chao-Yang Hospital. The comprehensive screening process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Patient flow chart

The general characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. Among the respondents, 71.1% were female, with a median age of 37.00 (IQR: 30.50–48.00) years. The median time from the initial COVID-19 infection to the survey was 365.00 (IQR: 345.25–381.00) days. Individuals with a history of smoking and alcohol consumption accounted for 5% and 2.1% of the participants, respectively. For pre-existing comorbidities, hypertension was the most prevalent, affecting 9.8% of the respondents; additionally, 7.0% of participants reported having two or more comorbidities. All participants had received at least one dose of the SARS-CoV-2 vaccine, with the majority (68.5%) having received two doses. Regarding the frequency of SARS-CoV-2 infections, over one-quarter of the patients (32.4%) reported experiencing multiple infections; specifically, 28.5% indicated they had tested positive twice, and 3.9% reported three positive diagnoses. Notably, 11.0% of participants indicated that COVID-19 had necessitated a reduction or suspension of their studies or work. Additionally, 34.3% of participants experienced other pathogenic respiratory infections, and 37.3% engaged in physical exercise or rehabilitation training during the COVID-19 recovery period.

Long COVID plagued a considerable proportion of patients

Among the study participants, 506 individuals (35.9%) reported experiencing at least one symptom associated with long COVID at 9–15 months after their initial SARS-CoV-2 infection(Table 1). An analysis of general characteristics revealed that a higher proportion of females (77.9% vs. 67.3%, P < 0.001) and individuals with two or more comorbidities (9.7% vs. 5.5%, P = 0.004) were present in the long COVID group compared to the recovered group. Additionally, statistically significant differences were noted in educational background (P = 0.022) and annual household income (P = 0.026) between the two groups. Hospitalization due to COVID-19 (11.9% vs. 7.9%, P = 0.013) and the use of antiviral medications (19.2% vs. 13.3%, P = 0.003) during the acute phase were also significantly more prevalent among participants with long COVID than those without. However, when comparing the long COVID group to the recovered group, no significant differences were found in age (P = 0.153), smoking history (P = 0.968), or alcohol consumption history (P = 0.933), vaccination status (P = 0.292). Notably, 30.3% of respondents who had experienced a single SARS-CoV-2 infection reported long COVID, with this percentage being significantly higher among those with multiple infections (46.1% for two infections and 59.3% for three infections, P < 0.001). Furthermore, a lower number of individuals in the recovered group reported experiencing other respiratory infections and engaging in physical exercise or rehabilitation training during the recovery period compared to the long COVID group, with a significant difference observed (P < 0.001).

Next, we analyzed the correlation between symptoms experienced during the acute phase of SARS-CoV-2 infection and the subsequent development of long COVID (STable 1). Among the 48 acute symptoms examined, 46 exhibited a significantly higher incidence in the long COVID group compared to the recovered group (P < 0.05). The top five most common short-term symptoms reported in both groups (Long COVID vs. Recovered) were consistent, namely fever (92.7% vs. 86.6%), cough (78.0% vs. 65.7%), fatigue (76.6% vs. 60.3%), sore throat (59.0% vs. 48.0%) and muscle pain (52.1% vs. 35.1%).

In terms of long-term effects, the survey indicated that 3% of patients suffering long COVID ceased work or study entirely, while 16.5% had to reduce their time and effort commitments (Table 2). Regarding overall relative health status, the long COVID cohort exhibited lower EQ VAS values, diminished the Barthel index of ADL and elevated WHODAS values compared to the recovered group with significantly differences. Analysis based on the EQ-5D scale demonstrated that the long COVID group performed poorly across all five dimensions (mobility, self care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and depression) (Table 2). Concerning specific symptoms associated with long COVID, our study identified six symptoms with an incidence rate of 5% or greater: fatigue (16.2%), cough (9.0%), decreased activity tolerance (7.3%), shortness of breath (6.0%), expectoration (5.8%) and forgetfulness (5.8%) (Table 3). Evaluation using specialized scales for adverse health outcomes revealed that patients with long COVID exhibited poorer performance one year post-infection compared to their pre-infection status, as evidenced by significantly higher scores in FAS, the CSS, the mMRC, the HAD, and the HIT-6 (SFigure 1, P < 0.05). Furthermore, the CFQ scores in the long COVID group were significantly lower than those in the recovered group (Table 2), indicating that COVID-19 infection adversely affected the cognitive function of certain patients even one year following the initial infection.

Identification of associated factors and construction of a nomogram

To identify factors independently associated with long COVID at 9–15 months after initial SARS-CoV-2 infection, we first performed univariable logistic regression for screening (complete results in STable 2). Variables with P-values < 0.05 in this analysis were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model, along with age, smoking status and vaccination status (retained as potential confounders based on existing evidence). To avoid collinearity, only the “no comorbidities” variable was included, while individual comorbid conditions and the total number of comorbidities were excluded despite their significant univariable associations. Collinearity diagnostics for the final model confirmed no serious multicollinearity (all tolerance > 0.3, VIF < 3; STable 3).

Multivariable logistic regression identified several factors independently associated with long COVID(Table 3). Female sex was associated with higher odds of long COVID (OR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.19–2.37, P = 0.003), whereas the absence of comorbidities was associated with lower odds (OR = 0.55, 95% CI: 0.38–0.81, P = 0.002). Both the frequency of SARS‑CoV‑2 reinfection (OR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.28–2.01, P < 0.001) and the occurrence of other respiratory infections during the post‑infection period (OR = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.06–1.85, P = 0.019) independently increased the likelihood of long COVID. Several acute‑phase symptoms also remained independently associated with Long COVID, including shortness of breath/chest distress (OR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.30–2.49, P < 0.001), dyspnea (OR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.11–2.82, P = 0.016), palpitations (OR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.02–2.34, P = 0.042), skin rash (OR = 3.88, 95% CI: 1.60–9.42, P = 0.003), back or spine pain (OR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.11–2.45, P = 0.014), brain fog (OR = 2.10, 95% CI: 1.32–3.33, P = 0.002), and forgetfulness (OR = 2.67, 95% CI: 1.67–4.28, P < 0.001). No other candidate variables reached statistical significance in the multivariable model (all P > 0.05).

Based on the multivariable logistic regression analysis, we selected nine available predictors—all derived from baseline characteristics and acute-phase symptoms—to construct a nomogram for early prediction of persistent long COVID status at approximately one year after initial infection (Fig. 2). To enhance its clinical utility in the early post-infection period, we deliberately excluded rehabilitation period indicators (SARS-CoV-2 reinfection and other respiratory infections). Although these factors were independently associated with persistent long COVID, they are not ascertainable at the time of initial risk assessment. The nomogram demonstrated good discriminative ability. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) for the model was 0.731 (95% CI: 0.704–0.758) in the training cohort (SFig.2). After internal validation using 1,000 bootstrap resamples, the bootstrap-corrected C-index was 0.731 ± 0.014 (95% CI: 0.708–0.762). The calibration curve showed satisfactory agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes (SFig.3).

SARS-CoV-2 reinfection alleviates short-term symptoms but raises the risk of long COVID

Reinfection with SARS-CoV-2 appears to have distinct effects on short-term symptoms and long-term symptoms associated with COVID-19. Our analysis revealed that the frequency of acute symptoms in COVID-19 patients experiencing a secondary SARS-CoV-2 infection (n=401) was generally lower than that observed during their initial infection (n=1408). Specifically, 31 out of 48 symptoms we assessed demonstrated statistically significant differences (P<0.05) (Figure 3 & STable 4), such as the top four common symptoms: fever (88.8% vs 69.8%, P<0.001), cough (70.1% vs 58.5%, P<0.001), fatigue (66.2% vs 57.5%, P=0.001) and sore throat (52.0% vs 38.3%, P<0.001). Conversely, individuals who experienced multiple infections exhibited a heightened likelihood of suffering more long COVID symptoms with increased severity (Fig.4 & Fig.5). Notably, among the 48 long COVID symptoms we investigated, 25 demonstrated a significantly higher incidence in the multiple infections group compared to those with single infection (P<0.005) (Table 4).

Other pathogenic infections during the recovery period aggravate long COVID

Our investigation indicated that patients who did not experience concurrent respiratory infections during their rehabilitation reported a significantly higher rate of perceived complete recovery compared to those who did (61.9% vs 48.5%, P<0.001) and suffered fewer long COVID symptoms (Table 5). The symptoms that we were interested in and had an incidence rate greater than or equal to 2% (n=18) were more common in patients with other respiratory infections than those without, and eight of them were statistically significant (P<0.05), as illustrated in Figure 6. In terms of symptom severity, individuals with a history of other infections tended to experience more pronounced symptoms (Fig.7).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study was the most comprehensive study covering long COVID symptoms, and is the one with the largest sample size and the longest follow-up time to develop a nomogram for forecasting occurrence of long COVID thus far. Besides further confirming the impact of SARS-CoV-2 reinfection on the frequency and severity of long COVID symptoms, our study is the first study to investigate the impact of other pathogenic infections during the recovery period on it. Independent associated factors associated with long COVID were identified by logistic regression analysis and subsequently utilized to construct a nomogram with good performance and high clinical applicability for visualizing the risk of long COVID. This pre-hospital tool is designed to identify high-risk groups for long COVID, thereby facilitating early intervention strategies.

In our research, we found that the prevalence of long COVID one year following the initial SARS-CoV-2 infection during the period of the Omicron variant in China was 35.9%. The most frequently reported symptoms among individuals experiencing long COVID were fatigue, followed by cough and reduced activity tolerance. This finding is consistent with existing scholarly literature, which has consistently identified fatigue as the most common symptom associated with long COVID [32–34], even two years post-infection [35]. Additionally, alongside fatigue, a range of other symptoms such as cough, chest tightness, shortness of breath, anxiety, and myalgia have been extensively documented [32, 35]. This suggests that long COVID is a chronic and heterogeneous condition with effects across multiple bodily systems. Recent research further underscores the prolonged occupational impact of SARS-CoV-2 infection, reporting that 1.1% of individuals remained on continuous sick leave even two years after the acute infection [36]. Therefore, early identification of individuals at high risk for long COVID and timely intervention are crucial to mitigate the long-term burden of this condition. Beyond established approaches such as physical rehabilitation, complementary therapies—including aromatherapy and music therapy, which have been shown to alleviate psychological symptoms [37]—may also represent promising supportive strategies worthy of further investigation in the context of long COVID.

Previous research has predominantly focused on identifying independent risk factors associated with long COVID. Only a limited number of studies have developed predictive models. For instance, Fang et al. constructed a nomogram utilizing general demographic and laboratory test indicators, such as procalcitonin and interleukin-6, based on data from a cohort of inpatients with COVID-19 who were assessed 4 to 8 weeks post-discharge in Wuhan [10]. Naresh Doni Jayavelu et al. utilized baseline clinical and immunologic factors—including antibody titers and viral load measured at hospital admission—to develop machine learning models for predicting the risk of long COVID in hospitalized patients [38]. However, the applicability of these models is constrained, as the majority of COVID-19 patients experience mild symptoms and are not hospitalized, and laboratory tests are invasive and inaccessible in regions with limited medical resources. Furthermore, some studies have concentrated solely on predicting individual symptoms or specific systemic impacts [9, 39]. To address these gaps, we developed a model incorporating general demographic indicators and acute phase symptoms that can be utilized prior to hospitalization.

In the present study, female sex was verified as an independent risk factor for persistent long COVID (OR = 1.68, P = 0.003), which aligns with numerous population-based studies and meta-analyses [18, 33, 34, 40]. Additionally, the absence of comorbidities was found to be protective, consistent with global evidence indicating that pre-existing chronic conditions increase vulnerability to post-acute COVID-19 syndrome [32, 38, 41]. Advanced age, a risk factor widely recognized in previous long COVID research [32, 33, 40, 42], showed no significant associations in either our comparative or regression analyses. This may be attributed to the relatively narrow age distribution of our study population, which was predominantly comprised of young adults with a median age of 37 years. Similarly, BMI did not retain significance after multivariable adjustment, suggesting potential confounding by other factors. Regarding acute-phase severity, hospitalization was associated with a higher risk of long COVID in univariable analysis, supporting the well-documented link between acute infection severity and prolonged post-COVID manifestations [32, 33, 43]; however, this association did not reach statistical significance in the multivariable model. With respect to vaccination, a previous meta-analysis demonstrated that at least one dose of SARS-CoV-2 vaccine significantly reduces long COVID risk (OR = 0.539, P = 0.045) [20]. However, vaccine dose number was not significant in our multivariable model. This likely reflects the high coverage in our cohort (no unvaccinated individuals), which limited our ability to detect a dose-dependent effect. The potential waning of vaccine-induced immunity over time may have also contributed. Beyond these factors, our study uniquely identified SARS-CoV-2 reinfection frequency (OR = 1.60, P < 0.001) and other respiratory infections (OR = 1.40, P = 0.019) as independent contributors to long COVID, extending the current understanding of modifiable post-acute risk factors for this condition.

In relation to the reinfection of SARS-CoV-2, our research indicates a reduced likelihood of experiencing short-term symptoms following reinfections when compared to initial infections. Conversely, reinfections occurring within the same timeframe are associated with an elevated risk of long-term symptoms. This contrasting phenomenon has also been corroborated by another large-scale survey conducted in China [43]. However, there is no consensus in the limited existing literature regarding the risk of new long COVID cases after reinfection. A study utilizing data from the US Veterans Health Administration database [44], along with a multinational network cohort analysis that employed primary care data from England and Spain [45] indicated that reinfection is linked to an increased risk of a wide range of long COVID complications in individuals previously infected with SARS-CoV-2 compared to those with no reinfection. This suggests that people remain at risk of developing persistent symptoms despite previously built-up immunity. However, studies by the Office of National Statistics [46], the German National Cohort [47] and an N3C RECOVER EHR-based cohort [13] indicated lower risk for self-reported post-acute COVID-19 symptoms among adults after a second COVID-19 infection in comparison to first infection.

The vigilance of respiratory protection should not be relaxed after COVID-19 infection turns negative. Previous studies suggest that the virus may facilitate subsequent infections with influenza viruses, bacteria, and mycoplasma, leading to an atypical surge in multiple pathogenic infections [43, 48]. “Immune debt” was proposed as a potential explanation for this occurrence; however, the underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation [49]. Besides, there has been less previous evidence for the impact of other pathogenic infections on long COVID. Only an observational cohort study in the US indicated that long COVID-like symptoms (long Flu) can be observed after influenza and the incidence of long Flu is comparable with long COVID among outpatients [50]. Our research further indicates that the presence of other pathogenic infections during the recovery phase is associated with an increased risk and severity of long COVID, which may reflect multiple assaults on the immune system.

The present study is subject to several limitations that should be considered. Firstly, the participants were recruited through convenience sampling, which may introduce selection bias. Secondly, the data regarding the acute phase of COVID-19 infection were collected retrospectively, which may introduce potential recall bias. Thirdly, constrained by the cross-sectional questionnaire design, long COVID was identified based on self-reported symptoms aligned with WHO criteria, without verification via clinical diagnosis or ICD-10 codes; similarly, although other respiratory infections were limited to those confirmed by pathogen testing or physician diagnosis, the confirmation status was still based on participant self-report. Fourthly, this design also limits our ability to establish causality between identified factors (e.g., reinfection) and long COVID, and the findings reflect an association rather than causation. Additionally, while internal validation confirmed good model accuracy, the absence of external validation in an independent cohort limits the generalizability of our nomogram. Therefore, validation in future independent, multi-center cohorts is imperative to confirm its broader applicability. Given these considerations, it is recommended that clinicians apply the nomogram model developed in this study in conjunction with real-world clinical contexts.

Conclusion

In summary, around 35.9% of patients exhibited symptoms of long COVID 9 to 15 months following their initial SARS-CoV-2 infection. We constructed a visualized and user-friendly nomogram utilizing pre-hospital indices to predict the occurrence of long COVID and demonstrated its relatively good predictive capability. Besides, our results highlight the importance of implementing respiratory protection strategies to avoid SARS-CoV-2 reinfection and other respiratory infections, thereby mitigating the risk of long COVID.
