A phosphoinositide-mediated switch of GET pathway receptor dimerization in Arabidopsis
Authors: Lei Zhang, Aron Struß, Miaojing Qin, Annika Dombrowski, Tingting Li, Stefanie Viebahn, Dietmar Mehlhorn, Christopher Grefen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe plasma membrane serves as a dynamic reaction platform, where the spatiotemporal regulation of phospholipids enables functionalization of membrane-associated proteins. The endoplasmic reticulum (ER), as the first station of protein trafficking, remains poorly understood in terms of its membrane composition and function. We identified a PI4P-related mechanism in which turnover of PI4P to PI within the ER, in coordination with the ER-localized phosphatase RHD4, influences the spatial organization of GET pathway coreceptors AtGET1 and AtGET2. This reorganization leads to the formation of a functional receptor complex, thereby promoting TA protein targeting and root hair growth.

Root hairs are single protrusions from rhizodermal cells (= root epidermis) that absorb water and nutrients from the surrounding soil. Once initiated, root hair elongation is directed by polar cell expansion, also known as tip growth (1). This process necessitates precise regulation of protein secretion through a tightly controlled secretory pathway that guides proteins and membrane material toward the tip and into the cell wall (1, 2). Soluble N-ethylmaleimide–sensitive factor attachment protein receptor (SNARE) proteins play a critical role in regulating root hair tip growth in Arabidopsis (3–5). Most SNARE proteins belong to the family of Type II tail-anchored (TA) membrane proteins, defined by a cytosolic N-terminus and a single C-terminal transmembrane domain (TMD) (6). These proteins are essential for facilitating membrane fusion, which is crucial for vesicle trafficking. By enabling this process, they support critical functions such as membrane expansion, protein delivery, and cell wall deposition which in turn direct polar growth, cytokinesis, and pathogen resistance, to name but a few (7–9). The hydrophobic nature of their TMD presents a significant challenge for the biogenesis of this class of proteins, as the journey from their synthesis at the ribosome to integration into the ER membrane requires overcoming the aqueous environment of the cytoplasm (10).

The Guided Entry of Tail-anchored proteins (GET) pathway is a posttranslational mechanism responsible for targeting TA proteins to the Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER). This pathway has been identified in yeast, mammals, and higher plants (11–14) and involves three key checkpoints for the nascent TA protein: recognition, shuttling, and membrane insertion. The first step involves a pretargeting complex (comprising Sgt2, Get4, and Get5 in yeast; SGTA, TRC35, UBL4A, and BAG6 in metazoans; AtGET4 in Arabidopsis) (13, 15, 16) that captures TA proteins from the ribosome and transfers them to the ATPase Get3 (TRC40 in metazoans; AtGET3a in Arabidopsis). Get3 facilitates shuttling by forming a homodimer that shields the hydrophobic TMD of the TA proteins from the cytoplasm delivering them to the ER membrane insertase complex. This complex consists of two membrane proteins: the ER receptors Get1 and Get2 (WRB & CAML in metazoans; AtGET1 & AtGET2 in Arabidopsis) (13, 17, 18), which enable the final step of membrane insertion.

The GET insertase exhibits conformational plasticity during TA protein insertion (19, 20) requiring interfacial lipid binding for the dimerization of GET receptors (21). This involves two positively charged amino acids in the first TMD of Get2 (CAML). Mutations at these residues impair phosphatidylinositol (PI) accumulation and destabilize the complex (21), preventing the formation of an aqueous channel (22). While AtGET2 shows low sequence similarity to Opisthokonts Get2/CAML, it maintains high structural conservation (17), yet the potential for PI binding in Archaeplastida GET2 orthologs remains unclear.

The Atget2 mutant exhibits a short root hair phenotype similar to other known get pathway mutants (17). Root hairs are an established model system for studying polar tip growth in eukaryotic cells (23). Phosphoinositides also known as Phosphatidylinositol-phosphates (PIPs) control directional membrane trafficking necessary for delivering cell wall material and membrane components to the growing tip (1, 24). Disrupting phosphatidylinositol 4-phosphate (PI4P) homeostasis, either by knocking out PI 4-kinases β1 and β2 or the PI4P-phosphatase ROOTHAIR-DEFECTIVE 4 (RHD4), which function in converting PI to PI4P or dephosphorylating PI4P to PI, respectively, results in root hair growth defects (25–27).

Given the conserved function of the GET pathway across eukaryotes and its critical role in regulating root hair growth alongside PI/PI4P homeostasis in plants, a key question emerges: How do phospholipid interactions with the GET pathway shape root hair development in plants? To address this, we investigate the functional interplay between PI4P, its phosphatase RHD4, and the GET pathway, utilizing the PI4P-binding-deficient mutant AtGET2R172A and the PI4P-dephosphorylation-deficient mutant rhd4. This approach allows us to explore how PI4P dynamics regulate GET receptor interaction, proximity, and TA protein delivery during polar tip growth.

Results

AtGET2 Contains a PIP-Binding Residue.

Recent structural analyses of the GET complex suggested that PI stabilizes receptor dimerization, particularly through two conserved positively charged residues within the TMD1 of Get2 (CAML) serving as potential binding sites (21). AtGET2, despite having low sequence similarity (17), contains two positively charged amino acids–Arginine 172 (R172) and Histidine 187 (H187)–in its TMD1, facing the cytosol and ER lumen, respectively (Fig. 1A). To assess the role of these residues in phospholipid binding, we performed alanine-scanning mutagenesis and conducted protein–lipid overlay (PLO) assays. Our findings revealed that AtGET2 interacts preferably with acidic phospholipids such as PI4P, phosphatidic acid (PA), and cardiolipin (CL) which is a signature lipid of the inner mitochondrial membrane (28), and somewhat weaker with sulfatides – but contrary to previous findings in mammalian and yeast orthologs, PI-binding was not detected (Fig. 1B).

Combinatorial alanine-scanning mutagenesis helped to identify a critical site for lipid binding. In the AtGET2R172A, H187A line, the mutation of both residues eliminated lipid binding. Further analyses revealed the significance of the arginine residue: While AtGET2H187A phenocopied the binding patterns of wild-type AtGET2, AtGET2R172A showed no binding to any of the tested phospholipid species. (Fig. 1B).

Subsequently, we employed a liposome binding assay to corroborate the phospholipid binding ability of AtGET2 and its mutant versions. Unlike the protein–lipid overlay assays, AtGET2 bound PI, with a higher affinity for PI4P than for PI (Fig. 1C), as did AtGET2H187A (Fig. 1D). However, AtGET2R172A and the double mutant showed reduced binding, corroborating the findings of the PLO assay (Fig. 1D).

Loss-of-function of AtGET2 causes short root hairs (17). To understand whether the PIPs defective binding mutants of AtGET2 function in root hair growth, we generated Atget2 mutant complementation lines expressing GFP-tagged AtGET2 variants under the UBQ10 promoter alongside markers for the ER and Golgi apparatus (Fig. 1E). Interestingly, AtGET2R172A and AtGET2R172A, H187A failed to restore the Atget2 phenotype, while lines expressing AtGET2 and AtGET2H187A exhibited normal root hair growth (Fig. 1F).

Mutation of critical residues can often lead to mislocalization or protein stability issues (29). To validate that the mutations we have identified do not alter protein topology or subcellular localization, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was employed to confirm that both AtGET2 mutants remained ER-localized in their respective complementation lines (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A–H) or colocalized with AtGET1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–H). Additionally, the ratiometric Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation (rBiFC)-based topology assay (30, 31) illustrated that the topology of AtGET2R172A, AtGET2H187A, and AtGET2R172A, H187A remained identical to that of AtGET2, with the N-terminus facing the cytosol and the C-terminus oriented toward the ER lumen (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–E). Moreover, phospholipid-binding-deficient AtGET2 mutants showed no protein instability in Arabidopsis complementation lines. (SI Appendix, Fig. S3F).

Proximity Between GET Receptors Relies on PIP-Bound AtGET2.

It has been demonstrated that PI plays a crucial role in stabilizing Get receptor dimerization in both yeast and mammalian cells, as shown by cryo-EM studies (21). This, however, raises the question whether AtGET1 is still able to interact with AtGET2 in a membrane environment when the PIP-binding site of AtGET2 is mutated.

We previously found that the interaction between the AtGET receptors depends on their TMDs (17). To distinguish between an interaction dependent on the physical properties of the R172 residue and its PIP binding capacity, we first performed a structural in silico analysis using AlphaFold3. Both potential receptor complexes, AtGET1–AtGET2 and AtGET1–AtGET2R172A, showed a similar structure (Fig. 2 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B). Mutation in R172A does not alter the hydrogen bond between AtGET1 and AtGET2 and their respective third TMD which is also the interacting TMD in the yeast and human receptor complexes (20). We then employed DDMut-PPI (32), a deep learning model that predicts changes in protein–protein interaction binding free energy upon single-point mutation. The predicted affinity change (ΔΔGAffinity WT→R172A) was –1.366 kcal/mol (root mean squared error: 1.33 kcal/mol), suggesting minimal impact of the R172A mutation on the binding affinity of AtGET2 to AtGET1.

In vitro Glutathione S-transferase (GST) pulldown assays were conducted to test our prediction experimentally. Bacteria-expressed GST-tagged AtGET2 (and its mutant versions) were incubated with His-tagged AtGET1. Immunoblotting analysis revealed that AtGET1 was successfully pulled down by all GST-AtGET2 variants, but not by GST alone (Fig. 2C). This confirms that the mutations do not impact the ability of AtGET2 to physically interact and dimerize with AtGET1.

Next, we conducted a coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) assay. AtGET1-EGFP was coexpressed with mCherry-AtGET2, -AtGET2R172A, -AtGET2H187A, and -AtGET2R172A, H187A and transiently transformed in tobacco (Fig. 2 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B). Our findings revealed that the AtGET2 variants with the mutated R172A residue failed to coimmunoprecipitate with AtGET1 in contrast to AtGET2 and AtGET2H187A (Fig. 2 D and E and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B).

To validate the ex vivo interaction data from the CoIP assay, we employed the in vivo proximity-based protein interaction technique rBiFC (31). YFP complementation was absent in samples coexpressing AtGET1-nYFP with cYFP-AtGET2 carrying the R172A mutation or with the negative control, cYFP-AtGET4 (Fig. 2F). In contrast, strong YFP signal intensity was observed in samples coexpressing cYFP-AtGET2H187A and the positive control, cYFP-AtGET2 (Fig. 2G), highlighting the critical role of the arginine residue in receptor dimerization.

An additional PLO and liposome binding assay revealed a similar phospholipid binding profile for AtGET1 to AtGET2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A and B), with the notable exception that AtGET1 lacks affinity for PI and displays additional affinity for PI(3,4,5)P3, a phosphoinositide typically absent in higher plants (33, 34).

A key concept of PIP function is its ability to recruit proteins to target sites within post-Golgi membranes (35, 36). Our data suggest that PI4P, which has high affinity for both AtGET1 and AtGET2, may fulfill this function already at the ER membrane in recruiting AtGET1 and AtGET2, bringing them into close proximity to facilitate their interaction (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A and B).

Synthetic Dimerization of GET Receptors Overrides AtGET2-PI4P Binding.

The AtGET2R172A mutant fails to complement the short root hair phenotype of Atget2 (Fig. 1F), as it is unable to be recruited to its coreceptor AtGET1 within the ER membrane. To override the necessity of lipid-binding for receptor recruitment, we created synthetic fusions of AtGET2 (and mutant versions) with AtGET1 through a flexible glycine-serine (G-S) linker, (GGGGS)₃ (Fig. 2H). This linker, with a known length of approximately 5.7 nm (37), ensures physical connection between AtGET2 and AtGET1, mimicking a functional receptor complex. A similar construct, Get2-1sc, has been shown to be functional in yeast (18). All fusion proteins that we created exhibited lipid-binding properties similar to AtGET1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 C–F). When introduced into the Atget1Atget2 double mutant as GFP-tagged constructs, the N-terminal mutant version AtGET24E that lacks binding with the upstream AtGET3a pathway component (17), failed to complement the root hair growth phenotype. However, AtGET2-(GGGGS)₃-AtGET1-GFP and – more importantly – the arginine mutant rescued the root hair growth defect (Fig. 2I), demonstrating the importance of proximity for function.

The ER-Localized PI4P-Phosphatase RHD4 Interacts with AtGET1.

The GET complex exhibits conformational plasticity during TA insertion (20, 22), suggesting that constitutive PIP binding could influence this dynamic process. Interestingly, the AtGET2R172A-(GGGGS)₃-AtGET1-GFP fusion construct was functionally capable of complementing the Atget1Atget2 root hair phenotype (Fig. 2I). This observation suggests that phospholipid binding is not essential once AtGET2R172A “finds” AtGET1 within the ER membrane.

To elucidate the molecular mechanism of PI4P-bound AtGET2 in AtGET receptor dimerization, we analyzed previously published IP-MS data to identify AtGET receptor interactors (17). Interestingly, RHD4, a PI4P-phosphatase responsible for converting PI4P to PI and crucial for root hair development in Arabidopsis (26), was detected to interact with the GET receptor AtGET1 (17).

The subcellular localization of Type II PI4P-phosphatases in eukaryotes remains a topic of ongoing controversy and debate. This includes RHD4 as well as its homologues SAC6 and SAC8 (26, 38–40). To clarify this point, we created trimeric constructs including GFP-tagged phosphatase under control of the RHD4 promoter, as well as an ER- (CFP-HDEL) and Golgi-marker (mCherry) (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A). To validate functionality of these fusion proteins in Arabidopsis, we stably expressed the construct in the rhd4 mutant. All constructs complemented the root hair growth defect of rhd4 lines (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B) confirming redundant function of all three phosphatases (27).

To determine the localization of RHD4 and its homologs in root hair cells, we conducted a colocalization study using ER and Golgi markers. GFP-RHD4, GFP-SAC6, and GFP-SAC8 were observed to colocalize with the CFP-HDEL ER marker but not with the Golgi marker in root hairs and leaf epidermal cells (SI Appendix, Figs. S10 A–F and S11 A–F). To further explore this, we performed FM4-64 labeling followed by brefeldin A (BFA) treatment. FM4-64, a lipophilic dye, initially stains the plasma membrane and is subsequently incorporated into vesicles through endocytosis (41). BFA, an inhibitor of vesicle formation and protein trafficking between the ER and the Golgi apparatus, also blocks trafficking from endosomes back to the plasma membrane, leading to the formation of BFA bodies in cells. We hypothesized that if RHD4 localizes to post-ER compartments (Golgi, TGN, or plasma membrane), it would colocalize with these BFA bodies. After 30 min of BFA treatment, BFA bodies decorated with FM4-64 were prominent in root hair cells. However, GFP-RHD4 consistently colocalized with the ER marker (Fig. 3 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S12 A and B) and was absent from BFA bodies. These findings suggest that SAC proteins, including RHD4, are primarily localized to the ER in Arabidopsis root hair cells.

Our PLO and liposome binding assay showed RHD4 exhibiting a similar PI4P-binding feature to AtGET1 and AtGET2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C), suggesting they may coassemble at the ER. A topology study revealed that SAC6, RHD4, and SAC8 share similar orientation, with both their N and C termini facing the cytosol (SI Appendix, Figs. S13 A–F and S14 A and B), which is relevant to the spatial positioning of PI4P-AtGET2 binding. To date, there is no direct evidence demonstrating the presence of PI4P within the plant ER membrane, although some studies have speculated that PI4P may be transported from the PM to the ER via PM–ER contact sites (34, 42, 43).

In plant cells, PI4P is primarily enriched at the PM (42, 44, 45), which makes translocation-based PI4P biosensors less effective in detecting PI4P at the ER, as RHD4 rapidly converts PI4P to PI. Previous studies have shown that, in rhd4 mutants, PI4P signals accumulate within intracellular membrane systems (26, 46), but whether this includes the ER has remained unclear. To address this, we generated a PI4P biosensor line (CITRINE-2xPHFAPP1) in the rhd4 mutant background through genetic crossing and introduced the ER marker mCherry-HDEL. In the rhd4 mutant, we observed that CITRINE-2xPHFAPP1 associates with ER bodies—ER-derived compartments in Arabidopsis connected to ER tubules and cisternae (47), with partial signal overlap, suggesting that PI4P may be enriched at ER and other compartment contact sites or PI4P donor membrane (Fig. 3 E–G). In contrast, PI4P was not enriched around ER bodies in the Col-0 background (it may of course be below the detection threshold of the marker) (Fig. 3 H–J). A similar phenomenon was observed in sac1 mutant, where PI4P does not accumulate excessively at the ER, a dual-safeguard mechanism that restricts PI4P transfer to the ER (48, 49). Together, these findings suggest that PI4P might be present on the plant ER, albeit at a very low abundance.

To better understand their potential functional connection, it is essential to clarify the interaction between RHD4 and the GET pathway in Arabidopsis. Noteworthy, SAC6 and SAC8 are functionally redundant with RHD4 (27), although the latter outweighs the former two by being constitutively expressed. SAC6 and SAC8 were absent in our IP-MS data, possibly due to low (SAC8) or spatially restricted expression levels (SAC6 primarily in flowers) (27, 38). Given the functional redundancy of RHD4, SAC6, and SAC8 in Arabidopsis, we hypothesize that the AtGET insertase complex might also interact with these proteins. To validate this hypothesis, we performed co-IP analyses, using AtGET1-EGFP with mCherry-RHD4, -SAC6, and -SAC8, each in a coexpression construct to avoid unequal gene dosage and transiently transformed in tobacco. Our results demonstrate that AtGET1-EGFP could copurify with mCherry-RHD4, mCherry-SAC6, and mCherry-SAC8 but not with mCherry alone (Fig. 3 K and L). Furthermore, we conducted rBiFC. The YFP complementation signals were observed in samples coexpressing AtGET1-nYFP with cYFP-AtGET2, cYFP-RHD4, cYFP-SAC6, and cYFP-SAC8, with all these signals notably stronger than the biological negative control cYFP-AtGET4 (Fig. 3 M and N).

Conversion of PI4P to PI Is Essential for GET Receptor Stability.

RHD4 dephosphorylates PI4P to PI (26). The GET receptors exhibit strong binding affinity for PI4P; however, they undergo multiple conformational changes during TA protein targeting to the ER (20, 22). Since the liposome-binding assays showed that AtGET2 interacts with both PI and PI4P (Fig. 1C), whereas AtGET1 and the functional AtGET2R172A–AtGET1 complex interact only with PI4P (SI Appendix, Figs. S7B and S8E), this raises the question whether the removal of PI4P is essential to GET complex assembly and function in plants.

Given that our previous experiments indicate PI4P may primarily function to bring GET receptors into proximity, the absence of RHD4 should leave AtGET1 and AtGET2 in proximity. To assess this, we employed a proximity-based protein–protein interaction approach using Förster Resonance Energy Transfer by Fluorescence Lifetime Imaging (FRET-FLIM) analysis in Arabidopsis roots. We utilized 2in1 constructs (50) which express both AtGET1-EGFP and mCherry-AtGET2 (or mCherry-AtGET2R172A) in Col-0 and rhd4 background (Fig. 4A). The fluorescence lifetime of AtGET1-EGFP/mCherry- AtGET2R172A is significantly reduced in comparison to the AtGET1-EGFP/mCherry, while the AtGET1-EGFP/mCherry- AtGET2 shows an even shorter fluorescence lifetime (Fig. 4 B and C), indicating vicinity of GET coreceptors only when the Arginine residue is present.

To corroborate the FRET-FLIM results, we performed rBiFC analyses in both Col-0 and rhd4 mutant backgrounds to assess the proximity of the receptors as a prerequisite for their interaction (Fig. 4D). Interestingly, we observed YFP complementation in both backgrounds when AtGET1-nYFP was coexpressed with cYFP-AtGET2, suggesting that overexpression, along with the incidental vicinity of AtGET1 and AtGET2 (Fig. 4E) is sufficient for YFP complementation, a feature inherent of the technique (31). Conversely, lines containing the R172A mutant revealed only faint YFP signal in either the Col-0 or rhd4 mutant background supporting the AtGET1 and AtGET2R172A interaction study in tobacco (Figs. 2 E and G and 4B).

We also performed chemical crosslinking to explore the proximity of AtGET1 and AtGET2 in the different backgrounds using the lines from the FRET-FLIM experiment. The plants were divided into two groups: One infiltrated with crosslinking buffer, and the other with a control buffer lacking formaldehyde, disuccinimidyl suberate (DSS), and 10 mM bis(sulfosuccinimidyl) suberate (BS3). After the crosslinking reaction, both AtGET1-EGFP and mCherry-AtGET2 exhibited a shift to ~104 KDa on SDS-PAGE in both Col-0 and rhd4 backgrounds (Fig. 4F). By contrast, AtGET1-EGFP and mCherry-AtGET2R172A failed to generate crosslinking in both Col-0 and rhd4 backgrounds. These results suggest that AtGET1 and AtGET2 are in close proximity in both backgrounds.

Next, we examined whether PI4P (and lack of its dephosphorylation) influence GET insertase stability; we generated transgenic lines harboring AtGET1-mVenus and 3xHA-AtGET2 under the RPS5a promoter in a 2in1 CoIP construct (17) in Col-0 and rhd4 background (SI Appendix, Fig. S15A). AtGET1 and AtGET2 coimmunoprecipitate in the wild-type background, yet significantly weaker in rhd4 lines (SI Appendix, Fig. S15B).

To validate this, we utilized stable transgenic lines expressing AtGET1-EGFP and mCherry-AtGET2 (as well as mCherry-AtGET2R172A) under the constitutive 35S promoter in a 2in1 FRET vector (50) in Col-0 and rhd4 background (Fig. 4A). We observed again that AtGET1 and AtGET2 interacted readily in Col-0 plants but not in the rhd4 mutant background, while the AtGET1 and AtGET2R172A show a generally weaker binding affinity either in Col-0 or rhd4 background (Fig. 4 G and H).

Genetic Interaction of GET Receptors with PI4P-Phosphatases.

To investigate the functional connection between RHD4 and the AtGET pathway, we dissected the genetic relationship between RHD4 and the GET pathway (Fig. 5A). Since loss-of-function mutation in either Atget or rhd4 results in root hair growth defects, we aimed to confirm their involvement in the same pathway by generating double mutants through crossing. The Atget1, Atget3a, and rhd4 mutants exhibit shorter root hairs compared to Col-0, with rhd4 displaying longer root hairs than Atget1 and Atget3a. In the Atget1rhd4 and Atget3arhd4 double mutants, root hair length appears as intermediate between that of the respective single mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S16A), suggesting that RHD4 and AtGET are involved in similar or related pathways, with each gene contributing partially to the final trait. Complementation experiments confirm that AtGET1, AtGET3a, and RHD4 can rescue their specific root hair defects under their native promoters in both single and double mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S16A).

To further investigate the genetic relationship between RHD4 and GET pathway, we considered that the intermediate phenotype observed is due to the genetic redundancy of the RHD4 homologs (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B), particularly the root expression pattern of both RHD4 and SAC8 (27). Since double mutants of both RHD4 and SAC8 are embryo lethal (27), we employed an inducible CRISPR/Cas9 approach to conditionally delete SAC8 in rhd4, Atget1, Atget3a, and Atget(1/3a)rhd4 double mutants. Seedlings were initially grown on ½ MS-free plates for 4 d before being transferred to plates containing either DMSO or estradiol, with root tip positions marked to quantify new root hair growth (Fig. 5B). Remarkably, the root hair length of Atget1(3a) rhd4sac8 triple mutants was shorter than that of the single Atget mutants but resembled that of the rhd4sac8 double mutants (Fig. 5C).

In addition to the short root hair length, the rhd4 mutant also exhibits branched, bulged, wavy, and jagged root hair morphologies (26). We analyzed this phenotype in all lines compared to Col-0. While the Atget single mutants showed no significant difference compared to Col-0, their self-complementation and complementation with RHD4 in double mutants were observed. However, the double mutants and those complemented with AtGET1 or AtGET3a resembled the phenotype of rhd4 (SI Appendix, Fig. S16 B–E and Table S1). Taken together, these results suggest that RHD4 acts as a component of the GET pathway in root hair development.

A GET Pathway Substrate Also Depends on RHD4.

Given that RHD4 and the GET pathway function within the same genetic pathway to promote root hair growth and that the GET receptor complex is molecularly unstable in the rhd4 mutant, further investigation is needed to explore the involvement of RHD4 in GET pathway function. We decided to focus on the SNARE SYNTAXIN OF PLANTS 123 (SYP123), which is essential for polar root hair growth, and its expression is disrupted in Atget mutants (4, 13). Individual loss-of-function lines of syp123, rhd4, and Atget1 all reveal root hair growth defects with increasing severity (Fig. 5D). Crossing syp123 with either rhd4 or Atget1, respectively, did not exacerbate the individual rhd4 or Atget1 root hair phenotypes corroborating that SYP123 functions downstream of both GET pathway and RHD4.

To validate this conclusion, a GFP-SYP123-expressing line, driven by the SYP123 promoter (4), was crossed with rhd4 and Atget1rhd4 mutants to analyze GFP signal intensity using CLSM. In both the rhd4 and Atget1rhd4 backgrounds, GFP fluorescence intensity in root hairs was significantly weaker compared to Col-0, and comparable to the previously observed levels in Atget1 single mutants (13) (Fig. 5E). Subsequent immunoblot analyses of membrane protein fractions from these lines revealed lower levels of GFP-SYP123 in the membrane fraction of Atget1, rhd4, and Atget1rhd4 mutants compared to Col-0, indicating reduced SYP123 protein levels not only in the plasma membrane of Atget1 but also when RHD4 is missing (Fig. 5 F and G). Additionally, quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) analyses demonstrated reduced SYP123 transcript levels in rhd4 mutants compared to Col-0 (SI Appendix, Fig. S17 A and B), corroborating previous findings with Atget mutants (13). These results support our genetic data and hypothesis that RHD4 functions in GET receptor assembly which is necessary for TA protein abundance.

Discussion

We found a unique mechanism by which the turnover of PI4P to PI within the ER regulates protein trafficking. PI4P serves as a key lipid cue that recruits AtGET1 and AtGET2 together, while RHD4 subsequently converts PI4P to PI, thereby triggering stable receptor dimerization and the formation of a functional insertase complex.

In yeast, PI4P at the ER has a negative role and must be continuously degraded via the Osh–Sac1–mediated PI4P cycle, with the ER acting as a sink for PM-derived PI4P (48). In plants, PI4P is well established at the Golgi (51), TGN (35), and PM (36, 44), where it recruits effector proteins to mediate trafficking. Here, we identify PI4P-based local signaling at the ER in Arabidopsis, where the phosphatase RHD4 efficiently converts PI4P to PI. In the absence of RHD4, PI4P was barely detectable on the ER and instead accumulated around ER bodies (Fig. 3 E–J), suggesting that plants may employ a PI4P regulatory mechanism analogous to that in yeast (48, 49), thereby maintaining low ER PI4P levels.

The PI4P-RHD4 module exhibits remarkable spatial specificity in regulating the assembly of the GET receptor complex. Specifically, PI4P interacts with an R172 located near the cytoplasmic leaflet within TMD1 of AtGET2 (Fig. 1A), while the phosphatase domain of RHD4 is also positioned on the cytoplasmic side (SI Appendix, Fig. S65 A–H). This spatial colocalization enables PI4P to undergo dephosphorylation, catalyzed by RHD4, converting it into PI, thereby facilitating the assembly of the GET complex. This process highlights the precise spatial regulation and functional significance of the PI4P-RHD4 module in orchestrating GET receptor complex formation.

Our study also found that when AtGET2R172A was physically linked to AtGET1, mutating the lipid binding residue did not prevent rescue of the double mutant phenotype (Fig. 2 H and I). This suggests that the physical proximity of both receptors can override the requirement for PI4P recruitment. Interestingly, although AtGET2R172A lost its ability to bind PI4P, it retained an albeit weak interaction with AtGET1 (Fig. 2 D–G and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B).

In yeast and mammals, PI binding to Get2 (CAML) is critical for GET insertase stability (21). In Arabidopsis, AtGET2 interacts with both PI and PI4P, with stronger affinity for PI4P, whereas AtGET1 and RHD4 interact only with PI4P. The AtGET2R172A mutant binds neither lipid and cannot find AtGET1 within the ER; however, a synthetic AtGET2R172A–AtGET1 fusion, which binds only PI4P, rescues this defect, indicating that PI4P is essential in the proximity stage of AtGET receptor assembly. In the rhd4 mutant, AtGET1 and AtGET2 fail to form a stable complex, suggesting PI4P acts as a negative factor—similar to its ER role in yeast (39, 48). Our study does not exclude a potential role for PI in GET receptor formation. Overexpression of AtGET2R172A failed to complement the Atget2 mutant, as root hair length remained comparable to that of the knockout mutant (Fig. 1 E and F), indicating that this weak interaction is nonfunctional—possibly due to the absence of PI involvement. RHD4 may convert PI4P to PI during assembly, and PI’s interaction with AtGET2 might stabilize the complex and promote conformational changes. The GS linker in the synthetic fusion could provide similar stabilization.

Collectively, our study highlights the pivotal role of the ER-localized PI4P-RHD4 module in regulating GET receptor assembly. By dynamically modulating lipid–protein interactions, this module provides essential spatiotemporal control over TA protein insertion at the ER and is crucial for promoting polarized cell growth during root hair development.

Materials and Methods

Plant Materials and Growth Conditions.

All experiments were carried out in Arabidopsis thaliana Col-0 background lines, including T-DNA insertion mutants, CRISPR/Cas9 knockouts, and transgenic reporter lines. Seeds were germinated on ½ MS medium and plants grown under long-day conditions in controlled growth chambers (SI Appendix, Supporting Materials and Methods).

Construct Design and Transformation.

Reporter and interaction constructs were generated by Gateway or In-Fusion cloning, verified by sequencing, and introduced into Arabidopsis via floral dip or into Nicotiana benthamiana by syringe infiltration. A full list of constructs and oligonucleotides is provided in SI Appendix, Supporting Materials and Methods.

Protein Interaction Analyses.

Protein–protein and protein–lipid interactions were analyzed by pull-down assays, lipid strip and PolyPIPosome binding, coimmunoprecipitation, rBiFC, FRET-FLIM, and in vivo chemical cross-linking (SI Appendix, Supporting Materials and Methods).

Microscopy.

Subcellular localization of fluorescent protein fusions and PI4P distribution were examined by confocal microscopy and microsomal fractionation. Root hair imaging was performed using stereomicroscopy and quantified in Fiji (SI Appendix, Supporting Materials and Methods).

Gene Expression and Statistics.

Transcript levels were quantified by RT–qPCR, normalized to reference genes, and analyzed using the ΔCt method. Statistical analyses were performed in R and Excel, as detailed in SI Appendix.
