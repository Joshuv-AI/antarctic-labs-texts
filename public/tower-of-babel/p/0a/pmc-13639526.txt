Increased Extracellular-to-Total Body Water Ratio in Community-Dwelling Individuals in their 20s to 90s is Associated with Decreased Grip Strength and Gait Speed
Authors: Akemi Hioka, Naoki Akazawa, Naomi Okawa, Shinji Nagahiro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn 2019, the European Working Group of Sarcopenia in Older People indicated the importance of assessing muscle mass and quality in diagnosing sarcopenia. The extracellular water-to-total body water ratio (ECW/TBW) has recently attracted attention as an indicator of muscle quality. This study aimed to clarify the associations of ECW/TBW and grip strength and gait speed in community-dwelling individuals in their 20s to 90s.MethodsThis study employed a cross-sectional design. We enrolled 661 community-dwelling individuals (aged ≥20). The ECW/TBW was measured using multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analysis. Associations between grip strength, gait speed, and ECW/TBW were analyzed separately for males and females using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to examine factors associated with grip strength and gait speed. ResultsCorrelation analyses revealed significant negative correlations between ECW/TBW and grip strength (males: ρ=-0.413, p<0.001; females: ρ=-0.384, p<0.001) and gait speed (males: ρ=-0.122, p=0.037; females: ρ=-0.206, p<0.001). In the multiple linear regression analysis, ECW/TBW was significantly and independently associated with grip strength (males: β=-0.207, p=0.006; females: β=-0.215, p=0.001) and gait speed (males: β=-0.213, p=0.013; females: β=-0.305, p<0.001). ConclusionThe present study identified negative associations between ECW/TBW and both grip strength and gait speed among community-dwelling individuals in their 20s to 90s. Additionally, these associations were confirmed in males and females.Keywords: Extracellular water-to-total body water ratio, Hand strength, Walking speed

INTRODUCTION

In 2019, the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People (EWGSOP) revised its diagnostic criteria, underlining the significance of evaluating both muscle mass and muscle quality in identifying sarcopenia.1) Furthermore, the Sarcopenia Definition and Outcomes Consortium reported that skeletal muscle mass measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) is not a reliable predictor of mortality.2) Based on this suggestions, assessing skeletal muscle mass alone may be insufficient for diagnosing sarcopenia.

Recently, the extracellular water-to-total body water ratio (ECW/TBW) measured by bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) and muscle echo intensity measured by ultrasound imaging have attracted attention as a potential indicator of muscle quality.3,4) Age-related deterioration in muscle quality is associated with changes in body water distribution. Total body water (TBW) is divided into intracellular water (ICW) and extracellular water (ECW). ICW primarily reflects muscle cell mass, whereas ECW represents the water content of the extracellular space, including plasma and interstitial fluid.5) The ECW/TBW is an index indicating the proportion of ECW relative to TBW, and its increase is driven by a reduction in ICW and/or an increase in ECW. Age-related muscle atrophy reduces ICW, while conditions such as edema increase ECW. Therefore, an elevated ECW/TBW may indirectly reflect impaired muscle quality.6) Although extracellular water-to-intracellular water ratio (ECW/ICW) has also been used as a potential indicator of muscle quality, ECW/TBW can be more readily obtained from some models' BIA measurements and may be more applicable in clinical and community settings. Several studies7,8) reported that ECW/TBW increases with age, and individuals with higher ECW/TBW are more likely to have sarcopenia and severe sarcopenia in community-dwelling older individuals. Moreover, elevated ECW/TBW has been reported to be associated with higher levels of disability among older individuals with osteopenia, sarcopenia, or osteosarcopenia.9)

In community-dwelling older females, ECW/ICW has been reported to be negatively related to grip strength and gait speed.10,11) However, this study,10) targeted only community-dwelling older females. Therefore, whether the ECW/TBW is negatively associated with grip strength and gait speed in young and middle-aged males and females is unclear. Clarifying these relationships in younger and middle-aged adults, as well as in both sexes, may extend the applicability of ECW/TBW as a useful potential indicator of muscle quality beyond older female populations. These associations may highlight the importance of utilizing ECW/TBW as a muscle quality indicator in diagnosing sarcopenia. This study aimed to clarify the associations of ECW/TBW and grip strength and gait speed in community-dwelling individuals in their 20s to 90s.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design and Participants

This study employed a cross-sectional design. Participants were community-dwelling individuals aged 20 years and older from Yoshinogawa City and Kitajima Town in Tokushima, Japan. Recruitment was conducted through advertisements placed in public magazines distributed in these municipalities. The study included community-dwelling individuals who were able to walk independently. The exclusion criterion was the inability to perform BIA due to a cardiac pacemaker or pregnancy. A total of 682 individuals initially consented to participate. Of these, 21 were excluded: five due to cardiac pacemakers, two due to pregnancy, and 14 due to missing data or incomplete questionnaires. Consequently, 661 community-dwelling individuals in their 20s to 90s were included in the final analysis. The study was approved by the Tokushima Bunri University Ethics Committee (Approval No. R1-17 and R3-34) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Bioelectrical Impedance Analysis

ECW/TBW was measured with participants in a standing position using direct segmental multifrequency BIA (DSM-BIA) (BWA 2.0; InBody, Tokyo, Japan). The DSM-BIA employed an 8-point tactile electrode system, conducting 40 impedance measurements across eight frequencies (1, 5, 50, 250, 500, 1,000, 2,000, and 3,000 kHz). The device used to assess body water did not apply statistical corrections for age, sex, or race. Extracellular water, intracellular water, and ECW/TBW were calculated using built-in software formulas based on the participant’s height, weight, and measured impedance. Touch-type electrodes were placed on both wrists and ankles. During the measurement, participants were instructed to maintain arm and leg positions that prevented contact between body segments.

Other Measurements

Additional variables considered in this study included age, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), number of medications, presence of pain, and presence of medical history. BMI (kg/m²) was calculated by dividing body weight (kg) by height squared (m²). Previous studies have reported that a higher number of medications and the presence of pain are associated with reduced motor function in community-dwelling older individuals.12-14) Accordingly, information on the number of medications, the presence of pain, and the presence of medical history was obtained through a self-reported questionnaire. Medical history included conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia, heart disease, cerebrovascular disease, fractures, and osteoarthritis. Grip strength was measured using a Jamar dynamometer (model 563213; Patterson Medical, Nottinghamshire, UK). The measurement position was 90° elbow flexion while sitting,15) and the measurement was performed on the right side. Grip strength was assessed twice, and the maximum score was recorded. To measure gait speed, we asked the participants to walk a distance of 6 m at a comfortable speed.1,16) Gait speed (m/s) was calculated based on the time required to walk the middle 4 m of the route.

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software version 28 (IBM SPSS Japan, Tokyo, Japan) and R software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The normality of continuous variables—age, height, weight, BMI, number of medications, grip strength, gait speed, and ECW/TBW—was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Parametric data are reported as mean±standard deviation, whereas nonparametric data are expressed as median (25th–75th percentile). In males, the p-values of the Shapiro–Wilk test for grip strength, gait speed, and ECW/TBW were <0.001, 0.134, and <0.001, respectively. In females, the corresponding values were p=0.441, p<0.001, and p<0.001. Because several variables did not satisfy the assumption of normality, non-parametric analyses were considered more appropriate. Therefore, Spearman correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the associations between variables. Additional analyses were performed in females by stratifying participants according to ECW/TBW status (<0.400 group and ≥0.400 group). Within each subgroup, the associations between ECW/TBW and grip strength and gait speed were examined using Spearman’s correlation coefficients. Similar analyses were not conducted in males because the number of participants with ECW/TBW ≥0.400 was insufficient for meaningful statistical analysis. Associations between grip strength, gait speed, and ECW/TBW were analyzed separately for males and females. Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to examine factors associated with grip strength and gait speed. Independent variables included age, BMI, number of medications, presence of pain, medical history, and ECW/TBW. In these analyses, pain and medical history were coded as binary variables (1=presence, 0=absence). To assess whether the associations between ECW/TBW and grip strength and gait speed were linear, restricted cubic spline analyses were performed. Independent variables included age, BMI, number of medications, presence of pain, and medical history. When significant nonlinearity was detected (p for nonlinearity <0.05), segmented regression analyses were conducted to examine potential threshold effects. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05. The effect size (f²) for the multiple linear regression analysis was calculated using the formula f² = R²/(1−R²).17) Based on this effect size, statistical power was estimated using an alpha level of 0.05, the total sample size, and six predictor variables. Power and sample size analyses were conducted using G*Power version 3.1.9.2 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität, Düsseldorf, Germany).

Sample Size Calculation

A recent study10) reported an effect size (f²) of 0.522 for a multiple linear regression analysis predicting grip strength. The independent variables in that study were ECW/ICW, age, BMI, number of medications, presence of pain, and presence of medical history. In that study’s multiple linear regression analysis, ECW/ICW and the other two variables were independently and significantly associated with grip strength. Notably, ECW/ICW is considered to be substantially equivalent to ECW/TBW.11) Based on these findings, we expected a similar effect size in our multiple linear regression analyses for both grip strength and gait speed. An a priori sample size calculation—using an effect size (f²) of 0.522, statistical power of 0.95, an alpha level of 0.05, and six predictor variables—indicated that a minimum of 47 participants would be required.

DISCUSSION

The present study identified negative associations between ECW/TBW and both grip strength and gait speed among community-dwelling individuals in their 20s to 90s. These associations were observed consistently in both males and females.

A previous study targeting older females aged 65 years or older has reported negative correlations between ECW/ICW and grip strength and gait speed.10) The ECW/ICW and ECW/TBW are interchangeable and reflect muscle quality.3,10,11) Considering this, although the previous study's findings were limited to a specific population (i.e., older females aged ≥65 years), they support the results of the present study. Unlike the previous study10) that focused on older females, the present study included both males and females across a wide age range from young adulthood to older age. Therefore, the novel finding of this study is that the negative associations between ECW/TBW and grip strength and gait speed were consistently observed across both sexes and a wide range of age groups.

It has been reported that community-dwelling older individuals who have high ECW/TBW (≥0.391) are more likely to have sarcopenia and severe sarcopenia than older individuals who have low ECW/TBW (<0.391).8) In addition, elevated ECW/TBW has been reported to be associated with higher levels of disability among older individuals with osteopenia, sarcopenia, or osteosarcopenia.9) Based on the results of previous studies8,9) and the current study and the EWGSOP recommendations emphasizing the importance of evaluating not only skeletal muscle mass but also muscle quality in the diagnosis of sarcopenia,1) it is believed that ECW/TBW will increasingly attract attention as an indicator of muscle quality. The results of this study demonstrate the usefulness of ECW/TBW as a muscle quality indicator. In addition, our results may be important foundational references in discussions about whether to include ECW/TBW in the diagnosis of sarcopenia in the future.

In diagnosing sarcopenia according to EWGSOP and Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia guidelines, skeletal muscle mass index (SMI) measured by BIA is often used.1,15) However, it has been reported that the muscle mass measured by BIA may be overestimated due to the influence of overhydration.18) Traditionally, ECW/TBW has been utilized as an edema indicator in research and clinical settings and a value of 0.400 or higher suggests the consideration of the presence of edema.9,19,20) In this study, among all participants, 45 out of 661 individuals (6.8%) had an ECW/TBW of 0.400 or higher. When focusing on participants aged 65 and older, 41 out of 227 individuals (18.1%) indicated an ECW/TBW of 0.400 or higher. In stratified analyses of females, the associations between ECW/TBW and both grip strength and gait speed were generally consistent across ECW/TBW groups (<0.400 and ≥0.400), although the number of participants in the ≥0.400 group was limited; therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. In other words, the impact of overhydration on the overestimation of SMI cannot be ignored in this study. A previous study targeting older females found a significant correlation between SMI and grip strength in the group with an ECW/TBW of less than 0.400, but this relationship was not observed in the group with an ECW/TBW of 0.400 or higher.3) Considering the results of this study and the findings of the previous study,3) using ECW/TBW as an indicator of muscle quality is important.

This study has four limitations. First, because this study was a cross-sectional design, the longitudinal associations between ECW/TBW and grip strength and gait speed remain unclear. Future longitudinal studies are necessary to clarify these associations. Second, the study population consisted solely of community-dwelling individuals. As a result, the findings may not be generalizable to older individuals in inpatient settings, and the associations between ECW/TBW and physical function in that population remain unclear. Third, although significant associations were observed between ECW/TBW and grip strength and gait speed, the strength of these associations varied across outcomes and sexes. In particular, the association with gait speed in males was weak. Moreover, given the large sample size, some statistically significant findings may reflect modest effect sizes. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, we did not assess hydration status, nutritional status, edema status, or physical activity, all of which may contribute to an increased ECW/TBW. Because these factors can influence ECW/TBW, the observed associations should be interpreted with caution. Furthermore, although SMI was measured, it was not included in the adjusted models because this study aimed to examine the association between ECW/TBW and grip strength and gait speed exploratorily, and BMI was selected as an indicator of body size. Given the close relationship between BMI and SMI, inclusion of both variables may have resulted in potential overadjustment. Nevertheless, muscle mass may have influenced the observed associations. Future studies should consider these factors when evaluating the relationship between ECW/TBW and outcomes.

In conclusion, the present study identified negative associations between ECW/TBW and both grip strength and gait speed among community-dwelling individuals in their 20s to 90s. These associations were observed consistently in both males and females. These findings suggest that ECW/TBW may reflect aspects of muscle quality indirectly.
