Comment on: “The status and determinants of demoralization in patients with colorectal cancer: a cross-sectional study in China”
Authors: Kunyu Han, Jiahe Wang, Jiumao Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Zhou et al. investigated demoralization syndrome (DS) among Chinese patients with colorectal cancer and identified cancer metastasis, anxiety, and depression as factors independently associated with greater DS severity. Their study provides valuable evidence for psychological screening and supportive care in this population. In this comment, we highlight two analytical considerations that may further strengthen the interpretation of their findings. First, although social support was associated with DS severity in univariate analysis, the loss of statistical significance after multivariable adjustment should not be interpreted as evidence of irrelevance; rather, social support may influence DS indirectly through anxiety, depression, or disease burden. Mediation or path analysis may therefore better reflect the underlying causal structure. Second, dichotomizing the continuous DS-II score into mild versus moderate-to-severe categories may reduce statistical power and obscure clinically meaningful variation. Analyzing the DS-II score as a continuous or ordinal outcome could provide a more informative assessment of the determinants of DS.

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the study by Zhou et al. on the status and determinants of demoralization syndrome (DS) in Chinese patients with colorectal cancer (CRC) (Zhou et al. 2025). In a sizeable single-centre sample, the authors document a moderate level of DS and identify cancer metastasis, anxiety, and depression as independent correlates of greater severity, providing useful evidence for psychological screening in this population. We would offer two suggestions concerning the analytical approach, which we believe could draw further insight from these valuable data.

First, social support differed across DS severity in the univariate analysis but was not retained in the multivariable model. We would caution against reading this as evidence that social support is unimportant. A variable that is associated with the outcome univariately but loses significance after adjustment is frequently acting through other variables in the model rather than exerting no effect of its own. Here, the influence of social support on DS is plausibly mediated by anxiety, depression, or disease burden, a pattern consistent with reports that social support affects demoralization through intermediate psychological resources (Ping et al. 2025). The authors themselves raise an analogous possibility, noting that depression may mediate the association between metastasis and DS. A mediation or path model, rather than a single multivariable regression that treats covariates as competing independent effects, would therefore represent the likely causal structure more faithfully and would avoid prematurely discarding social support as a target for intervention.

Second, the DS-II generates a continuous score, yet the outcome was dichotomised into mild versus moderate-to-severe demoralization and analysed by binary logistic regression. Because the dichotomy was drawn at essentially the median score, this approach carries a statistical cost: dichotomising a continuous variable at its median reduces the power to detect associations by approximately the same amount as discarding a third of the observations (Altman and Royston 2006). Analysing the continuous DS-II score (for example by linear regression), or alternatively an ordinal model preserving the mild-moderate-severe gradient, would recover this power and yield a more granular picture of the determinants of DS. It would also supply the continuous outcome required for the mediation analyses suggested above.

These are constructive suggestions rather than reservations about the study’s substance; the identification of metastasis, anxiety, and depression as correlates of DS is a valuable contribution, and the focus on psychological distress in CRC is welcome. Re-analysing the continuous outcome within a mediation framework would, in our view, further strengthen the mechanistic interpretation and the clinical utility of these findings.
