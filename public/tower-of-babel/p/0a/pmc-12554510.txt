Neochlorogenic acid inhibits gastric cancer cell growth through apoptosis and cell cycle arrest
Authors: Wenqiang Sun, Yongchao Li, Menglong Xu, Haocheng Guan, Ruijun Shi, Shuwei Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGastric cancer is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide. It remains a significant clinical challenge due to its frequent diagnosis at advanced stages and the development of resistance to conventional therapies. Neochlorogenic acid (NCA) is a phenolic compound with reported anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-tumor properties. This study aims to elucidate the mechanisms underlying NCA’s anti-tumor effects in gastric cancer.MethodsIn vitro, HGC-27 and NUGC-3 cells were treated with NCA. The half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values of the two cells at 48 hours after drug treatment were 229.9 and 130.0 µM, respectively, and cell proliferation, migration, cell cycle, and apoptosis were evaluated. Western blot analysis was used to assess the expression of apoptosis-related proteins. RNA sequencing was performed to identify differentially expressed genes and affected pathways. In vivo, a xenograft model was used to assess the effect of NCA on tumor growth.ResultsNCA inhibited the proliferation and migration of gastric cancer cells in vitro. Cell cycle analysis showed that NCA induced G1 phase arrest. Apoptosis assays and Western blot analysis revealed that NCA promoted apoptosis. In NUGC-3 cells, NCA treatment upregulated BAX (BCL2-associated X protein, 2.2-fold), CASP3 (caspase-3, 1.5-fold), BID (BH3 interacting domain death agonist, 1.3-fold), and CYCS (cytochrome c, somatic, 1.5-fold), while downregulating BCL2 (B-cell lymphoma 2, 0.8-fold). In HGC-27 cells, more pronounced effects were observed, with BAX dramatically upregulated (9.9-fold), along with increases in CASP3 (1.7-fold), BID (2.4-fold), and CYCS (1.4-fold), and a significant downregulation of BCL2 (to 0.2-fold). RNA sequencing identified significant changes in gene expression related to cancer pathways. In vivo, NCA significantly suppressed tumor growth.ConclusionsNCA demonstrates significant anti-tumor activity against gastric cancer cells both in vitro and In vivo, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic agent. These findings suggest that NCA suppresses gastric cancer via apoptosis and cell cycle arrest and warrants further preclinical and clinical evaluation as a potential therapeutic strategy for gastric cancer.

Introduction

Gastric cancer remains a formidable global health challenge, marked by high incidence and mortality rates, despite a decline in its global incidence over the past century. As one of the most prevalent malignancies of the digestive system, its development is influenced by various factors, including Helicobacter pylori infection, advancing age, high salt intake, and diets low in fruits and vegetables (1,2). Gastric cancer continues to be a leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, ranking as the 5th most common cancer and the 4th leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally in 2022, with an estimated 1.1 million new cases and 800,000 deaths annually. The 5-year survival rate remains disappointingly low, particularly in advanced stages, often falling below 30% in many regions (3,4). The aggressive nature and heterogeneity of gastric cancer present significant challenges in diagnosis, treatment, and early screening (5), particularly for patients diagnosed at advanced stages who face a median survival of less than 12 months (6,7). Consequently, the urgent need for innovative treatment strategies to improve patient outcomes has prompted increased exploration of natural compounds with anti-cancer properties.

Neochlorogenic acid (NCA, Figure 1A), a phenolic acid abundant in various plants (8,9), possesses a range of biological activities that contribute to its overall therapeutic potential (10,11). Initial studies focused on its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, demonstrating its ability to modulate immune responses and alleviate oxidative stress (12,13). In addition, NCA has shown promise in improving metabolic disorders and viral infections (14-16). More recently, the focus has shifted towards exploring the anti-cancer potential of NCA. Studies have revealed that NCA can inhibit tumor growth, induce apoptosis, and suppress metastasis through various mechanisms (17-19). Furthermore, NCA has been shown to enhance the sensitivity of cancer cells to chemotherapeutic agents while protecting normal cells from toxicity (20). While its diverse anti-cancer properties have been reported in various malignancies such as breast cancer and intestine cancer, a comprehensive understanding of NCA’s specific role and underlying mechanisms in gastric cancer remains largely underexplored, representing a significant knowledge gap in the field. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the effects of NCA on gastric cancer cells, with a particular emphasis on its influence on apoptosis and cell cycle regulation. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR and ARRIVE reporting checklists (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-696/rc).

Methods

Reagents and cell culture

In this study, NCA was sourced from Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. in Shanghai, China (Cat No. B21396). Upon arrival, the compound was immediately stored at −20 ℃ to maintain its stability. The gastric cancer cell lines HGC-27 and NUGC-3 were cultured in a nutrient-rich solution of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM). This medium was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and a 1% solution of penicillin-streptomycin to prevent contamination. All cell lines were incubated at 37 ℃ under a humidified, 5% CO2 atmosphere. Additionally, the HGC-27 (Cat No. CL-0103) and NUGC-3 (Cat No. CL-0878) cell lines, along with the FBS (Cat No. 164210), penicillin-streptomycin (Cat No. PB180120), and DMEM (Cat No. PB180120) used for their culture, were purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology, located in Wuhan, China. The passages of HGC-27 and NUGC-3 cells used in the experiment were within 10 to 20 generations.

Cell proliferation assay

To assess cell proliferation, the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay was performed. HGC-27 and NUGC-3 cells (3×104 cells/well) were cultured in 96-well plates and treated with varying concentrations of NCA in DMEM for 24 and 48 hours. Following incubation, the medium was replaced with 100 µL of DMEM containing 10% CCK-8 solution (Cat No. CA1210; Solarbio, Beijing, China) and further incubated for 1.5–2.0 hours. The absorbance at 450 nm was then determined using a microplate reader. Half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were derived from the data using GraphPad Prism 9.5 software.

Cell migration assay

Cell migration was assessed using a wound-healing assay. HGC-27 and NUGC-3 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured until a confluent monolayer was formed. A linear wound was created in the cell monolayer using a 200 µL pipette tip. Following washing with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) to remove non-adhering cells, the cells were maintained in serum-free DMEM. Images of cell migration were captured at selected time points. The area of the wound was analyzed using ImageJ software, and the migration rate was calculated as: migration rate (%) = (initial wound width − wound width at the end of the experiment)/initial wound width ×100%.

Cell cycle analysis

For cell cycle analysis, HGC-27 and NUGC-3 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 2×105 cells/mL. The cells were then treated with NCA at concentrations corresponding to their respective IC50 values: 229.9 µM for HGC-27 cells and 130 µM for NUGC-3 cells. As a control, cells were treated with an equal volume of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as a vehicle. Following a 48-hour incubation period, cells were harvested via trypsinization and fixed overnight in 70% ice-cold ethanol at −20 ℃. After fixation, the cells were washed with PBS, resuspended, and then incubated in the dark with 2 µL of RNase A (1 mg/mL) and 50 µL of propidium iodide (PI; 100 µg/mL) for 20 minutes. Analysis of cell cycle distribution was performed using a flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), and the percentage of cells in each phase (G1, S, and G2/M) was determined with FlowJo_V10 software (BD Biosciences).

Cell Transwell assay

For the cell Transwell assay, HGC-27 and NUGC-3 cells (2×104 cells/well) were seeded in serum-free medium onto the upper chamber of Transwell chambers (Cat No. 3464; Corning, New York, USA). These chambers were pre-coated with growth factor-reduced Matrigel (BD Biosciences, CA, USA). Both the cells and NCA were added to the upper chamber; the NCA concentration was 281.6 µM for HGC-27 cells and 246 µM for NUGC-3 cells. In the lower chamber, 600 µL of DMEM medium containing 10% FBS was added. The cells were incubated for 24 hours. After incubation, the migrated cells (on the lower surface of the membrane) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes and stained with crystal violet at room temperature for 5 minutes. The Transwell chambers were washed with distilled water, and the migrated cells were photographed under an optical microscope. The number of migrated cells was then quantified using ImageJ software.

Apoptosis assay

Cell apoptosis and necrosis were detected using the YO-PRO-1/PI cell apoptosis and necrosis detection kit (Cat No. C1075S; Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). HGC-27 and NUGC-3 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated for 24 hours at 37 ℃ in a humidified 5% CO2 atmosphere. The HGC-27 cells were treated with NCA at 229.9 µM (IC50), and the NUGC-3 cells were treated with NCA at 130.0 µM (IC50). Control cells received only the vehicle. Following incubation, the medium was aspirated, and the cells were washed once with PBS. The cells were then incubated with 1 mL of YO-PRO-1/PI staining solution per well at 37 ℃ in the dark for 20 minutes. Fluorescence staining was observed using a fluorescence microscope.

Western blot analysis

For Western blot analysis, total protein from HGC-27 and NUGC-3 cells was extracted and separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE). The separated proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). Non-specific binding was blocked by incubating the membranes with blocking buffer (Cat. No. PS108; Epizyme, Shanghai, China) for 15 minutes. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies for BCL2 (B-cell lymphoma 2) (Cat. No. bs-0032R; Bioss, Beijing, China), BID (BH3 interacting domain death agonist) (Cat. No. 10988-1-AP), CYCS (cytochrome c, somatic) (Cat. No. 10993-1-AP) from Proteintech (Wuhan, China), as well as β-actin (Cat. No. 81115-1-RR, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and BAX (BCL2-associated X protein) (Cat. No. 50599-2-Ig, Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Following three washes with 1× Tris-Buffered Saline with Tween-20 (TBST) buffer, a goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Cat. No. bs-0295G-HRP; Bioss, Beijing, China) was applied for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were then detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent kit (Cat. No. SQ101; Epizyme) after further washes. ImageJ software was used to quantify band intensities, with β-actin serving as the loading control.

For quantification, the integrated optical density (IOD) of each target protein band was measured and normalized to its corresponding β-actin band. The normalized protein expression was then calculated as fold changes relative to the average normalized value of the untreated control group (set to 1), using the following formulas:

❖ Normalized value (sample) = IOD (target protein, sample)/IOD (β-actin, sample);

❖ Normalized value (sample) = IOD (target protein, sample)/IOD (β-actin, sample);

❖ Relative expression (sample) = normalized value (sample)/average normalized value (control group).

❖ Relative expression (sample) = normalized value (sample)/average normalized value (control group).

RNA sequencing and analysis

HGC-27 cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin until a confluence of 70–80% was achieved. Subsequently, the cells were treated for 48 hours with NCA at its IC50 concentration, alongside a DMSO control. Following treatment, the cells were washed with RNase-free PBS, detached, and then centrifuged at 500 ×g for 5 minutes at 4 ℃. The resulting cell pellets were lysed in 1 mL of TRIzol reagent (for ≤107 cells), and lysates were obtained through pipetting until clarity was observed. Samples were stored at −80 ℃ prior to shipment on dry ice to OmicShare Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China) for RNA sequencing. For this study, three independent biological replicates were prepared for each treatment group (NCA treated and vehicle control). The RNA sequencing data processing and bioinformatics analysis were performed using OmicShare Biotechnology’s cloud analysis platform (www.omicshare.com). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified with a significance threshold of adjusted P value <0.01 and an absolute log2 fold change >1. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed utilizing OmicShare Biotechnology’s cloud analysis platform, with reference to the Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes databases.

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the ethical guidelines of Tarim University and were approved by the Science and Technology Ethics Committee of Tarim University (protocol code 2024071). Male BALB/c nude mice (6 weeks old, 19–21 grams) were purchased from Sipeifu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and used to establish subcutaneous xenograft models. Each mouse received a subcutaneous injection of 5×106 HGC-27 cells in the right axillary region. When tumors reached approximately 50 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to two groups (n=5 per group): an NCA treatment group (50 mg/kg) and a control group (5% DMSO in PBS). The NCA group was gavaged daily with NCA (50 mg/kg in 200 µL of 5% DMSO in PBS), while the control group received the vehicle (200 µL of 5% DMSO in PBS) by daily gavage. Tumor dimensions were measured with calipers, and tumor volume was calculated as: volume = (length × width2)/2. After tumors reached a specified endpoint, the mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised, weighed, measured, and photographed.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently performed at least three times, and data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Prior to statistical analyses, data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene’s test. All data met the assumptions for parametric testing. Statistical analyses of the differences between groups were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.5 software, employing either a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Student’s t-test, as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined as a P value less than 0.05.

Results

NCA inhibits in vitro proliferation of HGC-27 and NUGC-3 cells

The capacity of NCA to inhibit the proliferation of HGC-27 and NUGC-3 cells was evaluated using CCK-8 assays to establish IC50 values. When these cells were treated with different concentrations of NCA for 24 and 48 hours, a reduction in cell viability was observed, which was dependent on both time and concentration. Specifically, the IC50 values after 24 hours of treatment were determined to be 281.6 µM for HGC-27 cells and 246.0 µM for NUGC-3 cells. After 48 hours of treatment, the IC50 values decreased to 229.9 µM for HGC-27 cells and 130.0 µM for NUGC-3 cells (Figure 1B,1C).

NCA induces G1 phase cell cycle arrest in HGC-27 and NUGC-3 cells

Cell cycle progression is vital for successful cell division. To assess the impact of NCA on cell cycle dynamics, HGC-27 and NUGC-3 cells were treated with NCA for 48 hours. Following this, the cells were stained with PI and subjected to flow cytometric analysis (Figure 2A). The results showed a significant increase in the proportion of HGC-27 cells in the G1 phase, rising from 52.73% to 67.04% (P<0.01). In contrast, the percentage of cells in the S phase decreased from 41.85% to 25.67%, while a slight increase in the G2 phase was observed, rising from 5.42% to 7.29% (Figure 2B). Similarly, NUGC-3 cells demonstrated a substantial increase in the G1 phase, with proportions shifting from 57.39% to 69.63% (P<0.01). This increase was accompanied by reductions in the S phase population (from 36.23% to 21.29%) and a minor rise in the G2 phase (from 6.38% to 9.09%) (Figure 2C). The results showed that the proportion of NUGC-3 cells in G1 phase increased significantly, from 57.39% to 69.63% (P<0.01). On the contrary, the percentage of cells in S phase decreased from 36.23% to 21.29%, while that in G2 phase increased slightly from 6.38% to 9.09% (Figure 2D).

NCA inhibits tumor cell migration and invasion

Cell migration is a critical factor in tumor metastasis. To evaluate the effect of NCA on the migratory capacity of gastric cancer cells, wound-healing assays were performed. After a 24-hour exposure to NCA, the migration rate of HGC-27 cells decreased from 78.50% in the control group to 15.64% in the NCA-treated group (P<0.001) (Figure 3A,3B). Similarly, the migration rate of NUGC-3 cells also decreased from 74.71% to 11.10% (P<0.001) in the NCA-treated group compared to the control group (Figure 3C,3D).

Microscopic examination revealed that control cells exhibited a high degree of transmigration through the Matrigel-coated membrane, forming a dense layer of cells on the lower surface after crystal violet staining. Quantitatively, the invasion rate of HGC-27 cells decreased from 64.1% in the control group to 33.4% in the NCA-treated group (P<0.001) (Figure 4A,4B). YO-PRO-1/PI assay reveals NCA-mediated cell death in gastric cancer cells

Apoptosis, a regulated process of cell death, is crucial for maintaining cellular balance. To determine if NCA could stimulate apoptosis and necrosis, HGC-27 and NUGC-3 cells were incubated with NCA for 48 hours. Following incubation, cells were assessed using the YO-PRO-1/PI apoptosis and necrosis detection assay. Microscopic observations revealed that, relative to controls, NCA-treated HGC-27 and NUGC-3 cells exhibited a substantially greater proportion of YO-PRO-1 positive cells (indicating early-stage apoptosis) and PI positive cells (denoting late-stage apoptosis or necrosis). In particular, NCA exposure visibly intensified YO-PRO-1 staining in both cell lines, suggesting the induction of apoptosis in its early phases (Figure 5). Furthermore, augmented PI staining in NCA-exposed cells implied a rise in the occurrence of late apoptosis and/or necrotic cell breakdown.

NCA impact on apoptosis-related protein expression in HGC-27 and NUGC-3 cells

Western blot assays were performed to determine if NCA alters the expression of proteins associated with apoptosis in HGC-27 and NUGC-3 gastric cancer cells.

Pro-apoptotic effects

Following a 48-hour exposure to NCA, protein levels of BAX, CASP3 (caspase-3), BID, and CYCS were significantly elevated in both HGC-27 and NUGC-3 cells (Figure 6A,6C) compared to control cells. This increase was confirmed via densitometric quantification (Figure 6B,6D).

NCA modulates the HGC-27 cell transcriptome

Transcriptomic profiling of NCA-treated HGC-27 cells revealed notable shifts in gene expression. Principal component analysis (PCA) (Figure 7A) showed distinct clustering of control (NC) and NCA-treated (NCA2) samples (PC1 =62.9%, PC2 =25.5%). Volcano plot analysis (Figure 7B) identified 3,670 DEGs with 2,438 upregulated and 1,232 downregulated (adjusted P<0.01). Enrichment analysis (Figure 7C) highlighted significant pathways, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signaling pathway, proteoglycans in cancer, lipid and atherosclerosis, P53 signaling pathway, microRNAs in cancer, Wnt signaling pathway, axon guidance, neurotrophin signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, breast cancer, basal cell carcinoma, hepatitis B, insulin resistance, mTOR signaling pathway and gastric cancer.

NCA suppressed tumor growth in nude mice

While in vitro studies have demonstrated NCA’s ability to inhibit the proliferation of HGC-27 and NUGC-3 gastric cancer cells, its anti-cancer efficacy In vivo remained to be fully assessed. To this end, we established an HGC-27 xenograft model in nude mice (Figure 8). Macroscopic observation (Figure 8A) indicated that tumors in NCA-treated mice were visibly smaller than those in the DMSO-treated control group. Furthermore, quantitative analysis of tumor volumes (Figure 8B) showed a statistically significant reduction (P<0.01) following NCA treatment.

Discussion

This study provides compelling evidence for the anti-tumor effects of NCA in gastric cancer, encompassing both in vitro and In vivo investigations. Our key findings demonstrate that NCA inhibits the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HGC-27 and NUGC-3 gastric cancer cells. Furthermore, NCA induces G1 phase cell cycle arrest and promotes apoptosis in these cells. Mechanistically, NCA modulates the expression of apoptosis-related proteins, upregulating pro-apoptotic factors (BAX, CASP3, BID, CYCS) and downregulating the anti-apoptotic protein BCL2. In vivo, NCA significantly suppressed tumor growth in a nude mouse xenograft model.

Prior research has established the anti-neoplastic potential of NCA across a spectrum of cancer types, underscoring its broad anti-cancer activity. Consistent with our findings, studies have demonstrated that NCA can effectively trigger apoptosis, restrain migration, and facilitate cytoskeleton collapse in cancer cells by anchoring mitochondrial calcium uniporter (MCU)-based calcium overload (18). Furthermore, NCA enhances the antitumor effects of pingyangmycin in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) via regulating TOP2A (20). Moreover, bread enriched with NCA can inhibit the growth of colon cancer cells in vitro (21). Our study extends these findings by demonstrating similar effects in gastric cancer, specifically highlighting the role of G1 arrest and apoptosis via modulation of BAX, CASP3, BID, CYCS, and BCL2.

Crucially, our RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis of NCA-treated HGC-27 cells revealed significant alterations in gene expression profiles, particularly enriching pathways such as MAPK, p53, and TNF signaling. These findings provide critical mechanistic insights into NCA’s anti-tumor effects in gastric cancer. NCA’s influence on the MAPK pathway aligns with its observed effects on cell proliferation and apoptosis (22-24). Its modulation of the p53 pathway underscores its ability to induce G1 arrest and apoptosis by affecting this critical tumor suppressor (25-27). Furthermore, NCA’s impact on the TNF signaling pathway suggests its role in modulating the inflammatory microenvironment and influencing cell fate (28,29). Together, these RNA-seq findings highlight NCA’s multi-faceted action on key oncogenic and tumor-suppressive pathways in gastric cancer.

Our findings strongly suggest that NCA has the potential to be developed as a novel therapeutic agent for gastric cancer. However, the successful translation of NCA from preclinical studies to clinical application necessitates addressing several key aspects. As a natural compound with limited existing clinical data and, to our knowledge, no human clinical trials to date, the clinical relevance of our findings is primarily established at the mechanistic and preclinical efficacy level. Our study provides a strong rationale for initiating the crucial translational research required to bridge this gap.

To advance NCA towards clinical development, several critical steps are required. Firstly, comprehensive pharmacokinetic (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) studies are essential. Future research should focus on characterizing its absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, dose-response, and duration of action, thereby addressing challenges inherent to many natural compounds like bioavailability and formulation. Addressing NCA’s inherent challenges, such as its bioavailability, will be critical, potentially through advanced delivery strategies like nanoparticle-based systems. Secondly, ensuring tissue selectivity to cancer cells while minimizing off-target effects on healthy tissues is paramount. This requires comprehensive preclinical toxicity testing to establish a robust safety profile necessary for future human trials. Furthermore, investigating the optimal dosage and delivery methods for NCA, as well as its potential synergistic effects with existing chemotherapeutic drugs, will be crucial. For example, combination studies with commonly used agents like 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) or oxaliplatin are highly recommended to identify potential synergistic interactions that could improve clinical efficacy and reduce toxicity.

The strengths of this study include the use of both in vitro and In vivo models to demonstrate the anti-tumor effects of NCA in gastric cancer. In addition, we have provided mechanistic insights into the mode of action of NCA by examining its effects on cell cycle regulation and apoptosis. However, there are also some limitations in this study that warrant consideration for future research.

First, the RNA-seq findings, while comprehensive, require direct mechanistic validation. Future studies will employ targeted experimental approaches (e.g., p53 knockdown, MAPK inhibition, BCL2 overexpression, TNF-α receptor blockade) to precisely identify the molecular mechanisms underpinning NCA’s anti-tumor effects, which may also reveal novel drug targets.

Second, our findings are based on only two gastric cancer cell lines, and the absence of normal gastric cell controls limits the assessment of NCA’s selectivity. Future studies should include a broader panel of cancer cell lines alongside appropriate normal cell controls to ensure generalizability and evaluate differential effects.

Third, we recognize that proliferation may confound migration and invasion assays, even with serum starvation. To isolate NCA’s specific effects on cell motility, future experiments will incorporate mitotic inhibitors (e.g., mitomycin C).

Conclusions

This study unequivocally demonstrates the significant anti-tumor efficacy of NCA in gastric cancer, both in vitro and In vivo. Our work reveals that NCA exerts its effects by inducing G1 cell cycle arrest and apoptosis, alongside a broad modulation of gene expression in key cancer-related pathways such as TNF, P53, and MAPK signaling. The consistent suppression of tumor growth in the xenograft model validates these promising in vitro observations.

These findings highlight NCA’s potential as a novel therapeutic strategy for gastric cancer. Its ability to simultaneously target multiple pathways involved in cancer progression suggests a promising avenue for future drug development. To advance NCA towards clinical application, future efforts are warranted to: (I) fully characterize its PK and PD properties; (II) conduct comprehensive preclinical toxicity studies; and (III) investigate its potential in combination therapies to enhance efficacy and mitigate resistance.
