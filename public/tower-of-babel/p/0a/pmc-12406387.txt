Exploring the associations between physical inactivity and cognitive function, mental health, and metabolic parameters in U.S. adolescents: results stratified by sex
Authors: Dan Zhang, Baoli Zhang, Qianlan Wu, Xiaoju Peng, Yan Shao, Rong Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood is an important window for early identification of health risk factors, shaping health behaviors, and preventing future chronic diseases. As a major risk factor, low physical activity (PA) is currently highly prevalent among children worldwide. Meanwhile, long-term low PA has been shown to be associated with short-and even long-term poor health outcomes. Therefore, this study first analyzed the association of low PA with some early chronic disease predictors, followed by further sex-stratified analysis.MethodsThis study used cross-sectional survey data from the National Health and nutrition examination between August 2021 and 2023 August. The Functioning Questionnaire, Global Physical Activity Questionnaire and self-assessment questionnaire were used in the investigation, and metabolic parameters were assessed according to the results of biochemical examination and human body morphological measurements. Binary logistic analysis and linear regression was used to analyze the relationships between PA and chronic disease predictors and their sex stratification.ResultsThe mean age of the 3 957 participants was 8.94 years (SD = 1.15), and 1 940 (40.0%) were girls, 2 017 were boys. Chi-square analysis showed that girls had lower levels of PA, boys had lower memory, adaptability, self-control and sociability, and girls were more likely to suffer from depression and anxiety, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) was higher in girls than boys (all P < 0.05). On multivariate analysis, boys' lower PA was associated with lower sociability, higher HDL, greater waist circumference (WC), and higher body mass index (BMI). Lower PA in girls was associated with lower self-control ability, higher hypersensitive C-reactive protein (H-CRP), higher WC, and higher BMI.ConclusionsThe lower the level of PA of children is, the higher the health risk of cognitive function and metabolic parameters, and these association differ by sex. On the basis of this difference, targeted interventions can be proposed to reduce the burden of disease in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24321-8.Keywords: Physical activity, Chronic disease, Children

Introduction

Chronic non-communicable disease has become one of the major global public health challenges. In recent years, increasing evidence has shown that many risk factors for chronic diseases in adulthood can be traced back to childhood and adolescence. In this context, early identification of chronic disease risk factors and implementation of interventions is particularly important. Recent decades have witnessed growing scientific interest in health behavior-disease risk associations, and the role of health-related behaviors in the management of disease has been highlighted, a growing number of studies have focused on the adverse health effects of health-risk behaviors such as physical inactivity, sedentary behavior, and circadian disruption. These health risk behaviors may increase the risk of chronic diseases such as anxiety and depression, cardiometabolic diseases and even dementia [1–3]. As a major risk factor, low physical activity (PA) is currently highly prevalent among children worldwide and has been shown to be closely related to the occurrence and development of various chronic diseases [4, 5]. Extensive research demonstrates that regular low PA not only increases the risk of chronic disease [6, 7], but also impairs cognitive performance, especially executive function (EF) in later life [8–10]. Specifically, lower PA is strongly associated with lower cognitive function [8], mood disorders [9, 11], and metabolic disorders [12]. The health risks of chronic physical inactivity are clear and extend across race, sex, and age [4, 5].

Childhood is not only a critical period for shaping behavior, but also a golden age for early detection of future chronic disease risk. Growing epidemiological evidence confirms that many adult chronic diseases originate from pathophysiological processes beginning in childhood, highlighting the importance of early risk detection over post-onset treatment. Recent advances in life course epidemiology have elucidated strong associations between childhood health indicators and adult disease risk. The landmark 1932 Scottish Psychological Survey Cohort Study demonstrated a dose-response relationship between childhood cognitive performance and later-life dementia risk, with notable sex differences [13], providing novel insights into early neurodegeneration predictors. Similarly, multicenter meta-analyses have established childhood depressive symptoms as significant predictors of impaired adult psychosocial functioning [14]. In metabolic health, puberty emerges as a pivotal period for metabolic syndrome development, with adolescent lipid and glucose profiles strongly predicting later Mets risk [15], underscoring the value of early metabolic monitoring. These findings suggest that multidimensional health risk assessment and targeted interventions early in life can not only improve current health status, but also significantly reduce the lifelong chronic disease burden through cumulative trajectory effects.

Remarkably, physical activity (PA) in different sex there are significant differences, especially in children and adolescents [16, 17]. Cross-national studies reveal boys consistently engage in higher levels of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) than girls across diverse populations [16], with similar findings documented in Latin American youth regarding both activity levels and sedentary behaviors [17]. Correspondingly, the effects of PA on the occurrence and development of different chronic diseases also have sex differences. Samoan data demonstrate PA inversely correlates with obesity risk exclusively in boys but not girls [18]. Likewise, Chinese adolescent research reveals vigorous activity (VPA) protects against depressive symptoms in girls (OR = 0.62, 95%CI:0.41–0.83), while moderate activity (MPA) shows benefits for boys during/post puberty (OR = 0.58, 95%CI:0.39–0.77) [19]. A study from Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development Study confirms differential metabolic impacts, with low activity predicting higher BMI in males but not females [20]. Therefore, there may be significant sex differences in how PA modulates cognitive function, mental health, and metabolic health trajectories during childhood.

While existing evidence highlights the detrimental health effects of physical inactivity on short-and long-term adverse health outcomes, several critical knowledge gaps remain. First, current research predominantly examines isolated risk factors (e.g. BMI, waist circumference, inflammatory markers) for specific chronic conditions (metabolic disorders, mental health, or cognitive decline), failing to capture the multidimensional nature of chronic disease risk profiles. This fragmented approach may underestimate an individual’s comprehensive risk of chronic disease. Second, despite established sex differences in health behaviors, few studies have systematically investigated how physical inactivity differentially impacts boys’ and girls’ cognitive, psychological, and metabolic health. Our study specifically addresses this gap by analyzing sex-stratified analyses between inactivity and these three key health domains. Last, while most research focuses on middle-aged and older populations [21, 22], chronic disease trajectories often originate in childhood. To address this, we analyzed 2021–2023 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data to: (1) examine the associations between physical inactivity and children’s cognitive function, mental health, and metabolic markers; and (2) further perform sex-stratified analyses of these associations. These findings will inform early interventions targeting cognitive impairment, mental health issues, and cardiometabolic risk reduction in pediatric populations.

Materials and methods

Participants and procedure

This study utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES 2021–2023) [23], a nationally representative cross-sectional program that employs multistage cluster sampling to assess the health status of civilian populations in the United States. The data collection involved two components: (1) structured interviews conducted by trained personnel using validated questionnaires, and (2) comprehensive physical evaluations performed at mobile examination units, incorporating standardized anthropometric assessments, body fluid collection and testing.

The NHANES study design adheres to federal guidelines detailed in the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services Policy for Human Research Protections. All survey instruments, operational protocols, and informed consent procedures were approved by both the NCHS Institutional Review Board and the Office of Management and Budget. Participation necessitated written consent from all subjects or their legal guardians.

For this specific analysis, we excluded: (a) individuals over 18 years old, (b) pregnant adolescents, and (c) cases with missing data for basic demographic variables (ID, sex, age, place of birth, ratio of family income to poverty, head of household-related variables), resulting in a relatively complete-case analytical approach.

Measure

Physical activity

The Physical Activity Questionnaire for adolescents in the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) was designed based on the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire [24]. Children aged 2–11 and 16–17 were interviewed by trained interviewers according to the NHANES guidelines. For respondents aged 12 to 15 years of age, the questionnaire was self-administered at the Mobile Examination Center (MEC) using the Audio Computer-Assisted Self-Interview (ACASI) system.

According to the WHO PA Guidelines, children and adolescents need at least 60 points of moderate-to-high intensity PA (MVPA) per day, so based on the feedback results of questionnaire: children who achieved 60 min of MVPA per day (~ 420 min/week; 420 × 3 MET = ~ 1260 MET-min/week) were included as the high PA group, coded as 0, i.e., the reference group; children who did not achieve 60 min of MVPA per day were included as the low PA group, the code is 1. PA was included in the analysis as a dichotomous variable.

Cognitive function

The cognitive function questionnaire of the NHANES was based on the Functioning Questionnaire (variable name prefix FNQ). The FNQ includes 16 questions from the Child Functioning Module (CFM) that collect information for participants aged 5–17 years. The CFM questions ask about the child’s level of difficulty (no difficulty, some difficulty, a lot of difficulty, or cannot do at all) in 13 basic domains of functioning, including seeing, hearing, walking, self-care, communication, learning, cognition (memory and attention), adaptability, self-control ability, sociability, and the frequency (daily, weekly, monthly, a few times a year, never) of experiencing anxiety and depression.

This study refers to Diamond A [25] on executive function, a pointed out that the core indicators of cognitive function include: memory, attention, adaptability, self-control, in addition to, Bornstein MH et al. [26] points out that social ability is very important for children’s cognitive function, so this study selected“ memory, attention, adaptability, self-control ability, and sociability” 5 indicators to measure cognitive function. In this study, the 5 indicators were analyzed as a binary classification (no difficulty, difficulty), and the “difficulty” group was coded as 1, and the“no difficulty” group was coded as 0. All indicators were included in the analysis as dichotomous variables.

Mental health

Depressive symptoms were primarily assessed using the frequency of depressive symptoms in the past 2 weeks using the PHQ9 scale, with a score of 10 as the cut-off value, with ≥ 10 being the group with depression and < 10 being the group without depression [27]. Anxiety symptoms were assessed using the item“How often are you very anxious, nervous or worried? Would you say daily, weekly, monthly, a few times a year or never?” in the questionnaire, and subjects chose “daily, weekly, monthly, a few times a year or never”. We used 1 episode per week as the cut-off, ≥ 1 episode per week as the anxiety group, and < 1 episode per week as the no anxiety group. All indicators were included in the analysis as dichotomous variables.

Metabolic parameters

In this study, waist circumference (WC), body mass index (BMI), hypersensitive C-reactive protein (H-CRP) and HDL were selected as metabolic parameters according to the definition of metabolic parameters by Zhang Y et al. [28] and Perona JS et al. [29].The subjects were sent to a mobile health center to collect data on metabolic parameters.

The body measurement data (WC and BMI) were collected by trained health technicians. Height and weight were measured by trained health technicians using calibrated equipment. Participants stood upright on a stadiometer (SECA 213, Hamburg, Germany) without shoes, recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm. Measured in light clothing using a digital floor scale (Toledo 2186, Ohio, USA), calibrated daily, recorded to the nearest 0.01 kg. BMI was calculated as weight (kg)/height (m)2. WC was measured at the end of normal expiration using a steel tape (Gulick II, USA) placed horizontally at the iliac crest, perpendicular to the long axis of the body. Three consecutive measurements were taken; the average was recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm.

The blood samples were collected via venipuncture. The serum specimens were then processed, stored, and transported to the Advanced Research and Diagnostic Laboratory (ARDL) at the University of Minnesota, Minneapolis, MN, for analysis. Fasting venous blood samples (≥ 8.5 h) were collected in serum separator tubes (SST), centrifuged at 1,200×g for 10 min, and analyzed within 24 h. HDL was quantified via direct enzymatic two-reagent immunoassay (Roche Cobas 6000, Roche Diagnostics, USA; CV < 3.2%). Serum H-CRP was measured by latex-enhanced nephelometry (Siemens BN II ProSpec, Germany; detection range: 0.2–1440 mg/L, CV < 6.0%). Values > 10 mg/L were excluded (potential acute inflammation).

In this study, we analyzed the values of 4 metabolic parameters as continuous variables.

Sleep duration and screen time

This questionnaire was asked by trained interviewers using the Computer-Assisted Personal Interview (CAPI) system in the participant’s home or by telephone. Sleep duration on weekdays was typically collected using entry “Number of hours usually sleep on weekdays or workdays”, sleep duration on weekends was collected using entry “Number of hours usually sleep on weekends or non-workdays”, and daily screen time was collected using entry “On a typical day during the school year, about how many hours do you usually spend playing with a smartphone or computer, watching TV or movies, or playing video games?”, We used 2 h per day as the cut-off, ≥2 h/d as the long group, and < 2 h/d as the short group. Sleep duration was included in the analysis as continuous variables, screen time was included in the analysis as dichotomous variables.

Data analysis

All the statistical tests were two-sided, and significance was set at P < 0.05. All the data were analyzed via SPSS version 23.0, and the graphs were plotted via GraphPad Prism 8. In the present study, categorical variables were summarized using frequencies (n) and percentages (%), whereas continuous variables with a normal distribution were represented as the means and standard deviations (SDs). For cognitive function, anxiety and depression, we used multiple imputation to fill in the missing values, while for metabolic indices, we simply deleted the missing values.

Firstly, Chi-square test and T test were used to analyze the differences in the distribution of cognitive function, mental health and metabolic indicators among children with different PA levels. Second, in unadjusted models, we used logistic regression models to analyze the association of PA with cognitive function and mental health, and to apply linear regression models to analyze the association of PA with metabolic parameters. In the adjusted model, participants’ age, sex, place of birth, and family income-to-poverty ratio, as well as the head of household’s age, sex, educational level, and marital status were included as covariates in the model for adjustment, then logistic regression and linear regression models were used to analyze the association of PA with mental health, cognitive function, and metabolic parameters. Finally, we further performed a sex stratification analysis.

Distribution of predictors of chronic diseases in different groups

The distribution of predictors of chronic diseases by sex is shown in Table 1. With regard to cognitive function, we did not observe sex differences in the distribution of attention, but we found significant sex differences in the distribution of memory, adaptability, self-control ability and sociability (P < 0.05). Specifically, boys have higher rates of low memory (22.9% vs. 19.6%, P = 0.036), low adaptability (39.6% vs. 33.8%, P = 0.002), low self-control (29.5% vs. 23.4%, P < 0.001) and low sociability (19.6% vs. 16.0%, P = 0.015) than girls. In contrast, girls have higher rates of depression (19.4% vs. 14.4%, P = 0.001) and anxiety than boys (37.6% vs. 31.5%, P < 0.001).

Table S1 and Fig. 1 show the distribution of predictors of chronic diseases in children across PA subgroups. The results showed that compared with children in the high PA group, children in the low PA group had lower memory (19.4% vs. 23.1%, P = 0.037) and sociability (14.0% vs. 19.2%, P < 0.001), and higher self-control (31.0% vs. 24.2%, P < 0.01). In addition, children who were physically inactive also showed higher levels of depression (12.9% vs. 18.9%, P < 0.001) and anxiety (31.5% vs. 36.9%, P = 0.009). Meanwhile, H-CRP (1.33 mg/L vs. 2.02 mg/L, P = 0.034), WC (61.99 cm vs. 72.37 cm, P < 0.001) and BMI (18.36 kg/m2 vs. 21.55 kg/m2, P < 0.001) of low PA children were higher than those of high PA children.

Regression analysis between PA and predictors of chronic disease

Figure 2 and Table S2 summarize the regression models assessing the association of PA in children with predictors of chronic disease. In crude models, we observed that low PA was positively associated with low memory, low self-control, low sociality, depression, anxiety, high waist circumference, and high BMI (all P < 0.05). However, after adjusting for age, sex, place of birth, ratio of family income to poverty, head of household’s age, head of household’s sex, head of household’s education level, head of household’s marital status and other covariates only a positive association of low PA with high WC (OR: 2.81, 95%CI: 1.60 ~ 4.02, P < 0.001) and high BMI (OR: 1.16, 95%CI: 0.65 ~ 1.66 P < 0.001) was observed.

Sex stratification analysis

Further sex stratified analyses are shown in Table S3 and Fig. 2, in models with crude variables, lower PA in boys was strongly associated with lower sociality, lower HDL, higher WC and higher BMI (all P < 0.05). After adjusting for covariates such as age, sex, place of birth, ratio of family income to poverty, head of household’s age, head of household’s sex, head of household’s education level, head of household’s marital status and other covariates, the results did not change significantly, with boys’ lower PA still associated with lower sociality (OR: 1.56. 95%CI:1.11 ~ 2.20, P = 0.011), lower HDL (OR:−2.01, 95%CI: −4.08~−0.11, P = 0.038), higher WC (OR: 3.41, 95%CI:1.67 ~ 5.14, P < 0.001) and higher BMI (OR: 1.34, 95%CI: 0.64 ~ 2.03, P < 0.001).

At the same time, in the results of the analysis on girls, we observed that in the crude model, girls who are less physically active tend to have lower self-control ability, higher depressive symptoms, higher H-CRP, higher WC, and higher BMI (all P < 0.05). However, after adjusting for covariates such as age, sex, place of birth, ratio of family income to poverty, head of household’s age, head of household’s sex, head of household’s education level, head of household’s marital status and other covariates, we did not observe an association between low PA and depressive symptoms (P > 0.05) in girls, and other associations did not change significantly. In other words, girls who were less physically active showed lower self-control ability (OR: 1.42, 95%CI: 1.02 ~ 1.97, P = 0.038), higher H-CRP (OR: 0.72, 95%: 0.04 ~ 1.39, P = 0.037), higher WC (OR: 2.46, 95%CI: 0.75 ~ 4.17, P = 0.005), and higher BMI (OR: 0.96, 95%CI: 0.21 ~ 1.71, P = 0.013).

Discussion

Identifying early behavioral interventions for chronic disease is critical. Our findings support evidence from previous studies that PA is strongly associated with metabolic health, specifically, insufficient PA significantly correlates with elevated WC and increased BMI. After further sex-stratified analyses, we found that the association between physical inactivity and cognitive function differed by sex. Low PA boys showed lower sociability, lower HDL, higher WC, and higher BMI. In contrast, girls with low PA showed lower self-control ability, higher H-CRP, higher WC, and higher BMI.

As expected, we found that the association between low PA and cognitive function differed between sexes, we discovered that boys with low PA showed lower sociability and girls with low PA showed lower self-control ability. As early as 2010, Junger et al. [30] found in a study of adolescents that those with higher self-control tended to exhibit greater PA levels—a result consistent with our findings. However, our study further suggests that physical inactivity may impair the development of self-control, implying a potential bidirectional relationship between PA and self-control ability. But in a recent study of medical undergraduates, Zafar U et al. [31] did not observe an association between PA and self-control ability. This may be due to the observation of medical undergraduates, who tend to exhibit lower PA and higher self-control ability due to academic stress. Our study observed an association between physical inactivity and low self-control ability only in girls. This may be due to pronounced sex differences in types of PA, a study of Chinese adolescents found that girls are more inclined to participate in endurance sports such as running and swimming, whereas boys are more inclined to participate in group physical activities such as basketball and football [32]. Besides, our study also suggests that less physically active boys are less social. Previous research on American college students has suggested an association between PA and sociability [33]. Hinkley T’s study [34] also found that among preschoolers, more outdoor activity was associated with higher sociality, while more screen time might lead to lower sociability. Studies in early childhood also suggest that longer screen time and physical inactivity are associated with poorer sociability [35]. Although there is no direct evidence that this association is found only in boys, there is some indirect evidence that peer support is important for men to participate in PA, this may suggest that boys are more willing to engage in physical activities with greater social attributes [36], which may affect their sociability.

Unlike other studies, no association of PA with depressive or anxiety symptoms was observed in our study. In univariate analysis, there were significant differences in the distribution of depressive and anxiety symptoms across PA and sex, but this association disappeared after adjusting for a large number of covariates. This may be because the initial association of PA with depressive and anxiety symptoms is driven by socioeconomic and HH-related confounders, or indirectly through mediating pathways such as socioeconomic. A large number of studies [9, 11] have confirmed the association between PA and symptoms of anxiety and depression, but the main mechanism of this association is still unclear. Although the present study did not find an association between PA and mental health, it provides insight into how the association may be influenced by the covariates (age, place of birth, ratio of family income to poverty, head of household’s age, head of household’s sex, head of household’s education level, head of household’s marital status and other covariates) we included. Future longitudinal studies are needed to verify the causal direction and further control for residual confounding.

In addition, we found that children with low PA levels are at significant metabolic risk, and this result is consistent with evidence from previous studies [37–39]. In the present study, a low PA level was associated with a disrupted metabolic system (higher WC and BMI). From observational studies and animal studies to clinical trials in different populations and age groups, a growing body of evidence suggests that PA plays an important role in the prevention of obesity and metabolic disease [40, 41]. Notably, compared with the overall analysis, sex-specific analysis has more practical significance for the prevention of metabolic diseases. In our study, we observed that greater PA was associated with greater HDL levels, lower WC and lower BMI in boys; high PA was associated with lower H-CRP levels, lower WC, and lower BMI in girls. Sex may be an important factor affecting the association between physical inactivity and metabolic health in adolescents, but the conclusions are inconsistent. In a clinical trial of 199 Swedish adolescents aged 8–12 years, the effect of PA on blood glucose levels was more pronounced in boys than in girls of the same age [42]. In contrast, a Spanish randomized controlled trial of 712 8- to 10-year-olds in 20 schools who received a PA intervention revealed that, compared with those in the control group, obesity rates and low-density lipoprotein levels were lower in girls in the intervention group, but no significant differences were found in boys [43]. Inconsistencies in these findings may be attributed to differences in sex-specific behaviors or the timing of puberty onset [33]. Huus et al. [42] noted that boys had higher levels of PA than girls did, which may explain the improvement in their blood glucose levels. Martinez-vizcaino et al. observed only a significant effect of PA intervention on obesity in girls after sex stratification [43]. In a review of PA and metabolic health in adolescents, Whooten R et al. suggested that these sex differences may be due to behavioral differences and may change at different ages [44]. These mechanisms may indirectly contribute to sex differences in PA and metabolism, although there are no consistent conclusions about sex differences.

This study has several limitations. First, this study used a predominantly self-report format to assess PA, cognitive function, and mental health, which may be difficult to avoid reporting bias, further exploration could be undertaken in the future using accelerometer-assessed physical activity data. Second, our cross-sectional study design made it difficult to establish causal links between PA and predictors of chronic disease. Nevertheless, evidence regarding the single effect of PA on chronic disease risk has been identified in previous Mendelian randomization studies [45, 46], implying that reverse causation was less likely to occur in our study. Thus, high-quality prospective population-based studies should be conducted to verify this causal relationship in the future. Third, this study did not assess participants’ maturational status (e.g., pubertal stage or skeletal age), which may influence the observed outcomes. Future research should consider controlling for maturation-related confounders, especially in populations with heterogeneous developmental trajectories. Besides, The study variables exhibited varying degrees of missing data. While multiple imputation was applied to address missingness in questionnaire-derived measures (e.g., PA, cognitive function, and mental health), metabolic biomarkers with higher missingness rates could not be reliably imputed due to systematic gaps or insufficient auxiliary data. This partial handling of missingness may introduce bias and limit the generalizability of findings, particularly for analyses involving metabolic outcomes. Future studies should prioritize rigorous prospective data collection protocols to minimize attrition in objective physiological measures. Fourth, given that NHANES is a nationally representative survey with a complex sampling design, we didn’t apply sampling weights in their analyses to ensure population-level estimates (after accounting for missing data), it may bias the results and limit generalizability. Fifth, the physiological indicators of metabolic health in this study included only H-CRP, HDL, WC and BMI, but some important indicators such as blood pressure, glucose, insulin, etc., were not included in our study, therefore, it is necessary to incorporate these indicators into the design of future studies. Sixth, while our analysis presents sex-stratified results, it is important to note that these findings should be interpreted with caution as we did not formally test for statistical interactions between sex and other variables. The observed differences between boys and girls subgroups may reflect variations in sample characteristics rather than true biological or social sex differences. Future studies incorporating sex-by-predictor interaction terms would help elucidate whether these stratifications represent meaningful effect modifications. Finally, while we examined PA’s associations with cognitive, mental, and metabolic health domains separately, an integrated analysis using composite health indices (e.g., z-score combinations) could better capture cumulative effects. Future studies could adopt such multidimensional approaches to assess holistic health impacts.

Despite these limitations, the current study has several strengths. First, we evaluated the early predictors of chronic diseases from three dimensions, namely, cognitive function, mental health, and metabolic indicators, to evaluate the risk of chronic diseases more comprehensively. Second, we used children data to observe the impact of modifiable health risk behaviors on predictors of chronic disease and to intervene early in life to reduce future risk. Finally, we investigated the differences in the associations between PA and predictors of chronic diseases in different sex groups by sex stratification, which is beneficial for targeted interventions. In a word, our findings extend prior evidence by delineating sex-specific pathways linking physical inactivity to metabolic and cognitive health in children and adolescents. Physical inactivity in girls is strongly associated with elevated H-CRP and deficits in self-control, whereas inactivity in boys may contribute to low HDL and social deficits, suggests that tailored interventions are critical for mitigating chronic disease risks. From a translational perspective, our results advocate for integrating sex-responsive strategies into school-based health programs, leveraging combined behavioral-metabolic screenings. Future research should explore whether targeting these sex-specific factors (e.g., inflammation reduction in girls, social-physical activities for boys) enhances intervention efficacy.

Conclusions

Using cross-sectional data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (August 2021-August 2023), this study examined sex-specific associations between physical activity levels and chronic disease risk factors. In boys, lower PA was associated with lower sociability, higher HDL, larger WC and higher BMI; in girls, lower PA was associated with lower self-control ability, higher H-CRP, increased WC, and higher BMI. Future longitudinal research should further investigate the causal relationship and explore whether mitigating these sex-specific risks (e.g., reducing H-CRP in girls or improving HDL in boys) through targeted interventions alters long-term health trajectories.
