Upper gastrointestinal symptoms and Gulf War Illness in a clinical cohort of US veterans: a retrospective, cross-sectional study
Authors: Abdelrahman Yousef, Sarah T Ahmed, Theresa H Nguyen Wenker, Alice B S Nono-Djotsa, Stephen H Boyle, Elizabeth J Gifford, Deeksha Malhotra, Helena Chandler, Sandhya Bandi, Drew A Helmer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveApproximately 30% of the 700 000 US Gulf War Veterans (GWVs) report symptoms collectively termed Gulf War Illness (GWI), a multisymptom illness of uncertain pathophysiology. Prior studies in GWI focus on overlap with irritable bowel syndrome. This study examines the associations between upper gastrointestinal (UGI) symptoms, GWI and specialty GI care.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analysed GWVs referred to a Veterans Health Administration clinical War-Related Illness and Injury Study Center (2008–2020). Symptoms, demographics, military service and clinical history were obtained from self-reported intake packets. GWI was defined by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention criteria requiring moderate-to-severe symptoms in at least two of three domains: fatigue, musculoskeletal and mood cognition. UGI symptoms were analysed individually as a composite variable and additively (0–5). Logistic regression models estimated ORs for associations between UGI symptoms, GWI and GI specialty care.ResultsThe cohort included 596 GWVs (mean age 49.3 years, 88% men). Most (93.5%) reported at least one UGI symptom, with a mean of 2.8 symptoms. GWI was identified in 413 (69%). Veterans with GWI were more likely to report UGI symptoms (98.3% vs 82.5%) and had a higher mean symptom count (3.1 vs 2.1). Adjusted ORs for UGI symptoms in GWI ranged from 1.79 (dysphagia) to 3.57 (nausea/vomiting).ConclusionUGI symptoms are common among GWVs and strongly associated with GWI. Clinicians should screen for UGI symptoms and follow standard protocols for treatment and referral.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Up to three decades after the Gulf War, upper gastrointestinal (UGI) symptoms remain a common problem for US Gulf War Veterans (GWVs) and are nearly universal among those with Gulf War Illness (GWI) (98.3%). Most published research studies of GWI focus on neurological dysfunction, fatigue and pain in GWVs despite the fact that GWI is a multisymptomatic illness and affects several body systems, including the GI tract. Previously, most of the reported research studies focused on lower GI symptoms in GWI. Even the Veterans Affairs/Department of Defense Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Management of Chronic Multisymptom Illness focus on lower GI symptoms consistent with irritable bowel syndrome and make no recommendations for the treatment of UGI symptoms.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

Our study provides a comprehensive and detailed analysis of upper GI symptoms in GWVs with and without GWI, as well as GI specialty utilisation for these symptoms in a cohort of 596 GWVs who were referred to a tertiary clinical evaluation centre in the VHA between 2008 and 2020. Our findings are consistent with and expand upon previous literature. Our study presents important data regarding the frequency of chronic UGI symptoms in this clinical cohort of GWVs referred for unexplained, chronic symptoms. The findings underscore the importance of evaluating all patients with GWI for the range of functional GI disorders and UGI symptoms. In association with poor mental health, untreated chronic symptoms are consistently related to lower quality of life in GWVs with GWI. Given the well-established, accessible and efficacious treatments, astute clinicians can recognise and recommend interventions to improve UGI symptoms in GWVs with GWI.

Our results reiterate that UGI symptoms, including nausea, vomiting, dysphagia, poor appetite, reflux and stomach pain, are significantly associated with GWI and merit focused clinical and research attention. Introduction

In 1990, approximately 700 000 US military personnel were deployed to the Persian Gulf region as part of Operations Desert Shield and Desert Storm.1 Within 1 year upon return, around a third of the Gulf War (GW) troops2 3 experienced diverse symptoms that have persisted and been described as Gulf War Illness (GWI).4 5

GWI manifests as a spectrum of chronic symptoms such as fatigue, widespread pain, cognitive disturbances, memory problems, skin rashes, gastrointestinal (GI) and respiratory difficulties.4 6 There is also no uniformly accepted case definition that exists for GWI,7 although the Institute of Medicine (IOM),1 in its independent review of GWI as mandated by the US Congress, recommended the use of either the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) criteria4 or the Kansas GWI criteria.5 The CDC case definition is based on three symptom domains— fatigue, musculoskeletal and mood cognition—and is labelled severe when symptoms in two or more domains are rated as moderate or severe by the veteran. Efforts are still ongoing to update and improve the case definition,7 8 identify biomarkers of the disease and discover an effective, specific treatment for GWI.9

GI symptoms have been widely reported by GWVs.10 11 In a population-based sample, GI symptoms were more prevalent among GWVs deployed to the combat theatre than contemporaneous servicemembers who were not deployed.12 The most commonly endorsed GI symptoms were diarrhoea, dyspepsia, heartburn, non-cardiac chest pain, functional GI disorders and constipation.7 13 14 The exact cause or mechanism of these GI symptoms has not yet been confirmed; however, some research studies have hypothesised that it could be partially explained by intestinal hyperpermeability that leads to higher abdominal pain ratings, greater stool frequency and looser bowel movements.13 Other research studies pointed to multiple potential contributing factors for these GI symptoms related to GW deployment, including infection, diet, stress, neurotoxic chemical exposures affecting GI motility and alteration of gut microbiome1519

Unexplained GI symptoms that persist despite treatments and interventions in a primary care setting, symptoms that worsen or the presence of multiple GI symptoms often warrant specialty evaluation (eg, specialist consultation, endoscopy, imaging). Studies of the GWVs’ experience of healthcare indicate that veterans with GWI report clinician ignorance of and even resistance to diagnosing GWI, which can negatively impact the evaluation and management of symptoms associated with GWI.20 Little is known about the referral patterns for GI specialty services in the context of GWI despite the uncertainty around its cause and evaluation and the high frequency of GI symptoms. The GWVs’ experience of symptoms consistent with irritable bowel syndrome, that is, lower GI symptoms, has been reported in the literature and explicitly addressed in the most recent Veterans Affairs/Department of Defense (VA/DoD) Clinic Practice Guideline for the Management of Chronic Multisymptom Illness.21 22

Little is known about the association of upper gastrointestinal (UGI) symptoms and GWI. The purpose of this study was to examine the association between GWI and UGI symptoms and to evaluate the patterns of GI specialty care utilisation for UGI symptoms among GWVs with and without GWI in a clinical cohort of veterans.

Methods

This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guideline for cohort studies (online supplemental file 1).

Setting and participants

Participants included 596 veterans who were deployed to the GW for any period between August 1990 and November 1991 and completed a clinical intake questionnaire at one of three War-Related Illness and Injury Study Center (WRIISC) sites between 2008 and 2020.23 In 2001, the VA Office of Public Health implemented the WRIISC as a tertiary referral clinical evaluation programme to focus on veterans with unexplained or difficult-to-diagnose health concerns possibly related to their deployment.24 25

Data collection and questionnaires

Veterans completed standard and WRIISC-specific questionnaires as part of the intake process for WRIISC evaluation. Questionnaires collected demographic, military service, symptom, diagnoses and deployment information.23 26 Healthcare utilisation data were abstracted from the electronic health record data maintained in the VHA Corporate Data Warehouse.

Data management and definitions

Gulf War Illness

In accordance with the CDC criteria,4 individuals were classified as having GWI if they self-reported experiencing at least one symptom for >6 months in two of three symptom categories. These categories included fatigue, musculoskeletal symptoms (eg, joint and muscle pain, stiffness) and mood and cognitive issues (eg, difficulty remembering, speaking and concentrating, mood changes, anxiety).27 The severity of GWI was classified as severe if one or more symptoms in two or more categories were rated as moderate or severe. We use the CDC severe GWI classification in all reported analyses.

UGI symptoms

Questionnaires collected information about UGI symptoms, focusing on ‘nausea/vomiting’, ‘stomach pain’, ‘dysphagia’ and ‘poor appetite’ in the last 6 months and ‘lifetime history of ulcer or reflux’. To assess ‘nausea/vomiting’ and ‘stomach pain’, questionnaire item responses related to the frequency of symptoms in the past 6 months, degree of bother and intensity were recoded into a dichotomous ‘yes/no’ symptom variable. The ‘dysphagia’ and ‘poor appetite’ items asked about the frequency of these symptoms in the last 6 months, with response options ranging from ‘almost never’ to ‘every day’. These were also dichotomised into symptom present or absent. The ‘lifetime history of ulcer or reflux’ item only had ‘yes’ or ‘no’ response options (see online supplemental table S1 for more details about the symptom items and classification).

The study analysed UGI symptoms as a composite binary variable (any or none of the five UGI symptoms) and as an additive symptom burden variable (0–5 of the UGI symptoms). The burden of UGI symptoms was also described as the mean (SD) and median (IQR) of the number of symptoms.

Healthcare utilisation

We defined the utilisation of GI specialty care as visiting a GI clinic (clinic stop code 307 and 321) from 1999 to 2022 or having an esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) procedure (CPT codes 43 200-43259, excluding 43246) from 1999 to 2021 anywhere in the VHA system and documented in the VHA electronic health record.

Veteran characteristics and covariates

Questionnaires collected information about veterans’ demographics, education and military branch.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive analyses compared the sociodemographic, UGI symptoms, GI specialty utilisation and other patient characteristics of those with and without severe GWI using χ2 tests for categorical variables (or Fisher’s exact test for expected values<5) and t-tests for continuous variables. Logistic regression models were used to estimate ORs and 95% CIs to determine the associations between severe GWI and UGI symptoms. Separate sets of logistic regression models estimated the association between GWI status and GI specialty care utilisation controlling for UGI symptom burden (dichotomised as a median split) and between UGI symptom burden and GI specialty care utilisation. All models were adjusted for effects of covariates (age, sex, race/ethnicity and education). All analyses were performed using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc, Cary, North Carolina, USA) and Stata V.15 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA).

Results

Cohort sociodemographic characteristics

The study cohort consisted of 596 GWVs seen at the WRIISC. Demographic, military, deployment and health characteristics of the sample are summarised in table 1. The mean age of the cohort was 49.3 years (SD 6.2), and most were men (88.3%). The majority self-identified as non-Hispanic white (74.8%). Of all individuals, 413 (69%) had GWI. Sex, smoking status, alcohol use and branch of service were not different between those with and without GWI (all p values>0.33). However, those with GWI had less formal education (9.0% vs 16.9% for bachelor’s degree, p<0.05) and were slightly younger (mean 48.8 years (SD 5.1) vs 50.2 years (SD 8.1), p=0.01) than those without GWI.

UGI symptoms

Most veterans reported at least one UGI symptom (n=557, 93.5%), and more than half had two or three symptoms (n=322, 54.1%), while a smaller number had four or five symptoms (n=190, 31.9%). The mean number of UGI symptoms was 2.8 (SD 1.3) and median of 3 (IQR 2). Stomach pain was the most common symptom (n=529, 88.8%), followed by nausea or vomiting (n=479, 80.4%), poor appetite (n=248, 41.6%), history of ulcer or reflux (n=216, 36.2%) and dysphagia (n=195, 32.7%). Almost all veterans with GWI (98.3%) reported at least one UGI symptom compared with 82.5% of those without GWI (p<0.001) and the mean number of UGI symptoms was 3.1 (SD 1.1) in veterans with GWI compared with 2.1 (SD 1.4) among veterans without GWI (p<0.01) (table 2).

The associations between GWI and each UGI symptom or composite measure of UGI symptom burden were statistically significant, with adjusted ORs ranging from 1.91 (95% CI 1.24 to 2.94) for dysphagia to 3.41 (95% CI 2.13 to 5.48) for nausea/vomiting and 10.55 (95% CI 4.34 to 25.63) for presence of any UGI symptom. Notably, when the number of GI symptoms was considered, there was an incremental increase in the odds of association with GWI relative to the reference of 0–1 symptom (adjusted OR (aOR) 4.56 (95% CI 2.65 to 7.85) for 2–3 symptoms and aOR 9.86 (95% CI 5.23 to 18.59) for 4–5 symptoms) (table 3).

GI specialty care utilisation

Just over one-third (n=218, 36.6%) of the veterans in our cohort had one or more GI clinic visit with a mean number of 4.5 (SD 11.2) visits over the study period; 214 (35.9%) of the cohort had three or more GI clinic visits. In our cohort, 244 (40.9%) of the veterans had at least one EGD with mean number of 0.92 (SD 3.1). A small number of veterans had three or more EGDs (n=55, 9.2%). The mean number of GI clinic visits between veterans with and without GWI was not significantly different (4.6 vs 4.4, p=0.87). Moreover, the mean number of EGDs was not significantly different (1.01 vs 0.73, p=0.31) between veterans with and without GWI. Similarly, no association was found in GI clinic visit or EGD between those with and without GWI (42.6% vs 37.7%, p=0.26). On the other hand, and as expected, having a higher burden of UGI symptoms (compared with low burden based on a median split) was consistently associated with higher GI specialty care utilisation (all p values<0.01) (table 4).

Discussion

More than two decades after the GW, UGI symptoms remain a common problem for GWVs and are nearly universal among those with GWI (98.3%) in this clinical cohort. It is reassuring that in this clinical cohort, GWVs with and without GWI were equally likely to receive GI specialty evaluation after considering the burden of UGI symptoms. Most published research studies of GWI focus on neurological dysfunction, fatigue and pain in GWVs28 29 despite the fact that GWI is a multisymptomatic illness and affects several body systems including the GI tract.10 15 Our results reiterate that UGI symptoms, including nausea, vomiting, dysphagia, poor appetite, reflux and stomach pain, are significantly associated with GWI and merit focused clinical and research attention.

Our study provides a comprehensive and detailed analysis of UGI symptoms in GWVs with and without GWI, as well as GI specialty utilisation for these symptoms in a cohort of 596 GWVs who were referred to a tertiary clinical evaluation centre in the VHA between 2008 and 2020. In a population-based study conducted by Camilleri et al using a telephone survey of the general US population, 44.9% of respondents reported experiencing one or more UGI symptoms within the past 3 months. The most commonly reported symptoms included early satiety, heartburn and postprandial fullness.30 In comparison, the prevalence of UGI symptom burden in our cohort of GWVs appears to be higher, consistent with findings reported in previous literature.11 12 15 A review published in 2015 showed that even though the risk of military personnel facing trauma in ground combat has decreased due to the emergence of high-tech wars such as the GW, non-battle injuries such as neuropsychological disorders and digestive diseases seemed to have increased.15 Another population-based epidemiologic study showed that GWVs are twice as likely to have irritable bowel syndrome and functional dyspepsia compared with Gulf Era veterans who served during that time but were not deployed to the GW.12

Koch et al showed in a prospective cohort of 24 GWVs the evidence of oesophagitis and gastritis on endoscopies and emphasised the importance of evaluation of patients with UGI symptoms, not only patients with chronic diarrhoea.10 Zhang et al showed that GWVs with persistent GI symptoms (n=73) commonly have increased intestinal permeability that potentiates the severity of abdominal pain, diarrhoea and stool consistency.13 Some studies have shown alteration of the gut microbiome,31 and alteration of the neural and immune systems of the intestine in mouse models of GWI.32

Previously, most of the reported research studies focused on lower GI symptoms in GWI.4 7 13 33 Even the VA/DoD Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Management of Chronic Multisymptom Illness focus on lower GI symptoms consistent with irritable bowel syndrome and make no recommendations for the treatment of UGI symptoms.21 22 Our study presents important data regarding the frequency of chronic UGI symptoms in this clinical cohort of GWVs referred for unexplained, chronic symptoms. The findings underscore the importance of evaluating all patients with GWI for the range of functional GI disorders and UGI symptoms. In association with poor mental health,34 untreated chronic symptoms are consistently related to lower quality of life in GWVs with GWI.29 35 Given the well-established, accessible and efficacious treatments, astute clinicians can recognise and recommend standard interventions to improve UGI symptoms in GWVs with GWI.

The need for GI specialty care for UGI symptoms in GWVs is clear based on the frequency and burden of UGI symptoms. Our analyses examined GI specialty clinic encounters and EGDs but were unable to look at specific diagnoses or resultant treatments. The strong association between UGI symptoms and GI specialty care is reassuring in the general sense, however; ‘on average’ people with greater severity and burden of UGI symptoms are getting GI specialty care. Although not statistically significant, the slightly elevated odds of GI specialty care for those with GWI relative to those without may also be an indication that in the presence of chronic, multisymptom illness, clinicians are more likely to be conservative in their management and refer GWVs with GWI for additional GI specialty care. This is counter to the perception among some GWVs' advocates that providers may ignore their symptom concerns.36 Further investigation of healthcare patterns and barriers and facilitators of optimal care would be helpful in the context of GI specialty care for GWVs with GWI.

This study represents the real-world experience of a cohort of GWVs up to 30 years after their deployment. It is one of the best characterised, large clinical cohorts of GWVs, although the findings must be considered in the context of the national tertiary referral centre. Veterans evaluated at the WRIISC are likely sicker and more engaged in VHA healthcare than the general population of GWVs as reported in other case series from the WRIISC.25 37 Findings should be generalised to other GWVs only with caution given the biased clinical sample. Another strength of the study is that symptoms were reported by the patients themselves (not recorded by clinicians in the medical record), enhancing their validity while the healthcare utilisation information came directly from the electronic health record and is therefore not subject to patient recall bias or memory lapses. On the other hand, the symptom items and response options from the questionnaire, although face valid, represented inconsistent framings (eg, frequency, severity) and were created specifically for the clinical programme. This weakness should motivate future efforts to characterise UGI symptoms using more widely accepted and validated instruments. Finally, ascertaining GWI status from self-reported symptoms according to the CDC severe GWI criteria represents a best practice as recommended by the IOM.

This study demonstrates the high frequency and burden of UGI symptoms in GWVs, a strong association between UGI symptoms and GWI, and evidence of GI specialty care in accordance with need as indicated by UGI symptom burden. The link between GWI and UGI symptoms and the evaluation and management of these symptoms in GWVs should be further examined to characterise the underlying mechanisms of disease and enhance care.
