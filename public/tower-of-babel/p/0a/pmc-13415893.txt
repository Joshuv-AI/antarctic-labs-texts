Catalysis does not explain expansin-mediated wall creep: Distinguishing catalytic ERPs from creep-active expansins
Authors: Daniel J. Cosgrove
Published: 2026

Delgado Santamaría et al. (1) report low but detectable xylan cleavage after 48-h treatments with high concentrations of GtEXPN_133317, a fungal single-domain ERP (“expansin-related protein”). They suggest that this finding may illuminate the mechanism by which expansins rapidly (<1 min) promote sustained wall creep, a process central to plant growth and potentially related to biomass accessibility. Their biochemical observations of lytic activity are well supported by mutagenesis, but their speculations blur important distinctions between canonical two-domain expansins that induce plant wall creep and single-domain ERPs whose wall creep activities remain unproven.

Active expansins characterized to date share two empirical features (2). Structurally, they consist of a double-psi beta-barrel (DPBB) domain (D1) tightly packed to a C-terminal carbohydrate-binding domain (D2) (3, 4), creating a long, open glycan-binding surface (Fig. 1). This rigid configuration differs from the loose tethering of carbohydrate-binding domains to some enzymes, e.g., cellobiohydrolase-I/Cel7. Functionally, expansins rapidly (within seconds to minutes) induce plant cell wall creep in extensometer assays, without chemical or mechanical evidence of wall lytic activity (2, 5). Numerous biochemical investigations of creep-active expansins from plants, bacteria, and fungi have not detected wall lysis, even using very sensitive assays. Single-domain DPBB proteins such as GtEXPN_133317 fall within various ERP groupings based on sequence similarity and phylogeny, which do not establish functional equivalence. The ancient DPBB fold underlies many ERP families including glycoside hydrolase family-45 (GH45) (see cd22191). Despite robust lytic activity, GH45s did not induce wall creep (4). Studies using complex mixtures of wall-digesting enzymes reach similar conclusions, indicating that lysis per se is insufficient to induce expansin-like creep (2, 5). These findings suggest that wall creep requires coordinated action of both expansin domains rather than isolated catalytic chemistry.

This interpretation is reinforced by studies of the Bacillus subtilis expansin EXLX1 (6). The intact two-domain protein induced wall creep in extensometers, whereas individual D1 or D2 domains did not. Although the D1 domain retains the conserved ASP that serves as catalytic acid in GH45 enzymes and potentially GtEXPN_133317, in EXLX1 this ASP may promote glucan twisting without bond cleavage (9). Coordinated D1–D2 actions may initiate structural dislocations within cellulose networks, enabling sliding of cellulose microfibrils and wall creep without lysis (10).

Other ERPs—including swollenin, cerato-platanin, and loosenin—can disrupt cotton fibers or weaken filter paper, yet lack creep activity (Table 1) (4, 7, 8). It is noteworthy that GtEXPN_133317 is closely related to Phanerochaete loosenin-like12 (1), which weakens filter paper (though not as effectively as BsEXLX1) yet lacks creep activity in extensometer assays (7). Similar characterization of the actions of GtEXPN_133317 and its mutants would clarify the biomechanical significance of its lytic activity.

The analysis of GtEXPN_133317 broadens our understanding of functional diversity among DPBB-containing proteins, but implications regarding expansin-mediated creep remain unsubstantiated. Based on current evidence, GtEXPN_133317 is best viewed as a weakly catalytic DPBB protein whose relevance to expansin-mediated creep awaits direct experimental tests.
