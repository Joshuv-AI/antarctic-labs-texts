Endothelial ferroptosis: a novel mechanism and therapeutic target in diabetic microvascular complications
Authors: Zhongyin Xie, Luxin Li, Yanhui Chu, Yan Wu, Yanan Cao, Lijiao Jin, Haiying Shu, Zhen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetic complications, which are grave sequelae of diabetes, are the primary cause of mortality among diabetic individuals. Effective prevention and management of these complications are essential for reducing mortality rates. Controlling blood glucose levels is a fundamental aspect of treatment, yet it is fraught with challenges. The etiology of diabetic complications is multifactorial, encompassing hyperglycemia, inflammation, and endothelial cell dysfunction. Ferroptosis, a novel form of regulated cell death characterized by heightened lipid peroxidation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, has been implicated in endothelial cell injury and dysfunction. Inhibition of ferroptosis has been shown to mitigate endothelial cell dysfunction, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic target for diabetic complications. This article comprehensively reviews the mechanism of ferroptosis, the targeting regulation of endothelial cells, the interaction between endothelial cells and parenchymal cells, and the relationship between diabetes complications and endothelial cell injury. Through an in-depth examination of the nexus between endothelial ferroptosis and diabetic complications, we aim to offer innovative insights and perspectives for advancing treatment strategies.

Introduction

Diabetes, a pervasive metabolic disorder, is characterized by hyperglycemia resulting from inadequate insulin production or insulin resistance. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), approximately 537 million adults (20–79 years) were living with diabetes in 2021. Projections indicate this number will rise to 643 million (11.3%) by 2030 and 783 million (12.2%) by 2045 (Sun et al. 2022). The prevalence of diabetes is on an upward trajectory, with its complications posing a significant risk to health (Born et al. 2016; Shi and Vanhoutte 2017), particularly those involving macrovascular and microvascular systems. Complications such as diabetic cardiomyopathy (Jia et al. 2018), diabetic nephropathy (Thomas et al. 2015), diabetic retinopathy (Duh et al. 2017), diabetic neuropathy (Feldman et al. 2019), and diabetic cerebrovascular disease (Abdul et al. 2021), seriously affect patients' health and quality of life.

Endothelial cells serve as the frontline guardians within the vascular lumen, forming a critical barrier between the blood and vascular smooth muscle cells. They play a pivotal role in maintaining vascular homeostasis, and their dysfunction contributes to the pathogenesis of vascular diseases. These cells, which are a monolayer of flat epithelial cells, possess specialized functions such as regulating blood vessel tone, modulating immune responses, and maintaining the equilibrium between coagulation and anticoagulation (Godo and Shimokawa 2017). Exposure to a hyperglycemic environment can inflict damage on endothelial cells, triggering alterations in their structure and function. Such changes can disrupt vascular homeostasis, predisposing individuals to the development of both macrovascular and microvascular complications associated with diabetes (Love et al. 2021; Liu 2013).

Recent research has illuminated that ferroptosis within endothelial cells is a significant contributor to endothelial cell injury and endothelial dysfunction (ED) (Zhang et al. 2022). Characterized by lipid peroxidation driven by disruptions in intracellular metabolic pathways, ferroptosis is intricately linked to iron metabolism and lipid homeostasis (Chen et al. 2021). Evidence suggests that the inhibition of ferroptosis may attenuate the incidence of ED. This paper presents a comprehensive review of the pivotal mechanisms underlying ferroptosis, delineates methods for assessing endothelial cell functionality, and outlines targeted intervention strategies. It further provides a detailed account of certain diseases associated with diabetic complications, highlighting the pivotal role of endothelial cell injury and ferroptosis in their pathogenesis. Additionally, it synthesizes the relevant pathways and therapeutic targets from the current literature for managing diabetic complications.

Overview of ferroptosis

Ferroptosis was initially observed by Dixon in 2003, coinciding with Dolma and colleagues' discovery that erastin could selectively target RAS-mutated oncogenes in human foreskin fibroblasts, specifically BJeLR cells (Dolma et al. 2003). Subsequently, in 2008, the novel compounds RSL3 (Ras-selective lethal small molecule) and RSL-5 were identified, which were found to selectively induce cell death in BJeLR cells through a non-apoptotic mechanism (Yagoda et al. 2007). The term 'ferroptosis' was formally coined by Dixon and colleagues in 2012 to describe this unique cell death pattern (Dixon et al. 2012). Iron metabolism

Iron is an essential trace element crucial for sustaining life in organisms and plays a vital role in the body's normal physiological metabolism (Li et al. 2023a). It is absorbed by the small intestine as Fe2+, oxidized to Fe3+ by ceruloplasmin, and then bound to transferrin (TF) and transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1) on the cell membrane. Subsequently, it is reduced back to Fe2+ by the six-transmembrane epithelial antigen of the prostate 3 (STEAP3) (Frazer and Anderson 2014). During this process, a portion of iron enters the labile iron pool (LIP) in the cytoplasm, regulated by divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1). Concurrently, some iron is sequestered in ferritin (FT), composed of ferritin subunits (FTH1/FTL). Additionally, ferritin mediates its own degradation through autophagy, facilitated by the nuclear receptor coactivator 4 (NCOA4), thereby promoting the accumulation of Fe2+ (Tang and Kroemer 2020). Excess Fe2+ can then reduce hydrogen peroxide to produce hydroxyl radicals and catalyze the formation of ROS and lipid peroxides via the Fenton reaction, culminating in the induction of ferroptosis (Feng et al. 2022) (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Iron metabolism mechanism. After binding with TF, non-heme iron is internalized into cells through TFR1 and reduced to Fe2⁺ by the metal reductase STEAP3 in the endosome. Iron ions are subsequently released into the cytosol through DMT1 or transient receptor potential mucin 1 (TRPML1). In addition, non-transferrin-bound iron can be directly ingested into cells through the metal transporter SLC39A14. Heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) catalyzes heme degradation, leading to iron release. Ferritin releases a large amount of iron through ferritin autophagy mediated by NCOA4. At the same time, hepcidin reduces iron output from cells by inhibiting the membrane iron transporter protein (FPN). These processes collectively lead to an increase in LIP, which promotes the generation of ROS through the Fenton reaction, thereby increasing the sensitivity of cells to ferroptosis. Lipid peroxidation

Lipid peroxidation, the oxidative degradation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), proceeds via both enzymatic and non-enzymatic mechanisms (Feng et al. 2023). Mitochondria not only generate energy through the electron transfer chain (ETC) and tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA), but also provide a key raw material for fatty acid synthesis, acetyl-CoA. In the fatty acid synthesis pathway, acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) serves as a catalytic key step. Concurrently, lipophagy releases fatty acids from lipid stores, contributing to the accumulation of free fatty acids within cells. These accumulated fatty acids include PUFAs-arachidonic acid (AA) and adrenic acid (AdA), which are key participants in the lipid peroxidation process (Yan et al. 2021). Focusing on AA, it undergoes catalysis by acetyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) to bind with coenzyme A (CoA), yielding AA-CoA intermediates. Subsequently, these intermediates are esterified by lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3) to phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), culminating in the formation of PE-AA (Kagan et al. 2017). PE-AA primarily relies on the enzymatic activity of lipoxygenases (ALOXs), yet it can also engage in non-enzymatic pathways, such as the Fenton reaction, to produce hydroxyl and peroxygen free radicals. These radicals catalyze the formation of lipid hydroperoxides (PE-AA-OOH), thereby facilitating the onset of ferroptosis (Costa et al. 2023) (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Lipid peroxidation mechanism. The accumulation of intracellular free fatty acids is driven by two key processes: fatty acid synthesis catalyzed by acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) and fatty acid release from lipid stores mediated by lipophagy. Among the accumulated fatty acids, polyunsaturated fatty acids (AA/AdA) are esterified by long-chain fatty acid coenzyme A ligase 4 (ACSL4) and lysophosphatidylcholine acyltransferase 3 (LPCAT3), and then incorporated into phospholipids to form polyunsaturated fatty acid phospholipids (PE-AA/PE-AdA). PE-AA/PE-AdA is highly susceptible to oxidation by lipoxygenases (ALOXs), producing reactive free radicals. System Xc−/Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) axis

Cystine/glutamate reverse transporter (xCT) is an amino acid exchanger localized within the phospholipid bilayer (Li et al. 2020a). It is integral to the cell's antioxidant defenses, existing as a heterodimer consisting of the light chain subunit SLC7A11 and the heavy chain subunit SLC3A2 (Yan et al. 2021). xCT facilitates the 1:1 exchange of cystine for glutamate across the cell membrane and is capable of reducing cystine to cysteine, which is instrumental in glutathione (GSH) synthesis (Dixon et al. 2012). Inhibition of xCT activity impedes this exchange, potentially disrupting GSH production and leading to an accumulation of ROS and the induction of ferroptosis (Su et al. 2020).

GPX4 is a selenoprotein with an approximate molecular weight of 20–21 kDa, comprising approximately 197 amino acids (Reichert et al. 2020). It is a critical player in ferroptosis, functioning to convert GSH to its oxidized glutathione (GSSG) and to reduce toxic lipid hydroperoxides (PL-OOH) to their corresponding alcohols (PL-OH) (Li et al. 2020a). GSSG can be regenerated into GSH using the reducing power provided by NAD(P)H under the catalysis of glutathione reductase (GSR). FSP1/CoQ10/BH4/GCH1 system

Ferroptosis suppressor protein-1 (FSP1), also known as AIFM2, is an emerging regulator in ferroptosis research. Unlike the traditional GPX4 dependent pathway, FSP1 operates as an autonomous system (Doll et al. 2019; Bersuker et al. 2019). It has been demonstrated that FSP1 catalyzes the reduction of CoQ10 using NAD(P)H as a cofactor, mitigating lipid peroxidation through the reduction of CoQ10 and vitamin K, thereby effectively countering ferroptosis (Doll et al. 2019; Mishima et al. 2022). From a pharmacological standpoint, dual targeting of FSP1 and GPX4 presents a strategy to effectively induce ferroptosis in cancer cells by completely abolishing phospholipid peroxidation defense (Doll et al. 2019). Furthermore, GCH1 is recognized as a potential inhibitor of ferroptosis and a rate-limiting enzyme in the biosynthesis of BH4. BH4, an efficacious antioxidant capable of trapping free radicals, necessitates the involvement of DHFR for its regeneration. Ferroptosis inducers and inhibitors

Conversely, in the context of diabetes, the induction of ferroptosis may exert detrimental effects on target organs. Consequently, the investigation and development of ferroptosis inducers and inhibitors have emerged as a focal point in contemporary research endeavors. Overview of endothelial cells

Endothelial cell structure, function, and metabolism

Endothelial cells (ECs) form a squamous layer that lines the luminal surface of blood and lymphatic vessels. Although ECs form a continuous monolayer lining the vascular endothelium, the endothelial glycocalyx (EG) envelops the EC surface, playing an essential role in preserving vascular integrity and homeostasis (Becker et al. 2010; Hennigs et al. 2021). Under physiological conditions, ECs primarily serve as a semipermeable barrier, maintaining the body's material equilibrium and shielding against invasion and infection by diverse pathogens (Krüger-Genge et al. 2019). Moreover, they are implicated in the immune response of inflamed tissues by modulating blood flow and orchestrating the production and release of inflammatory mediators (Hennigs et al. 2021). Ultimately, ECs are capable of secreting an array of physiologically active substances to mediate signaling processes, such as nitric oxide (NO), a key vasodilatory and antiproliferative signaling molecule (Suvorava et al. 2022), prostacyclin, which facilitates vasodilation and prevents thrombosis (Peshavariya et al. 2014), and the vasoconstrictive agents endothelin-1 (ET-1) and thromboxane A2 (TXA2) (Avogaro et al. 2011; Yanagisawa et al. 1988).

As an integral endocrine organ, the endothelium possesses significant metabolic capabilities (Ding et al. 2021). Approximately 85% of energy metabolism is attributed to the process of glycolysis (Bock et al. 2013). Nevertheless, ATP synthesis can also be facilitated through alternative metabolic pathways, encompassing glucose, fatty acids, amino acids, and glutamine metabolism. However, the onset of disease can disrupt the functional signaling pathways and metabolic processes of ECs, leading to ED. This dysfunction can activate pro-inflammatory and pro-coagulant responses, augment ROS production, and diminish NO bioavailability, thereby laying the groundwork for macrovascular and microvascular diseases (Ali 2020). Additionally, ROS can induce programmed cell death in ECs, including ferroptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis (Zheng et al. 2022). Excessive iron can increase ROS levels, curtail NO synthesis, intensify inflammation, and inflict EC injury. Subsequent research has substantiated that EC injury and demise are intricately linked to the pathogenesis and progression of a spectrum of diseases, such as atherosclerosis (Gimbrone and García-Cardeña 2016), diabetes (Zhang et al. 2016), cerebrovascular diseases (Hu et al. 2017), renal impairment (Yung and Chan 2023), and Alzheimer's disease (Yamazaki and Kanekiyo 2017). Endothelial cell-parenchymal cell interaction drives organ damage

In the pathological environment of diabetes, EC damage directly drives multiple organ damage by destroying the steady-state interaction network with their vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), pericytes (PCs) and podocytes. The intercellular communication between ECs and VSMCs is crucial for maintaining vascular homeostasis (Méndez-Barbero et al. 2021). This communication is achieved through multiple mechanisms. For instance, platelet-derived growth factor B (PDGF-B) secreted by EC forms a concentration gradient, and surrounding smooth muscle precursor cells sense this gradient through their surface tyrosine kinase receptor (PDGFR-β), thereby being recruited, promoting migration and proliferation, and ultimately completing the assembly of the vascular wall (Lilly 2014); at the same time, ECs dynamically regulate the contractile state of VSMCs by releasing NO vasodilator and contraction factors such as endothelin (ET) and angiotensin II (Ang II) (Triggle et al. 2012). However, disturbance in the communication between ECs and VSMCs leads to EC dysfunction, manifested as a decrease in NO bioavailability or an increase in the release of vasoactive contractile substances, thereby causing abnormal increase in vascular tone (Hirase and Node 2012). This abnormal tension environment and dysregulated signals promote the transition of VSMCs from a resting contractile phenotype to an activated state, characterized by abnormal proliferation and migration. These key events caused by ECs-VSMCs communication disorders constitute the core pathological basis for the development of vascular diseases such as hypertension and atherosclerosis (Tabas 2017; Wu et al. 2021).

The close interaction and signaling crosstalk between ECs and PCs are crucial for ensuring the normal development, stability, and barrier function of the vascular system (Li et al. 2025). Pericytes tightly wrap around microvascular endothelial cells, providing critical structural support, regulating contraction, and participating in maintaining vascular permeability (Li and Fan 2023). ECs recruit, encapsulate, and maintain pericytes by releasing a series of signaling factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), platelet-derived growth factor-B (PDGF-B) and Notch signaling pathway, jointly promoting vascular maturation and stability. However, in the pathological environment of high blood sugar, this balance is significantly disrupted. The signal secretion pattern of ECs undergoes abnormal changes, with reduced PDGF-B secretion, increased VEGF levels, and abnormal activation of the Notch signaling pathway (Yang and Liu 2022). More importantly, abnormal Notch signaling and elevated VEGF signaling cause harmful crosstalk in retinal blood vessels, further leading to EC dysfunction (Li et al. 2020b). The pathological changes in the secretory profile of ECs gradually weaken the tight and functional connection between ECs and PCs, ultimately leading to PC migration and significant loss. Once a large number of PCs are lost, the integrity of blood vessels will be severely damaged, manifested as retinal microvascular leakage and pathological neovascularization (Ren et al. 2022).

The crosstalk between glomerular endothelial cells(GECs) and podocytes is crucial for maintaining the integrity of the glomerular filtration barrier (Wu et al. 2017). Under pathological conditions, this crosstalk imbalance can affect each other's functions. For example, high glucose stimulation can overactivate NAD(P)H oxidase in GECs, leading to excessive production of ROS (Love et al. 2021). These ROS not only directly damage the window structure of GECs themselves, but also impair podocyte function through paracrine signaling. Specifically, the deficiency of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) in ECs has been shown to directly lead to podocyte injury and albuminuria (Yuen et al. 2012). Conversely, endothelin-1 released by podocytes also acts on ECs, triggering mitochondrial oxidative stress and exacerbating podocyte loss and glomerulosclerosis (Daehn et al. 2014). Meanwhile, the secretion of vascular endothelial growth factor-A (VEGF-A) and angiopoietin 1 (ANG1) by podocytes are key signals for maintaining endothelial signaling survival. The absence of VEGF-A or ANG1 significantly increases ECs damage, cell death, and proteinuria (Sison et al. 2010; Jeansson et al. 2011). Therefore, the loss of podocytes will further exacerbate GECs dysfunction, forming a vicious cycle of "EC window damage-podocyte loss-VEGF-A signaling disorder-endothelial injury exacerbation". This cycle ultimately destroys the integrity of the glomerular filtration barrier, leading to a large amount of proteinuria (Gil et al. 2021). Diabetes amplifies vascular and organ damage through a cascade of disrupted interactions between ECs and VSMCs, PCs, and podocytes. This hierarchical crosstalk highlights the central role of ECs as intercellular signaling hubs in diabetic pathology.

Endothelial cell targeting intervention strategy

Given their pivotal role in vascular function, ECs are regarded as a crucial target for therapeutic interventions in vascular pathologies. These cells possess a vast surface area in contact with blood, serving as the primary barrier and critical interface between blood and surrounding tissues (Hamilton et al. 2004). Given their functional attributes and strategic positioning, ECs are integral to the pathogenesis of numerous diseases and represent a central therapeutic target for conditions such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, hypertension, and tumor growth (Sakurai et al. 2019).

Drug delivery systems (DDS) are engineered to facilitate targeted drug delivery to ECs, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy, safety, and stability (Shuvaev et al. 2015). To attain precise and secure targeting, active targeting strategies often employ specific affinity ligands, which involve the conjugation of DDS with a ligand that selectively binds to ECs (Schnitzer 1998). In contrast to affinity targeting, non-affinity targeting is predominantly achieved without specific molecular interactions, relying on passive diffusion mechanisms. In addition, non-affinity targeting lacks selectivity, exerts minimal control over cellular localization, and poses a higher risk of inducing adverse reactions (Farshbaf et al. 2018). Evaluating EC function is increasingly acknowledged as an invaluable asset in clinical settings, serving as a predictive biomarker for the early detection of cardiovascular complications. FMD

Flow-mediated dilation (FMD) is a non-invasive technique that assesses endothelial function by augmenting arterial blood flow, thereby inducing vasodilation through the action of shear stress, which in turn stimulates the release of NO by ECs (Boo et al. 2002). Specifically, brachial FMD involves the occlusion of the brachial artery using a sphygmomanometer cuff, leading to reactive hyperemia after a 5-min interval. During this phase, the brachial artery's response to the release of endothelial-derived NO is measured (Flammer et al. 2012). This approach is the most widely utilized method for evaluating endothelial function due to its non-invasive nature.

PAT

Peripheral Arterial Tonometry (PAT), as a technique for assessing endothelial function, shares a fundamental principle with FMD. This method primarily assesses the level of microvascular reactive hyperemia by measuring the changes in the amplitude of finger pulse waves, providing an index of endothelial function (Kuvin et al. 2003). PAT offers more consistent and reproducible results than brachial artery FMD. Research has shown that PAT performs better in predicting the incidence of cardiovascular events, even surpassing the traditional Framingham Risk Score (FRS) that evaluates 10-year coronary heart disease risk based on multiple risk factors such as age, gender, blood lipids, blood pressure, and smoking. This highlights the potential of PAT in widespread clinical applications (Rubinshtein et al. 2010).

ezFMD

The endothelial function assessment technique known as easy Flow-Mediated Dilation (ezFMD) is an innovative, non-invasive approach designed to evaluate the vascular response of the brachial artery to shear stress-induced hyperemia. Research has demonstrated that ezFMD correlates with a variety of cardiovascular risk factors, including age, body mass index, blood pressure, and smoking behavior, suggesting its utility as an independent prognostic indicator (Idei et al. 2013). Unlike traditional FMD, which assesses endothelial function based on alterations in arterial diameter, ezFMD measures changes in vascular volume. Additionally, ezFMD offers a more straightforward and less subjective assessment method compared to FMD.

Evaluation of coronary endothelial function

In individuals with coronary artery disease (CAD), acetylcholine is administered via the coronary arteries, and the consequent changes in coronary artery diameter are monitored as a response to this agent. Typically, a normal endothelium responds to acetylcholine with a mild vasodilatory effect. In contrast, patients with compromised endothelial function may exhibit an atypical response, which can include vasodilation (Ludmer et al. 1986). Despite its diagnostic value, this procedure is invasive and carries significant costs, thereby restricting its routine clinical use. It is generally reserved for patients who are candidates for coronary angiography.

Biomarkers of endothelial dysfunction

Assessing plasma levels of endothelial-associated biomarkers can provide insights into the activation status and functional integrity of ECs (Mocco et al. 2001). Prominent among these endothelial markers are NO, intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), and von Willebrand factor (vWF). Moreover, these biomarkers hold substantial clinical significance, particularly in monitoring the progression of atherosclerosis in patients over a defined time frame (Fernández-Friera et al. 2014).

Ferroptosis of endothelial cells in diabetic complications

Research has consistently demonstrated that hyperglycemia induces a spectrum of cellular and tissue alterations, including mitochondrial dysfunction, accelerated vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation, foam cell formation and infiltration, and increased cell death (Dogné et al. 2018; Kolka and Bergman 2013). Notably, ECs exhibit heightened susceptibility to injury compared to other cell types, predisposing them to oxidative stress and metabolic dysregulation. This vulnerability manifests as an overabundance of ROS, the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs), and elevated levels of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (ox-LDL) (Knapp et al. 2019; Katakami 2018). Furthermore, the pathophysiological cascade in a high-glucose milieu escalates the permeability of the myocardial endothelial layer, inflicts damage upon glomerular, retinal, and cerebral microvascular endothelial cells, culminating in EC dysfunction. Subsequent discussions will concentrate on the role of endothelial ferroptosis in the context of diabetic cardiovascular disease, diabetic nephropathy, diabetic retinopathy, diabetic wounds, and diabetic cerebrovascular disease.

Diabetic cardiovascular disease

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) stands as the predominant cause of mortality among diabetic patients, with diabetes firmly established as a cardinal risk factor for CVD (Glovaci et al. 2019). Diabetic cardiomyopathy (DC), a specific type of CVD, is distinguished by a spectrum of myocardial structural and functional alterations that are not attributed to coronary artery disease or hypertension (Jia et al. 2018; Ke et al. 2023). Early in the disease's progression, myocardial fibrosis and incipient diastolic dysfunction are observed, while advanced stages are characterized by systolic dysfunction, reduced ejection fraction (EF), and extensive, irreversible myocardial necrosis, leading to heart failure (HF) (Maya and Villarreal 2010). The pathogenesis of DC is multifactorial, involving a variety of pathways and signaling mechanisms, primarily encompassing hyperglycemia, endothelial dysfunction, mitochondrial impairment, ROS overproduction, insulin resistance, and microvascular anomalies (Dhalla et al. 2020; Qiu et al. 2022; Ritchie and Abel 2020). There is a wealth of evidence supporting the notion that ED serves as a harbinger of cardiovascular disease.

In diabetic cardiovascular diseases, the activation and subsequent damage of ECs play a pivotal role, leading to the secretion of a multitude of cytokines through inflammatory pathways. These cytokines include tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), IL-10, IL-1β, and fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) (Prabhu and Frangogiannis 2016; Ge et al. 2021). The release of these cytokines exacerbates the cardiac fibrosis process. Concurrently, ECs generate pro-fibrotic mediators such as ET-1 and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), which contribute to the progression of cardiac fibrosis and heart failure (Yang et al. 2023). Consequently, inhibiting EC damage is paramount for curbing the onset and progression of cardiovascular diseases.

A multitude of studies have substantiated the pivotal role of ferroptosis in the etiology of cardiovascular diseases, encompassing cardiomyopathy, atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, and heart failure (Zhang et al. 2023a). Moreover, the inhibition of ferroptosis has been demonstrated to ameliorate ED, indicating that it may represent a promising target for the preservation of ECs integrity (Zhang et al. 2022; Bai et al. 2020). Meng et al. have illustrated that ferroptosis is instrumental in the progression of diabetic atherosclerosis, with heme oxygenase-1 (HMOX1) emerging as a critical catalyst for the escalation of ferroptosis. Further studies have revealed that diabetes accelerates HMOX1 upregulation and ferroptosis. Notably, the suppression of HMOX1 mitigates iron surplus and ROS levels, curtailing lipid peroxidation and consequently diminishing ferroptosis in the ECs of diabetic patients (Meng et al. 2021). Thus, strategies targeting the inhibition of HMOX1 overexpression or employing the ferroptosis inhibitor Ferrostatin 1 (Fer-1) could present efficacious therapeutic avenues for diabetic atherosclerosis. Isoflavone (ISO) has attracted significant attention for its antioxidant and cardioprotective properties. Research by Chen et al. indicates that ISO administration and overexpression of peroxiredoxin 2 (PRDX2) can impede ferroptosis in ECs. This action subsequently attenuates diabetic cardiac microvascular dysfunction and rectifies cardiac structural and functional irregularities (Chen et al. 2023). This research corroborates the significant role of endothelial ferroptosis in the progression of diabetic cardiomyopathy (DCM). Furthermore, the overexpression of mitofusin 2 (MFN2) exerts an inhibitory effect on the mitochondrial translocation of acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), thereby curbing mitochondria-associated ferroptosis. In summary, the study affirms that ISO treatment inhibits the mitochondrial translocation of ACSL4 through the PRDX2-MFN2 pathway, thereby safeguarding cardiac microvasculature and averting ferroptosis. Li and Xu, among other scholars, have reported that certain natural products could be instrumental in addressing cardiovascular diseases and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) (Xu et al. 2017; Li et al. 2019). Subsequent studies have shown that hydroxylsafflower yellow A (HSYA) can markedly decelerate the progression of atherosclerosis incited by T2DM and potently inhibit ferroptosis in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVEC) (Rong et al. 2023). Collectively, the research substantiates the protective efficacy of HSYA against ferroptosis in ECs and strengthens the evidence base for HSYA's therapeutic potential in treating atherosclerosis induced by T2DM through the inhibition of ferroptosis.

Diabetic nephropathy

Diabetic nephropathy (DN) stands as a principal microvascular complication of diabetes and is a predominant catalyst for end-stage renal disease (ESRD) (Umanath and Lewis 2018; Kosiborod et al. 2018). Clinically, DN is characterized by the progressive onset of proteinuria and declining renal function; pathologically, it is marked by glomerular basement membrane thickening, glomerulosclerosis, and tubular interstitial fibrosis (Rayego-Mateos et al. 2020; Anders et al. 2018). The prevalence of DN is increasing, with hypertension and hyperglycemia identified as the most significant risk factors (Alicic et al. 2017). The emergence of hypertension can exacerbate the deterioration of renal function, potentially culminating in renal failure, commonly referred to as uremia (Paul et al. 2023). The increasing prevalence of insulin resistance in patients leads to disruptions in glucose and lipid metabolism and impaired glucose tolerance. In advanced stages, patients often require dialysis for survival, significantly compromising their quality of life. The etiology and pathogenesis of DN are still not fully understood, but are believed to be primarily associated with metabolic dysregulation, heightened oxidative stress, and inflammatory activation (Zhang et al. 2023b; Jiménez-Castilla et al. 2021).

A growing body of research underscores the pivotal role of GECs injury in the genesis and advancement of DN (Fu et al. 2015). Pioneering studies have identified that GECs injury is instrumental in the development of microalbuminuria, with subsequent investigations revealing that GECs may further the progression of DN through their interactive dynamics with podocytes (Fu et al. 2015). Moreover, DN animal models have substantiated that eNOS deficiency exacerbates glomerular and podocyte injury, with endothelial abnormalities exhibiting a closer correlation to elevated urinary albumin excretion compared to podocyte injury (Yuen et al. 2012; Zhao et al. 2006). Notably, Qi and Wang et al. demonstrated that increased mtDNA oxidative stress and mitochondrial damage are exclusively present in GECs (Qi et al. 2017; Wang et al. 2023). They also detected oxidized lesions in GECs from renal biopsies and urine of patients with progressive diabetic kidney disease, but not in non-progressors. These findings collectively imply that ECs injury, particularly through increased mitochondrial ROS in the kidney microvasculature, occupies a central role in the development of albuminuria and is key for disease progression among individuals with susceptibility/risks for developing diabetic complications such as diabetic nephropathy. Thus, these studies further reinforce the significance of GECs in the glomerular alterations observed in DN patients (Weil et al. 2012).

In recent years, an emerging body of research has highlighted the significant role of ferroptosis in the progression of DN, suggesting its potential as a novel therapeutic target (Li et al. 2021a; Wang et al. 2020). Tan and colleagues employed network pharmacology to identify the bioactive constituent in licorice, specifically Glabridin (Glab), and its associated targets (Tan et al. 2022). Subsequent in vivo experiments corroborated that the diminished expression of SLC7A11 and SLC3A2 in the kidneys of diabetic rats' NRK-52E cells signifies the onset of ferroptosis during DN. This downregulation is implicated in the disruption of glutathione (GSH) synthesis, the inhibition of GPX4 activity, and the consequent reduction in cellular antioxidant defenses, leading to the accumulation of ROS and oxidative damage to renal endothelial cells. This sequence of events is proposed to foster the progression of DN by impairing the cysteine uptake mediated by the system Xc− (Zhang and Li 2022). In contrast, Glab administration has been observed to effectively mitigate ferroptosis by normalizing the expression levels of SLC7A11 and SLC3A2. These findings underscore the potential of Glab as a countermeasure against oxidative stress and ferroptosis in the context of DN.

Diabetic retinopathy

In developed countries, diabetic retinopathy (DR) is recognized as the most prevalent and severe ocular complication of diabetes (Cheung et al. 2010). It primarily arises from the detrimental effects of sustained hyperglycemia on ocular blood vessels. DR is clinically categorized into two phases: the initial non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) and the advanced proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR). NPDR is predominantly marked by a reduction in PCs within retinal capillaries, which alters vascular permeability, precipitates macular edema, and results in blurred vision, further exacerbating the disruption of the blood-retina barrier (Gui et al. 2020). In contrast, PDR is characterized by the proliferation of new, fragile blood vessels on the retinal surface, posing a risk for vitreous hemorrhage and retinal detachment, which can lead to in vision loss (Miller and Fortun 2018). DR not only impairs visual acuity but also increases the risk of other systemic vascular complications, some of which may be life-threatening (Yang and Liu 2022).

ED is a pivotal factor in the pathogenesis of DR, with several confirmed contributors, including AGEs, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPARγ), inflammation, and oxidative stress (Roy et al. 2017). AGEs, which accumulate as a consequence of prolonged hyperglycemia, contribute to the initiation and progression of DR (Xu et al. 2018). Accumulation of AGEs in the retinal vasculature has been shown to be detrimental to EC permeability, leading to vascular leakage (Roy et al. 2017). The role of PPARγ involves upregulating eNOS activity, thereby reducing oxidative stress, curbing inflammation and apoptosis, and effectively mitigating diabetic-induced retinal leukostasis and microvascular leakage (Chhabra and Sharma 2019). Collectively, these findings underscore the critical function of retinal endothelial cells in the evolution of DR (Zhang et al. 2021).

Zhang and his colleagues have made a groundbreaking discovery, identifying ferroptosis as the key pathway leading to the death of retinal vascular endothelial cells in high-glucose (HG)-induced DR (Zhang et al. 2021). Their research further establishes that the TRIM46 gene plays a significant role in the dysregulation of retinal capillary endothelial cells (hRCECs) within the context of DR. Notably, the study was the first to recognize that HG triggers the ubiquitination of GPX4, with TRIM46 identified as an upstream regulator of this process. The precise function of TRIM46-mediated GPX4 ubiquitination in DR was elucidated through comprehensive animal model studies, confirming that TRIM46 can effectively suppress HG-induced ferroptosis and the decline of GPX4 in human hRCECs. Additionally, this study revealed that elevated miR-93 expression promotes inflammation and oxidative stress in retinal pigment epithelium, thus hastening the progression of DR (Ahmed et al. 2020). Su et al. discovered that the upregulation of miR-93 is attributable to its activation of the RhoA/ROCK signaling pathway, which in turn amplifies the oxidative stress and inflammatory response (Su et al. 2019). Further studies have indicated that 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 significantly represses miR-93 expression and iron content in HG-induced cells, while concurrently enhancing GPX4 and SLC7A11 levels. Importantly, the overexpression of miR-93 has been shown to counteract the protective effects of 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 on human retinal microvascular endothelial cells (hRMVECs), primarily by exacerbating oxidative stress and ferroptosis (Zhan et al. 2023). Consequently, 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 has been demonstrated to effectively mitigate ferroptosis and oxidative stress in hRMVECs induced by HG. Extensive evidence has pointed to the upregulation of long non-coding RNA ZFAS1 in human retinal endothelial cells (hRECs) cultured in high-glucose conditions, with the knockdown of ZFAS1 shown to alleviate HG-induced ferroptosis (Liu et al. 2022). Using the starBase database, the research team selected miR-7-5p for subsequent investigation. As reported by Zhuang et al., the elevation of miR-7-5p levels is predominantly due to its regulatory effects on the transferrin receptor, iron uptake, and lipid ROS production in cardiomyocytes, thereby accelerating the ferroptosis process (Zhuang et al. 2023). Moreover, the study accentuates the pivotal role of ACSL4 in DR. The overexpression of ACSL4, induced by HG, is inhibited by ZFAS1, with miR-7-5p partially neutralizing this inhibition. This interplay suggests that the ZFAS1/miR-7-5p/ACSL4 axis may present a promising therapeutic target for DR treatment.

Diabetic wound

Diabetic wounds (DWs) are one of the recognized complications of diabetes, characterized by challenging healing dynamics and a heightened susceptibility to infection and ulceration. Particularly severe are diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs), which predominantly manifest in the lower extremities and, in extreme cases, can necessitate limb amputation (Holl et al. 2021). The consequences of DFUs include significant patient morbidity and mortality, as well as substantial healthcare expenditures and economic losses due to reduced productivity (Jeffcoate et al. 2018). The convoluted healing trajectory of DFUs is impeded by a multitude of factors, such as hyperglycemia, compromised angiogenesis, persistent inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress (Xiong et al. 2023). Among these myriad influences, the pivotal role of ECs stands out as a central element in the wound healing process (Huang et al. 2020; Katayama et al. 2019).

ED in diabetic wounds initially impedes angiogenesis, thereby significantly hindering the wound healing process (Lou et al. 2022). Concurrently, the diminished angiogenesis associated with diabetes expedites the progression of diabetic ulcers, with a particularly pronounced effect on DFUs (Cheng and Ma 2015). In vivo studies have demonstrated that HG exposure compromises the integrity of ECs, augmenting their susceptibility to apoptosis and adversely affecting blood circulation, which in turn precipitates angiogenesis dysfunction (Huang et al. 2020). Additionally, ECs have the capacity to establish a "neurovascular niche" in conjunction with nerve cells. This interaction not only bolsters cellular functionality but also fosters the wound healing process of DWs (Nowak et al. 2021). These findings underscore the indispensable role of ECs in the healing mechanism of DWs.

Research has found that in in vitro models, a high-glucose environment increases the levels of ROS, lipid peroxides, and ferroptosis-related proteins, and reduces cell survival rate; and Fer-1 treatment can effectively reverse these high glucose effects. In the diabetic rat model, Fer-1 treatment significantly accelerated wound healing. These results indicate that inhibiting ferroptosis can reduce ROS levels and promote wound healing in diabetes, suggesting that ferroptosis is a potential new therapeutic target for treating the disease(Li et al. 2021b). Aurora kinase A (AURKA) is an essential serine/threonine kinase for mitosis. Its role in limb ischemia still needs to be studied. Dawei and his colleagues validated that the ablation of AURKA precipitates ROS accumulation and apoptosis, reinforcing the potential antioxidant function of AURKA in diabetic limb ischemia (Dawei et al. 2018). Moreover, investigations have revealed that AURKA activation in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) is associated with the upregulation of hypoxia-induced VEGFA and VEGFR2 expression, modulating EC dynamics (Ki et al. 2020). AURKA's role in diabetes holds promise for stimulating high-glucose metabolism in microvascular endothelial cells, potentially mitigating VEGFA resistance (Yin et al. 2020). The accumulation of ROS, depletion of GSH, and altered expression of ferroptosis-related genes in diabetic limb ischemia provide further evidence implicating ferroptosis. Researchers propose that AURKA may negatively regulate ferroptosis in ECs under diabetic and ischemic conditions. Collectively, these findings highlight AURKA's key regulatory function in diabetes-related post-ischemic angiogenesis, promoting ECs proliferation and enhancing oxidative stress response (Bai et al. 2023). Autophagy has been implicated in vascular formation and the oxidative stress function of ECs (Mizushima and Komatsu 2011). Recent studies have elucidated the role of secretory autophagosomes (SAPs) in diseases such as cancer and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), offering promising avenues for diagnostics and therapeutics (Xu et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2019). Further exploration indicates that SAPs could be harnessed in the treatment of high-glucose-induced skin repair cells like HUVECs. The findings demonstrate that SAPs effectively mitigate free iron accumulation and ROS production while upregulating FTH1 and GPX4 expression levels. This suggests that SAPs can rejuvenate the functionality of endothelial and other skin repair cells in a high-glucose milieu, presenting significant potential for diabetic wound treatment (Cui et al. 2023). The study illustrates that SAP in HUVECs can expedite DW healing by inhibiting ferroptosis, thereby aiding in the restoration of skin repair cell function.

Diabetic cerebrovascular disease

Diabetic cerebrovascular disease represents a spectrum of intracranial macrovascular and microvascular disorders stemming from diabetes mellitus. Its primary clinical presentations include cerebral infarction and cerebral thrombosis, among other ischemic cerebrovascular events. Furthermore, diabetes-induced alterations in coagulation function predispose individuals to a higher risk of cerebral hemorrhage. These complications impose substantial financial and emotional burdens on patients, their families, and society at large (Hostettler et al. 2019). The etiology of diabetic cerebrovascular disease is multifactorial, predominantly instigated through a confluence of pathways such as EC dysfunction, arterial injury, and inflammatory responses (Huang et al. 2022).

Based on current research data, the dysfunction of brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMVECs) is identified as a primary etiology of diabetic cerebrovascular diseases (Chen et al. 2021). This dysfunction involves a disruption in the normal regulatory functions of ECs, potentially leading to abnormal vascular alterations. Moreover, ECs play a critical role in vascular repair and integrity maintenance (Cohen-Boulakia et al. 2007). Damage to these ECs can result in the obstruction of microvessels, leading to the formation of microthrombi (Li et al. 2016). Additionally, the progressive degeneration of BMVECs further heightens the risk of stroke among diabetic individuals (Morohoshi et al. 1996). These insights underscore the pivotal role of ECs in the pathogenesis of diabetic cerebrovascular disease.

Recent studies have brought ferroptosis to the forefront of research in neurological diseases and brain injuries, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target (Derry et al. 2020). Accumulation of iron has been noted in stroke patients, and the administration of ferroptosis inhibitors such as Fer-1 and desferrioxamine in mice with cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury has demonstrated significant neuroprotective effects (Tuo et al. 2017). Cheng and his team discovered that silencing the p53 gene could effectively shield BMVECs from ferroptosis induced by oxygen–glucose deprivation (OGD) and hyperglycemic reperfusion. Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), particularly the maternally expressed gene 3 (Meg3), are considered pivotal biomarkers in diabetes research (Tong et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2019). Inhibition of Meg3 has been proposed to confer robust neuroprotection against cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury in both in vivo and in vitro models (Sommerkamp et al. 2019). Furthermore, Meg3 knockout has been shown to protect BMVECs from OGD and HG treatment-induced harm. The interplay between Meg3 and p53 is intriguing; overexpression of p53 leads to a significant decrease in GPX4 gene expression, while p53 depletion results in increased expression. This suggests that Meg3 modulates ferroptosis in BMVECs induced by OGD and HG through p53-mediated regulation of GPX4 transcription (Chen et al. 2021), thereby paving the way for novel therapeutic strategies in diabetic cerebral ischemia. Cefopurine (CEP) is an alkaloid with anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and antiviral properties, and has been shown to be neuroprotective. (Bailly 2019). 15-Lipoxygenase-1 (ALOX15) is a key enzyme that catalyzes phospholipid oxidation, and its activity is closely related to various central nervous system diseases. The aim of this study was to elucidate the specific mechanism of action of ALOX15 in subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). Previous studies have shown that CEP can downregulate the expression of ALOX15, alleviate oxidative stress and inflammatory response after cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury (Zhao et al. 2020). The in vitro and in vivo experiments of this study further revealed that ALOX15 expression was upregulated after SAH, promoting ferroptosis in ECs, which may lead to vascular damage and bleeding. Mechanistically, CEP has been shown to effectively inhibit ROS accumulation within ECs and reduce ALOX15 expression levels. Therefore, the results indicate that CEP alleviates neurological deficits after SAH by inhibiting the ALOX15 mediated EC ferroptosis pathway. (Gao et al. 2022).

Conclusions and future prospects

This article provides an in-depth exploration of the mechanisms underlying ferroptosis, alongside the fundamental functions, metabolic processes, interactions with other cells, assessment methodologies, and targeted intervention strategies of ECs. Subsequently, we focus on the complex interactions between ECs and the spectrum of diabetes-related complications. We conduct a comprehensive review of the role of endothelial ferroptosis in diabetic cardiovascular disease, diabetic retinopathy, diabetic nephropathy, wound healing in diabetes, and diabetic cerebrovascular disease, probing for potential therapeutic targets. However, the majority of existing research concentrates on the impact of modulating ferroptosis on disease onset and progression, while few studies have examined the specific nexus between endothelial ferroptosis and diabetic complications. Therefore, this paper summarizes therapeutic strategies targeting ferroptosis modulation in ECs within the context of diabetic complications.

To date, a growing body of evidence underscores the significant roles of both ferroptosis and ECs in the progression of diabetic complications (Meng et al. 2021; Fu et al. 2015). Ferroptosis, a distinct mode of regulated cell death, is characterized by the substantial accumulation of lipid peroxides (Sun et al. 2023; Li et al. 2023b). Emerging research identifies ferroptosis as a promising therapeutic target for the management of diabetic complications (Li et al. 2021b). Notably, certain ferroptosis inhibitors, natural compounds, and long non-coding RNAs have demonstrated efficacy in mitigating ferroptosis, thus offering protection to ECs against its detrimental effects. Despite the identification of multiple genes through in vivo and in vitro experimental studies that can significantly suppress ferroptosis in various ECs and retard the advancement of diabetes-associated complications, the intricate mechanisms underlying ferroptosis remain to be fully elucidated. Further research is imperative to fully elucidate its role. A profound comprehension of the ferroptosis mechanism could yield pivotal insights into its targeting of ECs within the diabetic milieu. Additionally, pinpointing key ferroptosis biomarkers could greatly enhance our comprehension of their role and progression in diabetic complications. Moreover, the detailed mechanisms of EC damage have not yet been thoroughly investigated, and the precise impacts within a high-glucose environment are not completely understood. Ultimately, given the scarcity of research literature and the limited scope of existing content, there is a recognized insufficiency in the investigation of EC ferroptosis in the context of diabetic complications.

In summary, research into the impact of endothelial cell ferroptosis on diabetes-related complications has begun to uncover a range of potential genetic factors and molecular targets. While certain pharmacological agents have demonstrated efficacy in preclinical animal models in inhibiting ferroptosis, their therapeutic efficacy in human patients with diabetic complications has yet to be conclusively established. Nonetheless, we firmly believe that deeper investigations into the interplay among ferroptosis, endothelial cells, and diabetic complications will pave the way for the discovery of novel therapeutic targets. This will require detailed elucidation of the underlying regulatory signaling pathways. Such advancements hold profound implications for the future clinical diagnosis and management of diabetes-related complications.
