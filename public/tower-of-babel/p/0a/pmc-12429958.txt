Nuclear transfer leads to aberrant cell cycle regulation and translation, as determined by gene expression, and selection of mitochondrial DNA in porcine blastocysts
Authors: Alexander Penn, Eryk Andreas, Takashi Okada, Justin C St John
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNuclear transfer technologies, such as metaphase II spindle transfer (MII-ST), are being developed to overcome poor oocyte quality and prevent children from acquiring severe mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) disease. However, it is unclear whether these technologies perturb gene expression, and mtDNA carryover can be reduced to minimal levels. Using our pig model, we isolated individual karyoplasts with limited mtDNA carryover (306 ± 49 copies) from mature oocytes. First, we reintroduced the spindle into its original cytoplast (autologous), fertilized and cultured the resultant embryos to the hatching blastocyst stage of development. Following RNA-seq, 64 genes were differentially expressed at false discovery rate < 0.05 and 1327 genes at P< 0.05. Pathways associated with cell cycle and epigenetic regulation and genes associated with nuclear–mitochondrial interactions were upregulated whilst protein translation pathways were downregulated. We then transferred karyoplasts to third-party cytoplasts (heterologous) and undertook mtDNA next-generation sequencing of two-cell to hatching blastocyst stage preimplantation embryos. Nine of twenty embryos possessed two mitochondrial genomes. Our outcomes show that autologous nuclear transfer alone significantly changes hatching blastocyst gene expression; very low levels of mtDNA carryover can be preferentially replicated during early development; and nuclear and mitochondrial synchrony has not been re-established.

Introduction

There has been increasing interest in the use of nuclear transfer technologies to treat couples with fertility related issues such as repeated fertilization failure or embryonic arrest during preimplantation development (1). Nuclear transfer has also been proposed to help couples who are at risk of transmitting mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutations or rearrangements to their offspring (2–4). The nuclear transfer technologies comprise several approaches that include germinal vesicle (5), metaphase II spindle (MII-ST) (3) and pronuclear (2,6) transfer, which have their origins in somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) that gave rise to Dolly the sheep (7). In each case, the patient’s nuclear genome, whether from a germinal vesicle, metaphase II (MII) oocyte or pronuclei, is/are transferred into a donor recipient cytoplasm from the same stage of development, i.e. an enucleated oocyte or zygote. In the case of infertility, the donor is likely to be a younger person who has viable oocytes that would overcome the cytoplasmic defects that have been attributed to failed fertilization or the arrest of their embryos (8,9). In the case of carriers of mtDNA disease, the donor would be a non-carrier of mtDNA mutations or rearrangements that lead to disease (10).

mtDNA is a double-stranded circular genome that is ∼16.6 kb in size and is located in the mitochondrion of our cells (11). It encodes 13 of the subunits of the electron transfer chain that generates the vast majority of cellular ATP through oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). It also encodes 2 rRNAs and 22 tRNAs and has one major non-coding region, the D-loop, that interacts with the nuclear-encoded mtDNA-specific transcription and replication factors (12). mtDNA is maternally only inherited (13) and from the population present in the mature MII oocyte at the time of fertilization. Interestingly, the mature, haploid, MII oocyte with fertilization potential possesses more copies of mtDNA than any other cell type, normally >200 000 copies in larger mammalian species. Consequently, on a nucleotide per nucleotide basis, at the time of fertilization, mtDNA is an equal genomic contributor (14).

A number of mtDNA mutations are associated with mtDNA disease either maternally inherited point mutations or mtDNA rearrangements such as deletions, which are mostly sporadic (15). In most of these disorders, mutant and normal mtDNA co-exist (heteroplasmy). Some of the more severe diseases include neuropathy, ataxia and retinitis pigmentosa (NARP) (16), mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis and stroke-like episodes (MELAS) (17) and Leigh syndrome (18,19). In these instances, there may be a threshold load of mutant mtDNA, often between 60% and 80%, beyond which a disease phenotype is apparent (15). However, carriers of mtDNA disease can have variable levels of mutations and rearrangements in their oocytes (20–24). Importantly, the levels in oocytes and embryos are not necessarily a clear indicator of whether the offspring might be affected (10). For the severe forms of mtDNA disease, 18% mtDNA mutation or rearrangement in the embryo has been proposed as a threshold for the transfer of embryos to the mother following assisted reproduction (10). However, when heteroplasmic load is higher, nuclear transfer has been proposed though, in this context, it is referred to as ‘mitochondrial donation’. It would allow carriers to transfer their nuclear genetic material into enucleated donor oocytes or zygotes from non-carriers of pathogenic mtDNA mutations. In recent years, approvals for human trials have been granted in the United Kingdom to apply these processes to help carriers of mtDNA disease (2,25). In this case, the preferred approach is pronuclear transfer (25). Nevertheless, MII-ST has led to the birth of a child (4) and rhesus macaque offspring (3).

There are four areas of potential concern, whether for fertility or mitochondrial donation, that could affect embryonic development and health of the offspring. First, the method of transfer could cause direct damage to the oocyte or zygote and the karyoplast. Damage to the karyoplast may result in aneuploidy in the resulting embryos (26), whilst the cytoplasm may be damaged by disruption of the actin filaments, whether by the mechanical removal of the karyoplast or the treatments to remove the karyoplast (27). These disruptions could result in a failure to produce viable embryos, or resulting embryos may have metabolic disturbances. Second, mtDNA can be carried over with the nucleus, otherwise termed mtDNA carryover, and this population could be preferentially amplified. Whilst mtDNA carryover is generally below 1%, in offspring, it can be as high as 60% (28). Furthermore, it could be disadvantageous to have two competing populations of mtDNA (one as a result of mtDNA carryover and the other in the recipient oocyte) in an embryo or offspring (29). Third, matching of appropriate mtDNA genotypes or haplotypes is likely necessary (30). Indeed, the combination of competing mtDNA genotypes could affect the levels of preferential replication of mtDNA carryover based on the specific crosses used (31,32), which could be further influenced by oocytes from different mtDNA haplotypes having different levels of mtDNA copy number (33). Fourth, removal of the spindle may affect the interactions between the nucleus and the mitochondrial genome. Given that mtDNA replication and changes to the epigenome throughout oogenesis are strictly regulated, moving the spindle to a new environment could affect the nucleus’s epigenome and expression of genes (34,35), and result in aberrant replication of the mitochondrial genome, as is the case following SCNT. The latter case would be mediated through increased expression of the mtDNA-specific transcription and replication factors, for example, polymerase γ (POLG) and mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) (36).

To address two of the areas of concern, we have generated a series of embryos up to the hatching blastocyst stage, the final stage of preimplantation development when embryonic genome activation has been completed and the embryo is ready for implantation (34). First, in a series of hatching blastocysts generated using autologous MII-ST, we determined if the process of nuclear transfer induced changes to gene expression, without the confounding effects of heteroplasmy. Second, we generated a series of embryos using heterologous MII-ST to determine if heteroplasmy would persist when mtDNA carryover was limited to minimal levels in a model where oocyte mtDNA copy number is higher (323 038 ± 71 650) than in the human (250 454 ± 29 730) (cf. (37) and (38)). We have undertaken this work in a pig model. The pig is considered to be an excellent model of human development (39), anatomy and physiology (40) and its mtDNA replication events also resemble those of the human during preimplantation development (38).

Materials and methods

All animal work was registered with the University of Adelaide’s Animal Ethics Committee. However, as the material was scavenged from abattoir material for food production and animals were not killed for the purpose of research, further approval was not required.

All materials were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich unless otherwise specified.

Oocyte collection and in vitro maturation

Gilt ovaries were collected in pairs from a local abattoir and transported to the laboratory in warm 0.9% NaCl solution (Baxter, Old Toongabbie, NSW, Australia). Cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs) from each ovary pair were aspirated from follicles with diameters of 3–6 mm using an 18 G needle. Once isolated, the COCs were washed three times in handling media (25 mM HEPES-TCM199; Gibco Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, H199) supplemented with 10% filtered gilt follicular fluid (FF) and 5 μg/ml cycloheximide (CHX, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). The COCs from each ovary pair were cultured in individual wells in 500 μl pre-equilibrated in vitro maturation (IVM) media (TCM199 media [Gibco] supplemented with 0.80 mM Na-pyruvate, 0.61 mM L-glutamine, 0.88 M cysteamine, 5 μg/ml insulin, 10 IU/ml pregnant mare’s serum gonadotrophin [PMSG, Folligon, Pacific Vet Pty Ltd., Braeside, VIC, Australia], 10 IU/ml chorionic gonadotrophin [hCG Chorulon, Pacific Vet Pty Ltd.] and 0.10 μg/ml epidermal growth factor [EGF] and 10% FF), which was supplemented with 5 μg/ml CHX at 38.5°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 in air for 18–20 h. Oocytes were then washed three times in IVM media without CHX and cultured in the same media for an additional 25 h at 38.5°C in 5% CO2.

To collect MII oocytes, 10 ml of 25 μg/ml hyaluronidase was added to an individual 500 μl well of expanded COCs from each ovary pair, which were pipetted to mix and transferred into H199 + 10% FF. Oocytes were denuded using a narrow glass pipette to fully remove all cumulus cells, and MII oocytes were selected by the presence of a polar body and held in a 50 μl drop of H199 + 10% FF in readiness for microinjection. For experiments requiring heterologous transfer, COCs from a different ovary pair in a second culture well were also denuded to provide a donor and recipient group for spindle transfer.

MII-ST

Immediately prior to spindle transfer, isolated MIIs were incubated with 5 μg /ml cytochalasin B in handling media for 5 min. Oocytes were then transferred to 3 μl droplets of Porcine X media (PXM, Supplementary Table S1), with one oocyte per droplet. The first oocyte drop was located, and the oocyte was held in position using a holding pipette (H-30/120–30, ICSION Medical Pty. Ltd., Thebarton, SA, Australia). The location of the oocyte’s spindle was confirmed via Oosight (Cambridge Research Instruments), and the spindle was oriented to the 2–4 o’clock position under brightfield. The CRi filter was re-engaged, and the spindle with minimal cytoplasm was aspirated using a 11 μm spike intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) pipette (I-11–30, ICSION Medical Pty. Ltd.). In preliminary experiments, we determined that the 11 μm diameter spiked pipette was ideal for porcine spindle transfer. It provides sufficient diameter to aspirate the metaphase plate with minimal disruption, reducing the possibility of damage to the chromosomes, as well as minimizing any additional carryover of cytoplasm containing mtDNA alongside the MII spindle. Furthermore, excess cytoplasm was removed by expelling the spindle into the culture media, and re-aspirating to further reduce the amount of attached cytoplasm. A 12 μm diameter spiked pipette resulted in 1244 ± 441 (mean ± SEM) copies of mtDNA being carried over whilst 306 ± 49 copies were carried over with a 11 μm diameter spiked pipette. This represents a statistical difference (P = 0.0077), independent samples t-test.

For heterologous spindle transfer, the first enucleated spindle was discarded. An oocyte from another ovary pair was then selected and enucleated as previous. Any excess cytoplasm was removed from the enucleation pipette by gentle expiration, with the spindle being removed from the pipette if necessary and re-aspirated. The initial recipient cytoplast was then relocated and held as previous. For experiments involving cell fusion, the enucleation pipette was re-inserted through the original break in the zona pellucida, and the spindle deposited within the perivitelline space. For experiments not involving cell fusion, the spindle was deposited directly into the cytoplasm. For autologous spindle transfer, enucleation was carried out as above; however, after enucleation, the pipette was removed and the spindle transferred back into the original cytoplasm through the same break. For copy number assessment, enucleated karyoplasts were placed into 2 μl of phosphate buffered saline (PBS) without Ca2+ or Mg2+ and frozen at −20°C. After all available oocytes had undergone spindle transfer, reconstructed embryos were collected into a droplet of handling media.

MII-ST fusion

For reconstructed oocytes undergoing cell fusion, oocytes were transferred together grouped by ovary pair (donor or recipient) into handling media. Oocytes were fused using a BTX ECM2001 Lite, using BTX-450 microslides with platinum electrodes (0.5 mm gap) in fusion media (300 mM D-mannitol, 0.1 mM CaCl2·2H2O, 0.2 mM MgSO4·7H2O, 0.01% polyvinyl alcohol [PVA], 0.5 mM HEPES, pH 7.3–7.4). Fusion comprised of two 50 V DC pulses (1 kV/cm) for 14 μs, with a 1-s interval between pulses. Putatively fused oocytes were washed three times in fusion media, and another three times in handling media. Lysed oocytes were removed, with all other oocytes kept in handling media for fertilization.

Fertilization and embryo culture

Semen in extender (Terminal Mix, Sabor Ltd., Clare, SA, Australia) was centrifuged at 300 g for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and spermatozoa were washed in pre-equilibrated sperm preincubation media (SPM, Medium 199; Gibco) supplemented with 0.91 mM sodium pyruvate, 4.1 mM calcium lactate, 37.5 μg/ml penicillin-G, 75.0 μg/ml streptomycin sulfate and 12% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Gibco), equilibrated for a minimum of 4 h at 38.5°C in 5% CO2, then allowed to cool to room temperature before resuspension of the sperm pellet. Spermatozoa were then centrifuged again at 300 g for 5 min and resuspended in SPM and kept in the dark at room temperature until ICSI dish preparation.

Approximately 30 min before fertilization, dishes for ICSI were prepared, using SpermSlow™ (CooperSurgical Inc., Sydney, NSW, Australia), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 2 × 10 μl droplets of SpermSlow™, followed by 5 μl droplets of handling media to contain the oocytes, were placed onto the lid of a 60 mm culture dish (Falcon). Approximately 1–2 μl of the prepared semen was added immediately to the right of the leftmost drop of SpermSlow™ and merged. The droplets were overlain with mineral oil (SAGE, CooperSurgical Inc.), and the dish was incubated at 38.5°C in 5% CO2 for 15 min. ICSI was performed using a 6 μm pipette (ICSION), with active spermatozoa ‘moving in place’ within the junction between the sperm catch and sperm droplet selected. A single spermatozoon was immobilized mechanically and picked up into an ICSI pipette pre-charged with SpermSlow™. A reconstructed oocyte was held with the reconstructed karyoplast at ∼12 o’clock by referencing the position relative to the polar body; the ICSI pipette inserted at the 3 o’clock position, and the spermatozoon released toward the 6 o’clock position. This positioning ensured that the injected karyoplast was not perturbed during the ICSI process.

After ICSI, embryos were cultured in porcine zygote media (PZM, Supplementary Table S1) at 38.5°C in 5% CO2 and 5% O2. After 48 h (day 2), embryos were changed into fresh media and again at 120 h (day 5). Embryos were washed once in sterile PBS without Ca2+ or Mg2+ and frozen in 2 μl of PBS at −80°C until required.

Early blastocysts were characterized as embryos where there was development of a blastocoele; however, this was below 50% of the total volume of the blastocyst. Blastocyst stage embryos were characterized by ≥50% of the total volume of the blastocyst being of the blastocoele. Expanded blastocysts were characterized as those that, after volume expansion had occurred, there was also thinning of the zona pellucida and a tightly packed inner cell mass.

mtDNA copy number verification

mtDNA was extracted using the QIAmp DNA Micro kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions, with elution into 30 μl Buffer AE (Qiagen). Approximately 2 μl was added to 5 μl of SensiFAST SYBR No-ROX (Meridian Bioscience, Cincinnati, OH), 2.6 μl of autoclaved Milli-Q water and 0.4 μL of each forward and reverse primer (400 nM). See Supplementary Table S2 for primer sequences. Reactions were run in a Bio-Rad CFX-384 instrument (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA) in triplicate, according to the following conditions: 95°C for 5 min; 43 cycles of 95°C for 5 s, 59°C for 10 s and 72°C for 15s. A melt curve was run at 95°C for 10 s, followed by temperature ramping from 60 to 95°C, with acquisition every 0.5°C. mtDNA copy number was extrapolated from standard curves of 10-fold serial dilutions of target polymerase chain reaction (PCR) product and calculated according to the target PCR length.

Nuclear DNA verification in karyoplasts

Confirmation of nuclear DNA in karyoplasts was carried out against a primer pair set developed against short interspersed nuclear elements (SINEs) to increase detection within the single karyoplast (Supplementary Table S2). PCR amplification was performed by adding 0.5 μl of DNA template to 2.5 μl of 5X Phusion High Fidelity buffer (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA), 0.3 μl of 10mM dNTPs (Thermo Fisher), 0.5 μl of primers, 0.375 μl of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 0.1 μl of Phusion DNA polymerase and 8.225 μl of autoclaved Milli-Q water. Reactions were run in an MJ Research PTC-200 thermal cycler (MJ Research, Bio-Rad) according to the following conditions: 95°C for 2 min, followed by 32 cycles of 94°C for 10 s, 60°C for 15 s and 72°C for 20 s, with a final step of 72°C for 2 min. Samples were mixed with 5X DNA loading buffer (Meridian Bioscience) and loaded into a 2% agarose gel prepared in 0.5X Tris-Acetate-EDTA (TAE) buffer with SYBR-Safe (Thermo Fisher) and run at 100V for ∼50 min. The gel was imaged on a Gel Doc EZ imager (Bio-Rad).

RNA extraction, library preparation and sequencing

RNA was extracted from hatching blastocysts using the Arcturus PicoPure RNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher), according to the manufacturer’s instructions and those outlined in (41,42). Extracted RNA was stored at −80°C until required. Library preparation and sequencing were performed by the Australian Genome Research Facility. The whole extract was used for library preparation, using the Illumina SMART-Seq Ultra Low Input Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s directions, with 16 amplification cycles and ribosomal RNA (rRNA) depletion using Illumina Ribo-Zero Gold (Illumina). Sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq X Plus (Illumina) using 150 bp paired-end sequencing.

RNAseq data analysis and identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

The primary sequence data were generated using the Illumina DRAGEN BCL Convert 07.021.645.4.0.3 pipeline and screened for the presence of Illumina adapter/over-represented sequences and cross species contamination. The cleaned reads were then aligned against the Sus scrofa genome (GCF_000003025.6) using the STAR aligner V2.5.3a (43) to map reads to the genomic sequence. The counts for reads mapping to each known gene were summarized (Supplementary Table S3). The transcripts were assembled with the StringTie Tool v2.1.4 utilizing the aligned reads and reference annotation-based assembly option (RABT).

Functional pathway enrichment and gene network analysis

Functional pathway enrichment analyses for differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were performed using gene ontology (GO) through PANTHER (44). REACTOME analysis was performed through reactome.org using the Analyse gene list tool (45). Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (46) pathway analysis was performed through the NCBI Database for Annotation Visualization and Integrated Discovery (DAVID) (47,48), with the pathways identified using over-representation analyses. The DEG list of all genes with a P-value of P< 0.05 was used to search for human orthologue gene symbols. First, the gene symbols from the DEG analysis were converted to Sus scrofa IDs, and subsequently human ortholog ENSEMBL IDs using Ensembl Biomart (49). Analysis for GO was performed against the default Homo sapiens reference list using human ENSEMBL IDs. If a DEG was not initially mapped to the reference list by PANTHER by ENSEMBL ID, the official gene symbol was used to map these DEGs. Analysis through REACTOME was performed using official gene symbols, with non-human identifiers projected to human. Human ENSEMBL IDs were used for select genes which were not recognized by REACTOME for the official gene symbol, but were recognized by ENSEMBL ID. Analysis through KEGG was performed using the official gene symbols uploaded through DAVID. For all analyses, pathways with a false discovery rate (FDR) of <0.05 were deemed to be over-represented in terms of the number of genes present in the pathway. For each of GO, REACTOME and KEGG, over-representation analyses were run for the full gene list, as well as performing over-representation analyses for solely the upregulated genes, or downregulated genes, as it allows for the greatest statistical power for discovering biologically significant pathways (50).

Reaction pathways were drawn using Cytoscape version 3.10.2, with the plugins Legend Creator v1.1.7 and ReactomeFI Plugin v8.0.6. Pathways were drawn using the Reactome Functional Interaction (FI) (ReactomeFI) pathway sourced from NDEx, authored by jkhuang (51). The node list was narrowed down for each specific pathway using the list for the respective pathways from REACTOME. The node list was further narrowed down to DEGs present within the original pathway, unless their presence was required to connect DEGs with the rest of the network. Pathways were colored for transcripts present within the DEG list by inputting those DEGs present from the pathway list and colored according to P< 0.05 for differential gene expression.

mtDNA amplification and next-generation sequencing

mtDNA amplification was carried out through four separate reactions to amplify the whole mitochondrial genome (see Supplementary Table S2 for individual information). DNA was extracted from embryos as described under ‘Mitochondrial DNA copy number verification,’ save for extraction from individual embryos as opposed to karyoplasts. For each embryo, amplification was performed using Platinum Taq High Fidelity (Thermo Fisher). Approximately 4 μl of sample was used as template for amplification of each portion of the mitochondrial genome, with the addition of 5 μl of 10X High Fidelity buffer, 2 μl of 50 mM MgSO4, 1.0 μl of 10 mM dNTP mix (Meridian Bioscience), 1.0 μl of each primer at 10 μM (500 nM each), 35.8 μl of autoclaved Milli-Q water and 0.2 μl of High Fidelity Taq polymerase. PCR amplification was performed in an MJ Research PTC-200 dual block thermal cycler according to cycling conditions in Supplementary Table S4 for 35 cycles.

After amplification, 15 μl of each sample were mixed with TriTrack DNA loading Dye (6X) according to the manufacturer’s direction and loaded into a 1% agarose (Meridian Bioscience) gel with 0.5X TAE running buffer, plus SYBR-Safe, according to manufacturer’s instructions to confirm the presence of amplified product. The gel was run at 100V for ∼1 h, with presence confirmed using a GelDoc EX imager set to 100 ms exposure and compared against 10 KB Plus ladder (Thermo Fisher). Once confirmed, the remaining 35 μl of PCR product was purified using the ISOLATE II PCR and Gel Kit, according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with elution into 15 μl of autoclaved Milli-Q water. Purified PCR products were then assessed for DNA concentration via Qubit DNA HS 1X (Thermo Fisher) using a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer, according to manufacturer’s directions. Each fragment was then combined to produce 50 ng of total product, with 3 ng per kb of template (i.e. 21.78 ng Fragment 1, 9.39 ng Fragment 2, 9.69 ng Fragment 3 and 10.98 ng Fragment 4).

Library construction and sequencing were carried out by Australian Genome Research Facility. Initial quality control was performed using 1% E-Gel (Thermo Fisher) to confirm the presence of amplicons, in addition to confirmation of DNA concentration by the QuantiFluor® dsDNA system (Promega). After initial QC, 9 μL was used for library preparation using Prep-M library preparation, with 12 amplification cycles (Nextera DNA Flex, Illumina Inc. CA, USA) to generate mtDNA amplicon libraries. These were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq X platform using the NovaSeq X 10B lane (300 cycles, 150 bp paired end reads).

mtDNA mapping

Sequences were aligned in CLC Genomics Workbench Version 23.0.5 to a porcine reference pig mitochondrial genome—AJ002189.1 (52). Reads were trimmed by one nucleotide from each of the 5′ and 3′ ends, and those of <15 bp in length were excluded. Reads that surpassed a Phred quality score of 15 were used for mapping. The mapping parameters were as follows: a match score of 1, a mismatch cost of 2, an insertion/deletion cost of 3 and a similarity fraction of 0.80. This resulted in 98% of the reads aligning to the reference genome. Duplicate reads were then excluded. To undertake sample specific variant calling to determine heteroplasmic load, a consensus sequence (reference genome) was generated for each sample. The trimmed reads were then remapped against their consensus sequence, as described above, including removal of duplicate reads. Consensus sequences were then aligned using the Create Alignment tool (CLC Genomics Workbench) and using a gap cost of 10; and gap extension cost of 1. Phylogenetic trees were constructed after the alignments had been imported into the Maximum Likelihood Phylogeny tool (CLC Genomics Workbench) and trees were generated using the Neighbour Joining method, the General Time Reversible nucleotide substitution model and bootstrapping of 1000 replicates.

Analysis of single nucleotide variations

From the reads mapped to each individual’s consensus sequence, variant calling was performed using the Low Frequency Variant Detection module (CLC Genomic Workbench Version 23.0.5). The parameters applied were exclusion of all duplicate reads, presence of all variants on forward and reverse reads and a minimum of 50 reads for each variant. A minimum threshold of 2.0% was set for the calling of each variant.

Microhaplotype calling

Mapped reads with duplicate reads removed were subjected to microhaplotype calling against the five mtDNA haplotypes associated with commercial pigs in Australia (Accession numbers: KT279758, KT261429, KT279759, KT279760 and KT261430) using the Microhaplotype Caller Tool in CLC Genomics Workbench (Version 23.0). Parameters were sets as follows: required significance of 1.0%; maximum phasing distance of 50; minimum allele fraction = 0.01.

Determination of preferential amplification

To calculate fold preferential selection of variant load, using our starting mean value of 306 mtDNA copies (Figure 1A), and a total of 323 000 copies normally present in a mature MII oocyte (38), the variant load percentage was divided by the percentage of the starting material (0.1%).

Statistical analysis

For DEG analysis, the Bioconductor package edgeR v4.0.9 (53) was used within R v4.3.1 (54). The default trimmed mean of M values normalization method within edgeR was used to normalize the counts between samples. A generalized linear model was then used to quantify the differential expression between the groups, generating P-values. The FDR was generated using the Benjamini–Hochberg method within edgeR. For all pathway statistical analyses, a FDR of <0.05 was deemed to be over-represented in terms of the number of genes present in any pathway.

Results

Refining mtDNA carryover

In order to reduce the amount of mtDNA that could be carried over from one MII oocyte to another, we refined the process by reducing the size of the enucleation pipette and gentle propulsion of the extracted spindle within the pipette. From 12 individual aspirated karyoplasts, the mean (± standard error of the mean (SEM)) mtDNA copy number was 306 ± 49, ranging from 20 to 767 copies (Figure 1A). The presence of the MII spindle in the aspirated karyoplast was confirmed using PCR probes against SINEs (Figure 1B). Using this modified approach, we performed MII-ST to generate embryos for subsequent analyses.

Autologous spindle transfer changes gene expression in hatching blastocysts

In order to determine the effects of manipulating the MII spindle, we performed autologous MII-ST. To this extent, we reintroduced the spindle into the same oocyte, thus negating the confounding effects that might arise from the presence of two mtDNA genotypes. Following the transfer of the spindle, reconstructed oocytes were fertilized by ICSI and cultured to the hatching blastocyst stage. Seven of the hatching blastocysts were generated by injecting the spindle directly into its cytoplasm (Cyto-ST, ST_HB_1 to ST_HB_7). Two were generated by transferring the spindle into its perivitelline space followed by electrical fusion (ST-fusion; ST_HB_8 and ST_HB_9). We then performed RNA-seq on the nine MII-ST-derived and 10 ICSI-derived (control) hatching blastocysts.

RNA-seq from each hatching blastocyst resulted in 46 to 115 M total reads, with 31 to 80 M paired reads per blastocyst mapped to the reference genome (Supplementary Table S3). Of these, 13 to 34 M reads per blastocyst mapped a single feature, which, after Trimmed mean of M-values normalization were used for analysis of DEGs. The normalized MDS plot, showed that hatching blastocysts derived through Cyto-ST and ST-fusion did not cluster separately and, thus, did not vary significantly after multidimensional analysis (Supplementary Figure S1).

The comparison between ICSI and MII-ST-derived hatching blastocysts identified 64 DEGs based on a FDR of P< 0.05 (Table 1). In all, 20 of these genes were over-expressed as a result of MII-ST, whilst 44 exhibited reduced expression (Table 1). Of these, 55 mapped to ENSEMBL IDs, with 54 then mapping to human orthologues for GO analyses. Notably, clustered mitochondrial homolog (CLUH) was upregulated in the MII-ST cohort (log fold change [logFC] 0.79, FDR < 0.01, Table 1), whilst proline rich acidic protein 1 (PRAP1) was downregulated (logFC −2.43, FDR < 0.005, Table 1). Although examination of single DEGs can be useful in determining changes, association of DEGs within a pathway produces a more robust determination of cellular changes.

Pathway analysis

To determine how the DEGs resulting from MII-ST influenced specific pathways, we undertook over-representation analyses using GO. The 64 DEGs based on an FDR of <0.05 resulted in no over-represented pathways. The gene list was then expanded to include DEGs with an uncorrected P-value (P< 0.05) to minimize the effect of false negatives arising from a highly restricted FDR DEG list. This expanded the number of DEGs to 1327 (Supplementary Table S5). Of these, 585 were upregulated, and 742 were downregulated for the MII-ST cohort relative to ICSI. Notable DEGs included upregulation of DNA methyltransferases DNMT1 (logFC 0.67, P< 0.05) and DNMT3B (logFC 0.62, P< 0.05), Ten-eleven translocase 1 (TET1) (logFC 0.54, P< 0.05), the catalytic subunit of the mtDNA-specific replication factor POLG (logFC 0.55, P< 0.01) and the mitochondrial-specific transcription factor TFAM (logFC 0.78, P< 0.001). In addition, there was downregulation of activating transcription factor 5 (ATF5) (logFC −0.63, P< 0.05) and glycogen synthase kinase 3ß (GSK3B) (logFC −0.54, P< 0.001).

Reactome pathway analysis

To examine protein pathways modulated by MII-ST, the list of DEGs was analyzed using REACTOME. REACTOME pathways are curated for human networks of genes allowing for more specific analyses of orthologous gene networks altered by MII-ST. When analyzing all DEGs (Supplementary Figure S2), 32 reactions were identified at an FDR < 0.05 (Supplementary Table S9). The top three terms were Activation of Gene Expression by SREBF (SREBP) (R-HSA-2426168, Figure 2A), Peptide Chain Elongation (R-HSA-156902, Figure 2B) and SRP-dependent Co-translational Protein Targeting to Membrane (R-HSA-1799339, Figure 2C). Of note, Unfolded Protein Response (UPR) (R-HSA-381119, Figure 2D) was also over-represented, which is involved in the response from endoplasmic reticulum overload (55).

From the 585 DEGs with increased expression following MII-ST (P< 0.05) (Supplementary Figure S3), 58 reactions were identified (Supplementary Table S9), with the top three terms being G0 and Early G1 (R-HSA-1538133, Figure 3A), Mitotic G1 Phase and G1/S Transition (R-HSA-453279, Figure 3B) and Cell Cycle (R-HSA-1640170, Figure 3C). Epigenetic Regulation of Gene Expression (R-HSA-212165, Figure 3D) was also over-represented, which suggests changes to regulation of gene activation or repression. Consequently, these terms indicate that nuclear transfer embryos undergo upregulation in cell proliferation with concomitant changes to gene regulation.

The top three terms identified were Peptide Chain Elongation (R-HSA-156902, Figure 4A), SRP-dependent Co-translational Protein Targeting to Membrane (R-HSA-1799339, Figure 4B) and Eukaryotic Translation Elongation (R-HSA-156842, Figure 4C). Combined with the upregulated genes, it is evident that cell proliferation is upregulated with a concomitant decrease in global translation of proteins. Analysis by Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG)

The DEGs were also analyzed by KEGG, which includes assessment of Sus scrofa specific non-coding RNAs (ncRNAs) whereas REACTOME is specific to human proteins. Using the full list of DEGs (n = 1327), 23 pathways were identified that were over-represented following MII-ST (Supplementary Table S10). Of these, six pathways were specific to the full dataset only and were not present in either the upregulated or downregulated lists. These included biosynthesis of amino acids, autophagy and glycosphingolipid biosynthesis.

When examining the upregulated genes (n = 585), 11 pathways were over-represented, with the top terms being cell cycle, DNA replication and cellular senescence (Supplementary Table S10). Of these, five pathways were specific to the upregulated genes and six were shared with the full gene list. When examining the downregulated genes (n = 742), 12 pathways were over-represented. The top terms were Lysosome, protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum and metabolic pathways (Supplementary Table S10). In all, five pathways were specific to upregulated genes and six were shared with the full gene list. Interestingly, there were no shared pathways between the upregulated and downregulated pathways, indicating that entire pathways are being changed in one direction only as a result of MII-ST.

Interactions between the nucleus and mitochondrial genomes

Within our expanded list of DEGs, upregulation of POLG, the DNA polymerase specific to mtDNA replication and TFAM, the transcription factor that activates mtDNA transcription and replication (Supplementary Table S5), were upregulated in MII-ST hatching blastocysts. This suggests the potential to mediate mtDNA replication. In the case of heterologous MII-ST (i.e. from one individual to another), resultant embryos could possess two mitochondrial genomes (heteroplasmy) at varying amounts.

Determination of mtDNA heteroplasmy

In order to determine if the reduced mtDNA content attached to the MII spindle would be transmitted through to the hatching blastocyst stage of development, we performed mtDNA next-generation sequencing on two-cell to hatching blastocyst stage embryos produced by heterologous MII-ST. Again, we analyzed a combination of embryos derived by Cyto-ST (n = 17; Table 5) and ST-fusion (n = 3; Table 5). We then performed two forms of analysis. First, we undertook variant calling to identify single nucleotide variants (SNVs) which would be indicative of heteroplasmy present in the population of mtDNA within the original oocyte and those indicative of the carried over mtDNA. Second, we performed microhaplotype calling to confirm if the SNVs were indicative of one (homoplasmy) or two (heteroplasmy) genomes, as determined by phasing (linkage to one or more neighbouring variants) and to potentially identify further relevant heteroplasmic sites. As can be seen from Figure 5A, the distribution of the mitochondrial genotypes for the embryos aligned with the mtDNA haplotypes previously identified in Australian commercial pigs.

Two hatching blastocysts (E17 and E18) generated through Cyto-ST were both homoplasmic, as determined by variant and microhaplotype calling (Table 5). E18 possessed one heteroplasmic site (nt11303; A→T), however, this was not confirmed by microhaplotype calling and the variants amongst sequences used for microhaplotype calling were greater than one base pair difference. Consequently, this heteroplasmic site is likely to be present in the oocyte itself. Nevertheless, both E17 and 18 possessed several insertions and deletions which were present at the same sites in the majority of sequences analyzed. We further analyzed two hatching blastocysts generated through ST-fusion (E13 and E14). Neither possessed any heteroplasmic sites following variant calling and homoplasmy was confirmed by microhaplotype calling (Table 5). However, E15 which was generated in the same manner was heteroplasmic.

Analysis of three blastocyst stage embryos showed that one (E20) exhibited 164 heteroplasmic sites at a mean ± SEM heteroplasmic load of 7.47% ± 0.11 (Table 5). Of these, 11 were confirmed by microhaplotype calling with no further heteroplamic sites present. Some of these sites suggested the presence of heteroplasmy as a result of phasing. Similarly, blastocyst E21 possessed 11 SNVs of which one was confirmed to be heteroplasmic and phased with another site that possessed two alleles. The mean heteroplasmic load was 7.32% ± 0.5 (Table 5). One further blastocyst (E01) and one early blastocyst (E19) were deemed to be homoplasmic. On the other hand, a morula stage embryo (E10) did not show any SNVs after variant calling, but microhaplotype calling revealed some heteroplasmy with some phasing (Table 5).

To determine if the mtDNA replication event that takes place between fertilization and the two-cell stage (38) might enhance the amount of mtDNA carryover, we assessed two-cell embryos. In all, four out of the six were heteroplasmic. In two samples (E06 and E09), we were able to confirm the presence of the second genome through phasing of these variants with sites specific to another mitochondrial genome through microhaplotype calling (Table 5). These samples and another (E04) exhibited other heteroplasmic sites identified by microhaplotype calling and two, E04 (Figure 5B) and E06 (Figure 5C), were confirmed by phasing. Consequently, it is evident that two genomes can co-exist at the two-cell stage following Cyto-ST at a mean heteroplasmic load of 4.28% ± 1.75, which represents a fold increase in preferential replication of 42.75 ± 17.5 for carried over mtDNA. We further assessed one three-cell, two four-cells and one six-cell embryo and observed both homoplasmy and heteroplasmy (Table 5).

Overall, 9 out of 20 (45%) embryos were deemed heteroplasmic (Table 5) with heteroplasmy observed both in Cyto-ST and ST-fusion derived embryos. Consequently, it is evident that there is preferential replication of the minimal levels of carried over mtDNA in a subset of the embryos produced by MII-ST.

Discussion

It is well-documented that there are four rate-limiting issues associated with nuclear transfer, namely the interaction of the nucleus with its acquired cytoplasmic environment; the effect of mtDNA carryover; interaction of the nucleus with the mitochondrial genome; and selection of the most appropriate mtDNA haplotype through recruitment of donor oocytes (56). In our work, we have focused on the first two using MII-ST and the pig as our model and, inadvertently, the third issue. Nevertheless, we, first, specifically set out to determine the effect of spindle transfer on hatching blastocyst gene expression by removing the MII spindle from an oocyte and re-introducing it into the same cytoplasm either directly or through fusion with minimum levels of mtDNA carryover. These are the most direct forms of autologous transfer that enable the interactions between the two genomes and the nucleus and cytoplasm to be explored. They also avoid the use of chemical agents or viruses, such as Hemagglutinating virus of Japan Envelope (HVJ-E)/Sendai virus protein that can induce intracellular reactive oxygen species and affect developmental outcome (57). Second, we used heterologous transfer to determine if there was preferential selection for the minimum levels of mtDNA accompanying the spindle that might result in heteroplasmy. We have focused on MII-ST transfer rather than pronuclear transfer since, during the pronuclear stage of embryo development, there are active changes to DNA methylation occurring in the maternal pronucleus (34), which could impair offspring health (58,59). Furthermore, MII-ST has been proven to produce children to overcome fertility issues (60) and as a means to potentially overcome mtDNA disease (4).

Autologous MII-ST resulted in 64 DEGs being identified at an FDR < 0.05. For subsequent pathway analyses, this was then expanded to 1327 DEGs by assessing all DEGs at P< 0.05 from the RNAseq data. In terms of GO and REACTOME pathways, when analyzing only DEGs upregulated via MII-ST, we observed DNA replication and cell cycle as the most over-represented terms along with DNA damage repair terms. In addition, the top downregulated pathways included metabolic processes and endoplasmic reticulum terms under GO, and translation for REACTOME pathways (Supplementary Table S6–S9). Combined, these terms show there is a decrease in gene expression for ribosomal components, as well as translation that leads to a reduction in endoplasmic reticulum response to mis-folded proteins. Therefore, it is likely that the MII-ST embryo is prioritizing rapid cellular proliferation to increase blastocyst cell number, with reduced production of protein.

There are two likely rationales contributing to this phenomenon. Firstly, MII-ST-derived hatching blastocysts could have developed at a faster than normal rate. A similar effect has been observed in embryos from overweight/obese women that reached the morula stage 17 h earlier than embryos from normal body mass index (BMI) women (61). However, these embryos had fewer cells at the expanded blastocyst stage and consumed less glucose (61). Consequently, it is likely that hatching blastocysts derived through nuclear transfer are less developmentally mature than their counterparts since they exhibited characteristics more similar of earlier stage human blastocysts. This likely arises as MII-ST blastocysts have not yet begun transcription that occurs later in embryogenesis in preparation for gastrulation, and earlier stages are priortizing the rapid growth to the blastocyst stage after compaction.

The second rationale highlights the trauma to the oocyte caused by nuclear transfer that could induce premature hatching during blastocyst expansion. The enlarged break and re-entry through the zona pellucida is more severe than for ICSI derived embryos, where there is only one smaller break in the zona as a much finer pipette is used. Indeed, this outcome resembles some of the assisted hatching approaches used in assisted reproduction to promote hatching (62). Consequently, the stage of embryo development for nuclear transfer embryos is not a reliable measurement of an embryo's readiness to implant, as also evidenced in SCNT, with low implantation rates for high-quality by appearance blastocysts (63). Our rationale is supported by the downregulation of PRAP1 (Table 1 and Supplementary Table S5), a component of the over-represented KEGG pathway Autophagy—Animal (Supplementary Table S10), where PRAP1 is implicated in embryo implantation (64). On the other hand, embryos produced by SCNT have been shown to increase the rate of implantation after induction of autophagy during embryo development (65).

SCNT is also associated with incomplete reprogramming that has implications for offspring health and well-being (66). In terms of interactions that may induce changes to DNA methylation (Figure 3D), we observed upregulation in the DNA methyltransferases DNMT1 and DNMT3B (Supplementary Table S5), which are required for de novo DNA methylation. Expression of DNMT1 is expected to decrease in the porcine blastocyst (34), and DNMT3B to plateau from the morula stage (34). Interestingly, we also observed upregulation of TET1, which conversely actively demethylates DNA (34,67–69). By the hatching blastocyst stage, TET1 should be reducing its activity to be replaced by de novo DNA methylation (34). Indeed, in human embryos, the conversion to 5hmC up to the eight-cell stage and loss of its activity results in failure to activate embryonic genes (70); and that loss of its activity at the four-cell stage is indicative of abnormal development (71). However, expression of the TET enzymes appears to be important for trophoblast differentiation and, specifically TET2 (72). Nevertheless, our data suggest that the nucleus is developmentally delayed and is not in synchrony with its cytoplasm. This is again similar to porcine embryos generated through SCNT, where TET1 is increased in blastocysts (66), as well as demonstrating asynchrony between nuclear and cytoplasmic maturity, which would also affect the embryo’s ability to implant.

In the context of nuclear-cytoplasmic interactions, we observed increased expression of CLUH (FDR < 0.05), which participates in signaling from the mitochondria to the nucleus (73) and increases energy production within individual mitochondria (74). This may be entirely due to the location of the mitochondria to the spindle, where nuclear-mitochondrial signaling from the nearby mitochondria may be preferential. The use of cytochalasin B disrupts the actin filaments (27) as part of the enucleation process but, consequently, reduces signaling between the nucleus and the cytoplasm. It is, therefore, likely that the upregulation in CLUH is in response to cytoplasmic disruption. Furthermore, standard concentrations of cytochalasin B can increase levels of heteroplasmy in MII-ST and pronuclear transfer embryos (27), which suggests that changes to cytoplasmic structure may restrict the distribution of carried over mtDNA following cell division.

We also observed downregulation of ATF5 and GSK3B. ATF5 is a transcription factor that activates genes required for the mitochondrial UPR (75–77), while GSK3B promotes mitochondrial motility (78). In MELAS patients harbouring the 3243A > G mtDNA mutation, ATF5 is elevated when mutant load is high coupled with a concomitant decrease in GSK3B (77). However, we observed a decrease, with a matching phenotype of reducing the unfolded protein response (Figure 4D, (77)). This would indicate that autologous MII-ST does not induce mitochondrial stress; however, this may be different following heterologous MII-ST, if two competing mitochondrial genomes were present.

The current interest in nuclear transfer technologies is as an application for rescuing defective oocytes that are unlikely to fertilize (28,60), and as an option for carriers of mtDNA disease who wish to have children that would not be burdened by these sometimes severely debilitating, if not fatal diseases (4,79). For both procedures, it is critical to minimize the amount of carried over mtDNA. In the case of mtDNA disease, it is essential to restrict the possibilities of the mutant population becoming the predominant population to avoid mtDNA disease. In the context of infertility, the transmission of two populations of mtDNA could result in one population (i.e. from another haplotype) being more or less efficient at generating ATP through OXPHOS, as has been observed in cybrid studies (80). Likewise, mtDNA haplotypes impact differently on the DNA methylome of differentiating cells and the presence of two haplotypes could differentially affect gene expression (81,82) and ultimately phenotype. Furthermore, mtDNA is normally maternally only inherited (13) and bi-maternal inheritance would compromise the mtDNA genetic integrity of the resultant offspring. Here, we have limited the amount of mtDNA carryover to ∼300 copies. In mouse models, mtDNA carryover has been reported to be as high as ∼14 000 copies (7.22% of total copy number) (83). However, refinement reduced carryover to ∼2000 copies (1.23%), which resulted in live offspring (83). Our outcome is more remarkable in the context that porcine oocytes possess higher levels of mtDNA than human oocytes (cf. (37) and (38)). It now appears that mean mtDNA can be reduced to ∼ 0.1% of the reconstructed oocyte’s total mtDNA. Nevertheless, we still observed preferential replication of carried over mtDNA in 45% of preimplantation embryos across the spectrum of mtDNA haplotypes previously identified (Table 5 and Figure 5A).

Human embryonic stem cells (ESCs) generated from MII-ST blastocysts (84) possessed, on average, 0.2% carryover before cell line derivation (84). However, in one cell line, the carryover increased to 53.2% by passage 36 but then reverted to minimal levels by passage 60 (84). Furthermore, clonal expansion of single ESCs resulted in variable levels of heteroplasmy ranging from 0% to 90% due to carryover (84). Indeed, variable levels of transmission and patterns of segregation have been reported in Rhesus macaques and the one child generated from a carrier of mtDNA disease through MII-ST (3). Perhaps the most disconcerting is the report of six pregnancies resulting in live births where carryover measured in the blastocyst was below 1% (60). However, one child possessed carryover at far higher levels in cord blood (36%), peripheral blood (37.7%) and urine (51.1%) (60) suggesting that low levels of heteroplasmy in the embryo can result in partial reversion later during development.

It is highly likely mtDNA replication is aberrantly regulated following MII-ST. Two key nuclear-encoded transcription and replication factors, namely POLG and TFAM, were upregulated in MII-ST hatching blastocysts (85). These higher levels of expression could suggest that there are fewer inner cell mass cells relative to trophectodermal cells in these embryos. Indeed, it is not uncommon to observe an increased proportion of trophectodermal cells and fewer inner cell mass cells in cases of metabolic disease. For example, an increase in trophectodermal cells was observed in the blastocysts of overweight/obese women (61), although POLG and TFAM were not assessed in this case. At the blastocyst stage, mtDNA replication is restricted to the trophectodermal cells, which give rise to the placenta. On the other hand, the inner cell mass cells, which give rise to the embryo proper and foetus, do not replicate their mtDNA with each newly formed undifferentiated cell possessing fewer copies of mtDNA (86). Consequently, there would be fewer ICM cells suppressing mtDNA replication (38,87), whilst the large number of trophectoderm cells have initiated differentiation and, thus, need to replicate mtDNA accordingly (88). If a similar change occurs in heterologous MII-ST embryos, this would lead to the observed increases in carried over mtDNA in blastocysts.

Nevertheless, it is evident that POLG and TFAM RNA and protein expression are prematurely upregulated in SCNT-derived embryos (36). This is also likely to be the case for MII-ST embryos given the preferential selection for carried over mtDNA by the two-cell stage. Consequently, if the carried over mtDNA is not actively extruded during preimplantation development (89) or diluted out prior to gastrulation when the mtDNA set point is established, i.e. the lowest level of mtDNA content present in undifferentiated cells (90), then these founder copies of mtDNA are present as mtDNA replication is initiated (91) and could contribute to the offspring’s mtDNA genomic content. However, it is very unlikely that all cells of the embryo would harbour the carried over mtDNA since very low levels accompany the MII spindle into the oocyte and these molecules would unlikely be uniformly distributed. It is far more likely that they are segregated to one or a few cells after each stage of division. If the carried over mtDNA persists, there is a greater chance that these molecules could be transmitted to one or a few tissues and organs of the offspring. If the mutant molecule is carried over and persists, it could manifest to affect the offspring. Indeed, in SCNT derived porcine offspring, donor cell (third party) mtDNA was transmitted to one progeny at a range of 0 to 44% amongst individual tissues (32). Therefore, it is highly important to remember that any analysis of a few cells of the embryo will not be a true indication of potential transmission to the offspring but is indicative of those cells only. Consequently, the analysis of trophectodermal cells through blastocyst biopsy will only indicate the potential levels in a few placental cells and will not necessarily be indicative of mtDNA carryover in the foetus or offspring. Perhaps, this is best exemplified by the carryover observed in children derived from MII-ST for either fertility (60) or mtDNA disease (4) reasons.

In all, we have generated two sets of hatching blastocysts to address two specific questions. First, we asked whether the removal and reinsertion of the MII spindle would affect the developmental potential of resultant blastocysts using autologous nuclear transfer. From this, we determined that a large number of genes were differentially expressed that affected key developmental pathways associated with DNA replication, cell cycle regulation and DNA methylation. In addition, we noted that the key players in mtDNA replication were upregulated at the blastocyst stage, which link the interactions between the two genomes (14). Second, we generated a series of embryos (two-cell to hatching blastocyst) using heterologous nuclear transfer to determine if even low levels of mtDNA carryover would be preferentially selected. Indeed, this was the case in 9 out of 20 embryos from various stages of preimplantation development. It appears that the process of nuclear transfer requires a period of acclimatization to take place between the nucleus and cytoplasm in order that the interactions between the nuclear and mitochondrial genes can be reestablished. This would allow the two genomes to cooperate or reestablish ‘genomic balance’ in order for development to ensue (14). At the hatching blastocyst stage, this appears to not have been fully completed which could affect the implantation potential of these embryos that is frequently associated with nuclear transfer. However, if implantation were successful, it could lead to some of the anomalies associated with SCNT (92). Such abnormalities might include birth defects and high post-natal losses which are observed in large animals as well as large offspring syndrome, respiratory distress, circulatory problems, immune dysfunction, kidney and heart failure, and movement and balance disorders, as discussed in (93) where the causes, amongst other rationale, have been attributed to epigenetic reprogramming and, potentially, mitochondrial dysfunction. Nevertheless, the use of a limited number of rhesus macaques suggested there were no apparent developmental problems, although there were noted abnormalities in the transmission of mtDNA (94).
