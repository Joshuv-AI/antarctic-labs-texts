Enhanced osteoconductivity of green synthesized hydroxyapatite-functionalized Nd2O3/Fe3O4 nanocomposite using Elaeagnus angustifolia L. seed extract
Authors: Elham Salari, Hadi Sadeghzadeh, Nasrin Valizadeh, Fahimeh Kazeminava, Saeed Jafarirad, Roya Salehi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study describes the green synthesis of a neodymium oxide/magnetic iron oxide (Nd2O3/Fe3O4) nanocomposite, functionalized with hydroxyapatite, using Elaeagnus angustifolia L. seeds extract. Then the effectiveness of electrospun polycaprolactone-collagen (PCL-COL) loaded with nanoparticles composites as scaffolds for bone tissue engineering was evaluated. The synthesis of nanoparticles and their elemental identification were confirmed using XRD, FT-IR, and EDX techniques. The DLS, TEM, and SEM analysis demonstrated the generation of Nd-Fe3O4@HAp NPs with an average diameter of 14–18 nm. Vibrating Sample Magnetometry (VSM) validated the ferromagnetic and superparamagnetic characteristics of the nanoparticles. Tensile and contact angle analysis revealed that NP-loaded electrospun scaffolds exhibited markedly enhanced mechanical characteristics and hydrophilicity relative to pristine polymer specimens, owing to the homogeneous distribution of nanofillers throughout the polymer fibers. Additionally, cellular investigations and osteogenic potential were evaluated in vitro using adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADMSCs). Assessments of cell attachment, spreading, and proliferation of ADMSCs were conducted using SEM observation and thiazolyl blue (MTT) test. The osteogenic differentiation potential of ADMSCs on the fabricated nanofiber scaffolds was evaluated using alkaline phosphatase activity, calcium content test, and western blot analysis. ADMSCs showed better initial adherence and infiltration in nanoparticle-enhanced scaffolds compared to PCL/COL scaffolds. Additionally, our Western blot analysis demonstrated that scaffolds can effectively induce osteogenic differentiation in ADMSCs by up-regulating key proteins associated with osteogenesis (p-value < 0.0001). Thus, nanoparticle-loaded electrospun nanofibers exhibit considerable potential as effective scaffolds for applications in bone tissue engineering.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04676-w.Keywords: Green synthesis, Magnetic nanoparticles, Hydroxyapatite, Scaffold, Differentiation, Adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells

Introduction

Bone tissue is an essential and dynamic form of connective tissue that serves multiple critical functions within the body. These functions include supporting structures, safeguarding internal organs, enabling mobility, supporting weights, and aiding in the production of blood and maintaining mineral balance [1]. Apart from these mechanical purposes, bone tissue is vital for metabolic activities as it stores minerals like calcium and phosphorous, which are necessary for several physiological functions [2]. Furthermore, the bone marrow contained within the bones is responsible for the production of blood cells that facilitate oxygen transport and are essential for immunological defense [3, 4]. Any structural changes to bone tissue resulting from injury or disease can disrupt the body’s homeostasis and significantly impact an individual’s overall health. For example, conditions such as osteoporosis lead to reduced bone density, thereby increasing the risk of fractures. Similarly, traumatic injuries can result in fractures that compromise the integrity of the skeletal system. These disruptions not only hinder mobility but can also result in chronic pain and disability. Consequently, maintaining the health of bone tissue is essential for sustaining both physical function and overall well-being [5–7]. Bone tissue can self-repair partial injuries through specialized cells like osteoblasts and osteoclasts. However, this regenerative ability is limited in critical cases, such as major fractures, infections, or significant bone loss, where the healing process may become inefficient and often requires external intervention for full recovery [8–10].

Tissue engineering offers a potential solution to these problems; it aims to improve the body’s inherent healing mechanisms by creating biological replacements that either restore or increase functioning [11]. Tissue engineering approaches for bone regeneration emphasize three essential requirements: an appropriate cellular source, optimal biochemical conditions, and a biocompatible scaffold [12, 13]. For effective integration of the scaffold with host tissue, it is essential that it exhibits chemical and physical properties analogous to those of natural bone [12]. The porous architecture of electrospun nanofiber scaffolds is particularly advantageous, as it can effectively replicate the natural extracellular matrix of bone tissue. This structural mimicry not only supports cellular activities but also enhances nutrient and oxygen exchange, which are vital for effective regeneration [14]. In summary, tissue engineering represents a multifaceted approach to bone regeneration, integrating cellular sources, biochemical conditions, and advanced scaffold designs to create effective solutions for restoring bone function [8, 15, 16].

New scaffold materials are designed to improve angiogenesis and osteogenesis by imitating the structure of real bones [17]. Efforts to imitate the ECM’s nanostructure have resulted in the creation of hydrogels, nanofibers, nanotubes, and scaffolds that encourage cell proliferation and direct tissue regeneration by virtue of their biomimetic characteristics [18, 19]. Nanoparticles (NPs) promote bone formation, transport bioactive compounds, and alter immune responses, allowing for targeted, less invasive treatments [20–22]. NPs can be synthesized using a diverse range of materials, such as metals, ceramics, and both synthetic and natural polymers. A high surface area with adjustable surface properties and improved penetration capabilities are just two of the unique advantages that each of these materials offers [23, 24].

Nano-hydroxyapatite (HAp) represents a notable improvement in bone regeneration technology, recognized for its superior interaction with biological tissues owing to its nanoscale characteristics. It enhances osteoconductivity, osteoinductivity, and biocompatibility, making it ideal for tissue engineering [25]. HAp serves as a scaffold supporting osteoblast attachment and proliferation, promoting new bone formation. Its nanostructure aids in protein and growth factor adsorption, fostering osteoinduction at implant sites. HAp is used to coat metallic implants for better bone integration and is incorporated into composites for repairing bone defects [26–28]. Research focuses on combining HAp with other biomaterials to improve bone tissue engineering (BTE) strategies.

Magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) provide a multifaceted strategy for bone tissue engineering (BTE), improving scaffold characteristics, facilitating stem cell therapies, and introducing innovative treatments for bone malignancies [29]. Iron oxide NPs like Fe3O4 and γ-Fe2O3, possess distinctive magnetic characteristics owing to their superparamagnetic nature. These NPs exhibit catalytic properties and have been formulated into diverse composites for biological purposes, allowing responsiveness to external magnetic fields [30, 31]. Their incorporation into scaffolds is significant for advancing BTE, as they promote osteogenic differentiation and cellular that are needed for bone growth and treating bone disorders. Also, integrating MNPs into polymeric scaffolds makes them more hydrophilic, stiffer, and better at promoting mineral induction, all beneficial for bone regeneration. The creation of magnetic scaffolds through the functionalization of nanoparticle surfaces aims to prevent aggregation and enhance biocompatibility, improving their dispersibility and effectiveness in BTE [32–35]. Innovative approaches are also exploring the incorporation of these NPs into polymer scaffolds or bioactive glasses; however, difficulties remain in enhancing their magnetic properties within biomaterial matrices [36, 37]. In addition, research has shown that magnetic NPs may enhance bioactivity and increase osteoblast proliferation when combined with HAp and polymeric matrices. This highlights the NPs’ potential for use in bone regeneration.

Green synthesis employs biological ingredients like plant extracts, bacteria, fungi, algae, and yeasts as reducing, capping, and stabilizing agents. Plant extracts are favored for nanoparticle production due to their simplicity and scalability compared to bacteria or fungi-mediated synthesis [38, 39]. This approach provides an environmentally safe substitute for traditional physical and chemical processes, addressing concerns about biological risks and environmental degradation. It is also an affordable option that has the potential for large-scale manufacturing [40]. The ability of plants to reduce metal ions and their presence of metal-complexing agents attract the interest of scientists in using plants to synthesize nanoparticles and control their size and shape, particularly for low reduction potential metals [41, 42].

Plant extracts control the morphology and overcome the problem of aggregation during the preparation of a wide range of nanoparticles. Kalaiselvi et al. synthesized Moringa oleifera flower extract-capped hydroxyapatite (MOFE: HAp) powders, using the flower extract directly as a capping agent. This direct approach, avoiding specific phytochemical extraction, likely stemmed from Moringa oleifera’s abundance of polyphenols, alkaloids, tannins, flavonoids, vitamins, minerals, and carotenoids. These antioxidants facilitate the reaction through their reduction abilities and control nanoparticle morphology [43, 44]. Similarly, Gopi et al. used tamarind, grape, and banana fruit extracts directly as green templates for HAp synthesis, aiming to leverage the tartaric acid content, especially the phenolic molecules in grapes, for templating [45]. Plant extracts (Solanum trilobatum, Ziziphora tenuior, Persia Americana, Abutilon indicum, Azadirachta indica, Camellia sinensis, green tea) are used to synthesize Fe3O4 NPs. Current reports also speculate that nanoparticles which are synthesized by using plant extracts are much more stabilized than the conventionally synthesized nanoparticles [46–48].

E. angustifolia is mainly used as a medical plant due to the presence of active phytochemical compounds. Phytochemical elements of the plant include several chemical groups, including phenols, phenyl ethers, phenolic acids, flavonoids, cardiac glycosides, β-carboline alkaloids, pyrimidines, esters, ketones, terpenes, and steroids [49–51]. Furthermore, considering the exceptional antioxidant characteristics of polyphenols, it can be used as an important source for the bioreduction of metallic ions and producing the nanoparticle [52–54].

This study endeavors to expand green chemistry methodologies for the synthesis of nanomaterials. We develop and characterize a hydroxyapatite-functionalized Nd2O3/Fe3O4 nanocomposite using Elaeagnus angustifolia L. seed extract, focusing on its potential to enhance stem cell differentiation for bone tissue engineering applications. Different characterization techniques will be employed to analyze its structural and chemical properties. In vitro investigations will evaluate the impact of the nanocomposite on stem cell proliferation and differentiation. Ultimately, this work seeks to elucidate the potential of materials obtained from Elaeagnus angustifolia in regenerative medicine, contributing to innovative solutions for bone repair and addressing current limitations in bone defect treatments.

Experimental

All compounds used in this investigation were of analytical grade. Oleaster seeds were collected from the Tabriz University campus, ferrous chloride tetrahydrate (FeCl3.4H2O) and Ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O) were purchased from Merck company. Nd(NO)3.6H2O (Neodymium(III) nitrate hexahydrate) was supplied by the Sigma-Aldrich company, natural CaCl2 obtained from cuttlebone that it was purchased from a local market. Na2HPO4 was also acquired from Merck company. Polycaprolactone (~ 80, Sigma-Aldrich), The as-synthesized samples were heated in an oven of Behdad-model code 3491 BC (Behdad Co., Iran). The as-synthesized samples were calcinated at 400 °C by a furnace of Kawoosh Dian Azema-model: KDA-C12 (Medical Kawoosh mega Co., Iran).

Preparation of plant seed extract

Oleaster seed extract was prepared by separating the seeds from the fruits, washing them with deionized water (DI), and drying them at ambient temperature. The dried seeds were pulverized using a grinder. 50 g of seed powder was added to 800 mL of DI in a glass bottle and placed on a shaker for 48 h at 100 rpm and then transferred to an ultrasonic bath for about 20 min. After filtering and centrifuging the mixture for 10 min at 4000 rpm, the resulting extract was stored at 4 °C for further experiments.

Green synthesis of Fe3O4 and Fe3O4/Nd2O3

30 mL of DI was combined with 1.87 g of FeCl₂0.4 H₂O and 5 g of FeCl₃0.6 H₂O (Fe³⁺:Fe2⁺ at a molar ratio of 2:1) and swirled for 30 min at room temperature to create Fe₃O₄ MNPs. Next, 20 mL of seed extract was added. The combination was then sonicated for ten minutes in an ultrasonic bath. To achieve pH 11, 2 mL of 4 M NaOH was gradually added to the mixture. The reaction solution was refluxed under argon gas for two hours at 70 °C. The resulting solution was then transferred to an autoclave (180 °C, 6 h) to complete the synthesis and then gradually cooled to room temperature. A Magnet was used to gather the black Magnetic precipitate, which was then dried at 70 °C for 24 h [55]. Fe3O4/Nd2O3 nanoparticles were synthesized using a method similar to the green synthesis of Fe3O4 Nanoparticles. Briefly, 4.8 g of FeCl3.6H2O, 1.7 g of FeCl2.4H2O, and 0.78 g of Nd(NO3)3.6H2O were added to 30 mL of DI water under stirring. The mixture, after adjusting pH and refluxing under argon gas, was added to an autoclave (180 °C, 6 h). Finally, a magnet was used to gather the as-synthesised Fe3O₄/Nd2O₃ precipitate, which was then dried at 70 °C for 24 h.

Extraction of CaCl2 of cuttlebone

At first, to obtain the calcium carbonate from the cuttlebone, 5 g of cuttlebone was powdered by an electric grinder. After washing, about 10 mL of DI was added to the prepared powder and boiled for 60 min. Then it was dried at 60 °C for about 100 min, followed by 2 h of calcination in the oven at 400 °C. The powder was washed 3 times with DI and 10 mL of methanol to remove Lipid, carbohydrate, and protein traces. Then, it was transferred to an oven at 60 °C for 7 h, and thus the calcium carbonate powder was obtained.

Concentrated hydrochloric acid (10 mL) was gradually added to calcium carbonate powder under heat. Chlorine was replaced by carbonate, producing carbonic acid (H2CO3), which is not stable and decomposes into CO2 and H2O. The resulting precipitate (CaCl2) was washed with DI and allowed to dry at 70 °C.

Preparation of Fe3O4@HAp and Nd-Fe3O4@HAp

We prepared the core-shell structure using a 5% molar ratio of HAp. First, 1 g of Fe3O4 NPs was added to 10 mL of DI water and sonicated for 10 min until fully dispersed. Next, 0.022 g of CaCl2 was included into the Fe3O4 dispersion while Maintaining strong agitation. Then, 0.017 g of Na2HPO4 gradually add to the mixture. Adjust the pH to 10 by adding 4 M NaOH dropwise.The resulting black suspension was transferred to an autoclave, sealed, and heated at 140 °C for 2 h. After cooling to room temperature, the precipitate was separated by centrifugation (12000 rpm for 10 min), washed four times with DI water and ethanol, and freeze-dried for 3 h [56].

Nd-Fe3O4@HAp nanocomposites were synthesized using the same method. 1 g of Nd-Fe3O4 NPs in DI water was dispersed by sonication. Added 0.022 g CaCl2 to the dispersion while stirring vigorously. 0.017 g Na2HPO4 was added, and the pH was adjusted to 10. Transfer the black suspension to an autoclave and heat at 140 °C. The suspension was centrifuged, washed, and then freeze-dried.

Collagen extraction from rat tail

Type I collagen was extracted from rat tails using the reported method with some modifications [57]. Imprimis, the rat tails were washed by soaking in DI followed by disinfection with NaCl solution (9%). Then their skin was broken apart. At that point, raw tendons were exposed and separated by using a tweezer. The isolated tendons were collected in PBS solution (pH: 7.4), and after that, dissolved in 0.02 N acetic acid at a w/v ratio of 1:40 over one night at 4 °C. The obtained viscous mixture solution was precipitated with 5 mL of NaCl (2 M) solution and centrifuged to discard the supernatant. To gain purified collagen, this process was repeated. The resultant precipitate was then collected and dried for 15 h at −20 °C in a freeze dryer.

Electrospinning process

Polymeric solutions of polycaprolactone (PCL) and collagen (COL) in a 4:1 ratio were prepared using glacial acetic acid as a solvent at 25% w/v. First, 1.2 g of PCL was dissolved in 6 mL of acetic acid while stirring overnight. Subsequently, 0.3 g of collagen type I was included, and the mixture was agitated for an additional hour. Subsequently, four types of nanoparticles (Fe₃O₄, Nd-Fe3O₄, Fe₃O₄@HAp, and Nd-Fe3O₄@HAp) were dispersed using a probe sonicator and incorporated into the PCL/COL solutions at 3% wt. Finally, the solutions containing nanomaterials were subjected to ultrasonic treatment in a water bath for about 15 min to ensure complete dispersion.

To prepare magnetic nanofibers using the electrospinning method, the viscous solutions (PCL/Col with and without magnetic nanomaterials) were filled into a syringe pump with a metal needle (internal diameter = 0.4 mm). Then it was transferred to an electrospinning machine (nanofanavaran, Iran) and connected to a high-voltage power source (25.5 kV). To get a nanofiber structure, the injection rate was set at 0.7 mL/h, and the aluminized collector was turned at 350 rpm. Nozzle and collector distance Maintained at 16 cm.

The electrospun nanofibers were collected on aluminum foil and formed a thin membrane. Before subsequent investigations, all solvent traces were eliminated from the scaffolds through vacuum drying.

Characterization of nanofiber scaffolds

The phase analysis was carried out by X-ray diffractometer (XRD Tongda TD-3700, Siemens Diffractometer-China) over the range 10–80° 2θ, with a step size of 0.02° and a 60 s count time. Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM LEO 906 Zeiss, Germany) was used to visualize the smallest structures of nanocomposites in matter. FESEM equipped with EDX (MIRA3 FEG-SEM, Czech Republic) was used for the evaluation of the morphological properties of nanocomposites. This microscope has an LVSTD (Low Vacuum Secondary Electron Tescan Detector) at a voltage of 15 kV. DLS (Nanotrac Wave, Microtrac Co. USA) was applied to quantify the hydrodynamic diameter, size distribution, and zeta potential of samples. DW was used as a dispersant agent, and the pH of it was adjusted to pH 5.7, and a scattering angle of 90° was used for DLS evaluations at k = 632.8 nm. Before measurement, the samples were ultrasonicated with a probe sonicator (MISONIX Sonicator XL2020, USA). To investigate the chemical composition of the samples, FTIR spectra were recorded using the KBr pallet on a Tensor-Bruker27, Germany, spectrometer over the range 400–4000 cm−1. The magnetic properties of all the samples were investigated using a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM; Lakeshore Cryotronics, Westerville, OH, USA) at room temperature.

Evaluation of ADMSCs attachment, proliferation and differentiation by SEM techniques

In this study, mesenchymal stem cells isolated from the abdominal adipose tissue of male Wistar rats (250–300 g, 6–8 weeks old) following previously described method [58]. After anesthetizing the rats with xylazine (10 mg/kg) and ketamine (50 mg/kg), adipose tissue was excised, washed with PBS containing 5% Pen-Strep, minced, and digested with collagenase type I (1 mg/mL) at 37 °C for 70 min. The digest was filtered, centrifuged, and the pellet containing adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADMSCs) was re-suspended in DMEM complete medium. The cells were incubated in a T25 flask in 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Flow cytometry analyzed surface markers (CD29, CD105, CD45, CD34) and multilineage differentiation potential of ADMSCs was assessed using Oil Red O, Alcian blue, and Alizarin Red S staining at P3. ADMSCs were cultivated in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Media (DMEM, Gibco) in this investigation. The media was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 2 mM L-glutamine. NaHCO₃ was used to bring the medium’s pH down to 7.2 to 7.4 (Gibco provided all of the chemicals). To cell adhesion, composite nanofibers were cut into 1 cm-diameter circular scaffolds, sterilized by UV Light for 30 min on each side, and immersed in cell culture media for the whole night before cell seeding. In order to promote cell proliferation, the ADMSCs were sown on the scaffolds at a density of 5 × 10³ cells/cm² and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. Cell-scaffold constructs were cultivated in standard culture medium (Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum). Tissue culture polystyrene (TCP) was utilized as the control. Cells were chemically fixed for each sample in order to assess cell adhesion to the composite nanofibrous scaffolds. The scaffolds were incubated for 14 and 21 days, then rinsed twice with PBS and fixed for an hour in 2.5% glutaraldehyde. Scaffolds were dehydrated in 50, 70, 90, and 100% ethanol solutions after the elimination of glutaraldehyde. In order to study the cell morphology using SEM, the samples were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold after being stored in a vacuum chamber.

MTT assay

The viability of cultured ADMSCs was assessed using the colorimetric 3-(4,5-dimethyl-2-thiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyl tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. In this process, scaffolds were positioned at the base of 96-well culture plates (SPL, Korea). A total of 0.5 × 104 cells were seeded onto the scaffolds and cultured for durations of 3, 5, and 7 days. Following the incubation period, the media in each well were removed. Subsequently, 150 µL of complete media and 50 µL of MTT solution were added to each well and incubated for 4 hours. After the incubation, the MTT solution was discarded, and 200 µL of DMSO was added to each well to stop the reaction and dissolve the purple formazan crystals. Finally, 200 µL from each well was transferred to a new 96-well plate, where the number of viable cells was quantified using an ELISA reader at a wavelength of 570 nm.

Alkaline phosphatase activity assay

An alkaline phosphatase activity (ALP) test was used to assess the scaffolds’ biomineralization capacity. To synthesize p-nitrophenol (PNP), osteoblast cells generate alkaline phosphatase, an enzyme that converts p-nitrophenyl phosphate into phosphate. Following the specified amount of time for cell culture, the ALP kit from Pars-Azmoon in Iran was used to measure the enzyme’s activity by measuring the Light absorbance of the yellow PNP at 405 wavelengths with a microplate reader.

Alizarin red staining assay

Calcium mineral deposition on scaffolds and TCP by ADMSCs during osteogenic differentiation was assessed via a calcium content test. After a 10-minute fixation in 1% paraformaldehyde, ADMSCs were rinsed with distilled water. Scaffolds were then stained with 2% Alizarin red at pH 4.2 for 45 min. Following a 30-minute incubation and two rinses, the staining results were analyzed using a light microscope (Olympus, Japan) to evaluate mineral deposition during the 2nd and 3rd weeks of culture.

Western blotting

The principal proteins involved in the regulation of osteogenesis were examined using Western blotting. Cell scaffolds were lysed, and the resulting cell lysates underwent ultrasonication followed by centrifugation. The total protein concentration was measured using Nanodrop®. After running protein extracts via 10% SDS-PAGE electrophoresis, nitrocellulose membranes were applied to each sample. Membranes were blocked with TBS and 5% non-fat dry milk for 2 h, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies targeting Runx2, β-actin, osteonectin, BMP-2, and osteocalcin. After that, a secondary antibody coupled with horseradish peroxidase was allowed to incubate the samples for an hour at ambient temperature. Ultimately, immunoreactive bands were found using the BioRad ECL reagent. A version of Image J (1.4) was utilized to compute the density of each band.

Statistical analysis

All data were expressed as the means ± standard deviation. Statistical comparisons were performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant (n = 3).

Result and discussion

Characterization of the synthesized particels

The X-ray diffraction patterns of all the synthesized samples over the range of 10–80° are shown in Fig. 1. The diffractions of crystalline structures of the pure Fe3O4 MNPs were observed at 2θ 18.3°, 30/1°, 35/5°, 37/08°, 43/2°, 57/1°, and 62/7° and indexed to their (hkl) planes (111), (220), (311), (400), (422) (511) (440), respectively. These diffractions are attributed to the standard XRD cubic phase of Fe3O4 (ICSD 20–596). The same indicator peaks also were revealed in the XRD patterns of Nd-Fe3O4 MNPs. The peaks related to the Nd phase appeared in the range of 30.6°, 53.3°, 57.5° and 64°, which is consistent with the standard card (JCPDS No.43_1023) related to the (011), (103), (201) and (202) diffraction planes, respectively [59]. The peaks related to the crystalline structure of Fe3O4 MNPs at regions of 30.1° and 43.2° have disappeared in the XRD pattern of Fe3O4@HAp, which could be due to the presence of HAp shell around MNPs, although the peaks related to HAp structure at the areas of 26.2°, 39.9° and 0.64° has seemed correspond to the (002), (310) and (304) diffraction planes, respectively, according to the standard card (JCPDS; 1998-84) [60]. However, owing to the low amount of HAp in the composition of the nanocomposites, the intensity of those peaks is low. These points can confirm the addition of the HAp shell to the Fe3O4 structure. In the XRD pattern of Nd-Fe3O4@HAp nanocomposites, it is still observed that the main peaks belong to pure Fe3O4 and the peak at 39.9° belongs to the HAp phase. The peak corresponding to Nd in the region 53.3° has disappeared, but the peak of 30.6° is still visible and sharper, which is not observed in the Fe3O4@HAp diffractogram and it could be a result of the presence of the Nd phase.

XRD pattern of Fe3O4, Nd-Fe3O4, Fe3O4@HAp and Nd-Fe3O4@HAp

The FTIR spectra of all of the NPs are shown in Fig. 2. Owing to the presence of the Fe3O4 structure, the two distinctive bands in the regions of 565–585 cm−¹ and 3400–3420 cm−¹ that correspond to Fe-O stretching bonds and (O-H) groups, respectively, are visible in the spectra of all samples. The peaks around 2800–2900 cm−1 and 1421–1655 cm−1 are attributed to C-H bonds and carbonyl groups, respectively. These can refer to the presence of secondary metabolites in the seed extract [61]. In the Nd-Fe3O4 spectrum, the peak observed at the wave number of 438 cm−¹, which is related to the Nd-O stretching bond, indicates the addition of Nd to the Fe3O4 MNPs structure [62]. A characteristic peak has appeared in the spectrum of Fe3O4@HAp in the region of 962–963 cm−¹, which is attributed to the P-O stretching bonds in the phosphate (PO43−) group, and it confirmed that the HAp shell is present in the structure of the nanocomposite. In addition, in the Nd-Fe3O4@HAp spectrum, the peaks related to the Fe-O, O-H, and Nd-O stretching bonds in the above-mentioned regions are visible, and the peak that appeared around 1030–1045 cm−1 is related to the P-O bond [56].

TEM micrographs reveal that the green synthesized Nd-Fe3O4@HAp nanoparticles have a spherical shape and a disperse distribution (Fig. 3A), while SEM images reveal some particle agglomeration (Fig. 3B), confirming their overall morphology as nearly spherical with an average diameter of approximately 14–18 nm.

EDX analysis of Nd-Fe3O4 and Nd-Fe3O4@HAp nanocomposites confirmed that the chemical compositions and the mass ratio of the nanocomposites were mostly reliable with the precursor materials. The presence of Nd, O, and Fe elements, as shown in Fig. 4A, indicates the addition of Nd to the Fe3O4 crystalline structure. The chemical composition of the Nd-Fe3O4@HAp nanocomposite was verified by analyzing the amount of O, Fe, Nd, Ca, and P components in Fig. 4B, which were assigned to form the HAp shell. The presence of element C may also be due to secondary metabolites in the plant seed extract. In addition, the corresponding energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy element mappings of Nd-Fe3O4@HAp show the homogeneous distribution of O, Fe, Nd, Ca, and P elements in the entire sample (Fig. 5).

The superparamagnetic response was evident across all the magnetic samples based on the hysteresis loops depicted in Fig. 8. Saturation magnetization of each sample was also mentioned in Table 2. The addition of the neodymium Nd into the Fe3O4 NPs structure has led to an increase in magnetization. This observation can be attributed to the superparamagnetic properties associated with Nd [65]. Moreover, the magnetization of the nanocomposites significantly decreased after the addition of a HAp shell onto the Fe3O4 and Nd-Fe3O4 cores. The observed trend may be explained by the decrease in the weight fraction of Fe3O4 after the addition of non-magnetic components. Also, the HAp shell acts as a non-magnetic coating that can isolate the magnetic cores from each other. This reduces the overall magnetic interactions between the particles. As the HAp shell increases in thickness, the volume fraction of the magnetic core decreases. A smaller core volume leads to reduced overall magnetization [56, 66].

In-vitro biological assays

The MTT assay was conducted to evaluate the cytocompatibility of scaffolds containing nanoparticles. Figure 11 presents the results of the cytocompatibility assay for the scaffolds on days 3, 5, and 7. The non-cytotoxicity and ADMSCs compatibility related to NPs-loaded scaffold are observed compared to the control. All nanocomposite scaffolds induced the proliferation of seeded ADMSCs over time (3–7 days), which confirm the biocompatible nature of these scaffolds for clinical applications. According to results, the PCL/COL/Fe3O4@HAp and PCL/COL/Fe3O4 nanofibrous scaffolds showed higher cell viability values compared to the control and other groups during the initial 3 days (p-value < 0.001, p-value < 0.0001). At day 5, the cell viability showed no significant difference between the nanocomposite scaffolds groups and the control group. On the seventh day, the proliferation rate of composite scaffolds was significantly higher than that of the control group (p-value < 0.0001). Also, compared to the third and fifth days, the cell viability rate was significantly higher, especially for the PCL/COL/Nd-Fe3O4@HAp group. Based on the MTT assay results, it can be concluded that the developed nanocomposite scaffolds exhibit biocompatibility and do not demonstrate toxicity towards the cells. Zeng, Xiao Bo, et al. developed a magnetic nanoparticle/hydroxyapatite (MHA) scaffold for bone repair. MTT assays demonstrated that ROS17/2.8 and MC3T3-E1 cell proliferation increased over time on MHA scaffolds with varying MNP content and HA controls, suggesting good biocompatibility [71].

To investigate the differentiation of adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells into osteogenic cells in the presence of the prepared nanocomposite scaffolds, an Alizarin Red staining assay was performed. Alizarin red interacts with calcium ions to form stable red complexes, indicating that the bone matrix has mineralized. The presence of calcium reflects its critical role in bone formation and stability. The images depicting the color change of Alizarin Red on adipose-derived mesenchymal cells cultured with nanocomposite scaffolds and a control scaffold (without nanocomposites) are shown in Fig. 15A, B for 14 and 21 days of the cell culture process, respectively. The images reveal the formation of red spots on the cells in the presence of scaffolds containing nanomaterials, indicating the presence of calcium and bone mineralization. Notably, the number of red spots on day 21 significantly increased compared to day 14, suggesting enhanced differentiation of osteogenic cells. Additionally, compared to other groups, mineralization was greater in the presence of nanocomposite scaffolds containing HAp shells, demonstrating that HAp has a beneficial influence on bone formation. The quantification of deposited calcium area fraction provides supporting validation for the findings of ARS images. The extent of red area coverage along with the subsequent formation of calcium nodules were observed to be considerably enhanced across all treatment groups in comparison to the control group (p-value < 0.0001).

Osteoblasts synthesize alkaline phosphatase, an enzyme that plays a crucial role in bone regeneration by aiding in the mineralization and formation of bone tissue. High levels of alkaline phosphatase are linked to higher osteoblastic activity, which shows that these cells help make the extracellular matrix and make mineral deposition easier [72]. The osteogenic differentiation of ADMSCs cultured on PCL/COL/Fe3O4, PCL/COL/Fe3O4@HAp, PCL/COL/Nd-Fe3O4, and PCL/COL/Nd-Fe3O4@HAp nanofiber scaffolds was assessed by ALP activity analysis (Fig. 16). The ALP activity was a preliminary Mark of osteogenesis. In all instances, it reached its peak after 14 days of culture. The findings indicated that the integration of HAp and Nd into the PCL/COL scaffold might enhance the ALP activity of ADMSCs. Alkaline phosphatase activity results related to the PCL/COL scaffold were discussed in a previous article [58]. The ALP activity is significantly higher in the PCL/COL/Fe3O4@HAp and PCL/COL/Nd-Fe3O4@HAp groups compared to the PCL/COL/Fe3O4 and PCL/COL/Nd-Fe3O4 groups (p-value < 0.0001). The results exhibited that the addition of HAp and Nd increased the ALP activity and had a positive effect in osteogenesis. Furthermore, the positive impact of hydroxyapatite and neodymium on osteogenesis indicates its potential as a beneficial additive in bone regeneration and repair strategies.

Krishnamoorthi et al. found that HAP-decorated MNP (MHAP) promote bone differentiation by modulating intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, which affects enzyme activity and encourages calcium deposition. MHAP NPs significantly increased ALP activity compared to controls, consistent with early osteoblast development in MC3T3-E1 cells. This aligns with previous research showing that lower ROS levels impact the expression of bone development genes, including ALP [73]. Alizarin red staining revealed considerably higher calcification in all treated concentrations compared to controls. MC3T3-E1 cells cultured with MHAP NPs exhibited greater calcium deposition. A 5 µg/mL MHAP NP treatment for 21 days proved optimal for both ALP activity and calcification, indicating that even low concentrations of MHAP NPs synergistically promote osteogenic differentiation and calcium mineralization for bone tissue reconstruction. This is supported by the known physical and biochemical stimuli of HAP and MNPs, which elicit chromosomal responses regulating the MAPK pathway, leading to upregulation of ALP, BMP2, and Smad proteins. This, in turn, results in RUNX2 expression, an early osteogenic differentiation marker crucial in major signaling pathways promoting osteogenesis [74, 75]. These findings was in line with the results of our study in which HAP-decorated Fe3O4 containing scaffolds promote bone differentiation of ADSCs proved by ALP and Alizarin red staining assays.

Western blot analysis was performed to assess the expression levels of osteoblast lineage markers, including Runx2 (Runt-related transcription factor 2), BMP2 (bone morphogenetic protein 2), OCN (Osteocalcin), and ON (Osteonectin) after 21 days of ADMSCs culturing on nanocomposites scaffolds. The purpose of this investigation was to learn more about the differentiation state of ADMSCs osteoblasts that were exposed to PCL/COL/Fe3O4@HAp and PCL/COL/Fe3O4@HAp nanofibrous scaffolds. It was possible to ascertain the extent of MSC differentiation into osteogenesis by looking at the presence and quantity of these markers, shedding light on the mechanisms underlying their capacity for bone repair and regeneration. The results from the Western blot enhance our understanding of the molecular pathways involved in the osteogenesis process.

The results from the Western blotting assay (Fig. 17) indicated that after a 21-day of ADMSCs incubation period, these scaffolds notably increased the expression levels of RunX2, BMP2, and ON compared to nontreated control group (p-velue < 0.001). Quantitative analysis showed that protein expression levels of all osteoblast lineage markers were significantly higher in the PCL/COL/Nd-Fe3O4 and PCL/COL/Nd-Fe3O4@HAp scaffolds compared to the control group (p-value < 0.0001).

RunX2 is a crucial transcription factor that plays a vital role in the osteogenic differentiation process. It controls the expression of multiple genes related to bone formation and is necessary for the commitment of adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells to the osteoblastic lineage. In both treatment groups, the expression of RunX2 was markedly increased in comparison to its expression in osteoblast cells, which acted as the control (p-value < 0.0001). Furthermore, the RunX2 expression in the PCL/COL/Nd-Fe3O4 group (3.12-fold) was marginally higher than that observed in the PCL/COL/Nd-Fe3O4@HAp group (2.74-fold).

The expression of BMP2, a critical protein involved in the development of mineralized bone tissue as well as the initiation and regulation of osteoblast differentiation, was increased in the PCL/COL/Nd-Fe3O4 (1.51-fold) and PCL/COL/Nd-Fe3O4@HAp (1.30-fold) scaffolds when compared to the control group (p-value < 0.0001).

Osteocalcin, a non-collagenous protein, constitutes a vital component of the extracellular matrix in bone tissue. It functions as a key biomarker in the formation of mineralized tissues, which are essential for preserving the structural integrity and functionality of bone. Additionally, osteocalcin plays a regulatory role in the osteogenic differentiation of adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells, significantly influencing their capacity to differentiate into osteoblasts. The results indicated that the expression levels of osteocalcin in the PCL/COL/Nd-Fe3O4 and PCL/COL/Nd-Fe3O4@HAp groups did not differ significantly from those observed in the control group.

The expression levels of the osteonectin protein were significantly elevated in all experimental groups in comparison to the control group (p-value < 0.0001). The expression levels of ON protein increased 10.45-fold for PCL/COL/Nd-Fe3O4 and 17.82-fold for PCL/COL/Nd-Fe3O4@HAp compared to the control group. This enhancement indicates that the both treatment groups effectively facilitated the synthesis of osteonectin, a critical glycoprotein involved in the processes of bone remodeling and mineralization.

Marycz et al. developed an nHAp-based composite co-doped with iron oxide nanoparticles that acts as an intelligent biomaterial for guided regeneration of osteoporotic bone. Western blot analysis revealed that the composite increased intracellular RUNX-2 and osteopontin expression, promoting functional mineralized matrix formation. Exposure to a magnetic field further modulated RUNX-2 and OPN levels, reflecting in vitro osteogenesis dynamics. These findings suggest this composite’s potential for in situ guided regeneration of osteoporotic bone by modulating osteoblast and osteoclast activity [76].

Conclusion

This study synthesized magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles and neodymium-doped iron oxide nanoparticles using an eco-friendly, simple, and cost-effective green synthesis method against chemical methods with Elyagnus angustifolia L. seed extract. Magnetic core-shell Fe3O4@HAp and Nd-Fe3O4@HAp were synthesized using a novel method that combines co-precipitation and hydrothermal treatment with an average size of 23.94 nm. All the samples show typical superparamagnetic behavior at room temperature. NP-loaded PCL/COL electrospun nanofibers with uniform, bead-free morphologies and average diameters ranging from 110 to 150 nm were successfully fabricated. The electrospun nanofiber scaffolds showed superparamagnetism, with notably lower magnetization than the nanoparticles due to the presence of non-magnetic components. NP-loaded electrospun scaffolds exhibited enhanced mechanical properties and hydrophilicity compared to PCL/COL scaffold, owing to the uniform distribution of nanofillers within the polymer matrix. NPs- PCL/COL scaffolds showed better osteoconductivity and mineralization as compared to neat PCL/COL scaffolds owing to bioactivity of NPs, surface morphology, and improved hydrophilicity. These biocompatible NP-loaded scaffolds can induce the osteogenic differentiation of ADMSCs by creating a conducive microenvironment for osteogenesis. The effect was originally shown by markedly increased osteogenic markers, including ALP activity, calcium accumulation, and significantly enhanced the expression of proteins associated with osteogenesis, including BMP2, Runx2, OCN, and ON compared to control group (p-value < 0.0001). Our findings suggest that prepared electrospun nanofiber scaffolds have significant potential for the osteogenic differentiation of ADMSCs, making them suitable candidates for bone tissue engineering applications.
