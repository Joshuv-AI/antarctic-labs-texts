Ophthalmological findings in Brazilian Cornelia de Lange syndrome patients with NIPBL variants
Authors: Thainá Vilella, Beatriz Carvalho Nunes, Giulia Steuernagel Del Valle, Isabel Furquim Pinheiro, Hiromi Aoi, Takeshi Mizuguchi, Rie Seyama, Yuri Uchiyama, Naomichi Matsumoto, Chong Ae Kim, Juliana Maria Ferraz Sallum, Maria Isabel Melaragno, Priscila Cardoso Cristovam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeCornelia de Lange Syndrome (CdLS) is a rare genetic disorder that affects multiple organs, including the eyes. Ophthalmological findings in individuals with CdLS can vary, as occurs with certain features commonly associated with the syndrome, whose presence and severity can differ among patients. In this exploratory cross-sectional study, we aimed to report the ophthalmological phenotypes in a Brazilian CdLS cohort and evaluated possible genotype-phenotype associations.MethodsSixteen individuals with Cornelia de Lange Syndrome participated in this study. Only patients who had pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in the NIPBL gene were included. Ophthalmological exams were conducted to investigate alterations in CdLS patients according to tolerance. The ophthalmic assessment comprised measurement of facial distances, ectoscopy, visual acuity, pupillary reaction, extrinsic ocular motility, slit lamp exam, tonometry, refraction under cycloplegia, and dilated fundus exam.ResultsOcular anomalies were identified in all CdLS patients, including abnormalities of the eyebrows, eyelashes, palpebral fissures, and anterior and posterior segments, as well as refractive errors, strabismus, and nystagmus. Although some phenotypes were exclusively present in patients with specific NIPBL variant types, no statistical significance was identified.ConclusionsThe ophthalmological findings in patients with CdLS are diverse and often significantly impact vision and quality of life. We recommend regular ophthalmological exams from the time of diagnosis to enable detection of treatable eye conditions, including refractive errors, ptosis, dry eyes, and strabismus, as early detection is essential for appropriate interventions and the prevention of long-term visual complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-025-04401-4.

Introduction

Cornelia de Lange Syndrome (CdLS; OMIM #122470, #300590, #610759, #614701, #300882, #608749, and #611192) is a rare genetic disorder that received this name due to the first description of two children with the disease by the Dutch pediatrician Cornelia de Lange. The prevalence of CdLS is estimated to be between 1 in 10,000 and 1 in 30,000 live births [1, 2].

CdLS patients present several main clinical features, such as a distinct craniofacial appearance, pre- and post-natal growth retardation, intellectual disability, developmental delay, behavioral issues, and limb anomalies with variable expressivity [2, 3]. To date, CdLS is classified as a spectrum, and the clinical diagnosis is defined according to a combination of ‘cardinal’ and ‘suggestive’ features, to which specific scores are assigned [3]. Based on the final score given to the patient, they may be classified as having a ‘classic’ or ‘non-classic’ CdLS phenotype.

Variants in seven genes associated with the cohesin complex have been reported in CdLS patients: NIPBL, SMC1A, SMC3, RAD21, HDAC8, BRD4, and ANKRD11 [3–5]. Cohesin is a chromatin-associated multi-subunit protein complex involved in several aspects of chromosome biology and transcriptional regulation, such as chromatid cohesion, DNA recombinational repair, and correct chromosome segregation upon cell division. Thus, CdLS is a cohesinopathy [6, 7].

Variants in the NIPBL gene are the most frequent in CdLS, accounting for approximately 70% of cases, while variants in the other six genes are reported in approximately 10% of CdLS cases [3]. In some cases, the gene variant cannot be identified in peripheral blood but can be found in other tissues such as buccal cells or fibroblasts [8]. It is estimated that 15–20% of all NIPBL variants are present as mosaics [3, 7, 9–12]. Patients with mosaic variants present phenotypes as severe as those with germline variants, making both groups indistinguishable from a clinical perspective [13, 14].

The ophthalmological aspects of CdLS are not well defined, as few studies focus on these phenotypes. However, synophrys, thick eyebrows, and long eyelashes are universally observed, regardless of the affected gene, thus forming one of the syndrome’s facial hallmarks. Previous studies reported cases of CdLS patients with a variant in the NIPBL gene who, in addition to the facial hallmarks, also presented hypertelorism, telecanthus, myopia, nasolacrimal duct obstructions (NLDO), ptosis, strabismus, nystagmus, retinal detachments, retinal pigmentary changes, cupping and tilted optic discs, cataract, microcornea, corneal scarring secondary to ulcer, and congenital glaucoma [15–18].

Ophthalmological evaluations are crucial for patients with CdLS due to the significant phenotypic variability among individuals. These examinations can characterize the overall phenotype and allow for early diagnosis and treatment of conditions that can lead to blindness or vision impairment. Additionally, few studies explore the potential link between genetic variants and ophthalmological changes in CdLS patients. Elucidating the ophthalmological comorbidities in patients with CdLS and its association with specific variant types may lead to more effective treatment, as early intervention can improve ocular function and prevent vision loss, for example, due to amblyopia.

Methods

This exploratory cross-sectional study aimed to evaluate the association between different NIPBL variants and ophthalmological alterations in CdLS. It represents the first investigation into the ophthalmological aspects of CdLS in Brazilian patients and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Federal University of São Paulo/UNIFESP (CAAE nº 53275421.0.0000.5505; CEP/UNIFESP nº 1300/2021). Written informed consents were obtained from the legal guardians of patients, who were previously informed about the nature of the study and possible consequences. All individuals in this study were evaluated by clinical geneticists following the criteria established by Kline et al. (2018) [3] at the Instituto da Criança, Hospital das Clíínicas, Faculdade de Medicinada Universidade de São Paulo (ICr-FMUSP), also located in São Paulo, Brazil. Only patients with known variants in the NIPBL gene were recruited for the ophthalmological research.

Molecular diagnosis

Multiple tests were conducted to screen for pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants that could explain the patients’ phenotypes: (1) whole-exome sequencing (WES) from DNA extracted from peripheral blood was performed for all patients, (2) RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) from total RNA isolated from lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCLs) derived from each patient was performed for patients that remained undiagnosed after trio-based WES from peripheral blood, and (3) WES from DNA extracted from buccal cells was performed to investigate mosaicism in cases that were still undiagnosed after WES from peripheral blood and RNA-Seq from LCLs. WES from peripheral blood and RNA-Seq from LCLs were conducted as described by Aoi et al. (2019) [9] and Seyama et al. (2022) [19], respectively. WES from buccal cells was performed with paired-end libraries on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), with 80x-110x coverage. Reads were trimmed with Cutadapt [20] and aligned to the reference genome hg19 with BWA [21]. Variants were called by the Genome Analysis Tool Kit (GATK) [22, 23] and annotated and inspected using Franklin by Genoox (https://franklin.genoox.com). Variants were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics (ACMG)/the Association for Molecular Pathology criteria [24], including the updates by the Human Gene Mutation Database Professional (https://my.qiagendigitalinsights.com/bbp/view/hgmd/pro/start.php) and the ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation (SVI) Working Group (https://clinicalgenome.org/working-groups/sequence-variant-interpretation/). Sanger Sequencing confirmed all variants.

Eye exam and vision testing basics

After CdLS diagnosis by molecular testing, the patients were evaluated by a pediatric ophthalmologist at the Department of Ophthalmology and Visual Sciences/Paulista School of Medicine, Universidade Federal de São Paulo. Each participant had an ophthalmological assessment according to tolerance, including measurements of facial distances, ectoscopy, visual acuity, pupillary reaction, extrinsic ocular motility, slit lamp exam, tonometry, refraction under cycloplegia, and dilated fundus exam.

Visual acuity was assessed by observing the fixation with E-game or LEA symbols, depending on the patient’s cognitive ability. Oculofacial measurements of outer and inner canthal distance (OCD and ICD, respectively, in mm) were taken using an acrylic ruler. The measurements were plotted against the normal standards for 14-year-old or younger children developed by Feingold and Bossert (1974) [25], as recommended by Wygnanski-Jaffe et al. (2005) [17]. Patients over 14 years old did not participate in the plot as there is no standard in the literature for their normal OCD and ICD measurements [17].

The Interpupillary Distance (IPD) values ​​were also plotted against the normal standards for 14-year-old or younger children, and, in addition, IPD calculation was also performed according to Feingold and Bossert (1974), as recommended by Wygnanski-Jaffe et al. (2005) [17], following the formula: IPD = 0.7 + (0.59 x ICD) + (0.41 x OCD). ICD, OCD, palpebral fissure length, and corneal diameter were evaluated.

Through ectoscopy, the presence of synophrys, arched and broad eyebrows, long and curly eyelashes, dermatochalasis, ptosis, and epicanthus was assessed. Regarding the palpebral fissure, the obliquity of the fissure was defined as negative (downslanted) in patients in whom the lateral corner was below the medial corner, and positive (upslanted) when the lateral corner was above the medial corner.

Slit lamp examination was performed with a fixed or portable device, according to the patient’s tolerance. The examination included ocular surface evaluation using 1% fluorescein eye drops to calculate the Tear Film Break Up Time (TBUT). A TBUT measurement of less than 10 s was considered suggestive of Dry Eye Disease, according to DEWS II criteria [26]. When examination was not possible, the anterior ocular evaluation segment was performed by magnification and illumination using indirect ophthalmoscopy. In tolerant patients, tonometry was also performed with a Goldmann applanation tonometer, using topical anesthesia with 0.5% proparacaine. Due to the unavailability of a rebound tonometer, tonometry was not performed when Goldmann applanation was not possible.

The assessment of extrinsic ocular motility and strabismus included a Hirschberg or cover test, depending on the patient’s visual acuity, and evaluation of versions. Pupillary dilation was achieved using 1% cyclopentolate eye drops associated with 1% tropicamide in patients up to 30 years old and with 1% tropicamide in patients over 30 years old. Cycloplegic refraction was performed objectively with a retinoscope and lens interposition thirty minutes after the eyedrops were instilled. High myopia was defined as a spherical equivalent of −5,0 diopters or less. Fundus exam was performed with a binocular indirect ophthalmoscope, which could be associated with fundus biomicroscopy for better macular and optic disc assessment, when necessary.

The procedures to which the patients were subjected did not cause pain, but only local discomfort due to the eye drops used. No patient was sedated to perform the exams.

Statistics analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in RStudio (version 2023.12.0) with R version 4.2.3. Fisher’s exact test was applied to analyze associations between categorical independent variables and the outcome variables, and Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons correction. P ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Boxplots were used to visually explore the relationships between age and the phenotypes when each phenotype level had three or more occurrences.

Results

Sixteen CdLS patients aged 5 to 44 years (17.5 ± 12) were enrolled in this study, of whom seven were female (43.8%). Eleven patients were diagnosed with WES from peripheral blood (68.8%), two patients (P13 and P16) were diagnosed with RNA-Seq from peripheral blood (12.5%), and three patients (P1, P2, and P9) had mosaic variants that could only be identified by WES from buccal cells (18.8%). All 16 patients had NIPBL pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants: five of them had splicing variants (31.2%), four had frameshift variants (25%), four had missense variants (25%), and three had nonsense variants (18.8%). Ophthalmological findings

All 16 patients evaluated exhibited ocular anomalies. All ophthalmological findings are reported in Supplementary Table S1. The most frequent phenotypes were synophrys (100%), arched and broad eyebrows (94%), long (94%) and curly (88%) eyelashes. Thick eyebrows were also observed, as well as changes in the anterior and posterior segments and ocular motility.

Regarding ocular motility, strabismus was present in six (37.5%) probands, with exotropia observed in four (25%) patients and esotropia in two (12.5%). Nystagmus was present in three (18.8%) of the patients. We analyzed the ocular versions (coordinated, simultaneous movements of both eyes in the same direction) of fifteen patients, among whom twelve (80%) had normal versions, and three (20%) had abnormal versions. Specifically, one patient exhibited elevation deficit in both eyes, one had superior oblique muscle overaction in both eyes, and one had impaired ocular abduction in both eyes.

In the anterior segment, iris atrophy was observed in nine patients (56.2%) (Fig. 1), inferior punctate keratitis was observed in four subjects (25%), and blepharitis in two patients (12.5%). No lens abnormalities were observed. Many patients displayed sudden and intense behavioral shifts in response to light or touch near their eyes, which limited our ability to conduct the TBUT measurements in only eight patients (50%). Seven (87.5%) had a TBUT of less than 10 s (7.375 ± 2.09), suggesting an abnormal tear film. Unique findings, each occurring in a single case (6.2%), comprised epicanthus, dermatochalasis, microcornea, and one patient had bilateral NLDO duct obstruction.

In the posterior segment, eleven patients (68.75%) had an unremarkable fundus exam. Notable findings included temporal optic disc pallor in two subjects (12.50%) and optic nerve hypoplasia in one (6.25%). Additionally, a peripapillary pigment ring was observed in two probands (12.50%), and posterior staphyloma was present in two patients (12.50%), both high myopes. Unique findings, each occurring in a single case (6.25%), comprised a bilateral blunted foveal reflex, peripheral pigmentary alterations, and one case of unilateral myelinated retinal nerve fibers. Due to a lack of patient cooperation, multimodal imaging acquisition, including Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) and retinography, was not possible.

Visual acuity was measured in six patients, and five of them (83.3%) had impaired vision (≤ 0.7 on the decimal scale). Among the patients with impaired vision, 2/5 (40%) presented visual acuity ≥ 1 with correction, characterizing normal vision, and 1/5 (20%) continued to have impaired vision even with correction. It was not possible to evaluate visual acuity with correction in 2/5 (40%) subjects.

Regarding the refractive error, myopia was observed in twelve (75%) subjects, with high myopia (≤−5.0 D) present in three (18.8%). Hyperopia was present in three patients (18.8%). Spherical equivalent refraction ranged from − 16.75 to + 2.75 D (−3.71 ± 6.38). Cylinder refraction ranged from − 0.50 to −4.50 D, and an astigmatism of 2.5 D or greater was present in eight (50%) subjects.

We were able to obtain OCD and ICD values for all 16 study patients; nine of them were 14 years old or younger. When plotted against the normal standards for age, 2/9 (22.2%) patients had OCDs below the third percentile; and 1/9 (11.1%) patient’s ICD fell below the third percentile. Regarding IPD, there were no patients classified below the third percentile or above the ninety-seventh percentile in any of the documented manners.

Three probands presented unilateral ptosis, one mild and not affecting the visual axis, while the other two had moderate ptosis partially obstructing the visual axis. One patient had bilateral mild ptosis.

Ophthalmological features across different variant types

After adjusting for multiple comparisons, Fisher’s exact test revealed no genotype-phenotype significant associations (Supplementary Table S2). However, some phenotypes were variant-specific. Strabismus was not reported in patients with nonsense variants. Strabismus with esotropia was only reported in patients with splicing variants, while strabismus with exotropia was only observed in patients with frameshift and missense variants. Abnormal OCD and versions were solely observed in patients with nonsense and frameshift variants. Meanwhile, abnormal ICD, epicanthus, and dermatochalasis were only reported in one patient each (the same patient had both abnormal ICD and epicanthus), both carrying splicing variants. Iris atrophy and hyperopia were absent in patients with frameshift variants, whereas strabismus was not observed in patients with nonsense variants. Downslanted palpebral fissures were exclusively associated with frameshift and nonsense variants, whereas upslanted palpebral fissures were only reported in patients with missense and splicing variants (Fig. 2). Finally, moderate ptosis only occurred in patients with frameshift variants, while mild ptosis was solely reported in those with missense and splicing variants. Fisher’s exact test was also performed to explore associations between the presence of heterozygous or mosaic variants and ophthalmological phenotypes, but no statistically significant associations were found (Supplementary Table S3). However, epicanthus, dermatochalasis, abnormal ICD, strabismus with esotropia, high myopia, hyperopia, ptosis, and upslanted palpebral fissures were not observed in patients carrying mosaic variants (Supplementary Figure S1).

Ophthalmological features across different ages

Nine phenotypes had at least three patients in each level: astigmatism, high myopia, hyperopia, inferior punctate keratitis, myopia, strabismus, strabismus with exotropia, iris atrophy, and versions. Hyperopia and high myopia were only present in three patients each. However, the mean age of the affected patients (8.3 ± 1.5 for hyperopia, 11.3 ± 8.5 for high myopia) was considerably lower than that of the unaffected patients (19.6 ± 12.4 for hyperopia, 18.9 ± 12.4 for high myopia). Similarly, versions were only reported as abnormal in three patients, who also had a considerably lower mean age (9 ± 1) than patients with normal versions (20.1 ± 12.9). The six patients with strabismus (21.8 ± 17.2) had a higher mean age than the unaffected patients (14.9 ± 7.5). Although the mean age of patients who had (18.3 ± 10.71) or did not have myopia (15.8 ± 15.8) was similar, among the five patients who did not have myopia, only one was more than 10 years old (44 years old). All other phenotypes had similar mean ages between the groups and similar age distributions in the boxplots. These results are described in Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table S4.

Discussion

Cornelia de Lange Syndrome is a rare multisystemic disorder with a vast phenotypic spectrum that also overlaps with several other syndromes, especially among patients with non-classic phenotypes [3]. Studying the phenotypic variability among molecularly diagnosed patients can help to better understand and characterize this phenotypic spectrum, which could help patients and clinicians during the diagnostic odyssey. Few studies in the literature focused on CdLS patients’ ophthalmological phenotypes and their association with the patients’ genotypes, and, to the best of our knowledge, none have been performed on the Latin-American population. In this study, 16 Brazilian CdLS patients with known NIPBL pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants were evaluated by an ophthalmologist.

The main findings, such as synophrys, arched and broad eyebrows, and long and curly eyelashes, are well-known classic features of CdLS and are even used for the clinical diagnosis of patients, as recommended by the first CdLS global consensus [3]. However, other relevant phenotypes were also found in our study. Interestingly, more than half of the patients exhibited iris atrophy. Iris abnormalities have been previously reported in CdLS patients, such as corectopia, partial pupillary membrane, iris thinning, and irregularity of the pupillary annulus minor [28]. Previous studies, including a murine model with neural crest cell-specific inactivation of Nipbl or Mau2, suggest that cohesin dysfunction can compromise late stages of neural crest cell development, such as differentiation and tissue maintenance, without necessarily markedly affecting initial migration. Considering that the neural crest is the embryonic origin of the iris structures, such alterations may result in hypodevelopment or structural degeneration of this tissue [29]. These hypotheses are consistent with observations in other syndromes involving neural crest cell development, such as Axenfeld-Rieger and aniridia, in which iris atrophy is a well-recognized feature, further supporting the notion that disruptions in neural crest-derived ocular structures can lead to structural iris defects [30–35]. Therefore, the iris abnormalities observed in CdLS patients might be associated with cohesin dysfunction due to the presence of pathogenic variants in cohesin-related genes, such as NIPBL. However, the scarcity of studies specifically addressing iris alterations in CdLS underscores the importance of future investigations to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

A peripapillary pigment ring was reported in two patients in our study (12.5%). This is a common ocular finding in CdLS patients and has been reported to be present in up to 83% of cases [17]. Peripheral retinal pigmentary changes were also noticed in one patient with high myopia (<−15.00 SE in both eyes). Although this finding has been previously described to be present in patients with a CdLS diagnosis [18, 36], high myopia itself can cause progressive retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) thinning and attenuation, generating a retinal tessellated appearance which corresponds to the irregular distribution of RPE atrophy [37].

Poor macular reflexes were previously noted in two cases, and we report one case of a blunted foveal reflex, suggesting the possibility of foveal hypoplasia. Unfortunately, the acquisition of OCT imaging was not possible due to a lack of patient cooperation. We reported the first case of unilateral myelinated retinal nerve fibers in a CdLS patient. As we report this finding for the first time, we cannot assert whether it is related to variants in the NIPBL gene.

Although ocular motility and strabismus evaluations have been frequently assessed in CdLS patients, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to report abnormalities in ocular versions. We identified the following phenotypes: double elevator palsy, superior oblique muscle overaction, and impaired ocular abduction, each affecting both eyes of a single patient. The strabismus prevalence of 37.5% in this cohort was similar to what has been reported by Nallasamy et al. (2006) [18] for patients with NIPBL variants (34.6%). These are significantly higher than the strabismus prevalences observed in studies with patients with only a clinical diagnosis: 13.6% in Levin et al. (1990) [36] and 16% in Wygnanski-Jaffe et al. (2005) [17]. Although Wygnanski-Jaffe et al. (2005) [17] noted esotropia as the most frequent type of strabismus in CdLS patients, in our study, exotropia predominated, affecting 66.6% of patients with strabismus. In our cohort, strabismus was systematically assessed by a strabismus specialist, so we believe that the high frequency of exotropia in our cohort is most likely related to the small sample size. Larger studies are needed to determine if this reflects a true difference in phenotype distribution.

The ophthalmological alterations in this study were compared with those of three other major previous studies (Levin et al., 1990; Wygnanski-Jaffe et al., 2005; and Nallasamy et al., 2006) [17, 18, 36]. The first two studies included cohorts with only clinical diagnoses, while the cohort from the Nallasamy et al. (2006) [18] study comprised both molecularly diagnosed patients and those without a molecular diagnosis. Only one patient in our study had NLDO, whereas Levin et al. (1990) [36] and Nallasamy et al. (2006) [18] reported it in more than half of the patients. Ptosis was identified in 25% of our patients, contrasting with the 45% reported by Levin et al. (1990) [36], Wygnanski-Jaffe et al. (2005) [17], and Nallasamy et al. (2006) [18]. Downslanted palpebral fissures were found in 7% of the patients reported in Wygnanski-Jaffe et al. (2005) [17], whereas we reported them in 18.75% of our patients. Blepharitis was found in 25% of the cases by Wygnanski-Jaffe et al. (2005) [17], while we identified it in 12.5% ​​of our cases. Microcornea was reported in 21% of the cases in Wygnanski-Jaffe et al. (2005) [17]. In our study, we only observed it in one patient (6.25%). Staphyloma was reported in one patient by Nallasamy et al. (2006) [18], and in our study, we identified it in two patients (12.5%). Myopia was identified in 68.75% of our cases, similar to the approximately 60% observed by Levin et al. (1990) [36], Wygnanski-Jaffe et al. (2005) [17], and Nallasamy et al. (2006) [18]. Nystagmus was found in 18.75% of our patients, a percentage akin to those reported by Wygnanski-Jaffe et al. (2005) [17] and Nallasamy et al. (2006) [18] (14% and 16.7%, respectively), while Levin et al. (1990) [36] reported it in 36.4% of patients, nearly double that of our study. In our study, we reported four patients with inferior punctate keratitis, which is characterized by small punctate lesions in the inferior region of the cornea. Two of them also had dry eyes, while for the other two, dry eyes were not measured due to the lack of patient cooperation during the examination. We believe that inferior punctate keratitis and dry eyes may be associated, as the “ocular surface damage” in patients with dry eyes can be considered keratitis and a sign of disease worsening [38].

Regarding the genotype-ophthalmological phenotypes association, we did not observe any statistically significant associations; however, we did note some similarities with previous studies, such as severe ptosis being more frequent in patients with LoF variants than in patients with missense variants [18]. Moreover, one patient carrying a nonsense variant had optic disc hypoplasia, a finding previously reported by Nallasamy et al. (2006) [18]. In our study, 37.5% of patients had strabismus. Nallasamy et al. (2006) [18] reported a similar prevalence of strabismus among patients with variants in the NIPBL gene (34.6%), which was lower for those with no NIPBL gene variants (21.4%). Our study showed a higher prevalence of hyperopia in children and adolescents than in older patients. This finding is well-documented within the field of ophthalmology, where it is observed that the prevalence of hyperopia decreases with increasing age [39]. Thus, we note that this pattern is replicated in our cohort of patients with CdLS, as the average age of affected patients was 8.3 years. High myopia also prevailed in the younger patients of our cohort, with an average age of 11.3 years. Ocular morbidity is commonly observed in children and adolescents, often because younger children may be unaware of their symptoms, while older patients are more capable of recognizing and communicating vision issues [40, 41]. We must take into account that some of the parameters evaluated, such as hyperopia and versions, can be considered physiological and independent of age. We speculate that, although many patients with CdLS cannot communicate verbally, their caregivers may, over time, detect certain ocular issues even during childhood. However, some phenotypes may go unnoticed, underscoring the importance of an early ophthalmological evaluation in these patients.

Some ocular findings in CdLS patients can significantly impact vision. Individuals with CdLS may present several treatable eye conditions, including refractive errors, ptosis, dry eyes, and strabismus. We recommend an initial ophthalmological examination at the time of diagnosis and regular follow-ups to identify abnormalities that could benefit from early intervention, preventing vision loss and improving ocular function [15]. The cohesin is essential for cell function, and patients with variants in genes associated with this complex may exhibit various alterations in transcription and cell division, potentially affecting multiple tissues [7]. As far as we know, no studies correlate visual alterations with cohesinopathies. However, Kamel et al. (2022) [42] conducted a study to evaluate the Sgo1 protein in mutant zebrafish with loss-of-function SGO1 variants using CRISPR/Cas9 genome-editing technology. These mutants exhibited phenotypic changes in the eye due to disruption in the photoreceptor layer and reduced sensitivity to the light/dark cycle, as the layer of photoreceptor cells and the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) were compromised in the genetically edited embryos. The SGO1 protein works with the PP2A protein, interacting with the cohesin during cell division, preventing the removal of the complex ring during prophase and thereby avoiding the separation of sister chromatids. Thus, this study demonstrated that, in zebrafish, vision can be affected by variants in a cohesin-related gene. However, there is no guarantee that this will also occur in humans or cohesinopathies associated with other cohesin genes, such as CdLS.

In our study, the lack of cooperation from some patients was the main limiting factor, which prevented the ophthalmologist from evaluating some parameters in a few cases. As many CdLS patients are non-verbal, the visual acuity in some instances was not precise but rather approximate. Moreover, due to the small sample size in our study, the statistical analysis was limited. Even though Fisher’s exact test is a robust approach for small-sized samples, the large number of groups restricted its statistical power and could inflate the Type II error. Similarly, no statistical test could be applied to the age data without inflating the Type II error. However, some tendencies could be observed even in our small sample, and more studies about ophthalmological phenotypes in CdLS patients carrying different types of NIPBL variants should be performed to further elucidate the patients’ phenotypic spectrum.

It is important to note that all our patients were evaluated by the same ophthalmologist, a specialist in pediatric ophthalmology. Therefore, she could apply an excellent approach with the patients, thus achieving the best possible results for each one. Hence, we managed to describe sixteen Brazilian patients with a detailed ophthalmological evaluation, all with confirmed genetic variants in the NIPBL gene, which can better guide clinical conduct and future research.

Conclusion

The ophthalmological findings in patients with CdLS are diverse and often have a significant impact on vision and quality of life. Some findings might be related to the variant type and also appear to be more frequent in specific ages, although no significant associations were found. Understanding the phenotypic differences across patients with variants in the same gene can help improve clinical management and visual outcomes for this population. We recommend regular ophthalmological exams from the time of diagnosis, as early detection is essential for appropriate interventions and the prevention of long-term visual complications.
