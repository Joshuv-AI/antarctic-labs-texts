Background

Acute type A aortic dissection during pregnancy is a rare but life-threatening condition with an incidence of approximately 4 cases per 1 million women aged 15–45 years [1]. Acute aortic dissection during pregnancy is estimated to occur approximately 4–6 cases per 1 million pregnancies [2]. Aortic dissection most commonly develops during the third trimester and the postpartum period. Hormonal fluctuations, gestational hypertension, and hemodynamic and circulating blood volume changes are proven factors contributing to the development of aortic dissection [3, 4]. In addition, genetic syndromes such as Marfan Syndrome (MFS) play a significant role and are associated with an increased risk of aortic dissection [3]. Despite the known association between pregnancy and acute aortic dissection in patients with Marfan syndrome, the implementation of clinical guidelines for prevention, surveillance, and treatment in affected women remains suboptimal.

The present report is noteworthy because it describes the successful management of third-trimester acute type A aortic dissection in a patient with genetically confirmed Marfan syndrome, using a hybrid multidisciplinary approach that combined cesarean delivery, prophylactic uterine artery embolization, and immediate ascending aortic replacement.

Case presentation

A 41-year-old woman (gravida 4, para 3) at 31 weeks of gestation with genetically confirmed Marfan syndrome was admitted with a first episode of acute, severe, sharp retrosternal chest pain lasting four hours, radiating to the neck, accompanied by weakness and dizziness. Screening transthoracic echocardiography revealed dilation of the ascending aorta without significant aortic valve regurgitation, and suprasternal views clearly demonstrated an intimal flap consistent with aortic dissection. CTA confirmed an acute Stanford type A aortic dissection. The aortic root was dilated, measuring 45.9 mm at the level of the sinus of Valsalva. The maximal diameter of the ascending aorta was 32.3 mm. The dissection extended over a length of 9.94 cm within the ascending aorta (Fig. 1).

On admission, the patient was hemodynamically stable: blood pressure 113/61 mmHg, heart rate 86 beats/min with regular rhythm, and no pathological heart murmurs. Lung auscultation revealed bilateral vesicular breath sounds, and oxygen saturation was 96% on room air. Laboratory investigations showed: red blood cells 3.72*10¹²/L(4.2–5.4*10¹²/L), hemoglobin 10.7 g/dL (12.0–16.0 g/dL), hematocrit 32.7% (37–47%), leukocytes 11.92*10⁹/L(4.0–10.0*10⁹/L), and D-dimer 1.53 mg/L(< 0.5 mg/L). Coagulation parameters (PT, APTT, TT, fibrinogen) were within normal limits, and renal function tests were unremarkable. Electrocardiography confirmed sinus rhythm. The diagnosis of Marfan syndrome had been established in 2012 based on both genetic and phenotypic findings according to the revised Ghent nosology. Genetic testing identified a pathogenic FBN1 mutation. Phenotypic manifestations included tall and slender body habitus, scoliosis, chest wall deformity, ectopia lentis, and aortic root dilation measuring approximately 40 mm. The patient also reported a positive family history of acute aortic events. These findings were considered sufficient to establish the diagnosis of Marfan syndrome. She was subsequently followed with serial echocardiographic examinations without consultation with a cardiac surgeon. The most recent echocardiographic assessment, performed three months before the current presentation and pregnancy-related intervention, demonstrated dilation of the ascending aorta at the level of the sinus of Valsalva, measuring approximately 42 mm. No signs of fetal distress were identified before surgery based on standard obstetric evaluation. Comprehensive fetal monitoring, including cardiotocographic assessment, demonstrated reassuring findings with no evidence of fetal compromise. Patient denied use of any medication.

The patient’s condition and associated risks were explained to her, and a multidisciplinary team meeting was convened involving a cardiologist, an emergency medicine physician, an obstetrician-gynecologist, a cardiothoracic surgeon, an anesthesiologist, and an invasive radiologist.

The patient was urgently transferred to a hybrid operating room for cesarean delivery, followed by uterine artery embolization and thoracic aortic repair. Under local anesthesia, bilateral common femoral arterial access was obtained. Through 6 F introducer sheaths, the common iliac arteries were selectively catheterized using a Cobra catheter, and 6 × 40 mm EV3 balloon occlusion catheters were positioned. Rapid sequence intubation was subsequently performed according to the emergency cesarean section protocol. Following delivery, the balloon occlusion catheters were inflated during closure of the uterine incision and anterior abdominal wall to reduce intraoperative blood loss. Both uterine arteries were then superselectively catheterized using a Progreat microcatheter, and embolization with 300–500 μm microparticles was performed until complete angiographic stasis was achieved. Finally, the left common femoral artery was closed using an Angio-Seal vascular closure device (Fig. 2).

(A) Positioning of balloon catheters before cesarean section. (B) Roadmaps before uterine artery embolization. (C) Bilateral uterine artery embolization with microparticles. (D) Stasis in both uterine arteries

Prophylactic uterine artery embolization was performed to reduce the risk of severe perioperative hemorrhage following cesarean delivery, particularly in the context of subsequent full systemic anticoagulation required for cardiopulmonary bypass. Upon completion of uterine artery embolization, the team proceeded without delay to definitive repair of the ascending aorta. The right groin vessels were surgically exposed via cutdown for cardiopulmonary bypass cannulation. A 26 F venous cannula was inserted into the right femoral vein, and a 17 F arterial cannula was placed into the right femoral artery over the previously inserted 6 F introducer sheath. Cardiopulmonary bypass was then initiated. Median sternotomy and pericardiotomy were performed, revealing no significant pericardial effusion. The ascending aorta was cross-clamped proximal to the brachiocephalic trunk and opened longitudinally. The coronary ostia were identified, and Custodiol cardioplegia was administered directly into both ostia. Intraoperative inspection revealed an intimal tear located approximately 0.5 cm from the right coronary artery ostium. The ascending aorta was resected proximally to the sinotubular junction, fully encompassing the tear. Complete dissection of the non-coronary sinus and partial involvement of the right coronary sinus were observed and reinforced using Teflon felt interposition. In the absence of preoperative imaging or intraoperative evidence of aortic arch involvement on computed tomography angiography, and to minimize procedural risk, systemic hypothermia and arch intervention were not performed. Reinforcement of approximately half of the distal aortic circumference was achieved using Teflon felt. Supracoronary replacement of the ascending aorta was then performed using a 28-mm Hemashield Platinum straight graft (length 7 cm). Following removal of the aortic cross-clamp, spontaneous cardiac activity resumed. After meticulous hemostasis was achieved, the chest was closed in standard fashion. The patient was subsequently transferred to the intensive care unit for postoperative monitoring and management.

The patient spent the first two postoperative days in the cardiothoracic intensive care unit and was subsequently transferred to the cardiothoracic surgery ward, where gradual mobilization was initiated. Persistently elevated inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein 251.21 mg/L, procalcitonin 0.24 ng/mL) and low-grade fever (37.8 °C) were observed. After consultation with infectious disease specialists, blood cultures were obtained and were negative. Empirical antibiotic therapy with vancomycin and piperacillin–tazobactam was initiated and discontinued after 11 days. The patient demonstrated gradual clinical improvement, with declining inflammatory markers (CRP 57.48 mg/L, procalcitonin 0.05 ng/mL). A follow-up CTA of the aorta was performed, showing a satisfactory surgical result with residual dissection in the aortic arch and the proximal descending aorta. The patient and newborn were successfully discharged in satisfactory condition on postoperative day 20 (Figs. 3, 4).

Discussion

Marfan syndrome, like other connective tissue disorders, is known to be a predisposing risk factor for aortic dissection [3]. Marfan syndrome is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by mutations in the FBN1 gene, which encodes fibrillin-1, a glycoprotein that is a major component of the extracellular matrix. The clinical manifestations of this disease are highly variable; however, the musculoskeletal, ocular, and cardiovascular systems are most affected in patients with MFS [5]. Evidence suggests that the type of FBN1 mutation is associated with the severity of cardiovascular manifestations [6]. Aortic complications, including dissection, occur in more than one-third of patients with MFS [7].

Aortic dissection is a rare but life-threatening cardiovascular emergency with high maternal and fetal mortality rates (maternal mortality can reach up to 30%, and fetal mortality reaching 50%) [3]. The risk of aortic dissection development is significantly increased in patients with underlying connective tissue disorders, such as Marfan syndrome [3]. This case illustrates the complex diagnostic and therapeutic challenges in the management of aortic dissection in a third-trimester pregnancy and highlights the importance of prompt diagnosis and multidisciplinary care.

During pregnancy, hormonal fluctuations, gestational hypertension, and hemodynamic and circulating blood volume changes are proven risk factors, contributing to the development of aortic dissection in women with known aortopathy, including hereditary connective tissue disorders, such as Marfan syndrome [3, 4]. Aortic dissection may be observed throughout pregnancy or in the postpartum period, lasting up to 1 year, with a peak around the third trimester and persisting for up to 3 months postpartum [3]. It is recommended that women with a risk for aortopathy who are planning a pregnancy undergo imaging, including echocardiography, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or computed tomography (CT), to elucidate the entire vascular condition [3]. Surveillance imaging throughout pregnancy and several weeks post-partum using ultrasound is recommended to monitor for congenital changes in aortic size [3]. An important aspect of this case is the patient’s long-standing diagnosis of Marfan syndrome before pregnancy. Although cardiovascular surveillance was performed, management remained limited to serial imaging and no pre-pregnancy multidisciplinary assessment or cardiac surgical consultation was undertaken despite documented aortic root enlargement and a family history of acute aortic events. This case highlights the importance of comprehensive preconception counselling and specialized aortic surveillance in women with Marfan syndrome, as the risk of pregnancy-associated aortic complications may not be fully predicted by aortic diameter alone. Current guidelines recommend pre-pregnancy risk assessment and serial aortic imaging in women with Marfan syndrome because the risk of aortic complications increases with progressive aortic enlargement. Although the reported risk is highest in patients with an aortic root diameter exceeding 45 mm, the present case demonstrates that acute type A dissection may occur despite only moderate documented aortic enlargement (approximately 42 mm before pregnancy). This observation highlights the importance of individualized risk assessment and continued surveillance throughout pregnancy [3, 8, 9].

Clinical presentation of aortic dissection during pregnancy can be atypical and nonspecific. In many cases, symptoms like back pain could be overlooked because they can be attributed to pregnancy or urinary tract infections during pregnancy, which may delay diagnosis. Acute chest pain, which was presented in this case, remains the most common symptom; it is nonspecific and could be related to other differential diagnoses, such as acute myocardial infarction, pulmonary embolism, and pneumothorax, all of which pose significant risks to both maternal and fetal survival. Therefore, a high level of clinical suspicion is essential, especially with a known or suspected Marfan syndrome patient presenting with acute chest pain. In this case, transthoracic echocardiography served as a critical initial diagnostic tool, revealing ascending aortic dilation and an intimal flap.

Concerns about the safety of radiological imaging may further complicate early diagnosis. Nevertheless, computed tomography angiography remains the gold standard for diagnosing acute aortic dissection, offering superior diagnostic detail, including three-dimensional aortic reconstruction, which is crucial in evaluating and selecting appropriate surgical interventions. Current guidelines endorse the use of low-dose radiation CTA as an essential diagnostic tool for aortic diseases during pregnancy, given its acceptable safety profile [3].

Management of acute type A aortic dissection during pregnancy requires individualized multidisciplinary decision-making based on gestational age and maternal condition [3, 10, 11]. In this case, fetal viability at 31 weeks allowed cesarean delivery immediately before definitive aortic repair, resulting in favorable maternal and neonatal outcomes.

In the present case, supra-coronary ascending aortic replacement was selected because the acute pathology could be adequately managed without formal root replacement. Intraoperatively, the intimal tear was identified approximately 0.5 cm above the right coronary artery ostium and was completely excised by resection up to the sinotubular junction. Despite complete dissection of the non-coronary sinus and partial involvement of the right coronary sinus, the aortic root diameter was approximately 4.5 cm, with no significant preoperative aortic regurgitation or major valvular pathology. The dissected root was therefore reinforced with Teflon felt, preserving the native root and valve. The exceptional clinical context, including 31-week pregnancy, immediate cesarean delivery, uterine artery embolization, and the need for urgent life-saving aortic repair strongly influenced the choice of procedure. In this setting, a Bentall or valve-sparing root replacement would have increased operative complexity and prolonged cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamp times. Therefore, supra-coronary replacement was considered the most appropriate emergency strategy to minimize operative burden. Furthermore, given that Marfan syndrome is associated with a lifelong risk of progressive aortic disease, future reinterventions may still be required despite successful proximal repair. In the absence of severe aortic regurgitation, significant root destruction, or other indications mandating immediate root replacement, a conservative approach with supra-coronary replacement and root reinforcement was deemed appropriate. Given the anticipated need for immediate transition to cardiac surgery, effective control of uterine bleeding was essential to minimize maternal morbidity. Additionally, given that the patient had two previous children, and after thorough counseling regarding the associated risks and implications, informed consent for this approach was obtained.

This report illustrates that type A dissection may occur in Marfan patients with aortic dimensions below the highest-risk guideline thresholds, lack of dedicated pregnancy surveillance may contribute to delayed recognition of progressive aortic disease, and prophylactic uterine artery embolization may be a useful adjunct when urgent cesarean delivery is followed immediately by cardiac surgery requiring systemic anticoagulation.

Conclusions

Although aortic dissection during pregnancy is rare, clinicians must maintain a high level of suspicion for pregnant women presenting with severe chest pain. Careful monitoring of patients with suspected or confirmed Marfan syndrome is essential, and interdisciplinary treatment and prevention strategies are required. With Stanford type A aortic dissection, after 28 weeks of gestation, cesarean delivery should be prioritized. There is reason to believe that patients with confirmed connective tissue weakness syndromes may require a more aggressive, individualized strategy, with careful review and assessment of the indications for elective surgery to effectively prevent the development of aortic dissection when planning pregnancy.
