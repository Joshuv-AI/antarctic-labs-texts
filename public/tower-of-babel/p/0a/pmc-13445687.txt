Base deficit and alactic base excess are promising early biological markers in pediatric sepsis
Authors: Amani Assaad, Marianne Majdalani, Ali Ismail, Jihane Moukhaiber
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsThe diagnosis and management of pediatric sepsis remain challenging and time-sensitive. Early biological markers that may identify patients at risk of progression to septic shock are needed. Base deficit (BD) has been associated with adverse outcomes in adult sepsis, while alactic base excess (ABE) reflects non-lactate metabolic acidosis. This study aimed to evaluate the performance of BD and ABE compared with lactate in pediatric sepsis and to assess their association with time to progression to septic shock.MethodsThis retrospective single-center cohort study included pediatric patients with sepsis between January 1, 2020, and September 1, 2024. Biomarkers were obtained at initial presentation prior to resuscitation. BD and ABE were categorized into normal, abnormal, and extremely abnormal groups based on distribution thresholds, while lactate was classified as normal (≤2.2 mmol/L) or abnormal (>2.2 mmol/L). Diagnostic performance was assessed using ROC curve analysis. Time-to-event analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier curves and multivariable Cox regression models adjusted for BMI, CRP, albumin, and Phoenix Sepsis Score.ResultsA total of 44 patients were included, of whom 30 (68.18%) developed septic shock. Lactate demonstrated lower discriminative performance (AUC 0.624, 95% CI 0.447–0.800) compared with BD (AUC 0.940, 95% CI 0.875–0.999) and ABE (AUC 0.905, 95% CI 0.813–0.997). Both BD and ABE showed higher sensitivity and specificity for identifying patients who progressed to septic shock. In adjusted analyses, abnormal and extremely abnormal ABE and BD were associated with an increased hazard of progression to septic shock. Median time to shock was shortest in patients with extremely abnormal BD and ABE (approximately 8–9 hours), followed by abnormal lactate (13 hours), abnormal ABE (17 hours), and abnormal BD (18 hours).ConclusionsBD and ABE were associated with progression to septic shock in pediatric sepsis and may provide clinically relevant information for early risk stratification. In this cohort, these markers demonstrated stronger discriminative performance than lactate and may reflect metabolic disturbances present earlier in the disease course. Background

Pediatric sepsis is a major public health problem, affecting almost 25.2 million children worldwide and causing 3.35 million deaths annually, with associated costs exceeding US$24 Billion [1]. Early recognition and timely management are critical, as disease progression can be rapid, often leading to septic shock, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), and death within the first 48–72 h of presentation [2, 3]. However, early diagnosis remains challenging due to non-specific clinical manifestations, the ability of children to compensate physiologically, and the lack of reliable early biomarkers [4].

Recent advances, including the development of the Phoenix Sepsis Score (PSS), have improved the identification of sepsis and septic shock in children [5]. Despite its strengths, the PSS has notable limitations. It is primarily designed around mortality outcomes, is calculated within the first 24 h of presentation, and may not function effectively as an early screening tool prior to the onset of overt organ dysfunction. Additionally, it does not fully capture dysfunction across all organ systems, such as renal, hepatic, and integumentary involvement [5]. These limitations highlight the need for adjunctive biological markers that can support early risk stratification and guide timely clinical decision-making.

Serum lactate is widely used as a biomarker of tissue hypoperfusion in sepsis and is incorporated into current diagnostic frameworks [5]. However, its clinical utility in pediatric populations is limited. Lactate may remain within normal ranges during the early or compensated stages of sepsis, resulting in low sensitivity for early disease detection [6–8]. Furthermore, lactate primarily reflects anaerobic metabolism and does not fully capture the complex metabolic and inflammatory alterations associated with sepsis, including cytokine-mediated responses, capillary leak, hypovolemia, and altered cellular metabolism [9–11]. This raises an important clinical question: are there alternative biomarkers capable of detecting early metabolic derangements and predicting progression to septic shock before lactate elevation occurs?

Base Deficit (BD) and Alactic Base Excess (ABE) are emerging biomarkers that may better reflect the underlying metabolic disturbances in sepsis. BD represents the amount of acid or base required to normalize blood pH under standardized conditions and serves as a global indicator of metabolic acidosis [12]. In adult populations, BD has been shown to correlate with disease severity and mortality in sepsis [11, 13, 14]. Pediatric studies have also demonstrated an association between worsening BD and increased mortality [15–19], however, its role in predicting progression to septic shock has not been specifically evaluated.

ABE, a more recently described biomarker introduced by Gattinoni et al. in 2019, aims to distinguish metabolic acidosis due to lactate accumulation from that caused by fixed acids [20]. It is calculated as the sum of standard base excess and serum lactate and reflects the body’s ability—particularly renal function—to manage non-lactate metabolic acid loads [21]. In adult sepsis, ABE has been shown to independently predict mortality and, in some cases, outperform both lactate and BD [21–23]. However, its role in pediatric sepsis remains largely unexplored.

Given the limitations of current biomarkers and the need for earlier identification of high-risk patients, there is a strong rationale to investigate BD and ABE as potential tools for early risk stratification in pediatric sepsis.

In this context, we hypothesized that BD and ABE may outperform lactate as early biomarkers associated with progression to septic shock in children with sepsis. Accordingly, the objectives of this study were:

to evaluate the diagnostic performance of BD and ABE compared with lactate in identifying pediatric patients at risk of progression to septic shock; and

to evaluate the diagnostic performance of BD and ABE compared with lactate in identifying pediatric patients at risk of progression to septic shock; and

to assess their association with time to progression to septic shock.

to assess their association with time to progression to septic shock.

If our hypothesis is confirmed, these findings would support the use of BD and ABE as adjunctive biomarkers in the early assessment of pediatric sepsis, potentially facilitating earlier risk stratification and timely escalation of care in this time-sensitive condition.

Methods

This is single-center, retrospective cohort observational study, at the American University of Beirut Medical Center (AUBMC). The Institutional Review Board approved this study (BIO-2024-0043) on October 24, 2024, in accordance with the ethical principles of Belmont, 1979. A chart review was performed for all pediatric patients (inpatients during their hospital stay, or presented to the pediatric emergency department) diagnosed with sepsis between January 1, 2020 and September 1, 2024.

Inclusion criteria were all pediatric patients aged 1 month up to 18 years who presented to AUBMC with a diagnosis of sepsis (defined according to PSS criteria as a score ≥ 2 in the presence of suspected or confirmed infection [5]). Exclusion criteria included: children presenting with septic shock (defined according to PSS criteria mentioned above as sepsis accompanied by at least one cardiovascular dysfunction point [5]); patients with documented fluid administration and/or initiation of vasoactive therapy prior to blood gas sampling; patients with pre-existing chronic conditions known to cause metabolic acidosis (such as chronic kidney disease, renal tubular acidosis, type I diabetes mellitus, chronic liver disease, or inborn error of metabolism); patients with code-limiting statuses (e.g. do not escalate, comfort care) and those with incomplete or inaccurate data (such as patients transferred from another hospital after initial stabilization, or patients with only peripheral venous blood gas showing lactate above 2mmol/L). This latter exclusion was made because arterial and central venous lactate levels are highly correlated [24, 25], while peripheral venous lactate correlates well only at levels (≤ 2 mmol/L), and becomes unreliable at higher levels [26, 27].

Data collection methods

Data was collected through chart review using the EPIC system. Waiver for consent was approved by the Institutional Review Board as this is a retrospective observational study, and the research team are not involved in the clinical care of the collected patients. The waiver did not adversely affect the rights or welfare of the subjects, as their information was coded and handled with confidentiality, ensuring privacy and compliance with the ethical standards. The data collection sheet consisted of four major sections: 1- Demographics: date of birth and gender; 2- Clinical characteristics at presentation: vital signs, height, weight, Glasgow coma scale, capillary refill time, urine output, oxygen requirement, patient disposition, fluid responsiveness and inotropic support; 3- Laboratory results at presentation: Complete blood counts and differentials, C-reactive protein (CRP), Procalcitonin level, coagulation profile, creatinine, blood gases, lactate, liver function tests, BD, ABE; 4- Outcome: Comorbidities, the progression to septic shock as well as the time needed in hours to develop septic shock. Data management and analysis plan

ABE (mmol/L) was computed for all included patients using the Sum of standard base excess (mmol/L) with serum lactate (mmol/L). By definition [21–23], it is considered normal/neutral when it ranges between (≥-3; <4) and it is negative (abnormal) when (<-3). The patients were categorized further into 3 sub-categories according to the 25th percentile as follows: normal ABE (≥-3; <4), abnormal ABE (>-8.2; <-3) and extremely abnormal ABE (≤-8.2).

BD (mmol/L) is normal when it is between (≥-2; ≤2) [31]. Patients were categorized into 3 sub-categories according to the 25th percentile as follows: normal BD (≥-2; ≤2), abnormal BD (>-11; <-2) and extremely abnormal BD (≤-11).

Lactate level was classified as normal (≤ 2.2 mmol/L) and abnormal (> 2.2 mmol/L) based on PSS [5] and laboratory reference values.

Body Mass Index (BMI) was categorized into 3 sub-categories (underweight, normal, overweight), according to the World Health Organization BMI for age percentiles [32].

AKI was defined as having 1 or more of the following criteria based on Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) [33]: increase in serum creatinine ≥ 0.3 mg/dL; or increase in serum creatinine by 1.5–1.9 times baseline; or decreased urine output by at least < 0.5 mL/Kg/h for 6–12 h.

We used the statistical software SPSS version 29 for data entry, data management and analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and continuous variables as median and IQR.

To address the first objective, we evaluated how well BD, ABE, and lactate could predict pediatric septic shock. We did this by analyzing Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves, which allowed us to measure each marker’s sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV), along with the area under the curve (AUC). The cut-off for ABE (< − 3 mmol/L) was selected based on previously reported thresholds in the literature. For BD, no universally accepted cut-off exists; therefore, the threshold (≤ − 5 mmol/L) was chosen as it is within physiologically relevant ranges and demonstrated optimal diagnostic performance in the current cohort based on ROC curve analysis. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of BD, ABE, and lactate in predicting progression to septic shock. For the second objective, we looked at how long it took septic patients to progress to septic shock for each variable (BD, ABE, and lactate) using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. Given the limited number of septic shock events, a parsimonious multivariable Cox regression approach was used to reduce the risk of overfitting. Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and potential association in univariable analysis (p < 0.20), including BMI, CRP, albumin, and Phoenix Sepsis Score. Oxygen requirement was not included separately as it is part of the Phoenix score.

Because BD, ABE, and lactate are physiologically and mathematically related variables, each biomarker was analyzed in a separate multivariable Cox regression model to minimize multicollinearity.

We then plotted the survival curves for BD, ABE, and lactate separately.

Among the excluded patients, 17 had documented fluid administration prior to blood gas sampling, 11 had incomplete data, and 8 presented already in septic shock. Among the included patients, 30 (68.18%) developed septic shock (Supplementary Material S1).

Discussion

The diagnosis and management of pediatric sepsis remain challenging and time-sensitive despite the availability of tools such as the Phoenix Sepsis Score (PSS), which has recognized limitations [5]. Early identification of children at risk of deterioration is critical, as delays in recognition or escalation of care may result in rapid progression to septic shock and increased mortality. In this context, the role of metabolic biomarkers has been increasingly explored. While most previous studies have focused on mortality as the primary outcome, our study specifically evaluated progression to septic shock and, importantly, the time to development of shock, providing clinically relevant insight into early disease evolution and risk stratification.

Consistent with prior literature, biomarkers such as lactate, BD, and ABE have been associated with adverse outcomes in sepsis [6–8, 13–19, 21–23, 34]. Outcomes worsen significantly once patients progress from sepsis to septic shock, as demonstrated in international cohorts [35, 36]. Our findings align with these data, reinforcing the role of metabolic markers in risk stratification and highlighting the potential utility of BD and ABE, alongside lactate, in identifying children at higher risk for rapid clinical deterioration, before organ dysfunction develops.

Among these biomarkers, lactate has been the most widely studied. Low lactate levels do not exclude sepsis or septic shock, particularly during early or compensated stages of disease. Nevertheless, elevated lactate remains associated with adverse outcomes and retains prognostic value. Scott et al. [8] reported that lactate levels > 4 mmol/L within the first 8 h of admission had low sensitivity (20%) but high specificity for mortality. Similarly, several studies have demonstrated an association between elevated lactate levels, or reduced lactate clearance over the first 24 h, and increased mortality risk [6, 7, 34, 37–39]. In our study, lactate showed a weaker discriminative ability compared to BD and ABE. The DCA further supports these findings, demonstrating that BD and ABE provide greater net clinical benefit than lactate across a range of clinically relevant decision thresholds. This suggests that these markers may provide additional clinical utility in guiding early decision-making, particularly in identifying patients who may benefit from closer monitoring or early escalation of care.

Several physiological mechanisms may explain the relatively lower sensitivity of lactate observed in this cohort. In early or compensated stages of sepsis, global oxygen delivery may remain preserved despite ongoing microcirculatory dysfunction or mitochondrial impairment, resulting in limited lactate production. In addition, lactate elevation may be delayed relative to the onset of organ hypoperfusion, particularly when systemic perfusion is partially maintained. Pediatric patients may also exhibit more efficient lactate clearance due to preserved hepatic perfusion, which can further limit early accumulation. Moreover, sepsis is a heterogeneous syndrome, and not all patients develop significant global hypoxia, particularly in distributive shock states where perfusion abnormalities may be regional rather than systemic. Finally, the timing of biomarker sampling in this study—prior to resuscitation and early in the clinical course—may have captured patients before substantial lactate elevation occurred. These findings highlight the importance of interpreting lactate within its physiological context, particularly in early sepsis where metabolic derangements may precede overt hyperlactatemia.

Importantly, in the adjusted analysis, abnormal lactate was associated with an intermediate time to progression to septic shock, rather than representing a purely early or late marker. This suggests that lactate reflects a distinct stage in the pathophysiological cascade, likely corresponding to more advanced metabolic or hemodynamic compromise at the defined time origin, namely the onset of tissue hypoperfusion and anaerobic metabolism. Therefore, while elevated lactate remains clinically significant, its value may be limited in the earliest phases of sepsis when compensatory mechanisms are still preserved. Beyond lactate, other markers of metabolic acidosis may provide additional insight. BD represents a global measure of metabolic acidosis and captures a broader range of metabolic disturbances than lactate alone [12, 13, 22]. In this study, worsening BD was associated with both increased risk and faster progression to septic shock. This may reflect early alterations in acid–base balance, including accumulation of non-lactate acids and reduced buffering capacity. In this context, ABE, as described by Gattinoni et al. [20], has emerged as a complementary metric. It provides additional insight by isolating the contribution of non-lactate metabolic acids. In this cohort, ABE demonstrated strong association with progression to septic shock and its timing. These findings support its role as a composite metabolic marker reflecting broader physiological derangements. As such, ABE complements BD in capturing early metabolic disturbances by integrating multiple pathways of acid generation and clearance, before the development of overt hyperlactatemia, and provide clinically relevant information for early risk stratification, particularly in compensated shock states. However, these markers are not specific to sepsis-related hypoperfusion, and may be influenced by a variety of factors, including electrolyte disturbances, renal dysfunction, exogenous chloride load, ketosis, and other sources of metabolic acidosis. Therefore, their interpretation should be contextualized within the overall clinical picture, and caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings to broader critically ill populations. Moreover, it is important to note that these findings should be interpreted with caution, as the performance of selected cut-offs may be overestimated in small cohorts and requires confirmation in external datasets. In addition, although a parsimonious modeling strategy was adopted, the number of events relative to covariates remains limited, which may contribute to variability in effect estimates, as reflected by the wide confidence intervals observed for some predictors.

Taken together, our findings suggest that BD, ABE, and lactate reflect complementary but distinct aspects of sepsis pathophysiology. BD and ABE primarily capture early metabolic and acid–base disturbances, whereas lactate reflects impaired tissue oxygen utilization and hypoperfusion. Their combined interpretation may enhance early risk stratification.

From a clinical perspective, these findings support the integration of BD and ABE into early assessment strategies for pediatric sepsis. Their use, in conjunction with clinical evaluation and established scoring systems such as the PSS, may allow for earlier identification of high-risk patients, facilitating timely escalation of care, closer monitoring, and early transfer to the PICU. Future research should explore whether combining these biomarkers into multimodal risk stratification models can further improve early detection and clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, BD and ABE were associated with progression to septic shock in children with sepsis and demonstrated stronger diagnostic performance than lactate within this cohort. These biomarkers may provide complementary information for early risk stratification, and may support timely decisions regarding escalation of care when interpreted alongside clinical assessment and established scoring systems such as the Phoenix Sepsis Score. Given the observational design and limited sample size, these findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require prospective multicenter validation before incorporation into routine clinical practice.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single tertiary care center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other settings with different patient populations, clinical practices, or resource availability, and may introduce practice-pattern bias. Second, due to its retrospective observational design, causal relationships between biomarkers and progression to septic shock cannot be established, and the analysis is subject to potential residual or unmeasured confounding. Although patients with underlying conditions predisposing to metabolic acidosis (such as chronic kidney disease and inborn errors of metabolism…) were excluded, and acute kidney injury was systematically assessed using KDIGO criteria, other variables—including chemotherapy phase and intensity, transfusion status, and additional electrolyte or acid–base disturbances—were not fully captured and may have influenced BD and ABE values. Third, although a parsimonious multivariable modeling strategy was adopted, the relatively small sample size, combined with a high proportion of events, as well as the number of events relative to the number of covariates remains limited, which may contribute to variability in effect estimates and potential overfitting, as reflected by the wide confidence intervals observed for some predictors.

Fourth, uncertainty in the temporal relationship between biomarker measurement, sepsis recognition, and shock onset remains a key limitation. Because sepsis was retrospectively identified using the Phoenix Sepsis Score, the exact timing of sepsis onset (when PSS ≥ 2 was first met) could not be precisely determined, as it was inherently derived rather than clinically recorded, introducing uncertainty in the temporal alignment between biomarker measurement and disease onset. Similarly, although biomarker sampling was defined as pre-resuscitation based on chart timestamps and patients with documented fluid administration prior to sampling were excluded, the exact timing relative to completion of fluid boluses could not always be precisely established. Given that BD, ABE, and lactate can change rapidly during early resuscitation, these factors may introduce measurement bias and limit causal interpretation, as biomarkers may reflect early concurrent physiological deterioration rather than strictly preceding septic shock. Therefore, larger prospective, multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings and further clarify the role of these biomarkers in pediatric sepsis.

Fifth, treatment-related confounding cannot be fully excluded, as early supportive interventions such as fluid administration may have modified subsequent biomarker trajectories and the natural history of disease progression. Sixth, only baseline biomarker values were analyzed, and dynamic changes over time, which may provide additional prognostic information, were not assessed. Seventh, the study population included only patients with negative BD and ABE values, precluding evaluation of the prognostic significance of positive values.

Finally, although biomarker thresholds were informed by prior literature and physiological considerations, their performance may be subject to optimism bias in this relatively small cohort, and external validation in larger, prospective, multicenter studies is required. Internal validation techniques such as bootstrapping or cross-validation were not performed due to sample size constraints. In addition, the study was not designed to develop or validate a predictive model; therefore, calibration metrics were not assessed. While decision curve analysis was included to evaluate clinical utility, further validation using comprehensive model performance metrics is warranted.
