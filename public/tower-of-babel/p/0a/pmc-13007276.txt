The Evolving Landscape of Dermatophytosis in the UAE: Trichophyton indotineae and Related Genotypes
Authors: Hari Pankaj Vanam, Akela Ghazawi, Dana Aljneibi, Mushtaq Khan, Fouzia Jabeen, Ahmed R. Alsuwaidi, Jens Thomsen, Mohammad AlBataineh, Stefan Weber, Connie Gibas, Nathan P. Wiederhold, Fatima Al Dhaheri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDermatophytosis remains one of the most prevalent superficial fungal infections worldwide, increasingly complicated by the emergence of terbinafine‐resistant Trichophyton indotineae. Despite its global spread, molecular and clinical data from the United Arab Emirates (UAE) remain limited.ObjectivesWe conducted the first comprehensive molecular and phenotypic characterization of dermatophyte infections in the UAE to define the local epidemiology, antifungal resistance patterns and clinical outcomes.MethodsBetween September 2024 and March 2025, 33 consecutive clinical dermatophyte isolates associated with tinea infections were collected through passive laboratory‐based surveillance across the Abu Dhabi Emirate. Isolates underwent macroscopic and microscopic examination, urease testing, internal transcribed spacer (ITS) genotyping for species identification and sequencing of the squalene epoxidase (SQLE) gene to detect mutations associated with terbinafine resistance. Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using reference ITS genotypes. Antifungal susceptibility testing was conducted on 28 isolates following CLSI M38 guidelines.ResultsAmong the 33 isolates, 26 (78.8%) were confirmed as T. indotineae, with SQLE mutations detected in 88.5% of isolates, most frequently Phe397Leu and Ala448Thr. Terbinafine demonstrated high non‐wild‐type rates (63.6%; MIC₅₀/MIC₉₀: 2/> 2 μg/mL), while itraconazole and griseofulvin retained activity. Significant inter‐laboratory discordance was observed (6.1% agreement), with conventional methods failing to detect any T. indotineae cases. Most patients (84.8%) reported no travel history, indicating autochthonous transmission. Clinical outcomes were poor despite prolonged combination therapy: only 30.8% achieved improvement while 69.2% experienced recurrence.ConclusionsThis study provides baseline evidence of endemic terbinafine‐resistant T. indotineae in the UAE, underscoring urgent needs for molecular diagnostics, routine susceptibility testing and antifungal stewardship programs to guide effective management strategies.

Introduction

Dermatophytosis, the most widespread fungal infection globally, is rising in prevalence and causing increased utilisation of healthcare resources. This trend is driven by factors such as increased human‐to‐human transmission, host adaptation, climate change and antifungal resistance [1, 2, 3]. While Trichophyton rubrum has historically dominated, a shift is underway: Southeast Asia has emerged as the epicentre of Trichophyton mentagrophytes species complex (TMSC) activity, particularly the relatively newly described species T. indotineae, a highly transmissible, drug‐resistant fungus now reported in over 40 countries [4, 5, 6]. Its spread has driven a surge in persistent tinea corporis and cruris infections, especially in tropical and subtropical regions [7, 8, 9].

Tinea is a highly transmissible fungal infection spread through direct contact, contaminated fomites, or animals, with auto‐infection to other body sites commonly observed. Transmission is amplified in certain settings such as overcrowded environments, poor hygiene and with occlusive clothing [10, 11, 12]. Recent reports suggest evolving sexual transmission routes involving T. mentagrophytes ITS genotype VII (Genotype VII) and T. indotineae (Genotype VIII), further complicating containment [13, 14, 15]. Resistance to terbinafine, which can exceed 70% in T. indotineae isolates in some regions, is primarily driven by squalene epoxidase (SQLE) mutations [6, 16]. This has prompted increased reliance on itraconazole and, more recently, on reserve azoles like posaconazole, voriconazole and isavuconazole, despite limited safety data and emerging evidence on resistance due to CYP51B overexpression. These trends underscore a growing therapeutic challenge and the urgent need for antifungal stewardship [17, 18].

Unregulated use of over‐the‐counter corticosteroid‐antifungal‐antibiotic combinations may contribute to resistance by masking symptoms without eradicating infection, thus promoting selection of resistant strains [4, 11, 18]. The TMSC, which includes T. indotineae, demonstrates significant evolutionary adaptability. The predominantly human‐associated T. indotineae (ITS Genotype VIII) is likely a clonal derivative of a zoonotic ancestor, a theory supported by its recent detection in dogs across Africa, India and Iran [19, 20, 21, 22]. These findings underscore evolving transmission dynamics and suggest that established animal reservoirs could sustain environmental niches for resistant strains, thereby complicating control and eradication efforts. Effective mitigation is further hampered by critical infrastructure gaps, including a lack of molecular diagnostics, antifungal susceptibility testing (AFST) and SQLE mutation surveillance, which collectively limit evidence‐based management. Consequently, continuous genotypic and epidemiological surveillance within a One Health framework is essential to clarify the role of animals in the transmission and persistence of T. indotineae.

In contrast, the epidemiology of dermatophytosis in the United Arab Emirates remains poorly characterised. To date, published data are limited to isolated reports, including a single case report and a multinational genomic epidemiology study in which UAE isolates comprised a small proportion of the overall cohort [23, 24]. Systematic surveillance data describing circulating dermatophyte species, resistance patterns, or transmission dynamics within the UAE have been lacking.

In this study, we present the first molecular and phenotypic characterisation of Trichophyton infections in the UAE, highlighting the local emergence of T. indotineae, its association with terbinafine resistance, and the regional distribution of circulating genotypes.

Methodology

Strains and Clinical Data Retrieval

Between September 2024 and March 2025, 33 consecutive clinical dermatophyte strains were obtained through passive laboratory‐based surveillance recovered from tinea patients in Abu Dhabi, UAE. Initial fungal cultures were performed as part of routine patient care in clinical diagnostic laboratories across the Emirate. A central infectious disease referral laboratory collected dermatophyte isolates from these sites and forwarded all mould isolates to the UAEU‐Fungal Reference Laboratory (FRL) for centralised analysis.

Participating clinical laboratories did not perform molecular diagnostics for dermatophytes, including commercial kits. Species identification was performed using conventional methods and MALDI‐TOF MS (BioMérieux), with ambiguous identifications resolved by Sanger sequencing of the ITS region at the UAEU‐FRL. Isolates were cryopreserved at −80°C in 20% glycerol with unique accession identifiers. Anonymised clinical metadata—including demographics, travel/animal contact history, comorbidities, immunosuppression status, clinical presentation, treatment and outcomes—were collected under Abu Dhabi DOH IRB approval (DOH/CVDC/2023/484) to correlate dermatophyte genotypes and antifungal resistance patterns with clinical outcomes.

Phenotypic/Morphologic Characteristics and Urease Activity

Morphological and microscopic features of the 33 TMSC isolates were examined, focusing on colony attributes and growth rates on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) and potato dextrose agar (PDA). Microscopic characteristics were observed using slide cultures on PDA. For urease testing, all isolates were inoculated onto Christensen's urea agar following Kano et al.'s protocol, with a well‐characterised T. mentagrophytes strain (UAE1) as positive control. Results were interpreted per Larone's Medical Mycology, 7th edition [25, 26].

Molecular Confirmation and Phylogenetic Analysis of TMSC Isolates

Genomic DNA was extracted using the Fungi/Yeast Genomic DNA Isolation Kit (Norgen Biotek Corp., Canada). The ITS region was amplified using fungal primers BMB‐CR (https://sites.duke.edu/vilgalyslab/rdna_primers_for_fungi/) and ITS4 [27], with PCR and sequencing performed per Gibas et al. [6]. Sequences were edited and assembled using MEGAX v10.2.6 and BioEdit v7.7.1 [28, 29]. Species identification was confirmed by ITS BLASTn search with ≥ 99% sequence identity to reference sequences. For phylogenetic analysis, ITS sequences and GenBank references were aligned using MUSCLE in MEGAX [28]. A maximum likelihood tree was constructed using the Tamura‐Nei (TN93 + G + I) substitution model with 1000 bootstrap replicates and visualised using FigTree v66 and iTOL v6 [30].

Squalene Epoxidase (SQLE) Gene Amplification and Mutation Analysis

To investigate terbinafine resistance mechanisms, the SQLE gene was amplified from all ITS‐confirmed TMSC isolates using primers per Kano et al. [25], with PCR and sequencing as above. Sequences were edited using Chromas Lite v2.1 and BioEdit v7.7.1, then aligned against wild‐type SQLE sequences from terbinafine‐susceptible T. mentagrophytes strains using MEGA X v10.2.6 [28]. Terbinafine resistance mutations, including Leu393Phe, Phe397Leu and Ala448Thr, were identified manually and verified against GenBank resistant allele sequences.

Structural Modelling of SQLE Protein Variants

To assess structural impacts of terbinafine resistance‐associated SQLE mutations, 3D protein models were generated for wild‐type and representative mutant variants using SWISS‐MODEL, an automated homology modelling platform (ExPASy) [31], accessible at SWISS‐MODEL REST API (accessed on 1 May 2025).

Anti‐Fungal Susceptibility Testing of TMSC Isolates

In vitro antifungal susceptibility testing was performed for 28 TMSC isolates (22 T. indotineae, four T. mentagrophytes and two T. interdigitale) per CLSI M38 broth microdilution methods at the Fungus Testing Laboratory, University of Texas Health Science Center, San Antonio. Testing concentrations were: terbinafine 0.004–2 μg/mL; fluconazole 0.125–64 μg/mL; itraconazole, voriconazole and griseofulvin 0.03–16 μg/mL [6, 32]. Stock solutions were prepared in DMSO at 100× concentration with dilutions in DMSO and RPMI buffered to pH 7.0 with 0.165 M MOPS (Sigma‐Aldrich, Germany). QC strain Trichophyton mentagrophytes MRL 1957 (ATCC MYA‐4439) was included daily.

Statistical Program Used

Analyses were performed using R v4.3.1 [33] and Microsoft Excel. Diagnostic metrics (specificity, PPV, NPV) and odds ratios (95% CI) were calculated from 2 × 2 contingency tables using Fisher's Exact or Fisher–Freeman–Halton tests. Interlaboratory agreement was assessed using Cohen's κ and McNemar's test. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher's tests, and non‐parametric data (median [IQR]) with Kruskal–Wallis and Dunn's post hoc tests (Benjamini–Hochberg correction). For AFST, MIC₅₀, MIC₉₀, median and modal MICs were calculated per CLSI M38 [32] and interpreted using published ECOFFs [6, 16] in the absence of clinical breakpoints.

Results

Diagnostic Accuracy and Interlaboratory Concordance

Among 33 culture‐confirmed Trichophyton strains, potassium hydroxide (KOH) direct examination reports from referral laboratories demonstrated poor sensitivity, with only 29.0% (9/31) of cases correctly identified, resulting in a high false‐negative rate (71.0%). Specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) could not be calculated due to the absence of true‐negative samples, highlighting systemic diagnostic limitations in KOH‐based methods.

Significant differences in fungal identification results were observed between the referral laboratories using morphology and MALDI‐TOF and those conducted at the UAEU FRL using morphology, urease testing and DNA sequencing reflecting the shortcomings of phenotypic analysis alone. The UAEU FRL identified T. indotineae in 78.8% (26/33) of isolates, T. mentagrophytes in 12.1% (4/33) and T. interdigitale in 9.1% (3/33). In contrast, the referral laboratories reported the same isolates as T. interdigitale (75.8%, 25/33), T. mentagrophytes (18.2%, 6/33), T. tonsurans (3.0%, 1/33), or unidentified (3.0%, 1/33).

Molecular Identification, Phylogenetic Analysis and Phenotypic Characterisation

Molecular Identification and Phylogenetic Assignment of Trichophyton Isolates

ITS sequencing of 33 clinical TMSC isolates identified 26 T. indotineae (78.8%), four T. mentagrophytes (12.1%) and three T. interdigitale (9.1%). Species‐level identifications were confirmed by phylogenetic analysis of the ITS region. The majority of the isolates clustered within the ITS Genotype VIII clade, aligning with T. indotineae reference sequences, while four isolates (UAE1, UAE43, UAE44 and UAE104) grouped within the T. mentagrophytes ITS Genotype III clade. Three isolates (UAE107, UAE156 and UAE166) clustered with T. interdigitale ITS Genotypes I, II, XI and XII (Figure 1). All ITS sequences generated in this study have been deposited in the NCBI GenBank database with accession numbers PV500833‐PV500858 and PV903172‐PV903178.

Morphological and Phenotypic Characterisation

Morphological and phenotypic characterisation of the 33 clinical dermatophyte strains showed four distinct morphotypes, based on microscopic and macroscopic features and urease activity assays (Figure 2). Type 1 (T. interdigitale, zoophilic morphotype) colonies exhibited a star‐like, granular to powdery texture with irregular margins (cream obverse, yellow‐brown reverse), comprising septate, branched hyphae with abundant spirals, and sessile/short‐stalked microconidia (3–5 μm) in dense clusters, representing holoblastic ontogeny. Macroconidia (20–40 μm) were thin‐walled, distorted and produced solitarily on terminal conidiogenous cells, indicating holothallic ontogeny. Occasional chlamydoconidia and nodular organs were also observed (Figure 2A–A1). Type 2 (T. interdigitale, anthropophilic variant) displayed mature, circular, cottony to woolly colonies (cream obverse, yellow‐brown reverse) with reduced spirals in aged cultures, sparse clusters of microconidia, distorted macroconidia and prominent chlamydoconidia and nodular organs (Figure 2B–B1). Type 3 (T. indotineae) colonies showed rapid radial growth with a powdery to granular texture (cream obverse, yellowish‐brown to orange reverse). Microscopy revealed moderately branched hyphae with rare spirals, spherical to pyriform microconidia (4–6 μm) in small clusters, and fusiform macroconidia (30–50 μm). Additionally, a distinctive ‘snake bean–like’ arthroconidial form was noted, arising by enteroarthric ontogeny through thallic fragmentation of fertile hyphae (Figure 2C–C1,E). Type 4 (T. mentagrophytes) colonies were fast‐growing and powdery with radial spread (cream obverse, brownish‐yellow to orange reverse), showing dense spiral hyphae, grape‐like clusters of microconidia (2–4 μm), smooth cigar‐shaped macroconidia (25–45 μm), and characteristic favic chandelier‐like structures (Figure 2D–D1).

Urease activity results supported species differentiation between T. mentagrophytes, T. interdigitale and T. indotineae (Figure 2F). T. mentagrophytes and T. interdigitale strains were urease‐positive within 5–7 days, whereas all 26 T. indotineae strains remained urease‐negative after 14 days. The morphological variations were consistent with molecularly confirmed species, but overlapping features (e.g., spirals, macroconidia) necessitated molecular resolution.

Demographic, Clinical and Host‐Related Factors Associated With Trichophyton Species

Age and Gender Distribution

Males accounted for 66.7% (22/33) of cases; however, no significant association between gender and fungal species was observed (p = 0.624). Notably, T. mentagrophytes infections, albeit identified in a small number of cases (12.1%, 4/33), occurred in significantly younger patients (median age, 8.5 years; IQR, 6.5–15) compared to those with T. indotineae infections (median age, 31 years; IQR, 21–34; p = 0.009), suggesting that age may influence species‐specific dermatophytosis patterns, while acknowledging the limitation of the small sample size.

Ethnicity, Occupation, Travel History and Transmission Patterns

Among 33 Trichophyton cases, Emirati patients predominated (72.7%, 24/33), with single cases among nine other nationalities. The cohort included 26 T. indotineae and 7 non‐T. indotineae (4 T. mentagrophytes, 3 T. interdigitale) infections. Most patients (84.8%, 28/33) reported no travel history, indicating local acquisition; the five travellers (15.2%)—to Jordan, Egypt, Oman, or unspecified destinations—all had T. indotineae infections. Occupation was not documented in 42.4% (14/33); among those with available data, office workers (18.2%) and students (15.2%) were the most common, followed by police officers, housewives and unemployed individuals (2/33, 6.1% each). A taxi driver and a hairdresser were represented by 1 case each (3.0%). Within the T. indotineae subgroup, 76.9% (20/26) were Emirati, 80.8% (21/26) had no travel history, and office workers (23.1%) were the most represented. The non‐T. indotineae group showed a comparable pattern, with 57.1% (4/7) Emiratis and 100% (7/7) reporting no travel; students were most frequent (42.8%). No significant differences were observed between T. indotineae and non‐T. indotineae groups for ethnicity, travel history, or occupation (p > 0.05), supporting autochthonous, community‐based transmission with T. indotineae as the dominant local pathogen.

Zoonotic Exposure and Transmission Dynamics

Assessment of animal exposure revealed a predominant involvement of domestic and companion animals, reported in 36.4% (12/33) of cases, primarily associated with cats (33.3%), followed by rabbits and household pets (16.7% each).

Among all species, T. mentagrophytes infections showed a strong link to rabbit contact, with 66.7% (2/3) of affected individuals reporting exposure, implicating rabbits as a probable zoonotic reservoir (p > 0.05, not statistically significant due to limited cases). In contrast, among T. indotineae cases (26/33), 30.8% (8/26) reported animal contact, including cats, a police dog, birds, farm animals and unspecified pets, but no significant association with zoonotic transmission was observed (p = 0.12). One T. interdigitale case (UAE156) had documented cat exposure, consistent with its zoophilic lineage.

These findings highlight heterogeneous zoonotic transmission routes, with rabbits and domestic animals serving as potential reservoirs in select T. mentagrophytes and T. indotineae infections.

Clinical Manifestations by Trichophyton Species

T. indotineae was the predominant pathogen in our cohort (78.8%, 26/33), demonstrating a significant association with specific clinical manifestations. It presented most frequently as tinea corporis (38.5%, 10/26), tinea cruris (19.2%, 5/26) and multisite infections (34.6%, 9/26). In contrast, T. mentagrophytes (12.1%, 4/33) was primarily linked to tinea corporis (75.0%, 3/4), while T. interdigitale (9.1%, 3/33) was associated with a mix of multisite, tinea pedis and tinea unguium. There is a significant association between the fungal species and the clinical pattern of infection (p = 0.007), highlighting distinct pathogenic profiles. These findings underscore that T. indotineae exhibits a broader and more varied clinical presentation compared to non‐T. indotineae species in this cohort.

Association of Topical Steroids With Chronicity and Clinical Outcomes

Of the 33 dermatophytosis cases analysed, topical corticosteroid use was documented in 57.7% (19/33). Treatment outcomes were available for all 26 T. indotineae cases, which showed a 69.2% (18/26) recurrence rate. Among non‐T. indotineae cases, outcomes were available for only 4 of 7 patients (57.1%), all of whom experienced recurrence. Among steroid‐exposed T. indotineae infections, fixed‐dose combinations (FDCs) were most common (6/15), comprising betamethasone–fusidic acid and betamethasone–clotrimazole (3 cases each). Other exposures included mometasone (4 cases), hydrocortisone (3) and clobetasol (1), with one patient using multiple agents (mometasone, hydrocortisone and hydrocortisone–fusidic acid FDC). No statistically significant association was observed between steroid exposure and clinical outcome in this group (p = 0.695, Fisher's Exact Test). In non‐T. indotineae infections, all steroid‐exposed cases involved FDCs—specifically betamethasone–fusidic acid (3 cases) and betamethasone–clotrimazole (1 case)—including one patient (UAE43) with multiple steroid exposures. Non‐steroidal immunomodulators (topical tacrolimus and pimecrolimus) were documented in two cases but excluded from corticosteroid exposure analysis.

Chronic infections (> 3 months) were observed more frequently in T. indotineae cases (57.7%, 15/26) than in non‐T. indotineae cases (28.6%, 2/7), though this difference was not statistically significant (Fisher's Exact Test, p = 0.406). Similarly, recurrence rates were high among steroid‐exposed patients in both groups, occurring in 73.3% (11/15) of T. indotineae and 75.0% (3/4) of non‐T. indotineae cases. The limited sample size may explain the lack of statistical significance, yet clinically relevant trends of high recurrence and more frequent chronic T. indotineae infections were observed.

Host Immunologic and Predisposing Factors in TMSC Infections

Immunocompromised conditions were identified in 6.1% (2/33) of patients, both associated with breast cancer, which were managed using dexamethasone and chemotherapy. A broad category of immune‐altering conditions or treatments, encompassing both underlying conditions and drug‐induced suppression or immunomodulation, was identified in 27.3% (9/33) of patients. Immunosuppressive or immunomodulatory therapies were reported in 27.3% (9/33) of cases, distributed across T. indotineae (23.1%, 6/26), T. mentagrophytes (25.0%, 1/4) and T. interdigitale (66.7%, 2/3) infections. Agents included systemic corticosteroids (5) and dupilumab (3) as immunosuppressive drugs, and colchicine (1) as an immunomodulatory therapy, primarily prescribed for atopic dermatitis, gout, or malignancy. Systemic or metabolic comorbidities were documented in 30.3% (10/33), comprising diabetes mellitus, obesity with dyslipidemia, hypertension, gout with autoimmune hypothyroidism, and breast cancer. Predisposing or confounding skin conditions were observed in 21.2% (7/33), including seborrheic dermatitis, acne vulgaris, erythema multiforme–like lesions, dermatitis and lichen planus. One patient reported recurrent sexually transmitted infections (STI), while another had a STI associated with a partner presenting a similar rash and a history of multiple partners, representing both a confounding clinical presentation and a predisposing factor for probable sexual transmission. No statistically significant associations were found between fungal species and any host‐related factors (p > 0.05). Antifungal Susceptibility Testing (AFST) Results

The antifungal susceptibility profiles of 28 Trichophyton mentagrophytes complex isolates revealed distinct species‐specific patterns as summarised in Table 4. Trichophyton indotineae (n = 22) demonstrated high non‐wild‐type (NWT) phenotypes to terbinafine (63.6%; MIC₅₀/MIC₉₀ 2/> 2 μg/mL) and voriconazole (40.9%), whereas itraconazole maintained better activity (13.6% NWT; MIC₅₀/MIC₉₀ 0.06/0.125 μg/mL). Griseofulvin showed excellent preserved activity (9.1% NWT). Among non‐T. indotineae isolates (n = 6), both T. mentagrophytes (n = 4) and T. interdigitale (n = 2) isolates demonstrated low MIC values for terbinafine (0.015–1.0 μg/mL and 0.015–0.06 μg/mL, respectively) and azoles such as itraconazole and voriconazole (≤ 0.03–0.06 μg/mL). In contrast, markedly higher MICs were recorded for fluconazole (16–32 μg/mL for T. mentagrophytes; 2–4 μg/mL for T. interdigitale) and to a lesser extent for griseofulvin (1.0 and 0.125 μg/mL, respectively). It is worth mentioning that only one T. mentagrophytes strain exhibited an elevated terbinafine MIC (1 μg/mL). Epidemiological cut‐off (ECOFF) thresholds (as per Ebert et al. [16] and Cañete‐Gibas et al. [6]) served as provisional interpretive criteria for NWT designation, given the current lack of breakpoints for dermatophytes.

Clinical Outcomes and Mechanistic Basis of Antifungal Resistance in T. indotineae

Treatment outcomes for 26 T. indotineae cases demonstrated limited effectiveness of first‐line systemic therapy amid a high prevalence of SQLE mutations (88.5%, 23/26). Most patients (73.1%, 19/26) required prolonged, multi‐cycle combination regimens involving both systemic and topical agents, yet overall responses remained poor: only 30.8% (8/26) achieved clinical improvement, while recurrence occurred in 69.2% (18/26). Among patients who received itraconazole as the first‐line systemic antifungal (11/26), only 45.5% (5/11) achieved clinical improvement—including one case that ultimately required isavuconazole salvage therapy (UAE 41)—whereas 54.5% (6/11) experienced recurrence, despite most undergoing prolonged treatment. In the terbinafine first‐line group (8/26), 75.0% (6/8) experienced recurrence and only 25.0% (2/8) improved, with SQLE mutations identified in seven of the eight isolates. The single case treated with fluconazole also recurred. Detailed case‐level treatment courses, durations, antifungal combinations and outcomes are provided in Table S1.

Antifungal Susceptibility Testing (AFST) provided a mechanistic basis for these clinical outcomes. Elevated terbinafine MICs (≥ 2 μg/mL) were strongly associated with SQLE mutations, with the highest MICs (> 2 μg/mL) frequently linked to the Phe397Leu substitution, particularly in double mutants with Ala448Thr. In addition, elevated itraconazole MICs (≥ 0.25 μg/mL) and voriconazole MICs (≥ 0.5) were observed in three strains harbouring the Phe397Leu mutation. Strains harbouring only Ala448Thr or other single mutations typically remained susceptible to itraconazole, demonstrating mutation‐specific resistance patterns (Figure 3).

Correlation of SQLE Structure and Mutations With Terbinafine Resistance

SQLE Mutations and Their Association With Terbinafine Activity

A comprehensive molecular analysis of 33 clinical Trichophyton isolates identified SQLE mutations in 27 (81.8%) isolates, with species‐specific variation: 88.5% (23/26) of T. indotineae, 100% (4/4) of T. mentagrophytes and none of the 3 T. interdigitale isolates. Notably, the Leu419Phe substitution was observed exclusively in T. mentagrophytes. Among mutant T. indotineae isolates, the most prevalent single amino acid substitutions were Phe397Leu (n = 6) and Ala448Thr (n = 6), followed by Leu393Ser (n = 4) and Gln408Leu (n = 3). Double mutations (Phe397Leu + Ala448Thr) were identified in four isolates (UAE‐16, UAE‐112, UAE‐117, UAE‐178). A low terbinafine MIC was strongly mutation dependent. The Phe397Leu was associated with a NWT phenotype, with MIC ≥ 2 μg/mL whereas Gln408Leu and Leu393Ser mutations were consistently associated with a lower MIC (2 μg/mL), where data were available. In contrast, Leu419Phe and Ala448Thr—when present alone—correlated with a WT phenotype. All six isolates lacking SQLE mutations (UAE‐78, UAE‐119, UAE‐155, UAE‐107, UAE‐156, UAE‐166) exhibited WT terbinafine MICs; MIC results were unavailable for five isolates (UAE‐39, UAE‐41, UAE‐78, UAE‐166, UAE‐167) (Figure 3).

Structural Modelling of SQLE and Terbinafine Interaction

Structural modelling of SQLE mapped five‐point mutations (Leu393Ser, Phe397Leu, Gln408Leu, Leu419Phe and Ala448Thr) identified in clinical Trichophyton isolates across the core catalytic domain. Leu393Ser, Phe397Leu, and Gln408Leu were embedded within α‐helices, potentially affecting local folding or steric interactions with terbinafine, while Ala448Thr and Leu419Phe at the protein periphery suggested more limited structural impact (Figure 4). Docking analysis revealed terbinafine binding within the catalytic pocket proximal to mutation sites. Notably, Phe397Leu and Ala448Thr—frequently detected in resistant isolates—were directly adjacent to the binding site, potentially reducing drug access or binding affinity, whereas Leu393Ser, Gln408Leu and Leu419Phe occupied peripheral positions but may still influence conformational dynamics.

Discussion

This study provides the first comprehensive molecular, clinical and antifungal activity characterisation of dermatophytosis in the UAE, integrating genomic identification, antifungal susceptibility, SQLE mutation profiling, clinical metadata and real‐world treatment outcomes. We identified a high prevalence of T. indotineae (78.8%) marked by widespread terbinafine tolerance and frequent SQLE mutations, alongside clear evidence of local transmission. To date, only two reports have addressed T. indotineae in the UAE, one as a single case report and the other as the inclusion of UAE isolates representing 7% of samples in a recent multinational genomic epidemiology study [23, 24]. That multicenter analysis demonstrated that UAE isolates belong to a globally disseminated T. indotineae lineage, reinforcing our findings and highlighting the international spread of resistant strains. The substantial local disease burden, together with the high frequency of SQLE mutations and the poor first‐line clinical response patterns observed in our cohort, suggests that T. indotineae is likely endemic in the UAE and underscores the need for its incorporation into regional surveillance and antifungal resistance management programs.

The predominance of T. indotineae in our UAE cohort (78.8%) reflects its established endemicity in regions from India to the Middle East, including Iran, Iraq, Bahrain and Oman [4, 7, 8, 11, 16, 19, 34, 35, 36], and coincides with its emerging global spread as a leading cause of tinea corporis and cruris in Asia and increasingly reported cases in Europe and North America [6, 12, 16, 19, 37, 38]. This stands in contrast to the historically lower prevalence rates (10%–45%) documented in European populations [39, 40, 41]. Trichophyton mentagrophytes was more common in paediatric cases in our cohort compared to those with T. indotineae infections. This may reflect increased zoonotic exposure in younger individuals, although the limited number of T. mentagrophytes cases precludes determining whether this represents a true age‐related pattern or sampling variation. No significant gender association was observed, consistent with global trends [4, 7, 8, 11, 16, 19, 34, 41]. In our UAE T. indotineae cohort, the majority were Emirati (76.9%), with the majority reporting no travel, strongly supporting autochthonous transmission. The lack of association with ethnicity or occupation in our cohort echoes findings from endemic regions [7, 8, 11, 16, 19, 34, 41], and highlights the need for tailored public health interventions.

The marked discordance between clinical and reference laboratory diagnoses in our cohort (agreement 6.1%) highlights significant diagnostic limitations. Although MALDI‐TOF MS systems (bioMérieux VITEK MS, Bruker Biotyper) are widely used, their performance depends heavily on database completeness, and most commercial libraries still lack validated T. indotineae spectra. Normand et al. [42] demonstrated that using MSI‐2, an online tool (Université de Lille, France) enabling spectra from any MALDI‐TOF platform to be queried against a curated fungal database containing sequence‐confirmed T. indotineae, increased identification accuracy from 5% to 96%, with 99.6% specificity [42, 43]. Incorporating MSI‐2 therefore represents a practical approach to improving diagnostic accuracy and strengthening species‐level surveillance in clinical laboratories.

Before the 2016 dermatophyte taxonomy revision, hundreds of Genotype VIII sequences were misclassified in GenBank as T. mentagrophytes or T. interdigitale, illustrating widespread diagnostic limitations across regions [39, 44]. Its reclassification as T. indotineae by Kano et al. [25], later supported by WGS and phenotypic markers [8, 25, 44, 45], clarified species identity but also generated debate, particularly in India, given the geographic specificity of the proposed name [46]. Our findings underscore the importance of accurate species‐level identification and support molecular sequencing as the diagnostic gold standard, with urease testing providing a practical adjunct to distinguish urease‐negative T. indotineae from other TMSC members in resource‐limited settings. In contrast, KOH microscopy demonstrated poor sensitivity (29%) and a high false‐negative rate (71%), reinforcing WHO recommendations to strengthen diagnostic capacity through enhanced technician training and improved methodologies such as Calcofluor White staining [47, 48, 49].

Despite frequent topical corticosteroid use in our cohort (57.7%) among T. indotineae infections, no association with steroid exposure was observed, likely due to the small sample size—contrasting with the steroid‐driven epidemic reported in India [11, 16, 19, 38]. However, treatment failure and recurrence occurred more often in steroid‐exposed patients, suggesting that corticosteroid misuse may still contribute to chronicity and delayed recovery. Additionally, a subset of patients receiving immunomodulatory or immunosuppressive therapies (27.3%, 9/33) with T. indotineae infections (23.1%, 6/26), often with comorbid or predisposing dermatologic conditions, represent an emerging host‐related risk profile.

Our findings corroborate global reports of SQLE mutation–mediated terbinafine tolerance in T. indotineae, consistent with the high mutation prevalence reported by Ebert et al. [16], who identified Phe397Leu in 91% of resistant isolates and Ala448Thr in both resistant and susceptible strains. In our cohort, SQLE mutations were identified in 81.8% of isolates, including 88.5% of T. indotineae—most frequently Phe397Leu and Ala448Thr (n = 6 each), while Gln408Leu and Leu393Ser were less common—and in all T. mentagrophytes isolates, which exclusively harboured Leu419Phe. Double mutations (Phe397Leu + Ala448Thr), identified in four isolates, similar to the pattern described by Kong et al. [34], who demonstrated that such combinations were associated with significantly higher fluconazole and itraconazole MICs compared to single mutants. Similarly, our findings showed that Phe397Leu and Gln408Leu correlated with NWT terbinafine and azole MICs, whereas Ala448Thr or Leu419Phe alone were linked to WT phenotypes—observations consistent with Ebert et al. and Kong et al. [16, 34], but contrasting with Gupta et al. [50], who proposed broader azole cross‐tolerance. Collectively, these results highlight Phe397Leu as the principal molecular determinant of terbinafine–reduced susceptibility within the T. mentagrophytes complex, particularly T. indotineae, and reinforce the importance of routine SQLE genotyping to guide antifungal stewardship and resistance surveillance. Mechanisms of azole resistance were not explored in this study and represent an important direction for future investigation.

Given the absence of standardised breakpoints, conservative ECOFF thresholds—terbinafine ≥ 0.5 μg/mL, itraconazole ≥ 0.25 μg/mL, voriconazole ≥ 0.5 μg/mL and griseofulvin ≥ 4.0 μg/mL—were applied following Ebert et al. and Cañete‐Gibas et al. [6, 16], representing values one dilution lower than those proposed by Shaw et al. [51], to enhance detection sensitivity for emerging non–wild‐type strains. Using these criteria, T. indotineae demonstrated substantial NWT rates (in vitro terbinafine resistance) (63.6%; MIC₅₀/MIC₉₀ 2/> 2 μg/mL), while voriconazole showed moderate NWT rates (40.9%) and itraconazole retained low rates (13.6%). Griseofulvin remained highly active (9.1% NWT), affirming its continued therapeutic value.

Comparative antifungal susceptibility analysis showed that terbinafine tolerance in the UAE cohort (MIC₅₀ 2 μg/mL; MIC₉₀ > 2 μg/mL) parallels levels reported in India and North America but exceeds those in Iraq (Table 5), despite the shared predominance of the Phe397Leu mutation [6, 7, 16, 34, 35]. The Ala448Thr substitution, frequent in our isolates (45.5%), occurred at a lower rate than in Indian studies [16], but showed a similar cooccurrence with Phe397Leu in double mutants associated with higher terbinafine and azole MICs. Fluconazole MICs (MIC₉₀ 64 μg/mL) were uniformly elevated across regions, reflecting intrinsic low activity, whereas itraconazole (MIC₉₀ 0.125 μg/mL) retained consistent low MICs, in agreement with Gibas et al., Kong et al. and Ebert et al. [6, 16, 34]. Furthermore, the voriconazole (MIC₉₀ 0.5 μg/mL) in our study was comparable to the values reported by Mahmood et al. from Iraq [35] and Ebert et al. [16]. Griseofulvin susceptibility showed lower values with an MIC₉₀ of 2 μg/mL in our UAE cohort compared to the ones described by Kong et al. [34]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the UAE resistance profile mirrors global trends dominated by T. indotineae harbouring Phe397Leu‐based SQLE mutations, while azole efficacy remains largely preserved.

Treatment outcomes in our cohort highlight the limited effectiveness of current first‐line systemic therapies for T. indotineae, reflecting both the high prevalence of SQLE mutations (88.5%) and the organism's growing therapeutic resistance. Despite prolonged multi‐cycle combination regimens and the frequent need for salvage therapy in our cohort, overall outcomes remained poor, with clinical improvement observed in only 30.8%. Although itraconazole performed better than terbinafine—achieving clinical improvement in 45.5% of cases, including those with SQLE mutations—it still failed in more than half of treated patients, with one requiring escalation to isavuconazole. Terbinafine outcomes were notably poorer, with 75.0% recurrence and only two responders, mirroring the predominance of SQLE‐mutated isolates. These findings support azoles as empiric first‐line therapy when susceptibility data are unavailable but also underscore that itraconazole's partial efficacy may be influenced by dosing, formulation variability and patient tolerance. Further studies are needed to optimise treatment regimens and elucidate azole cross‐resistance mechanisms that can better guide therapy for T. indotineae infections. Despite outcome data being available for only four non‐T. indotineae cases, each of these four experienced recurrence, suggesting a multifactorial aetiology involving treatment‐related, host‐related and reinfection factors rather than antifungal resistance alone.

While T. mentagrophytes remains a classically zoonotic pathogen [52], T. indotineae has adapted to efficient human‐to‐human transmission [6, 7, 11, 14, 15, 16]. Yet the frequent reports of animal contact in our cohort and documented spillback of T. indotineae into dogs [8, 19, 20, 21, 22, 53], suggest a more complex ecology with the potential for emerging animal reservoirs. Such reservoirs may sustain antifungal‐resistant strains, underscoring the need for coordinated One Health surveillance across medical, veterinary and environmental sectors to monitor transmission and prevent reservoir establishment.

This study is limited by a small sample size, restriction to Abu Dhabi centers, incomplete clinical metadata and a cross‐sectional design, which may limit generalizability and preclude assessment of recurrence or resistance evolution. Nevertheless, the integrated molecular, phenotypic and clinical approach provides a valuable baseline for national surveillance, underscoring the need to strengthen reference laboratory capacity, genomic monitoring and antifungal stewardship, alongside future studies addressing azole resistance mechanisms and transmission drivers across the UAE and wider MENA region.
