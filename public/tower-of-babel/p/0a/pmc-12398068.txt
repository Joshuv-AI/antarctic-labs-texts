Helicobacter pylori, peptic ulcer disease, and colorectal cancer: a prospective study with genome-wide interaction analysis and Mendelian randomization
Authors: Ziqi Wan, Jiarui Mi, Xiaoyin Bai, Dong Wu, Sunny Hei Wong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionHelicobacter pylori (HP) is a well-established gastric carcinogen. But it shows inconsistent association with colorectal cancer (CRC) across diverse study populations.MethodsWe investigated participants from the UK biobank. HP-seropositive cases and peptic ulcer diseases (PUDs) were identified. The primary outcome was CRC. We estimated the cumulative incidence using a competing risk model measured by the hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) with adjustment. We did a genome-wide interaction analysis to identify genetic variants that modify HP-serology-CRC associations with exploratory gene-based and gene-set analysis. We further did a bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) to investigate the causal relation of gene-proxied PUD on CRC of the European ancestry and of the East Asian ancestry.ResultsWe included 492,490 participants with a median follow-up of 14.7 years. HP sero-positivity did not significantly increase the incidence of CRC (adjusted HR = 0.76, 95% CI 0.53 − 1.10, p = 0.15). No SNP was identified to be significantly interacted with HP serology to modify CRC risk. The risk of CRC for PUD cases was not significantly different from non-PUD cases after adjusting (adjusted HR = 0.88, 95% CI 0.76 − 1.02, p = 0.09). The gene-proxied PUD causally increased the incidence of colon cancer of the East Asian ancestry (OR = 1.47, 95% CI = 1.00 − 2.15, p = 0.047), not of the European ancestry (OR = 1.03, 95% CI = 0.79 − 1.34, p = 0.82).ConclusionsNeither seropositivity for HP nor PUD showed a robust increase on the risk of CRC in a 15-year follow-up. The causal relation of PUD on CRC was significant of East Asian ancestry, not of European ancestry.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13027-025-00686-8.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) represents one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide, ranking as the third most commonly diagnosed cancer and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally [1]. Its pathogenesis involves a complex interplay of genetic predisposition, environmental factors, and chronic inflammatory processes [2]. Among emerging etiological hypotheses, the potential role of microbial dysbiosis—particularly infections by onco-pathogenic bacteria—has garnered significant interest.

Helicobacter pylori (H.pylori, HP) is a Gram-negative bacterium colonizing the human gastric mucosa. HP infects approximately half of the global population, with transmission routes primarily involving fecal-oral or oral-oral contact [3]. Upon colonization, HP induces chronic inflammation through virulence factors such as cytotoxin-associated gene A (CagA) and vacuolating cytotoxin A (VacA), which disrupt epithelial integrity and promote oxidative stress [4]. Clinically, HP infection manifests along a spectrum from asymptomatic carriage to severe complications, including peptic ulcer disease (PUD)—a condition characterized by mucosal erosion in the stomach or duodenum [5]. Despite declining HP prevalence in developed regions due to improved sanitation and antibiotic therapies, its residual burden continues to disproportionately affect socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, contributing to persistent gastrointestinal morbidity [3].

HP’s carcinogenic impact on the upper gastrointestinal tract is well-established [6], but its extrapolation to colorectal carcinogenesis remains controversial. Epidemiological evidence is incongruent in linking HP to CRC risk. Several meta-analyses report an increased CRC risk among HP-infected individuals [7], while others demonstrate null or even inverse associations [8]. These discrepancies may relate to the diverse population. HP has a high rate of DNA recombination and thus has genetic diversity [9]. Genes on its cag pathogenicity island (cagPAI) could be grouped into the East-Asian-type and the Western type [10]. The East-Asian type, which is more prevalent in East Asia, was reported to be associated with higher risk of gastric cancer than the Western type [10]. Therefore, we hypothesized that HP’s carcinogenic impact on CRC could be population-dependent. However, the paucity of prospective cohort data and the limited exploration of causal inference methodologies have hindered definitive conclusions.

Resolving the HP-CRC relationship requires transcending observational study limitations through causal inference frameworks. Mendelian randomization (MR), which utilizes genetic variants as instrumental variants (IVs) to approximate randomized controlled trials, offers a robust strategy to minimize confounding and reverse causation [11]. It holds three assumptions: relevance (IVs are associated with the exposure), independence (IVs are not associated with confounding factors) and exclusion restriction (IVs influence the outcome only through the exposure) [12, 13]. MR thus serves as a strong tool to investigate the causal relation of gene-proxied exposures (here PUD) on outcomes of interest (here CRC) [14]. Previous MR mainly focused on samples of the European ancestry. But HP infection is prevalent across the world, including Europe and East Asia [15]. By utilizing genetic statistics of the East Asian and the European ancestry, our study will first reveal the ethnic disparities of such relationship.

To address the HP-CRC relationship, we performed a multi-phasic investigation with the following analyses: we evaluated the longitudinal association between HP seropositivity and CRC incidence within a large European population-based cohort and investigated gene-environment interaction; we delineated PUD—the hallmark clinical outcome of HP infection—in relation with CRC risk; we inferred the ancestry-specific causality by two-sample MR analyses of both European and East Asian ancestry. By integrating prospective epidemiological data with genetic causal inference, this study aims to resolve longstanding controversies regarding HP’s extra-gastric oncogenicity. The ancestral comparative component further advances precision oncology by clarifying context-dependent pathogen-host interactions.

Methods

Data sources

The UK Biobank consists of more than 500,000 participants, aged 40 to 69 at the time of recruitment, and follows their health outcomes prospectively. This study was conducted under UK Biobank application number 100,787. A comprehensive description of the UK Biobank study design has been published elsewhere [16]. Ethical approval and informed consent were obtained through the UK Biobank study. Patients or the public contributed to the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of our research.

Participants

Participants with consents were eligible for inclusion. We excluded individuals with total colectomy, inflammatory bowel disease, or CRC before the recruitment. To avoid a lead-time bias, we also excluded CRC cases that were diagnosed within 180 days from the recruitment date.

Exposures

For HP infection, there are serological and non-serological measures to detect the infection status. The non-serological tests include C14 breath test, C-urea breath test, rapid urease test, and histological diagnosis of biopsy specimens. These measurements detect the active infection and reflect the recovery. But unfortunately, the tests or their results are not available in the UK biobank. It is also hard to measure the persistent period of active infection even with available non-serological test results. Therefore, the non-serological tests were not included in our study.

The HP-specific assay consists of six antigens, including CagA, VacA, OMP, GroEL, catalyse, UreA. It meets the pre-specified validation criteria of 85% for sensitivity and specificity. The serum tests represent whoever is infected, and thus did not distinguish active infection from previous infection. We therefore regarded the seropositivity as a binary exposure. Seropositive cases are a combination in two definitions (UKB data field: 23073 and 23074). Seronegative cases are negative in both definitions. The UK biobank did a pilot study in 10,000 samples to test serological antigens of 20 infectious pathogens in 2016. Thus, test results were not available for the whole.

For PUD, cases are a combination of gastric ulcer cases, duodenal ulcer cases, other site peptic ulcer cases, and gastro-jejunal ulcer cases, with ICD-10 codes K25-K28. The remaining individuals are PUD controls.

Covariates

We included following covariates: sex (male and female), age at recruitment, smoking status (current, previous, or never), body mass index (BMI), education level (less than high school, high school or equivalent, or college or above), family history of digestive cancers (yes or not), regular use of aspirin or ibuprofen (yes or not), and race/ethnicity [17].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was incident CRC. We identified cases of CRC with codes C18 − C20 following ICD-10. We retrieved the cause of death from the primary cause of death coded by ICD-10 with corresponding dates (field ID 40001).

Statistical analysis

We presented descriptive variables as numbers and percentages, and continuous variables as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). The follow-up began at the date of recruitment (or the date of PUD if diagnosed after recruitment) and ended at death from any cause or censoring. The primary outcome was the cumulative incidence of CRC. We estimated the effect of exposures on the cumulative incidence of CRC using a competing risk model, regarding any non-CRC death as a competing risk event. We estimated the effect by the hazard ratios (HRs) from the Cox regression as well as 95% CIs with adjustment. Random forest imputation was performed to deal with missing values (under the R package mice) when conducting Cox regression.

All analyses were performed using R programming v4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Sensitivity analysis

In the sensitivity analysis for HP serology, we explored that whether the positivity of a single HP antigen was associated with CRC. In the sensitivity analysis for PUD, we excluded participants with regular take of aspirin or ibuprofen, which is the second common cause of PUD. We further excluded participants with baseline gastric cancer (with ICD-10 code C16) or abnormal secretion of gastrin (with ICD-10 code E16.4).

Genome-wide interaction analysis

Inspired by the interplay between HP infection and tumor immunotherapy, we wondered if there was an interaction between HP serology and genotype to affect CRC. Therefore, we performed a genome-wide interaction analysis for participants with HP serological measurements. We further excluded participants without imputed genetic data. For per-individual quality control, we excluded individuals with a high missing rate of available genotype (> 0.1). For per-single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) quality control, we removed SNPs with a low imputation score (< 0.3), high missingness (> 0.1), low minor allele frequency (< 0.01), and deviation from the expected Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (P < 1 × 10− 6). Therefore, we included 9,206,503 SNPs with a mean sample size of 9385 for a genome-wide interaction (GWI) analysis to identify any loci that modify the association of HP serology with CRC risk using a conditional logistic model. The summary statistics were further utilized for gene-based and gene-set analyses to identify genes and pathways that multiple SNPs tended to converge. Genotype data were handled in plink 2.0 and entered into the R program, version 4.4.1 (R Project for Statistical Computing) for interaction analyses. Gene-based and gene-set analyses were performed using a web-based FUMA tool. We determined SNPs that both suggestively (two-sided p < 1 × 10− 5) and significantly (two-sided p < 5 × 10− 8) interact with HP serology on CRC risk, adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and the top five principal components. A two-sided P < 3.24 × 10− 6 (0.05/15,429) was deemed to be significant in gene-based analysis. We considered a Bonferroni–adjusted two-sided P < 0.05 as significant in gene-set enrichment analysis.

Mendelian randomization

We did a bidirectional two-sample MR to investigate the causal relation of gene-proxied PUD on the outcome of CRC of the European ancestry and of the East Asian ancestry. The summary statistics for the HP-related PUD is not available. We therefore extended the phenotype to the all-cause PUD. Of the European ancestry (EUR), we retrieved the summary statistics for PUD from cnsgenomics (from UK biobank) with 16,666 cases and 439,661 controls and for CRC from FinnGen with 11,790 cases and 378,749 controls (R12 release version, 4 Nov 2024, https://r12.finngen.fi/) [18, 19]. Of the East Asian ancestry (EAS), we obtained the summary statistics for PUD from a recent GWAS meta-analysis with 29,739 Japanese ancestry cases and 240,675 controls [20], and for CRC from China Kadoorie Biobank (for colon cancer [ICD-10 C18] with 434 Chinese ancestry cases and 75,513 controls; for rectal cancer [ICD-10 C20] with 447 Chinese ancestry cases and 75,488 controls) [21]. The genetic instrumental variants were identified by P < 5 × 10− 8 and clumped for independence following the criteria (R2 < 0.001 and distance > 5,000 kb referred to the 1000 Genomes European or East Asian data) [22]. The details of MR analysis were provided in the supplementary file.

Results

Baseline characteristics

We included 502,133 individuals with consents into further screening after excluding 238 withdrawals. We excluded 3,771 individuals with histories of total colectomy, 4,392 individuals with IBD at baseline, and 1,480 cases with CRC before recruitment. We therefore included 492,490 into further analysis (Fig. 1). The mean age at recruitment was 56 ± 8 years old and 224,260 (46%) were men. The mean BMI was 27.43 ± 4.80 kg/m2. Regarding the country of birth, most participants (78%) were born in England. For ethnic background, the majority (95%) were white. Regarding education, college or above, high school or equivalent, and less than high school each accounted for around 1/3 of the participants. More than half (55%) never smoked, while the others (45%) were either previous or current smokers. There were 144,518 (29%) participants who regularly took aspirin or ibuprofen.11% had family history of digestive diseases and another 11% had diagnoses of DM (Table 1).

The serological tests for HP were only available for a random sample group of around 10,000 individuals (9,521 individuals after exclusion). Serological measurements and CRC

There were 9,505 individuals (positive for 3,198 and negative for 6,307) included in the analysis after excluding 16 individuals with less than 180-day follow up. Baseline characteristic was supplemented in Table S1. The median follow-up was 14.7 years (0.5 − 17.4). The 10-year cumulative incidences of CRC for the seropositive and seronegative group were 0.8% (95% CI 0.5 − 1.1%) and 1.1% (95% CI 0.9 − 1.4%), respectively (Fig. 2A). Thus, being positive for serologic antigens did not significantly increase the incidence of CRC (HR = 0.76, 95% CI 0.53 − 1.10, p = 0.15, adjusted for age, sex, and BMI). However, seropositivity increased the risk of non-CRC death by 23% (adjusted HR = 1.23, 95% CI 1.07 − 1.42, p = 0.005). The sensitivity analysis showed that the antigen VacA is marginally associated with CRC, in a negative relationship (OR = 0.64, 95% CI 0.37 − 1.04, p = 0.07). The survival analysis also showed that positivity for VacA decreased the risk of CRC (adjusted HR = 0.57, 95% CI 0.35 − 0.95, p = 0.03). We did not find the association between the other five antigens and CRC.

For the GWI analysis, we identified 10 SNPs that suggestively interacted with HP serology to modify CRC risk (Supplementary Figure S1). However, none reached the genome-wide significance threshold. Peptic ulcer diseases and CRC

There were 491,201 individuals included in this analysis after excluding 1,289 individuals with less than 180-day follow up. We identified 13,710 individuals diagnosed with PUD and the other 477,491 as non-PUD controls. Baseline characteristic was supplemented in Table S2. The median follow-up was 14.7 years (0.5 − 17.7). The 10-year cumulative incidences of CRC for PUD and non-PUD were 1.3% (95% CI 1.1 − 1.5%) and 1.1% (95% CI 1.0 − 1.1%), respectively (Fig. 2B). The risk of CRC was increased by 12% without reaching a statistical significance (HR = 1.12, 95% CI 0.96 − 1.30, p = 0.14). The risk of CRC was not significantly different from each other after adjusting (HR = 0.88, 95% CI 0.76 − 1.02, p = 0.09, adjusted for age, sex, and BMI). The diagnoses also increased the risk of non-CRC death by 1.44 times (adjusted HR = 2.44, 95% CI 2.33 − 2.55, p < 0.001).

In the sensitivity analysis, the 10-year cumulative incidence of CRC for articipants with regular take of NSAIDs (N = 144,062) was 1.1% (95% CI 1.0 − 1.2%). By excluding them, the 10-year cumulative incidences of CRC for PUD and non-PUD were 1.4% (95% CI 1.1 − 1.7%) and 1.1% (95% CI 1.0 − 1.1%), respectively, without reaching a statistical significance after adjusting (adjusted HR = 0.97, 95% CI 0.81 − 1.15, p = 0.71). By further excluding baseline cases with gastric cancer (N = 153), the trend for statistical significance did not alter (adjusted HR = 0.96, 95% CI 0.80 − 1.14, p = 0.62).

Causal relations

Of the European ancestry, we have 6 IVs that explained 1.8% of the variance for PUD. The F statistics was 1421 (Fig. 3). We did not find the casual relation of gene-proxied PUD on CRC (OR = 1.03, 95%CI 0.79 − 1.34, p = 0.82). For the reverse MR, we identified 19 IVs that explained 9.2% of the variance for CRC with the F statistics of 2090. In the analyses for the reverse causation, the genetically predicted CRC did not have significant effects on PUD (OR = 1.03, 95% CI = 0.96 − 1.10, p = 0.49). There was not significant pleiotropy for both MR analyses (p = 0.46 for forward MR and 0.65 for the reverse MR).

Discussion

We found that seropositivity for HP infection did not show an impact on the risk of CRC in a 15-year follow-up. Instead, PUD, which is a short-term clinical outcome of HP infection, increased the risk of CRC by 12%, though not reaching the statistical significance and being attenuated after adjusting. The MR analysis showed that the causal relation of PUD on CRC is ancestry-dependent: significant of East Asian ancestry, not of European ancestry.

The seropositivity for HP was not associated with the risk of CRC. This null association was parallel to the previous findings in the Irish and Spanish cohort [23, 24]. The positive association was previously observed in the US cohort which had a similar sample size as our study, consisting of 8126 participants in a case: control ratio of 1:1 [25]. They also found that the positive association was especially strong in the African American cohorts. In addition, Asian studies have tended to report associations [26]. Such dichotomy on the association between serological responses to HP and CRC might be dependent on racial/ethnic make-up of the study population. Our finding added another evidence to support the race/ethnicity-dependent association between seropositivity for HP and CRC.

Moreover, we did not find a significantly positive association between VacA-specific seropositivity and CRC, at least in this random sample of the UK population. Conversely, they were negatively associated with statistical significance. It aligned with Genua’s observation [23]. However, Butt et al. observed that VacA-postivity was with an increased risk of CRC in the European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition (EPIC) cohort [27]. On the one hand, such association, derived from a small sample consisting of 970 individuals, which is 10 times less than ours, was attenuated after adjusting. One the other hand, we noticed that the prevalence of sero-positivity in their study is higher than that in ours (47% vs. 34%). Their analysis defined sero-positivity for HP as ≥ 4/13 proteins in serology panel and collected data from seven European countries. Therefore, the regionally varied sero-prevalence and the difference serology panel may contribute to different observations.

HP-infectious diseases numerically increased the risk of CRC. Though not reaching the statistical significance, the 10-year cumulative incidence is higher by 0.2% in the absolute difference. The risk of CRC is higher by 12% in the relative number, which is consistent to the previous conclusion that it is a weak strength of association [28]. However, it did not show a consistent effect after adjusting. PUD, at least, is not an independent risk factor for CRC. Studies showed HP infection only had a small increased risk for CRC [29]. Therefore, the impact could be covered in a relatively small group of cases.

The results from MR analyses showed us that the causal relation of PUD on CRC is ancestry-dependent. In agreement with our investigation of the European ancestry, two MR studies that merely focused on the European sample came to the same conclusion as ours that there was no evident causal association of HP infection and CRC [30, 31]. This is the first MR analysis that revealed the PUD-to-CRC causal relation of the East Asian ancestry. Biologically, HP is highly genetically diverse owing to frequent DNA recombination [32]. The delivery of CagA and non-protein substrates into host cells mediated by the Cag T4SS, encoded by East Asian type cagPAI genes in certain HP strains, has been linked to the stimulation of chronic gastric inflammation.​​ [10, 33] This thus contributes to their more higher pathogenic potential to cause gastric cancer [10]. This could also be the biological mechanism of the ancestry-dependent HP-CRC causation.

The ancestral difference also explained the discrepancy between high prevalence of HP infection and low incidence of CRC in some areas [34]. It also hinted us that why HP eradication therapy provided inconsistent benefits on the reduction of developing CRC. The American cohort showed that eradication therapy reduced the incidence of CRC [29]. However, the Sweden cohort did not find the long-term benefit compared with the general population [35]. Therefore, our post-GWAS genetic finding, along with the clinical observation, provided evidence to the hypothesis that the association between HP and CRC is dependent on race/ethnicity/ancestry.

The GWI analysis did not provide strong genetic hints on the mechanisms of that HP arises CRC risk. The small sample size hindered us to reveal what reached a genome-wide significance level. HP could arise CRC risk from multiple mechanistic pathways. First, HP induces inflammatory responses, thus fostering a pro-carcinogenic microenvironment in the colon through elevated circulating cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) and reactive oxygen species [36]. Second, HP infection alters gastric acidity, potentially inducing colonic microbiota dysbiosis that favors carcinogenic bacterial proliferation [37]. It could interacts with genotypes. Future research with larger sample size would dig more into the SNP-HP interaction.

Our findings, combined with previous studies, indicate that the fact that HP is a carcinogen for CRC cannot be generalized. Therefore, HP eradication therapy would not be a prophylactic therapy for reducing CRC for everyone. Researchers in different areas would investigate how HP infection relates to CRC in their respective regions. Given the findings from the US and Asian cohort, it is recommended for individuals in American or Asian with HP infection to have successful HP eradication therapy regardless of symptoms. It is recommended to provide the therapy in Western European countries for symptomatic individuals and/or with active infection to not overuse the anti-biotic.

Here are some limitations. First, the sample size for HP serum tests is not as large as 0.5 a million. It is from a pilot study with a 1/50 random sample of the UK biobank. Therefore, the bias caused by a small sample is not unavoidable. But we did a comparison between the tested group and the non-tested group, and also adjusted variables that showed significant difference. Second, the non-serological measurements for HP infection, which indicate the active infection, were not available in our study, such as C14 breath test. In this case, we are unable to analyze how active infection, its recovery, or persistent infection exert their impacts on CRC. Third, not all PUD is caused by HP infection. Therefore, the causal relation of PUD on CRC could not directly suggest how HP infection relates to CRC.

Conclusions

Neither seropositivity for HP nor PUD showed a robust increase on the risk of CRC in a 15-year follow-up. The causal relation of PUD on CRC was significant of East Asian ancestry, not of European ancestry. Thus, HP eradication therapy would not be a prophylactic therapy for reducing CRC for all.
