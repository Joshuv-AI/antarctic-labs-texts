More than one pathway: ECMO training and credentialing☆
Authors: Michael Kouch, Nitin Puri, Emily Damuth, Christopher Noel, Jason Bartock, Adam Green
Published: 2024

To the Editor,

There is a paucity of literature regarding credentialing pathways and training requirements for cannulation. The excellent article by Duffin et al. in the March 2024 edition of Critical Care and Resuscitation fills this void and provides a solid framework for training that other programs can emulate.1 We believe this is a great opportunity to discuss options and benefits of alternative methods to achieve competency.

The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the need for access to ECMO centres throughout the world.2 The extensive training pathway described by Duffin et al. requires resources that may not be possible at all institutions thereby limiting formal accreditation and ECMO delivery. Our program also has medical intensivists as the primary cannulating physicians, but our credentialing pathway is different from that described. In order to be credentialed for cannulation, our institution requires the physician to be a specialist in critical care medicine, cardiology, surgery, or anaesthesia who is an active member of the ECMO team and has completed the following requirements: simulation training using a ballistic gel model, completion of an ECMO course, participation on ECMO rounds prior to the first week of ECMO service, involvement of a senior ECMO provider in clinical decisions during the first few months of service, 10 supervised cannulations in a two-year period, competency attestation by the chief of cardiac surgery, and greater than 80% attendance of bi-monthly ECMO meetings.

After reviewing the article by Duffin et al., we reviewed our own data to assess safety and outcome measures. We queried our ELSO database for adult patients cannulated by an intensivist between January 2019 and May 2024. The intensivist group was responsible for 213 cannulations in 106 separate cannulation episodes involving 99 patients. This included initiation of 92 V–V (3 dual-lumen cannulas), 9 V-A (4 ECPR), 3 VA-V (initiation or conversion) and 3 venous cannula additions to pre-existing circuits. Of the 213 cannulation attempts, one required assistance from surgery and one from interventional cardiology. There were 9 complications (4.23% event rate) of which 2 (0.94% event rate) were low significance, 1 (0.49% event rate) was medium significance and 6 were high significance (2.82% event rate). The 6 high significant events occurred in 5 patients. Four of the events were due to cannulation-related bleeding requiring transfusion of at least 2 U PRBCs, and 2 of these events were deemed not clinically significant. Transesophageal echocardiography and fluoroscopy were used sparingly (1.97% and 0.49% event rate, respectively). Chi-squared testing showed our rate of high-significance complications did not meet statistical significance when compared to Duffin et al. (p = 0.36).

The centre in Duffin et al. performs approximately 60 ECMO runs which are significantly higher than the average centre, which begets the question of whether their training model can be replicated at centres with lower volume.3 While our credentialing pathway is significantly different, similar safety and complication outcomes highlight the possibility of alternative competency pathways which may be more achievable globally. We believe that vascular access is a basic skill set of critical care physician and placing large bore access can be taught safely and relatively quickly to critical care fellows under appropriate supervision. This is especially true for two-site V–V cannulation with the use of ultrasound even without the use of transesophageal echocardiography or fluoroscopy.

We propose a blended approach to training and credentialing acknowledging that placement of retrograde reperfusion cannulas and dual-lumen cannulas are advanced skills that require higher training and a tiered approach to credentialing. We appreciate the focus Duffin et al. bring to the need for credentialing criteria and encourage discussion that will allow safe and effective access to training at a global scale and ultimately expand access to mechanical circulatory support.
