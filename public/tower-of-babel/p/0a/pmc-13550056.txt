Human Umbilical Cord Mesenchymal Stem Cells in Metabolic Dysfunction-associated Fatty Liver Disease (MAFLD) Therapy: Mechanisms, Clinical Efficacy, and Future Perspectives

ABSTRACT
There is currently no approved drug treatment for metabolic dysfunction-related fatty liver disease (MAFLD). Umbilical cord-derived mesenchymal stem cells (UC-MSCs) show therapeutic potential, but their mechanism of action is remains incompletely understood. Based on the above analysis, we also propose specific suggestions for standardized GMP production, mandatory genomic stability testing, and long-term safety registration. Introduction

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) has long been the main term used to describe chronic liver disease, which is closely related to obesity and the metabolic syndrome. However, as an exclusive diagnosis, NAFLD and alcoholic fatty liver disease (AFLD) fail to reflect the core metabolic characteristics of the disease and has certain limitations in clinical practice. In 2020, an international expert group released a consensus suggesting that it be renamed as MAFLD and proposed positive diagnostic criteria based on metabolic abnormalities. This change marks a deeper understanding of the disease, viewing it as a specific manifestation of systemic metabolic disorders in the liver, rather than as an independent liver disease.

MAFLD is a metabolic stress-induced liver injury closely related to insulin resistance (IR) and genetic susceptibility. Its histopathological features include simple steatosis, MAFLD, advanced fibrosis, and cirrhosis [1].

This process significantly increases the risk of end-stage liver complications, including liver failure and hepatocellular carcinoma. In addition, MAFLD is also independently associated with a significantly increased risk of systemic complications, especially cardiovascular diseases, which is attributed to the common underlying pathophysiological mechanisms, such as chronic inflammation, IR, and dyslipidemia [2].

Currently, the treatment of MAFLD mainly relies on lifestyle changes (diet control and exercise) and the management of metabolic complications. No FDA-approved specific therapeutic drugs are available for this condition. Liver transplantation is generally considered a definitive treatment.

Nevertheless, owing to factors such as donor shortage, surgical complications, rejection risks, and high costs, it is not applicable for the treatment of all patients with end-stage liver disease (ESLD) [3].

Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel treatment strategies for MAFLD. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) -a type of multipotent stem cells-possess the potential for self-renewal and multidirectional differentiation. They can differentiate into hepatocyte-like cells and, through paracrine effects, regulate immune responses, inhibit inflammation and fibrosis, and promote tissue repair and regeneration [4]. Compared with MSCs from other sources, UC-MSCs are a widely used source of mesenchymal stem cells, because contrary to other sources of MSCs (such as bone marrow (BM) and adipose tissue (AT)), UC-MSCs possess more primitive stem cell characteristics, stronger proliferative ability, lower immunogenicity, and better paracrine function.Consequently, they have become an ideal choice for cell therapy for MAFLD [5].

This review aims to systematically elucidate the multiple mechanisms of action of UC-MSCs and their derivatives in the treatment of MAFLD, with a particular emphasis on the complex molecular network established through direct cellular interactions and paracrine-mediated “multi-target synergistic effects.”

Pathological Mechanism of MAFLD

The pathogenesis of MAFLD is complex, entailing the interaction of multiple factors. The “double-hit” theory is widely accepted: the first hit is the accumulation of lipids in liver cells caused by IR; the second hit is the inflammatory response and fibrosis triggered by oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation [6]. These factors will activate hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) to transform into myofibroblasts, thereby leading to extracellular matrix deposition and fibrosis. The key signaling pathways involved include TGF-β/Smad, PDGF, CTGF and Wnt/β-catenin [7, 8] (Fig. 1). The pathogenesis of MAFLD provides a foundation for understanding the intervention points of UC-MSCs.

Characteristics and Acquisition of UC-MSCs

Umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells are derived from Wharton’s jelly and perivascular tissues of the umbilical cord, and they possess multi-directional differentiation potential [9], low immunogenicity [10], immunomodulatory function [11], and paracrine effects [12]. Compared with MSCs derived from bone marrow or fat, they have higher proliferation ability and are easier to obtain [5]. These characteristics make them highly attractive for the treatment of MAFLD.

Effective homing is a prerequisite for successful therapy. UC-MSCs constitutively express CXCR4, and SDF-1 released by injured liver tissue guides cell migration toward the lesion site. In MAFLD animal models, enhancing CXCR4 expression via ultrasound microbubbles significantly improves the accumulation efficiency of UC-MSCs in fibrotic regions [13]. When homing efficiency is low, subsequent effects such as anti-inflammation or mitochondrial transfer cannot be fully realized.

Inflammation and Immune Regulation

UC-MSCs secrete IL-10 and TSG-6, and utilize indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO1) to convert tryptophan into kynurenine. This process achieves dual effects: suppressing T-cell proliferation while inducing differentiation of naïve T cells into TREG (s) [15]. Additionally, UC-MSCs activate the PI3K/Akt pathway, driving Kupffer cells to shift from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby reducing TNF-α and IL-6 release [14].

Alleviation of Oxidative Stress

The Nrf2/HO-1 pathway plays a critical role in this phase. UC-MSCs activate Nrf2, which in turn drives the expression of antioxidant enzymes such as NQO-1 and HO-1. In galactosamine-induced liver injury models, this mechanism significantly reduces reactive oxygen species levels and restores mitochondrial membrane potential [16].

Modulation of the Gut-liver Axis

Intravenously administered UC-MSCs can also migrate to damaged intestinal mucosa, repairing the intestinal barrier through secretion of factors like TSG-6 [21]. Once the barrier is restored, the translocation of endotoxins (LPS) into the liver via the portal vein decreases, thereby reducing persistent activation of liver Kupffer cells through the TLR4/NF-κB pathway [22]. Recent studies suggest that exosomes derived from UC-MSCs may even reshape the gut microbiota composition [23, 24], although the causal link between microbiome changes and histological improvement in the liver remains to be further validated.

In summary, the core objective of Step 2 is to eliminate the “background noise” of inflammation and oxidative stress, creating a favorable microenvironment for subsequent repair processes.

Once the microenvironment stabilizes, UC-MSCs initiate multiple repair programs.

Mitochondrial Transfer—direct Metabolic Rescue

This is one of the most prominent advances in recent years. UC-MSCs not only exert effects through paracrine mechanisms but can also directly transfer functionally intact mitochondria to damaged hepatocytes. Two main pathways have been identified: tunneling nanotubes (TNTs) and exosomes [25]. Xue et al. (2025) observed via live-cell imaging that within four hours of co-culture, mitochondria moved along TNTs at a speed of approximately 0.5 μm/s; after 12 h, mitochondria derived from donor cells were detectable in about 35% of recipient hepatocytes [25].

Functionally, mitochondrial transfer significantly improves the metabolic capacity of hepatocytes. In a mouse model combining MAFLD with type 2 diabetes, UC-MSCs preloaded with healthy mitochondria (Mito-MSCs) reduced fasting blood glucose from 22.1 mmol/L to 15.9 mmol/L (a 28% decrease) and decreased liver triglyceride levels by 44%. Mechanistic studies revealed that transferred mitochondria restored expression of genes involved in fatty acid β-oxidation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{CPT}\mathrm{-1}\mathrm{{\alpha}}\mathrm{,}\mathrm{PPAR{\alpha}}$$\end{document}, increasing ATP production by 2.1-fold while reducing reactive oxygen species levels by 63% [17].

Notably, pretreatment further enhances therapeutic efficacy: pre-incubating UC-MSCs with isolated healthy mitochondria increased mitochondrial transfer efficiency by approximately 2.5-fold, reducing NAS scores from 5.2 to 1.8—significantly better than untreated conventional MSCs [17]. This provides a clear direction for engineering next-generation UC-MSC products.

Anti-fibrotic and Anti-apoptotic Signaling

Even after inflammation subsides, established fibrosis does not resolve spontaneously. UC-MSCs act on hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) through at least two pathways. They secrete HGF and BMPs, which block the TGF-β/Smad signaling pathway—the primary driver of HSC activation [18, 26]. In animal experiments, this intervention reduced collagen deposition and α-SMA expression by approximately 50% [26]. Regarding anti-apoptosis, UC-MSCs modulate the Bcl-2/Bax ratio; in an acute liver failure model, the number of TUNEL-positive hepatocytes was reduced by more than half [19].

Lipid Metabolism and Insulin Sensitivity

Improving hepatocyte lipid handling is another critical component of functional repair. Exosomes derived from UC-MSCs carry CAMKK1, which activates AMPK, thereby suppressing SREBP-1c (a key transcription factor for lipid synthesis) and promoting PPARα-mediated fatty acid oxidation [27]. In patients with type 2 diabetes, a single infusion of UC-MSCs (1 × 10⁶ cells/kg) improved HOMA-IR and reduced HbA1c [28], although it remains unclear whether this effect stems from overall systemic metabolic improvement or direct benefits to the liver.

Non-coding RNA Networks in Exosomes—a New Layer of Regulation

In addition to protein factors, UC-MSC-derived exosomes are rich in various regulatory RNAs capable of actively reshaping recipient cell functions. Three recently discovered molecular axes are particularly representative:

circ-Tulp4 / miR-34a / NF-κB axis: Han et al. (2025) found that circular RNA circ-Tulp4 is enriched in UC-MSC exosomes. This molecule acts as a sponge for miR-34a, thereby relieving miR-34a’s inhibition of its target gene ABHD6, subsequently suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis. In mice with diabetes combined with MAFLD, exosomes loaded with circ-Tulp4 reduced serum ALT from 185 U/L to 72 U/L and decreased liver IL-1β levels by 58% [29].

miR-499a-5p / ETS1 / GPX4 ferroptosis axis: Wang et al. (2025) demonstrated that miR-499a-5p in exosomes directly targets ETS1, a transcriptional repressor of GPX4. By downregulating ETS1, miR-499a-5p upregulates GPX4 expression, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis in hepatic stellate cells—a form of iron-dependent cell death that exacerbates fibrosis. In a CCl₄ plus high-fat diet-induced fibrosis model, this intervention reduced type I collagen deposition by 51% and α-SMA-positive area by 47% [30].

miR-627-5p / FTO axis: Cheng et al. (2021) found that miR-627-5p silences FTO (fat mass and obesity-associated protein), thereby downregulating the expression of SREBP-1c and FAS. In hepatocyte culture systems, this led to approximately 40% reduction in triglyceride accumulation [31].

Taken together, these findings indicate that UC-MSCs do not simply release a “mixed cocktail” of factors, but instead deliver precisely engineered RNA combinations via exosomes, simultaneously targeting multiple nodes within disease networks.

Integration and Crosstalk among the Three Steps

The above three steps are not strictly linear but interact with one another. For example, exosomes released during the microenvironment remodeling phase already carry miRNAs that function in the subsequent functional repair stage [12, 20]. Nevertheless, conceptualizing the process as “homing → microenvironment remodeling → functional repair” helps explain how a single cell infusion can generate multifaceted therapeutic effects—more importantly, this framework highlights future optimization directions: enhancing homing efficiency [13], optimizing mitochondrial transfer through preconditioning [17], and engineering exosomes to enrich specific circRNAs [29, 30] (Fig. 2).

Signaling pathways involved in UC-MSC treatment of MAFLD

UC-MSCs exert therapeutic effects by regulating a complex signaling pathway network. The mechanism of action can be summarized at two levels: Direct interventional effects: Recent studies have demonstrated that UC-MSCs mediate mitochondrial transfer to hepatocytes, thereby improving mitochondrial function and alleviating hepatic steatosis in NAFLD [17]. Mechanistically, UC-MSC treatment has been shown to activate the AMPK/mTOR pathway through secreted factors such as HGF, which regulates glucose and lipid metabolism [39]. Additionally, UC-MSCs enhance cellular antioxidant capacity through activation of the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway, leading to improved mitochondrial function, reduced reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and restored mitochondrial membrane potential and ATP levels in injured hepatocytes [16].

Paracrine regulation: UC-MSCs secrete a variety of bioactive factors, including HGF and extracellular vesicles (EVs), which synergistically contribute to their therapeutic effects [40].

Notably, UC-MSC-derived EVs have been shown to carry circular RNA circ-Tulp4, which attenuates diabetes-associated NAFLD by inhibiting hepatocyte pyroptosis through the HNRNPC/ABHD6 axis [29]. Furthermore, MSC-derived exosomes downregulate key pro-fibrotic factors including TGF-β and Smad2, thereby alleviating hepatic fibrosis and inflammation [41]. These paracrine mediators collectively modulate inflammatory and fibrotic responses, contributing to the restoration of liver homeostasis.

Table 1 further expands this network, listing over ten key pathways affected by UC-MSCs, including insulin signaling (PI3K/Akt), apoptosis (Bcl-2/Bax), inflammation (IL-6/JAK/STAT), and cell differentiation (Notch), etc. This indicates that the therapeutic effect of UC-MSCs is based on the global and networked reprogramming of liver metabolism, inflammation, fibrosis, and cell fate, rather than the action of a single target.

Clinical and Preclinical Research Progress

Extensive animal studies have demonstrated the therapeutic efficacy of UC-MSCs in MAFLD. However, the clinical translation of UC-MSCs for MAFLD remains in its early infancy. To date, only a very limited number of human clinical trials have been reported (as summarized in Table 2). It is crucial to note that these existing human studies primarily enrolled patients with Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM), where liver-related metrics were often evaluated as secondary or exploratory endpoints rather than the primary focus. Furthermore, the reported clinical outcomes have been largely restricted to improvements in systemic metabolic parameters (such as HbA1c) and surrogate biochemical markers (such as ALT and GGT). Currently, there is a distinct lack of clinical trials utilizing definitive, disease-specific histological endpoints evaluated via liver biopsy, elastography, or MRI-PDFF. Consequently, while the early data is promising, it remains challenging to definitively determine whether the observed benefits represent a true histological resolution of MAFLD/MASH pathology or are primarily driven by a metabolic “spillover” effect resulting from improved systemic insulin sensitivity.

Research utilizing animal models has advanced significantly in depth and sophistication. In addition to traditional models such as those induced by a high-fat diet (HFD) or a methionine-and choline-deficient (MCD) diet, recent studies have begun to employ models that more closely recapitulate human disease pathology.

Examples include hamster models fed a high-fat, high-fructose, and high-cholesterol (FFC) diet, as well as SREBP-1c transgenic mice.

These models allow for a more comprehensive evaluation of the effects of UC-MSCs on complex metabolic dysregulation and fibrosis progression [42].

The intervention strategies have also changed accordingly. The previous approach of relying solely on simple cell infusion is no longer applicable. Current methods increasingly utilize conditioned medium (CM) or engineered exosomes loaded with specific microRNAs for targeted therapy.

These improved methods have demonstrated superior safety and higher controllability in preclinical studies [43].

However, at present, there is still a lack of large-scale Phase III clinical trials to confirm the exact histological improvement (such as fibrosis reversal) effect of this treatment, and the optimal cell dose, infusion interval, and treatment course have not yet been standardized.

Challenges and Future Prospects

Although Table 2 summarizes the preliminary research that has confirmed the potential of UC-MSCs and their derivatives in treating MAFLD/MASH, translating these findings into widely accepted clinical therapies still faces multiple challenges and uncertainties and requires critical examination.1. Limitations of preclinical models and depth of mechanisms

1. Limitations of preclinical models and depth of mechanisms

1. Limitations of preclinical models and depth of mechanisms

Although existing animal studies have revealed that UC-MSCs reduce liver fat metabolism and fibrosis through various mechanisms (such as exosome delivery of CAMKK1 [27] or miR-499a-5p [30]), these models have inherent flaws.

Commonly used diet-induced (such as HFD, MCD) or chemical injury (CCl₄) models can only simulate some pathological features of human MAFLD/MASH, especially in terms of disease progression speed, systemic metabolic complications, and immune microenvionment, where significant differences exist.

Studies mainly focus on local effects in the liver, but the systemic network of how UC-MSCs regulate the “gut-liver axis” and improve peripheral IR is still poorly depicted.

Although pioneering work has shown that MSC treatment can reverse gut microbiome and metabolome disorders in NASH models [44], the precise signaling mechanisms by which they orchestrate this complex inter-organ crosstalk remain to be fully elucidated.

Moreover, novel mechanisms such as mitochondrial transfer have emerged as a critical mode of action. Recent studies demonstrate that UC-MSCs can directly transfer mitochondria to damaged hepatocytes viatunneling nanotubes (TNTs) to alleviate lipid accumulation [25], and strategies to enhance this transfer, such as mitochondrial preconditioning, significantly boost their therapeutic efficacy in NAFLD [17]. These findings open new avenues for understanding and enhancing UC-MSC therapy beyond the classical paracrine paradigm.

However, translating these findings to human clinical settings presents significant challenges. While TNT-mediated transfer is highly efficient in controlled in vitro environments or small animal models, the efficiency of this transfer in the complex, highly inflamed microenvironment of a human fibrotic liver remains uncertain. Furthermore, the longevity and functional persistence of transplanted xenogeneic or allogeneic mitochondria within human hepatocytes are currently unknown, necessitating advanced in vivo tracking techniques in future clinical trials.2. Production processes, regulation and commercialization barriers

2. Production processes, regulation and commercialization barriers

2. Production processes, regulation and commercialization barriers

The large-scale application of UC-MSC therapy faces strict production and regulatory thresholds. Regulatory pathways and GMP requirements: Whether as an advanced therapeutic medical product (ATMP, EMA) or a cell and gene therapy product (FDA), UC-MSC preparations must follow Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) for production [45].

This involves full-chain control from donor screening, cell collection, expansion, cryopreservation to release, ensuring the consistency, purity, efficacy, and sterility of the product [45, 46].

Batch-to-batch variations and quality control: Individual donor variations, culture generations, and culture conditions (such as serum usage) can all lead to batch-to-batch variations in cell activity and functional properties [47].

Future standardization must establish release standards based on key quality attributes (CQAs), such as specific immune regulatory molecule expression profiles, exosome production, or mitochondrial function indicators [48, 49].

Cost-benefit analysis: The current preparation costs of autologous or allogeneic UC-MSC therapies are high [50].

Promoting the construction of cell banks (such as donor master cell banks and working cell banks), developing serum-free media [51], and the transformation to cell-free products (such as exosomes, conditioned media [52]) are important directions to reduce production costs and improve accessibility. In the future, rigorous health economics research is needed to prove its long-term cost-effectiveness compared to traditional treatments or liver transplantation.3. Core obstacles in translating from animal to clinical

3. Core obstacles in translating from animal to clinical

3. Core obstacles in translating from animal to clinical

The success of preclinical studies has not directly addressed the key bottlenecks in the transition.

Heterogeneity and standardization of formulations: Different studies use UC-MSCs or their exosomes from various donor sources [53]. There are significant differences in donor origin, culture conditions, isolation methods, and dose (such as the number of cells in the table), which makes it difficult to directly compare and replicate the efficacy [54, 55]. Currently, there is a lack of unified quality control standards based on functional efficacy (such as immune regulatory ability, specific active factor content) [55].

Uncertainty of the best treatment plan: The clinical studies in Table 2 used different doses ranging from 50 to 100 × 10⁶ cells to 1 × 10⁶ cells/kg, and involved either single or short-term infusions [56].

There is no consensus on the optimal dose, administration route (intravenous vs. potentially more targeted hepatic artery infusion), treatment interval, and course [57].

Retrospective studies suggest improvements in biochemical indicators, but lack histological endpoints; Phase II trials improve metabolic parameters, but the impact on the core lesion of liver fibrosis is unclear [58].4. Insufficient clinical evidence and long-term uncertainty

4. Insufficient clinical evidence and long-term uncertainty

4. Insufficient clinical evidence and long-term uncertainty

There are obvious gaps in the existing clinical data: Limited evidence of efficacy: Most clinical studies (such as Table 2) have the primary endpoint of serum (ALT, GGT) or metabolic indicators (HbA1c), lacking strict large-scale randomized controlled trials with a hard endpoint of liver histological improvement (steatosis, inflammation, fibrosis). This makes it difficult to assess whether UC-MSCs can truly reverse the disease process [31, 59].

Ambiguity in patient selection and efficacy prediction: Studies mostly focus on patient groups with T2DM, and the efficacy of UC-MSCs for non-diabetic MAFLD patients is unclear. Meanwhile, there is an urgent need to identify reliable biomarkers capable of predicting therapeutic response [59]. To achieve personalized treatment, future studies should prospectively evaluate specific biomarker candidates, such as circulating exosomal microRNAs \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{e}\mathrm{.}\mathrm{g}\mathrm{.}\mathrm{miR}\mathrm{-122,}\mathrm{miR}\mathrm{-34}\mathrm{a}$$\end{document} or baseline serum cytokine profiles (e.g.IL-6, TNF-α, adiponectin), to stratify MAFLD patients and identify those most likely to benefit from MSC therapy [31].

Unknown long-term safety and efficacy persistence: All studies have not provided long-term follow-up data beyond 5 years [60]. For cell therapy, potential unexpected differentiation, abnormal immune responses, long-term effects on tumor occurrence risk, and the possibility of efficacy decline over time (requiring repeated administration? ) are key safety issues that remain unresolved. After more than 20 years of MSC research, the efficacy of MSC therapy remains inconsistent and many trials have failed to meet their efficacy endpoint, highlighting the need for more robust long-term data [61, 62]. Tumorigenicity risk and long-term safety monitoring: Although UC-MSCs are considered to have a relatively low tumorigenic risk, their fate after long-term implantation, potential abnormal differentiation, or genomic instability still need to be closely evaluated [63, 64].

According to regulatory guidelines for advanced therapy medicinal products (ATMPs), a series of safety assessments should be conducted on UC-MSC preparations, Given the current 5-year follow-up gap and potential risks, we strongly emphasize the necessity of establishing standardized genomic stability assays—such as high-resolution karyotyping and chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA)-as mandatory release criteria for all clinical-grade UC-MSC products prior to patient infusion. This includes karyotype analysis and in vivo tumorigenicity tests in immunodeficient mouse models [65]. Future clinical research protocols must establish a long-term follow-up registration system and track patients for extended periods through regular imaging examinations (such as liver ultrasound/elastography) and tumor marker monitoring to clarify their long-term safety [66]. This will be one of the cornerstones for the clinical translation of this therapy.5. Future breakthrough directions

5. Future breakthrough directions

5. Future breakthrough directions

To overcome the aforementioned challenges, future research is needed:(1) Utilize more precise models, such as organoids or humanized animal models, to verify the mechanism [67].(2) Promote the standardized production of cell-free preparations based on exosomes or conditioned media.(3) Conduct phase III clinical trials with the main endpoint of histological improvement, including patients at different disease stages. Critically, given the significant disparity in current clinical doses (ranging from 1 × 10⁶ cells/kg to flat doses of 100 × 10⁶ cells), there is an urgent call to action for rigorous, multi-arm dose-finding studies to establish standardized pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic-equivalent models for cell therapy.(4) Combine multi-omics technologies to search for efficacy prediction markers.(5) Establish a strict long-term safety monitoring registration system.
