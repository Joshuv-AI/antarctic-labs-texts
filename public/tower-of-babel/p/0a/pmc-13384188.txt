Damage control surgery in large-scale combat operations: still a life-saving strategy or a bridge to nowhere?
Authors: Mason Hil Remondelli, Kennedy Nye, Todd Collin, Jonathan Wang, Amy Wang, Rex Atwood, Joseph Bozzay, Woo Do, Ronald Hardin, Eric Elster, Patrick F Walker, Matthew J Bradley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDamage Control Surgery (DCS), originally developed to stabilize critically injured trauma patients, was widely adopted during the Global War on Terror, supported by rapid evacuation and advanced medical infrastructure. Future large-scale combat operations will likely severely disrupt this model due to contested airspace and delayed transport. Patients who have undergone DCS may then be stuck without access to definitive care, overwhelming facilities with limited critical care capacity. A more nuanced context-driven approach to surgical decision-making and embracing a strategy of definitive repair for carefully selected patients could potentially help alleviate the expected challenges we will face in future resource-constrained environments.

Introduction: damage control surgery—a paradigm under pressure

Born out of the Navy’s compartmentalized approach to repair warships in combat, damage control surgery (DCS) has replaced a ‘fix everything at once’ mentality of surgery in traumatically injured patients. First employed in urban civilian trauma centers and refined over two decades of military conflict in the Global War on Terror (GWOT), this staged approach prioritizes physiological stabilization over immediate anatomical repair (‘physiology over anatomy’).1 2 DCS involves an abbreviated initial operation focused solely on controlling life-threatening hemorrhage and gross contamination, followed by resuscitation to restore physiological derangements and correct the ‘lethal triad’ (or diamond) of hypothermia, acidosis, coagulopathy and now recognized hypocalcemia.3 4 Only after physiological stabilization is achieved does the patient return for definitive surgical repair of initial injuries.

The adoption and refinement of DCS, alongside damage control resuscitation (DCR) strategies emphasizing early blood product use and permissive hypotension, were hallmarks of military medical advancement during GWOT.5 This era was primarily characterized by uncontested air superiority, which enabled rapid medical evacuation, often within the ‘Golden Hour’.6 Robust, well-resourced role 3 (in-theater) and role 4 (stateside or overseas) medical treatment facilities (MTFs) provided the necessary critical care and definitive surgical capabilities essential for the later stages of the DCS paradigm.7 This tiered military trauma system, reliant on swift movement between echelons of care, contributed significantly to unprecedented combat casualty survival rates.8

However, the current geostrategic landscape is shifting. The prospect of large-scale combat operations (LSCO) against peer or near-peer adversaries presents a starkly different reality from recent conflicts.9 Future battlefields are expected to involve contested domains, challenging the fundamental assumptions that enabled DCS’s success in GWOT.10 Air superiority may no longer be guaranteed, resulting in prolonged evacuation timelines—from hours to potentially several days. Forward surgical elements (role 2) will likely face overwhelming Mass Casualty (MASCAL) events, with daily casualty estimates reaching up to 3000.11 Resources such as blood products and surgical/critical care capabilities and capacity will be severely constrained. Meanwhile, irregular warfare (IW), including operations by non-state actors and special operations forces, may introduce unpredictable disruptions in care phases.12 Even the reliance of partner nations and indigenous forces as a solution to combat IW introduces questions about the continuity of care through different trauma systems.13

Ultimately, these converging challenges demand critical re-evaluation: Is DCS, a life-saving bridge in past conflicts, the optimal primary surgical strategy for LSCO? When evacuation is delayed and resources scarce, does the DCS procedure become a ‘bridge to nowhere’, stabilizing casualties yet stranding them without follow-on care? This paper examines DCS’s principles and limits and argues that the harsher realities of future LSCO may require a more tailored approach, including earlier definitive repair for selected patients.

Damage control surgery and the open abdomen

Initiation of DCS in trauma hinges on recognizing impending physiological collapse, often manifested by the lethal triad (or diamond).14 While specific thresholds vary, common indicators include decreased core body temperature, profound metabolic acidosis and clinical or laboratory evidence of coagulopathy. Massive transfusion requirement is another strong indicator of physiological depletion, mandating an abbreviated approach in the surgical management of traumatic injuries.15

Beyond physiology, certain complex anatomical injuries often necessitate DCS, including major vascular injuries (abdominal, thoracic, pelvic), severe solid organ trauma (especially liver injuries requiring packing), combined vascular and visceral injuries or the inability to achieve primary fascial closure without tension or precipitating abdominal compartment syndrome (ACS).1517 However, many indications rely on surgeon judgment and expert consensus rather than high-level evidence, leading to variability in practice and concerns about potential overuse.

During GWOT, the DCS paradigm matured within a specific logistical framework. Role 2 facilities (forward surgical care) became adept at performing the initial abbreviated surgery, contingent on expeditious evacuation timeframes to transfer patients to role 3 or 4 MTFs for the critical Intensive Care Unit (ICU) resuscitation phase (phase 2) and subsequent definitive repairs (phase 3). The evolution of DCR, emphasizing permissive hypotension and early, balanced, hemostatic transfusion (1:1:1 ratios or whole blood), also optimized initial stabilization.18

Furthermore, at the outset of the GWOT conflict, advanced imaging modalities such as CT and MRI were seldom available in-theater. In the absence of reliable imaging, surgical decision-making relied heavily on clinical assessment, leading to a higher rate of exploratory laparotomies and reliance on DCS. Over time, however, the progressive integration of advanced imaging capabilities into deployed medical facilities significantly reduced the rate of non-therapeutic laparotomies by enabling more accurate preoperative diagnostics.19

A key component and significant source of morbidity in DCS is the management of the open abdomen (OA) using temporary abdominal closure techniques. While necessary to avoid ACS and allow for planned re-exploration, the OA exposes patients to risks including intra-abdominal infections/abscesses (rates cited from 8.5% to 49%), devastating enteroatmospheric fistulae (4.5% to 25%), fascial retraction, leading to loss of domain and planned ventral hernias, and frozen abdomen.2024 Additionally, ACS can still occur despite an OA, particularly with massive resuscitation.25 Beyond the abdomen, systemic complications can significantly influence the ICU course of an OA patient. Challenges such as nutritional support, ventilator-associated complications (up to 8.1%) and prolonged systemic inflammation can further complicate the DCS paradigm.26 27 Moreover, the injury pattern, severity and concomitant injuries may influence complication rates; for instance, colon injuries managed with DCS are associated with significantly higher colonic complications (30%) when compared with those treated by definitive surgery (12%).28 Established evidence also underscores the importance of minimizing OA time to 5–8 days, as delayed management and closure exacerbate complications.29 30

Comparing DCS to initial definitive repair reveals important trade-offs. While DCS demonstrably saves lives in the most critically injured, studies examining patients where surgeons felt either approach might be viable (equipoise) suggest that definitive repair, when successful, is associated with significantly fewer hospital, ICU and ventilator days.31 Mortality and major abdominal complication rates appear similar between appropriately selected DCS and definitive repair groups in matched analyses, though definitive conclusions are hampered by selection bias and the lack of large RCTs.20 32 Initial operative times are shorter for DCS, but the cumulative time across multiple stages may be longer. These findings suggest that a lower threshold for employing definitive repair may be warranted for patients who might otherwise receive DCS in the civilian system. This strategy avoids prolonging the critical care burdens associated with DCS in a resource-limited military trauma system.

Understanding the problem of DCS in future conflicts

LSCO upend the damage-control surgery (DCS) model refined during the GWOT, which depends on swift, unobstructed evacuation. Peer adversaries can deny airspace and block evacuation routes, leaving casualties at role 2 facilities far beyond the doctrinal 2-hour post-DCS window—often 8 to 24 hours, or even days, as seen in Ukraine.33

Evacuation delays expose a critical gap. Role 2 units—small, mobile teams such as Forward Surgical Teams, Ground Surgical Teams (GSTs) and Austere Resuscitative Surgical Care (ARSC) teams—are equipped for phase 1 DCS and brief stabilization (≤6–12 hours).34 They lack the beds, ICU capability, specialist staff, advanced diagnostics and logistical depth required for prolonged critical care (phase 2) or definitive repair (phase 3).35 Holding critically ill post-DCS patients beyond an estimated 8-hour ‘tipping point’ significantly increases clinical risk due to these limitations.

The projected flood of casualties in LSCO will amplify evacuation challenges. Thousands of wounded each day could overwhelm forward medical units, demanding far stricter triage than in the GWOT and forcing painful choices about who receives limited blood, ventilators and surgical intervention.36 This anticipated surge in casualties would not only strain completion of the full DCS pathway, but the entire trauma infrastructure, which remains largely unprepared for the demands of LSCO.37

Moreover, the complexities extend to multinational and humanitarian operations. Caring for partner nation forces or indigenous personnel raises concerns about the continuity of care if allied systems lack the integrated capability to manage patients through all DCR phases after an initial DCS procedure performed by US forces.38 This discontinuity argues for attempting definitive repair more readily in such patients, provided their condition and the injury pattern allow.

Previous studies in resource-limited settings have highlighted critical challenges in implementing DCS, particularly during the resuscitative phase following the initial operation. In a retrospective cohort study conducted at Aga Khan University Hospital, the majority of DCS-related deaths (86%) occurred before the first take-back operation, underscoring the vulnerability of patients in the second phase of care.39 Delays in definitive treatment were associated with worsened physiological states, including profound acidosis, hypothermia and coagulopathy. These complications were exacerbated by limited access to blood products, warming devices and advanced pharmacologic agents. Moreover, the absence of standardized critical care protocols hindered adequate resuscitation and optimization. Multivariate analysis revealed that the need for packed red blood cell transfusions and the presence of major vascular injuries were independent predictors of mortality. These findings suggest that DCS may inadvertently serve as a bridge to nowhere without adequate resources and system-wide support.

In essence, the LSCO environment threatens to sever the critical link between the initial DCS procedure and the subsequent, necessary phases of care. If the “bridge” built by DCS at role 2 leads only to a prolonged, unsupported hold with delayed or impossible evacuation to definitive care, its utility as a universal strategy must be questioned. It risks becoming a bridge to nowhere, leaving stabilized but ultimately untreatable patients in facilities ill-equipped for their long-term needs.

Patient selection: rethinking the indications and threshold for definitive repair

The future reality of LSCOs requires a fresh look at how we choose an initial surgical strategy. DCS is still crucial for casualties in profound physiologic collapse (the lethal triad/diamond) or with injuries too complex for lengthy procedures. For patients in the ‘gray zone’, however—especially when evacuation will be delayed—the calculus shifts.

Could initial definitive surgical repair be advantageous for certain patients under these constraints? The ideal candidate for definitive repair in an austere, delayed-evacuation setting would need to meet multiple domains:

Physiological resilience: the patient must demonstrate not just initial stability, but a rapid and sustained response to resuscitation (ideally DCR principles), achieving near-normal physiological parameters (temperature, normalizing lactate/base deficit, no coagulopathy), with minimal ongoing support needed. A patient initially triggering DCS criteria who rapidly normalizes might be reconsidered, but only if other factors permit.

The concept of surgeon equipoise, genuine uncertainty about the best approach, becomes relevant here. Recent prospective, multicenter data from Harvin et al have characterized a cohort of patients who underwent damage control laparotomy (DCL) but were retrospectively adjudicated by institutional panels to have been physiologically and anatomically appropriate for definitive laparotomy (DEF) at the index operation. This ‘potential DEF’ subgroup demonstrated comparable rates of major abdominal complications relative to matched DEF controls (21% vs 31%, posterior probability 56%). Yet, DEF was associated with a higher likelihood of increased hospital-free (72%), ICU-free (77%) and ventilator-free days (72%). Determinants of intraoperative decision-making in this context included physiologic trajectory, anatomical burden of injury, anticipated operative duration, institutional resource availability and anticipated delays in evacuation or access to higher echelons of care.40

In the resource-constrained, evacuation-limited environments anticipated during LSCO, the traditional indications for DCL may lose discriminatory value, and the threshold for pursuing definitive repair at the initial operation may appropriately decrease. As the feasibility of staged reoperation and critical care support diminishes in austere theaters, the margin of equipoise correspondingly narrows, favoring definitive intervention when survivable physiology and tactical conditions permit. Nevertheless, DCL should remain an accepted and indispensable strategy in forward surgical care when employed for clear and purposeful indications. When used reflexively or without a feasible pathway to follow-on care; however, its benefits may be lost and outcomes compromised, turning a means of survival into an end in itself.

Resource and personnel considerations: the austere surgery reality

The choice between DCS and definitive repair is inextricably linked to resource availability, a factor brought into sharp focus by the anticipated constraints of LSCO. Given the projected volume of casualties, role 2 teams may not have the time or resources to perform planned reoperations, particularly those requiring complex reconstruction, necessitating prioritization based on survivability, physiologic stability and operational necessity. Managing a patient after DCS, particularly with an OA, often imposes a substantial resource burden. Although some patients with an OA could potentially be extubated and managed expectantly, most usually necessitate ICU-level care for physiological monitoring and correction, mechanical ventilation (often prolonged), specialized nursing for OA management, significant fluid and nutritional support and multiple planned returns to the operating room for re-exploration and eventual closure. While a single, successful definitive repair avoids this specific burden, it requires sufficient initial resources (OR time, personnel, blood products) and adequate postoperative monitoring capability.

Forward surgical elements (Role 2, ARSC, GST, etc.) are deliberately lean, designed for mobility and rapid intervention, not sustained critical care. Personnel often consist of a single surgeon and anesthesia provider, with limited nursing/technical support and a lack of dedicated intensivists or extensive critical care experience. Equipment is tailored for a small number of patients, often lacking advanced imaging, comprehensive labs, or multiple OR suites. Blood product availability is a significant constraint, relying on limited supplies or walking blood banks. The Joint Trauma System has recognized these challenges in relation to future conflicts and recently developed the ARSC Curriculum, which emphasized a continuum of care including damage control, prolonged resuscitation and include definitive repair.41 42 While this demonstrates an important forward-thinking initiative, it should mark only the beginning of a broader cultural shift in how we view surgical readiness and capabilities for future conflicts.

LSCOs expose a structural flaw in current doctrine. Role 2 units are designed to perform DCS and expeditiously evacuate patients, often within hours—a timeline that may become unrealistic in LSCO. Without rapid evacuation, automatic DCS can turn these facilities into holding units for complex, critically ill casualties, quickly exhausting their limited beds, staff and supplies. Each patient must, therefore, be thoughtfully evaluated: for some, performing definitive surgery up front may use fewer resources and not compromise outcomes. In carefully selected cases, definitive repair may better serve both the casualty and the operational effectiveness of the role 2 team, in addition to reducing the downstream surgical burden for next levels of care.

Conclusion: rebuilding the bridge or charting a new course?

Considered revolutionary in prior conflicts, DCS hinges on rapid evacuation and access to ICU-level follow-on care, assumptions that may collapse in LSCO. In environments marked by delayed evacuation, contested logistics and overwhelming casualties, defaulting to DCS risks stabilizing patients only to strand them without definitive treatment. While DCS still remains essential for those in extremis, military surgeons must employ its use with deliberate purpose and clear rationale, otherwise, it risks becoming a bridge to nowhere. In selecting patients with stable physiology, straightforward injuries and a conducive environment, early definitive repair may prevent the complications and resource burdens of prolonged damage control. Embracing a more nuanced, context-driven approach to surgical decision-making is critical to sustaining survivability in future wars.
