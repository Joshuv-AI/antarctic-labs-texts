Efficacy and safety of modified Gwakjeongtang (herbal medicine) for diarrhea-predominant irritable bowel syndrome: A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial
Authors: Yujin Choi, Na-Yeon Ha, Ae-Ran Kim, Ojin Kwon, Haein Jeong, Heung Joo Yuk, Ki-Sun Park, Jieun Kim, Jinsung Kim, Hyungjun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIrritable bowel syndrome (IBS) significantly impairs quality of life, and current treatments often provide insufficient symptom relief. Gwakjeongtang is the most frequently prescribed herbal medicine for IBS patients in Korea. We aimed to explore the efficacy and safety of modified Gwakjeongtang (mGJT) compared with placebo in patients with diarrhea-predominant IBS (IBS-D).MethodsThis pilot double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group randomized clinical trial was conducted at a single academic medical center from September 2023 to January 2025. Adults with IBS-D meeting Rome IV criteria were enrolled. Of 63 participants screened, 60 were randomized 1:1 to receive mGJT granules (10 g) or placebo administered orally three times daily for 4 weeks. The primary outcome was overall response rate, defined as achieving daily response criteria (≥30% reduction in worst daily abdominal pain intensity from baseline and Bristol Stool Scale ≤5 for all bowel movements) on ≥50% of treatment days over 4 weeks.ResultsAmong 60 randomized participants (16 male; mean age 46.0 years), 59 (98.3%) completed primary outcome assessment. The overall response rate was significantly higher with mGJT than placebo (55.2% vs. 26.7%; odds ratio 3.4; 95% confidence interval 1.1–10.1; P=.03). Sensitivity analysis assuming the missing participant was a nonresponder showed consistent results (53.3% vs. 26.7%; odds ratio 3.1; 95% confidence interval 1.1–9.3; P=.04). No intervention-related adverse events occurred.ConclusionThese pilot findings suggest mGJT may benefit patients with IBS-D. Confirmation in a larger definitive trial is warranted given the pilot study limitations and small sample size.Trial registrationClinical Research Information Service of Korea (KCT0008523).Keywords: Irritable Bowel Syndrome, Herbal Medicine, Randomized Controlled Trial, Huoxiang-zhengqi

Introduction

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a chronic functional gastrointestinal disorder characterized by recurrent abdominal pain associated with changes in bowel movement frequency or stool consistency.1,2 It affects approximately 1 in 10 people globally3 and represents a significant financial burden on employers.4 Patients with IBS experience significant impairment in their quality of life5 due to recurrent symptoms, making effective symptom management crucial. Current pharmacological treatments for IBS include antispasmodics, probiotics, and antidiarrheal agents, with most patients receiving a combination of these.6 However, these symptom-based therapeutic approaches often fail to provide adequate relief, creating unmet medical needs.

An overview of systematic reviews showed superior efficacy of herbal medicine compared with placebos, conventional medicine, or probiotics in treating IBS, although the quality of evidence generally remains low.7 In traditional East Asian medicine, Gwakhyangjunggi-san (also known as Gwakjeongtang [GJT], Huoxiang-Zhengqi-San in Chinese, and Kakko-Seiki-San in Japanese) is the herbal medicine formula most frequently prescribed for treating IBS, with 55.0% of Korean medicine doctors reporting its use in a survey.8 GJT is a traditional multi-herb formula containing key ingredients such as Agastache rugosa, Magnolia officinalis, Citrus unshiu, and Pinellia ternata, among other herbs.9 The Korean Medicine Clinical Practice Guidelines for IBS include a specific recommendation for GJT based on its high rate of clinical use; however, this recommendation was derived from formal expert consensus due to insufficient scientific evidence.10

Previous research on GJT includes four randomized controlled trials, with only one small-scale study using a placebo control.11 One comparative study showed that the efficacy of GJT was similar to that of standard care,12 and two add-on therapy studies showed superior effects when GJT was added to standard care compared with standard care alone.13,14 However, the placebo-controlled study showed inconsistent results across different outcome measures.11

In clinical practice, Korean medicine doctors typically modify the original GJT formula by adding various herbs according to individual patient symptoms. The modified GJT (mGJT) used in this study consists of the original GJT with some herbs removed and additional herbs added, including Taraxacum platycarpum, Geranium thunbergii, Lablab purpureus, and Plantago asiatica, among others.15 These mGJT granules were approved by the Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety as an investigational new drug (IND).16

This study aimed to explore the efficacy and safety of mGJT compared with a placebo in patients with diarrhea-predominant IBS (IBS-D), addressing the evidence gap in the literature despite GJT’s widespread clinical use. Additionally, we sought to obtain preliminary clinical data for sample size calculation and appropriate outcome measure selection for future definitive randomized clinical trials.

Methods

Study protocol

This trial was prospectively registered at the Clinical Research Information Service (KCT0008523; https://cris.nih.go.kr/cris/search/detailSearchEn.do?seq=24920) on June 14, 2023. The trial protocol has been published previously.17 The protocol was approved by the Korean Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (No. 101,281) and by the Institutional Review Board of Kyung Hee University Korean Medicine Hospital (KOMCIRB 2022–09–005). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The study was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines and related regulations. The study report followed CONSORT 2025 reporting guidelines18 and the CONSORT extensions for randomized pilot and feasibility trials19 and Chinese herbal medicine formulas.20 A CONSORT checklist is provided in Supplement 1.

Study design

This pilot double-blind, placebo-controlled, parallel-group randomized clinical trial was conducted at Kyung Hee University Korean Medicine Hospital in Seoul, Republic of Korea. Eligible participants were randomized 1:1 to receive either mGJT granules or placebo orally three times daily for 4 weeks, followed by a 4-week post-treatment follow-up period. Comprehensive IBS symptom assessments were conducted at baseline (week 0), end of treatment (week 4), and follow-up (week 8).

Participants

Inclusion criteria

Adults aged 19–65 years with IBS-D meeting the Rome IV criteria were eligible. Key inclusion criteria included weekly average worst abdominal pain intensity ≥3.0 on a 0–10 scale over the past 10 days and Bristol Stool Scale type 6 or 7 stools on ≥3 days with ≥25% of total bowel movements being type 6 or 7 over the past 10 days. Participants were required to have had a normal physical examination and laboratory findings, as well as a colonoscopy within the past 5 years to rule out organic gastrointestinal diseases.

Exclusion criteria

Major exclusion criteria included organic gastrointestinal diseases (inflammatory bowel disease, celiac disease, malignancy), major abdominal surgery (except appendectomy and hemorrhoidectomy), conditions causing chronic diarrhea (hyperthyroidism, chronic pancreatitis, lactase deficiency), and major psychiatric disorders within the past 2 years. Participants with recent use of medications affecting gastrointestinal function within 2–4 weeks prior to enrollment were excluded. The complete inclusion and exclusion criteria are provided in the trial registry (KCT0008523).

Randomization and blinding

Through computer-generated block randomization with mixed block sizes of 4 and 6 without stratification, participants were randomized 1:1 to receive mGJT or a placebo. The randomization sequence was generated by an independent biostatistician using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and provided it to the National Institute for Korean Medicine Development, which served as the independent third party responsible for drug preparation, labeling, and allocation concealment. Study medications were prepared and labeled according to the randomization sequence using sequentially numbered containers. The biostatistician was not involved in participant enrollment or treatment assignment. The study medications were identical in appearance, taste, and packaging, with labels containing the randomization numbers. Participants, investigators, outcome assessors, and pharmacists remained blinded throughout the study period.

Interventions

Participants were randomly assigned to receive either mGJT or placebo granules, which were administered orally in 10 g sachets, taken three times daily for 4 weeks, preferably 30 min after meals. Each sachet of mGJT granules contained 4.34 g of dried herbal extract, standardized to contain a minimum of 16.1 mg of hesperidin. The placebo granules were formulated with cornstarch, lactose monohydrate, cocoa extract, caramel color, gardenia yellow color, and ginseng flavor powder to match the appearance, taste, and odor of the active treatment without containing any active herbal ingredients. Both investigational products were manufactured at the Herbal Medicine Production Center, National Institute for Korean Medicine Development, following Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) guidelines. Detailed information on the mGJT and placebo granules is provided in Supplement 2. Chemical analysis results are provided in Supplement 3. No rescue medication was provided; participants with worsening symptoms were eligible for discontinuation at the investigator's discretion.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome

The primary endpoint was the overall response rate, defined as the proportion of participants achieving daily response criteria on ≥50% of treatment days over the 4-week treatment period. Daily response was defined as meeting both of the following criteria on the same day: (1) ≥30% reduction in worst daily abdominal pain intensity (measured on a 0–10 scale) compared to baseline and (2) Bristol Stool Scale ≤5 for all bowel movements on that day. This composite endpoint was selected to capture clinically meaningful improvements in both key symptoms of IBS-D.21,22

Secondary outcomes

Secondary efficacy outcomes included individual component response rates and symptom severity measures. Abdominal pain intensity response rate was defined as achieving ≥30% reduction in worst daily abdominal pain intensity from baseline on ≥50% of treatment days. Stool consistency response rate was defined as ≥50% reduction in the number of days with Bristol Stool Scale type 6 or 7 stools compared with baseline over the 4-week treatment period. Global assessment of symptoms was evaluated using 5-point Likert scales (−2 to +2) for overall abdominal pain, diarrhea, and IBS symptoms over the past 7 days. Additional symptom measures included mean daily abdominal pain intensity, number of pain-free days (defined as days with abdominal pain intensity <3), mean daily frequency of Bristol Stool Scale type 6 or 7 stools, and number of days with loose/watery stools.

Disease-specific outcomes included the scores of two scales: (1) the IBS Symptom Severity Scale (IBS-SSS), a validated five-item questionnaire assessing abdominal pain intensity, bloating, bowel habit satisfaction, and life interference (total score 0–500)23; and (2) the IBS Quality of Life questionnaire (IBS-QOL), a 34-item disease-specific quality-of-life measure using five-point Likert scales.24 General quality of life was assessed using two instruments: (1) the EQ-5D-5 L, evaluating mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression across five levels, with scores converted to Korean population-based utility values25; and (2) the WHOQOL-BREF, a 26-item instrument assessing physical health, psychological health, social relationships, and environmental domains.26 All outcomes were assessed at baseline (week 0), end of treatment (week 4), and follow-up (week 8), with daily symptom diaries maintained throughout the treatment period and 4-week post-treatment follow-up.

Sample size calculation

This pilot study was designed to obtain preliminary evidence of efficacy and to assess feasibility for a future definitive trial. The sample size of 30 participants per group was determined based on pragmatic and feasibility considerations,27 consistent with the commonly observed sample size in pilot RCTs.28

Adverse events

Safety outcomes included all adverse events systematically assessed at each study visit. All adverse events were classified according to MedDRA terminology and categorized by severity and causality assessment in relation to the intervention.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using the full analysis set, which included all randomized participants who received at least one dose of study medication and had at least one post-baseline efficacy assessment, with the per-protocol set used for sensitivity analysis. The per-protocol set comprised participants with ≥70% medication compliance, no missing primary outcome data, and no prohibited concomitant medications.

For the primary outcome, the number and proportion of overall responders in each group were presented, with between-group differences assessed using logistic regression to calculate odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals. No imputation was performed for missing binary outcome data. Additionally, sensitivity analysis was conducted assuming that participants with missing data were nonresponders to assess the robustness of the primary results.

Secondary binary outcomes were analyzed similarly using logistic regression. Continuous secondary outcomes were analyzed using mixed-effects model repeated measures (MMRM) with baseline value as a covariate, treatment group, visit, and treatment group-by-visit interaction as fixed effects, and participants as a random effect. For continuous variables analyzed using MMRM, no imputation was necessary.

For safety outcomes, between-group comparisons of adverse event frequencies were assessed using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate. All statistical tests were two-sided with significance set at P < 0.05. Analyses were performed using R version 4.5.1.

Results

Participants

Of the 63 participants assessed for eligibility, 3 were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria after 2 weeks of screening. Sixty participants (16 male and 44 female; mean [SD] age, 46.0 [10.6] years) were enrolled and randomized (Fig. 1). The first participant was enrolled in September 2023, and the last one completed the study in January 2025. One participant in the mGJT group withdrew their consent, resulting in 59 participants (98.3%) available for primary outcome analysis. As planned, no imputation was performed for the missing binary outcome data. All clinical and baseline demographic characteristics were balanced between the groups (Table 1). Medication compliance was high in both groups, with mean [SD] compliance rates of 94.5% [4.5%] in the mGJT group and 92.9% [4.9%] in the placebo group. All participants who remained in the study achieved ≥70% compliance.

Sensitivity analysis was performed assuming that the participant who withdrew from the study was a nonresponder. This analysis showed similar results, with the overall response rate remaining higher in the mGJT group (P=0.04) (Supplement 4).

Secondary outcomes

For the secondary outcomes, the abdominal pain intensity response rate did not show a statistically significant improvement (P=0.09). The stool consistency response rate was significantly higher in the mGJT group than in the placebo group (P=0.03).

The global assessment of symptoms showed significant improvements in abdominal pain in the mGJT group compared with the placebo group (P=0.02). Global assessment for diarrhea did not show a statistically significant difference between groups (P=0.10).

Continuous symptom measures showed a consistent pattern favoring mGJT, particularly for abdominal pain-related outcomes. Mean daily abdominal pain intensity was significantly lower (P=.030) and the percentage of pain-free days was significantly higher (P=0.01) in the mGJT group. Diarrhea-related measures did not show significant between-group differences. The IBS-SSS showed significantly greater improvement in the mGJT group (P=0.03), while the IBS-QOL showed no significant difference between the groups (P=0.29).

The response rates and symptom scores improved over time in both groups, with the mGJT group generally showing greater improvements than the placebo group (Fig. 2). Notably, abdominal pain intensity response rates improved from 36.7% at week 2 to 72.4% at week 8 in the mGJT group versus 20.0% to 43.3% in the placebo group. Mean abdominal pain intensity and pain-free days (%) also showed significant between-group differences at weeks 4 and 8 favoring the mGJT group. Quality-of-life measures showed improvements in both groups, but with minimal between-group differences (Supplements 5 and 6).

Adverse events

In the mGJT group, 2 participants (6.7%) experienced adverse events, while 3 (10.0%) did so in the placebo group (Supplement 7). No serious adverse events were reported in either group, and all reported adverse events were assessed as mild in severity. All reported adverse events were evaluated as either probably or definitely unrelated to the intervention.

Discussion

Summary of key findings

This pilot randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial showed that mGJT produced a significant improvement in the primary composite endpoint, overall response rate, compared with placebo. Among secondary outcomes, stool consistency response rate was also significantly higher in the mGJT group, and abdominal pain-related measures including pain intensity and pain-free days showed consistently greater improvements with mGJT. No intervention-related adverse events were reported in either group.

Comparison with previous studies

A placebo-controlled trial of the original GJT formula conducted in Korea showed a significant improvement in proportion of responders but no significant differences in weekly adequate relief and IBS symptom scores.11 The most rigorous trial to date, the multicenter CHAIRS trial evaluating Huoxiang Zhengqi (GJT) oral liquid in IBS-D patients across 14 hospitals in China, showed significant improvements in adequate relief responder rates and IBS-SSS scores over placebo, but no significant difference in quality of life—a secondary outcome pattern consistent with the present study.29 The present study extends this evidence by employing the composite FDA-recommended primary endpoint,21,22 which combines the assessment of the two main IBS symptoms: abdominal pain and stool consistency. Notably, this is the first placebo-controlled trial to evaluate a modified GJT formula that reflects actual Korean clinical prescribing practice.

The placebo response rate observed in our study (26.7%) was moderately higher than the pooled rate of 17.9% (95% CI 15.2–21.0) reported in a systematic review of IBS trials using composite FDA endpoints.30 Despite this relatively high placebo response, mGJT showed superior efficacy. The pattern of benefit was most pronounced for abdominal pain outcomes, which aligns with observational data indicating that clinicians preferentially prescribe mGJT for patients presenting with severe abdominal pain.15

Clinical and scientific implications

The improvement in abdominal pain-related outcomes across multiple measures suggests that mGJT may exert specific analgesic and anti-inflammatory effects in IBS-D. At a mechanistic level, this may reflect the combined pharmacological activities of key herbal constituents: Taraxacum platycarpum shows anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties with protective effects against colitis and gut microbiota dysbiosis,31 Geranium thunbergii suppresses pro-inflammatory mediators including TNF-α, IL-1β, and iNOS,32 and Lablab purpureus exhibits antinociceptive and antimotility effects relevant to abdominal pain and diarrhea.33 At the compound level, hesperidin, the standardized marker, has been associated with visceral pain reduction and intestinal barrier function enhancement,34 and rosmarinic acid, one of the detected active constituents, modulates inflammatory signaling through regulation of gut microbiota.35 The absence of intervention-related adverse events and the high completion rate (98.3%) indicate that mGJT is well tolerated, supporting its potential as a therapeutic option for patients with IBS-D, particularly those with predominant abdominal pain.15

Strengths and limitations

Key methodological strengths include the double-blind, placebo-controlled design with allocation concealment, GMP-manufactured investigational products with defined chemical standardization, and adherence to CONSORT 2025 reporting standards. The single-center design and small sample size (N = 60) limit generalizability and resulted in wide confidence intervals. The absence of an active comparator prevents direct comparison against conventional pharmacological therapies such as antispasmodics, antidiarrheals, or 5-HT3 receptor antagonists. The 4-week treatment duration and follow-up period are insufficient to characterize long-term efficacy and safety. Finally, the large number of secondary endpoints without multiplicity adjustment necessitates cautious interpretation of those results.

Conclusions and suggestions for future research

This pilot study provides preliminary evidence supporting the efficacy and safety of mGJT in IBS-D, justifying progression to a larger definitive trial. Based on the observed difference in response rates between groups (28.5%), a definitive trial would require approximately 61 participants per group (N = 122) to achieve 90% power at a 5% significance level. Further trials should adopt a multicenter design with extended treatment (8–12 weeks) and longer follow-up (12–24 weeks). Integration of objective biomarkers including gut microbiome profiling and intestinal permeability markers alongside patient-reported outcomes would deepen mechanistic understanding of mGJT's therapeutic effects.
