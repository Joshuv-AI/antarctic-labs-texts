Psychological Impact of BRCA Genetic Testing: Analysis of Positive and Negative Affect Changes according to Test Results
Authors: Gyul Jung, Soo Young Hur, Sheng-Min Wang, Youn Jin Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveBRCA1/2 genetic testing may provoke complex emotional reactions that extend beyond simple relief or distress. This study examined affective changes before and after testing and identified demographic, clinical, and familial factors associated with these responses using overall and facet-level scores from the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS).MethodsA total of 640 individuals undergoing BRCA1/2 testing were enrolled between 2018 and 2025; 392 with complete pre- and post-test PANAS data were included. Changes in affect were modeled according to test result (positive versus negative/variant of uncertain significance [VUS]), age, menopausal status, personal cancer history, and family cancer history. Facet-level analyses explored discrete emotions.ResultsDistinct affective patterns were observed. Among 41 to 60-year-olds, positive affect decreased in BRCA-negative/VUS participants, whereas negative affect increased in BRCA-positive individuals. Postmenopausal women and participants with a cancer history who received negative/VUS results showed reduced positive affect. In contrast, BRCA-positive individuals with cancer histories exhibited increases in both positive and negative affect. Absence of family cancer history was linked to reduced positive affect after negative/VUS results. Facet analyses showed diminished self-assurance and greater guilt in negative/VUS participants, and heightened fear, hostility, guilt, and distress in BRCA-positive individuals.ConclusionBRCA testing elicits heterogeneous emotional responses shaped by test results, cancer history, and demographic and familial factors. Facet-level analysis revealed nuanced shifts in discrete emotions—such as decreased self-assurance in non-carriers and heightened fear and guilt in carriers—highlighting the need for tailored, culturally informed genetic counseling, while acknowledging that these findings reflect short-term affective responses and result-related uncertainty.

INTRODUCTION

Advances in genomic medicine have made hereditary cancer risk assessment an integral part of precision oncology [1]. Pathogenic variants in BRCA1 and BRCA2 confer markedly increased lifetime risks for breast, ovarian, and related cancers, often leading to intensified surveillance or preventive surgery [2,3]. As testing expands through population and family programs, growing numbers of individuals face complex emotional reactions to their genetic risk results [4]. Understanding these responses is essential for ethically responsible counseling and tailored psychosocial support.

Emotional reactions to BRCA testing occur within a broader psychosocial context involving personal identity, perceived vulnerability, and family relationships [3]. Earlier studies showed that women experience fluctuating emotions—anticipatory anxiety before testing and mixed relief or distress afterward [4,5]. However, findings remain inconsistent: some report elevated anxiety among carriers, whereas others find persistent worry or reduced reassurance among those testing negative or with variants of uncertain significance (VUS) [6,7]. Such variability suggests that emotional outcomes are shaped not only by test results but also by personal and contextual factors, including age, menopausal status, cancer history, and family dynamics [8].

Personal cancer history is particularly influential. Cancer survivors often carry fears of recurrence and altered self-image, and BRCA testing can reactivate these concerns [9,10]. A positive result may heighten vigilance through confirmation of hereditary risk, while negative or VUS results can evoke guilt or unresolved uncertainty about familial transmission [11]. In contrast, individuals without cancer may experience anticipatory anxiety about future vulnerability or the implications for relatives [12].

Family context adds another layer of complexity. BRCA mutations are inherited, so testing occurs within shared genetic networks that shape perceptions of responsibility and solidarity [13]. Individuals often experience guilt for potentially transmitting mutations, relief when relatives test negative, or tension when family results are discordant [14]. Moreover, uncertain findings such as VUS can complicate communication and sustain anxiety by leaving risk interpretations unresolved [11].

Recent investigations have advanced understanding but continue to leave important gaps. One study of 354 participants in a population genomic screening program found that negative emotions declined within six months, suggesting transient psychological effects [14]. However, the unselected sample and lack of subgroup analysis limited relevance to high-risk settings. Another study involving 295 women undergoing multigene panel testing for hereditary breast and ovarian cancer reported greater distress among carriers of pathogenic or VUS results compared with negatives [15]. Interestingly, women with moderate-penetrance variants showed higher distress than those with high-penetrance mutations, indicating that interpretive uncertainty, rather than objective risk magnitude, may drive emotional burden. Although the Multidimensional Impact of Cancer Risk Assessment (MICRA) scale used in the study is a well-validated measure of general psychological adjustment—including distress, uncertainty, and positive experiences—but it does not capture discrete emotional states [16]. Consequently, the measure may have overlooked specific affective nuances such as fear, reassurance, or self-assurance that fluctuate throughout the testing process.

Evidence from Asian cohorts offers additional cultural insight. In a Malaysian multiethnic sample, anxiety and depression decreased after BRCA1/2 cascade testing and remained low over two years, though ethnic differences persisted, with some groups reporting higher cancer worry and post-disclosure anxiety [17]. Comparative research involving Swiss and Korean BRCA carriers identified culturally specific “modes of responsibility” in communicating results, with Korean participants more likely to delay or limit disclosure to maintain family harmony [13]. These findings indicate that emotional adjustment to BRCA testing is influenced not only by clinical and personal factors but also by cultural norms related to family obligation, stigma, and emotional restraint.

While the overall psychological impact of BRCA testing is generally modest, emotional responses vary across time, context, and result type [18,19]. Individuals with uncertain or moderate-risk findings often display complex affective patterns that broad distress measures cannot fully capture. Multidimensional assessment that differentiates emotions such as reassurance, guilt, vigilance, and fear—alongside adaptive reactions like relief or empowerment—is crucial for understanding adjustment to hereditary cancer risk [20]. Examining these dynamics in a culturally homogeneous cohort provides valuable insight. Shared social norms emphasizing collectivism, family obligation, and emotional restraint influence how genetic risk is perceived and communicated. In such contexts, hereditary cancer is often regarded as a collective family concern, heightening feelings of guilt, duty, or protective motivation [21]. A demographically uniform sample minimizes social variability, allowing clearer identification of mechanisms underlying emotional responses to BRCA results.

The present study investigates affective changes before and after BRCA1/2 testing using the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS) and explores variation by test result, demographic, and clinical factors to identify subgroups that may benefit from targeted psychosocial support within a culturally informed framework.

METHODS

Participants and Study Design

Participants were recruited through BRCAStory website, which individuals voluntarily registered and agreed to participate in BRCA genetic testing between January 2018 and April 2025. Their willingness to participate was reconfirmed by telephone, and genetic counseling and testing were performed at Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital. Eligible participants met the following criteria: (1) individuals with a personal history of breast, ovarian, thyroid, or pancreatic cancer, which are known to be related to BRCA1/2 gene mutations; and/or (2) individuals with a family history of these cancers within third-degree relatives. There was no age restriction for enrollment. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital (IRB No. [KC17TESI0690]), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before study participation.

Measurements

The PANAS was used to evaluate participants’ emotional states before and after genetic testing. This self-report questionnaire includes 20 items, consisting of 10 positive and 10 negative adjectives that describe affective states experienced during the past week [22]. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“not at all”) to 4 (“extremely”). Total positive and negative affect scores were calculated by summing the respective item scores, with higher values indicating greater affect intensity.

Procedure

The study participants visited the hospital three times. During the first visit, participants provided basic information, including age, date of birth, menopausal status, parity, past medical history, and family history of cancer. After a gynecologic oncologist explained the purpose and process of BRCA1/2 genetic testing, written informed consent was obtained, and participants completed the pre-test PANAS questionnaires. At the second visit, blood samples were collected for BRCA1/2 genetic testing, which was conducted by Macrogen Inc. The analysis required approximately 3−4 weeks for completion, and therefore, the third visit took place about one month later. During this visit, gynecologic oncologists provided detailed explanations of the test results, including the presence of any BRCA1/2 variants, zygosity, and their clinical interpretation. Results were classified into three categories: (1) pathogenic or positive, (2) VUS, and (3) benign or negative. Participants were counseled on the meaning of each result. Those with VUS were informed that such variants currently have no established pathogenic significance but were advised to continue regular medical surveillance. Post-test PANAS assessments were administered immediately after disclosure of genetic test results.

Statistical Analyses

Participants were categorized by age (≤ 40 years, 41−60 years, and ≥ 61 years) and menopausal status (yes or no). Past medical history was classified as having a history of cancer (breast, ovarian, thyroid, or pancreatic cancer) or having no cancer history, with or without benign medical conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, thyroid dysfunction, uterine myoma, lupus, anemia, etc. Family history referred to the presence of breast, ovarian, thyroid, or pancreatic cancer within three degrees of kinship. BRCA test results were divided into two categories: negative or VUS and positive (pathogenic).

For the PANAS questionnaire, each of the 20 items (10 positive and 10 negative) was rated on a 5-point scale converted from the original 0−4 response. Thus, the total scores for both positive and negative affect ranged from 10 to 20 points, with higher scores indicating stronger affect intensity. Changes in affect scores before and after genetic testing were analyzed using paired-sample ttests, and facet-level analyses were additionally conducted to assess detailed emotional changes. A two-tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.2.0 (IBM Corp.).

RESULTS

Study Population and Overall Sample Characteristics

A total of 640 patients from Seoul St. Mary’s Hospital, The Catholic University of Korea, were screened for this test. Four participants were excluded due to missing information on past medical history. A total of 392 individuals completed both pre- and post-BRCA testing PANAS questionnaire, for a response rate of 61.2%. Participants’ baseline characteristics are detailed in Table 1. Their mean age was 41.9 years. Half of the participants were 41 to 60 years old at the time of questionnaire (range 19−73). 30% of the participants reported a personal history of breast and/or ovarian cancer, and 25.5% reported no family history with breast or ovarian cancer. All individuals underwent BRCA test, and 85% were BRCA negative or VUS. Among the total 392 participants, 91 individuals were members of families with multiple participants, whereas 301 were unrelated participants. Most clinical variables did not significantly differ according to BRCA test results, except for parity and family history of breast or ovarian cancer, which showed significant differences between the groups (Supplementary Table 1; available online).

Psychological Outcomes with Variables

Significant changes in affect scores were observed only in the 41−60-year age group. BRCA-negative/VUS participants showed a decrease in positive affect (p = 0.002), whereas BRCA-positive participants showed an increase in negative affect (p = 0.021) (Table 2). When the participants were stratified by menopausal status, there was a noticeable difference in emotional response after genetic testing. In postmenopausal women with BRCA-negative or VUS results, positive affect scores significantly decreased following BRCA testing (p = 0.002). No significant changes were found in other subgroups, including those with BRCA-positive results or premenopausal women (Supplementary Table 2; available online).

When stratified by past cancer history, distinct emotional responses were observed. Among participants with a previous history of cancer, positive affect significantly decreased in the BRCA-negative/VUS group (p = 0.006), while both positive and negative affect increased in those with BRCA-positive results (p = 0.050 and 0.020, respectively). No significant changes were found in participants without a prior cancer history (Table 2). Among participants without a family history, BRCA-negative/VUS individuals exhibited a significant reduction in positive affect (p = 0.003). No other significant emotional changes were observed according to family history or BRCA result (Supplementary Table 2; available online). When analyzing only participants with genetically related family members, negative affect significantly decreased among BRCA-positive individuals whose relatives were BRCA-negative/VUS (p = 0.039). No other significant affective changes were found across family BRCA result combinations (Supplementary Table 3; available online).

Psychological Outcomes with Facets

Negative affect scores significantly increased in the BRCA-positive group (p = 0.025), while no significant change was observed in the BRCA-negative/VUS group (Supplementary Table 3; available online). In facet-level analysis of PANAS, the BRCA-negative/VUS group showed a significant decrease in the positive facet of Self-Assurance and a mild increase in Guilt among negative affect subscales. In contrast, participants with BRCA-positive results demonstrated significant increases in Fear, Hostility, Guilt, and Distress facets after genetic testing. No other facet-level differences reached statistical significance between the two groups (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

This study examined affective changes before and after BRCA1/2 genetic testing using the PANAS in a large, culturally and ethnically homogeneous cohort. By capturing both overall affect and discrete emotional facets, we found that the psychological impact of BRCA testing was generally modest but varied by test result, cancer history, and demographic factors. Carriers of pathogenic variants were associated with increased negative affect—especially fear, guilt, and distress—while those with negative or uncertain results showed reductions in positive affect, particularly reduced self-assurance. These findings highlight that affective responses to BRCA testing are heterogeneous and shaped by both clinical and psychosocial contexts.

Our results align with earlier research showing that emotional reactions to BRCA testing depend on individual interpretation and context rather than test outcome alone [3,5,6]. The rise in negative affect among BRCA-positive participants is consistent with prior evidence suggesting that confirmation of hereditary risk heightens anxiety and vigilance [10,11]. Conversely, the decline in positive affect among BRCA-negative or VUS participants indicates that relief after “favorable” results is not universal. Uncertain or inconclusive results may evoke guilt, doubt, or perceived responsibility toward relatives, consistent with previous findings that family relationships profoundly shape emotional meaning in hereditary testing [13,14].

The use of PANAS provided insight beyond global distress measures such as the MICRA. Although the MICRA captures overall distress and uncertainty, it overlooks specific emotional nuances that fluctuate during testing [16]. In contrast, the PANAS identified distinct patterns—heightened fear, guilt, hostility, and distress among BRCA-positive participants—suggesting multiple emotional pathways linked to perceived threat and moral concern [23]. The modest rise in guilt across all groups reflects the relational dimension of hereditary testing, where individuals evaluate results not only for personal implications but also for what they mean to family members. Reduced self-assurance among those with negative or uncertain results likely reflects lingering uncertainty or diminished confidence about residual familial risk.

Demographic and clinical characteristics moderated these responses. Declines in positive affect among postmenopausal women and those aged 41 to 60 years suggest that emotional interpretation of results varies with life stage. For older participants, testing may carry less relevance for prevention and more for existential or familial reflection. Among cancer survivors, both positive and negative affects increased after a positive result, suggesting that testing may be associated with reactivation of illness-related emotions while also reinforcing vigilance and engagement with health behaviors. This dual response supports the view that genetic testing may elicit both distress and adaptive motivation simultaneously.

Family context further influenced emotional adaptation. Participants without a family history of cancer showed reduced positive affect after negative or uncertain results, possibly reflecting persistent ambiguity about their own risk significance. Among related family members, BRCA-positive individuals whose relatives tested negative or uncertain displayed decreased negative affect, suggesting emotional relief derived from favorable outcomes within the family. These findings emphasize that BRCA testing is not an isolated event but a relational process embedded in shared genetic and emotional networks [21].

Cultural factors provide an additional interpretive lens. In collectivist contexts, hereditary risk is often seen as a family issue rather than an individual one. Social norms emphasizing emotional restraint and interdependence may intensify feelings of duty or guilt while limiting overt distress expression. Studies in Asian populations have demonstrated that such values shape disclosure patterns and emotional responses to BRCA results [17,21]. The present findings are consistent with this framework, suggesting that emotional adaptation to genetic testing is influenced by cultural meanings of responsibility and family harmony.

The study has several clinical implications. Our results suggest that pre-test counseling should address the full range of potential emotional reactions, including the possibility of reduced reassurance or guilt even among those with negative or uncertain results [24,25]. Framing uncertainty as a normal aspect of genomic interpretation may reduce distress related to ambiguous outcomes. For cancer survivors, counseling should integrate genetic information into ongoing survivorship narratives, acknowledging that testing can reawaken prior fears [26]. Thus, family-based counseling approaches may be particularly beneficial in collectivist settings, promoting open discussion and shared coping strategies rather than individual burden.

Methodologically, this study demonstrates the value of multidimensional affective assessment in genomic medicine. The PANAS detected subtle emotional variations often overlooked by single-score measures, underscoring the need to assess both adaptive and maladaptive responses. Future studies should adopt longitudinal designs to capture longer-term emotional trajectories and include qualitative exploration of how individuals make sense of hereditary risk within family and cultural contexts. Expanding research across more diverse populations would also clarify how cultural norms shape emotional adaptation and communication patterns.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. As this study employed an observational pre–post design without a control group, the observed affective changes should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects. Emotional assessment was based on self-report and may be influenced by cultural tendencies toward emotional restraint. Not all enrolled participants completed both pre- and post-test PANAS assessments, raising the possibility of selection or attrition bias. Individuals lost to follow-up may have experienced different emotional trajectories, which could limit the generalizability of the observed affective changes. Only two assessment points were used, capturing short-term affective responses following result disclosure, and therefore precluding assessment of longer-term psychological adjustment. The grouping of negative and VUS results, though clinically practical, may have obscured meaningful psychological differences, as VUS findings can entail persistent uncertainty distinct from definitive negative results. Finally, as participants were recruited from a single institution, findings may not generalize across regions or healthcare systems. Despite these limitations, the study’s large sample, inclusion of both carriers and non-carriers, and use of multidimensional emotional assessment strengthen the robustness and clinical relevance of the results.

In summary, BRCA1/2 genetic testing elicits complex, multidirectional emotional responses shaped by result type, cancer history, and familial and cultural context. Carriers show heightened negative affect dominated by fear and guilt, whereas non-carriers or those with uncertain results exhibit reduced positive affect and self-assurance. These patterns highlight that emotional adjustment to hereditary cancer risk is not uniform but shaped by intersecting personal, relational, and sociocultural factors. Incorporating multidimensional affect assessment and culturally informed counseling strategies will be essential to support psychological adaptation in the expanding landscape of precision oncology.
