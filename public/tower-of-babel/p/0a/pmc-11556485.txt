Effect of Stress on Sleep Quality among Medical Students: A Cross-sectional Study at Jazan University, Saudi Arabia
Authors: Ahmed I. Sayed, Sarah J. Mobarki, Imtenan A. Oberi, Yazan Z. Omar, Sarah H. Moafa, Raum A. Ayoub, Yara Ajeebi, Faisal Hakami, Abdulrahman Hakami, Mohammed Somaili
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction:It has long been understood that sleep is a necessary therapeutic element of human physiology and is crucial for healthy functioning, mental health, and quality of life. Medical students frequently minimize their sleep and habits to cope with their workload and their stressful lives, which has a negative influence on both mental health and academic performance. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the prevalence of stress and its effect on sleep quality among medical students (in both the preclinical and clinical years) at Jazan University in Saudi Arabia.Materials and Methods:A cross-sectional research design was applied in this study. The sample constituted medical students at Jazan University, Jazan region, Saudi Arabia. A self-administered online survey was distributed actively to measure the variables of interest.Results:This study examined the sleep quality and psychological distress among 347 students. The findings revealed that a significant proportion of students experienced sleep difficulties, with 52.8% reporting mild sleep problems and 46.6% reporting moderate sleep difficulties. In addition, a notable percentage of participants (28.2%) reported very high psychological distress, while 27.4% experienced severe psychological distress.Conclusions:This study found that medical students had a high level of stress and poor sleep quality, which led to unhealthy habits, such as consuming many energy drinks. Hence, it is recommended that concerns should be addressed to minimize the risk of developing chronic illnesses.

INTRODUCTION

Sleep has long been recognized as critical for optimal functioning, mental health, and quality of life.[1] Furthermore, it has a significant effect on mental performance. A good night’s sleep is defined as adults sleeping seven or more hours per night on a regular basis and ensures optimal health.[2] Poor sleep reduces general alertness, hinders concentration, and inhibits cognitive processing.[34] Poor sleep quality is more prevalent among medical students than nonmedical university students, that is because of their academic obligations which eventually can affect their sleep quality. According to many studies from East and West, 16% to 40% of medical students have poor sleep quality.[5] Many studies have shown that students are frequently chronically sleep-deprived, and both the quality and quantity of sleep are related to students’ capacity for learning and academic achievement.[6] Several studies have shown that >60% of college students have poor sleep habits, which contributes to daytime sleepiness and an increase in physical and mental health issues such as depressive symptoms. Greater declines in sleep quality result in lower grades, more distracting behaviors, and less arousal.[7] We believe that evaluating the level of mental and physical health among our students is essential to improve their quality of life. Consequently, the goal of this study was to determine the incidence of and the association between stress and poor sleep quality among medical students (in both the preclinical and clinical years) at Jazan University in Saudi Arabia.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional research study design was used and conducted from January 1 to March 31, 2023, at Jazan University, which is a Saudi governmental university located in the Jazan region, Southwest of Saudi Arabia. This study includes subjects from the college of medicine, involving 2nd to 6th-year level students. Moreover, the study excluded any student not from the college of medicine or a medical student in the 1st or 7th year.

Sample size and sampling procedure

The number of students in the faculty of medicine (male and female) at Jazan University was 956 in the 2023 academic year. The sample size required for this study was calculated to be 347 participants for a confidence level of 95% and a margin of error of 5%. These calculations were made using the StatCal function of Epi Info software. We employed a convenient sampling technique.

Data collection tools

An online, self-administered, validated questionnaire was used in this study. Cronbach’s alpha of the Arabic-translated questionnaire was low in reliability, which is statistically acceptable. The questionnaire included several sections to achieve the objectives of the study. The first section includes participant demographic data, such as age, gender, marital status, employment status, education level, monthly income, residency, and geographic area. The second section was used to assess stress using the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10). This tool has been used widely in numerous epidemiological studies to determine the severity of current (within the past 1 month) discomfort caused by psychiatric disorders. The K10 questionnaire, which consists of 10 items and was found to have excellent psychometric properties, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.93, includes five response options for each item: (1) “none of the time,” (2) “a little of the time,” (3) “some of the time,” (4) “most of the time,” and (5) “all of the time.” Total scores, which varied from 10 to 50, were categorized as follows: <20 indicates that the participant is likely to be in good health, 20–24 indicates light stress, 25–29 indicates moderate stress, and 30 or more indicates that the participant is likely to be experiencing severe stress.[6] The final section included the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), a validated questionnaire that is an essential tool for evaluating subjective sleep quality in both clinical and nonclinical populations. This questionnaire examines seven factors: daytime dysfunction, habitual sleep inefficiency, sleep disorders, use of sleep medications, sleep quality, sleep latency, and sleep duration. The PSQI consists of five items that are scored by a sleeping partner or roommate in addition to 10 self-reported components (if the focal person is available). The final question (number 10) does not count toward the final score. Scoring considers only self-rated questions. Each factor is graded on a scale from 0 to 3, with 0 denoting no difficulty sleeping and 3 denoting extreme difficulty sleeping. The seven component scores are then combined to provide a single global score, which ranges from 0 to 21, with 0 denoting almost no difficulty sleeping and 21 denoting extreme difficulty sleeping.[7]

Statistical analysis

IBM-SPSS statistics version 23 (Statistic Package for Social Sciences, Armonk, NY) was used to enter and analyze data. Measures of central tendency were determined for continuous variables, while frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical variables. Independent sample t-tests, analysis of variance, and the Chi-squared test of independence were utilized. In addition to calculating odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals, binary logistic regression was employed to identify factors predicting sleep quality. All P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

The required official approvals were obtained before gathering the data. The objectives of the study were explained to each participant. They were notified that participating in the study would not harm them and that their data will be confidential. Consent for participation in the study was obtained their permission to participate. The questionnaire did not contain any personal data, such as names, phone numbers, or addresses. In addition, all data collected have been kept confidential and used merely for research. Ethical approval was given by the Standing Committee for Scientific Research at Jazan University (approval number REC-44/06/465, dated January 3, 2023).

RESULTS

The study population consisted of 347 participants, including 205 male students (59.1%) and 142 female students (40.9%). The majority of participants were between the ages of 20 and 22. We found that 94.5% of students were single, with an average monthly income of <1000 Saudi Riyals (SR) (55.6%). 2nd- and 3rd-year students had the highest representation, consisting of 26.5% and 25.1% of participants, respectively. Students who had a grade point average (GPA) of 4.5 or higher constituted (52.2%) of the studied sample, with the remaining students reporting a GPA of 3.5–4.5 (35.2%) or below 3.5 (12.7%). Most of the students lived in rural areas (55%). We found that 23.9% of participants had a physician among first-order relatives. The vast majority of students used a private car for transportation. Energy drinks were the most commonly consumed type of caffeine (74.2%), with the majority of students consuming them on most days (77.2%).

A substantial number of participants reported having good attendance records (88.5%), while 19.3% reported having failed or repeated a year. Furthermore, only a small minority of participants reported having a perceived chronic medical or physical problem (4.6%). These findings contribute to our understanding of the demographic and health-related characteristics of this population and can be used to inform health promotion and intervention strategies [Table 1].

Male students were more likely to report low, moderate, or high psychological distress than female students; however, female students were more likely to report very high distress than male students. Moreover, the distribution of distress differed significantly between genders (P = 0.003). Participants with a low monthly income of <1000 SR had more psychological distress than those with a greater income; however, this difference was not statistically significant. Students in every academic year except the sixth experienced a high amount of stress. There was no correlation between current GPA and psychological distress (P = 0.141). The presence of a physician among a student’s family members was associated with less psychological distress than experienced by students who did not have a physician in the family, though this difference was not statistically significant. The frequency of energy drink consumption was not significantly associated with distress in different groups (P = 0.811).

The results presented in Table 5 show the association between the sleep quality of participants and various study variables. We found that 16% of students under the age of 20 had mild sleep difficulties, whereas the majority of students between the ages of 20 and 22 had moderate sleep difficulties (59.7%). Male students were more likely to have mild sleep difficulties (63.3%), but female students were more likely to have moderate sleep difficulties (47.0%). There was a statistically significant difference (P = 0.044) between the degree of sleeping difficulties in men and women. Students with <1000 SR of monthly income had a higher prevalence of low (55.6%) and moderate (56.4%) sleep difficulties than those with higher monthly income. However, the distribution of sleep quality did not differ significantly between these income categories (P = 0.59).

Students of all academic years had little difficulty sleeping; however, 2nd- and 3rd-year medical students were more likely to have moderate sleep difficulties (29.5% and 27.5%, respectively). The distribution of sleep quality did not differ significantly (P = 0.347) among academic years. DISCUSSION

Stress is a natural psychological response to a perceived threat or challenge. It can be beneficial within low leveled doses, but prolonged stress can lead to adverse outcomes, such as anxiety, depression, and physical health problems.[8] Stress produces a hormonal response in the body that activates the fight or flight response, leading to increased heart and respiratory rates, and the release of stress hormones, such as cortisol and adrenaline. Furthermore, chronic stress can lead to adverse health outcomes, such as poor sleep, anxiety, depression, and other medical diseases.

The relationship between stress and poor sleep has been well documented in the literature. Stressful events often lead to difficulty falling or staying asleep, resulting in decreased sleep quality and duration which negatively affecting overall health and well-being. Poor sleep has been associated with numerous health outcomes, such as an increased risk of hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease.[7]

Several studies have found that medical students are subjected to significant amounts of stress during their training.[910] Academic expectations, tests, clinical rotations, patients’ care responsibilities, financial constraints, personal life challenges, and other variables may all be stressors.[11] Excessive stress in medical school can result in interpersonal issues, sleep problems, reduced focus, cheating temptations, depression, higher error rates, and increased likelihood of neglect. It can also harm students’ health, leading to illnesses, cardiovascular problems, poor judgment, absenteeism, and substance abuse.[1213]

Evidence suggests that men may experience higher stress levels in relation to academic achievement, specifically regarding maintaining a high GPA. A study by the American Psychological Association found that college students, particularly men, who prioritized academic achievement reported higher stress levels and lower satisfaction with life.[14]

In our study, male and female students with higher GPAs had much higher stress levels than students with lower GPAs. Such increased stress might stem from the increased pressure of trying to maintain a higher GPA so they can continue pursuing their dream jobs. Students who had GPAs >4.5 reported having high (54.1%) and very high (50.5%) stress levels. In contrast, students who had a GPA lower than 3.5 reported high (14.3%) and very high (12.6%) levels of stress, respectively. In contrasting studies examining stress levels among medical students in Saudi Arabia, distinct gender-related patterns emerge. Our research at the Jazan College of Medicine indicated that male students, regardless of age, reported higher stress levels, with 59.1% affected, compared to 40.9% of female students. This finding starkly contrasts with the results from a cross-sectional study at the College of Medicine, King Saud University, where female medical students reported significantly higher stress levels than their male counterparts. Specifically, in the King Saud University study, 75.7% of female students experienced stress, markedly higher than the 57% prevalence among male students.[15] These divergent results highlight the variability in stress experiences across different academic environments and underscore the influence of potentially varying cultural, educational, and social factors at different institutions within the same country.

Excessive caffeine consumption might lead to intoxication, which is characterized by restlessness, agitation, excitement, meandering thought or speech, and even insomnia. The amount of caffeine in one cup of coffee can cause people to take longer to fall asleep and have poorer quality sleep, especially during non-Rapid eye movement (REM) deep sleep. Stopping coffee drinking is one of the most important sleep hygiene measures a person can adopt because a dose of caffeine ingested in the morning can cause these effects the following night.[16]

Consuming energy drinks before bedtime can also significantly impact sleep quality in several ways. Caffeine is a stimulant that can block the action of adenosine, a neurotransmitter that promotes sleep. Therefore, consuming caffeine-containing beverages, such as energy drinks, before bedtime can interfere with the natural sleep–wake cycle, leading to insomnia or poor sleep quality. In addition, the high sugar content in energy drinks can further disrupt sleep quality by causing fluctuations in blood sugar levels, leading to nocturnal hypoglycemia, or low blood sugar levels during sleep.[1718]

According to some studies, people frequently consume energy drinks when they are experiencing a great deal of stress.[19] The high quantities of caffeine and other stimulants in energy drinks are thought to cause physiological effects that improve alertness and mood. However, consuming energy drinks has also been linked to many negative consequences, including elevated heart rate, anxiety, jitteriness, and sleeplessness.[2021] Furthermore, excessive and frequent consumption of energy drinks has been associated with negative health outcomes, such as cardiovascular issues, kidney damage, and dental erosion.[22]

Although there are coffee shops throughout Jazan, the preferred caffeinated drinks of the medical students in our study were energy drinks (74.2%), with the majority of students consuming them regularly (57.7%). Energy drinks may be preferred because they are widely available at the local grocery store, are less expensive, and can be stored simply in a small refrigerator.

The major objective of this study was to test for an association between poor sleep quality and stress among medical students at Jazan University. In our study, the PSQI score suggested that 169 (52.8%) of the students had a mild sleep problem, 149 (46.6%) had a moderate sleep problem, and 2 (0.6%) had a severe sleep problem. This is consistent with the findings of another study, which was conducted at Sulaiman Al-Rajhi Colleges in Saudi Arabia’s Al-Qassim region and found that the prevalence of poor sleep was 63.2% and was higher in physically inactive students.[23] However, the rates of poor sleep that we observed were substantially higher than those of another study conducted between 2011 and 2012 in Saudi medical schools, which found that 30% of students had poor sleep quality, 40% had excessive daytime sleepiness, and 33% had symptoms of insomnia.[24] This difference may be attributed to increased medical school competition, academic load, and Internet usage, which was substantially lower in the previous decade. Another study used the PSQI to assess preclinical medical students at a Saudi medical college. The average PSQI score was 8.13 ± 3.46, with 77% of students reporting poor sleep quality and 63.5% expressing some level of psychological stress.[25]

The second goal of our study was to identify a method or plan for improving student study habits and controlling their stressful educational environment because we discovered a substantial increase in energy drink consumption (74.2%) among medical students. According to one study, excessive caffeine consumption affects the cardiovascular system by increasing blood pressure (both systolic and diastolic) and heart rate. Furthermore, caffeine appears to have a larger negative impact on medical students’ academic performance and increases sleep deprivation.[22] One study assessed medical students who were stressed, sleep-deprived, and emotionally exhausted with the aim of improving sleep quality. The intervention protocol was to replace existing alarm clocks with sunrise alarm clocks provided by the study and to turn off all electronic devices, including smartphones, before going to bed; participants were instructed to follow this protocol for 2 weeks. The number of participants who reported poor sleep quality decreased significantly from 77.2% to 41.8% (P = 0.026).[26] Additional research on sleep deprivation in medical students is necessary to identify its causes, as well as preventative measures and stress-reduction approaches, with the ultimate goal of improving medical students’ general well-being.

Limitations and strengths of the present study

The present study found a significant relationship between stress and sleep quality among medical students at Jazan University. However, it had some limitations. First, one of the main drawbacks of this study was the use of a self-administered questionnaire, which may have resulted in higher rates of missing data and inaccurate answers than an interview-based questionnaire. Second, most of the study participants were junior students (in their 2nd or 3rd years), resulting in indirect selection bias, which may have affected the results of the study. Third, no physical examination was performed on the study participants to rule out other relevant medical disorders. We hope that these limitations will be addressed in similar survey-based observational studies in the future.

In contrast, there were several strengths of this study, including the use of a validated and Arabic-translated questionnaire. Furthermore, the study sample was adequate and representative of the study population. In addition, the results and conclusion of this study help to fill an important gap in the literature. Finally, the present study provides useful epidemiological data on stress and sleep difficulties.

CONCLUSIONS

The present study found a significant correlation between medical students’ high-stress levels and their poor sleep quality, which may result in unhealthy habits, such as the consumption of numerous energy drinks and a decreased likelihood of exercising. If these issues are not addressed, they will increase the risk of developing chronic illnesses, which may affect the quality of healthcare provided to their future patients. Therefore, as part of their clinical rotations, we advise offering medical students sufficient support, counseling, direction, and mental health prevention programs. This study is a good starting point for relevant discussions and further research.
