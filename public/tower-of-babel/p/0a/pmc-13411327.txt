Microhabitat-driven acclimation links functional and germination traits in the narrow endemic Saxifraga berica (Bég.) D.A. Webb
Authors: Marco Canella, Sara Natale, Anna Pasinato, Valentina Boscariol, Nicoletta La Rocca, Alessandro Alboresi, Francesco Dal Grande
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe concept of seed germination niche provides a framework for linking germination data with ecological conditions, helping to reveal how regeneration traits mirror the environments in which species evolve and persist. Understanding these relationships is especially relevant for narrow endemics, whose restricted distributions often reflect fine-scale environmental heterogeneity. We investigated the sub-alpine Saxifraga berica to test whether microhabitat variability influences functional traits and seed germination patterns. Individuals were sampled across contrasting light microenvironments, characterized through field measurements of irradiance, light spectra, temperature, and humidity. Functional traits related to photosynthetic efficiency, photoprotection, pigment composition, and leaf morphology were assessed in the field, while seed germination percentage and timing were evaluated under controlled conditions. Multivariate analyses revealed two distinct environmental clusters associated with consistent differences in photosynthetic performance, pigment composition, and leaf structure, indicating divergent physiological acclimation strategies to light availability. Germination percentage remained generally high across clusters, but germination timing varied markedly, suggesting that microenvironmental conditions experienced by plants influence seed germination rate. Overall, S. berica exhibits two ecologically differentiated germination strategies reflecting the contrasting microhabitats. These findings highlight the importance of integrating functional traits for understanding persistence strategies in narrow-distributed endemics and provide key insights for their conservation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09083-8.Keywords: Ecological niche, Far-red light, Functional traits, Germination, Light spectrum, Photosynthesis, Seed germination niche

Introduction

Germination is a critical phase in the life cycle of seed plants, linking dispersal to seedling establishment and shaping population dynamics. It is regulated by a combination of environmental and physiological cues that collectively define a species’ germination niche that is the range of conditions under which radicle emergence and seedling development occur [1]. Understanding this niche provides insights into habitat specialization and regeneration strategies and helps connecting experimental findings with field-based patterns of recruitment [2–4].

From a conservation perspective, knowledge of germination requirements is essential for ex situ propagation, reintroduction, and translocation planning [4, 5]. This is particularly relevant for narrow-range endemics, whose small and fragmented populations are highly susceptible to demographic fluctuations, genetic drift, inbreeding, and environmental stochasticity [6, 7]. Identifying the environmental cues that trigger or inhibit germination can thus inform both habitat management and species recovery protocols.

Trait-based approaches offer a complementary framework to investigate plant propagation [8–11]. Functional traits, defined as measurable features that affect organism performance, allow us to link phenotype to environment and demographic processes [12, 13]. Germination traits themselves can reflect broader life history strategies, including bet-hedging, where seeds delay or stagger emergence to avoid risks in unpredictable environments. This strategy is often mediated by seed dimorphism or dormancy variability [14, 15].

While morphological traits have often been correlated with germination responses (e.g. seed coat quality and composition, seed size, shape), physiological traits (e.g. photosynthesis rate, water-use efficiency, stomatal opening) provide more direct information on how plants respond to microclimatic gradients [16–18]. Photosynthetic performance and pigment composition are particularly informative under conditions of variable light and temperature [19]. Parameters derived from chlorophyll fluorescence, such as maximum photosystem II efficiency (Fv/Fm) and non-photochemical quenching (NPQ), indicate respectively how effectively plants convert light into energy or dissipate excess energy as heat to prevent photooxidative damage [20]. The abundance and the quality of photosynthetic pigments acclimate to the different light environments [21, 22]. Indeed, in shade conditions plants enhance light harvesting by e.g. increasing total chlorophyll content and lowering chlorophyll a: b ratio. Under high irradiance, instead, plants favour photoprotection, particularly when coupled with water deficit or temperature extremes (e.g. by increasing carotenoid content and sustaining NPQ) [23]. Together, these traits provide a powerful lens to understand how plants balance energy acquisition with stress tolerance, informing both ecological specialization and reproduction output [24, 25].

In this context, ecological niche can modulate germination traits by aligning seed physiology with the conditions experienced by the mother plant [26]. These transgenerational effects, including changes in seed dormancy or germination rate, may be mediated by hormone balances (e.g. GA: ABA ratio), nitrate availability or seed coat permeability [27], and allow progeny to match emergence timing to local microclimatic conditions, a mechanism of increasing relevance in fragmented landscapes [28, 29].

The genus Saxifraga (~ 440–500 spp.; [30]) offers a valuable model to explore the interplay between functional traits, microenvironmental variation, and germination. It is distributed across alpine and montane ecosystems. Many of the species belonging to this genus are chasmophytes, growing in narrow rock crevices under extreme microclimatic regimes, with limited soil and pulsed water availability. This ecological specialization has been associated with multiple alpine radiations and fine-scale endemism, making the genus ideal for studying how physiological and reproductive traits evolve and respond to environmental heterogeneity [1, 30]. Notably, recent work has shown that Saxifraga species with broader germination niches tend to be more widespread, while narrowly distributed endemics often exhibit more constrained germination requirements [31], suggesting that recruitment traits may play a key role in delimiting range size and habitat specialization.

Saxifraga berica (Bég.) D.A. Webb is a steno-endemic chasmophyte confined to the Berici Hills of northeastern Italy [32]. It is known from around 20 localities and grows on an Area of Occupancy less than 1 km², with a total population likely fewer than 1000 individuals [32, 33]. Despite this restricted distribution, S. berica occurs across a wide range of microhabitats, including sun-exposed cliffs, canyons, moist crags, and shaded cave entrances [34], offering an opportunity to investigate how microenvironmental heterogeneity shapes both functional trait expression and reproduction strategies.

In this study, we test whether fine-scale environmental variation influences phenotypic plasticity and germination in S. berica. Specifically, we address the following research questions: (i) do individuals of S. berica form distinct clusters based on environmental variables? (ii) are physiological and morphological traits associated with these environmental conditions? and (iii) do germination parameters vary across individuals due to an environmentally driven maternal effect?

By addressing these questions in a narrowly endemic species occupying steep microclimatic gradients, we aim to clarify the role of vegetative and reproductive functional traits in S. berica. Rather than treating microhabitat conditions, adult plant phenotype, and seed germination as independent components, we integrate them within a single framework by linking fine-scale in situ environmental measurements to functional traits of plants and to the germination of their seeds. Such an integrative design remains uncommon, particularly for narrow endemic species, where germination processes are often investigated without connection to field-based traits and the growing site data. By combining detailed microenvironmental characterization with measurements of adult plant traits and laboratory germination assays, we assess whether microhabitat heterogeneity is consistently reflected across life stages. This approach provides a more mechanistic perspective on the interplay between environmental conditions and traits variation, offering a quantitative framework to inform conservation of chasmophytic endemics.

Materials and methods

Study area

The Berici Hills (Colli Berici) are an isolated hilly system (~ 200 km²) located in the southern part of the Vicenza province (NE Italy). The area shows a marked topographic asymmetry, with steep slopes on the eastern side and gentler relief to the west, shaped by its geological structure as a slightly tilted anticline with a NNE–SSW axis [35]. The substrate is mainly composed of carbonate rocks and calcarenites of Late Cretaceous–Early Miocene origin, which give rise to extensive limestone outcrops and karst features such as caves and dolines [36].

The climate is temperate sub-continental, with precipitation peaks in spring and autumn and minima in winter and summer, and limited orographic effects due to the modest elevation of the reliefs [37].

Vegetation is dominated by deciduous forests of the Querco-Fagetea class, including thermophilous communities (e.g. Ostrya carpinifolia Scop., Quercus pubescens Willd., Fraxinus ornus L.) and mesophilous stands (e.g. Acer pseudoplatanus L.) depending on local edaphic and microclimatic conditions. Although S. berica is mainly associated with rocky habitats, it can also occur in shaded and humid microhabitats within valley systems [38].

Sampling location and field sampling

We collected data from the literature [39] and GBIF database [40] to define the overall distribution of the species and to select an appropriate number of individuals and growing sites. After plotting the georeferenced data on QGIS (Fig. 1), we selected eight sampling sites in order to mirror the overall distribution of the species (Table 1). A total of 56 individuals have been sampled. To reflect the diversity of micro-habitats occupied by S. berica, we selected individuals growing on exposed cliff, inside the characteristic caves of the Berici Hills (locally known as ‘covoli’), and along crags with temporary streams (locally known as ‘scaranti’). Notably, several sampling locations encompassed more than one microhabitat type, allowing for the collection of individuals experiencing different environmental conditions within the same site. To ensure that sampling adequately represented site-specific conditions, the number of individuals selected per site was adjusted according to both population size and microhabitat heterogeneity. Sites hosting larger populations were sampled more intensively, whereas smaller populations were represented by a proportionally lower number of individuals. Within each site, individuals were deliberately chosen to capture the range of microenvironmental variation present. In heterogeneous habitats, such as cave systems, sampling included individuals spanning the full environmental gradient (e.g. from the outer, more exposed zones to the inner, more buffered areas), thereby ensuring a representative coverage of micro-environmental conditions. A detailed list of sampling sites and number of individuals per site is available in Table 1. We performed a two-step field sampling. In the first step (April-May 2023) we collected morphological and physiological traits (Table S1), while in the second one (late May 2023) we collected mature seed samples from the same individuals.

Functional traits

To investigate the effects of microenvironmental conditions on plant form and function, we measured a set of key morphological and physiological traits. A full list of functional traits measured is provided in Table S1.

Photosynthetic traits have been recorded in the field, while for morphological traits and pigments content we collected samples for laboratory measurements. All the traits have been collected in sunny days during the central hours of the day (11:00–14:00) to limit sampling effects related to the circadian rhythm, especially on traits linked with photosynthetic parameters. Leaves used for all analyses described below were selected to exclude those that were not fully expanded, or showed any signs of damage or senescence.

Morphological traits

Five leaves have been collected with petiole for each individual. On each measurement day, mature leaves were detached from shoots and immediately wrapped in cling film, put in plastic bags with a piece of wet paper and transported to the laboratory in refrigerated bags. Once in the laboratory, we removed the first part of the petiole with a blade and rehydrated leaves by soaking the petiole in tap water overnight. The day after fresh weight (FW) has been measured after petiole removal (AS 220.R2. analytical balance, Radwag, Poland). Leaves have then been scanned (MFC-L8650CDW, Brother, USA) to measure Leaf Area (LA) via Image J. As a last step, leaves have been enveloped in paper bags and dried at 63 °C for at least 72 h to measure Dry Weight (DW). We then calculated Leaf Dry Matter Content (LDMC) as the ratio DW / FW. Finally, Specific Leaf Area (SLA) was calculated as: LA / DW [41].

Pigment analysis

To investigate the possible effects of microenvironmental conditions on pigment concentration, we quantified leaf chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b and carotenoids content in each individual selected. For each individual we took a 0.4 cm2 leaf disk and immediately soaked it in a 1.5 mL Eppendorf filled with 0.5 ml of N-N Dimethylformamide (DMF). To prevent pigment degradation, tubes were wrapped with aluminium foil and kept in a cold bag in the dark during the transport to the laboratory, then stored in a 4 °C fridge for at least 72 h and periodically shaken until measurements to ensure complete extraction of the pigments. Samples were then centrifuged for 3 min at 10,000 r.p.m (MPW-55, MPW Instruments, Poland). Absorption spectra were recorded (Cary 300 UV–Vis, Agilent, USA) between 350 and 750 nm. Pure DMF was used as blank. The final pigment concentration was assessed using Wellburn equations [42], and normalised to the corresponding fresh weight.

Photosynthetic parameters and environmental variables

Photosynthetic parameters have been measured in the field using a MultispeQ (Photosynq, USA). This tool integrates a fluorometer, chlorophyll meter, and spectrometer functionalities. Among the available parameters measurable, we selected: variable fluorescence of photosystem II to maximal fluorescence of photosystem II (Fv′/Fm′), Total Non-Photochemical Quenching (NPQt), Quantum Yield of Photosystem II (ΦPSII), Quantum Yield of Non-Regulated Non-Photochemical Energy Loss (ΦNO), Quantum Yield of Regulated Non-Photochemical Energy Loss (ΦNPQ).

Moreover, by this tool we measured air temperature (°C), air humidity (% RH) and PAR light (µmol photons m−2s− 1). The instrument also measures leaf temperature (°C) and calculates the leaf temperature differential as “leaf temperature - ambient temperature” (negative numbers mean that the leaf is cooler than the surrounding air and vice versa). Additionally, per each individual we measured light quantity and quality with a portable spectrophotometer Li-180 (Licor, Lincoln, USA), recording the Photon Flux Density (PFD) for the following wavelength ranges: broad spectrum (380–780 nm), UV (380–400 nm), blue light (400–500 nm), green light (500–600 nm), red light (600–700 nm), far-red light (700–780 nm). We then calculated the red/far-red ratio (R: FR) and red/blue ratio (R: B).

Germination tests

In order to quantify eventual impacts of the different environmental conditions on seed germination, we measured the final germination percentage (G%) and Mean Germination Time (MGT) using controlled environment germination tests. We collected a seed sample for each individual. Seed collection has been performed taking into account not harvesting more than the 20% of ripe fruits per site as recommended by ENSCONET [43]. Once in the laboratory the fruits (capsules) have been kept in paper bags at room temperature for post-harvest ripening. After a few days seeds have been naturally released by capsules and isolated by use of sieves. Seed germination was tested within 1 month after harvest. Seeds were sown on 1% agar in 50 mm Petri dishes without any previous treatment. We sowed three replicates composed of 20 seeds per each individual. Petri dishes were enveloped in plastic zip locks and randomly arranged in a climatic chamber (Piardi, Castenedolo, Italy) at alternate temperature 17–25 °C using a 12-hour daily photoperiod (photosynthetically active radiation 100 µmol fot−1s). As S. berica is a species of conservation concern and seed availability was therefore limited, the experimental design focused on this single testing condition. Light and temperature regimes were chosen after germination trials on a mixed seed accession of all individuals and fostered by literature sources in order to maximise final germination percentage. Given the limited information available for S. berica, we referred to published data on ecologically comparable congeners, including S. rotundifolia, S. petraea, and S. paradoxa [27]. The selected conditions are consistent with those reported as effective for germination in these species and broadly reflect environmental conditions during the natural germination period. Seed germination was defined as visible radicle protrusion and elongation > 2 mm and it was checked daily for four weeks. At the end of the germination experiment, we calculated the G% and MGT per each Petri dish.

Data analysis

Environmental clustering

To partition individuals based on environmental conditions we first performed a correlation analysis to exclude co-correlating variables using corrplot v0.95 R package, in R software (https://www.r-project.org/). “Light_Intensity (PAR)”, “Ambient_Humidity”, “Ambient_Temperature”, “PPFD(400-700nm)”, “R: FR" and “R: B” standardized variables were used for K-means clustering implemented in the cluster v2.1.6 R package. To interpret the environmental conditions of the obtained clusters, we performed a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) using the package stats v4.4.2 and performed pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum test to assess the significantly different environmental variables between the two clusters using the rstatix v0.7.2 package. We verified the absence of overlap between environmental clusters in the PCA using convex hull (Jaccard Index), kernel density (Bhattacharyya Coefficient and Hellinger Distance), and distance-based separation metrics (Hopkins Statistic and Silhouette Index). First, the convex hull and Jaccard Index were calculated using grDevices v.4.4.2. Then, kernel density, Bhattacharyya Coefficient and Hellinger Distance were calculated using the package ks v.1.15.1. Hopkins Statistic and Silhouette Index were calculated using an original script available on an online repository (https://researchdata.cab.unipd.it/id/eprint/1816).

Functional traits correlation

To test whether plants grown in different environmental conditions exhibited overall differences in morphological and physiological characteristics, we performed a permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA). PERMANOVA tests whether centroids and dispersion of the groups are equivalent for all groups, therefore, we confirmed the positive result of the PERMANOVA by verifying that the group dispersions were homogeneous. PERMANOVA and dispersion tests were performed using vegan v2.7 R package. Pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to identify significantly different functional traits between the clusters, and to account for sample size difference between clusters (cluster 1 = 41, cluster 2 = 15) results were validated via 999-iteration bootstrap subsampling, balancing both groups to the size of the minority cluster. To understand the influence of the environment on the variation in morphological and physiological traits, we performed a redundancy analysis (RDA) using vegan v2.7 R package.

Germination

To assess whether environmental clustering influenced seed performance, germination parameters (final germination percentage, G%, and Mean Germination Time, MGT) were analysed with respect to the PCA-derived clusters. Each individual’s germination data were assigned to its respective cluster, allowing for comparison of germination dynamics across eco-physiologically distinct groups. To detect significant differences in G% and MGT between individuals belonging to the two clusters, we performed Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. To account for sample size difference between clusters (cluster 1 = 41, cluster 2 = 15) results were validated via 999-iteration bootstrap subsampling, balancing both groups to the size of the minority cluster.

Results

Environmental parameters and clustering

To describe the micro-environment experienced by each individual, we measured six non-correlated variables: photosynthetically active radiation (PAR), photon flux density in the 400–700 nm range (PPFD), red: far-red ratio (R: FR), red: blue ratio (R: B), ambient temperature, and ambient humidity (Table S2; Figure S1).

We then applied k-means clustering to the standardized environmental data to identify groups of individuals experiencing similar conditions. While the highest silhouette score is observed at K = 7, the improvement over K = 2 is modest. Given the goal of obtaining interpretable and actionable clusters, and considering the principle of parsimony, K = 2 was selected as it provides a simpler segmentation without a substantial loss in clustering quality (Figure S2). Cluster 1 (n = 41) comprised individuals growing under lower radiation, warmer air, and slightly higher humidity, whereas cluster 2 (n = 15) included individuals exposed to higher radiation and cooler, slightly drier air (Table 2).

We used principal component analysis (PCA; Fig. 2) to summarise these correlated variables into a few synthetic axes and to visualise the main environmental gradients in our dataset. The PCA was based on individuals with complete environmental data (n = 56). The first two components explained 64.2% of the total variance (Fig. 2) PC1 mainly captured a gradient from low to high light availability and R: FR, whereas variation in light quality (R: B) and the temperature-humidity combination mainly contributed to PC2. The two clusters identified by K-means corresponded directly to the groups visible along the primary environmental gradient in the PCA (Fig. 2). Overall, PCA Convex-Hull and Kernel density overlap indices and distance-based separation indices indicated minimal overlap between these two groups, confirming that they represent distinct environmental contexts (Table S3). These clusters provide a clear environmental framework for comparing plant traits and germination responses.

Functional diversity of clusters

To test whether individuals from different microhabitats also differed in their overall functional traits, we used a PERMANOVA on the multivariate trait dataset. This analysis showed a strong and significant effect of environmental clusters on trait composition (F = 33.14, R² = 0.38, p = 0.001), indicating that individuals from the two microhabitats occupy clearly distinct regions of trait space. Group dispersions were homogeneous, confirming that these differences were driven by separation between group centroids rather than by unequal within-group variability.

We then used redundancy analysis (RDA) to identify which environmental variables were most closely associated with these trait differences. Light conditions emerged as the dominant driver of functional differentiation: photosynthetically active radiation (F = 33.62, p = 0.001), red: far-red ratio (F = 4.17, p = 0.007), and red: blue ratio (F = 29.10, p = 0.001) significantly explained variation in trait composition, whereas ambient humidity (p = 0.257) and photon flux density (p = 0.655) had no detectable effect. Together, these results show that fine-scale variation in light intensity and spectral quality among microhabitats plays a central role in defining the ecological niche of S. berica individuals (Fig. 2, Figure S3; Figure S4).

Photosynthetic performance

To evaluate how individuals adjusted the balance between light use and photoprotection across microhabitats, we quantified a set of chlorophyll fluorescence-based photosynthetic traits. Photosynthetic traits differed consistently between the two environmental clusters (Fig. 3A). Individuals in cluster 1 relied more on photoprotective energy dissipation. They exhibited higher values of non-photochemical quenching (NPQt) and ΦNPQ, together with lower ΦNO (Fig. 3A; Table S4). This suggests that a larger proportion of the absorbed light energy was actively dissipated as heat rather than being transferred through PSII.

By contrast, individuals in cluster 2 showed higher photochemical capacity, as indicated by greater linear electron flow (LEF) and higher maximum efficiency of PSII measured in light conditions (Fv′/Fm′), (Fig. 3A; Table S4). This pattern suggests that plants in these microhabitats are able to make a more efficient use of absorbed light for photochemistry and better maintenance of open PSII reaction centres under the measuring conditions. The effective PSII quantum yield (ΦPSII) was only slightly higher in cluster 1 and did not differ significantly between clusters (Table S4). This suggests that the overall photochemical efficiency at the measuring light level was similar, but achieved through contrasting balances between photochemistry and photoprotective energy dissipation in the two clusters.

Photosynthetic pigment concentration

To assess whether microhabitat differences in light environment were accompanied by adjustments in light-harvesting and photoprotective pools, we measured leaf pigment contents. Individuals in cluster 1 had consistently higher pigment concentrations than those in cluster 2 (Table 3; Table S4). Moreover, the Chl a/b ratio was significantly higher in cluster 1, indicating a shift in antenna composition accompanying the overall increase in pigment content. By contrast, the (Chl a + b)/Car ratio did not differ significantly between clusters (Fig. 3B).

Leaf morphology

To evaluate whether microhabitat conditions were associated with shifts in leaf structure and thermal regulation, we measured specific leaf area (SLA), leaf dry matter content (LDMC), and leaf-air temperature differential (Fig. 3C). SLA was significantly higher in cluster 1 than in cluster 2, indicating the development of thinner leaves in cluster 1. By contrast, LDMC did not differ significantly between clusters. Leaf-air temperature differential was also similar in the two clusters (both ≈ − 1.3 °C; Table S4).

Germination

To assess whether microhabitat conditions were reflected in offspring performance, we conducted germination tests on seeds collected from individuals in each cluster. Final germination percentage (G%) was consistently high across all S. berica seeds and did not differ between clusters (Fig. 4A). By contrast, Mean Germination Time (MGT) differed markedly: seeds from cluster 1 required on average 28.1 ± 3.2 days to complete germination, almost twice the time observed in cluster 2 (15.2 ± 2.1 days; Fig. 4B; Table S5).

Discussion

This study examined how fine-scale environmental variation within the narrow distribution range of Saxifraga berica (Bég.) D.A. Webb influences its physiology and germination. By combining field measurements of functional traits with germination assays under controlled conditions, we tested the hypothesis that microenvironmental heterogeneity drives intra-specific traits variation through physiological plasticity and maternal effects. Our findings reveal that despite the species’ very limited geographic range, S. berica displays significant ecophysiological variation structured by local environmental conditions.

Individuals of S. berica cluster according to light-driven microhabitat variation

We found that individuals of S. berica are segregated into two distinct environmental clusters that reflect microhabitat differences in light availability and temperature. These clusters correspond to exposed cliff habitats and shaded rock crevices or caves (Figure S3). This differentiation, observed over a remarkably narrow spatial scale, highlights the extent to which steep environmental gradients, particularly those related to irradiance, structure species distributions in limestone landscapes.

Fine-scale environmental heterogeneity is a hallmark of chasmophytic systems and has long been recognized as a driver of microhabitat specialization in alpine and subalpine floras [44, 45]. In our study, gradients in both light intensity and spectral composition (R: FR and R: B ratios) likely act as primary ecological filters, influencing both energy balance and photoreceptor-mediated developmental responses. The observed clustering therefore provides not only a spatial but also a mechanistic foundation for functional traits divergence in this narrowly distributed endemic. Such fine-scale niche partitioning is ecologically significant in species with limited dispersal capacity, as it enhances persistence across heterogeneous landscapes and buffers against environmental change. In fact, seeds of S. berica are mainly dispersed by gravity, and no additional dispersal agents (e.g. insects or running water) have been documented. This limited dispersal capacity may constrain the species’ ability to rapidly colonise new surfaces and may partly explain its strongly localised distribution, despite populations remaining stable in terms of the numbers of individuals. Moreover, S. berica exhibits strong geotropism during fruiting, an adaptive trait that favours seed deposition near the rock crags typical of the Berici Hills. This limits the dispersal range but, at the same time, promotes deposition on substrates suitable for germination.

Functional differentiation is driven by physiological traits variation

Ecophysiological analyses revealed that physiological traits, particularly those associated with photosynthesis and pigments composition, varied significantly between microhabitat clusters, whereas leaf morphology showed only modest differentiation. The two clusters correspond to ecologically distinct microhabitats within the narrow Area Of Occupancy of S. berica: cluster 2 encompasses exposed limestone cliffs characterized by higher, but still limited, photosynthetic photon flux density (≈ 21 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ PAR), while cluster 1 includes shaded caves and crags with markedly lower light availability (≈ 6 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ PAR). In addition to reduced PAR, the two clusters also differed in the spectral composition of light. Cluster 1 corresponded to sites with a strongly higher proportion of far-red radiation, as indicated by a lower red: far-red ratio (R: FR). Low R: FR conditions are typical of shaded environments, such as beneath plant canopies, where chlorophyll strongly absorbs photosynthetically efficient blue and red wavelengths while far-red is preferentially transmitted or reflected [46–49]. For comparison, the R: FR ratio is around 1.2 in open daylight but can decrease to about 0.2 under a typical canopy [50]. The ability of S. berica to persist across this gradient highlights its capacity to exploit contrasting microsites within a highly restricted range [51, 52].

Photosynthetic results align with established models of photo-acclimation [53], in which plants modulate energy use through physiological plasticity to balance light harvesting and energy dissipation in response to local irradiance regimes. Indeed, both clusters experienced generally low irradiance, and the photosynthetic responses in both groups are therefore consistent with acclimation to low light rather than to true high-light conditions. Within this shared low-light context, plants from the relatively more exposed sites (cluster 2) exhibited higher LEF, ΦPSII and Fv′/Fm′, indicative of greater photochemical efficiency and sustained electron transport under the available irradiance. By contrast, the strong far-red enrichment in cluster 1 (low R: FR) likely underpins the distinct pigment and photo-physiological profile observed in this group, which closely resembles that described for plants acclimated to deep or canopy shade [54, 55]. The combination of higher Chl a/b and increased total chlorophyll in cluster 1, together with the invariance of ΦPSII (effective PSII quantum yield) between clusters, is consistent with a relative increase in PSII reaction centres compared with LHCII antenna complexes, suggesting a reorganisation of the photosynthetic apparatus in response to the far-red–enriched spectrum, with a concomitant enhancement of PSI efficiency, which preferentially absorbs in the far-red [56–59]. Overall, this suite of responses is consistent with photon limitation under a red-shifted spectrum, in which the photosynthetic apparatus adjusts antenna size, the balance between linear and cyclic electron transport (state transitions/CEF), and pigment composition to maximise efficiency under the prevailing light environment [60, 61]. In contrast, leaf morphological differentiations were less pronounced. Indeed, Specific leaf area (SLA) was slightly higher in shaded individuals, reflecting thinner, more efficient light-harvesting leaves, while leaf dry matter content (LDMC) remained stable across clusters. This pattern suggests a homogeneous vegetative growth strategy across different microhabitats, likely also due to similar soil nutrient availability [62].

This pattern suggests that in S. berica, structural investment seems to be relatively conservative (at least in term of SLA and LDMC) and that microhabitat adaptation could be primarily mediated by flexible physiological responses. Similar patterns have been observed in other alpine and subalpine species, where physiological traits respond more dynamically than structural ones to environmental gradients [63, 64]. Consequently, physiological photosynthesis-related plasticity appears to be the key mechanism of this species mediating functional differentiation. These observations support the hypothesis that environmental filtering and phenotypic plasticity underlie intra-specific variation in S. berica. Rather than being driven by fixed genetic divergence, trait differentiation appears to result from flexible, environmentally induced phenotypes, a strategy that may be particularly advantageous in fragmented, topographically complex habitats. This interpretation aligns with evidence from other narrow endemic taxa [7, 8] and reinforces the role of physiological plasticity as a critical mechanism for maintaining ecological breadth and resilience in species with limited ranges.

Germination timing varies across environmental clusters

The germination results further underscore the ecological continuity between vegetative functional traits and propagation processes. While final germination percentage (G%) was uniformly high across environmental clusters, indicating consistently high seed viability, in line with patterns described for many alpine taxa with opportunistic germination strategy [65], germination timing differed markedly. Seeds from more shaded habitats (cluster 1) exhibited significantly slower germination, with Mean Germination Time (MGT) nearly twice that of seeds collected from less shaded habitats (cluster 2).

Given the absence of major morphological differences between source plants in terms of SLA and LDMC, this variation is unlikely to stem from intrinsic developmental constraints. Instead, it points to environmental effect, wherein the microhabitat conditions experienced by plants, particularly light availability influences germination rates [66]. In low-light, humid environments, seed development may involve altered hormonal balances, notably a lower GA: ABA ratio, or changes in seed coat permeability, both of which are known to delay germination [67]. Consistently, experimental studies show that variation in the red: far-red ratio (R: FR), perceived by phytochromes, can tightly regulate germination through GA–ABA signalling, thereby linking canopy-filtered light environments to germination responses [68]. Such imprinting may thus align germination timing with the environmental context of the growing habitat, a phenomenon increasingly recognized as a key ecological mechanism in plants [65].

From an ecological perspective, these differences likely represent adaptive strategies fine-tuned to local microhabitat regimes. Slower germination in seeds from shaded environments may function as a conservative bet-hedging strategy, reducing the risk of emergence under unstable or suboptimal conditions. Conversely, faster germination in seeds from exposed habitats is consistent with an opportunistic strategy, maximising establishment success during brief periods of moisture availability in sunlit microsites [69].

These findings support the concept of a germination niche as an extension of the plant functional niche [70]. The alignment between physiological clustering in individuals and habitat-specific germination timing suggests that the same environmental gradients shaping photosynthetic performance may also influence germination timing via environmental conditioning [71]. Comparable habitat-dependent germination patterns have been reported in other alpine Saxifraga species, where recruitment traits often correlate with distribution limits [65, 72]. In S. berica, this cross-stage of functional and reproductive traits reveals a coherent ecophysiological strategy that enhances resilience under heterogeneous microclimatic conditions.

Implications for functional ecology and conservation practice

Taken together, our results offer broader insights into how narrowly distributed, microhabitat-specialised taxa can persist across heterogeneous environments through fine-scale functional differentiation and phenotypic plasticity. In S. berica, the combination of physiological plasticity in adult individuals and seed germination timing enables persistence across steep environmental gradients despite a highly restricted geographic range. This strategy may be particularly advantageous in topographically complex limestone systems, where chasmophyte species encounter sharp shifts in irradiance, humidity, and temperature over short distances [7].

These findings add to growing evidence that phenotypic plasticity and transgenerational effects are crucial components of plant adaptive capacity, especially under rapid environmental change [13]. Importantly, they also carry practical relevance for conservation and ecological restoration. Recognising habitat-specific functional syndromes can help guide the selection of donor populations and microsites for translocation or reinforcement programmes. Understanding how growing habitat influences germination requirements or timing allows for the design of propagation protocols that align with the environmental context defining the species’ regeneration niche [71, 72]. From an ex situ conservation perspective, these insights underscore the importance of capturing not only genetic diversity but also ecologically meaningful trait variation linked to environmental acclimation and propagation. For narrowly endemic species like S. berica, such approach is essential to ensure the long-term viability of both wild and cultivated populations.

By integrating traits expression across life stages, from adult functional profiles to seed germination dynamics, our study demonstrates the power of a life cycle-based functional framework to disentangle the mechanisms that enable persistence in fragmented and heterogeneous habitats. This approach can serve as a model for assessing adaptive potential and informing conservation strategies in other habitat specialists with limited ranges.

Limitation and future perspective

As with any field-based study, the observed patterns do not allow a direct separation of phenotypic plasticity from genetic differentiation. Similarly, germination assays conducted under a single controlled regime may not fully capture the species’ germination niche, and maternal effects remain inferred rather than experimentally tested. These open questions define a clear roadmap for future work. Common-garden experiments, reciprocal transplants, and germination trials across multiple environmental conditions will help disentangle the mechanisms underlying the patterns described here. Together, these efforts will address the fundamental question of whether the functional differentiation documented here reflects acclimation, adaptation, or both, and to build a comprehensive picture of how this narrow endemic persists across a heterogeneous microhabitat mosaic.

Conclusions

Our results reveal that steep microenvironmental variation, even within the narrow range of S. berica, is sufficient to generate pronounced differentiation in functional traits and germination strategy. Individuals segregate into two ecological clusters primarily shaped by light availability and quality, with distinct physiological profiles but minimal morphological divergence.

Germination dynamics echo this pattern: while overall seed viability remains high, the germination timing varies systematically with the microhabitat conditions experienced by the mother plant, suggesting a role for transgenerational plasticity in aligning germination timing with local conditions.

These findings underscore the continuity between adult functional strategies and seed-level responses, reinforcing the concept of a germination niche as an extension of ecological performance across life stages. For conservation, our findings emphasize the need to account for fine-scale habitat variability when planning propagation, translocation, or restoration interventions. Integrating trait-based approaches into conservation design offers a powerful means to support the resilience and ecological fidelity of narrowly endemic species like S. berica.
