Adherence to national guidelines for responsible and ethical health communication through digital media across the orthodontic profession: From influence to accountability
Authors: Prachi Anil Bhorawat, Vaibhav Kumar, Ramola Ashish Gaikwad, Meghna Vandekar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRapid expansion of digital media has transformed healthcare communication, and social media platforms are now widely used by the orthodontic profession for professional promotion, patient education, and dissemination of health information. While these platforms create valuable engagement opportunities, they raise concerns regarding professional accountability, ethical responsibility, and adherence to regulatory guidelines.AimTo assess awareness of and adherence to the National Guidelines for Responsible and Ethical Health/Wellness Communication through Digital Media in India among orthodontists and orthodontic residents, and to identify factors influencing compliance.Materials and methodsA descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted among practising orthodontists and orthodontic residents across India using the English version of the validated DISCOVER questionnaire, evaluating six domains: awareness, compliance, oversight, verification, ethics, and responsibility. The questionnaire was administered as a structured online form and responses were captured anonymously. Descriptive and inferential analyses were performed.ResultsA total of 1308 participants were surveyed. Awareness scores averaged 61.64 ± 28.37, indicating variability in understanding ethical and regulatory expectations, whereas Compliance was relatively high (84.74 ± 18.98). Oversight (70.45 ± 25.68) and Verification (81.52 ± 20.99) showed moderate engagement, while Ethics (96.25 ± 10.51) and Responsibility (96.46 ± 11.38) were highest. Orthodontists with more than 15 years of experience demonstrated higher Oversight, Verification, and Responsibility scores. Logistic regression identified Awareness, Oversight, and Verification as significant predictors of perfect compliance.ConclusionAlthough orthodontists and residents increasingly utilise digital platforms, adherence to national guidelines was inconsistent, with high ethical intent coexisting with limited regulatory awareness. Awareness, Oversight, and Verification emerged as the principal predictors of full compliance.Keywords: Digital health communication, Orthodontists, Social media ethics, Professional responsibility, Health communication guidelines, Orthodontic practice

Introduction

Digital media has emerged as one of the most powerful determinants of health behaviour in the twenty-first century, reshaping not only how patients access information but how they form beliefs, make treatment decisions, and place trust in professional authority.1 Nowhere is this more consequential than in orthodontics—a specialty whose inherently aesthetic, youth-centric, and image-driven character aligns closely with platform cultures that reward visibility and transformation. Orthodontic patients increasingly arrive having formed expectations online, shaped by influencer content, before-and-after imagery, and peer endorsements rather than validated professional guidance.2,3 In this ecosystem the orthodontist functions simultaneously as clinician, content creator, and inadvertent public-health communicator.

India presents a particularly critical context, with over 820 million internet users and among the world's fastest-growing social media markets.4 Audiovisual content that oversimplifies procedures, exaggerates outcomes, or omits risk disclosures can distort patient expectations and compromise informed consent.5 Influencer-style endorsements—particularly undisclosed promotional content—blur the boundary between education and advertisement, while false or sensational health content is known to propagate faster than accurate information.6 The World Health Organization's advisory explicitly identified unregulated health influencing and digital misinformation as emergent global threats.7

Recognising these risks, regulatory bodies have introduced explicit governance frameworks. The Advertising Standards Council of India (ASCI) released comprehensive Influencer Advertising Guidelines mandating transparent disclosures and prohibiting misleading health claims.8 The National Medical Commission (NMC) and Dental Council of India (DCI) expanded professional-conduct regulations to online communication, barring unverifiable claims and disguised promotions by healthcare professionals.9,10 International analogues—including the UK Advertising Standards Authority influencer guidelines, the Australian AHPRA advertising code, and the European Union Digital Services Act—underscore the global urgency of regulation.11, 12, 13

Despite these expanding regulatory expectations, empirical evidence on how orthodontists actually understand and operationalise digital-communication norms remains strikingly limited—particularly in India, where clinicians work in intensely competitive, digitally mediated markets serving a disproportionately young consumer base. Prior research has largely examined isolated constructs such as e-professionalism or advertising behaviour in silos, constrained by small institutional samples and the absence of validated, policy-oriented measurement tools.14, 15, 16 No instrument has yet captured, within a single framework, the full behavioural architecture of ethical digital practice—from awareness of guidelines through to verification, oversight, and accountable promotion. This represents a critical methodological gap.

The present study addresses this gap through the DISCOVER (Digital Influence, Compliance, Oversight, Verification, Ethics, and Responsibility) Questionnaire—a structured, theory-informed, and empirically testable instrument that, to our knowledge, is the first to operationalise the multidimensional determinants of ethical digital communication among orthodontists at national scale.17 The primary objective was to evaluate the adherence of orthodontists and orthodontic residents to national digital-communication guidelines across six behavioural domains, examine inter-domain relationships, assess variation across levels of professional experience, and identify predictors of high compliance.

Materials and methods

Study design and reporting framework

This nationwide descriptive cross-sectional survey was conducted over a two-month period among orthodontic and dentofacial orthopaedic practitioners in India. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational research.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Dr. G.D. Pol Foundation's YMT Dental College and Hospital, Kharghar (Approval No.: YMTDC/IBC/OUT/2025/317-P). Electronic informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrolment. No personally identifiable data were collected, ensuring complete anonymity.

Sample size and participant recruitment

Using the single-proportion formula (Z = 1.96; p = 0.23 derived from a pilot survey of 50 orthodontists; absolute precision d = 0.025), the minimum required sample was 1088 participants. After accounting for approximately 10% anticipated non-response, the target was set at 1308.

Recruitment followed a Probability Proportional to Size (PPS) sampling strategy, using the Indian Orthodontic Society (IOS) membership directory as the sampling frame, with members stratified into the six conventional geographic zones of India (North, South, East, West, Central, North-East). Under PPS, participants were allocated from each zone in direct proportion to that zone's share of total IOS membership rather than by equal allocation. The zone-wise proportions in the achieved sample are therefore deliberately unequal and mirror the true national distribution of the orthodontic workforce, which is concentrated in the southern, northern, and western zones and sparse in the North-East. Zone thus functions as a sampling stratum and marker of national representativeness, not as an administrative or regulatory category; equalising zonal counts would have over-represented low-density regions and produced a sample not reflective of the profession as it exists.

Data were collected using a structured online questionnaire built and hosted on Google Forms, which served as the sole data-capture platform. Responses were recorded directly and automatically into the linked Google Forms response database, with no manual transcription at any stage, and were exported to a spreadsheet for analysis. The secure participation link was disseminated to eligible members through IOS-affiliated channels (WhatsApp, Telegram, and email); these were used solely to distribute the link and captured no data. Submissions were restricted to one per respondent, mandatory fields prevented incomplete records, no personally identifiable information was solicited, and up to three reminders were sent at one-week intervals to non-responders.

Eligibility criteria

Registered orthodontists and orthodontic residents (postgraduate trainees) enrolled under the IOS—holding valid state dental licence or registration numbers and willing to provide informed consent—were included. Unregistered practitioners and those unwilling to participate were excluded.

Study population: The orthodontic workforce across the training-to-practice continuum

The study population comprised the orthodontic workforce in its entirety—both qualified, practising orthodontists and orthodontic residents undergoing postgraduate training. Residents are registered student members of the IOS and constitute the profession's emerging, digitally native cohort; their inclusion was a deliberate design choice, as digital-communication habits and the ethical reflexes governing online conduct are largely formed during training, when they are most malleable. Surveying trainees alongside established practitioners therefore characterises the profession across the training-to-practice continuum rather than only at its endpoint. Findings are reported for the combined workforce, with variation examined across tenure (including residents).

Study instrument

The English version of the DISCOVER Questionnaire was used. DISCOVER is a novel, pre-validated, copyrighted 43-item instrument, developed and validated by the authors, evaluating ethical and regulatory adherence in digital professional communication across six domains (Digital Influence, Compliance, Oversight, Verification, Ethics, Responsibility).17 It was developed, validated, and administered exclusively in English; no translated or adapted version was created or used, as English is the language of professional instruction, examination, and practice in orthodontics in India, and all eligible participants were English-literate registered professionals or postgraduate trainees. The instrument assesses familiarity with the ASCI, NMC, and DCI frameworks alongside self-reported adherence to disclosure norms, evidence verification, promotional ethics, and professional accountability. Content validity was established through iterative expert review, and pilot testing (n = 50) informed minor refinements. The scale demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.81).

Statistical analysis

Data were exported from the Google Forms response database, coded, and cleaned in Microsoft Excel before analysis. Descriptive statistics summarised demographic variables and domain scores. Chi-square tests examined associations between tenure categories, sociodemographic variables, and dichotomised high-domain scores (≥66.7). Pearson's correlation coefficients assessed inter-domain associations. Binary logistic regression identified independent predictors of high compliance (Compliance score = 100), adjusting for domain scores and tenure. All analyses used a two-sided significance level of p < 0.05.

Results

Participant profile

A total of 1308 participants—comprising qualified orthodontists (70.8%) and orthodontic residents (29.2%)—were surveyed (Table 1). The cohort was predominantly female (60.3%). Consistent with the PPS design, the zone-wise distribution mirrored the underlying IOS membership density, with the largest contributions from the South (36.2%), North (22.2%), and West (21.6%) zones. High compliance was observed in approximately 52% of participants, with significant variation across tenure (χ2 = 22.70, p < 0.001), geographic zone (χ2 = 20.77, p < 0.001), and influencer follower bracket (χ2 = 33.36, p < 0.001). Gender and qualification status showed no significant association.

Awareness and behavioural practices

As summarised in Table 2, fewer than half of participants (48.5%) were aware of ASCI/NMC digital influencer guidelines, and only 20.7% had received formal training—highlighting a critical educational gap. Awareness of ethical obligations (79.3%) and of the need for scientific substantiation of health claims (90.5%) was comparatively high. Among behavioural practices, 65.7% always disclosed paid endorsements and 87.0% ensured disclosure visibility, yet only 62.8% consistently verified scientific evidence before endorsing products. Most behavioural indicators varied significantly across tenure groups (p < 0.001).

Domain scores, reliability and variation by tenure

DISCOVER domain scores revealed a distinct behavioural gradient (Table 3A; Fig. 1A and B). Ethics and Responsibility demonstrated near-ceiling patterns, whereas Awareness and Oversight showed the greatest dispersion, with Compliance and Verification intermediate. Internal consistency was acceptable for Awareness (α = 0.79) and Oversight (α = 0.71). The lower α values recorded for Ethics (0.06) and Responsibility (0.23) are attributable to strong ceiling effects: because almost all respondents scored at or near the maximum, the variance available for the coefficient to detect was minimal, and these values therefore reflect restricted variance rather than construct inadequacy. One-way ANOVA revealed significant differences in all domain scores across tenure groups (all p < 0.001): practitioners with more than 15 years of experience demonstrated higher Oversight, Verification, and Responsibility scores, while residents and early-career orthodontists performed less well in the operational domains—suggesting a gap between knowledge acquisition and behavioural implementation.

Inter-domain correlations

Pearson correlations (Fig. 1C) revealed moderate positive associations between Awareness, Compliance, and Oversight (r = 0.44–0.50), forming a clustered behavioural construct. Verification showed weaker but meaningful associations (r = 0.17–0.34), while Ethics and Responsibility exhibited near-zero correlations with the remaining domains, consistent with their highly restricted variance.

Predictors of high compliance

Multivariable logistic regression (Table 3B; Fig. 2A) identified Awareness (OR 1.04, 95% CI 1.03–1.04), Oversight (OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.02–1.04), and Verification (OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.02–1.03) as the strongest independent predictors of full compliance (all p < 0.001). Interpreted per 10-point increase, Awareness had the greatest impact (OR ≈ 1.44), followed by Oversight (≈1.33) and Verification (≈1.30). Practitioners with more than 15 years of experience had significantly lower odds of perfect compliance than the 5–15-year group (OR 0.43, 95% CI 0.23–0.80; p = 0.008). The predicted-probability curve (Fig. 2B) demonstrated a monotonic increase in the likelihood of high compliance with rising Awareness scores.

Discussion

This nationwide study reveals a profession strongly anchored in ethical intent yet limited by inconsistent regulatory awareness and operational compliance—a pattern increasingly described in the global literature on digital professionalism.14,15 The near-ceiling scores in Ethics and Responsibility, juxtaposed with low Awareness and Oversight, suggest that orthodontists possess robust normative orientations but lack the structured regulatory literacy to translate these into consistent practice.

This 'ethical–operational gap' is documented internationally. Chretien et al. showed that professionals' online content frequently included unprofessional elements despite self-reported ethical confidence.16 Within dentistry, e-professionalism is an essential contemporary competency, with social media capable of both amplifying misinformation and undermining professional conduct.18 Analyses of orthodontic social media content have similarly documented variable accuracy and unverifiable claims regarding aligner therapy and cosmetic enhancements.3,15 That only 48.5% of participants were aware of ASCI/NMC influencer guidelines—despite India's established regulatory landscape—underscores the need for structured sensitisation.

The relationship between clinical experience and digital-professional behaviour is notable. While practitioners with more than 15 years of experience demonstrated higher Oversight and Verification scores, they showed paradoxically lower odds of perfect Compliance (OR 0.43), possibly reflecting established habits predating formal digital guidelines or greater confidence in discretionary judgement. Younger practitioners, despite higher digital activity, showed lower operational adherence. Because the patient–clinician relationship is increasingly mediated by online platforms,19 and patients frequently select providers on the basis of digital presence,2,3 embedding digital-professionalism competencies within residency curricula is a clear priority.

Awareness, Oversight, and Verification—the three principal predictors of full compliance—are teachable, trainable competencies, and the steep predicted-probability gradient with rising Awareness suggests that targeted educational programmes delivered through the IOS, postgraduate institutions, or continuing professional development may yield substantial real-world gains. Yet the digital landscape cannot be viewed through a purely regulatory lens. Social media is no longer merely a communication channel but a parallel clinical interface, where perceptions are shaped, expectations negotiated, and trust constructed long before the first consultation—creating a genuine equilibrium of opportunity and risk, in which the same platforms that democratise access to credible information can, without structured oversight, amplify oversimplified and commercially driven messaging.

Within this dynamic, the ASCI, NMC, and DCI frameworks 8, 9, 10 are best understood not as static compliance checklists but as living instruments of professional identity in the digital age, whose value lies less in enforcement than in cultivating reflective, accountable, evidence-based engagement. The central challenge revealed here is therefore not awareness of what is permissible, but the translation of principle into practice. Embedding these frameworks into training curricula, clinical workflows, and peer norms will be critical to ensuring that orthodontists evolve not only as clinicians but as responsible digital stewards, transforming digital platforms from arenas of ambiguity into instruments of credible influence. By providing the first validated, reproducible, and policy-aligned framework for this purpose, the present study advances e-professionalism in orthodontics from conceptual concern to measurable, actionable evidence.

Strengths, limitations, and future directions

Strengths: A large, nationally representative sample (n = 1308); probability-proportional-to-size sampling reproducing the true zonal distribution of the orthodontic workforce; a validated multidomain framework; and STROBE-compliant reporting with robust statistical analysis.

Limitations: The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Self-reported behaviour introduces potential social-desirability bias, particularly in ethically sensitive domains. Digital-only dissemination may have excluded less digitally engaged practitioners. Ceiling effects and lower internal consistency in Ethics and Responsibility limit their discriminative utility, although these likely reflect normative professional consensus rather than construct inadequacy.

Future Directions: Future work should examine how targeted educational interventions alter DISCOVER scores longitudinally, incorporate objective audits of clinicians' social media content, and pursue cross-national validation for global benchmarking.
