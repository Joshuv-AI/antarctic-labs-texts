Monteggia equivalent fracture associated with Salter-Harris I fracture of the radial head
Authors: Angelos Ndakuninahaza, Xinggang Lu, Adrien Mons, André Pierre Uzel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMonteggia fracture is an uncommon lesion, which associates ulnar shaft fracture and radial head dislocation. Equivalent Monteggia fracture can occur by associated lesions such as olecranon fracture or radial neck or head fracture.Case presentationWe present the case of a five-year-old girl, right handed, involved in domestic accident falling off a bed and sustained an isolated trauma of the left upper limb. Radiograph showed an unusual case of Monteggia equivalent lesion associating a fracture of the proximal third of the ulna shaft and a growth plate fracture Salter I of the proximal radial physis and anterior displacement of the proximal metaphysis. Management consisted of surgical care by open reduction with intramedullary pinning of the ulnar shaft fracture and osteosuture of the fracture line associated with non-operative management by closed reduction of radial growth plate fracture. Outcome after 24 months was very good without pain, full range of motion and very good healing.ConclusionDiagnosing rare injuries, such as equivalent Monteggia fracture, necessitates a high index of suspicion and scrutiny of the findings to don't miss the radial head fracture. Anatomic surgical reduction can yield optimal functional outcomes.

Introduction

Monteggia fracture is an uncommon lesion involving ulna fracture and radial head dislocation 1.7% in forearm traumas [1], [2]. Equivalent Monteggia lesions have been reported associating both a fracture of the ulnar shaft or olecranon and a dislocation of the radial head complicated by radial neck or head fracture.

We emphasize that, before bone maturity, a physis trauma can occur. In this case, radial head dislocation may be replaced by a growth plate fracture. Theoretically, every type of Salter and Harris fracture is possible. Nevertheless, these possibilities, especially for children, aren't well defined in any classification. There are few cases of equivalent Monteggia lesions documented in the literature with a growth plate injury to the radius without radial head dislocation.

We report an unusual case of Monteggia equivalent lesion associating a fracture of the proximal third of the ulnar shaft and a growth plate fracture Salter I of the proximal radial physis with anterior displacement of the proximal metaphysis.

Case presentation

We present the case of a five-year-old girl, right handed, involved in domestic accident falling off a bed and sustained an isolated trauma of the left upper limb. Initial physical examination noticed a painful, deformity and inability of forearm. There was no skin lesion or vasculo-nervous injury associated. Emergency radiographs of forearm including elbow and wrist revealed a fracture of the proximal third of the ulnar shaft and a growth plate fracture Salter I of the proximal radial physis with anterior displacement of the proximal metaphysis (Fig. 1). Following diagnosis, management was performed in operatory room under general anesthesia. Non-operative management with closed reduction by external maneuvers was unsuccessful to both ulnar and radial injuries and surgical care by open reduction was performed by elective posterior approach of ulnar shaft fracture which found a muscular entrapment. The reduced ulnar shaft fracture was stabilized by intramedullary fixation with Metaizeau pinning 2.0 mm diameter associated by osteosuture with vicryl 1 around the fracture line. The proximal radial physis fracture was non-operatively managed by closed reduction under fluoroscopic control (Fig. 2). There was no post-operative complication. The follow up including clinical and radiological examination was uneventful until 24 months. Serial radiographs demonstrated good bony union of ulna shaft with no loss of alignment of proximal radial epiphysis. At 6 weeks there was radiological evidence of fracture union and maintenance of anatomical reduction. The plaster was removed after 4 weeks and Metaizeau pinning after 6 months.

At the last follow up, 24 months postoperatively, clinical examination noticed excellent results with a good range of motion: flexion 140° (Fig. 3a), extension – 10° for the right side versus 0° for the contralateral side (Fig. 3b), and pronation 90° (Fig. 3c), supination 90° (Fig. 3d). The result was excellent according to the Oxford Elbow Score (47/48) or the Broberg and Morrey functional index. Verbal analogical numeric satisfaction scale was 95%, and the patient did not remark any trouble in sports activities or in daily life. Discussion

Giovanni Battista Monteggia in 1814 firstly described a fracture of the ulnar shaft associated with an anterior dislocation of the radial head in adults [3]. In 1967, Bado [1] classified the Monteggia lesion into 4 types plus 2 types of equivalent lesions, depending on the direction of the dislocation of the radial head and the angulation of the fracture in the ulna. The Bado classification was used by several investigators [4], [5], [6], [7], [8] most of whom found that it had no significant prognostic value. The classification was useful for the planning of a closed reduction in children, but no differences in the final outcomes of the individual types of Monteggia lesion were described.

The pediatric classification proposed by Letts et al. [6] is not focused on growth plate fracture but refers to Bado's classification considering the direction of radial head.

Monteggia's fracture-dislocation is not a common injury in childhood trauma, only 2 to 6.5% of elbow fracture-dislocations [9]. It has an average annual incidence of 2.6% cases per year [10], [11]. Many studies have investigated Monteggia lesion and the results of different types of management, but few have focused on ulna fracture associated with proximal–radial growth plate traumatism [4], [7] and in literature most of them are case report [12], [13]. Sahnoum et al. [14] reported 2 cases of Monteggia equivalent lesions in their study of 33 cases of Monteggia fracture dislocation in children.

Whilst Monteggia fractures are well documented and classified in adults, this is a relatively uncommon entity in children. Peak incidence in children occurs in the 4–10 age groups [15]. The case we presented here involves a 5 year old with the Monteggia equivalent lesion witch is not well classified in literature.

Monteggia fracture-dislocations in children are treated with various methods depending on possibility of reduction and post reduction stability such as non-operative management, surgical management of one or both fracture of ulna and radius by closed or open reduction. In surgical management, intramedullary pinning is more used than plate and screws in ulna fracture and solely used in internal fixation of growth plate fracture [13], [16], [17]. The internal fixation by intramedullary pinning or plate finds its place when orthopedic treatment fails due to persistence of instability or even irreducibility [18], [19].

In our case, the initial non-operative treatment was unsuccessful and surgical care performed found a muscular entrapment witch explains the irreducibility of ulna fracture.

In an oblique fracture line of the ulna an osteosuture is interesting around the fracture line like in our case.

In childhood trauma, the use of screw plate or intramedullary pin for the ulna depends on several factors, including the type and location of the fracture, the age of the child, and the presence of instability or associated complications [12], [20], [21].

In our case, follow up was uneventful till 24 month with excellent bony healing and complete joint motion. Initial Growth plate injury had not affected growing of the limb. The excellent outcome is noticed by several authors in Monteggia equivalent injury in children [12], [13], [20].

Conclusion

Monteggia equivalent fracture is a rare entity in childhood trauma. The attention is required to analyze radiographs of forearm with ulna shaft fracture not to miss the radial proximal growth plate fracture. We highlight that the prognosis depends on successful anatomical reduction and fixation stability.
