Deep learning for predicting patient drug response by transferring gene-level and cell-level knowledge to tumors
Authors: Inyoung Sung, Dongmin Bang, Sun Kim, Sangseon Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrediction of patient-level drug response is critical for precision oncology but remains limited by the scarcity of clinical data. While machine learning models trained on cell lines offer a scalable alternative, biological differences introduce domain shifts that hinder direct translation to patient tumors. Here, we present THERAPI (Tumor Heterogeneity-aware Embedding for Response Adaptation and Patient Inference), a deep learning framework designed to bridge this gap. First, THERAPI aligns patient tumors to cell lines through attention-based aggregation guided by tissue context, modeling each tumor as a linear combination of cell lines. Second, THERAPI transfers gene- and cell-level knowledge from pre-trained perturbation and rank embeddings to train drug response predictors. THERAPI outperforms 11 baselines on TCGA dataset, generalizes to external breast and colorectal cancer cohorts, and supports interpretable gene/pathway-level analysis. These results highlight the value of integrating tumor-biology context and perturbation-aware modeling for generalizable and interpretable drug response prediction towards precision oncology.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Cancer therapy, Systems biology

Introduction

Precision medicine aims to provide optimal therapeutic regimens for individual cancer patients based on the tumor status1. However, identifying the optimal therapy for each patient remains a major challenge, as exhaustively testing all potential drug regimens is not feasible financially and ethically. A promising step toward the realization of precision medicine lies in developing machine learning models that predict personalized drug responses2. The rapid advancement of high-throughput molecular profiling technologies offers unprecedented opportunities to predict drug responses directly from molecular data. However, developing such effective predictors requires large-scale labeled datasets, whereas the collection of patient-level drug response outcomes is severely limited due to ethical, financial, and logistical barriers. For example, The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)3, one of the resources combining gene expression and clinical data, provides rich molecular profiles but contains very few drug response labels, making them insufficient for training accurate prediction models. This lack of labeled data poses a significant obstacle to building reliable deep learning models for patient-level drug response prediction.

In response, many studies have explored preclinical datasets such as the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC)4 and the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE)5, which offer large-scale drug sensitivity measurements alongside pre-treatment gene expression. These datasets have enabled the development of predictive models that perform well within the cell line domain6,7. Furthermore, recent advances in cell line-level drug response prediction have expanded beyond static pre-treatment cellular states to incorporate the effects of drug-induced perturbations8. A representative approach involves using large-scale perturbation datasets such as the Library of Integrated Network-based Cellular Signatures (LINCS)9 or single-cell transcriptomes, that capture gene expression changes after drug treatment; models like CSG2A10 and Geneformer11 build on this by simulating perturbation responses through gene-gene interaction modeling. While these approaches have shown promising performance on in silico or in vitro datasets, the post-perturbation signatures they rely on are not available in the patient datasets. This gap between the model’s training data and the available patient data underscores the need for effective transfer strategies. As an example, when directly applied to patient tumors, these models often exhibit substantial performance degradation, for instance, AUROC scores drop from approximately 0.8 in cell lines to around 0.6 in patient cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 1). This arises from the distributional discrepancy between cell lines and patient tissues, commonly referred to as a ‘domain shift.’

Such a fundamental challenge stems from the biological complexity of tumors. Unlike homogeneous composition of cell lines, patient tumors consist of multiple genetically distinct subclones and diverse microenvironmental factors12. This Intra-Tumor Heterogeneity (ITH) indicates that a one-to-one mapping between a tumor and a single cell line is often biologically implausible. Thus, even with advances in modeling drug-induced perturbations, transferring predictive models trained on cell lines to patients necessitates a representation that captures the heterogeneous nature of tumors.

To address these challenges, we present THERAPI (Tumor Heterogeneity-aware Embedding for Response Adaptation and Patient Inference), a deep learning framework that combines perturbation-aware representations with consideration of tumor-intrinsic transcriptomic heterogeneity for patient-level drug response prediction. As shown in Fig. 1(a), THERAPI consists of two main steps: step 1 for alignment of patient tumors with cancer cell lines, and step 2 for perturbation modeling and drug response prediction in the shared embedding space. In Step 1 (Fig. 1(b, d)), the model takes unlabeled gene expression from TCGA patients and GDSC cell lines as input. Using encoders and an attention module, each patient is represented as a linear combination of 673 cancer cell lines, reflecting tumor-intrinsic transcriptomic heterogeneity, while a tissue classifier is applied to both latent and reconstructed vectors so that tissue context is guided during the alignment. In step 2 (Fig. 1(c)), for each GDSC cell line-drug pair, the model takes the gene expression and drug structure as input and encodes them using two pre-trained models, CSG2A10 and Geneformer11, without further fine-tuning. The resulting embeddings are used as input to a response predictor trained to estimate drug response label between sensitive and resistant. During testing, the aligned patient representation from Step 1 and the target drug’s structures are fed into the trained predictor to generate a patient-specific drug response prediction.

As a result, THERAPI constructed a shared embedding space that preserves tumor context, leading to improved performance over 11 state-of-the-art models in predicting drug responses in TCGA patients, as demonstrated through comprehensive evaluations. The attention-based representation of patient tumors yielded heterogeneity scores that reflect the diversity of contributing cell lines, which enabled survival stratification across multiple clinical endpoints. THERAPI also offers an interpretable framework, enabling gene- and pathway-level analyses that can help improve biological understanding. Lastly, the model showed generalization to external breast and colon cancer patient datasets, suggesting its potential utility in broader drug recommendation settings. Overall, THERAPI demonstrates that drug response signals learned from large-scale cancer cell line resources, LINCS and GDSC, can be effectively integrated and transferred to patient tumor drug response prediction.

Results

THERAPI’s integrative representation of tumor-intrinsic heterogeneity and knowledge from gene level

The objective of THERAPI is to accurately predict patient-specific drug response by aligning cell line and patient expression profiles in a way that reflects underlying biological characteristics of tumors, and by transferring gene-gene interaction information from pre-trained models. As shown in Fig. 1, THERAPI consists of two steps: aligning patient tumors with cancer cell lines, and modeling drug-induced perturbations and predicting drug response. Detailed model components and training objectives are described in the “Alignment step” and “Drug response prediction step”, and details of hyperparameters and experimental settings are provided in the Supplementary Table 1.

Step 1. Alignment of patient tumors with cancer cell lines: In the alignment step, THERAPI represents each patient tumor as a linear combination of GDSC cancer cell lines, reflecting tumor-intrinsic transcriptomic heterogeneity. Using unlabeled data from TCGA and GDSC, the model utilizes an autoencoder framework and learns an attention-based aggregation mechanism, embedding each patient as a weighted combination of cell line gene expression in a shared embedding space. To consider tissue type characteristics during alignment, THERAPI incorporates a tissue label classifier trained on both latent and reconstructed embeddings, enabling the model to account for biological context related to tissue type. The alignment process combines an unsupervised autoencoder trained with reconstruction loss and a tissue classifier trained in a supervised manner using tissue labels, optimized with both cross-entropy loss and center loss. While cross-entropy loss ensures the model to correctly identify the tissue labels, the center loss is further applied to ensure that the latent space is regularized to distinctively cluster samples by their tissues.

Step 2. Modeling perturbation and predicting drug response: In the drug response prediction step, THERAPI is first trained on cell line-drug pairs, which serve as inputs to two pre-trained models: a drug perturbation encoder from CSG2A10 and a rank-based gene interaction encoder from Geneformer11. The embeddings derived from the pre-trained encoders capture both gene-gene and drug-gene interactions, while jointly modeling transcriptomic states before and after drug treatment through Geneformer and CSG2A, respectively. The representations were extracted from the two models using the provided pre-trained weights without further fine-tuning. The resulting representations, including perturbed gene expression, drug embedding, and rank-based embedding, are concatenated and used to train a drug response predictor to accurately distinguish sensitive and resistant labels via binary cross-entropy loss. After training, the model is applied to predict drug response in patients. During inference, each patient expression profile is represented as a linear combination of cell lines using the attention-based aggregation module from the alignment step, and the resulting representation is used as input to the trained predictor. Importantly, patient drug response labels are used only for evaluation, preventing data leakage.

THERAPI trained on cell-line data effectively generalizes to patient samples

To investigate the effectiveness of THERAPI in predicting patient drug response, we conducted comparative experiments using the combination of GDSC as train and TCGA as test dataset, namely the GDSC-TCGA dataset. The training set consisted of 112,533 cell line-drug pairs from 673 cancer cell lines and 174 drugs in GDSC, where 10-fold cross-validation (CV) was performed internally during model training. The test set included 383 patient-drug pairs from 358 labeled TCGA patients treated with 21 unique drugs and was used only for evaluation. Further dataset details are provided in “Dataset”.

We first categorized the baseline models into two groups: cell line-trained models, which are trained solely on cell line data, and domain adaptation (DA)-based models, which are designed to reduce the distributional gap between cell line data (as the source domain) and patient tumor data (as the target domain) through an additional alignment step. The cell line-trained (also referred to as DA-free) group includes traditional machine learning models such as Random Forest, Support Vector Machine (SVM) with an Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel, AdaBoost, and XGBoost, as well as a simple Multilayer Perceptron (MLP, with grid search; details in Supplementary Table 2), and as well as state-of-the-art deep learning models including DeepCDR6, DEERS7, and CSG2A10. The DA-based group includes CODE-AE13, PANCDR14, DiSyn15, and our proposed method, THERAPI. Descriptions of the baseline models are provided in Method 4.5. We formulated patient drug response prediction as a binary classification task (responder vs. non-responder), and evaluated model performance using five metrics: AUROC, AUPRC, accuracy, precision, and F1-score (see “Evaluation Metrics” for details). As a result, THERAPI outperformed all baseline models across all metrics (Table 1). In most cases, DA-based models consistently outperformed DA-free models, highlighting the importance of addressing distributional differences between cell line and patient datasets through DA technology. However, Disyn displayed a noticeable drawback, implying that misalignment of cell-line and patient domain may cost significant performance drop. Furthermore, THERAPI demonstrated robust performance across random seed choices (Supplementary Table 3), while tissue- and drug-wise analyses revealed expected variability across cancer types and compounds (Supplementary Fig. 2).

In addition, we attribute THERAPI’s superior performance among DA-based models to its use of tissue labels and tumor-relevant heterogeneity information, which together support a more biologically informed alignment of cell line and patient data. Unlike previous DA-based models that align domains without incorporating biological context, THERAPI leverages these signals to guide the alignment. To qualitatively evaluate the alignments, we visualized the embedding space using t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) as dimensionality reduction technique for raw expression data and representations from two DA-based models, PANCDR and THERAPI (Fig. 2). Here, CODE-AE and DiSyn were excluded since they only produce drug-specific embeddings. While both PANCDR and THERAPI successfully integrated cell line and patient data into a shared space, only THERAPI preserved tissue structure in the embedding space. The raw expression embeddings retained tissue-level separation but showed clear domain-specific clustering, indicating poor cross-domain integration. Meanwhile, PANCDR achieved domain alignment but failed to maintain biological structure. This distinction is further supported by silhouette scores for tissue labels: raw expression (0.261), PANCDR (-0.219), and THERAPI (0.365).

To further assess the biological relevance of the learned embedding space, we examined whether it captures clinically meaningful molecular patterns. Using the well-annotated breast cancer cohort from TCGA (n = 1099), we evaluated how clustering in the embedding space corresponded to estrogen receptor (ER) status. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, nearest-neighbor clustering of the learned embeddings effectively separated ER-positive and ER-negative patients, with strong concordance to clinical subtype labels (adjusted Rand index = 0.44, normalized mutual information = 0.29). Moreover, differential expression analysis between the two major clusters identified FSD1, IGF2BP2, and TERT as the top differentially expressed genes. IGF2BP216,17 and TERT18,19 have both been linked to increased risk, aggressive progression, and poor prognosis in ER-negative and triple-negative breast cancers (TNBC), while the association of FSD1 with this subtype may occur through indirect mechanisms, potentially involving cell migration and invasion in interaction with HDAC620. These findings collectively support the biological interpretability and clinical relevance of the learned representations.

These results suggest that THERAPI not only achieves domain alignment but does so in a biologically meaningful manner, possibly explaining its superior performance among DA-based models.

Biology-aware design and input refinement contribute to THERAPI’s performances

As introduced, THERAPI operates in two steps: an attention-based alignment step and a gene-level knowledge-integrated drug response prediction step. To evaluate the contribution of each step, we conducted ablation studies on each core component. In addition, we examined whether refining the transcriptomic input through deconvolution-based tumor purification would influence alignment and downstream prediction performance.

Ablation on attention-based aggregation

On the alignment step, THERAPI models each patient tumor as a linear combination of 673 cell lines through attention-based aggregation. To evaluate the importance of this weighting mechanism, we compared it with different alignment strategies of non-learning and earning-based approaches.

Non-learning-based methods computes weights using Euclidean distance, cosine similarity, and Pearson correlation based on pairwise similarity between patient and cell line gene expression profiles. For the non-learning-based methods, we tested similarity-based weighting schemes using Euclidean distance, cosine similarity, and Pearson correlation between patient and cell line gene expression profiles, and the Random Weighted Sum (averaged over 10 iterations) and the Equal-Weighted Tissue Average (mean of cell lines sharing the same tissue label), which was evaluated only in terms of downstream performance for predicting patient drug response. For learning-based methods, we tested a logistic-style model that directly predicts weights from patient gene expression and a K-nearest neighbor (KNN, k = 10) approach on the latent embedding space. In both learning-based settings, a tissue classifier was jointly trained to preserve biological structure. Implementation details are described in the “Comparison of weight assignment strategies in alignment step”.

We first evaluated alignment quality in the embedding space using tissue label-based Silhouette Scores (Fig. 3(a)), where higher scores indicate stronger tissue-level separation. Among all strategies, learning-based methods consistently produced more tissue-compacted embeddings, with THERAPI achieving the highest Silhouette Score. We further investigated how tissue types are distributed among neighboring samples in the embedding space. We calculated tissue label similarity between each sample and its five nearest neighbors within the embedding space where both cell lines and patient samples are mapped. As shown in Fig. 3(b), THERAPI produced embeddings with the highest proportion of nearby samples sharing the same tissue type, implying improved tissue-level coherence in the learned representation space. Similar trends were observed for k = 2 and k = 10 nearest neighbors, with additional details provided in Supplementary Fig. 4.

Additionally, we evaluated downstream performance by predicting patient drug response using each alignment strategy. As shown in Fig. 3(c), THERAPI outperformed in downstream drug response prediction across all alignment strategies, with statistical significance confirmed via paired t-tests (full evaluation metrics are presented in Supplementary Table 4). Aside from THERAPI, the learning-based models generally achieved stronger performance. Notably, the Equal-Weighted Tissue Average, which simply averages GDSC samples sharing the same tissue label in TCGA, outperformed the Random weighted sum and performed comparably to the learning-based models, underscoring the importance of tissue context in cross-domain prediction. A similar pattern emerged in the gene expression reconstruction task (Supplementary Table 5).

We further assessed the role of tissue-level supervision in THERAPI by ablating the auxiliary tissue classifier and the center loss. As shown in Supplementary Table 6, removing the center loss disrupted tissue structure in the embedding space, with the silhouette score dropping from 0.37 to −0.08. This collapse in tissue-level organization translated into a substantial decrease in drug response prediction accuracy. Eliminating both the tissue classifier and center loss produced the greatest degradation, confirming that tissue-level supervision is essential for maintaining biologically coherent representations and supporting predictive performance.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that by learning attention-based weights under tissue-level supervision, THERAPI is able to form a biologically meaningful patient representation space, which in turn leads to improved drug response prediction.

Ablation on gene-level knowledge transfer

In the drug response prediction step, we evaluated the contribution of each gene-level embedding approaches utilized in THERAPI. Specifically, the model integrates three biologically motivated representations: a drug-induced perturbation embedding, a rank-based gene interaction embedding, and the attention-aligned expression profile. To assess the importance of each component, we performed ablation experiments by individually removing each module, referred to as w/o Perturbation embedding (emb.), w/o Rank emb., and w/o Alignment. Details of the ablated model architectures are provided in Supplementary Fig. 5 and Tables 7 and 8. As a result, the complete THERAPI model consistently achieved the highest performance across all shown metrics (Fig. 3(d); full results in Supplementary Table 9). Both the perturbation-based and rank-based modules also contributed to performance gains, with the gene-level and cell-level perturbation representation having the greatest impact on AUROC. These findings suggest that modeling both pre-treatment and post-treatment transcriptomic states is important for accurately predicting drug response in patients. In addition, the attention-based alignment module notably improved F1 score, demonstrating its effectiveness in mitigating domain differences through cross-domain aggregation, leading to balanced predictions. Overall, the results demonstrate the design of THERAPI and highlight the complementary strengths of its constituent modules.

Impact of deconvolution-based input refinement

THERAPI is designed to reflect tumor-intrinsic transcriptomic heterogeneity by representing each patient sample as a linear combination of cancer cell lines. However, TCGA bulk gene expression profiles are derived from heterogeneous tumor tissues composed not only of tumor cells but also of non-tumor cells such as immune cells and stromal cells. Therefore, we examined the extent of tumor cell presence in bulk gene expression profiles and evaluated whether using only tumor-specific transcriptomic signals as model input would influence predictive performance. Here, we evaluated two cancer types with available published single-cell reference datasets: TCGA-HNSC (n = 520) and TCGA-BRCA (n = 1099). For deconvolution, we used the HNSCC reference from GSE10332221 and the BRCA reference from GSE17607822 (see “Deconvolution analysis of TCGA bulk tumor transcriptomes” for details). First, to estimate cell type composition in bulk TCGA samples, we applied the DWLS algorithm23. As a result, tumor cells constituted the largest fraction in both cohorts (average fraction: HNSC = 0.471; BRCA = 0.258; Supplementary Fig. 6), indicating that malignant transcriptomic signals were prominent but not exclusive in bulk expression profiles. Next, we generated tumor-specific expression profiles using two deconvolution methods, CIBERSORTx24 and BayesPrism25. The resulting tumor-specific gene expression matrices were used as input to THERAPI, and alignment and drug response prediction were performed following the same procedure as with raw bulk data. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 7, both raw bulk and deconvolution-derived tumor-specific profiles aligned well with GDSC cell lines in the shared embedding space. Interestingly, when drug response prediction was evaluated using deconvolution-based inputs, performance improved compared to raw bulk inputs in both HNSC and BRCA (Supplementary Table 10). These findings indicate that refining transcriptomic inputs by accounting for tumor purity can further enhance predictive performance within the THERAPI framework.

External evaluation confirms generalizability and translational potential of THERAPI

To assess the generalizability of THERAPI beyond the GDSC-TCGA dataset, we conducted external validation on two independent transcriptomic datasets: I-SPY2 and GSE39582. Similar to the GDSC-TCGA dataset, the model was trained on GDSC cell line dataset and tested on the corresponding patient cohorts.

Paclitaxel response prediction in I-SPY2 breast cancer cohort

The I-SPY226 trial provides transcriptomic profiles of 988 breast cancer patients, among which 178 patients received Paclitaxel treatment (148 non-responders vs. 30 responders). The 810 untreated patients were used in the alignment step to train THERAPI’s patient-to-cell line mapping, and only the breast tissue samples were utilized from the GDSC dataset as training set for consistency. During testing, the 178 treated patients were utilized to evaluate whether GDSC-trained model could generalize to unseen clinical data. As shown in Fig. 4(a), THERAPI effectively aligned the I-SPY2 data into a shared embedding space. For quantitative evaluation, we computed the silhouette score based on data source labels, where values closer to 0 indicate better mixing of the two domains. The two domains were clearly separable before alignment (silhouette score = 0.927), but after alignment, the score dropped to 0.018, indicating successful integration even for an external dataset consisting of a single tissue type. In the prediction step, average logits from THERAPI were significantly different between responders and non-responders (independent samples t-test, p value = 0.0029) as shown by the logit distributions (Fig. 4(b)), demonstrating its robustness in predicting drug response on external patient data. These findings support THERAPI’s generalizability to external datasets and its potential to transfer knowledge from in vitro screens to real-world clinical settings.

Subtype-specific drug sensitivity prediction in colorectal cancer (GSE39582)

To further evaluate THERAPI’s potential for drug recommendation, we applied it to the GEO dataset with accession number GSE3958227, which contains transcriptomic profiles of 519 colorectal cancer patients, including 75 microsatellite instability (MSI) and 444 microsatellite stable (MSS) cases. Previous studies have reported that MSI tumors are generally more sensitive to chemotherapeutic drugs than MSS tumors, with notably lower IC50 values for Irinotecan and Oxaliplatin28,29. In this scenario, we simulated treatment with both drugs and evaluated whether THERAPI could differentiate molecular subtypes based on predicted drug sensitivity. Following alignment with the GDSC dataset, we visualized the embedding space and quantified alignment quality using the silhouette score based on domain labels (Fig. 4 (c)). Before alignment, the two datasets were clearly separable (silhouette score = 0.950), but after alignment, the score dropped to 0.082, indicating that THERAPI effectively aligned external colon cancer patient data. In the prediction step, THERAPI accurately predicted higher sensitivity in MSI tumors for both Irinotecan (independent samples t-test, p value = 0.0065) and Oxaliplatin (independent samples t-test, p value = 0.0104), consistent with known pharmacogenomic patterns (Fig. 4(d)). These results demonstrate that THERAPI can recover subtype-associated therapeutic effects solely from transcriptomic profiles, underscoring its potential for data-driven drug recommendation and personalized treatment stratification.

Entropy-based interpretation of THERAPI reflects tumor-intrinsic transcriptomic heterogeneity and prognostic relevance

In the alignment step, THERAPI represents each patient tumor as a weighted combination of multiple cancer cell lines, an approach that has also been attempted in previous studies30,31. Based on our alignment framework, we sought to investigate the biological meaning of representing patients through such cell line combinations. Since it is well established that higher intra-tumor heterogeneity is associated with poorer prognosis, we were motivated to examine whether the diversity of cell lines contributing to a patient’s representation in the alignment step could reflect tumor-intrinsic transcriptomic heterogeneity32–34. As illustrated in Fig. 5(a), we quantified this diversity of patient tumor by computing the Shannon entropy of the alignment weights for each patient. Based on this measure, patients were stratified into high- and low-entropy groups. We note that this analysis is not intended to evaluate predictive biomarkers, but rather to assess the biological relevance of the alignment-derived entropy as a surrogate marker of tumor-intrinsic transcriptomic heterogeneity, which is known to have prognostic implications.

Darker shades indicate more statistically significant differences between high- and low-tumor diversity groups. d Rank counts of THERAPI, DEPTH, and tITH across 19 survival analysis tests.

As shown in Fig. 5(b), Kaplan-Meier survival analysis based on overall survival (OS) revealed that highly tumor-diverse patients consistently exhibited worse prognosis, including in kidney (p value = 2.8e-4), liver (p value = 2.3e-3), bladder (p value = 3.3e-3), and lung cancers (p value = 3.9e-3). This trend was not limited to OS but was also observed across 14 combinations of clinical endpoints and tissue types, including disease-specific survival (DSS), disease-free interval (DFI), and progression-free interval (PFI) (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Table 11).

We further compared THERAPI’s entropy-based estimates of tumor-intrinsic transcriptomic heterogeneity estimates with two transcriptomic baselines, DEPTH35 and tITH36, both of which quantify heterogeneity directly from gene expression data. As shown in Fig. 5(c), a heatmap of −log10(p-value) across 19 clinical endpoint-tissue type combinations illustrates that THERAPI identified a greater number of statistically significant stratifications. In total, THERAPI showed significant separation in 14 combinations, compared to six for DEPTH and seven for tITH (the full results for all combinations are provided in Supplementary Tables 12 and 13). Furthermore, ranking the models by their p values across all comparisons shows that THERAPI most frequently achieved the lowest p value among the three models (Fig. 5(d)). Together, these results suggest that THERAPI’s attention-based alignment yields prognostically relevant representations of tumor-intrinsic transcriptomic heterogeneity through cell line diversity.

THERAPI captures drug mechanisms via SHAP and pathway enrichment in glioma and breast cancer

Unlike existing DA-based models, which often rely on simple MLP-based architectures for drug response prediction and thus underrepresent complex gene-gene or drug-gene interactions, THERAPI explicitly models these interactions by integrating drug-induced perturbations and rank-based gene expression representations. To evaluate the biological interpretability of this interaction-aware design, we conducted case studies on two clinically well-known drug-cancer type pairs: Temozolomide-treated glioma37,38 and Tamoxifen-treated breast cancer39–41. For each case, we used SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)42 to estimate the contribution of each gene to the model’s predicted drug response probability. Genes were ranked based on the absolute value of their SHAP scores, and the top 30% were selected for pathway-level interpretation. We then performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database43 to identify pathways enriched among influential genes, particularly focusing on whether known drug-target pathways were recovered. Details of the SHAP value computation and GESA are described in the “SHAP and Gene-set enrichment analysis”, and full SHAP scores and GSEA results are provided in Supplementary Tables 14–17.

Case 1: Temozolomide-treated with Glioma: As an alkylating agent, temozolomide exhibits its anti-tumor effects in glioma by introducing DNA lesions that activate repair pathways such as base excision repair and mismatch repair. In particular, temozolomide induces DNA damage in glioma cells that is primarily repaired through Base Excision Repair (BER) process, and the inhibition of BER, particularly via PARP inhibitors, can sensitize tumors by impairing repair and enhancing cell death. Consistent with this mechanism, as shown in Fig. 6(a), SHAP-based analysis revealed that PARP1 and PARP2 were among the top-ranked genes, and the BER pathway was significantly enriched in GSEA (adjusted p value = 6.8e-4). In addition to BER, other DNA repair mechanisms including nucleotide excision repair (adjusted p value = 5.0e-5) and mismatch repair (adjusted p value = 1.0e-3), were also significantly enriched44–47. Other pathways known to be involved in temozolomide response, including the p53 signaling pathway, known to mediate apoptosis and cell cycle arrest in response to DNA damage (adjusted p value = 1.98e-12)47,48, and the MAPK signaling pathway. which regulates cellular stress responses and has been implicated in temozolomide resistance (adjusted p value = 2.0e-5)49, were also significantly enriched in our GSEA results.

Case 2: Tamoxifen-treated with breast cancer: Tamoxifen is a selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM) widely used to treat estrogen receptor-positive (ER+) breast cancer50. It exerts its anti-tumor effect by binding to estrogen receptors and blocking the estrogen signaling pathway that promotes the growth of hormone-dependent breast cancer cells. Accordingly, SHAP-based analysis identified multiple genes involved in the estrogen signaling pathway51,52, which was significantly enriched in GSEA (adjusted p value = 1.3e-3), as visualized in Fig. 6(b). In addition to estrogen signaling, other pathways associated with tamoxifen resistance were also identified. The PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, known to promote cell survival and therapy resistance in breast cancer, was significantly enriched (adjusted p value = 1.1e-4)53,54. The NF-κB signaling pathway, which is implicated in inflammation-mediated resistance and recurrence, also showed enrichment (adjusted p value = 0.013)55,56, suggesting that THERAPI captured broader mechanisms relevant to tamoxifen response.

Together, these results suggest that THERAPI not only improves drug response prediction through its interaction-aware design, but also offers mechanistic interpretability by identifying functionally coherent gene modules relevant to known drug mechanisms. This capacity for MoA-consistent explanation underscores THERAPI’s potential in biomarker discovery, drug repurposing, and precision oncology.

Discussion

Precision oncology aims to tailor treatments to the unique biology of each patient’s tumor. Central to this goal is the ability to accurately predict individual drug responses by leveraging patient-specific molecular profiles. However, due to the limited availability of labeled patient data, many existing studies have relied on preclinical models, such as cancer cell lines, to directly train predictors and apply learned patterns to clinical settings. While these approaches have made significant progress, along with the advent of domain adaptation technique, they often bypass the two key aspects of patient tumors: the heterogeneous nature of tumor biology and the complex interactions between genes that govern cellular response to perturbations. To address these challenges, we introduced THERAPI, a tumor heterogeneity-aware domain adaptation framework designed to bridge the translational gap between cell line and patient tumor drug responses.

THERAPI consisted of two steps. First, it aligned cell lines and patient tumors within a shared latent space using an attention-based aggregation that represents each patient tumor as a linear combination of 673 cancer cell lines. This formulation enabled consideration of tumor heterogeneity while simultaneously leveraging tissue-type supervision to ensure tumor biology-informed alignment. Second, the model predicted drug responses using two complementary gene-level representations: drug-induced perturbation embeddings and rank-based interaction embeddings. These modules enabled THERAPI to model gene-gene interactions and transfer knowledge across domains in a biologically meaningful manner.

Our extensive experiments evaluated the utility of our proposed model. Compared to 11 baseline models, including eight cell line-trained and three DA-based models, THERAPI achieved improved performance in predicting drug response for TCGA patient data while trained on GDSC cell-line data. Furthermore, the shared embedding space of THERAPI was shown to more effectively preserve the tissue structure and integrate cross-domain samples compared to raw expression or other DA-based model. Ablation studies confirmed the contribution of each module: alignment strategies lacking attention-based aggregation or gene-level embeddings resulted in significantly diminished performance, underscoring the need for tumor-aware design in both alignment and prediction.

External evaluations further supported the generalizability of THERAPI. In the I-SPY2 breast cancer cohort, THERAPI not only formed shared embeddings with GDSC breast cell lines, but also significantly separated responders from non-responders to Paclitaxel (p value = 0.0029), confirming its robustness to unknown clinical data. In the GSE39582 colorectal cancer cohort, the model predicted subtype-specific drug sensitivities consistent with known biological properties. MSI tumors were generally more sensitive to Irinotecan and Oxaliplatin, and THERAPI successfully distinguished between MSI and MSS patients for both drugs (p value = 0.0065 and 0.0104, respectively). This suggests the potential for subtype-specific drug recommendations.

THERAPI also enabled biological interpretation from various perspectives. By representing each patient as a weighted combination of cell lines and quantifying diversity in cell line weights via information theoretic approach, we observed that patients with higher entropy tended to have worse prognoses across cancer types and clinical endpoints. Compared to transcriptome-based analyses such as DEPTH and tITH, THERAPI identified more endpoint-tissue combinations that showed significant survival differences, further supporting its biological relevance. Finally, SHAP-based interpretation and GSEA on model-influential genes revealed enrichment of drug-relevant pathways. For example, in glioma, Temozolomide-associated genes enriched the BER, mismatch repair, and p53 signaling pathways; in breast cancer, Tamoxifen-associated genes highlighted the estrogen, PI3K-Akt, and NF-κB pathways. These results indicate that THERAPI not only predicts drug response but also captures the mechanisms underlying therapeutic efficacy.

Despite its strengths, our framework has several limitations. THERAPI relies on bulk transcriptomic data, which include both malignant and non-malignant components; therefore, the current framework does not explicitly model tumor purity or fully capture the complexity of the tumor microenvironment. Although limited experiments in the TCGA HNSC and BRCA cohorts demonstrated that deconvolution-based tumor purification can further improve predictive performance, such approaches depend on the availability of cancer type-specific single-cell reference datasets, which remain limited. Furthermore, THERAPI depends on the scope and composition of the GDSC cancer cell line dataset. In this study, all analyzed TCGA cancer types had corresponding representations in GDSC; however, the number of available cell lines varies substantially across tumor types, for example, 140 cell lines for lung cancer but only 5 for prostate cancer. The current model does not explicitly account for this imbalance, which may influence performance across cancer types.

Beyond data composition constraints, there are also inherent limitations associated with preclinical models. Cancer cell lines may diverge from their tissues of origin during long-term in vitro culture, potentially limiting their biological fidelity. In THERAPI, however, the embedding space is designed primarily to support the prediction of drug-induced transcriptomic perturbations and downstream patient-level drug response, rather than to perfectly reconstruct tissue identity. Nevertheless, achieving a more biologically faithful reconstruction of the tissue landscape remains an important challenge for future work.

In addition, while we assessed tumor-intrinsic transcriptomic heterogeneity using entropy-based measures, more direct validation through spatial transcriptomics or multi-omics data would provide a richer and more comprehensive characterization of tumor architecture. Looking ahead, future directions include extending THERAPI beyond a single-omics framework by integrating complementary modalities such as proteomics and histopathology, incorporating spatial transcriptomics that preserve cellular and tissue context, and applying the framework to longitudinal treatment response prediction. Such extensions would enable the model to learn and interpret biological information from multiple complementary perspectives.

In summary, THERAPI introduces a tumor biology-aware domain adaptation strategy for drug response prediction, advancing both the accuracy and interpretability of cross-domain transfer models. By incorporating tumor-relevant transcriptomic heterogeneity and gene-level interactions, it demonstrates improved generalization and biological insight, offering a valuable tool for translational research towards precision medicine. As larger and more comprehensive datasets become available over time, we believe this work provides a proof-of-concept for realizing biologically grounded and generalizable prediction models in precision oncology.

Drug response prediction step

Training in the cell line data

During training, the drug response predictor is constructed using two pre-trained models, each capturing a distinct aspect of drug-gene or gene-gene interactions. First, we use CSG2A10 to generate drug-perturbation representations. Given a gene expression-drug SMILES pair as input, it outputs two components: a perturbed gene expression embedding zpert and a drug embedding zdrug, computed as [zpert, zdrug] = fpert(xs, d), where xs is the pre-treatment gene expression of a source domain sample and d is the molecular structure of the drug. Dataset

For patient drug response prediction, we mainly utilized the GDSC-TCGA dataset, built using GDSC4 as the source domain and TCGA3 as the target domain. Both datasets contain gene expression profiles, and 978 landmark gene set9 was utilized to ensure compatibility with the pre-trained CSG2A model. Tissue-type annotations were derived from the primary_disease annotation in GDSC, retaining tissues with at least five samples, resulting in 23 tissue types (Supplementary Table 18). All gene expression data are normalized by the standard transcripts per million bases for each gene, followed by log transformation.

Cell line-level drug response dataset GDSC

The GDSC dataset consists of 673 cancer cell lines and 174 unique drugs, forming a total of 112,533 cell line-drug pairs. The drug response values in GDSC are represented using IC50 (half-maximal inhibitory concentration) values, and we used these values in a binary form. To obtain binary drug response labels in GDSC dataset, we converted the original scalar pIC50 values (the negative base 10 logarithm of IC50) into binary labels based on whether each value was above or below the average pIC50 for each drug across all cell lines. To ensure consistency with the TCGA dataset, where drug response is available as a binary label, we binarized pIC50 values in GDSC as in previous studies13,15: samples above the mean were labeled as responsive (1), and those below as non-responsive (0). The GDSC dataset is publicly available at the GDSC portal (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/), and detailed cancer type-drug counts and visualization are provided in Supplementary Table 19.

Patient-level drug response dataset TCGA

The TCGA dataset consists of 8400 patient samples, including 8,042 unlabeled and 358 labeled patients. Among these, 7,978 samples were classified as primary tumors, 391 as metastatic, and 31 as recurrent, based on the sample_type annotation in TCGA. In this study, we used all TCGA samples regardless of sample origin for model training and evaluation. To assess the potential effect of tumor origin, we additionally evaluated the model using only primary TCGA samples, with results summarized in Supplementary Table 20.

The unlabeled patients were used exclusively during the alignment step to adapt the source and target domains. The 358 labeled patients were treated with 21 unique drugs, resulting in a total of 383 patient-drug pairs, and were used only in the drug response prediction step. The binary drug response labels were determined by following the strategy of prior work13, where for each drug, patients receiving single-drug therapy were retained, and responders and non-responders were defined based on the median response duration after the first treatment. Additionally, for survival analysis, we evaluated four clinical endpoints: overall survival (OS), progression-free interval (PFI), disease-free interval (DFI), and disease-specific survival (DSS) in the TCGA patients. The TCGA dataset is publicly available through the UCSC Xena Cancer Genome Browser (https://xena.ucsc.edu/)57 and detailed cancer type-drug counts and visualization are provided in Supplementary Table 21.

External datasets I-SPY2 and GSE39582

The I-SPY2 dataset consists of expression profiling by array data derived from fresh-frozen pre-treatment breast cancer tumor samples of 988 patients26. Among these, 178 patients received Paclitaxel, with drug response labeled based on pathological complete response (pCR) status: a value of 1 indicating complete response, and 0 indicating failure to achieve complete response. The remaining 810 untreated patients were used in the alignment step alongside the GDSC dataset, while the 178 drug-treated patients were used in the drug response prediction step. The I-SPY2 data were obtained from the GEO database under accession number GSE194040.

The GSE39582 dataset consists of mRNA expression profiles from 519 colorectal cancer patients, including 75 with microsatellite instability (MSI) and 444 with microsatellite stability (MSS)27. Patient status was determined based on five microsatellite markers: if two or more markers showed instability, the sample was classified as MSI; if one or none showed instability, it was classified as MSS. Since GSE39582 does not include drug response information, we randomly partitioned the dataset into alignment and prediction subsets. Using train_test_split from sklearn with a fixed seed of 42, the dataset was split in a 7:3 ratio. As a result, 311 samples (259 MSS, 52 MSI) were used for the alignment step, and the remaining 156 samples (133 MSS, 23 MSI) were used for the drug response prediction step. The GSE39582 dataset was downloaded from the GEO database.

While the external datasets differ in expression platforms (e.g., RNA-seq vs. microarray) and normalization schemes (e.g., log-TPM vs. RMA), THERAPI mitigates these distributional differences through a two-step process. First, each dataset undergoes per-dataset z-score normalization, standardizing gene expression values to a common scale. Next, domain-specific MLP encoders transform each dataset into a shared latent representation space. The alignment module then operates within this harmonized space, enabling robust cross-platform comparability while preserving biologically relevant variation.

Baselines

Cell line-trained models

Cell line-trained models are trained solely on GDSC cell line data and directly applied to predict drug responses in TCGA patient samples, without any alignment between the two domains. We evaluated the following DA-free models: Random Forest, SVM with RBF kernel, AdaBoost, XGBoost, MLP, DeepCDR6, DEERS7, and CSG2A10.

For classical machine learning baselines, we implemented four DA-free models using standard classifiers: Random Forest (RandomForestClassifier from sklearn.ensemble), SVM with RBF kernel (SVC from sklearn.svm), AdaBoost (AdaBoostClassifier from sklearn.ensemble), and XGBoost (XGBClassifier from xgboost58). All models were trained using their default hyperparameters.

For the MLP classifier baseline, we performed a grid search among widely utilized configurations for hyperparameters, including batch size, number of layers and hidden dimension (Supplementary Table 2).

We also included three sophisticated deep learning architectures designed for cell line drug response prediction, DeepCDR, DEERS, and CSG2A. DeepCDR is a UGCN designed for cancer drug response prediction by integrating multi-omics profiles of cancer cells with intrinsic chemical structures of drugs6. DeepCDR automatically learns latent representations of topological structures among atoms and bonds. The model consists of a UGCN and multiple subnetworks, capturing the relationships between genomic, transcriptomic, and chemical data.

DEERS is a deep learning-based recommender system designed for predicting drug sensitivity, specifically for kinase inhibitors7. The model leverages side information from both cancer cell lines (including gene expression, mutation profiles, and tissue type) and drugs (via kinase inhibition profiles). DEERS uses two autoencoders to compress cell line and drug features into low-dimensional representations, which are then combined through a feed-forward neural network to predict drug response.

CSG2A is a condition-specific gene-gene attention network designed to bridge the gap between transcriptomic-level perturbation data (LINCS L1000) and cell-line drug response datasets (GDSC)10. The L1000 assay directly measures the expression of 978 landmark genes using a bead-based detection method, with the remaining transcriptome computationally inferred. For pretraining, the CSG2A employed LINCS Level 4 consensus gene expression signatures, which are preprocessed and z-normalized by the consortium, without further modification. In our framework, we adopted the pretrained CSG2A model, including its linear mapping layer that aligns z-normalized LINCS and GDSC profiles. During this step, CSG2A dynamically learns condition-specific gene-gene interactions, incorporating both biological priors and data-driven learning. The model then employs a transfer learning strategy and fine-tuned the LINCS-trained CSG2A on GDSC while preserving gene interaction networks.

DA-based models

DA-based models aim to bridge the distributional gap between cell line and patient datasets by aligning their feature representations. Unlike DA-free models, these approaches explicitly account for domain shift, typically using unlabeled patient data during training to guide the alignment process. We compared THERAPI against three representative DA-based models: CODE-AE13, PANCDR14, and DiSyn15, all of which employ adversarial loss to reduce domain discrepancy and improve generalization from cell lines to patients in drug response prediction.

CODE-AE is a context-aware deconfounding autoencoder that addresses distribution shift between cell line and patient drug response datasets13. It learns a shared embedding space using unlabeled data from both domains and fine-tunes this space through supervised training on labeled cell-line data. To encourage better domain alignment, CODE-AE incorporates several regularization strategies, with its adversarial variant (CODE-AE-ADV) showing competitive performance. During inference, patient drug responses are predicted based on the aligned embeddings.

PANCDR is a domain adaptation-based model designed to improve cell line-to-patient generalization in drug response prediction14. It consists of two sub-models: an adversarial model that minimizes domain discrepancy between cell line and patient datasets, and a CDR prediction model that extracts drug-response features. PANCDR is trained on both cell line and unlabeled patient data.

DiSyn is a domain adaptation framework designed to enhance the generalizability of drug response prediction from cell line to patient settings15. It disentangles domain-invariant drug response features from domain-specific variations and synthesizes new samples to augment the target domain.

Comparison of weight assignment strategies in alignment step

In the alignment step, THERAPI learns attention-based weights to account for patient-specific tumor heterogeneity using cell line data. We compared it with both non-learning-based and learning-based weight computation approaches.

From the non-learning-based approach, we implemented three similarity-based methods: Euclidean distance, cosine similarity, and Pearson correlation coefficient. For each patient sample in TCGA, weights were computed by performing pairwise comparisons with all cell line samples in the GDSC dataset. In the case of Euclidean distance, after computing distances, we applied min-max scaling and converted them into weights using 1−scaled distance to assign higher weights to closer samples.

From the learning-based approach, we compared two alternative methods: logistic-style weighting and k-NN. In the logistic-style method, the model directly learns to assign weights based on the latent representations of TCGA samples produced by the encoder. These weights are applied to aggregate cell line embeddings, which are used to reconstruct the patient expression and optimized via reconstruction loss. In the KNN approach, separate autoencoders were trained for TCGA and GDSC samples to minimize reconstruction loss, and latent representations were obtained accordingly. After training, Euclidean distances were computed in the latent space, and for each TCGA sample, the k = 10 nearest GDSC samples were selected. The distances to these neighbors were used to assign weights.

Deconvolution analysis of TCGA bulk tumor transcriptomes

To evaluate the impact of tumor purity on model performance, we performed deconvolution analyses on TCGA bulk RNA-seq data using three complementary approaches: DWLS23 for estimating cell type proportions, and CIBERSORTx24 and BayesPrism25 for generating tumor-specific expression profiles. We focused on two cancer types for which high-quality single-cell references are available. For head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (TCGA-HNSC; n = 520), we used the single-cell RNA-seq dataset GSE103322 (5,902 genes across 23,691 cells)21. For breast invasive carcinoma (TCGA-BRCA; n = 1099), we used the single-cell RNA-seq dataset GSE176078 (29,733 genes across 100,064 cells)22. In both cases, genes shared between TCGA bulk data and the corresponding single-cell reference were retained for downstream analyses.

First, to estimate cell type composition within bulk samples, we applied DWLS using signature matrices constructed from the respective single-cell references. This step provided sample-level estimates of tumor and non-tumor cell proportions.

Second, to obtain tumor-specific gene expression profiles, we applied two independent deconvolution methods. CIBERSORTx was performed using the online platform (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/), where a signature matrix was constructed from the corresponding single-cell reference, and the cell type-specific expression imputation module was used to derive tumor-enriched gene expression profiles. In parallel, BayesPrism was implemented in R using the publicly available GitHub tutorial (https://github.com/Danko-Lab/BayesPrism) with default parameter settings. The single-cell reference was used as prior information to infer tumor cell-specific expression within each bulk sample. The resulting tumor-specific expression matrices from CIBERSORTx and BayesPrism were used as alternative inputs to THERAPI. Alignment and drug response prediction were conducted using the same procedure as with raw bulk transcriptomic data to enable direct comparison of model performance.

Survival analysis

To assess the clinical relevance of tumor heterogeneity accounted for by THERAPI, we performed survival analysis using entropy scores derived from the alignment step. Each patient tumor sample was represented as a weighted sum of cancer cell lines, where the attention-based weights reflect the contribution of each cell line. Since the weights for each patient sum to 1, we computed the Shannon entropy of the weight distribution to quantify the diversity of cell lines contributing to each patient’s representation. Patients were then stratified into two groups based on the median entropy: those in the upper 50% were categorized as having high entropy, and those in the lower 50% as having low entropy.

We conducted Kaplan-Meier survival analysis to compare survival outcomes between the two groups across 16 different cancer types. All survival analyses were performed using the lifelines59package in Python. Specifically, the log-rank test was applied via the logrank_test function in lifelines. statistics, and hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were estimated using the CoxPHFitter class. Survival curves were visualized using the Kaplan-Meier Fitter module.

SHAP and gene-set enrichment analysis

To interpret which genes were most influential in predicting patient drug response, we employed SHAP using the Python SHAP library (https://shap.readthedocs.io/). In our model, the input is composed of three embeddings: drug-induced perturbation representation, rank-based gene expression representation, and molecular structure embedding. For the purpose of identifying important genes specific to each drug-tissue type pair, we focused on the SHAP values computed from the perturbation-based embedding. SHAP scores range from −1 to 1, where higher absolute values indicate greater influence on the prediction. For each drug-tissue type pair, genes were ranked according to the absolute SHAP values, and the top 30% of genes were selected for further biological analysis. To further investigate whether the important genes reflected known biological mechanisms, we performed GSEA using the KEGG Human 2021 pathways via Enrichr60. Significance was assessed using adjusted p values, and through this analysis, we assessed whether key pathways associated with drug mechanisms were significantly enriched among the top-ranked genes.
