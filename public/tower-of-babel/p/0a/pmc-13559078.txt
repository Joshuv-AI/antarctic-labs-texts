Biomarkers of partial remission in paediatric type 1 diabetes: β-cell stress, immune-metabolic remodelling, and translational priorities—a narrative review
Authors: Hu Lin, Wei Wu, Ke Huang, Guanping Dong, Junfen Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivePartial remission after diagnosis of paediatric type 1 diabetes (T1D) is characterised by improved glycaemic control, lower insulin requirements and residual endogenous insulin secretion, but its onset, depth and duration vary substantially between children. Standard clinical indices such as HbA1c, insulin dose-adjusted HbA1c (IDAA1c), C-peptide and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) metrics capture functional status but provide limited mechanistic information. This narrative review summarises candidate biomarkers that may explain heterogeneity in partial remission and support remission-focused translational studies.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library were searched for English-language articles published from 1 January 2000 to 31 October 2025. Priority was given to paediatric and adolescent studies, longitudinal cohorts spanning diagnosis and the first year after onset, and studies addressing partial remission, IDAA1c or early C-peptide decline. Mixed-age/adult cohorts and experimental studies were included selectively when they informed biological interpretation.Key Content and FindingsCandidate biomarkers can be organised into two intersecting axes: a β-cell axis reflecting stress, dysfunction and death, and an immune-metabolic axis reflecting immune regulation, inflammatory activity and systemic metabolic adaptation. The proinsulin-to-C-peptide ratio, unmethylated INS-derived cell-free DNA, selected circulating or extracellular vesicle-associated microRNAs, immune-cell phenotypes, soluble mediators and compact omics-derived signatures have varying levels of paediatric evidence. Introduction

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is an immune-mediated disease characterised by progressive destruction of pancreatic β cells, loss of endogenous insulin secretion, and lifelong dependence on exogenous insulin (1). Although it represents a minority of all diabetes cases, it is the predominant form of diabetes in children and adolescents, and its incidence continues to increase worldwide. In this setting, understanding early disease heterogeneity is clinically important, particularly during the period immediately following diagnosis, when residual β-cell function is still present and potentially modifiable (2).

Following diagnosis and initiation of insulin therapy, many children experience a transient period of clinical partial remission (PR), traditionally termed the “honeymoon” phase, characterised by improved glycaemic control and reduced insulin requirements. The depth and duration of PR vary considerably between individuals. Longer remission has been associated with lower HbA1c, lower insulin requirements, fewer severe hypoglycaemic events, and better preservation of endogenous insulin secretion, making PR an important clinical window in paediatric T1D (3).

Several operational definitions have been used to characterise PR in clinical and research settings. The most widely applied is the insulin dose-adjusted HbA1c (IDAA1c), defined as HbA1c (%) + 4 × total daily insulin dose (U/kg/day), with IDAA1c ≤9 commonly used to indicate remission (4). Complementary functional indices include stimulated C-peptide, urinary C-peptide-to-creatinine ratio (uCPCR), and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) metrics (5). These indices capture different but overlapping dimensions of remission: IDAA1c integrates glycaemic exposure and exogenous insulin requirement; stimulated C-peptide and uCPCR approximate residual endogenous insulin secretion; and CGM metrics, including time in range, time above range, time below range, glucose management indicator and glycaemic variability, describe real-world glycaemic stability and metabolic load (6,7). These endpoints describe residual β-cell function and real-world glycaemia but do not directly report the mechanisms that drive divergent remission trajectories.

Remission depth and duration vary markedly among children even when age at onset, autoantibody profile, human leukocyte antigen (HLA) risk and metabolic presentation are similar, implying differences in β-cell stress and death, immune activation and systemic metabolic adaptation not fully captured by functional measures alone. There is therefore a strong rationale for mechanism-based biomarkers that quantify active biology during early disease (8,9). In paediatric PR, candidate biomarkers can be broadly considered along two related axes: a β-cell axis reflecting stress, dysfunction and death, and an immune-metabolic axis reflecting immune regulation, inflammatory activity and systemic metabolic adaptation (8-11). Within this framework, molecular biomarkers should be interpreted as mechanistic layers anchored to, rather than substitutes for, IDAA1c, C-peptide and CGM metrics. Proinsulin-to-C-peptide ratio (PI:C) may reflect residual secretory stress, circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) may capture recent β-cell injury, and immune-metabolic signatures may indicate divergent inflammatory trajectories among children with otherwise similar functional remission profiles. In this review we synthesise evidence for these biomarkers in children and adolescents with new-onset T1D and outline how standardised, panel-based strategies integrated with IDAA1c, C-peptide and CGM endpoints could support risk stratification and remission-focused trials. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0469/rc).

Methods

This narrative, mechanism-oriented review was conducted to summarise biomarkers relevant to PR in paediatric T1D, rather than to provide a formal systematic review or meta-analysis. PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and the Cochrane Library were searched for English-language articles published between 1 January 2000 and 31 October 2025. The search combined terms for type 1 diabetes, children/adolescents, partial remission/honeymoon phase, IDAA1c, C-peptide, β-cell stress or death, proinsulin-to-C-peptide ratio, unmethylated INS-derived cell-free DNA, microRNAs (miRNAs), extracellular vesicles (EVs), immune phenotyping, cytokines/chemokines, proteomics, lipidomics and metabolomics, gut microbiome, microbiota, microbial metabolites, short-chain fatty acids, bile acids, tryptophan metabolism, single-cell RNA sequencing, scRNA-seq and high-dimensional cytometry. A detailed search strategy is provided as Table S1.

Studies were prioritised when they included children or adolescents, used longitudinal sampling from diagnosis through the first year after onset, or directly addressed PR, IDAA1c, stimulated C-peptide, uCPCR or early C-peptide decline. Published peer-reviewed articles were prioritised; unpublished data, conference abstracts without full text, editorials not offering substantive evidence, case reports without biomarker data and non-English articles were excluded. Mixed-age/adult cohorts and experimental studies were considered selectively when paediatric remission-specific data were limited and when they clarified biological mechanisms. Reference lists of relevant reviews, guidelines and key studies were also screened.

For eligibility and interpretation, we considered studies to be remission-relevant if they used IDAA1c ≤9, explicit insulin-dose/HbA1c-based remission criteria, stimulated or fasting C-peptide/uCPCR thresholds or trajectories, or clearly defined early post-diagnosis remission windows with functional endpoints. We did not require a single universal PR definition because the literature uses heterogeneous criteria; instead, the remission definition, sampling time and clinical anchor used in each study were extracted and considered when interpreting biomarker performance (12). Studies using CGM-derived outcomes were included when CGM metrics were used as complementary functional endpoints rather than as stand-alone mechanistic biomarker measures.

H.L. and W.W. independently screened titles, abstracts and full texts for relevance. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and, when necessary, by consultation with J.F.F. Because this was a narrative review, no formal systematic-review protocol, quantitative risk-of-bias assessment or meta-analysis was performed. The search strategy summary is shown in Table 1.

Biomarkers of PR in paediatric T1D

PR in paediatric T1D reflects a transient rebalancing of metabolic load and immune injury after diagnosis. Insulin treatment reduces glucotoxicity and β-cell workload, while early longitudinal studies also suggest a short-lived immunoregulatory window marked by relative attenuation of effector activity and greater prominence of inhibitory and regulatory pathways (10,13-15). Within this framework, biomarkers can be organised into two intersecting axes (Figure 1): a β-cell axis reporting stress, dysfunction and death, and an immune-metabolic axis capturing immune phenotypes, soluble mediators and systems-level inflammatory or metabolic endotypes (9,10,16-18). Because most candidates are sensitive to sampling conditions and assay methods, current evidence is best interpreted in longitudinal, panel-based fashion alongside functional endpoints such as IDAA1c, stimulated C-peptide or uCPCR, and CGM metrics; the main biomarker classes and paediatric evidence are summarised in Table 2. Molecular signals may relate differently to IDAA1c, C-peptide and CGM-derived endpoints. IDAA1c combines glycaemic control and insulin dose, C-peptide reflects residual β-cell secretory reserve, whereas CGM metrics capture day-to-day glucose exposure and variability and may be influenced by treatment technology, insulin adjustment and adherence (6,7).

β-cell axis biomarkers

Residual β-cell health is a central determinant of remission depth and duration. Circulating molecules released by stressed or dying β cells can provide mechanistic information that complements glycaemic indices and C-peptide (9). We focus on three circulating biomarker families with strong biological rationale and emerging paediatric evidence: PI:C as a readout of proinsulin processing/endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, unmethylated INS cfDNA as a near-real-time signal of β-cell death, and selected circulating or EV-associated miRNAs that reflect β-cell injury and islet-immune communication (9,16-19,55).

PI:C

Proinsulin is normally processed efficiently within the β-cell secretory pathway, so that only a small precursor fraction enters the circulation. The PI:C, measured under standardised fasting or stimulated conditions, provides an accessible readout of impaired proinsulin processing and β-cell secretory stress (56,57). When β cells are exposed to glucotoxicity, inflammatory cytokines and ER stress, proinsulin processing becomes less efficient, increasing the proportion of secreted proinsulin relative to C-peptide (57). This biological rationale makes PI:C one of the most readily measurable mechanistic biomarker candidates in this field.

Across the preclinical-to-clinical spectrum, PI:C tends to rise as β-cell stress increases (58). In autoantibody-positive individuals, elevated PI:C may precede overt hyperglycaemia and can improve risk stratification beyond autoantibody status in some cohorts (58). In children at diagnosis, PI:C is higher than in controls without diabetes and shows an inverse relationship with age, consistent with a more stressed β-cell state in younger children (19,59,60). During the first year after diagnosis, longitudinal studies suggest that PI:C often remains elevated or rises despite insulin therapy; higher baseline PI:C and/or rising trajectories have been associated with faster subsequent stimulated C-peptide decline and failure to achieve or maintain PR as defined by IDAA1c (19-21). These findings support PI:C as a marker of persistent β-cell stress during the remission window rather than simple recovery of β-cell function.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Absolute PI:C values may vary across assays because of differences in antibody specificity, calibration and cross-reactivity, limiting comparability across centres, an issue likely to be compounded by broader standardisation challenges affecting peptide immunoassays, including insulin- and C-peptide-based measurements (61-63). PI:C is also sensitive to physiological state, so fasting and stimulated measurements should not be interpreted interchangeably. In newly diagnosed children, puberty, acute metabolic instability and early insulin resistance can further influence values and complicate longitudinal interpretation (5,19). Taken together, these considerations suggest that PI:C is currently most useful as a mechanistically informative marker for longitudinal risk stratification during the first year after diagnosis, particularly for identifying children with a more stressed β-cell trajectory, rather than as a stand-alone criterion for PR.

Unmethylated INS-derived cfDNA

cfDNA is released from injured cells and retains tissue-informative fragmentation and methylation patterns, making it a potential short-interval marker of tissue injury (64,65). In pancreatic β cells, selected CpG sites within the insulin gene are relatively unmethylated compared with most other tissues, providing the basis for β-cell-specific cfDNA assays (66). β-cell death can therefore increase the proportion of unmethylated INS-derived cfDNA fragments in plasma, and these low-abundance signals can be detected using ddPCR- or sequencing-based approaches (22,66,67).

Available evidence suggests that unmethylated INS cfDNA is most informative around the earliest phases of disease. In high-risk or dysglycaemic individuals, increases have been reported before clinical onset and linked to declining insulin secretion (23). In cohorts with early follow-up after diagnosis, unmethylated INS cfDNA peaks around onset and falls thereafter, suggesting that the strongest death signal is concentrated in the earliest post-diagnosis period rather than across the full duration of PR (22). Together with data from transplantation settings, these findings suggest that unmethylated INS-derived cfDNA is most informative during periods of active β-cell loss rather than across the full duration of clinically defined PR (24,25).

Although biologically attractive, the translational use of unmethylated INS-derived cfDNA in paediatric PR remains constrained by both pre-analytical and analytical heterogeneity. Results can vary according to sample matrix, processing delays, freeze-thaw exposure, assay design and the CpG loci selected for analysis, while the short half-life of cfDNA means that sampling time is closely tied to recent β-cell injury (55,68). In addition, paediatric studies that follow unmethylated INS cfDNA longitudinally across clearly defined stages of PR are still limited. For now, this marker is more appropriately viewed as an adjunct measure of recent β-cell injury in intensively phenotyped cohorts, and as a possible early pharmacodynamic or trial-enrichment biomarker in immunotherapy studies, than as a routine clinical marker for remission staging.

Circulating and EV-associated miRNAs

miRNAs regulate gene expression and circulate in stable carrier forms, including EVs, Argonaute complexes and lipoproteins. Because they can reflect both tissue injury and intercellular signalling, circulating miRNAs have attracted interest as blood-based biomarkers of early T1D biology (69).

In paediatric new-onset T1D, screening studies have identified panels of serum/plasma miRNAs that discriminate cases from controls, highlighting that composite signatures may be more robust than single candidates (26,27). In TrialNet and other cohorts, specific miRNA clusters have correlated with residual insulin secretion and helped stratify subsequent C-peptide decline (28). Early after diagnosis, individual miRNAs have been associated with stimulated C-peptide at 1 year, and remission-focused profiling has reported miRNAs enriched in remitters compared with non-remitters (29). Some of these signals also intersect mechanistically with immunoregulatory pathways, including programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)- and Treg-related biology in experimental systems. EV-associated measurements may improve biological specificity further, since inflammatory stress can alter vesicular miRNA loading and release from β cells and immune cells (17,29). These observations support the idea that miRNAs capture not only β-cell injury but also communication across the islet-immune interface.

However, miRNA studies are especially vulnerable to platform differences, normalisation strategy, haemolysis, sample handling and small cohort size. Effect sizes are often modest, and external validation in paediatric remission-focused cohorts is still limited. For these reasons, circulating and EV-associated miRNAs are better regarded as exploratory but mechanistically informative components of composite biomarker panels, where they may complement stress- and injury-related markers.

Immune-metabolic axis biomarkers

Beyond β-cell-centred measures, PR is accompanied by dynamic reconfiguration of immune activation and metabolism. Longitudinal studies suggest that early after diagnosis, effector programmes and immune glycolysis may diminish, while inhibitory checkpoints and regulatory circuits become relatively more prominent (14,15,18,30). This process unfolds against a background of age-dependent immune maturation and puberty-related insulin resistance (8,70). The immune-metabolic axis therefore spans three related domains: immunophenotypic and functional markers of circulating immune cells, soluble mediators reflecting inflammatory tone and immune trafficking, and omics-level proteomic, lipidomic and metabolomic fingerprints that define inflammatory and metabolic endotypes (8,10,30,41). At the cellular level, this axis reflects a changing balance among cytotoxic effector cells, regulatory and inhibitory pathways, antigen-presenting and innate inflammatory compartments, and metabolically active lymphocytes. PR can therefore be conceptualised as a period in which reduced glucotoxicity and insulin-treated metabolic stabilisation coincide with partial attenuation of effector programmes, relative recovery of checkpoint or regulatory signalling, and changes in systemic substrate and microbial-metabolite exposure.

Immunophenotypic and functional immune cell markers

Peripheral immune phenotyping provides surrogate measures of immune activation, regulation and exhaustion-like programmes that may influence the rate of β-cell loss during early T1D (70,71). Candidate features include expression of inhibitory and co-stimulatory receptors, regulatory T-cell states, helper phenotypes involved in B-cell help, and functional readouts such as antigen-responsive activity or immune-cell glucose uptake (31,70,71). These measures are attractive because they move beyond static immune cell counts and instead reflect the balance between effector pressure, regulatory restraint and immunometabolic demand during the remission window.

Paediatric studies around diagnosis suggest that regulatory circuitry is remodelled as PR begins. Detailed analyses of forkhead box P3 (FOXP3)-positive regulatory T cells have shown both quantitative and qualitative changes at onset (31). Longitudinal work has reported that recovery of inhibitory checkpoint signalling, including the PD-1/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) axis, may track entry into remission and is associated with better preservation of stimulated C-peptide (32). A regulatory-effector balance involving T-cell immunoreceptor with immunoglobulin and ITIM domains (TIGIT)-positive Tregs and countervailing effector programmes has also been linked to remission quality (15). Functional immune-metabolic measures provide similar signals. Higher glucose uptake and glycolytic activity in circulating T cells have been associated with faster β-cell decline, whereas remission has been associated with partial normalisation of T-cell metabolic activity (14,33). These findings suggest that remission-associated immune phenotypes are not limited to changes in cell frequency, but involve coordinated shifts in regulatory T cells, CD8+ effector programmes, checkpoint expression and T-cell metabolic activity (30-33). Helper T-cell phenotypes involved in B-cell help, dendritic-cell subsets and mucosal-associated invariant T cells may further connect adaptive, innate and mucosal immune signals, although paediatric PR-focused longitudinal validation remains limited (49-51). High-dimensional cytometry and single-cell transcriptomic approaches may help resolve these coordinated immune states in future studies (42,72).

These markers are biologically informative, but several factors currently limit broader translation. High-parameter flow cytometry and related functional assays require specialised infrastructure, harmonised panels and rigorous quality control, while results can be influenced by age, puberty, acute metabolic decompensation, intercurrent infection and concomitant treatment. Published paediatric studies are also often small and derived from specialised centres, with heterogeneous definitions of remission and limited external validation (70,72). Accordingly, immune phenotyping is currently best suited to mechanistic endotyping and to baseline enrichment and pharmacodynamic monitoring in remission-focused trials rather than routine clinical staging.

Soluble immune mediators

Soluble immune mediators are comparatively accessible biomarkers that can reflect inflammatory tone, immune-cell trafficking and activation during early T1D. Among the most studied is the C-X-C chemokine receptor type 3 (CXCR3) chemokine axis: C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10) is produced by stressed β cells and immune cells and can promote recruitment of CXCR3-positive effector T cells, providing a plausible link between islet inflammation and circulating signals (18,73). More broadly, circulating cytokines, chemokines and soluble receptors offer a peripheral snapshot of T-cell activation, monocyte-macrophage activation and tissue stress during the early post-diagnosis period (34-38).

In children with new-onset T1D, CXCL10 is often elevated near diagnosis and tends to decline during follow-up, broadly paralleling the clinical course into and out of PR (18). Other activation markers, including soluble interleukin-2 receptor alpha (sIL-2Rα) and mediators linked to innate or macrophage activation, have also been associated with poorer β-cell function or glycaemic outcomes (34). Several cohorts further suggest that children who later enter remission have a less inflammatory baseline mediator profile than non-remitters, and ex vivo cytokine-response studies indicate that lower cytokine-responder states may be associated with longer remission (39,40). These findings support the view that soluble mediators may be informative not because a single analyte defines remission, but because the broader inflammatory set point at diagnosis and early follow-up may influence remission depth and stability. Mechanistically, soluble mediators may connect β-cell stress with immune-cell recruitment and activation. CXCL10 reflects an islet-inflammation-related chemokine axis, whereas interleukin (IL)-1-, tumour necrosis factor (TNF)- and interferon (IFN)-related pathways may amplify β-cell stress, chemokine production and antigen presentation (18,35,37,39,40,73,74). Counter-regulatory mediators such as interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1ra) or IL-10 and soluble activation markers such as sIL-2Rα should therefore be interpreted as components of inflammatory networks rather than as single-analyte remission classifiers (34,36,39,40).

Despite biological plausibility, soluble mediators are influenced by age, puberty, infection, ketoacidosis and acute metabolic instability; single time-point measurements can be noisy; and results vary across platforms and sampling schedules (18,35). Therefore, no single cytokine/chemokine has sufficient specificity or stability to define PR or support clinical prediction of PR in routine care. The most defensible approach is to use small, prespecified mediator panels integrated with β-cell biomarkers and functional endpoints within standardised longitudinal designs (8,75).

Omics-level immunometabolic fingerprints

Multi-omics profiling can capture systems-level immunometabolic remodelling during early T1D, integrating coordinated changes in proteins, lipids and small molecules that reflect inflammation, oxidative stress and substrate handling (10,11). Such fingerprints can delineate endotypes not apparent from single-analyte measures and may map onto remission depth and stability (41,43,44). Compared with single biomarkers, omics-level data can connect several biological layers, including complement and coagulation activity, lipid handling, oxidative stress, growth factor signalling and amino-acid metabolism (10,11,41,43,44,76). These layers may help distinguish remission-prone from remission-poor endotypes by linking early metabolic stabilisation with persistence or resolution of inflammatory programmes (41,43,44). Emerging single-cell and high-dimensional immune-profiling approaches may add cellular resolution to these signatures, but paediatric datasets combining such methods with clearly defined PR stages, C-peptide and CGM endpoints remain scarce (42).

In paediatric new-onset cohorts, plasma proteomics at diagnosis has reported protein panels that differ between children who later meet IDAA1c-based criteria for PR and those who do not (41). Longitudinal proteomic studies through the first year have linked pathway-level features to C-peptide trajectories, while lipidomic profiling early after diagnosis has associated specific lipid patterns with subsequent decline in β-cell function (43,44). Proteomic, lipidomic and metabolomic features appear to provide complementary information for explaining early functional trajectories (10,41,43,44). Taken together, these studies suggest that a remission-prone or remission-poor systems-level signature may be detectable close to diagnosis, even if its individual components are not yet stable enough for direct clinical deployment.

Clinical translation is constrained by pre-analytical sensitivity, high dimensionality relative to cohort size and limited cross-cohort replication (76). To move from discovery to practice, signatures must be reduced to parsimonious, assayable panels and validated prospectively in multicentre paediatric cohorts using harmonised sampling schedules and endpoints. For now, omics fingerprints are best positioned for endotype discovery and as mechanistic adjuncts in remission-focused studies.

Gut microbiome and immune-metabolic regulation

The gut microbiome is an additional regulator of paediatric immune-metabolic biology that may influence remission-related pathways indirectly through mucosal immunity, microbial metabolites and systemic inflammation. Longitudinal birth-cohort studies have linked early gut microbial development with islet autoimmunity and early-onset T1D, while paediatric new-onset T1D studies have reported altered microbial functional capacity, including changes in short-chain fatty acid production, lipopolysaccharide-related pathways and bile acid metabolism (45,46). These findings suggest that microbiome-related signals may modify the inflammatory and metabolic context in which PR develops, although direct longitudinal evidence across clearly defined paediatric PR stages remains limited.

Microbial metabolites provide a plausible bridge between gut ecology and the immune-metabolic axis. Short-chain fatty acids, bile-acid derivatives and tryptophan-related metabolites can influence epithelial barrier integrity, Treg differentiation, antigen-presenting cell function, inflammatory tone and insulin sensitivity (47). In human T1D, metabolite-based dietary supplementation has been associated with changes in gut microbiota composition, increased short-chain fatty acids and immune modulation, but available interventional data are not specific to paediatric PR (48). Therefore, gut microbiome-related measures should currently be viewed as exploratory modifiers of remission biology rather than validated PR biomarkers. Future paediatric studies should consider paired stool, blood and functional endpoint sampling to test whether microbial composition or metabolite profiles add information beyond IDAA1c, C-peptide, CGM and circulating immune-metabolic markers.

Cross-talk between β-cell stress and immune-metabolic remodelling

The β-cell and immune-metabolic axes are biologically interdependent rather than parallel pathways. β-cell stress can increase antigen availability, chemokine production, EV release and cell-death-associated signals, thereby providing substrates for immune-cell recruitment, antigen presentation and inflammatory amplification (9,17,18,22,66,67,73). Conversely, immune-derived mediators such as IFN-related signals, IL-1- and TNF-associated pathways, cytotoxic effector activity and T helper 17 (Th17)-related responses can aggravate β-cell ER stress, impair proinsulin processing and promote further β-cell dysfunction or death (35,37,52,53,57,73). Thus, PI:C, unmethylated INS-derived cfDNA, EV-associated miRNAs, immune-cell phenotypes and soluble mediators should be interpreted as partially connected readouts of a shared injury-inflammation circuit rather than as isolated biomarker classes.

This cross-talk also provides a mechanistic explanation for heterogeneity in PR. Insulin treatment and improved glycaemia may reduce glucotoxicity and β-cell workload, while relative recovery of checkpoint or regulatory pathways may attenuate effector pressure on residual β cells (10,13-15,32). Children in whom these processes occur in parallel may show more stable C-peptide trajectories and deeper functional remission, whereas persistence of β-cell stress, recent β-cell injury or inflammatory immune-metabolic activity may be associated with shorter or less stable remission despite similar IDAA1c or CGM profiles. From a translational perspective, this supports panel-based strategies that combine one β-cell stress or death marker with selected immune-metabolic markers and functional endpoints, rather than biomarker strategies organised around a single pathway alone.

Emerging candidate biomarkers

Several additional biomarker domains may further refine the biology of PR, although paediatric evidence remains limited. These include islet-derived EV PD-L1, mucosal-associated invariant T-cell and dendritic-cell subsets, platelet-neutrophil aggregates, and Th17-related responses (49,50,52-54). Together, they point to more specific dimensions of remission biology, including β-cell-to-immune communication, mucosal-systemic immune crosstalk, thrombo-inflammatory activation and inflammatory endotypes.

Experimental and early clinical studies suggest that these signals may carry mechanistic relevance. For example, EV-associated PD-L1 has been linked to immune restraint, whereas platelet-neutrophil aggregates and Th17-related activity may reflect inflammatory pathways that accompany early disease progression (52-54). However, paediatric studies focused specifically on PR are sparse, and most available data are cross-sectional, derived from mixed-age cohorts, or based on experimental models rather than longitudinal remission phenotyping. At present, these markers are best regarded as exploratory tools for mechanistic substudies and hypothesis generation, rather than near-term candidates for routine clinical implementation.

Clinical translation and research priorities

In children and adolescents, stimulated C-peptide and IDAA1c remain the clinical foundation for defining and monitoring PR, with uCPCR and CGM-derived indices providing complementary information in selected settings (4,5). Mechanistic biomarkers are most useful when interpreted within this functional framework, because remission reflects overlapping processes including relief of β-cell stress, fluctuating β-cell injury and dynamic immune-metabolic adaptation. This argues against relying on any single biomarker. In an integrated framework, IDAA1c reflects combined glycaemic control and exogenous insulin requirement, C-peptide or uCPCR reflects residual β-cell secretory capacity, and CGM captures the realised glycaemic phenotype. Molecular biomarkers may add mechanistic context when these clinical anchors are discordant, such as persistent secretory stress, recent β-cell injury or immune-metabolic profiles associated with future C-peptide decline.

Age-specific factors in paediatric PR

Across all biomarker classes, interpretation in children should explicitly account for paediatric modifiers, including age at onset, sex, pubertal stage, body mass index z-score, diabetic ketoacidosis or acute metabolic instability at presentation, baseline HbA1c, time from diagnosis to sampling, insulin regimen and technology use, intercurrent infection, autoantibody profile, HLA risk, ethnicity/geography and assay platform. These factors should be prespecified as covariates or stratification variables in biomarker studies and remission-focused trials.

Paediatric PR also differs from adult-onset T1D in clinically relevant ways. Younger children often show more rapid early β-cell functional decline and shorter or less stable remission, whereas adult-onset autoimmune diabetes is more heterogeneous, with slower β-cell loss in some individuals and possible overlap with latent autoimmune diabetes in adults (77,78). Puberty-related insulin resistance, growth, immune maturation, family-supported diabetes management and differential access to CGM or pump therapy may further modify IDAA1c, C-peptide trajectories, CGM profiles and circulating immune-metabolic signals in children. Therefore, biomarker thresholds or trajectories derived from adult or mixed-age cohorts should be regarded as hypothesis-generating until validated in paediatric cohorts. Future studies should report age strata, pubertal status and treatment-technology exposure when assessing remission-related biomarkers.

Translational hierarchy and validation priorities

A realistic goal is to develop compact panels that combine complementary markers with analytically feasible assays (8,75). In practical terms, a paediatric panel might include one marker of β-cell stress, one marker of recent β-cell injury and a limited immune-metabolic component, all interpreted alongside functional endpoints. Such panels are not intended to replace existing clinical measures, but to explain heterogeneity within clinically similar remission states and to identify children at greater risk of early β-cell decline. Panel design should be matched to use-case, with lower-burden panels for routine follow-up and broader panels reserved for remission-focused trials (8).

A pragmatic translational hierarchy is therefore useful: PI:C and selected functional endpoints are the most feasible near-term components of low-burden follow-up panels; unmethylated INS cfDNA and immune-metabolic readouts are better positioned for trial enrichment and pharmacodynamic monitoring; and omics-derived signatures, EV-PD-L1, mucosal-associated invariant T cell (MAIT)/dendritic cell subsets, platelet-neutrophil aggregates and Th17/IL-17 measures should remain exploratory until prospectively validated.

Translation nevertheless remains limited by inconsistent remission definitions, small paediatric cohorts, short follow-up and marked methodological heterogeneity (8). Heterogeneity in PR definitions also affects biomarker interpretation. IDAA1c-based definitions, C-peptide-based definitions and CGM-anchored outcomes capture partly overlapping but non-identical remission constructs, so biomarkers associated with remission under one definition may not show the same performance under another (6,7,12). Sample type, timing, pre-analytical handling and assay platform all influence biomarker performance, while external validation remains sparse, particularly for omics-derived signatures (55,61,72,76). The immediate priority is therefore not to identify more isolated biomarkers, but to test whether parsimonious panels add useful information beyond age, pubertal status, baseline C-peptide and routine metabolic measures. Future studies should therefore prespecify the PR definition and report IDAA1c, insulin dose, C-peptide/uCPCR and CGM endpoints in parallel whenever feasible. This will require multicentre prospective paediatric cohorts with harmonised sampling, shared analytical standards and embedded biobanking, together with biomarker-rich sampling in stage 2 and early new-onset studies (8,75). Progress is likely to depend less on further biomarker discovery than on standardising, reducing and prospectively validating the signals already available.

Strengths and limitations of this review

This review has several strengths. It focuses specifically on paediatric PR, links biomarker classes to biologically plausible β-cell and immune-metabolic axes, and prioritises longitudinal evidence from diagnosis and the first post-diagnosis year. It also distinguishes biomarkers that are close to translational use from those that remain exploratory, which is important for designing remission-focused clinical studies.

The review also has limitations. It was designed as a narrative synthesis and therefore did not use a formal systematic-review protocol, quantitative evidence grading, risk-of-bias assessment or meta-analysis. The restriction to English-language articles may have excluded relevant studies, and some mechanistic interpretations rely on mixed-age cohorts or experimental studies because paediatric remission-specific longitudinal data are limited. In addition, because published studies used different PR definitions and clinical anchors, biomarker associations were interpreted qualitatively rather than pooled across IDAA1c-, C-peptide- or CGM-based outcomes. The conclusions should therefore be interpreted as a framework for hypothesis generation and trial design rather than as clinical biomarker recommendations.

Conclusions

PR in paediatric T1D is clinically important but biologically heterogeneous. Biomarkers of β-cell stress and death, together with markers of immune-metabolic remodelling, may help explain heterogeneity beyond HbA1c, insulin dose and C-peptide, but no single marker is ready for routine clinical use. The immediate priority is not to identify more isolated candidates, but to validate compact, standardised biomarker panels interpreted alongside functional endpoints such as C-peptide, IDAA1c and CGM metrics in prospective paediatric cohorts and remission-focused trials.
