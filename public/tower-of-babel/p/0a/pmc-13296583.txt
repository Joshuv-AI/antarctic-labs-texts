Managing Pompe Disease and Enzyme Replacement Therapy During Pregnancy: Challenges and Considerations
Authors: Maudy T. M. Theunissen, Zohra Hayat, Françoise Bouhour, Johanna M. P. van den Hout, Jean‐Baptiste Noury, Emmeline Lagrange, Aurélie Jouy, Nadjib Taouagh, Pieter A. van Doorn, Ans T. van der Ploeg, Pascal Laforêt, Nadine A. M. E. van der Beek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThere are no clear guidelines for enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) use and obstetric management in pregnant patients with Pompe disease receiving ERT. This study aims to explore outcomes and challenges for these patients.MethodsWe included French and Dutch patients who became pregnant during ERT. Main outcomes were pregnancy (ERT management, obstetric delivery, neonatal outcomes) and clinical results (6‐min walk test [6MWT], forced vital capacity [FVC] in supine and upright positions, infusion associated reactions [IARs]).ResultsData from 11 patients (median age at pregnancy onset 33 years) with 14 pregnancies were studied. One pregnancy ended in early miscarriage, and 12/13 live births reached term. Eight pregnancies had an induced birth or planned C‐section, 6/8 due to maternal condition (e.g., increased dyspnea or preference medical team to ensure patient's safety). ERT was continued in eight pregnancies, with three patients receiving an adjusted dose based on their increased weight. ERT was interrupted early in five pregnancies because of trial participation or safety concerns. No IARs were reported during pregnancy or after restart of ERT. Of the patients with clinical data during pregnancy, a (mild) decline was observed in 6/7 pregnancies for 6MWT, 3/10 for upright FVC, and 6/9 for supine FVC. Most patients recovered from their decline during pregnancy within 2 years.ConclusionIn patients who continued ERT during pregnancy, it appeared to be safe, with no documented adverse effects for mother or child. While most patients experienced a mild worsening of clinical condition during pregnancy, the majority recovered well postpartum.

Introduction

Pompe disease is a rare, autosomal recessive, glycogen storage disorder caused by the deficiency of the lysosomal enzyme acid alpha‐glucosidase [1]. The reduction or complete absence of this enzyme leads to accumulation of glycogen in the lysosomes, mainly manifesting in the muscles. The clinical presentation of Pompe disease varies widely, ranging from a rapidly fatal classic‐infantile phenotype to the more slowly progressive late‐onset phenotypes, and is mainly dependent on the level of residual activity of acid alpha‐glucosidase. The late‐onset form is characterized by weakness of the limb‐girdle, paraspinal and respiratory muscles, usually without cardiac involvement [1, 2, 3].

For over 20 years, enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) with recombinant human acid alpha‐glucosidase (rhGAA) has been available as a disease‐specific treatment. ERT has been demonstrated to slow down disease progression by improving skeletal muscle strength, pulmonary function, and the ability to perform daily‐life activities in adult patients with late‐onset Pompe disease [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Overall, ERT contributes to a better quality of life and increased life expectancy [12, 13]. Previously, only ERT with alglucosidase alfa was available. However, two next‐generation ERTs have now been approved by the regulatory agencies: avalglucosidase alfa in 2022 and cipaglucosidase alfa combined with miglustat in 2023 [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19].

Pregnancies in woman with Pompe disease should be considered as high‐risk, as the pregnancy can be a metabolic trigger [20] and worsen the muscle weakness and/or respiratory function of the mother [20, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27]. This can lead to maternal exhaustion which forms an acute risk to both the mother and the fetus [20, 21, 25, 28]. In some lysosomal storage diseases, such as Gaucher disease, continuing ERT is recommended to prevent complications (e.g., anemia and thrombocytopenia), ensuring that the patient is in optimum condition at the time of conception and delivery [29, 30]. Information on continuing ERT in Pompe disease during pregnancy and lactation is limited, with previous literature showing varied management approaches including interruption, continuation at the same dose, or continuation with an adjusted dose [22, 23, 24, 25, 26, 31, 32]. Currently, there are no clear guidelines regarding the use of ERT and the selection of different types of ERT for Pompe disease during pregnancy. The aim of this study is to explore the outcomes and challenges associated with pregnancy and delivery in a cohort of Dutch and French patients with Pompe disease, in order to contribute to the development of guidelines for ERT use and obstetric management in pregnant women with Pompe disease.

Methods

Patient and Study Design

The study included all women with Pompe disease who became pregnant while receiving ERT in the Netherlands or France. Pregnancies that occurred when patients were not on ERT at the time of conception were excluded. The diagnosis of Pompe disease was enzymatically and genetically confirmed in all patients. Patients could receive ERT at conception either as part of standard care (since its approval in 2006) or through participation in a clinical trial. If a patient was pregnant during standard care but switched to a clinical trial at a later stage, only data gathered before the switch were included.

Dutch patients were treated for Pompe disease under the coordination of Erasmus MC University Medical Center, where the data were also collected. French patients received treatment at various hospitals throughout the country and data were gathered through the French national Pompe registry. The current study is part of a larger follow‐up study on the natural disease progression and effects of ERT for Pompe disease at the Center for Lysosomal and Metabolic Diseases, Erasmus MC University Medical Center Rotterdam in the Netherlands and the 45 centers treating patients with Pompe disease in France, for which all patients provided written consent. The available data were gathered retrospectively between August 1, 2024 and January 31, 2025.

General Patient Data

We collected general patient data (date of birth, GAA genotype, presence or absence of the c.510C>T genetic modifier [Dutch patients only], year of symptom onset, year of diagnosis, wheelchair and ventilation use) as well as information on ERT (date of initiation of ERT, type of ERT, peak anti‐rhGAA antibody titres).

Pregnancy Outcomes

Data on the pregnancies were obtained from patients' medical records and were verified and supplemented through interviews and questionnaires with the patients, or through contact with their treating physicians. For each pregnancy, information was collected on conception, prenatal testing, type and management of ERT during pregnancy and birth, antenatal check‐ups, obstetric delivery, gestational age (GA), neonatal outcomes, and breastfeeding. Additionally, notable events during pregnancy or birth were obtained, including any occurrences that the clinician deemed important to note (e.g., hospital admissions or complications). In a consensus meeting, MT, ZH and NB determined whether each event was (potentially) related to Pompe Disease.

Clinical Outcomes

The 6‐min walk test (6MWT) was used to quantify the distance a person is able to walk in 6 min at their fastest pace [33, 34]. The results are expressed as a percentage of the predicted normal values, dependent on age, sex, height, and weight [33, 34]. Additionally, the use of a wheelchair was reported.

Pulmonary function was evaluated by measuring forced vital capacity (FVC) in both upright and supine positions using spirometry, following ATS/ERS standards [35]. The results are expressed as a percentage of the predicted normal value. A value of < 80% of the predicted normal was considered abnormal [35, 36]. Furthermore, the use of ventilation was reported.

Infusion Associated Reactions

An infusion associated reaction (IAR) was defined as an adverse reaction occurring during or shortly after ERT administration [37]. The number of IARs and associated symptoms were analyzed. Only adverse events that were clinically assessed as having a probable or definite relationship with the infusion, were analyzed in this study.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analysis was performed using IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS; version 28.0.1.0 (142)) and R (version 2023.12.0 + 369). Numerical data are presented as medians with interquartile ranges, whereas categorical data are shown as absolute numbers and percentages.

The clinical outcomes were analyzed at the individual level. The clinical course during pregnancy for each patient was assessed by MT, ZH, and NB. The disease course in the first years after pregnancy was also evaluated—if available. Due to the small number of patients and pregnancies, we did not conduct formal statistical analyses.

Results

Patient Characteristics

This study included 11 women with Pompe disease from France (n = 5) and the Netherlands (n = 6) (Table 1). The median age at the onset of symptoms of Pompe disease was 17 years (IQR 13–21, range 6–23) and the median disease duration when starting ERT was 11.2 years (IQR 5.8–15.3, range 1–18). The common splice site variant c.‐32‐13T>G (IVS1) was present in all patients except for patients 3 and 6 (Table 2). Additionally, one of the Dutch patients had the genetic modifier c.510C>T on the IVS1 allele, known to contribute to earlier onset of symptoms and more severe disease course [38] (patient 11, disease manifestation at the age of 6).

Pregnancies

Eleven patients were included, with 14 pregnancies while receiving ERT. One pregnancy ended in a miscarriage at 5–6/7 weeks' GA (patient 6 [pregnancy 2]). The cause of the miscarriage was unknown and no further investigation was conducted. All pregnancies were conceived spontaneously without medical assistance. The median maternal age at the start of pregnancy was 33 years (IQR 28–38, range 20–40) and the median time from the start of ERT to these pregnancies on ERT was 5.9 years (IQR 2.7–9.5, range −0.1 to 16.9). Table 3 provides an overview of the pregnancies ending in live birth. One patient (patient 4) started ERT during pregnancy, which was initially unrecognized and was only confirmed at 4 months. One reported notable event was considered related to Pompe disease: patient 10 was hospitalized for increased symptoms of dyspnea and exhaustion.

Antenatal Care

Antenatal care was provided in a nonacademic hospital (n = 5), an academic hospital (n = 5) or a combination of both (n = 3). No pregnancies received antenatal care solely by a midwife.

Prenatal Risk Assessment

Partner testing for Pompe disease was conducted for nine patients. All tested partners were confirmed to be non‐carriers. Of the two patients without partner testing, no prenatal genetic testing for Pompe disease was performed and one patient's newborn underwent testing for Pompe disease postpartum.

Birth

Terms

Thirteen pregnancies ended in live births; there were no stillbirths. Table 3 provides an overview of several delivery characteristics. Six babies were born at early term (considered between 37‐0/7 and 38‐6/7 weeks' GA) and one baby was born preterm (34‐1/7 weeks' GA) [41].

Mode of Delivery

Three pregnancies resulted in spontaneous natural deliveries (Table 3). As far as known, at least one patient received an oxytocin injection to support uterine contraction and ensure proper detachment of the placenta. Four women had medically induced vaginal births. One induction was performed for social/logistic reasons, while the other three were due to the maternal condition. In these cases, the pregnancies had become too physically demanding—for example, progressive respiratory discomfort—or the medical team opted for assisted delivery to ensure maternal safety. The three inductions due to maternal condition all ended in a vacuum extraction due to maternal exhaustion.

In the four patients who had a planned cesarean section (C‐section), the primary reason (n = 3) was the obstetric condition (pregnancy became too demanding or preference of the medical team for patient safety). The fourth planned C‐section was performed due to fetal macrosomia. Postoperatively, one of these patients was admitted to a high care unit as a precaution to ensure adequate (respiratory) monitoring (patient 7), two were admitted to a regular ward, and the postnatal care level of one patient was unknown. Two patients required an emergency C‐section—one due to inadequate labor progression despite two rounds of labor induction, and the other for unknown reasons, which could potentially be linked to Pompe disease.

An overview of all noteworthy events and their potential relationship with Pompe disease are shown in Table 3.

Location of Birth

Eight deliveries took place in an academic hospital and four in a nonacademic hospital. Additionally, one baby was born in a private clinic. None of the patients gave birth at home.

Neonatal Outcomes

Based on the available information, 10 babies had a good start with normal Apgar scores (scores between 7 and 10) [42] and development was documented as normal (Table 3). However, noteworthy conditions were reported in three babies. One baby had initially good APGAR scores at 1 and 5 min but developed shortness of breath due to a wet lung. This baby was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit for 5 days for oxygen support before being transferred to a regular hospital ward. Another baby had normal Apgar scores, but required weaning from opioid withdrawal treatment at birth with ICU follow‐up, since the mother was being treated with morphine for pain during the pregnancy. A third baby was born late preterm at 34‐1/7 weeks’ GA and spent 2 weeks in neonatal care due to this prematurity, but showed normal development thereafter.

ERT

At the time of conception, eight patients were being treated with alglucosidase alfa (20 mg/kg/every other week (eow)) and three with avalglucosidase alfa (20 mg/kg/eow or 10 mg/kg/eow, with the 10 mg/kg dose administered because of trial participation) (Table 2). No patients had experienced any IARs before pregnancy.

Continuation of ERT

ERT was continued in eight pregnancies (seven patients, of whom six treated with alglucosidase alfa and one with avalglucosidase alfa) that resulted in live births (Table 2). No IARs were reported during pregnancy. During five pregnancies, the dose of ERT was kept the same based on the patients' weight before pregnancy, while during three, the dose of ERT was adjusted during pregnancy based on their new weight.

Interruption of ERT

ERT was interrupted during five pregnancies (four patients) (Table 2), in all patients before 5 weeks' GA: in three this was due to clinical trial participation (avalglucosidase alfa) and in two due to safety concerns about alglucosidase alfa during pregnancy, discussed jointly between the doctor and the patient. After four out of five pregnancies, ERT was restarted at the same dose as before pregnancy. One patient specifically opted for a slight delay in restarting treatment to accommodate her wish to breastfeed. No IARs were reported after restarting ERT.

In one patient, ERT was discontinued only around delivery—2 and 6 weeks prior—in both pregnancies, at her specific request. She resumed ERT 2 months after her first pregnancy but waited 3 years after her second, restarting only when she experienced disease progression.

Breastfeeding

Six patients (seven pregnancies) breastfed, four of whom were receiving alglucosidase alfa treatment at that time (Table 2). Three of these patients were advised to avoid breastfeeding for 24 h after ERT, while one was initially informed about the 24‐h restriction, but was later told it was not necessary. For at least one of the patients who interrupted ERT treatment, breastfeeding was the main reason to delay ERT restart.

Clinical Outcomes

Individual Level

6MWT data during pregnancy were available for six patients (seven pregnancies) (Figure 1 and Table 2): one patient remained stable during pregnancy—while continuing ERT at weight‐adjusted dose—and a mild decline was observed in five patients (six pregnancies). In 4/6 pregnancies with a decline, ERT was interrupted before 5 weeks and in 2/6 pregnancies, ERT was continued at the same dose during pregnancy. Based on available data (five pregnancies), all patients recovered to pre‐pregnancy levels within 1 or 2 years.

Based on the available upright FVC data during pregnancy (eight patients, 10 pregnancies), four patients (5 pregnancies) had stable courses throughout pregnancy, and three patients showed a decline, all of whom recovered within 1 or 2 years. ERT management during pregnancy varied among both stable and declined patients. Interestingly, two patients showed improvements in upright FVC during pregnancy, while no changes in ERT regimen were made.

Data on supine FVC during pregnancy were available for seven patients (nine pregnancies). A decline was observed in five patients (6 pregnancies), all of whom continued ERT during pregnancy—either at the same or an adjusted dose. After 5/6 pregnancies, patients recovered to pre‐pregnancy levels within one or 2 years, whereas in 1/6 patients (with more than 10 years of follow‐up after pregnancy), the decline persisted postpartum. During the three pregnancies (two patients) with stable supine FVC, ERT was interrupted. One of these patients—who decided not to immediately restart ERT after delivery—experienced a postpartum decline that coincided with the ongoing ERT interruption.

No clear differences in clinical course during pregnancy or recovery time were seen between patients receiving alglucosidase alfa and those receiving avalglucosidase alfa. For the one patient who continued avalglucosidase during pregnancy, unfortunately no clinical outcome data were available for the pregnancy or postpartum period.

Discussion

This study carefully mapped pregnancy‐related outcomes and challenges in patients with Pompe disease receiving ERT in the Netherlands and France. By combining data from two countries, we aim to provide valuable insights into pregnancy care and are applicable to clinical practice in different countries, contributing to the development of guidelines for ERT administration and obstetric management in this population. In about half of the cases, delivery was medically planned due to (expected) decline in maternal condition, either through induction of labor or scheduled C‐section. ERT was continued in just over half of the pregnancies, with no IARs reported during pregnancy. While most patients experienced a mild worsening of clinical condition during pregnancy, all but one patient returned to pre‐pregnancy clinical levels within 2 years postpartum. Dose adjustments did not prevent the prepartum decline.

The guidelines of the European POmpe Consortium (EPOC), which includes the main centers in this study, suggest that continuation of ERT can be considered during pregnancy; however, the recommendations do not distinguish between specific types of ERT [43]. According to the product characteristics, alglucosidase alfa and avalglucosidase alfa can be used during pregnancy when the potential benefits to the mother outweigh the potential risks [44, 45]. The use of miglustat, which is recommended to be combined with cipaglucosidase alfa, appears to be inadvisable during pregnancy, as animal studies have demonstrated reproductive toxicity of miglustat [46]. Consequently, the decision to continue ERT during pregnancy should be made by the patient and their treating physician. In our study, no indications for safety concerns were observed for alglucosidase alfa or avalglucosidase alfa during pregnancy, and no IARs occurred. These findings suggest that both treatments can be safely used during pregnancy without evident adverse effects.

Compared to the general population without neuromuscular disorders (NMDs), the observed miscarriage rate in our cohort was similar to that reported in a large study of all pregnancies in Norway (7.1% vs. 12.8%) [47]. Additionally, the majority of live births in our study occurred at term (92%), which aligns with European birth statistics (92.1% in France and 92.3% in the Netherlands) [48]. One patient delivered preterm (8%), which is also comparable to the national averages in the Netherlands (6.6%) and France (6.9%) [48].

In our cohort, six patients (46%) underwent C‐sections and three (23%) had instrumental vaginal deliveries (vacuum extractions). These rates are higher than those observed in the general population (France: instrumental vaginal delivery 12.3% and C‐section 20.9%; the Netherlands: instrumental vaginal delivery 7.3% and C‐section 17.4%) [48]. However, assisted deliveries are commonly reported in NMDs as a precaution to ensure maternal and fetal safety [20], since these conditions—often with impairment of the muscles involved in labor and delivery—can cause complications, including weak uterine contractions and prolonged labor, increasing the risk of exhaustion in the mother and fetal distress. Therefore, discussing the possibility of an assisted delivery early in pregnancy is crucial to manage expectations and prevent maternal exhaustion. Also, pregnancy in itself in Pompe disease is considered high‐risk—for example, because of impaired respiratory function— [20], making thorough antenatal care crucial as well. A previous study on pregnant women with NMDs reported that only 35% had discussed pregnancy with their neuromuscular specialist, highlighting the need for clinicians to adopt a more proactive approach on pregnancy planning. Early and structured counseling would create better opportunities for implementing the multidisciplinary approach recommended by the European Neuromuscular Centre (ENMC) to ensure health and safety of both mother and child [49]. Such a multidisciplinary approach is especially important as pregnancy can exacerbate muscular and respiratory symptoms in Pompe disease, as seen in other NMDs [20, 21, 26]. Also in our study, no patients were exclusively managed by midwives and none had home births, which is expected in France given home births are not common, but contrasts with the general Dutch population, where 14% of births occur at home [50]—a difference that likely reflects the higher‐risk nature of these pregnancies.

In this current study, the advice provided regarding breastfeeding during ERT treatment varied—with some advising to avoid breastfeeding for 24 h after ERT and others not advising this restriction, and for some patients in this cohort, it is unclear whether the given advice influenced their decision to breastfeed. It was previously proposed that breastfeeding was safe during alglucosidase alfa treatment [23, 44], and the EPOC guidelines suggest that continuation of ERT can be considered during lactation [43]. However, as a precautionary measure, it was recommended to avoid breastfeeding for the first 24 h after the admission of the ERT infusion, as very low levels of alglucosidase alfa have been detected in breast milk during this period [23, 44]. This current study further suggests that breastfeeding during alglucosidase alfa treatment is safe, although the 24‐h precaution may be overly cautious because it is considered unlikely to have any clinical effects on the breastfed infant [23]. There is currently no available data on breastfeeding during treatment with avalglucosidase alfa [45]. In our cohort, the three patients on this ERT did not breastfeed, or only did so for a short period while still off ERT, so unfortunately, we were unable to gather additional data on this. Additionally, at this moment, it seems unadvisable to use miglustat, which is recommended to be combined with cipaglucosidase alfa, during lactation, as animal studies have demonstrated secretion of miglustat in breast milk [46].

Among patients with available clinical data during pregnancy, we observed a decline in a substantial number of patients for either 6MWT or FVC. When interpreting these findings, it is important to consider pre‐pregnancy disease progression, as the observed decline may reflect the natural course of Pompe disease. Additionally, pregnancy itself can also impact walking ability and lung function in the general population due to the growing uterus and associated physical changes [51]—which may be worsened by Pompe disease [50, 51]. Nonetheless, the decline during pregnancy underscores the importance of closely monitoring pregnant patients with Pompe disease. Most patients (all but one) recovered to their pre‐pregnancy levels within 1 or 2 years, indicating that the decline is not necessarily irreversible. These findings should be interpreted with caution given the relatively small sample size—although patients with a broad disease spectrum from two countries were included—as well as the limited availability of clinical data around birth, both of which are inherent to a retrospective study in a rare disease.

A decline in 6MWT or FVC during pregnancy was observed in some patients across all three groups: those with unchanged, adjusted or interrupted ERT. This may suggest that adjusting ERT dose (e.g., to a higher dose) during pregnancy does not prevent clinical decline. This finding aligns with observations in Gaucher disease, where ERT is typically administered in an unchanged dose based on pre‐pregnancy weight [30]. Additionally, recovery to pre‐pregnancy clinical levels postpartum was also observed in patients who temporarily discontinued ERT during pregnancy, indicating that interrupting ERT does not result in worse recovery or irreversible decline.

Taken together, we recommend a proactive approach to pregnancy planning in patients with Pompe disease, including multidisciplinary decision‐making to evaluate the need for additional support during pregnancy and to determine whether delivery should be medically planned. As no adverse effects were observed in our study in mothers or infants when ERT was continued and dose adjustments did not prevent clinical decline, continuation of ERT on the same dose with alglucosidase alfa or avalglucosidase alfa during pregnancy should be considered. Furthermore, breastfeeding while receiving alglucosidase alfa is considered safe. For now, there is limited data available on other ERTs, and future research will be needed to provide further insight.

Conclusion

In patients who continued ERT during pregnancy, it appeared to be safe, with no documented adverse effects for mother or child. While most patients experienced a mild worsening of clinical condition during pregnancy, the majority recovered well postpartum.
