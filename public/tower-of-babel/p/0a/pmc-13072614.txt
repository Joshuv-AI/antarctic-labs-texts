Tacrolimus therapy for ulcerative colitis: a retrospective study of factors associated with inducing and maintaining remission
Authors: Ayumi Ito, Shun Murasugi, Miki Koroku, Maria Yonezawa, Teppei Omori, Shinichi Nakamura, Katsutoshi Tokushige, Yousuke Nakai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTacrolimus (TAC) is highly effective pharmacotherapy for refractory ulcerative colitis. However, the factors involved in achieving and maintaining remission are unclear, and the optimal maintenance therapy after inducing remission with TAC remains in question.MethodsThis study involved 136 patients with ulcerative colitis who received TAC therapy from April 2009 to December 2021. The study examined the remission induction rate, overall and drug-specific remission maintenance rates after 52 weeks, factors associated with remission and maintenance, and adverse events.ResultsThe overall remission rate was 72.7%, with significant differences by sex, endoscopic scores before and after TAC therapy, prior experience with biologic therapy, previous biologic therapy, post-TAC Lichtiger Colitis Activity Index (CAI) score, and LCAI reduction at 52 weeks. The remission maintenance rate at 52 weeks was 51.5% and differed significantly by sex, prior experience with biologic therapy, length of hospital stay, and total prednisolone dose during hospitalization. The 52-week maintenance rates by medication were 67% for azathioprine, 54% for TAC, and 37% for biologic agents. No serious side effects were observed with TAC therapy.ConclusionsTAC therapy effectively induced remission in patients with refractory ulcerative colitis. Findings also suggested that such remission can be maintained for up to 52 weeks by treating with either TAC or a biologic agent after inducing remission, even if azathioprine cannot be used as maintenance therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-04670-3.Keywords: Azathioprine, Biological products, Remission induction, Tacrolimus, Ulcerative colitis

Background

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease. The number of cases of UC has increased globally, and this trend is expected to continue [1]. Although newer drugs for UC have recently become available [2], tacrolimus (TAC), a powerful calcineurin inhibitor, and infliximab, a biologic agent, remain mainstays of treatment for intractable and severe cases [2, 3]. Short-term outcomes for inducing remission are reported to be superior with TAC compared to infliximab [4].

The factors involved in successful induction of remission with TAC therapy in UC colitis are unknown. The remission rate with TAC is approximately 50%. If the remission-inducing effect of this drug is insufficient, surgery may be required. The rate of colorectal resection due to recurrence of UC at 52 weeks after inducing remission with TAC is as high as 30% to 40% [5]. Thus, in intractable UC, an important treatment goal is preventing relapse after achieving remission with TAC.

Maintenance therapy in UC often involves using azathioprine (AZA) as an immunomodulatory agent [6]. AZA has long been used for this purpose, and its effectiveness has been confirmed [7]. However, AZA can cause serious adverse events (AEs), such as cytopenia, hepatic disorder, and alopecia totalis [8]. The NUDT15 gene has been shown to be involved in the development of AEs during AZA treatment, and some serious AEs can now be avoided by examining NUDT15 in patients receiving this drug [9]. In clinical practice, however, it is difficult to predict the occurrence of mild to moderate AZA-related AEs solely on the basis of evaluating the NUDT15 gene. Patients receiving AZA maintenance therapy may need to switch from AZA to a biologic agent if relapse occurs.

We conducted this retrospective study in patients with UC to examine the rate of remission induction with TAC therapy, overall and drug-specific remission maintenance rates, factors associated with successful remission and maintenance, and drug safety outcomes.

Methods

Participants and study design

This study of TAC therapy for UC included 4 phases. Phase 1, which served as the foundation for the overall study, involved patients with UC who received TAC therapy from April 2009 to December 2021 (N = 136); this phase evaluated both the remission induction rate and factors associated with achieving remission. Phase 2, which included those patients in phase 1 who achieved remission and were followed up for 52 weeks (n = 99), assessed the overall rate of remission maintenance at 52 weeks (Fig. 1). Phase 3 evaluated drug-specific remission maintenance rates at 52 weeks (Fig. 2). Maintenance rates were assessed only among patients who received single-drug maintenance therapy with either TAC, AZA, or a biologic agent (n = 78). Phase 4 examined drug-specific AEs that occurred during induction of remission or maintenance therapy.

Tacrolimus treatment

In this study, TAC was administered twice daily (before breakfast and dinner) at a dose of 0.025–0.075 mg/kg of body weight. Blood samples were drawn every other day to measure TAC concentrations until the target drug concentration was achieved. The TAC dose was adjusted to achieve a target trough TAC blood concentration of 10–15 ng/mL within 2 weeks of the patient starting TAC induction therapy. At 2 to 3 weeks after achieving the initial target TAC concentration, the TAC dose was then readjusted to achieve a new lower target concentration of 5–10 ng/mL. In patients who received TAC as maintenance therapy for 90 days or longer, the target TAC concentration was also 5–10 ng/mL [3].

Definitions

Remission was defined as having an Lichtiger Colitis Activity Index (LCAI) score of 4 or lower at 4 weeks after starting TAC induction therapy and thereafter. Maintenance of remission was defined as requiring none of the following during the course of follow-up observation: 1) high-dose intravenous prednisolone (≥ 0.5 kg/day), 2) switching maintenance therapy to a biologic agent or Janus kinase inhibitor, 3) re-administration of TAC, 4) reinduction of remission by escalating doses to obtain a high trough TAC blood concentration (≥ 10 ng/dL), or 5) surgery. Maintenance drugs were defined as medications used as maintenance therapy after remission induction (ie, within 180 days of TAC therapy). In analyses that involved categorizing patients by maintenance drug, the TAC and biologic agent groups included only patients without concomitant use of AZA. Experience with a biologic agent was defined as any use of a biologic treatment within the past year.

Phase 1

The initial phase of the study evaluated the rate of remission induction with TAC and factors associated with achieving remission. Factors that were compared between patients who achieved remission (n = 108) and those who did not (n = 28) pertained to clinical history, previous biologic therapy, LCAI score before TAC treatment, blood sampling, endoscopic scores (ie, Mayo endoscopic score and Ulcerative Colitis Endoscopic Index of Severity [UCEIS] score), total prednisolone dose during hospitalization, time (days) to achieve the trough blood concentration of TAC, LCAI score and the LCAI reduction rate at the trough TAC level (defined as the LCAI score at admission minus the LCAI score when the patient’s TAC blood concentration reached the target level, divided by the LCAI score at admission), and endoscopic scores 2 weeks after achieving the trough TAC level (Table 1) [10–12].

Phase 2

The second phase, which included patients who achieved remission in phase 1, assessed follow-up retention rates at 52 weeks after induction of remission and evaluated the overall remission maintenance rate at 52 weeks. Patients who maintained remission (n = 51) and those who did not (n = 48) were compared by the following factors: clinical history, 5-aminosalicylic acid (5-ASA) allergy, AZA intolerance, history of AZA use, history of biologic agent use before TAC therapy, blood sampling, endoscopy score, length of hospital stay, total prednisolone dose during hospitalization, duration of TAC therapy, and trough TAC levels during the maintenance phase (Table 2).

Phase 3

In the third phase of the study, remission maintenance rates at 52 weeks and various patient characteristics were compared across maintenance therapy drug groups, including a comparison of those who received AZA (n = 58) vs those treated with TAC (n = 12) and comparison of patients treated with AZA vs those who received a biologic agent (n = 8; including adalimumab [n = 2], vedolizumab [n = 5], and ustekinumab [n = 1]). The patient characteristics examined in this phase included patient history, 5-ASA allergy, AZA intolerance, history of AZA use, LCAI score prior to starting TAC therapy, blood draw, endoscopy score, length of hospital stay, and total prednisolone dose during hospitalization (Table 3).

Phase 4

The final phase of the study examined the occurrence of AEs during TAC remission induction therapy or remission maintenance therapy and included drug-specific analyses of AEs associated with maintenance therapy.

Statistical analysis

Results were presented as the number of patients or the mean ± SD. Percentage reduction in the LCAI score was presented with a 95% CI. Groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney test or chi-square test. Remission maintenance rates were determined by the Kaplan–Meier estimator and compared using the log-rank test. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro Version 16 (SAS Institute Inc).

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Tokyo Women’s Medical University (approval number: 2022–0143). Written and verbal informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Results

Observation period

The mean observation period among all patients in the study was 1230 ± 175 days.

Remission induction rates

Data collected in phase 1 indicated that remission of UC was induced in 72.7% of patients who started TAC therapy (Fig. 1). Factors that differed significantly between patients who experienced remission and those who did not achieve remission included patient sex (percentage of female patients: 42.5% [46/108] vs 17.8% [5/28], respectively; P = 0.016) and prior experience with a biologic agent (18.5% [20/108] vs 57.1% [16/28], respectively; P = 0.0069). Patients without prior exposure to biologic agents were more likely to achieve remission (18.5% [20/108] vs 57.1% [16/28], respectively; P = 0.0069).

Mean endoscopic scores prior to TAC therapy were significantly different among patients who achieved remission and those who did not achieve remission (Mayo score: 2.9 ± 0.2 vs 2.7 ± 0.4, respectively; P = 0.018 and UCEIS score: 6.5 ± 0.7 vs 4.7 ± 6.9, respectively; P = 0.025). Mean endoscopic scores 2 weeks after achieving the trough TAC concentration also differed significantly between the remission and non-remission groups (Mayo score: 1.7 ± 0.7 vs 2.5 ± 0.7, respectively; P = 0.0009 and UCEIS score: 2.5 ± 0.7 vs 6.0 ± 0.7, respectively; P = 0.0047). Additionally, the remission and non-remission groups differed significantly with regard to the mean LCAI score (8.3 ± 3.2 vs 12.3 ± 3.0, respectively; P = 0.0008) and the percentage reduction in LCAI (95% CI, 41.7–55.1% vs 13.8–37%, respectively; P = 0.0007) at the time of achieving the trough TAC concentration. No significant differences were observed in other patient characteristics (Table 1).

Remission maintenance rates at 52 weeks

In phase 2 of the study, the overall rate of remission maintenance at 52 weeks was 51.5% (Fig. 3). Patients who maintained remission and those who did not differed significantly by sex (percentage of female patients: 52.9% [27/51] vs 33.3% [16/48], respectively; P = 0.0482), prior experience with biologic therapy (5.8% [3/51] vs 22.9% [11/48], respectively; P = 0.0151), and mean length of hospital stay (34 ± 12.3 days vs 29.1 ± 6.9 days, respectively; P = 0.0323) (Table 2).

Remission maintenance at 52 weeks

In phase 3, drug-specific remission maintenance rates at 52 weeks did not significantly differ between patients who received AZA and those treated with TAC or between patients treated with AZA and those who received a biologic agent (Table 3, Figs. 4 and 5). Among the patient characteristics that were examined across the drug-specific groups, the prevalence of AZA intolerance was the only factor that demonstrated a statistically significant (P = 0.0043) intergroup difference (Table 3).

Adverse events

Phase 4 of the study showed that AEs observed during remission induction therapy with TAC included tremor (23 patients, 16.9%), renal dysfunction (9 patients, 6.6%), headache (4 patients, 2.9%), hyperglycaemia (5 patients, 3.0%), hyponatremia (5 patients, 3.0%), and hepatic dysfunction (1 patient, 0.7%). In all affected patients, AEs improved with dose reduction and/or discontinuation of TAC, although this study did not allow for definitive statements about AEs in relation to trough TAC concentrations. Renal dysfunction improved with the addition of 500–1000 mL of fluid replacement per day.

During maintenance therapy, the AEs observed among patients who received AZA were headache (4 patients, 6.8%), cytopenia (7 patients, 12%), nausea (5 patients, 8.6%), hepatic dysfunction (4 patients, 6.8%), alopecia (3 patients, 5.1%), and pneumonia (2 patients, 3.4%). In the TAC maintenance therapy group, tremor (6 patients, 50%), renal dysfunction (4 patients, 33.3%), hyperglycaemia (3 patients, 25%), and hyponatremia (2 patients, 16.7%) were observed. Among patients whose maintenance therapy was a biologic agent, the observed AEs were pneumonia (2 patients, 25%) and influenza (1 patient, 12.5%). Pneumonia was reported with adalimumab (1 patient) and vedolizumab (1 patient), and bronchiectasis was observed with ustekinumab (2 patients)..

Discussion

The remission induction rate observed with TAC therapy in this population of patients with UC was higher than that reported in previous studies [3]. Patient-specific factors that were positively associated with achieving remission included: female sex, no prior use of biologic agents, high endoscopic scores before starting TAC therapy, long hospital stay, high total steroid dose, low endoscopic scores 2 weeks after achieving the trough TAC concentration, low LCAI score and/or a high percentage reduction in the LCAI score after achieving the trough TAC concentration.

Sex differences in UC have been examined in various studies, but it is unclear whether these differences play a role in the effects of therapy [13]. For example, it has been reported that women are more likely than men to adhere to prescribed oral medications [14]. Additionally, symptoms of UC in women are affected by menstruation and hormonal fluctuations. Such factors may have influenced the higher rate of remission induction observed among female patients in this study.

Patients with no prior experience with biologic therapies were more likely to achieve remission induction, compared to those who had previously used such agents. Of note, patients with prior biologics experience likely had refractory UC, potentially affecting the likelihood of inducing remission. Typically, patients with UC who have steroid resistance or dependence, frequent relapses, or chronic persistence are considered to have refractory disease [2]. In such cases, it is difficult to achieve remission even with the use of several drugs. Thus, the patients in this study who had a history of using biologic agents may have been more likely to have refractory UC and may thus have responded poorly to TAC.

Additionally, prior use of biologics by some patients in this study may have weakened the therapeutic effect of TAC in these individuals, given the specific biological mechanism of this drug. TAC is a calcineurin inhibitor that reduces inflammation by suppressing T-cell activation. The drug binds to the FKBP12 protein in cells, inhibiting the action of calcineurin. As a result, production of the proinflammatory cytokines interleukin 2, interleukin 3, and interferon gamma as well as T-cell differentiation and proliferation, cellular immunity, and humoral immunity are suppressed [15]. It is thought that the inflammation seen in UC is partly due to an excessive immune response [16]. Thus, in patients with UC and a history of using biologic agents, the flow of cytokines may be artificially altered. We have considered the possibility that the inflammatory process may become more complicated after a patient has received targeted treatment with biologics, resulting in alteration of the original pathology. Although tumour necrosis factor (TNF) α is relatively suppressed after anti–TNF-α treatment, for example, we postulated that other persistent cytokines, such as interleukin 6, may increase in this setting. Therefore, we hypothesized that proinflammatory cytokines may become more complex, thereby reducing the efficacy of TAC, in patients with a history of treatment with biologic agents.

Furthermore, the principal locus of inflammation can vary depending on the stage of UC. In clinical practice, remission is often not maintained, requiring intensified therapy; alternatively, the disease can become resistant to conventional treatment at the time of relapse. These factors (ie, drug concentrations and drug antibodies) are among the secondary causes of ineffectiveness of biologic treatments. With exposure to an increasing variety of therapies, the inflammatory pathway becomes more complex, and resistance to the therapeutic agents can occur [17]. Several authors have reported high efficacy of TAC in patients with UC who have no prior experience with biologics. This finding is expected, not only because of the level of refractoriness in these cases but also because previous therapy modalities may influence current treatment outcomes. Therefore, in the setting of TAC therapy for UC, it is important to consider the possibility that patients with prior experience with biologics may be less likely to achieve induction of remission with TAC [18, 19]. We believe that this principle may be relevant to other drugs as well.

In the present study, patients with higher endoscopic scores before starting TAC, longer hospital stays, and/or higher total steroid doses were more likely to achieve remission. We believe these findings suggest that TAC is an effective therapy even in patients with endoscopically severe disease. Furthermore the association between successful remission induction and both longer hospital stays and higher total steroid doses may indicate the effectiveness of TAC therapy in patients using steroids in stages of extreme inflammation and/or for long treatment periods. Appropriate treatment with both TAC and steroids when inflammation is most pronounced may influence the induction of remission. Steroids are highly effective in UC and are commonly used to treat moderate to severe UC, with proven efficacy [20, 21]. However, it is important to avoid diffuse and long-term steroid therapy, as these drugs are associated with various AEs [2, 22].

This study also demonstrated good clinical response to TAC therapy in patients with a low LCAI score and/or a high percentage reduction in the LCAI score at the time of achieving the trough TAC concentration. If the patient’s clinical symptoms have improved by the time the trough TAC level is achieved, subsequent remission is more likely to occur [15].

In this study, the remission maintenance rate at 52 weeks was 51.5%. The literature includes few reports on medium- to long-term maintenance therapy with TAC in patients with UC. Most studies have instead focused on avoidance of initial surgery rather than maintenance of remission as an outcome. The existing previous reports on TAC maintenance therapy have documented maintenance rates of 49.9% at 1 year and 37.8% at 2 years after TAC induction therapy [15]. By contrast, in one study, infliximab, which is also used as remission induction therapy in severe UC, demonstrated a maintenance rate of 68% after a median of 33 months of follow-up in patients with an initial response to treatment with this drug [18, 23].

In our study population, patient-specific factors that were positively associated with maintaining remission included female sex, no prior experience with biologic therapies, a long hospital stay, and a high total prednisolone dose during hospitalization. As previously stated regarding our findings on remission induction rates in this study, these results are likely related to higher levels of surgical avoidance and greater adherence to prescribed oral medications among women as compared to men. Additionally, appropriate use of steroids during the early stages of inducing remission may also benefit long-term prognosis in the maintenance stage of therapy. Lastly, similar to the setting of remission induction in UC, remission maintenance therapy is also considered difficult in patients with a history of prior biologic therapy, as the use of biologic agents complicates the inflammatory pathway, making it more challenging to maintain remission. Therefore, determining which drug to use for maintenance therapy in patients whose remission was induced with TAC is an important question. The influence of a patient’s prior biologic drug experience on the pathology of UC requires further investigation.

In general, 5-ASA allergy and AZA intolerance are associated with poorer prognosis and often make maintenance therapy more difficult [24, 25]. In the present study, however, we observed no significant associations between remission maintenance rates and the presence or absence of 5-ASA allergy, AZA intolerance, or history of AZA use (Table 2). Remission induction with TAC may be an effective option even in patients with 5-ASA allergy or AZA intolerance. Drug allergies and intolerances are difficult to diagnose. Therefore, it is important to continue treatment while maintaining open communication between the patient and clinician. For example, it is critical to maintain compliance with oral treatment for inflammatory bowel disease [26].

In phase 3 of this study, we observed no significant maintenance drug–specific differences in remission maintenance rates at 52 weeks. However, maintenance rates differed between the AZA and biologic groups, suggesting a need to re-examine this topic in a larger number of patients. Notably, better results were seen with AZA and biologic maintenance therapy using AZA. In clinical practice, biologic agents and AZA are used in combination for patients with refractory UC. The addition of AZA helps prevent secondary failure of the biologic agents and thus contributes to a more powerful maintenance therapy [2]. Therefore, the secondary failure of biologic agents may have made maintenance therapy difficult in these patients in our study, resulting in worse outcomes than those seen in patients treated with AZA. Also, the subgroup of patients who received biologic agents may have been biased toward more refractory cases, given that once remission is achieved with TAC, maintenance therapy with AZA is generally attempted [2].

AZA intolerance is observed in roughly 10% of patients. In such cases, the use of TAC is considered challenging. In the present study, maintenance of remission was achievable with TAC or a biologic agent, even in patients for whom use of AZA was infeasible. A study of patients who received maintenance therapy with TAC after undergoing remission induction with TAC reported a relapse rate of 32% at 300 days after inducing remission [27]. Thus, we believe it is possible to achieve maintenance of remission by using TAC alone. However, caution should be exercised with long-term use of TAC, given the associated risk of renal impairment [28].

Patients in the present study who received maintenance therapy with a biologic agent were treated with vedolizumab (n = 5), adalimumab (n = 2), or ustekinumab (n = 1). After 1 year, one of the patients who was being maintained on vedolizumab underwent surgery. A previous study on vedolizumab maintenance therapy after TAC therapy found that vedolizumab was effective for remission maintenance, with 67% of patients having avoided surgery after 1 year of treatment; the study reported no serious AEs, suggesting good safety with this regimen [29]. Another study of vedolizumab maintenance therapy reported a 68% rate of avoidance of intestinal resection at 11 months after remission induction with TAC [30]. Ustekinumab is used for remission induction and maintenance therapy in moderate to serious cases of UC [3]. In the present study, ustekinumab was used as secondary therapy in patients for whom adalimumab and vedolizumab were ineffective. The choice of ustekinumab as maintenance therapy was based on a report indicating that clinical remission was achieved and maintained at 44 weeks in patients with insufficient response to vedolizumab [30]. In our study, maintenance therapy with an anti–TNF-α agent (n = 2) used adalimumab. In these 2 patients, adalimumab was used as the initial treatment for induction of remission. As remission could not be achieved with adalimumab alone, however, TAC was added to induce remission. Therefore, adalimumab was continued as maintenance therapy after induction of remission.

Among the patient-specific factors that were examined across the maintenance drug–specific groups, AZA intolerance was the only factor that differed significantly between groups. The advantages of AZA include its efficacy in maintaining remission as well as its ability to suppress the production of anti-drug antibodies. Moreover, the addition of AZA to therapy with a biologic agent (eg, vedolizumab) can be expected to enhance the effects of maintaining remission [31]. AZA is also considered an important drug for long-term maintenance. Future studies are needed to further investigate optimal drug regimens for remission maintenance after TAC therapy.

In phase 4 of the present study, AEs observed during remission induction with TAC included tremors, renal dysfunction, and headache, all of which have also been reported in previous studies [3, 29]. In our study, adjusting the trough TAC concentration allowed patients with these AEs to continue treatment.

During maintenance therapy with AZA, the reported AEs included headache, cytopenia, nausea, hepatic dysfunction, alopecia totalis, and pneumonia. The AZA dose was reduced in patients with headache or nausea; discontinued in patients with hepatic dysfunction, alopecia totalis, or pneumonia; and either reduced or discontinued in those with cytopenia. Although some of the more serious AEs associated with use of AZA can now be prevented to some extent by examining the NUDT15 gene [9], AEs can sometimes arise during continued AZA treatment in clinical practice [32]. Therefore, AEs that may occur with AZA therapy must be discussed thoroughly with patients.

The AEs that occurred during maintenance therapy with TAC were tremor, renal impairment, hyperglycaemia, and electrolyte abnormalities. Patients who developed renal impairment or hyperglycaemia were advised to drink water, and we advised patients to drink approximately 1000 mL of water per day. Patients who developed renal impairment or hyperglycaemia were advised to drink water. Patients with a decrease of more than 30% in glomerular filtration rate should undergo appropriate monitoring of renal function, as progression to chronic nephropathy can be expected [30]. In patients with TAC-induced tremor or electrolyte abnormalities, the trough TAC concentration was lowered, and the patients subsequently improved on observation. Cochrane reviews have reported on AEs associated with TAC therapy but have also concluded that these data may be uncertain, as there is a risk of bias in studies comparing TAC to placebo [33]. Therefore, patients receiving TAC should be closely monitored for AEs previously reported as being potentially associated with this drug as well as any other AEs that may arise.

Patients who received a biologic agent as maintenance therapy were evaluated less frequently than patients in other treatment groups in this study, but no serious AEs were observed. However, caution is required regarding the AEs of biologic therapies. For example, anti–TNF-α agents have been suggested to reduce immune defenses against Mycobacterium tuberculosis, thereby posing a risk for the development of tuberculosis. The authors of one study have proposed that biologic agents should be used with caution in patients with rheumatoid arthritis or pre-existing lung disease [34].

This study has several limitations. Firstly, its design as a retrospective study conducted at a single institution involves the likelihood of some source of bias. Secondly, given the very small number of patients who received each type of biologic agent as maintenance therapy, this study may be insufficient, and the results should be interpreted cautiously. Lastly, the study lacked sufficient consideration of whether patients received anti–TNF-α drugs before starting maintenance therapy. Challenges for future studies aimed at furthering research in this area will include increasing the number of study participants and conducting a multicentre prospective study.

Conclusions

In this study, TAC effectively induced remission in patients with refractory UC. Remission induction therapy with TAC was particularly effective in women, patients with no prior use of biologic agents, and those with low LCAI scores and/or a high percentage reduction in the LCAI score at the time of achieving the trough TAC concentration. Additionally, the findings suggest that by using TAC or a biologic agent, maintenance of remission can be achieved for up to 52 weeks after inducing remission with TAC, even in patients who are unable to use AZA as maintenance therapy.
