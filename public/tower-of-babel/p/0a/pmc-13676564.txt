Calcium isotope ratio (δ44/42Ca) reflects kidney function and calcium excretion but not bone mineral density or bone turnover: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Felix N von Brackel, Mikolaj Bartosik, Robert Munzinger, Florian Barvencik, Thorsten Schinke, Ralf Oheim, Michael Amling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSummaryCalcium isotope fractionation (δ⁴⁴/⁴²Ca) was evaluated as a biomarker of bone metabolism in 301 patients. δ⁴⁴/⁴²Ca correlated with urinary calcium excretion but not with bone mineral density or fracture status. Age, kidney function, and calcium intake influenced results, limiting its diagnostic utility for osteoporosis in clinical practice. Data on Calcium isotope fractions (δ44/42Ca) in isolated bone diseases are limited. Previously proposed as a diagnostic marker in metabolic bone disorders, we now extend our findings from monogenic bone diseases to patients with osteoporosis and osteopenia.MethodsIn 301 patients (43 controls), serum and urine δ44/42Ca were analyzed alongside bone (such as bone density) and treatment parameters.ResultsAge, kidney function, and calcium intake significantly affected δ44/42Ca and must be considered in interpretation. δ44/42Ca correlated strongly with urinary calcium excretion (serum: r²=0.55, urine: r²=0.20; p<0.0001) but not with bone turnover markers or BMD. Higher β-CTX were associated with low δ44/42Ca levels (p<0.05). Osteoanabolic therapy increased δ44/42Ca (p<0.05), whereas antiresorptive treatment had no effect. No differences in δ44/42Ca appeared by fracture or BMD stratification, but age-adjusted δ44/42Ca stratification showed higher urinary calcium and PTH (both p<0.0001) in individuals above the threshold, a pattern consistent with neutral or positive bone mineral balance.ConclusionWhile δ44/42Ca reflects aspects of calcium metabolism, such as nutritional calcium supply, in this real-world setting, it failed to predict fractures or distinguish osteoporosis from healthy controls or osteopenia and vice versa, indicating limited clinical use. Thus, although δ44/42Ca is a parameter of interest for calcium metabolism, it does not substitute established bone diagnostics such as BMD or laboratory assessments. Future studies are needed to determine if and how δ44/42Ca can contribute to individualized diagnostics and treatment monitoring in bone disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00198-026-07935-5.Keywords: Bone metabolic balance, Bone mineral density, Calcium isotopes, Diagnostic parameter, Osteoporosis

Introduction

New methods for improving the diagnosis of osteoporosis are of growing interest due to the disease’s high clinical and economic burden. Although DXA is widely used to assess bone mineral density (BMD, also called areal BMD (aBMD)), it fails to identify many patients at risk for fracture, as a substantial proportion (about 50%) of those with osteoporotic fractures have BMD in the osteopenic or normal range [1]. These new methods include high-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography (HR-pQCT) [2] and clinical risk models such as FRAX and DVO score [3, 4], as well as a variety of laboratory parameters related to bone formation and resorption. In addition, markers of nutritional status and systemic calcium supply contribute to the assessment of skeletal health.

More than two decades ago, J. Skulan et al. proposed the ratio of the two calcium isotopes, 44Ca and 40Ca, as a potential marker of calcium use in vertebrates [5]. The new concept was, among other things, to refrain from administering additional, defined amounts of stable calcium isotopes (as in Eastell et al. [6]) and instead to use only the calcium naturally present in the diet. Although the application of this concept requires several theoretical assumptions and correction factors, the δ44/40Ca ratio is thought to reflect calcium mobilization from bone [5, 7]. During the last decade, Eisenhauer et al. applied this ratio [8] and later adapted it using δ44/42Ca instead [9] (while these ratios are equivalent besides a scaling factor (≈2)). The underlying theory suggests that 42Ca, as the lighter isotope, is more readily incorporated into the bone matrix due to its lighter nature and thus metabolic availability. Therefore, “The mean value of” δ44/40Ca “in the bone of an organism depends on the value of the ‘flux of calcium into bone’/’flux of (dietary) calcium into the organism’ during addition of new bone mass averaged over the life of the organism and on the” δ44/40Ca “of dietary calcium” [7]. Consequently, increased bone resorption is expected to elevate serum 42Ca values by release from bone tissue, thereby lowering the δ44/42Ca ratio. Low δ44/42Ca values are therefore considered indicative of enhanced bone loss and a potential risk for osteoporosis [8, 9]. Therefore, δ44/40Ca has been discussed not only as a “marker for bone mineral balance” by Skulan et al. [7] but also as indicative of rapid bone loss, such as in case of bed rest [10].

While this concept is appealing, studies testing the concept of δ44/40Ca for bone health in large adult cohorts are still limited. A large portion of available data come from pediatric populations in growth or from patients with advanced kidney disease or on dialysis [11, 12], conditions well known to induce renal osteodystrophy [13] and to alter calcium metabolism.

Although the measurement of calcium isotope ratios (δ44/42Ca) is now commercially available, prospective clinical studies validating its use are currently scarce. Furthermore, the only published study that applied δ44/42Ca to osteoporosis and defined thresholds for distinguishing between healthy and diseased individuals included only 14 patients with osteoporosis [9].

Following previous claims regarding the potential of δ44/42Ca to identify individuals without osteoporosis [9] or even to diagnose osteoporosis [9], even more effectively than DXA, based on age-adjusted thresholds [14], we tested whether the proportion of patients below the δ44/42Ca thresholds differed between those with normal BMD (controls), osteopenia, and osteoporosis. Specifically, we evaluated both absolute (global) and age-adjusted thresholds for serum and urine δ44/42Ca. While it has been suggested that δ44/42Ca may exclude osteoporosis and thereby limit the need for DXA [9], these claims have yet to be confirmed in larger, independent cohorts.

To address this gap, we designed a study to evaluate whether δ44/42Ca measurements in serum and urine can provide meaningful clinical information regarding BMD, fracture occurrence, or overall bone metabolic balance in a large real-world patient cohort using this measure in a cross-sectional framework like recently proposed by others.

Methods

This primarily cross-sectional study included 301 patients from the outpatient clinic of the Department of Osteology and Biomechanics, University Medical Center Hamburg-Eppendorf, to evaluate the clinical relevance of δ44/42Ca measurements in serum and urine for bone health assessment. All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was approved by the local ethics committee (Hamburger Ärztekammer, 2022-100817-BO-ff) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Blood and urine were collected under standardized conditions on the day of DXA examination, and δ44/42Ca analysis was performed by OsteoLabs GmbH (Kiel, Germany). Correction for calcium supplementation was carried out where necessary. Biochemical markers of bone and calcium metabolism, kidney function, and vitamin D were analyzed using standard laboratory procedures, while BMD was assessed at the lumbar spine and hip via DXA following ISCD guidelines. Statistical analyses included group comparisons, regression models, and ROC analyses with age-adjusted testing for robustness. Detailed information on patient handling, analytical procedures, data preprocessing, and statistical modeling are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 350 measurements were carried out, comprising 301 baseline assessments, 44 follow-up evaluations, and four measurements during a second follow-up (Fig. 1A). Additionally, one individual underwent three follow-up measurements after hypercalcemia and subsequent treatment with denosumab.

At baseline, 14.29% of the measurements were categorized as controls, all of which demonstrated a BMD above a T-score of −1. Meanwhile, 21.93% showed reduced BMD but remained above the osteoporosis threshold (T-score ≤ −2.5). Additionally, 38.54% of participants were diagnosed with osteoporosis without osteoporotic fractures, while 25.25% exhibited osteoporosis characterized by atraumatic, major osteoporotic fractures (Fig. 1B). In the entire baseline cohort, 54.82% of participants reported having experienced at least one fracture (any) in the past 10 years, while 45.18% had not sustained any fractures during that period (Fig. 1C).

Of the osteoporotic fractures (Fig. 1D), 83.10% were vertebral fractures, while 8.45% were fractures of the distal radius and femoral neck respectively. In total, 25.25% of all patients (including those with calcium supplements) were male, the remaining patients were female (Fig. 1E). A total of 79.07% of the patients did not supplement calcium at their first measurement, while the rest did (Fig. 1F).

When performing a multiple linear regression (forward method) for δ44/42Ca in serum, the significant model (p = 0.0022, r2 = 0.27) comprising two variables consisted of calcium per creatinine in urine (slope: −0.01660, β = −0.34243) and the GFR (slope: −0.0033, β = −0.28847) and a significant intercept of −0.526. Performing the same analysis for δ44/42Ca in urine, only calcium per creatinine in urine was a significant predictor for the model (p < 0.00001, r2 = 0.54). The model consisted of an intercept (0.5142) and a slope of −0.06088 and a β = −0.73443. Other parameters (laboratory, age, BMI, BMD, BMC) were not significant.

In a model predicting the lowest BMD, the significant predictors (forward method) were BMI, DPD, and PTH. While δ44/42Ca in both urine and serum were considered, these were not included due to missing significance. The same results were obtained for the backward method.

Including ln(Ca/Creatinineurine) mirroring the Rayleigh-type isotope distillation process did only slightly increase the regression to r2 = 0.28 and r2 = 0.55; yet in both cases it was chosen over Ca/Creatinineurine.

Discussion

We present the largest fully characterized cohort to date of patients with osteoporosis or osteopenia compared to controls, studied in a real-world clinical setting where bone is the primary reason for medical consultation, contrasting other published studies [8, 10–12, 14]. Our patient group size exhibits excellent power (above 0.95) to address questions even at low correlation coefficients.

Comparing potential influencing factors such as fasting status, calcium supplementation, age, kidney function, and vitamin D levels, our results clearly show that vitamin D levels or fasting versus non-fasting has no substantial impact on δ44/42Ca values.

The known effect of calcium supplementation on δ44/42Ca was also evident in our study. This underlines the importance of correcting for calcium intake when interpreting δ44/42Ca values [11, 14, 15], yet established corrections are limited to several assumptions (cf. Supplemental Material, e.g., isotope cycling, diet, and supplement compositions). Our data confirm that age significantly influences δ44/42Ca, in line with others [14, 16]. Thus, to reduce bias, we excluded individuals under 39 years from group comparisons. While many previous studies focused on children [11, 12], our findings meet the requirement of age-adjustment from literature [14], yet with a parallel, nonlinear pattern for both urine and serum.

Notably, kidney function also emerged as a significant cofactor. Patients with reduced kidney function exhibited higher δ44/42Ca values, whereas those without renal impairment had lower values. This effect is especially relevant given that lower δ44/42Ca values have been discussed to indicate an elevated risk of osteoporosis [9, 14]. While Shroff et al. write “Thus, model prediction is that δ44/42Caserum values are expected to be lower in CKD […] patients because of reduced Ca reabsorption.” [12], our results contrast this statement and other results from growing children with chronic kidney disease [11]. In particular, considering the hypothesis of a faster metabolic turnover of 42Ca [11], thus larger fractions of 42Ca to be reabsorbed and 44Ca excreted, our results may indicate changes in renal handling of calcium isotopes. Yet, urine and serum δ44/42Ca values run parallel and therefore do not indicate a compartment-specific enrichment in urine or blood. Therefore, δ44/42Ca analysis should be discussed to be corrected for kidney function at any stage. Additionally, differences could be explained by further (sometimes equally important) factors, such as dietary and lifestyle factors (alcohol intake, dairy or vegan diets) and physical exercise [16].

δ44/42Ca has been discussed as a potential marker for assessing bone metabolic balance [7, 8, 14, 17] and as a possible predictor of fracture risk [14]. We found a strong (hyperbolic, i.e., logarithmic) correlation between urinary δ44/42Ca and calcium excretion normalized to creatinine, in line with others [16]. This pattern aligns with findings by David et al. [18] and others [14] (r2 = 0.62 and r2 = 0.25 respectively — although fitted linearly in their work). With our dataset, a semi-logarithmic association explains about 55% of the variance in urinary δ44/42Ca. This interaction has been discussed to be related to the renal isotope fractionation between primary and secondary urine [8]. Interestingly, while described for urine, we firstly report similar effects for serum measures needing further research.

In our cohort, the proportion of patients above and below the δ44/42Ca thresholds was similar between controls and osteoporotic patients with fractures (Fig. 3). Although sensitivity values for serum and urine δ44/42Ca thresholds were moderate, specificity and negative predictive values remained low. This, along with non-significant frequency distributions, suggests that δ44/42Ca has limited value for reliably identifying individuals without osteoporosis in clinical practice. Fisher’s exact test showed no significant association between age-adjusted δ44/42Ca thresholds and fracture status, and the low specificity combined with modest sensitivity indicate that neither serum nor urine markers reliably discriminate fracture from non-fracture cases. This contrasts with other findings [9] that reported (on n = 14 osteoporotic patients) negative predictive values of 100% in serum and 94% in urine for identifying the absence of osteoporosis and proposed δ44/42Ca as a potential negative screening tool [9]. In our data, no significant differences in δ44/42Ca were observed when stratifying by BMD (normal vs. osteoporotic) or by fracture status, and ROC analyses did not demonstrate discriminatory ability in serum or urine. These results suggest that δ44/42Ca may reflect physiological processes other than bone mass or fracture risk but the test is not applicable to rule out osteoporosis as a diagnosis. Additionally, δ44/42Ca values do heavily depend on many other factors than only calcium supplementation and fasting but rather on vegetarian or vegan diet, alcohol consumption, physical exercise, excess soft drink consumption, and long fasting periods [16].

Several factors may explain these differences. First, Eisenhauer et al. included only a small number (n = 14) of patients with osteoporosis and acknowledged that they were “unable to achieve the target number of women with osteoporosis as estimated from initial assumption” [9]. Therefore, their cohort (and ours reverse) is unbalanced (66 controls vs. 14 OPO) inflating the negative predictive value (NPV) of δ44/42Ca as a rule-out test, whereas in ours the reverse prevalence drove the NPV below 25%. Because predictive values depend heavily on disease prevalence, a direct comparison of positive predictive value (PPV) and NPV across these cohorts is different. Second, the reported [9] positive predictive values were 31% for serum and 36% for urine, which is close to the expected baseline prevalence of osteoporosis in an average older population [19]. This suggests that the positive diagnostic utility of δ44/42Ca may not substantially exceed chance level.

When comparing the bone turnover status, higher δ44/42Ca values in patients with low bone resorption (β-CTX) support the idea that δ44/42Ca may reflect aspects of bone turnover. A similar trend was seen for bone formation (P1NP), although group separation was limited. These findings are consistent with previous results in children, where higher δ44/42Ca values were also observed under conditions of low bone resorption [11]. In contrast, no differences were seen when quantifying the metabolic balance by relating bone formation to bone resorption parameters (Fig. 5).

To further investigate bone metabolic balance, we examined δ44/42Ca in patients receiving antiresorptive or anabolic therapy (treatment monitoring). No significant increase in δ44/42Ca values was seen with antiresorptive treatment (Fig. 6). This contrasts with previous findings showing a rise in δ44/42Ca after denosumab therapy [15] at similar average follow-up timing, despite significant drops in β-CTX and DPD in our data (Suppl. Figure 2). This discrepancy may be explained by the strong dependence of δ44/42Ca on nutritional and other external factors [16]. These factors could have changed during therapy or with increased disease awareness also mirrored by changes in the course of therapy (Fig. 6). Importantly, these variations generally remain below the reported intra individual variations (∼ ± 0.2–0.3‰) reported in literature [16], except for the anabolic treatment. This effect also reflects the different models of using δ44/42Ca. While cross-sectional comparisons, as suggested by Eisenhauer et al., are challenging given the high inter-individual variability (∼ ± 2‰) [16], self-referencing seems more reasonable especially for long time periods between measurements. Moreover, osteoporosis is typically a chronic, slowly progressing condition characterized by subtle alterations in bone metabolism. Such gradual changes may not be captured by a spot δ44/42Ca measurement, even though established resorption markers like β-CTX and DPD clearly reflect reduced turnover. Low-turnover osteoporosis may further contribute to the limited isotopic response. In particular, this method has been criticized as a spot measurement [10, 16] with rather a need of a baseline measurement at peak bone mass in young age. Moreover, experiments such as bed rest studies [10] may also indicate that spot δ44/42Ca measures cover acute bone loss rather chronic bone loss and therapeutic changes of decreased chronic bone loss.

In contrast, δ44/42Ca values increased consistently with anabolic therapy, in line with Skulan et al., who proposed higher values in case of bone growth (i.e., anabolic) [5, 7]. This supports our previous findings showing that δ44/42Ca rises during active bone formation [20].

When stratifying by age-adjusted δ44/42Ca threshold, individuals with low (i.e., “pathological”) δ44/42Ca values exhibited better kidney function and higher urinary calcium excretion. Since no differences in resorption markers were detected between groups, this contrasts with the assumption that low δ44/42Ca reflects increased bone resorption. Instead, higher (i.e., “normal”) δ44/42Ca values were associated with lower calcium excretion, possibly indicating more efficient calcium reabsorption (in contrast to a reduced GFR) or increased calcium demand (Suppl. Figure 3). When considering PTH as an indicator of calcium need (i.e., low calcium supply or intestinal resorption or otherwise wasting), our findings may point to an increased need in patients with high (i.e., “healthy”) δ44/42Ca values. Higher PTH levels lead to forced renal calcium reabsorption, which may reduce calcium excretion. As a result, more of the heavier 44Ca could have been reabsorbed. This would mean that δ44/42Ca reflects general calcium availability, in particular dietary intake, rather than fracture risk, low BMD, or osteoporosis directly. Nonetheless, long-term calcium deficiency is known to contribute to bone loss [21, 22]. Taken together, this supports the idea that higher δ44/42Ca values may reflect increased calcium demand, possibly based on low calcium supply. Future work must jointly model dietary intake, PTH, GFR, and isotopes to disentangle these influences.

When considering the results of multiple linear regression, δ44/42Ca was not a significant predictor of BMD, nor did BMD predict δ44/42Ca. The strongest predictors of δ44/42Ca were urinary calcium excretion per creatinine and GFR, pointing to a close link with renal calcium handling. This questions the role of δ44/42Ca as a direct marker of bone metabolism and suggests it may reflect calcium supply instead. Therefore, our results underline the importance of adequate and individualized calcium and vitamin D intake in patients with bone diseases such as osteoporosis, reflected by δ44/42Ca values.

Our δ44/42Ca values showed poor correlation with most conventional bone turnover markers and aBMD. Nevertheless, natural stable-Ca isotopes permit repeated, non-invasive intraindividual monitoring of calcium isotopes ratios avoiding the logistical burden and exposure of administered radiotracers, making them attractive for longitudinal assessment [23].

Although radiotracer calcium-kinetic studies (e.g., 47Ca or 85Sr) remain a useful reference and have historically been used to validate biochemical turnover markers [23], the concordance among tracer kinetics, serum/urine markers, and bone histomorphometry is limited in many settings [24, 25]. For instance, Eastell et al. found urinary deoxypyridinoline (DPD) correlated reasonably well with exchange-corrected tracer estimates of resorption (r≈0.71), and osteocalcin correlated with tracer-derived accretion rates to a similar degree. In contrast, bone-specific alkaline phosphatase failed to show significant correlations with tracer measures, and histomorphometric indices did not correlate significantly with the accretion rate, illustrating the imperfect agreement between different measures of bone turnover [23–25]. Biochemical markers and calcium isotope ratios suffer from biological variability, nutrition, and renal dependence, limiting sensitivity and specificity. Radiotracer methods are resource-intensive and demand repeated, multi-pool sampling [25, 26]; also, their sensitivity is an order of magnitude different from other measurements and therefore may be requiring an integration over time. Prospective head-to-head validation against tracer kinetics, administered stable-isotope protocols [6], and bone histomorphometry is required before natural isotope ratios can be considered a replacement for established clinical monitoring methods.

While our study includes the largest clinically well-characterized cohort to date, it has some limitations. δ44/42Ca measurements were obtained in a real-world clinical setting; information on fasting status and calcium supplementation was self-reported. As a result, we excluded patients on calcium supplements from the primary analysis, except for follow-up measurements where the timing of supplementation was clearly documented. While GFR and age were identified as relevant covariates, only age was included as a covariate in all statistical comparisons, specifically GFR, as current recommendations do not yet suggest routine correction for GFR (nevertheless calculations are available). Our data was derived from real-world clinical cases and thus has a tendency towards patients with disturbed bone metabolism (high or low in specific parameters) to effectively test the ability of δ44/42Ca measurements to detect patients with disturbed bone metabolism. Therefore, to address possible insensitivity, we have analyzed the needed sample size for a respective power (which we exceed by far) and additionally separated the groups (laboratory) based on the highest and lowest percentiles. Bottled drinking water as a potential source of calcium was not considered in this study. Furthermore, no data on nutritional calcium was available. Thus, studies are needed to address the influence of calcium malnutrition and its effect on δ44/42Ca. The marginal and largely non-existent differences between the groups of bone metabolism were surprising. While we do not share the opinion that bone turnover “[…] markers are of marginal clinical value […],” “Processes other than bone formation may fractionate calcium and produce isotopic differences between soft tissue calcium compartments,” [7] potentially further explaining the data yet unknown today. Known from literature, cross-sectional variance may be up to 2.2‰, by far larger than threshold variability [16]. Furthermore, for correct interpretation of δ44/42Ca measures, a baseline measure (at peak bone mass around 30–40 years) is discussed to be necessary to interpret δ44/42Ca values correctly, [10, 16] which inherently cannot be provided for patients today at risk for osteoporosis at the age of 60 years or above. Therefore, a self-referencing approach (comparing T1 and T0) is preferable for detecting true shifts in calcium balance because it removes much of the interindividual variability. To reliably capture long-term changes in bone mineral balance, the two measurements may need to be separated by several decades (T0 preferably at peak bone mass around 30–40 years). However, to prove this idea, long-term studies are needed which are not available today or within the next decades given its inherent timely design, yet dealing with dietary and lifestyle factors may be an interim subject before holistic validation for clinical use is available.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study shows that δ44/42Ca does not significantly correlate with bone mineral density or fracture occurrence. δ44/42Ca values are strongly influenced by kidney function and age, needing adjustment. No bone turnover markers sufficiently separate groups to enable an assessment of bone metabolic balance by δ44/42Ca and vice versa. Overall, our findings suggest that δ44/42Ca reflects aspects of calcium demand. These results highlight the importance of adequate calcium and vitamin D intake, as δ44/42Ca appears to reflect primarily nutritional calcium status but not bone health.
