Maternal biological aging from pregnancy through four years postpartum with disrupted recovery by subsequent pregnancy
Authors: Laura Etzel, Patricia Garrett-Peters, Qiaofeng Ye, Abner T. Apsley, Aaliya Ahamed, John T. Kozlosky, Christopher R. Chiaro, Lorrie Schmid, Victoria K. Lee, Cathi Propper, Roger Mills-Koonce, Sarah J. Short, Idan Shalev
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPregnancy has been proposed as a biological “stress test” that may transiently accelerate maternal biological aging, followed by partial recovery postpartum, yet longitudinal evidence remains limited. This longitudinal study followed 130 women at mid to late pregnancy, 6–9 months postpartum, and 36–43 months postpartum, to characterize nonlinear changes in markers of biological aging, including telomere length, epigenetic aging measures, and pace of aging derived from saliva samples. Using generalized additive mixed models, results indicated relative stability or slowing of biological aging during early postpartum, followed by marker-specific patterns of stabilization or decline in later postpartum. Subsequent pregnancy in later postpartum was associated with shorter telomere length and disrupted recovery trajectories, particularly accelerating the pace of aging among women who became pregnant again. Findings suggest that while postpartum biological aging recovery may occur, such recovery may be vulnerable to interruption by additional pregnancies.

Introduction

Pregnancy requires profound physiological, metabolic, and immune adaptations to support fetal development and birth,1 placing substantial demands on the gestational parent’s body. These demands may leave lasting biological imprints, increasing risk for conditions such as cardiovascular disease and reduced healthspan.2,3 Preliminary evidence suggests biological aging fluctuates across pregnancy and postpartum,4 but its extent, timing, and permanence remain unclear. Biological aging, reflected in molecular markers like telomere length (TL) and DNA methylation (DNAm) clocks, such as GrimAge2, PhenoAge, and DunedinPACE,5,6,7 captures physiological decline that may diverge from chronological age. Shortened telomeres and DNAm-predicted age acceleration have been linked to cumulative biological stress.8,9,10,11

Pregnancy may simultaneously accelerate aging and open a window for postpartum recovery,4,12 driven by inflammatory, oxidative, and hormonal shifts during gestation followed by immunologic and metabolic recalibration.13 Repeated pregnancies may impose cumulative costs, with evidence linking higher parity to shorter telomeres and greater epigenetic age, even after adjusting for socioeconomic and behavioral factors.14,15 Longitudinal studies suggest partial reversal of biological aging post-birth, with decreases in GrimAge2 and PhenoAge through 12 months postpartum,12 though recovery may be blunted by weight retention or adverse metabolic profiles.12,15

Additional pregnancies may disrupt this recovery; for example, in Filipino women, more pregnancies predicted increased epigenetic aging over 4–9 years, though measures were taken post-pregnancy.14 Together, these findings support a dynamic model of aging during pregnancy and postpartum, with transient acceleration followed by incomplete or disrupted recovery across successive pregnancies. Yet, within-person data capturing repeated pregnancies remain limited. The following study addresses this gap using four validated markers of biological aging (TL, two epigenetic clocks, and pace of aging) measured from mid/late pregnancy to nearly four years postpartum. We test whether postpartum transition alters biological aging and whether a subsequent pregnancy modifies these trajectories, offering insight into pregnancy as both a biological stress test and a turning point in aging.

Nineteen women (14.6%) reported a subsequent pregnancy that occurred between 30 and 36 months postpartum (hereafter referred to as the “late subsequent pregnancy” group), while 111 (85.4%) reported no pregnancy. Women in the late subsequent pregnancy group were significantly younger at the prenatal visit (M = 29.47, SD = 3.44) compared to those without a later pregnancy (M = 31.56, SD = 5.46; p = 0.035). Biological aging markers showed expected associations with external indicators: shorter TL and higher epigenetic age or faster pace of aging were generally observed in those with higher BMI, lower income-to-needs, and greater illness burden. TL associations were in the expected direction but not significant after adjusting for maternal age. GrimAge2 was significantly higher in those with higher BMI (r = 0.29, p < 0.001), greater illness burden (r = 0.27, p < 0.001), and lower income-to-needs (r = −0.27, p < 0.001). PhenoAge showed similar, though non-significant trends. DunedinPACE showed the strongest and most consistent associations: faster pace of aging with higher BMI (r = 0.35, p < 0.001) and increased illness burden (r = 0.28, p < 0.001), and slower pace of aging with increased income-to-needs (r = −0.21, p = 0.001), underscoring its sensitivity to social and metabolic influences on biological aging.

GrimAge2

Compared to measurement during pregnancy, GrimAge2 acceleration declined early postpartum (approximately birth to 9 months postpartum; time spline 1: b = −2.11, SE = 1.06, p = 0.049), then stabilized (time spline 2: b = 0.77, SE = 0.80, p = 0.33). Women with a late subsequent pregnancy showed a non-significant increase in GrimAge2 acceleration (b = 1.02, SE = 0.69, p = 0.14). The time-by-group interaction in spline 2 was non-significant (b = 2.97, SE = 2.02, p = 0.14) (Figure 1B).

PhenoAge

Compared to measurement during pregnancy, PhenoAge showed a non-significant early decline (approximately birth to 9 months postpartum; time spline 1: b = −2.22, SE = 2.20, p = 0.31), and no evidence of change during the later interval (approximately 9–43 months postpartum; time spline 2: b = −0.16, SE = 1.64, p = 0.92), suggesting a possible early postpartum recovery pattern. Late subsequent pregnancy was not significantly associated with PhenoAge. Late subsequent pregnancy was not significantly associated with PhenoAge, though a large, non-significant interaction emerged in spline 2 (b = 6.21, SE = 4.16, p = 0.14), indicating potential late-postpartum acceleration (Figure 1C).

DunedinPACE

DunedinPACE declined significantly during the early postpartum interval (approximately birth to 9 months postpartum; time spline 1: b = −0.24, SE = 0.04, p < 0.001), indicating a slowing of the pace of aging relative to the within-pregnancy baseline. During the later postpartum interval (approximately 9–43 months), DunedinPACE remained stable (ime spline 2: b = 0.05, SE = 0.03, p = 0.12). There was no difference in pace of aging between those with and without a late subsequent pregnancy (i.e., no group difference emerged in the non-interaction model). In the interaction model, the early decline relative to pregnancy remained significant (spline 1: b = −0.23, SE = 0.04, p < 0.001), but the late postpartum period showed a significant increase in pace of aging for women with a later subsequent pregnancy (spline 2 × late pregnancy: b = 0.20, SE = 0.07, p = 0.006). In contrast, the reference group demonstrated relative stability across the later postpartum interval (Figure 1D).

Discussion

This study examined changes in biological aging from pregnancy through postpartum and how a subsequent pregnancy may alter these trajectories. Consistent with prior work,12 we found that the early postpartum period (∼9 months) may serve as a window of biological recovery, particularly among women who did not conceive again. In the later postpartum period, aging accelerated among those sampled during a subsequent pregnancy, especially for DunedinPACE, which showed a statistically and potentially clinically meaningful increase (∼2.35 months of biological aging per calendar year). This aligns with prior findings of greater epigenetic age gains among women who conceived between study waves14 and extends them by directly measuring aging during successive pregnancies.

Across TL, GrimAge2, and DunedinPACE, we observed interaction effects suggesting increased biological aging in women sampled again during a subsequent pregnancy, contrasting with the stabilization or deceleration observed among women who remained postpartum. Although measures of biological aging in our study showed directionally consistent patterns, DunedinPACE was the only measure for which the interaction term reached statistical significance, consistent with its heightened sensitivity to stress and adversity.16,17,18 These findings support the utility of DunedinPACE in capturing physiological strain linked to pregnancy and reproduction. Overall, our results suggest a dynamic model of maternal biological aging: acceleration during pregnancy, partial postpartum recovery, and renewed aging with additional pregnancies.

This model fits evolutionary theories on the costs of reproduction,19,20 with mechanisms potentially involving oxidative stress, inflammation, and hormonal shifts during gestation, followed by postpartum immune and metabolic remodeling.12,13 Recovery may involve clearance and regeneration of immune cells, but repeated pregnancies could deplete reserves, leading to cumulative aging. Context may shape these effects. Our sample, relatively healthy and well resourced, showed modest effect sizes compared to cohorts in lower-resource settings.14 Life history theory suggests that reproductive costs are amplified under nutritional or psychosocial stress.21,22 Thus, findings may underestimate the burden of pregnancy on biological aging in more vulnerable populations.

Strengths of this study include the longitudinal design spanning nearly four years, one of the longest follow-up periods in this literature to date. Additionally, repeated within-person measurements enhanced sensitivity to change, and our multimodal assessment allowed us to triangulate across biomarkers that may reflect different aspects of aging. Our measures converged on similar patterns, increasing confidence in the results. Further, our data were drawn from a cohort of women with healthy, uncomplicated pregnancies, underscoring that even in the absence of complications pregnancy is a significant biological challenge.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be noted. First, these aging measures were derived from saliva, while most aging clocks have been developed in blood. Prior work suggests moderate cross-tissue correlations,23,24,25 but tissue-specific variation in biological aging signals cannot be ruled out. Saliva differs from blood in cellular composition (e.g., mixtures of immune and epithelial cells), methylation architecture, and sensitivity to local immune activation, all of which could plausibly influence clock behavior. To address this, all methylation-based aging measures were adjusted for immune cell proportions, though residual tissue-specific variation may affect observed associations. At the same time, our analytic strategy emphasizes within-person change rather than between person differences in relationship with epigenetic aging measures and all inference is based on these within person trajectories. Future studies should confirm these patterns with matched blood-saliva longitudinal designs and evaluate whether these tissues yield comparable effects. Second, while sufficient for modeling within-person changes, our modest sample size may have limited power to detect smaller effects. Third, although demographically diverse, participants were generally well resourced. Fourth, we lacked postpartum BMI data, a key factor in metabolic recovery. Prior studies show that weight retention can blunt epigenetic age reversal,12,15 yet women who became pregnant again in our sample had lower pre-pregnancy BMIs, typically associated with less postpartum weight retention,26,27,28 suggesting our estimates may be conservative. A fifth limitation of this study is the absence of a non-pregnant control group, which precludes direct comparison to normative aging trajectories outside of pregnancy. Our inferences therefore rely on within-person change across pregnancy and postpartum; future work including age-matched non-pregnant controls will be important for contextualizing these patterns.

Additionally, our spacing between biological aging measurements limited fine-grained analysis of change across postpartum. Future studies would benefit from more frequent sampling and gestational age-specific measures during subsequent pregnancies to pinpoint inflection points and test the impact of interpregnancy intervals. Relatedly, although we statistically accounted for subsequent pregnancies, our design was not able to account for the gestational age at which measurement was taken within these pregnancies. As a result, we cannot isolate the precise timing of pregnancy-induced inflection points in biological aging, nor test whether shorter interpregnancy intervals impose greater aging strain than longer ones.

In summary, our findings suggest that pregnancy is linked to accelerated biological aging, followed by some postpartum recovery, which may be disrupted by a subsequent pregnancy. These shifts were evident across biomarkers and over an extended postpartum window. Understanding recovery as an ongoing process, and the role of timing between pregnancies, may have critical implications for maternal health across the lifespan.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants

Participants were drawn from the Brain and Early Experiences (BEE) study, a longitudinal investigation of pregnant women and their children conducted in central North Carolina.34 Eligible participants were English-speaking individuals carrying singleton pregnancies and residing within 45 min of the study site. Recruitment occurred between August 13, 2018 and October 30, 2020 during the second or third trimester of pregnancy. Participants attended a prenatal laboratory visit at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, during which saliva samples and psychosocial data were collected.

All procedures involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the University of North Carolina Institutional Review Board (Study #17–1914 and #21–2515) and were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation.

Saliva samples were collected at three timepoints: mid-to-late pregnancy (T1; mean gestational age = 29 ± 4 weeks), approximately 9 months postpartum (T2; mean = 9.0 ± 2.4 months from T1), and approximately 43 months postpartum (T3; mean = 43 ± 3.8 months from T1). Sample collection occurred between August 13, 2018 and December 19, 2024. Of the 233 enrolled participants, 113, 117, and 88 consented to analysis and provided saliva samples at T1, T2, and T3, respectively.

This study represents a secondary analysis of an existing longitudinal cohort; sample size was therefore determined by data availability rather than prospective power calculations. After quality control, the final analytic dataset included 271 telomere length (TL) measurements and 210 DNA methylation samples across 130 participants. Eighty participants contributed at least two samples, and 45 contributed samples at all three timepoints (see S1 for additional details). Analyses incorporated all available observations using mixed-effects modeling approaches that do not require complete data across timepoints.

Method details

Saliva collection and DNA extraction

Saliva samples were collected via passive drool and stored according to standardized protocols. Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Telomere length quantification by qPCR

Absolute telomere length (aTL) was quantified from saliva-derived genomic DNA using quantitative PCR (qPCR) (see S2 for telomere quantification reporting formatted in compliance with the Telomere Research Network recommendations). Saliva was collected via passive drool at three study timepoints (T1–T3). DNA from saliva was extracted using QIAamp kits (Qiagen). Following extraction, DNA was stored at −20°C in the Shalev Lab and underwent an average of four freeze–thaw cycles prior to telomere assay. Specifically, DNA was thawed for: (1) quantification of double-stranded DNA concentration using Quant-iT PicoGreen; (2) aliquoting for DNA methylation assays; (3) dilution preparation for qPCR; and (4) the telomere assay itself. Samples requiring reassessment (n = 48; 18%) underwent one additional freeze–thaw cycle. The average interval between PicoGreen quantification and telomere assay was 2.57 months.

DNA quality control was performed for all samples using spectrophotometric purity metrics (260/280 and 260/230 ratios; mean 260/280 = 1.80; mean 260/230 = 1.44). Double-stranded DNA concentration across samples averaged 17.84 ng/μL. A subset of samples (n = 27; 10%) additionally underwent integrity assessment using the Agilent 2200 TapeStation to generate a DNA Integrity Number (DIN; mean DIN = 2.55). No a priori exclusions were made based on these DNA QC metrics.

qPCR assays were designed to compute aTL (kb) using separate telomere (T) and single-copy gene (S) reactions. For each biological sample, two qPCR runs were performed on the same day using the same DNA aliquot: one run quantified telomere content in kilobases (T), and a second run quantified diploid genome copy number (S) using the single-copy gene interferon beta 1 (IFNB1). Between the T and S runs, the DNA aliquot was stored at 4°C for approximately 2.5 h.

All qPCR was conducted on a Qiagen Rotor-Gene Q thermocycler using 100-well discs connected to an uninterruptible power supply.17,18,19 Each run included triplicate reactions for 22 study samples, a six-point standard curve, five control samples (four study-derived positive controls plus one Jurkat DNA control with known short telomeres, <6 kb), and one no-template control (NTC). Reaction chemistry and volumes were identical for T and S assays: each 20 μL reaction contained 1× QuantiTect SYBR Green Master Mix (Qiagen), 0.2 U uracil glycosylase (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 0.2 μM forward/reverse primer pair, and 6 ng genomic DNA. Primers were purchased from IDT as premixed forward/reverse pairs (RxnReady Primer Pool), HPLC purified, at 10 μM in IDTE buffer (pH 8.0).

Primer sequences were as follows.

Telomere primers.

Forward: 5′-CGG TTT GTT TGG GTT TGG GTT TGG GTT TGG GTT TGG GTT-3′

Reverse: 5′-GGC TTG CCT TAC CCT TAC CCT TAC CCT TAC CCT TAC CCT-3′

IFNB1 primers.

Forward: 5′-TGG CAC AAC AGG TAG TAG GCG ACA C-3′

Reverse: 5′-GCA CAA CAG GAG AGC AAT TTG GAG GA-3′

Thermocycling conditions were: 50°C for 2 min; 95°C for 15 min; then 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s and 55°C for 1 min with fluorescence acquisition. Melt curves were generated from 60°C to 99°C in 1°C steps with 5 s per step.

Absolute quantification relied on synthetic double-stranded oligomer standards (IDT; lyophilized, PAGE purified). For telomere reactions, the standard was an 84 bp dsDNA oligomer comprising 16 canonical telomere repeats. For IFNB1 reactions, the standard was an 83 bp dsDNA oligomer corresponding to the IFNB1 target region flanked by IFNB1 primers. Six-point standard curves were generated via 1:10 serial dilutions on each run. For telomere standards, the highest standard concentration was 0.10 ng/μL, spanning 5.86 × 10ˆ8 to 5.86 × 10ˆ3 kb telomeric DNA per reaction (based on 6 μL input). For IFNB1 standards, the highest standard concentration was 0.00033 ng/μL, spanning 1.18 × 10ˆ7 to 1.18 × 10ˆ2 diploid genome copies per reaction (based on 6 μL input). Assay performance met prespecified efficiency criteria (acceptable range 1.90–2.10). Observed metrics were: telomere R2 = 0.99828, efficiency = 1.98; IFNB1 R2 = 0.99657, efficiency = 1.95.

Time 1 and Time 2 samples (n = 184) were assayed together in batch 1 across 11 days (May 27, 2024 to June 6, 2024). Time 3 samples (n = 88) were assayed in batch 2 across 12 days (October 17, 2024 to October 28, 2024). Within batch 1, T1 and T2 samples from the same participant were placed on the same plate when feasible, except when individual samples required reruns. For each run, telomeric kb (T) and diploid genome copies (S) were computed from standard curve alignment. When applicable, NTC estimates were subtracted from sample estimates prior to calculating aTL. Triplicate values were averaged to obtain per-run T and S estimates, and aTL was computed as T/S (kb per diploid genome). Replicate-level QC was conducted separately for T and S values. If the coefficient of variation (CV) across triplicates exceeded 10%, replicate values were evaluated for an outlier replicate using a distance-based rule: a replicate was excluded if its distance to the closest replicate exceeded twice the distance between the remaining two replicates. After excluding at most one replicate, the mean was recalculated using the remaining two replicates. If CV remained >10% (or if no clear outlier was identified), the sample was rerun for both T and S and re-evaluated under the same QC criteria. Across runs (excluding reruns/failures), an average of 4.6 telomere replicates and 2.9 IFNB1 replicates were dropped per run, yielding duplicate-based estimates when needed.

Between-run (plate) effects were controlled using the five control samples included on every T and S run. For each run, a normalization factor was computed by dividing each control’s estimated telomeric content (or genome copy number) by the across-run average for that control, then averaging across controls to yield a run-level normalization factor. Analytical sample estimates for that run were divided by the run-level factor. This normalization was performed separately for batch 1 and batch 2. To harmonize across batches, a batch adjustment factor was derived from the ratio of the five controls’ aTL values in batch 2 relative to batch 1, and all batch 2 analytical aTL values were divided by this factor. Well-position effects were not modeled because the Rotor-Gene Q’s rotary design is optimized to minimize positional variability.

Assay reproducibility was quantified as follows: mean intra-run CVs were 5.32% (telomere kb) and 4.33% (genome copies). Inter-assay CVs for aTL across the five controls averaged 8.63% in batch 1 and 9.81% in batch 2. The proportion of samples rerun was 15% (28/184) in batch 1 and 23% (20/88) in batch 2; failure resulting in exclusion occurred for 0% of batch 1 samples and 1% (1/88) of batch 2 samples. In an independent reproducibility assessment using 20 saliva samples with repeated DNA extractions assayed twice, reliability at the aTL level was ICC(2,1) = 0.68 and ICC(2,k) = 0.81. Across all samples contributing to analysis, mean aTL was 5.90 kb (SD = 2.04), median aTL was 5.90 kb, and values ranged from 0.06 to 18.68 kb.

Epigenetic aging measures

DNA methylation was assessed using the Infinium Methylation EPIC v2.0 BeadChip (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). 500 ng of genomic DNA was treated with sodium bisulfite using the Zymo EZ-96 DNA Methylation Kit (Zymo Research, Orange, CA, USA). 200 ng of the bisulfite-treated DNA was amplified, fragmented, and hybridized onto the EPICv2 array. Methylation analysis occurred in two batches, with two samples from each participant per timepoint placed on the same plate to reduce technical variability. A subset of samples (2.5%) from the first batch was rerun in the second batch to quantify batch effects.

IDAT files were read into R using the read.metharray.exp function in the minfi package. CpG probes with a bead count of <3 in 5% or more samples or with an average detection p-value of >0.05 across all samples were excluded. All samples were normalized using the beta-mixture quantile normalization (BMIQ) method. Batch effects were corrected for using control probes from the EPICv2 array. Resulting methylation data were used to calculate two second-generation measures of epigenetic age (GrimAge2 and PhenoAge) and a measure of the pace of aging (DunedinPACE). All epigenetic clocks required CpG probes not included in the EPICv2 array; missing probe values were imputed using average values from publicly available adult saliva DNAm data (GSE130153 and GSE111165) as previously done.20 Clocks were computed using modified code from the methylCIPHER and DunedinPACE packages. Given prior findings that clocks trained on prediction of chronological age may not be sensitive to transient stressors,4 we did not include first-generation clocks in our analyses.

Cell composition estimation

Cell-type composition was estimated from DNA methylation data using the Houseman reference-free deconvolution method,33 which is applicable to heterogeneous tissues including non-blood sources such as saliva. This approach estimates relative cellular proportions without reliance on external reference profiles and requires specification of the number of underlying cell types to be modeled.

To identify the best-fitting number of cellular subtypes present in saliva, we implemented the deviance-based model selection procedure provided by the Houseman-ReferenceFree framework. Deviance statistics were computed across a range of candidate models specifying increasing numbers of cell types (e.g., 2, 3, 4, …) using 500 bootstrapped subsamples drawn from the full study sample. Summary deviance metrics were compared across candidate solutions, and the number of cell types corresponding to the minimum deviance statistic was selected as the optimal solution.

The 10,000 CpG probes exhibiting the highest variability across samples were extracted and used as input for reference-free deconvolution. Based on the deviance minimization procedure, saliva was determined to comprise seven distinct cellular subtypes. Estimated proportions for these seven components were included as covariates in all DNA methylation–based analyses to account for cellular heterogeneity.

Assessment of subsequent pregnancy

Pregnancy status was self-reported at each assessment. Pregnancies occurring at or within six months prior to the T3 visit were classified as late subsequent pregnancies (N = 19). Pregnancies occurring between T2 and six months prior to T3 were classified as early subsequent pregnancies (N = 13) and were included as a covariate in all models. No pregnancies were reported prior to T2.

Covariates

Models were adjusted for self-reported pre-pregnancy BMI (kg/m2), gestational age at T1, illness burden, race/ethnicity, and income-to-needs ratio. Illness burden was the average number of self-reported conditions across timepoints (range: 0–14). Conditions included currently having or ever been diagnosed with: (1) cardiac problems or heart disease, including high blood pressure or high cholesterol; (2) diabetes (excluding gestational diabetes); (3) gestational diabetes; (4) cancer; (5) asthma; (6) epilepsy or seizure disorder; (7) sickle cell disease; (8) stroke; (9) depression or bipolar disorder; (10) anxiety; (11) attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder; (12) schizophrenia or borderline personality disorder; (13) other illness; and (14) other genetic disorder. Race/ethnicity was categorized as Black, White, or Other (Hispanic and multiracial). Income-to-needs was calculated as household income divided by the federal poverty threshold.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted using R version 4.1.2. To address missing data and improve statistical power, we implemented multiple imputation of covariates only via the ‘mice’ package using predictive mean matching. Imputation models were informed by observed demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related variables available for each participant. Of the 130 participants, pre-pregnancy BMI was imputed for 6 (5%) participants using measured anthropometrics and baseline demographic characteristics, income-to-needs ratio for 8 (6%) participants using reported income information, public assistance data, maternal education attainment, employment status, and household composition, and the average maternal illness burden for 3 (2%) participants using available clinical and health history indicators. No biological aging outcomes were imputed.

Group comparisons were conducted using Mann-Whitney U tests (continuous) and Chi-square tests (categorical). Correlations were computed using Pearson or Phi coefficients. TL was normally distributed with two outliers at the right end of the distribution (see S3 for Q-Q plot and histogram of original TL values); these values were winsorized to the 95th percentile. All outcomes were adjusted for cell proportions. TL, GrimAge2, and PhenoAge were adjusted for maternal age at collection using linear models. Residuals from these models, representing age- and cell-adjusted biological age, served as the primary outcomes. For DunedinPACE models, maternal age at collection was included as continuous covariate and grand mean centered to facilitate interpretation and reduce potential nonessential collinearity with time trends (see S4 for raw and age- and cell-residualized aging outcome correlations).

Outcomes were modeled separately as linear mixed-effects via the ‘lme4’ and ‘lmerTest’ packages. Time was modeled using a natural spline with 2° of freedom using the ns() function from the ‘splines’ package. This approach estimates a nonlinear trajectory of change over time by fitting two spline basis functions, each with its own coefficient. These basis functions allow the rate of change in the outcome to vary flexibly across the time span, without assuming a single linear slope. To test whether biological aging trajectories differed by late subsequent pregnancy status, we included a time-by-‘late subsequent pregnancy’ interaction term in our models with ‘earlier subsequent pregnancy’ included as a covariate. Models included a random intercept for participant ID to account for within-subject correlation across repeated measures and adjusted for covariates:Yij=β0+β1·Spline1(Timeij)+β2·Spline2(Timeij)+γ⊤Xij+b0j+ϵijWhere:

Yij: the outcome for observation i in subject j.

Timeij: time in months at observation i for subject j.

Spline1(⋅),Spline2(⋅): spline basis functions from ns(Time, df = 2).

Xij: vector of covariates for observation i, subject j

γ⊤Xij: combined linear effects of all covariates

b0j∼N(0,σb2): random intercept for subject j

ϵij∼N(0,σ2): residual error term.

Significance was assessed at p < .05. Modeled trajectories were visualized using ‘ggeffects’ and ‘emmeans’ packages35,36 to extract predicted values and generate line plots across time by late subsequent pregnancy status.
