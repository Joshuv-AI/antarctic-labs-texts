Association between medication adherence and glycaemic control among patients with type 2 diabetes in Luzhou: a retrospective cohort study (2018–2023)
Authors: Ming Yang, Dinglun Zhou, Lingxi He, Ningjun Ren, Yuansheng Li, Song Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the longitudinal association between medication adherence and glycaemic control among patients with Type 2 diabetes (T2D) in Luzhou City, identify modifiable factors and provide actionable evidence to inform targeted diabetes management strategies.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingWestern China.ParticipantsData from 30 508 T2D patients in the Luzhou City Health Information Management System (2018–2023).Primary and secondary outcome measuresPrimary outcome measure: glycaemic control rate. Secondary outcome measures: medication adherence, hypertension comorbidity and lifestyle factors.ResultsPatients with consistent adherence achieved significantly higher glycaemic control rates (39.6%, 95% CI 38.8% to 40.4%) compared with intermittent adherents (20.8%, 95% CI 19.9% to 21.7%, p<0.001). Conversely, male gender (aOR=0.879, 95% CI 0.813 to 0.950) and higher educational attainment (aOR=1.004, 95% CI 1.002 to 1.007) were positively associated with glycaemic control. Additionally, temporary residence status provided a marginal benefit (aOR=1.064, 95% CI 1.013 to 1.117), suggesting potential disparities in access to medical resources between urban and rural areas.ConclusionsWhile medication adherence remains fundamental for glycaemic control, its association with glycaemic control varied according to hypertension comorbidity and lifestyle factors. These findings advocate for integrated care models that simultaneously address cardiovascular risk factors and promote behavioural modifications, particularly in resource-constrained settings experiencing rapid epidemiological transitions.Keywords: diabetes mellitus, medication adherence, blood glucose control

Introduction

The global diabetes epidemic has entered a critical phase, with low- and middle-income countries bearing 79% of the disease burden yet achieving only 35% of optimal glycaemic control rates.1 China’s rapid epidemiological transition exemplifies this disparity: while national diabetes prevalence reached 11.2% in 2020, regional control rates in western provinces lag 15–20 percentage points behind eastern coastal areas.2 3 This geographical gradient underscores the urgent need to examine context-specific determinants of diabetes outcomes, particularly in regions where healthcare modernisation outpaces systemic capacity building. In the field of diabetes management, glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) is widely recognised as the gold standard for assessing long-term glycaemic control.4 Multiple studies spanning diverse populations and regions consistently demonstrate a statistically significant, moderate to strong positive correlation between Fasting Plasma Glucose (FPG) and HbA1c in individuals with type 2 diabetes. This implies that as FPG levels increase, HbA1c levels exhibit a corresponding upward trend.5

Among the factors influencing diabetes outcomes, medication adherence stands out as a cornerstone of effective disease management. Suboptimal adherence to glucose-lowering therapies has been consistently associated with higher risks of complications and mortality, with some studies reporting a 30%–50% increase in these adverse outcomes among non-adherent patients.6 However, despite its importance, much of the existing research on adherence has relied on cross-sectional designs or short-term follow-ups, which fail to capture the dynamic and longitudinal relationship between adherence patterns and glycaemic control trajectories.7 Furthermore, comorbid conditions, particularly hypertension, which affects over 60% of T2D patients, add another layer of complexity to glycaemic management. The synergistic effects of such comorbidities on glycaemic control, however, remain underexplored, particularly in resource-constrained settings.8

China’s vast geographical and healthcare disparities pose significant challenges to equitable diabetes management. Although national programmes like the Basic Public Health Service Project have enhanced diabetes screening coverage since 2016, implementation efficacy varies markedly across regions. Systematic evaluations of diabetes care initiatives remain scarce in western China, particularly in transitional zones like Luzhou City, Sichuan Province.9 This municipality of 5 million residents launched a high-risk chronic disease management programme in 2016, yet rigorous assessments of its long-term glycaemic control outcomes are lacking—a critical evidence gap hindering tailored intervention development for rapidly urbanising populations.

By the end of 2023, Luzhou’s permanent resident population stood at 4.267 million, with an urbanisation rate reaching 53.16%. The proportion of residents aged 60 and above exceeded 20%, indicating a pronounced ageing trend. Notably, Luzhou pioneered nationwide exploration of ‘universal preventive healthcare services’, achieving comprehensive coverage of resident health records and dynamic follow-up across the entire jurisdiction. This provided systematic, high-quality real-world data sources for this study. Luzhou’s experience garnered significant national attention and promotion, being shared at the 30th WHO Healthy Cities Conference.10 Its practices hold important pioneering and exemplary significance for comparable regions in western China.

This study addresses three persistent knowledge deficits in diabetes management research. First, predominant cross-sectional designs inadequately capture the temporal dynamics of glycaemic control essential for evaluating intervention sustainability. Second, the under-representation of western China in scholarly discourse constrains context-sensitive strategy formulation, given the region’s distinct sociocultural determinants of health. Third, the synergistic effects of prevalent comorbidities like hypertension with adherence patterns and lifestyle factors remain poorly characterised, especially in resource-constrained environments with fragmented healthcare delivery.

Leveraging a retrospective cohort of 30 508 T2D patients from Luzhou’s Health Information System (2018–2023), this study establishes three core objectives: (1) delineate longitudinal medication adherence-glycaemia relationships through serial fasting glucose measurements, (2) quantify modifying effects of hypertension and lifestyle factors across adherence strata and (3) generate stratified evidence to optimise diabetes management in underserved populations. By synthesising temporal and geographical specificity, this work provides evidence to inform both clinical guidelines and public health policies in regions confronting escalating diabetes burdens.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study used data from the Luzhou City Health Information Management System (LCHIMS), an integrated electronic health record platform that consolidates clinical data from primary care facilities, hospital admissions and follow-up visits across all public healthcare institutions in Luzhou City, Sichuan Province. The analysis encompassed data collected between 1 January 2018 and 31 December 2023. It should be clarified that the cohort for this study was drawn from Luzhou City’s universal preventive healthcare service programme, primarily covering T2D patients who voluntarily participated and received systematic management. It did not include individuals without established medical records or those lost to follow-up. Despite this bias, evaluating the effectiveness of the ‘universal preventive healthcare services’ programme—a major national public health initiative—necessarily requires targeting its intended population.

Study population

The study population consisted of adults aged ≥18 years with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes (ICD-10 E11) enrolled in the LCHIMS diabetes management programme during the study period. Inclusion criteria required documented baseline fasting blood glucose (FBG) measurements, complete demographic profiles and verifiable medication adherence records. Exclusion criteria included patients diagnosed with gestational diabetes (ICD-10 O24.4) or type 1 diabetes (ICD-10 E10), those with missing or incomplete adherence records, individuals managed solely through non-pharmacological interventions, subjects with follow-up durations shorter than 6 months and patients exhibiting severe cognitive impairment as defined by Mini-Mental State Examination scores below 18. Application of these criteria yielded a final analytical cohort of 30 508 patients. Figure 1 presents the inclusion flowchart for the study population.

Sample size calculation

Sample size determination was performed using PASS 2023 software (NCSS LLC), incorporating a two-sided significance level of 0.05, statistical power of 90%, estimated non-adherence prevalence of 30% from pilot studies and a minimum detectable OR of 1.2. These parameters indicated a required sample size of 28 721 participants. The final cohort of 30 508 patients exceeded this threshold by 6.2%, ensuring adequate statistical power for multivariable analyses.

Variables and definitions

The primary outcome of effective glycaemic control was operationalised as the transition from suboptimal FBG levels (>7.0 mmol/L) at baseline to within-target values (4.4-7.0 mmol/L) at final follow-up, consistent with the 2020 Chinese Guidelines for Type 2 Diabetes Prevention and Management. Medication adherence, the key exposure variable, was assessed by healthcare professionals during follow-up visits based on standard clinical evaluations and prescription renewal frequency within the system, categorised into consistent adherence (taking medication as prescribed (adherence ≥ 80%), meaning patients assessed as ‘regular’ demonstrate stable and timely renewal behaviour) and intermittent adherence (failure to take medication as prescribed, with insufficient frequency or dosage (adherence < 80%), encompassing all non-regular medication behaviours ranging from occasional missed doses to complete discontinuation). This dichotomisation was based on three primary considerations: First, the ≥ 80% threshold for defining ‘effective adherence’ is widely accepted and validated in diabetes management and prior research. Second, a primary aim was to identify and compare characteristics and outcomes across patient groups with differing adherence patterns. The categorical variable clearly demonstrates significant differences in demographic, clinical features and glycaemic control success rates between the ‘intermittent adherence’ group and the ‘consistent adherence’ group. Thirdly, employing categorical variables avoids making strong assumptions about the form of the relationship between adherence and outcomes (linear, logarithmic, S-shaped, etc.)

Covariates encompassed demographic characteristics (age, sex, education level, marital status, residency type), clinical parameters (Body Mass Index (BMI) categories, waist circumference measurements, hypertension and dyslipidaemia diagnoses) and lifestyle factors assessed through standardised instruments.

Residence type refers to a classification based on the relationship between an individual’s actual place of residence and their registered household registration location, primarily used to distinguish between permanent residency and temporary residency. Permanent residency refers to individuals who have actually resided in a particular area for 6 months or longer. Temporary residency denotes individuals whose place of residence differs from their registered household registration location and who have been away from their registered domicile for 6 months or longer. Glucose compliance: Assessment of whether the study population’s FBG met the preset target at study initiation. Meet the standard: FBG within the target range (4.4–7.0 mmol/L), indicating good glycaemic control at study initiation. Substandard: FBG>7.0 mmol/L, indicating poor glycaemic control at study initiation. Dietary control refers to the daily recommended intake of staple foods (in grams) calculated for diabetic patients by healthcare professionals in accordance with ‘Guideline for the prevention and treatment of diabetes mellitus in China (2024 edition)’, taking into account individual circumstances such as body weight, activity levels, glycaemic control targets and metabolic status. By comparing the patient’s actual staple food intake (in grams) as recorded in dietary logs or follow-up reports against the recommended intake, the assessment determines whether dietary control targets have been met. Compliance behaviour indicates whether patients adhere to medical guidance for lifestyle improvements. Healthcare professionals conduct subjective, comprehensive assessments during follow-up visits based on clinical judgement, categorising patient compliance as ‘good’, ‘fair’ or ‘poor’. ‘Good’ denotes high adherence to medical advice; ‘fair’ indicates partial compliance with notable omissions; ‘poor’ signifies frequent non-compliance with key recommendations.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using R statistical software (V.4.3.1). For continuous variables, normality is assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test supplemented by visual inspection of Q-Q plots and histograms. Continuous variables demonstrating normal distributions were expressed as means with SD and compared using independent Student’s t-tests. Non-normally distributed variables were summarised as medians with IQRs and analysed via Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies with percentages, employing χ2 tests for comparisons, with Fisher’s exact tests applied when cell counts fell below five observations.

Multivariable logistic regression models were developed to identify determinants of effective glycaemic control. The variable selection was guided primarily by clinical considerations and our prespecified conceptual framework, ensuring it was not a purely data-driven exercise. Candidate variables that demonstrated univariate associations at p<0.10 subsequently underwent stepwise selection, with entry and retention thresholds set at α=0.10 and α=0.05, respectively. This liberal entry criterion was chosen for our exploratory analysis to avoid prematurely excluding potentially relevant variables in our large sample. We interpret the results with caution, focusing our discussion on predictors with larger effect sizes and clinical significance to mitigate the risk of overinterpreting purely statistical findings. Final models reported adjusted ORs (aORs) with 95% CIs, incorporating all significant predictors while controlling for baseline characteristics.

Missing data were considered to be approximately meeting the MAR (missing at random) assumption based on the following assessments: First, the volume of missing data was minimal (<5% of total observations), and a systematic review indicated that these missing values were sporadically distributed across variables without clustering in specific subgroups. Second, to eliminate a key source of non-random missingness, patients with missing or incomplete adherence records were excluded from the analysis cohort, as this missingness directly relates to core study variables. Thirdly, under the MAR assumption, missing data were handled through fully conditional specification multiple imputation, generating ten complete datasets for analysis. Fourthly, sensitivity analyses comparing imputed and complete-case results confirmed model stability, with parameter estimates varying <10% across approaches.

Results

Study population characteristics

The analytical cohort comprised 30 508 type 2 diabetes patients followed from 2018 to 2023 (figure 2), with a median age of 62 years (IQR 54–69) and female predominance (56.65%, n=17 282). Most participants (62.91%) were aged 50–69 years, and 91.73% held permanent residency status in Luzhou City. Longitudinal educational attainment improvements were evident, with college-educated patients increasing from 2.99% to 7.66% over 6 years (χ²=523.107, p<0.001). Metabolic risk factors were prevalent: 43.06% had hypertension, and 71.28% exhibited abdominal obesity.

Stratified analysis revealed significant adherence-related differences (online supplemental table 1). Consistent adherents were younger (median 61 vs 64 years, p<0.001) with lower hypertension prevalence (39.60% vs 51.72%, p<0.001). Educational attainment gradients favoured the consistent adherence group.

Glycaemic control by medication adherence

Consistent medication adherence substantially improved glycaemic outcomes, with 39.6% (95% CI 38.8 to 40.4) achieving target fasting glucose versus 20.8% (95% CI 19.9 to 21.7) in intermittent adherents (χ²=967.311, p<0.001) (figure 3). Gender disparities persisted within adherence strata, with males demonstrating superior control rates (41.30% vs 38.21%, p<0.001). Among intermittent adherents, dietary compliance modified outcomes substantially—those maintaining good dietary control achieved 26.61% success versus 19.29% in non-compliant counterparts (p<0.001). Hypertension was particularly strongly associated with poorer outcomes in this group, with control rates being 23.63% in those without hypertension compared with 14.94% in those with hypertension (online supplemental table 2).

Factors associated with effective glycaemic control

Multivariate analysis revealed that suboptimal adherence constituted a significant barrier to glycaemic control, with particularly pronounced effects among intermittent medication adherents, whose success rate in achieving glycaemic control decreased by 29.4% (aOR=0.706, 95% CI 0.643 to 0.774). The associations of lifestyle factors with the outcome varied by medication adherence: often or occasional alcohol consumption increased the success rate among consistent medication adherents by 12.2% (aOR=1.122, 95% CI 1.044 to 1.205). For intermittent medication adherents, non-smoking status (aOR=1.234, 95% CI 1.050 to 1.451) and lack of regular exercise (aOR=1.375, 95% CI 1.228 to 1.540) similarly demonstrated such associations.

Protective factors included male gender (aOR=0.879, 95% CI 0.813 to 0.950) and educational level, with each additional year of schooling increasing the success rate by 0.4% (aOR=1.004, 95% CI 1.002 to 1.007). Temporary residence status conferred a marginal benefit (aOR=1.064, 95% CI 1.013 to 1.117), suggesting potential disparities in access to healthcare resources between urban and rural areas.

Notably, several traditional risk factors were positively associated with successful glycaemic control in this study. Concurrent hypertension consistently demonstrated a positive association among both consistent medication adherents (aOR=1.646, 95% CI 1.540 to 1.759) and intermittent medication adherents (aOR=1.737, 95% CI 1.533 to 1.968). Poor dietary control similarly demonstrated this pattern (consistent medication adherents: aOR=1.205, 95% CI 1.131 to 1.284; intermittent medication adherents: aOR=1.359, 95% CI 1.191 to 1.551). Regarding physical measurements considered risk factors, waist circumference exceeding the threshold also showed a marginal positive association among consistent medication adherents (aOR=1.081, 95% CI 1.005 to 1.163). Discussions

This study employs multivariate models to explore the complex factors influencing effective glycaemic control. These findings provide important insights for understanding therapeutic disparities in clinical practice and developing comprehensive management strategies. First, this study reaffirms the central role of medication adherence as a key determinant of glycaemic control. Suboptimal medication adherence, particularly among intermittent medication adherents, remains the most significant barrier to glycaemic control, reducing success rates by 29.4%—consistent with previous findings.11 12 However, hypertension and poor dietary habits—typically regarded as cardiovascular metabolic risk factors—both demonstrated a positive association with glycaemic control success in this study. This seemingly contradictory finding may reflect a ‘treatment intensity bias’ in clinical practice: diabetic patients with hypertension may receive more comprehensive and aggressive medical management, including more frequent follow-up monitoring and intensified treatment regimens, thereby partially improving glycaemic outcomes.13 14 Similarly, patients identified as having poor dietary control may consequently receive closer medical attention and more personalised lifestyle guidance, thereby improving their overall metabolic status.15 This finding suggests that intensified comprehensive management may yield positive clinical returns for patient groups with identified risk factors.

Regarding lifestyle factors, non-smoking status is associated with better glycaemic control (aOR=1.234, 95% CI 1.050 to 1.451), further underscoring the importance of smoking cessation in diabetes management.16 However, the positive association between lack of regular exercise and alcohol consumption with successful glycaemic control warrants cautious interpretation. These findings are likely influenced by complex behavioural comorbidity patterns and residual confounding. For instance, the lack of exercise may reflect ‘survivor bias’, where patients most in need of physical activity due to severe complications and disability were excluded from the study.17 Alternatively, these individuals may have adhered more strictly to other aspects (eg, diet) due to mobility limitations or heightened vigilance following diagnosis. Similarly, the association between alcohol consumption and effective glycaemic control may be confounded by drink type, quantity and accompanying sociopsychological factors.18 These findings do not endorse unhealthy behaviours but highlight that the observed association of a single health behaviour may be confounded by complex sociobehavioural factors in observational studies. Future research requires more precise measurement of these behaviours to clarify their true relationship with the outcome.

Sociodemographic factors also present a complex picture. The weak positive correlation between educational level and glycaemic control success underscores the foundational role of health literacy and self-management capabilities in chronic disease management.19 The association favouring residence status may reveal that, against the backdrop of China’s urbanisation, temporary residents gain access to more accessible and higher quality healthcare services by migrating to cities compared with rural areas with relatively scarce medical resources, thereby improving their prognosis.20 This finding holds significant policy implications for optimising primary healthcare resource allocation. Furthermore, gender disparities remain pronounced: even after adjusting for potential confounders, women’s glycaemic control rate remained significantly lower than men’s (38.21% vs 41.30%, p<0.001). This aligns with global trends, where women often face unique barriers due to family caregiving responsibilities, insufficient time for self-management and cultural biases surrounding chronic diseases.21 In rural China, social norms may further restrict women’s access to healthcare, exacerbating these disparities.22 Addressing these challenges requires targeted interventions. Community women’s health groups modelled after India’s accredited social health activist (ASHA) programme can provide peer support and education tailored to women’s needs.23 Furthermore, integrating diabetes care into maternal health services can enhance engagement among young women, who are more likely to access healthcare systems during this life stage.

The research findings offer transformative solutions for diabetes management in resource-constrained settings, highlighting three synergistic strategies: first, implementing integrated hypertension-diabetes care. Incorporating hypertension screening, fixed-dose combination therapies (such as metformin with angiotensin receptor blockers) and follow-up management into routine diabetes care effectively addresses the dual disease burden. This requires training primary healthcare providers in comorbidity management and establishing stable pharmaceutical supply chains in underserved areas. Second, implement precision-targeted adherence support. Differentiated interventions should address varying compliance levels: for intermittent adherents, artificial intelligence (AI)-enabled mobile health platforms (eg, WeChat reminders) provide intensive supervision; for consistent adherents with unhealthy lifestyles, community-based dietary education and exercise guidance should prioritise habit modification. Third, gender-responsive reforms must be implemented. These include extending outpatient service hours to accommodate caregiving schedules, deploying female community health workers and integrating diabetes services into maternal health programmes to optimise existing healthcare resources. These multidimensional interventions collectively address the interplay of biological, behavioural and structural factors affecting glycaemic control, providing actionable guidance for health systems navigating epidemiological transitions.

Luzhou stands as a representative case study in the urbanisation process of western China, embodying the typical characteristics of numerous rapidly developing small- and medium-sized cities. Of particular significance is Luzhou’s nationally pioneering implementation of universal preventive healthcare services. This initiative established a comprehensive health record and dynamic follow-up system covering the entire population, providing a replicable and practice-proven model for efficient chronic disease management in resource-constrained regions. Luzhou's approach has garnered high recognition at the national level and was shared as a notable practice at the World Health Organization's Healthy Cities Conference. Consequently, this study’s findings on the association between medication adherence and glycaemic control provide direct, reliable evidence-based guidance for refining chronic disease prevention strategies in comparable regions. Naturally, variations in policy implementation details, resource allocation and cultural aspects of residents’ health behaviours across different areas should be acknowledged. Further validation across regions with diverse development models is recommended for future research.

Limitations

Despite the strengths of this study (including its large population-based cohort sample), several limitations remain. First, given this study’s cross-sectional retrospective nature, the association between medication adherence and glycaemic control does not imply a causal relationship. The findings should be regarded as a basis for generating hypotheses for future prospective or interventional studies. Second, the measurement and definition of medication adherence in this study have several limitations. Using a categorical definition for adherence assessment carries a potential risk of overestimating actual adherence. Furthermore, our findings on adherence represent the aggregated effect of undifferentiated classes of antidiabetic therapies. The inability to analyse drug category-specific adherence patterns constrains the interpretation of results. This dichotomous measurement may also dilute the true strength of the association between adherence and glycaemic control. Nevertheless, the study design incorporated conservative parameters for sample size estimation, ensuring sufficient power to detect a statistically significant and clinically meaningful association under the current operational definition. Third, glycaemic control was assessed using FBG rather than HbA1c due to the absence of HbA1c records in the database (LCHIMS). This approach presents limitations because relying solely on FBG measurements at baseline and final follow-up may not fully reflect the dynamic trends in glycaemic control. Furthermore, compared with HbA1c, single FBG measurements exhibit greater variability, which may compromise the accuracy of assessing glycaemic control status. Future prospective studies should prioritise the use of HbA1c, combined with continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) technology, to more accurately characterise glucose fluctuation patterns.24 Fourth, the use of self-reported lifestyle variables may introduce recall bias. Fifth, the absence of psychosocial variables (such as depression and social support), broader socioeconomic factors and healthcare accessibility variables represents a significant gap, as these factors are well-known mediators of medication adherence and glycaemic outcomes.25 Finally, selection bias exists in this study, as it primarily included voluntary participants receiving systematic management while excluding those with missing records or lost to follow-up, potentially limiting the generalisability of findings. However, it also provides unique internal validity evidence for assessing the effectiveness of such nationwide grassroots public health interventions.

Conclusions

While medication adherence is paramount for glycaemic control, its association with glycaemic control is subject to complex relationships with hypertension-related comorbidities, lifestyle factors and sociodemographic considerations. Achieving effective glycaemic control necessitates moving beyond isolated medication management towards integrated care models that incorporate coordinated management of comorbidities, behavioural interventions and social support. This is particularly crucial in resource-constrained settings undergoing rapid epidemiological transitions.
