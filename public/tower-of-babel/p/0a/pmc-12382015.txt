A heart failure classification model from radial artery pulse wave using LSTM neural networks
Authors: Yi Lyu, Wen-Yue Huang, Hai-Mei Wu, Jing Hong, Yi-Qin Wang, Hai-Xia Yan, Jin Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure (HF) represents a pressing global health issue demanding innovative and accessible approaches for early detection. Non-invasive, rapid, and cost-effective techniques utilizing deep learning (DL) hold significant promise for addressing this challenge.MethodsThis study included 462 participants categorized into healthy, coronary artery disease (CAD), and HF groups. Raw radial artery pulse wave data underwent preprocessing, including denoising, normalization, and SMOTE-based balancing. Four deep learning algorithms adept at handling sequential data – Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM), Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU), and Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM) – were subsequently applied and evaluated for their classification performance. A 10-fold cross-validation strategy was employed to ensure a robust evaluation of model performance and stability.ResultsTo robustly evaluate performance and stability, a 10-fold cross-validation was performed. The LSTM model yielded the highest mean accuracy (0.8595 ± 0.0522) and demonstrated strong performance across other key metrics, including a high Area Under the Curve, establishing it as the most effective model in this study. To enhance model interpretability, the SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) framework was utilized to determine global feature importance and explain the final LSTM model’s predictions.ConclusionOur findings strongly suggest that an LSTM-based model analyzing radial artery pulse waves can effectively differentiate between healthy, CAD, and HF states. This approach represents a simple, non-invasive, and cost-effective methodology with significant potential as a valuable strategy to aid in the early screening and detection of HF. Further investigation and broader clinical validation are warranted to confirm the robustness and real-world applicability of this promising tool.

Introduction

Heart failure (HF) is a clinical status consisting of cardinal symptoms due to a structural and/or functional abnormality of the heart [1, 2]. It is a common complication of coronary artery disease (CAD) [3]. Chronic ischemia leads to the progression of left ventricular remodeling and systolic dysfunction that provide the substrate for HF development [4].Despite considerable improvements and significant ongoing efforts to treat and manage HF, the overall incidence is still increasing due to ageing [2]. Besides, mortality and hospitalization rates among HF patients remain poor [5, 6]. Given the growing HF burden, HF prevention and management are a major public health concern [7]. However, the diagnosis of HF often requires specialized equipment and skilled operators, which can be limiting in resource-constrained settings. Therefore, there is a pressing need for improvement in diagnostic methods facilitating early detection and intervention.

Radial artery pulse diagnosis is an integral component of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) diagnosis and one of the four diagnostic methods in TCM. It involves a practitioner using their fingers to palpate the superficial arteries at specific points on a patient’s body to assess the pulse and understand the patient’s condition and differentiate various diseases. Radial artery pulse diagnosis holds a significant position in both TCM theory and practice due to its close relationship with the heart, blood vessels, and the functions of the internal organs, making it a potential tool for early HF diagnosis. The pulse pattern can convey physiological and pathological information from various parts of the body, providing crucial evidence for diagnosing diseases and differentiating their severity [8–12], allowing TCM to integrate more seamlessly with modern medical practices [13].

Machine learning (ML) is a subset of artificial intelligence that focuses on building systems capable of learning from data, identifying patterns, and making decisions with minimal human intervention. Because of its unlimited abilities, ML is gaining popularity in various sectors [14–17]. Deep Learning (DL), a new subfield of ML, has achieved significant breakthroughs in various applications such as speech recognition and computer vision. By mimicking the neural connections of the human brain, deep learning models can hierarchically represent data features through multiple transformation stages, enabling the interpretation of complex data like images, sounds, and text.

Despite advancements in quantitative pulse wave analysis and DL, significant challenges remain in leveraging radial artery pulse signals for multi-class cardiovascular disease diagnosis. Existing studies predominantly focus on binary classification (e.g., healthy vs. disease) [18–20], failing to address the clinical need to distinguish overlapping pathologies such as CAD and HF, which often coexist and share similar pulse wave morphologies. Besides, traditional ML approaches rely on handcrafted features that may not fully capture subtle temporal variations in pulse waves, particularly in HF cases characterized by rapid ejection phases and fused main-pre-beat waves. This study hypothesized that HF patients exhibit unique time-domain signatures due to impaired ventricular ejection, distinguishable from CAD and healthy groups. And a case-control study was designed to explore whether DL with sequential learning capabilities could outperform ML in classifying pulse waves by automatically extracting latent temporal dependencies.

The primary objective of this study is therefore not to propose a new DL algorithm, but to conduct a systematic evaluation of established DL models to assess their feasibility and effectiveness for the multi-class classification of cardiovascular conditions using non-invasively acquired radial artery pulse waves.

Methods

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shanghai University of TCM (Approval Number:2023-3-10-08-08). All participating recruitment sites, including Shanghai Municipal Hospital of TCM, Shuguang Hospital, Yueyang Hospital, and Longhua Hospital, are affiliated hospitals of the university. This centralized IRB approval was subsequently submitted to and formally recognized by each participating hospital, which granted site-specific permission to recruit participants and collect data. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Study subjects

This case-control study comprised three groups: a healthy group of 202 individuals, a CAD group of 187 patients, and an HF group of 73 patients. All participants were recruited from Shanghai Municipal Hospital of TCM, Shuguang Hospital, Yueyang Hospital, and Longhua Hospital, all affiliated with the Shanghai University of TCM, from September 2019 to December 2021.

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria, based on a thorough review of participants’ official medical records or comprehensive health reports, were as follows:

CAD patients

Patients were included if their existing medical records contained a formal diagnosis of CAD made by an attending physician. This diagnosis had to be supported by clinical evidence that met the ‘Nomenclature and criteria for diagnosis of ischemic heart disease‘ [21].

HF patients

Patients were included in the HF group if their medical records confirmed a diagnosis of HF (New York Heart Association [NYHA] Functional Classification III or IV [22]) made by a cardiologist. This diagnosis had to be compliant with the ‘Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Heart Failure 2024‘ [23] and supported by relevant clinical data.

Healthy participants

Participants in the healthy group were recruited from physical examination center. Inclusion required a comprehensive health check-up report confirming the absence of any known cardiovascular disease. This verification was based on a report that included, at a minimum, a normal anteroposterior chest X-ray, and no self-reported history of cardiovascular conditions.

Collection of pulse wave

A SmartTCM-1 pulse wave digital acquisition analyzer was used to record radial artery pulse wave signals from participants’ left wrist. The SmartTCM-1 contains a general-purpose signal acquisition device, the working principle is based on mechanical pressure measurement. Key technical specifications include a pulse pressure measurement range of 0–40 kPa (0–300 mmHg) and a pulse rate measurement range of 40–250 bpm. During its development and validation phase, it was compared with standard pulse pressure monitors and ECG, demonstrating a pulse pressure error of ± 10% and a pulse rate error of ± 3 bpm, which confirms the reliability of the acquired signals.

Before pulse wave collection, the participants should sit or lie still for at least 30 min. During the collection, participants should assume either a seated or supine position with the forearm extended horizontally forward at heart level, palm up, fingers naturally curved, and wrist straight. A soft pulse pillow should be placed under the wrist to maintain local blood flow and facilitate pulse wave detection. Sampling time is 1 min with a sampling frequency of 720 Hz. After collecting, PulseSystem software and PulseAnalyseGraphic v1.1 software were employed to denoise, extract, and process the data.

Data normalization

The raw data underwent min-max normalization, also known as the standardization of deviation, it is a linear transformation of the original data, which maps the resulting values to the range of [0–1].

Data balance processing

Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE) [25] is a widely-used technique for addressing imbalanced datasets. By generating new synthetic samples to increase the number of instances in the minority class, SMOTE helps to balance the class distribution. Unlike simple replication, SMOTE employs a linear interpolation method to create new samples, mitigating the risk of overfitting. The synthetic samples will be randomly assigned to both the training and validation sets, while the test set will remain unchanged [26].

Deep learning classification

ML covers various knowledge and technologies such as probability theory, statistics, and complex algorithms [27–29]. DL is a specific type of ML that uses artificial neural networks with multiple layers to learn complex patterns from large amounts of data. These neural networks are inspired by the structure and function of the human brain. Traditional ML algorithms may be more suitable for smaller datasets or simpler problems. However, as computing power and data availability have increased, DL has become the dominant approach in many areas of ML.

This study used 4 DL algorithms such as Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) [30], Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) [31], and Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM).

LSTM is a type of recurrent neural network (RNN) architecture that is specifically designed to learn long-term dependencies in sequential data. Unlike traditional RNNs, LSTMs have a special cell state that can store information for long periods, making them well-suited for tasks. The core of LSTM lies in its unique memory cell structure, which controls the flow of information through three gates (input gate, forget gate, and output gate), effectively addressing the vanishing gradient problem in traditional RNNs. LSTM is particularly well-suited for analyzing sequential data like radial artery pulse waves. Its architecture, featuring input, forget, and output gates controlling a dedicated cell state, allows it to effectively learn long-term dependencies. This is crucial for pulse wave analysis, enabling the model to capture subtle, temporally distant patterns and morphological characteristics across the cardiac cycle that differentiate healthy, CAD, and HF states, while mitigating the vanishing gradient problem common in simpler RNNs.

The dataset was split into train set, validation set, and test set in the ratio of 60:20:20. A validation set is a portion of a dataset used to assess model performance during training, aiding in hyperparameter tuning and overfitting prevention. To ensure a robust evaluation of model performance and stability, we employed a 10-fold cross-validation strategy. The entire dataset was randomly partitioned into 10 equally sized folds. In each of the 10 iterations, one fold was reserved as the test set for final evaluation, while the remaining nine folds were used for model training and validation. The process was repeated until every fold served as the test set exactly once. The final performance metrics reported are the mean and standard deviation calculated from the results of the 10 folds. This approach provides a more reliable estimate of the model’s generalizability compared to a single, static train-test split [32].

Classification metrics

The selection of Accuracy, Precision, Recall (Sensitivity), Specificity, F1-Score, and Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) was deliberate to provide a comprehensive assessment of the model’s performance, particularly given the clinical implications of classifying cardiovascular conditions like CAD and HF:

Accuracy

Provides an overall measure of correct classifications (Healthy, CAD, HF). While useful, it can be less informative in cases of class imbalance, hence the need for additional metrics.

Precision

This metric is clinically relevant as it indicates the proportion of correctly identified positive cases among all instances predicted as positive. For example, high precision for the HF class means that if the model predicts a patient has HF, that prediction is likely correct, minimizing false positive diagnoses that could lead to unnecessary tests or treatments.

Recall (Sensitivity)

This is critically important in a diagnostic setting as it measures the model’s ability to correctly identify actual positive cases (e.g., patients who truly have HF or CAD). High recall minimizes the risk of false negatives (missed diagnoses), ensuring patients needing intervention are identified. Our results analysis specifically discusses recall for each class.

Specificity

This measures the model’s ability to correctly identify true negatives (e.g., healthy individuals). High specificity is important to avoid falsely classifying healthy individuals as having a disease.

F1-Score

As the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall, the F1-score provides a single metric that balances the trade-off between minimizing false positives and minimizing false negatives. This is valuable when both types of errors have significant clinical consequences.

AUC

This metric evaluates the model’s overall ability to discriminate between the different classes across all possible classification thresholds. A high AUC, like the 0.93365 achieved by our LSTM model, indicates strong overall diagnostic potential in distinguishing between the groups (Healthy vs. CAD, Healthy vs. HF, CAD vs. HF).

Evaluating all these metrics together provides a holistic view of the model’s clinical potential and limitations as a diagnostic aid.

Receiver operating characteristic curve

The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve is a graphical representation used to evaluate the diagnostic performance of a binary classification model. It plots the TP rate (sensitivity) against the FP rate (1-specificity) at various threshold settings. The AUC quantifies the model’s ability to discriminate between positive and negative classes, with values ranging from 0 to 1. An AUC of 0.5 indicates no discrimination (equivalent to random guessing), while an AUC of 1.0 signifies perfect discrimination. The ROC curve serves as a critical tool for assessing the trade-offs between sensitivity and specificity, thereby aiding in the selection of optimal thresholds for clinical decision-making [33, 34].

Model interpretability analysis

To enhance the transparency and explainability of our final LSTM model, we employed the SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) framework [35]. SHAP is a game-theoretic approach used to explain the output of any machine learning model by assigning each feature an importance value for a particular prediction. It calculates how much each feature contributes to pushing the model’s output from the base value to the final prediction. We calculated the mean absolute SHAP values for each feature across all samples in the test set to determine the global feature importance and understand which pulse wave parameters had the most significant impact on the model’s overall predictions.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the promising potential of DLodels, particularly LSTM, in analyzing radial artery pulse waves for the classification of CAD and HF. While not yet a clinically validated tool, this approach presents a simple, non-invasive, and cost-effective method for potential early HF detection, meriting further investigation. This study employed four DL algorithms to investigate the radial artery pulse wave characteristics of healthy, CAD, and HF participants. Table 1 demonstrates that LSTM achieved the highest classification performance, with an accuracy of 0.8587, precision of 0.87448, recall of 0.82164, F1-score of 0.83773, specificity of 0.92369, and AUC of 0.93365. Table 2 demonstrates that LSTM still achieved the highest accuracy of 0.8595 ± 0.0522 among four algorithms using 10-fold cross-validation. This approach presents a simple, non-invasive, and cost-effective method for potential early HF detection. Future research will focus on external validation to further validate the model’s applicability in diverse populations and settings. Longitudinal studies will also be conducted to evaluate the model’s ability to track changes in cardiovascular health over time.

Discussion

As mentioned in the “Data balance prcessing” section of the manuscript, we utilized SMOTE to address the class imbalance present in our original dataset. The original dataset consisted of 202 healthy individuals, 187 CAD patients, and 73 HF patients, highlighting an underrepresentation of the HF class. SMOTE works by generating new synthetic samples specifically for the minority class(es). Instead of simply duplicating existing minority samples (which can lead to overfitting), SMOTE creates synthetic instances by using linear interpolation between existing minority samples and their nearest neighbors. In our methodology, these synthetic samples generated by SMOTE were added to the training and validation sets only. The test set remained unchanged and consisted solely of original, unbalanced data to ensure our final model evaluation reflects performance on real-world data distributions. The primary goal of applying SMOTE was to mitigate the potential bias towards the majority classes during the model training phase and to reduce the risk of overfitting associated with imbalanced data. By synthetically increasing the representation of the HF class in the training and validation data, we aimed to enable the deep learning models (especially LSTM) to learn the distinguishing features of this critical minority class more effectively. While the final performance metrics reported (Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1, Specificity, AUC) were calculated on the unbalanced test set to provide a realistic assessment of the model’s generalization capability, the balancing of the training data is crucial for achieving this performance. Without balancing, the models might have performed poorly in identifying HF cases (lower recall for the HF class) due to insufficient exposure during training. Furthermore, as illustrated in Fig. 10, the t-SNE visualization comparing the original and synthetic datasets demonstrates that the SMOTE method successfully generated synthetic data that maintained the underlying distributional characteristics and cluster structures of the original data, supporting the fidelity of the balancing approach.

The SHAP analysis provides valuable insights that move our model beyond a ‘black-box’ prediction system. The high importance of features related to the timing and amplitude of the tidal and dicrotic waves (e.g., tf/6/tmax, t3/t1, and tmax) suggests the model is learning hemodynamically relevant patterns. For example, the prominence of t3/t1 in identifying HF patients may reflect the altered wave reflections and reduced arterial compliance that are often characteristic of heart failure. This ability to identify and rank the contribution of specific pulse wave features not only increases confidence in the model’s predictions but also aligns with clinical pathophysiology, enhancing its potential for real-world application.

While the SmartTCM-1 acquisition device has been validated for signal fidelity, this study did not include a direct, simultaneous comparison against the diagnostic gold standards for HF and CAD (e.g., coronary angiography) or provide its regulatory approval status. This should be considered when evaluating the model’s immediate clinical applicability, and its performance on data from other acquisition systems remains unevaluated.

A critical analysis of our model’s performance reveals a key issue: despite strong overall metrics, the model exhibited a lower recall (sensitivity) for the HF class compared to the Healthy and CAD groups. From a clinical perspective, this is a significant concern, as a lower recall translates to a higher risk of false negatives. This could potentially lead to missed diagnoses and delayed critical interventions for high-risk patients with advanced HF.

Several factors may contribute to this result. First, although the SMOTE technique was used to address data imbalance, the HF group was the smallest minority class in the original dataset. This may have limited the diversity of unique HF pulse wave patterns available for the model to learn, affecting its generalization. Second, Heart Failure is a complex clinical syndrome, and the patients in NYHA Classes III and IV included in this study likely present with significant pathophysiological heterogeneity, resulting in more varied pulse wave signals that are harder to unify into a single pattern. Finally, given that CAD is a common etiology for HF, it is possible that the pulse wave characteristics of advanced CAD overlap with those of HF, leading to some misclassification between these two groups.

Therefore, future work must prioritize improving the model’s sensitivity for HF detection. Potential strategies include targeted collection of more HF data, exploring advanced feature engineering specific to HF hemodynamics, or employing cost-sensitive learning algorithms that apply a higher penalty to the misclassification of this critical patient group.

HF diagnosis may be achieved through the analysis of electrocardiography, magnetic resonance imaging, ultrasound images as well as electronic health records [41–43]. These methods, however, are limited due to the requirement for specialized equipment and skilled operators. The ability to identify HF using only radial artery pulse wave could be used for early diagnosis and would enable referral for further investigation, offering a noninvasive, cost-effective solution. Wu et al. evaluated the potential of wrist pulse signals for use in the cardiac monitoring of patients with coronary heart disease (CHD). Decision tree (DT) and random forest (RF) algorithms were employed to construct models for classifying CHD patients with different brain natriuretic peptides. The pulse features of the three groups differed significantly, suggesting different pathological states of the cardiovascular system in patients with CHD. Moreover, the RF models outperformed the DT models in performance metrics [44]. Wu et al. proposed a ML-based strategy to identify left ventricular enlargement (LVE) in HF patients by means of pulse wave signals by means of classification and regression models. The classification model exhibited superior performance with an accuracy of 0.91 and an AUC-ROC of 0.93. The regression model achieved an accuracy of 0.88 and an AUC-ROC of 0.89, indicating that both models can quickly and accurately identify LVE in HF patients [45]. In 2023, Yan et al. utilized RF, Support Vector Machine, k-nearest neighbor, and DT algorithms to develop classification models using microcirculatory characteristic parameter set. The results showed that RF showed good classification performance, the identification accuracy of the model built on the microcirculatory characteristic parameter set and RF algorithm all reached more than 88%. The highest recognition accuracy was 95.51% for coronary heart disease samples, 92.11% for healthy samples, and 88.55% for hypertensive samples. These research demonstrated radial artery pulse waves as a key informational conduit within the cardiovascular system and ML as a more suitable method to deal with complex wrist pulse data.

This work advances non-invasive cardiovascular diagnostics by leveraging DL for automated radial artery pulse wave analysis, incorporating novel feature engineering, and offering potential integration with TCM perspectives. While previous studies have often utilized traditional ML techniques like DT and RF for pulse wave analysis, this study is among the first to systematically apply and compare multiple DL algorithms (LSTM, CNN, GRU, Bi-LSTM) for the challenging multi-class classification of healthy individuals, CAD patients, and HF patients. This approach allows for a more extensive understanding of how pulse wave morphology varies across these distinct cardiovascular states. Furthermore, the introduction and utilization of novel pulse wave features, specifically moving averages and the time points t0.8 and t0.9, enhanced the morphological resolution of the analysis, enabling the model to detect subtle signatures potentially missed by standard parameters or manual TCM palpation. By employing these DL techniques, particularly LSTM, our study achieves superior classification performance compared to typical results reported for traditional ML in similar diagnostic tasks, while also potentially mitigating overfitting and enhancing model scalability.

The superior performance of the LSTM model in this study strongly aligns with its inherent capability to model temporal dependencies, which is critical for analyzing sequential pulse wave data. Unlike architectures like CNNs that prioritize spatial hierarchies, LSTMs excel at capturing dynamic, phase-specific characteristics within the pulse cycle (e.g., systolic upstroke, diastolic decay) that correlate closely with underlying cardiac function. This is reflected in the high performance achieved (Accuracy 0.8587, AUC 0.93365), contrasting favorably with prior pulse-based diagnosis studies using traditional ML models where AUC values rarely surpassed 0.90. When comparing the DL models tested, while LSTM demonstrated the highest accuracy, it also incurred the longest training time; CNN offered computational efficiency but lower accuracy. Importantly, all models achieved rapid inference times suitable for real-time applications. Given its superior performance across the comprehensive suite of metrics, LSTM emerges as the preferred DL model for this specific classification task. Future optimization might involve exploring reduced LSTM architectures to balance performance and computational cost.

The clinical significance of developing accurate, non-invasive diagnostic tools for HF and CAD is immense, given that early diagnosis can significantly reduce patient mortality and morbidity. Wide efforts are underway to develop diagnostic algorithms. While ML techniques like Logistic Regression, SVM, Naïve Bayes, and Random Forest have shown commendable performance in diagnosing heart disease from various datasets, DL is increasingly applied to complex clinical data to overcome limitations of traditional methods, such as insufficient model capability or dependence on prior feature engineering. Studies using DL on clinical profiles or ECG data have shown high accuracy in predicting HF categories or identifying therapy nonresponders, highlighting DL’s ability to capture subtle patterns. However, many existing methods rely on data from ECG, MRI, ultrasound, or extensive EHRs, which require specialized equipment or infrastructure. Our approach, utilizing only the easily accessible radial artery pulse wave, offers a potentially disruptive non-invasive and cost-effective solution, particularly suitable for primary care screening or resource-limited settings. This work also builds upon previous studies that demonstrated the value of pulse wave analysis using ML for assessing cardiac function in CHD or identifying left ventricular enlargement, further validating the richness of information contained within the pulse wave and extending the analytical power through DL. Additionally, the potential correlation between objective pulse wave features identified by DL and traditional TCM pulse diagnosis concepts could aid the modernization and international development of TCM.

A core value of this research lies in its potential as a clinical decision support tool, particularly in primary care or resource-limited settings. Our model could be integrated into a portable, low-cost device to serve as a rapid point-of-care screening tool. A use-case scenario can be envisioned where a nurse or health assistant in a community clinic performs a one-minute, non-invasive pulse wave acquisition on residents during a routine check-up. The model’s output would not be a direct “diagnosis,” but rather an actionable risk stratification recommendation (e.g., “low risk” vs. “referral recommended”). This information would support the primary care physician in making a more informed triage decision—specifically, to refer high-risk individuals to a cardiologist for more definitive testing (e.g., echocardiography), thereby optimizing the allocation of scarce medical resources and facilitating early detection of cardiovascular disease.

Furthermore, this research serves as a potential foundation for developing wearable devices designed for cardiovascular abnormality monitoring. In contrast to the “snapshot” data collected in a clinical setting, a low-power version of the model could be deployed on consumer-grade devices like smartwatches or health bands to perform continuous, long-term monitoring of a user’s pulse wave. By leveraging the Internet of Things (IoT) and mobile health (mHealth) technologies, such a system could analyze data in real-time and provide alerts to the user or their caretaker upon detecting changes in pulse wave patterns that may indicate deteriorating cardiovascular health. This aligns with the vision of proactive, personalized health management through big data and artificial intelligence.

In summary, the primary informatics contribution of this work is not merely the classification accuracy itself, but the demonstration of how a complex biosignal (the radial artery pulse wave) can be translated into a simple, actionable data point to support the clinical workflow. It transforms an abstract risk assessment problem into a concrete decision support recommendation (i.e., “refer or not refer”). This approach of linking data analysis directly to a clinical decision pathway is a core theme in medical informatics and decision support research and highlights the practical value of our study.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations should be acknowledged regarding the present study. Firstly, the dataset, while providing valuable initial insights, is constrained by its size (N = 462) and scope, having been collected from affiliated hospitals of a single university in Shanghai. This may limit the direct generalizability of our findings to more diverse demographic groups, varying comorbidities, or different healthcare systems. Secondly, all pulse wave data were acquired using a single type of device (SmartTCM-1); consequently, the model’s performance with data from other acquisition systems remains unevaluated. Thirdly, this study lacks external validation using an independent cohort from a different clinical setting or population, which is essential for confirming the model’s robustness and real-world applicability. Fourthly, the case-control, cross-sectional design limits our ability to infer causality or track longitudinal changes in cardiovascular health. Additionally, the HF group was restricted to patients with NYHA Class III and IV, potentially limiting the applicability of the findings to individuals with milder forms of HF. Finally, while conducted in a controlled research environment, further validation in real-world clinical settings is necessary to assess performance under diverse practical conditions. These limitations highlight important areas for future research to build upon the current findings.

Future work and perspectives

Future research should focus on addressing the limitations identified previously to enhance clinical translatability. Key directions include expanding the dataset size and diversity, conducting rigorous external validation across different populations and clinical settings, and evaluating the model’s performance using data from various pulse wave acquisition devices. Exploring the impact of increasing the sampling frequency (currently 720 Hz) and optimizing model architecture for computational efficiency without sacrificing performance are also important next steps. Longitudinal studies are needed to assess the model’s ability to track changes in cardiovascular health over time. Finally, validation in real-world clinical environments is crucial to assess performance under diverse conditions and confirm clinical utility. Successfully addressing these points will be pivotal in realizing the potential of this DL-based pulse wave analysis approach as a transformative tool for early HF/CAD detection, particularly in underserved populations. Furthermore, while we compared four established architectures, future studies should evaluate more recent models, such as Transformer-based networks or hybrid CNN-LSTM frameworks, which have shown promise in other time-series analysis tasks. Such comparisons would be essential to further advance the state of the art for pulse wave-based diagnosis.
