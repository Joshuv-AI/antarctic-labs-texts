Perigastric Angioleiomyoma: A Rare Case Report
Authors: Kyeong Ah Jang, Ho Seung Lee
Published: 2026

Case: Angioleiomyoma is a rare benign tumor composed of blood vessels and smooth muscle that is commonly found in the skin or lower extremities, but its occurrence within the abdominal cavity is exceedingly rare, and limited reports are available. This paper presents the diagnosis and treatment of an angioleiomyoma located near the stomach in a 64-year-old woman who presented with abdominal pain.

A 64-year-old female patient presented with abdominal pain. Abdominal ultrasound revealed a mass-like lesion measuring ~1.7 cm in diameter located near the stomach. The patient had a medical history of hypertension that was controlled with medication, and the physical examination revealed no other relevant findings. The blood tests showed the following results: white blood cell count 6170/uL, hemoglobin 12.9 g/dL, platelets 279,000/uL, C-reactive protein 0.32 mg/L, carbohydrate antigen 19-9 8.9 U/mL, and carcinoembryonic antigen 1.83 mg/mL, with no other notable abnormalities.

A lesion measuring approximately 1.7 cm with contrast enhancement was observed at the lesser curvature side of the gastric antrum on abdominal computed tomography (CT) (Fig. 1). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a lymph node of a similar size at the same location that displayed high signal intensity on T2-weighted imaging (T2WI). The arterial phase contrast enhancement was observed on post-contrast T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), but no diffusion restriction was observed (Fig. 2). Based on the CT and MRI findings, differential diagnoses, including Castleman's disease, hemangioma, or gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST), were considered.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography findings. Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT demonstrating a 1.7-cm enhancing lesion located outside the stomach along the lesser curvature.

Magnetic resonance imaging findings of the lesion. (A) T2-weighted imaging showing relatively high-signal intensity compared with the surrounding tissues. (B) T1-weighted arterial phase image showing early arterial enhancement of the lesion. (C) Delayed phase image showing persistent enhancement.

An endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided biopsy was performed for an accurate diagnosis. On EUS, a hypoechoic cystic lesion approximately 1.5 cm in size was observed around the stomach, with developed vascular structures (feeding vessels) (Fig. 3). An EUS-guided core needle biopsy was performed three times using a 22-gauge needle, safely avoiding the surrounding blood vessels. The biopsy results revealed fragmented muscle tissue (Fig. 4A). Subsequently, the patient was referred for a surgical excision of the mass, and the pathological examination of the excised specimen confirmed the diagnosis of angioleiomyoma (Fig. 4B).

Endoscopic ultrasound findings. Endoscopic ultrasound showing a hypoechoic cystic lesion measuring approximately 1.5 cm adjacent to the stomach, with internal vascularity identified on color Doppler imaging, suggestive of feeding vessels.

Histopathological findings consistent with angiomyolipoma. (A) EUS-guided biopsy pathology: Hematoxylin and eosin staining at ×100 magnification showing fragmented smooth muscle tissue. (B) Surgical pathology: Hematoxylin and eosin staining at ×200 magnification showing spindle-shaped smooth muscle cells arranged around vascular structures.

Diagnosis: Angioleiomyoma

Angioleiomyoma is a benign tumor composed of blood vessels and smooth muscle, typically arising from the tunica media of blood vessels.1 This tumor can occur in any area containing smooth muscle, and according to Enzinger and Weiss (1995), 95% of leiomyomas are found in the uterus, 3% in the skin, and only 1.5% in the gastrointestinal tract.2,3 Among the gastrointestinal sites, angioleiomyomas most commonly occur in the colon (44%), ileum (37%), and duodenum (19%).4 Its occurrence in the abdominal cavity, particularly around the stomach, is extremely rare, with no cases reported in the literature. The pathogenesis of angioleiomyoma in this location is unclear, but it has been hypothesized to arise from the vascular smooth muscle of the gastric wall or adjacent blood vessels.5,6

The present patient presented with nonspecific abdominal pain, and imaging studies played an important role in characterizing the lesion. The abdominal CT and MRI findings suggested a well-defined, contrast-enhancing lesion outside the stomach along the lesser curvature, leading to an initial differential diagnosis that included Castleman’s disease, hemangioma, or GIST. The lesion exhibited arterial phase contrast enhancement on T1WI and high signal intensity on T2WI, imaging characteristics that may overlap with those of other hypervascular tumors. EUS with Doppler imaging contributed to lesion characterization by revealing a hypoechoic cystic lesion with feeding vessels, raising the suspicion of a vascular tumor. Considering the difficulty of establishing a definitive diagnosis solely on imaging, EUS-guided core needle biopsy was performed, which initially showed fragmented muscle tissue without clear diagnostic features. A surgical resection was necessary to reach a definitive diagnosis because the biopsy findings were inconclusive.

A histopathological examination of the excised specimen confirmed a diagnosis of angioleiomyoma, with spindle-shaped smooth muscle cells surrounding blood vessels, a hallmark feature of this tumor. Immunohistochemical staining is often helpful in distinguishing angioleiomyoma from other mesenchymal tumors such as GIST because angioleiomyomas typically stain positive for smooth muscle actin and desmin but negative for c-kit (CD117) and DOG1, which are markers for GIST. Morimoto classified angioleiomyomas into three histological subtypes (capillary, cavernous, and venous) based on the vascular and smooth muscle components.7 In the present case, spindle-shaped smooth muscle cells were regularly arranged, either surrounding or between blood vessels, allowing classification as a venous-type angioleiomyoma.

Despite its benign nature, surgical excision is the preferred treatment for angioleiomyoma, particularly in symptomatic cases, because it provides symptom relief and histological confirmation. Recurrence is extremely rare after a complete resection, and the long-term prognosis is excellent.8,9 This case highlights the importance of a multimodal diagnostic approach, including CT, MRI, EUS, and histopathological confirmation for evaluating rare gastric-adjacent tumors. Although imaging can suggest possible differential diagnoses, a definitive diagnosis often requires a histopathological examination, underscoring the need for biopsy or surgical excision in uncertain cases. Considering the rarity of abdominal angioleiomyoma, further case reports and studies will be needed to improve the understanding of its clinical and radiological characteristics.
