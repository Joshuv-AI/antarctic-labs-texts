Psychological distress and caregiver burden among caregivers of children with chronic liver disease: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Mohammed Jamal Junainah, Nawaf Ali Mansour, Rayan Faisal Halabi, Nawaf Mohammad Badawood, Nader Lafi Aljohani, Saeed Saleh Alghamdi, Abdulghani Muhyialdeen Alansari, Khouloud Abdulrahman Al-Sofyani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCaregivers of children with chronic liver disease (CLD) face substantial psychosocial demands, yet evidence outside transplant settings, especially in low- and middle-income contexts, remains limited. This study assessed caregiver burden, stress, and caregiving attitudes, and explored caregiver- and patient-related predictors of high burden.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted at a tertiary hepatology/transplant center between January and April 2025. Thirty-six family caregivers completed interviewer-administered questionnaires, including the Caregiver Strain Index (CSI), the Zarit Burden Interview–Short Form (ZBI-12), and a 5-item Attitudes Toward the Caregiving Role scale. High burden was defined as ZBI > 19.ResultsMean caregiver age was 45.7 ± 9.0 years; 63.9% were male. Mean ZBI was 19.33 ± 7.26, and 50.0% reported high burden. Mean CSI was 6.51 ± 3.03; 50.0% had high stress (CSI ≥ 7). Caregiving attitudes were generally positive (mean 21.92 ± 4.72; α = 0.908). ZBI scores correlated positively with CSI (r = 0.671, p < 0.001) and negatively with attitudes (r = − 0.335, p = 0.046). In adjusted models, high stress independently predicted high burden (OR 24.18, 95%CI 2.45–238.85; p = 0.006), whereas positive attitudes were protective (OR 0.04, 95%CI 0.002–0.63; p = 0.023; model R²=0.635). Female gender, homemaker status, greater disease severity, and complications were linked to higher burden in bivariate analyses, while shared caregiving and larger families appeared protective; however, none remained significant after adjustment.ConclusionsHalf of the caregivers experienced high stress and burden. Psychosocial factors, particularly stress and caregiving attitudes, were stronger predictors of burden than demographic or illness-related variables. Findings support the integration of family-centered care, which incorporates stress reduction, resilience-building, adaptive meaning-making, and structural supports.

Introduction

Chronic liver disease in children is a paramount concern; its burden does not remain confined to the child suffering, but extends to the psychosocial aspect of the entire family. Chronic and progressive, it almost invariably requires lifelong treatment and invasive procedures or transplantation, imposing immense uncertainty, distress, and financial constraints on caregivers [1, 2]. While better survival outcomes have come from current pediatric hepatology and transplantation, it has new and significant demands on the caregiver about managing the long-term treatments, frequent hospitalizations, and psychosocial needs of the child [3].

Family caregivers play a vital role in coordinating daily care, ensuring treatment adherence, and offering emotional support. This demanding role has deep consequences for their physical and psychological health: chronic stress, sleep disturbances, financial strain, and reduced quality of life [4]. Although the burden of caregiving has been well-understood within adult liver disease and transplantation [2], while less data is available to understand the challenges faced by caregivers of children with CLD [5]. In pediatric contexts, the emotional weight of caring for a child, heightened uncertainty regarding prognosis, and balancing caregiving responsibilities with employment, household, and other family responsibilities intensify the caregiving load [6].

Indeed, studies into the caregivers of children with chronic illnesses like cancer, cystic fibrosis, and congenital heart disease report high levels of psychological distress, social isolation, and financial burden [7]. Comparable patterns are reported - but seldom explored - within pediatric hepatology. For example, a recent qualitative investigation in Ghana showed that physical ill-health, role strain, and social withdrawal were key themes among the caregivers of patients with hepatitis-related CLD; not being sure about how to make ends meet exacerbated their states of stress [5]. Also, in research into pediatric liver transplant caregivers, the caregiving role was shown to be closely aligned with emotional exhaustion, role conflict, and decreased psychological resilience [8]. Yet, despite this developing wealth of evidence, there continues to be a significant dearth of psychosocial studies on the well-being of caregivers of children with non-transplant CLD, let alone those in low-and middle-income countries.

This gap is significant because caregiver burden can have disease-specific features that directly influence both caregiver health and child outcomes. High subjective burden is associated with increased risks of depression, anxiety, and physical morbidity among caregivers, while also compromising the quality of care given to the child [9]. Furthermore, cultural values and spiritual frameworks may further shape caregiving attitudes, possibly moderating psychological burden in some contexts. In Saudi Arabia and other similar cultural contexts, caregiving is often regarded as a moral and religious duty [10], which might be relieved or exacerbated depending on the surrounding support and available resources.

Given these considerations, the current study will assess the psychosocial well-being of family caregivers of children with CLD in terms of burden, stress, and attitudes toward caregiving. This article aims to explore the caregiver- and patient-related variables that predict high burden, with a view to designing culturally sensitive interventions that support the families. This paper will endeavor to bridge the lacuna in the literature, which is not only imperative to ameliorate caregiver outcomes but also important in enhancing holistic pediatric liver care.

Methods

Design and concept

A cross-sectional analytical study was carried out at the Hepatology and Liver Transplant Department of the hepatology/transplant center, King Abdulaziz University Hospital, between January and April 2025.

The study was grounded in the stress–appraisal model of caregiving, which conceptualizes burden as the interaction between caregiver characteristics, patient illness severity, and available coping resources [11]. Based on such a model, caregiving strain (or stress) was conceptualized as an exposure/stressor and caregiver burden as the broader psychosocial outcome reflecting the caregiver’s appraisal of caregiving consequences. Nonetheless, while a cross-sectional approach does not establish causality, it provides important hypothesis-generating evidence about associations between caregiving attitudes, stress, and burden, which may inform targeted interventions and longitudinal research in the future.

The characteristics of participants

The study targeted all family caregivers of children with confirmed CLD attending the participating center between January and April 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (1) being the parent or close family member primarily responsible for caregiving, (2) providing regular care for at least six months, (3) age ≥ 18 years, and (4) ability to provide informed consent in Arabic or English. Exclusion criteria included severe psychiatric illness or cognitive impairment, which could compromise valid self-reporting.

Recruitment was conducted through purposive sampling. Of the 44 caregivers approached, five declined, citing time constraints, and 3 were unreachable, yielding an 81.8% response rate.

Data collection

The data collection was done using structured interviewer-administered questionnaires, supplemented by review of hospital medical records to enhance accuracy and minimize recall bias for the relevant clinical data. Interviews were conducted in private rooms within the clinic to reduce social desirability bias and encourage candid responses.

The domains included in the data collection form were:

Caregiver data

These included age, gender, marital status, education, employment status, number of children, monthly household income, self-rated health status on a scale from 0 to 10, and the presence of chronic diseases. Self-rated health was included because it is a validated predictor of both physical and mental health dimensions [12, 13].

Patient data

Data was extracted from medical charts, and then patients’ data were verified with their caregivers. The following variables were identified: age; sex; diagnosis of CLD, such as biliary atresia and autoimmune hepatitis; disease duration; age at diagnosis; disease severity, categorized as mild, moderate, and severe; number of hospitalizations in the last year; and complications, including ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, and liver failure. These variables were selected based on prior evidence that the severity of illness and complication burden are critical drivers of caregiver strain and psychological distress.

Caregiver Strain Index

The Caregiver Strain Index is a 13-item questionnaire designed to understand the problems of parents caring for children with chronic liver disease. Its original version has been validated by Robinson [14] and Rodrigue et al. [15]. For this study, we used the 12-item version that looks into the strain the interviewed caregiver has undergone, excluding item 7: There have been other demands on my time; e.g., from other family members [16]. The scale provides a score, where the higher the score, the greater the strain. If the caregiver answers “yes” to seven or more questions (higher than the theoretical average of 6/12), it suggests he or she may be experiencing heavy stress or strain. The questions covered include those on: The ability to manage time, physical and emotional well-being, finances, and quality of sleep.

Zarit Burden Interview – Short form (ZBI-SF)

ZBI is a 22-item validated instrument to measure caregivers’ burden [17]. For the tool to fit a wider spectrum and be applicable in various research designs, Bédard et al. developed a shortened version of the original 22-item ZBI scale [18]. The ZBI-SF, a 12-item short-form, was highly correlated with the full 22-item version; its correlation coefficient ranged from 0.92 to 0.97. The higher the score of the ZBI-SF, with a maximum of 48, the more severe the caregiver burden. This tool addresses issues related to schedule, physical health, and mental and psychosocial burdens. Its concise nature does not deprive it of the value of the original scale but makes it also more practical in different settings.

Attitudes towards the caregiving role

The ATCR scale is a 5-item Likert-type instrument, constructed by the authors to reflect those aspects of attitudes and beliefs of caregivers related to their role. Responses can range in a 5-point system from “Strongly Disagree” to “Strongly Agree” on various statements. The statements examine the caregiver’s perspective on the moral, emotional, spiritual, and cultural dimensions of their caregiving experience. Items investigate such dimensions as the moral obligation of caregiving, the emotional bond it develops, its spiritual impact, the fit with cultural mores, and the overall meaning derived from the role, particularly in caring for a child with chronic liver disease. An attitude score is calculated (maximum = 25) as the sum of scores on items; higher values reflect a more positive attitude, while lower values signal less positive attitudes. The hypothesis was that the magnitude of the psychological impact on the caregiver is inversely related to the caregiving attitude score.

The tool was subject to face and content validation by an independent research methodologist and two independent experts in pediatric hepatology and psychology, who agreed that the items were clear, relevant, and comprehensive enough to meet the purposes of the study. Some minor refinements were made in light of their comments, especially in the wording of culturally sensitive items. Pilot testing with five caregivers of children suffering from other chronic diseases confirmed that the instrument was understandable and suitable.

Variable definition

The primary outcome was caregiver burden, assessed with the ZBI-SF and dichotomized as low versus high using the population mean as a cutoff. The secondary outcomes were caregiving stress and attitudes, dichotomized based on the mean scores of CSI and ATCR, respectively.

Caregiver burden was conceptualized as a global measure of the caregiver’s perceived impact of caregiving on overall well-being and daily functioning. Caregiving-related stress, measured using CSI, was evaluated as a related but distinct construct reflecting caregiving demands and role-related pressures. The analytical approach was informed by caregiving stress-process models that conceptualize caregiving stressors and strains as factors associated with caregiver burden [11]. In terms of caregiving attitudes, it was hypothesized that positive attitudes serve as a protective factor against high burden in a way that is concordant with resilience frameworks in caregiving research [9].

Independent variables were classified into caregiver-related variables: age, gender, marital status, education, employment, number of children, income, self-rated health, chronic diseases, and caregiving role; and patient-related variables: age, sex, type of CLD, severity and duration, complications, and hospitalizations. This conceptualization is based on the ecological models of caregiving burden, emphasizing the interaction among caregiver demographics, personal health, and disease-related stressors [19].

Statistical methods

The data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Caregivers’ socio-demographic and health-related data and demographic and clinical data of children with CLD were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were described as means and SD or median and 75th centile (P75), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Internal consistency for scales (Caregiver Strain Index, Zarit Burden Interview – Short form, and Attitudes towards caregiving) was measured using Cronbach’s alpha. Bivariate correlations between the three scale scores were studied using Pearson’s correlation coefficients.

Normality of data was checked by Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests, and also by observing the histogram. The normality test for the ZBI score revealed Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistics at 0.102 and p = 0.200, while in the Shapiro-Wilk test, the statistics were 0.980 and p = 0.735. Besides, the analysis of the histogram revealed a bell-shaped pattern, which suggests the normal distribution of the ZBI score.

The psychological well-being was assessed as the primary outcome, while the ZBI scale was categorized into low (score ≤ 19) versus high (score > 19) burden, considering the mean score value (19.33) as the cutoff. In the Caregiver Strain Index, any positive answer may indicate a need for intervention in that area. However, a score of 7 or higher indicates a high level of stress; thus, the scores were categorized into high versus low stress accordingly [20]. Attitudes towards the caregiving role were dichotomized into positive and negative attitudes, using the mean value as a cutoff.

Caregiver- and patient-related factors associated with psychological well-being were analyzed using independent t-tests for normally distributed continuous variables, and chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to identify independent factors associated with high caregivers’ burden. The significant predictors of high caregiver burden were identified by two logistic regression models (enter and forward methods), reporting odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95%CI). The p-value was considered significant at < 0.05.

Ethical statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Unit of Biomedical Ethics, Research Ethics Committee (REC) NCBE Registration No: (HA-02-J-008). All participants provided written informed consent. Confidentiality was assured, and data were anonymized before analysis in line with international ethical standards for caregiver research [21].

Results

Participants’ characteristics

A total of 44 caregivers were eligible to participate; 5 declined, citing time constraints, and 3 were unreachable. Consequently, the study involved 36 caregivers (participation rate = 81.8%), with a mean age of 45.67 years (SD = 9.01). The majority were male (63.9%), over half had completed high school (55.6%), and 19.4% had postgraduate degrees. Household income was under 5,000 SAR for 36.1% of the sample. The family history showed that 6 (16.7%) had another child with CLD, and 4 (11.1%) had another family member with CLD. In terms of caregiving roles, 22.2% were sole caregivers, 19.4% were primary caregivers, and 50% shared caregiving equally with another family member (Table 1).

Caregiving-related stress

In assessing caregiving-related stress using the CSI scale, most caregivers (63.9%) found their caregiving responsibilities inconvenient and confining, and nearly half reported sleep disturbances (47.2%) and physical strain (41.7%). Half of the participants (50%) reported requiring emotional and personal adjustments to cope with caregiving. Financial strain was experienced by 83.3% of the participants, and 83.3% also felt completely overwhelmed, being the most impacted dimension. The mean Caregiver Strain Index (CSI) score was 6.51 (SD = 3.03), with 50% of caregivers experiencing a high level of stress (CSI ≥ 7), considering the mean value as a cutoff. Cronbach’s alpha for the scale was 0.762, indicating acceptable internal consistency (Table 4).

Assessment of caregiver burden

The Zarit Burden Interview revealed that 77.8% of caregivers frequently or nearly always felt they should be doing more for the child, and 66.6% reported frequently or nearly always feeling that they could do a better job caring for the child. This is followed by 58.9% of caregivers frequently or nearly always feeling they lacked sufficient personal time due to their caregiving responsibilities. On the other hand, a minority of caregivers reported feeling angry (8.3%) or strained (19.4%) around their child, believed the child’s illness impacted their relationships (11.1%), or felt they had lost control of their life (11.1%) (Table 5).

Additionally, analysis of the histogram showed a bell-shaped distribution. This indicates that the ZBI score follows a normal distribution.

Caregiver-related factors of psychological well-being

Female caregivers were significantly more likely to experience high burden (ZBI score > 19.33, the average population score) compared to males (69.2% vs. 26.1%, p = 0.015). Employment status was also significant, with all housewives (100%) reporting high burden (p = 0.005). Caregivers with 1–2 children were more likely to have a high burden (77.8%, p = 0.014), whereas those with 5 or more children had a lower burden (15.4%). A negative association was also observed between the number of chronic diseases and burden, with caregivers having more chronic diseases reporting lower burden (p = 0.028). Caregiving stress, measured by the CSI, was strongly associated with burden; 72.2% of caregivers with high stress reported high burden compared to 11.1% with low stress (p < 0.001). Lastly, caregivers with negative attitudes towards caregiving were significantly more likely to experience high burden (88.9%) compared to those with positive attitudes (25.9%, p = 0.001) (Table 7).

Discussion

The current investigation examined the psychosocial well-being of parents/carers of children with CLD in terms of caregiver burden, stress related to caregiving, and attitude toward caregiving. The current investigation determined that 50% of the caregivers in this sample expressed high levels of stress as indicated by the CSI, and that 50% of them had high levels of burden according to the Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI). Furthermore, items such as financial problems and feeling overwhelmed resulted in the highest individual burden. The multivariate analyses confirmed these findings that strenuous caregiving stress and having negative attitudes toward the caregiving role are the significant predictors of high caregiver burden. In contrast, no demographic and disease-related variables remained in the multivariate model. These results raise some critical considerations when placed in the context of pediatric chronic diseases and CLD wellbeing literature in general.

The sample size was modest but defensible, due to the small size of the target population in a single-centre setting and a good participation rate. Importantly, the sample included both male (63.9%) and female (36.1%) caregivers, making this study more representative than earlier research, which often underrepresents fathers despite their growing interest in pediatric care [1]. Previous psychosocial research in rare pediatric diseases has concluded that sample sizes between 30 and 50 subjects are appropriately representative for exploratory analyses, especially when augmented by valid psychometric measures and regression modelling [22]. Not powered for definitive causal inference, the sample was adequate for identifying key associations and thus informing larger multicenter research.

Areas of caregiving burden in CLD

A detailed analysis of ZBI responses reveals which dimensions of caregiving are most disrupting well-being. Items indicative of lack of personal time, stress related to balancing caregiving and other responsibilities, and the perception of not doing enough for the child received the most frequent endorsement. Such specific items correspond to broader qualitative themes, including loss of personal time because of role restriction and resultant identity loss; inability to balance caregiving with other responsibilities, thereby mirroring conflict between duties and personal needs; and the perception of not doing enough, which manifests as pervasive guilt. These findings further align with prior work on caregiver burden in chronic pediatric conditions such as cystic fibrosis and congenital heart disease, wherein caregivers similarly report such defining themes as role restriction, erosion of personal identity, and pervasive guilt [6, 7]. Khanna et al. [1] also described parallel findings in spouse caregivers of adults with CLD, underscoring exhaustion, lost time, and tensions between caregiving duties and personal needs. The convergence across disparate disease populations suggests that these areas of burden are not peculiar to CLD, per se, but rather inherent to the psychosocial demands of long-term caregiving.

Another strong dimension of burden was financial strain, identified by over 80% of the participants as a major stressor. This agrees with the results of Abaah et al., who note that caregivers of hepatitis-related CLD in Ghana commonly endure financial insecurity arising from the cost of care and loss of income [5]. However, the near universality of financial strain across settings reflects a structural problem: caregiving is rarely recognized as labor and hence remains economically unsupported, despite its evident impact on caregivers’ ability to continue their role. In Saudi Arabia, where this study was conducted, the costs of healthcare may be partly subsidized, but indirect costs - among them transportation, lost wages, and household adjustments are still great. It is well-supported in the literature that financial strain is a predictor of caregiver depression, anxiety, and physical morbidity; hence, interventions need to address economic burden not as an ancillary issue, but at the core of the determinants of caregivers’ wellbeing [2, 4]. The present findings, therefore, add to the evidence that economic burden should be treated not as a peripheral issue but as a core factor in caregivers’ wellbeing.

The findings indicate that any intervention for CLD should focus on financial support, respite services availability, and psychosocial interventions aimed at reducing the feeling of guilt and role strain. However, the all-important question is whether or not healthcare systems are currently prepared to incorporate such supports in regular pediatric hepatology care. Considering the chronicity of CLD, failing to seek improvement in these domains may cause long-term deterioration not only to the parents but also potentially to the children, as parental distress has been identified to adversely affect treatment adherence and child adjustment processes [7].

The role of CLD type and disease characteristics

Biliary atresia and autoimmune hepatitis were the most common diagnoses in this cohort, each accounting for 19.4% of cases, followed by cirrhosis (13.9%) (Table 2). These distributions reflect international reports of pediatric CLD; however, no significant differences in caregiver burden were noted across disease types in the present study. This contrasts with adult populations, in which etiology can shape burden due to stigma associated particularly with alcohol related and viral liver diseases [23]. Such etiological differences may be absent in the pediatric context because caregiving demands (medication management, frequent monitoring, and hospitalizations, to name a few) are similar regardless of diagnosis [24]. In fact, the caregiving experience appears to be determined more by demand intensity and chronicity rather than by the diagnostic label itself, as no significant differences in caregiver burden were noted across disease types in the present cohort, a finding consistent with previous studies among pediatric chronic conditions [6, 7, 24].

Interestingly, whereas ascites, variceal bleeding, and encephalopathy increased the severity of the disease with complications that were associated with high caregiving burden in bivariate analyses, they did not remain independent in the adjustment analysis. This implies that the association between caregiving burden and disease severity may be confounded by other factors. The plausible confounders include higher levels of healthcare involvement, priority access to specialized services, and activation of social support resources for the more severe cases. For instance, a child with severe CLD may be prioritized for transplant evaluation, hence receive intensive counselling and structured support measures. Such institutional interventions reduce caregiver stress and, consequently, weaken the apparent association between disease severity and perceived burden. Evidence from pediatric hepatology has indicated that families facing advanced stages of disease often receive increased institutional support, which decreases caregiver stress and weakens the observed association between disease severity and perceived burden [3, 5, 15].

Hence, while direct clinical assumption may suggest that clinical severity directly predicts caregiver distress, our results indicate that psychosocial and attitudinal factors-i.e., negative perceptions of caregiving, feelings of inadequacy, and low endorsement of positive attitudes-as measured by our caregiver attitudes scale, may be stronger predictors. This is in line with Calinescu et al.‘s findings in cirrhosis caregiving, in which subjective stress appraisal outweighed disease stage in predicting burden [8]. This underlines the need for research that goes beyond disease characteristics to explore mediating variables, including resilience, coping strategies, and support networks.

Caregiver strain and burden: The CSI–ZBI relationship

The current cohort of caregivers reported high levels of strain associated with high burden, where several domains were frequently mentioned as challenging. These figures agree with previous research into pediatric chronic illnesses, where 40% to 60% of parents report clinically significant levels of stress and burden [7]. The correlation observed between the scores of CSI and ZBI was highly significant, at r = 0.67 and p < 0.001. This confirms that stress and burden are interrelated, with day-to-day strains translating into broader perceptions of being overwhelmed and constrained by the caregiving role.

It is worth noting that, while caregiver strain and caregiver burden are closely related constructs, they are not entirely overlapping. This is supported by the moderate-to-high positive correlation between ZBI and CSI scores observed in this study. Thus, caregiver strain was conceptualized as reflecting caregiving-related demands and disruptions, whereas caregiver burden was considered a broader subjective appraisal of the overall impact of caregiving on the caregiver’s well-being and daily life. Both this concept and the study findings are in agreement with the stress–appraisal model of caregiving proposed by Meyer et al. [11], which conceptualizes burden as the result of continuous appraisal of stressors in relation to available coping resources. Robinson’s validation of the CSI also underlined that cumulative strain predicts caregiver burnout [14]. In the present study, the predictive value of CSI scores for ZBI burden confirms that stress is not simply a correlate but a main determinant of the way burden is experienced. Nevertheless, given the conceptual overlap between these constructs and the cross-sectional nature of the study, the observed relationship should be interpreted cautiously, and alternative or reciprocal pathways cannot be excluded.

The association of sole caregiving with higher burden underlines the protective effect of shared caregiving. Families where several members contributed to care reported significantly lower burden, which agrees with Wang and Nguyen [25], who showed that shared caregiving among adult CLD families lessened emotional strain. Similarly, in our data, caregivers from larger families reported a burden significantly less than caregivers from smaller families, echoing the value of family-centered approaches where responsibilities are distributed across relatives. In the Saudi context, extended family networks are culturally ingrained and can offset caregiver stress by diffusing both emotional and practical demands across family members. Previous research supports the finding that family-centered caregiving reduces psychological distress and enhances resilience among caregivers of children with chronic conditions [25]. Promotion of such family-based support mechanisms could form an effective and culturally appropriate approach to alleviate caregiver burden.

Attitudes toward caregiving

Another important finding in this study involves the participants’ generally positive caregiving attitude characterized by high endorsement of caregiving as a moral duty, spiritual opportunity, and cultural responsibility. These attitudes reflect the Islamic and cultural norms of Saudi Arabia, where caregiving can be regarded as both a familial and religious obligation. Of interest is that the observed negative correlation between attitudes and burden (r = − 0.33, p = 0.046) supports the contention that positive reframing of caregiving roles and tasks reduces psychological distress. This validates our theory and provides an argument for the external validity of the ATCR.

The literature confirms that positive caregiving attitudes may serve as protective factors, enrich resilience, and provide meaning to caregiving experiences. Indeed, evidence exists that attachment styles in adulthood shape implicit caregiving attitudes and influence perceived stress and burden appraisal [26]. Simultaneously, adaptive strategies, such as attitude coping, positive cognitive reappraisal, and resilient trait, mitigate the manifestation of depression and anxiety, reinforcing the psychological well-being of caregivers [27].

These findings are replicated in caregiving related to cancer, where benefit finding and meaning-making processes were shown to alleviate the impact of stress and foster emotional well-being, contributing eventually to lower levels of depressive symptoms and improved quality of life [28]. However, the protective effect of attitudes is not consistent. As Schulz and Sherwood noted, spirituality and meaning can buffer stress but may also intensify it if caregivers internalize excessive responsibility or guilt [9]. While the majority of caregivers in this study had an overall positive attitude, those with negative or ambivalent attitudes were significantly more likely to have a high burden, suggesting that promoting adaptive meaning-making is as important as efforts to reduce negative appraisals.

These results point to a hitherto understudied aspect of pediatric CLD-cultural attitudes embedded in the population that can either alleviate or further increase stress. Culturally adapted interventions that enhance positive caregiving identities and eliminate deleterious beliefs may be particularly important; these could be aided, for example, by religious leaders and community health programs that facilitate meaning in caregiving, on one hand, and openness to self-care and support from outside, on the other.

Other factors associated with burden

Demographic analysis demonstrated that female caregivers reported a significant burden compared to male caregivers. This finding agrees with international literature that reports women are disproportionately exposed to caregiving responsibilities and have higher vulnerability to psychological distress because of this responsibility [2]. A meta-analysis of 229 studies indicates that women consistently report higher burden and depression scores than men, even though the overall effect sizes may be modest [20]. These findings are similarly seen in the region. In Jordan, a cross-sectional survey of 420 caregivers indicated that almost half, or 49.3%, had a very severe burden; mothers reported significantly greater psychological distress than fathers (p < 0.001) [29]. Regional data highlight how cultural expectations place women as the basic unit responsible for caregiving, thus heightening their role overload. This trend is even clearer in Saudi Arabia, in which a study of 206 caregivers found burden to strongly correlate with stress (ρ = 0.65, p < 0.01) and inversely with social support (ρ = −0.36, p < 0.01); it shows how poor external support seems to escalate disproportionately the excessive burden faced by female caregivers in societies [30].

Importantly, in adjusted models, gender was not an independent predictor, indicating that the differences in burden observed are more likely to be due to caregiving roles rather than to intrinsic gender-related stress or attitudes. Nonetheless, women, especially mothers, may internalize caregiving responsibilities more intensely, leading to higher stress and more negative appraisals, which in turn explain their elevated burden scores. In Turkey, for example, a study of 206 mothers of children with special needs reported a very strong correlation between maternal depression and burden (r = 0.846, p < 0.001), with depression explaining 36–64% of the variance in caregiver burden depending on the model [31]. Such findings support our interpretation that it is psychosocial strain, rather than gender alone, which drives the high burden scores among women.

Employment status was also a factor, as homemakers reported a disproportionately higher burden. In our data, this association seemed to weaken in adjusted models, insinuating confounding by gender roles. The same patterns have been recorded in Saudi Arabia, where employment intersects with cultural caregiving norms, hence reducing its independent effect. Nevertheless, quantitative evidence shows that employment may provide protective benefits: in Turkey, mothers whose household income is above the minimum wage have 6–11 points lower burden scores (p ≤ 0.005) compared with mothers whose household income is below the minimum wage. This reflects buffering from financial and occupational stability [31]. Conversely, homemakers may perceive caregiving as ‘all-encompassing,’ with limited opportunities for role diversification, thus increasing perceived burden. This assertion is supported by Lindahl Norberg et al., who documented that mothers of children with cancer quite often leave employment after diagnosis, resulting in long-term declines in income and well-being.

Family size was another variable that significantly influenced the level of burden; smaller family sizes placed a greater burden on the caregiver. In large families, there might be sharing of caregiving responsibilities, thereby decreasing the burden on one member. Indeed, Abaah et al. [5] remarked that the extended family relationship structures in Ghana were very helpful to carers of CLD patients. The implications include that interventions should not focus on the carer alone but must take into consideration the larger family system by encouraging the sharing of caregiving responsibilities among siblings, grandparents, and other relatives. Increased social support within larger families may explain the seemingly paradoxical result of reduced subjective burden despite objective difficulties.

Another important factor was the comorbidities of the caregiver, which seemed to have a negative association with burden in the unadjusted analysis but not in the adjusted model. This is possibly because caregivers with chronic illnesses themselves learn coping skills that generalize to their caregiving role and receive more formal support and counselling services, which may indirectly reduce the caregivers’ burden. This effect did not hold in adjusted analyses, suggesting complex interactions between family composition, caregiver health, and support systems available to them rather than a direct causal relationship. These findings raise some important questions about the nature of resilience and coping that require further investigation.

Strengths, limitations, and perspectives

Several limitations are identified in this study. The small sample (36 caregivers) reduces statistical power, generalizability, and the detection of small effects, increasing the risk of type II error. Recruitment remains a universal challenge in pediatric CLD due to its rarity, and this points to the need for a multicenter design. The cross-sectional design impedes any causal inference, particularly regarding the directionality of the association between caregiver strain and caregiver burden. Although our analyses were guided by theoretical frameworks that conceptualize caregiving strain as a contributor to caregiver burden, the two constructs encompass partially overlapping domains and may influence one another. Consequently, a reciprocal relationship is plausible and should be explored in longitudinal studies. Self-report data carry risks of recall and social desirability bias, although interviewer administration reduced some of these concerns. Conducting the study in a Saudi tertiary center may also limit applicability to other cultural and health care contexts. An additional limitation relates to the caregiver attitude interview, which was developed specifically for this study. Although the instrument underwent expert review to assess face validity and was pilot tested before implementation, it was not subjected to comprehensive psychometric validation. In particular, construct validity, criterion validity, and convergent validity were not formally evaluated through comparison with established and validated measures assessing caregiver attitudes, perceptions, or caregiving experiences. Consequently, there is a possibility that certain items may not have fully captured the intended constructs or may have been influenced by item wording and response interpretation, introducing measurement bias. Another limitation is that the study included a small number of children who had undergone liver transplantation (n = 5). While these patients remained under long-term care for liver-related conditions and were therefore considered eligible for inclusion, caregivers of transplant recipients may experience psychological challenges and caregiving demands that differ from those of caregivers of children awaiting transplantation or managed exclusively with medical therapy. Due to the limited sample size, subgroup analyses comparing transplant and non-transplant patients were not feasible. Consequently, the inclusion of transplant recipients may limit the generalizability of the findings to strictly non-transplant pediatric CLD populations.

Notwithstanding these limitations, the study contributes empirical evidence to an under-explored disease by identifying stress and caregiving attitudes as independent predictors of burden in pediatric CLD. These findings emphasize that psychosocial factors may be stronger than demographic and illness-related characteristics in influencing caregiver burden. Specifically, key themes emerging from the data involve financial strain, loss of autonomy, and guilt, while disease severity and complications exerted indirect effects on burden, likely through psychosocial and attitudinal pathways. Finally, the protective role of positive attitudes and the strong association between stress and burden point to the importance of resilience-building and meaning-making interventions in supporting caregivers.

Gender differences, family structure, and caregiver health status further underscore the relevance of culturally adapted, family-centered interventions. Larger multicenter prospective studies are needed to confirm these results and guide the design of interventions aimed at safeguarding caregiver well-being and improving child outcomes in CLD.

Conclusion

This study reveals a significant psychosocial burden among caregivers of children with CLD, with high levels of stress and high burden commonly reported. Multivariable analyses demonstrated caregiver stress to be the strongest predictor of burden, while positive caregiving attitudes were protective independently. Disease severity and complications were linked to burden in bivariate tests but not after adjustment, indicating that institutional support and psychosocial processes mediate their effects. Shared caregiving and larger families were associated with low burden, indicating the beneficial effects of family-centered role distribution. These findings contribute to a sparse literature on non-transplant pediatric CLD through the quantification of the relative weight of psychosocial as opposed to illness-related factors and by foregrounding culturally shaped attitudes in a Middle Eastern context.

Care models should, in practice, incorporate the reduction of stress, building resilience, and adaptive meaning-making, along with financial and structural supports. In countries like Saudi Arabia, the mobilization of extended family networks and community/religious resources could help leverage this effect. Internationally, there is a need for multicenter prospective studies to better refine targets and assess family-centered interventions.
