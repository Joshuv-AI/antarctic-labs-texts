Man With Dyspnea
Authors: Anupa D Ghimire, Katherine B Griesmer, Logan L Beach, Micah R Whitson, David C Pigott
Published: 2026

Case Presentation

A 63-year-old man with a history of emphysema with recent bilateral lung transplant and congestive heart failure presented to the emergency department with acute dyspnea. The patient arrived on a 100% nonrebreather mask, with an initial oxygen saturation of 82% on room air with respiratory distress and hypotension to 88/62 mm Hg without tachycardia. Electrocardiogram on arrival was notable for diffuse low QRS voltage. Point-of-care cardiac ultrasound was performed on arrival (Fig 1). While the patient was transferred for computed tomography (CT) imaging (Fig 2), he sustained a pulseless electrical activity cardiac arrest.

Parasternal long axis cardiac view with right ventricular hypertrophy (arrowhead), without diastolic collapse in setting of pericardial effusion (star).

Axial computed tomography of chest demonstrating moderate pericardial effusion (star), right pleural effusion, and atelectatic lung (arrowhead).

Discussion

Cardiac Tamponade Without Classic Echocardiographic Findings From Mycobacterium tuberculosis Effusion

Emergent ultrasound-guided pericardiocentesis was performed with removal of 200 mL of straw-colored fluid, with return of spontaneous circulation obtained; cardiac arrest was presumed to be secondary to cardiac tamponade. Pericardial studies were later found to be positive for Mycobacterium tuberculosis, likely from donor lung. The patient had a complicated hospital course and subsequently expired 6 weeks later.

Traditional echocardiographic findings of impending tamponade (right atrial systolic collapse, right ventricular diastolic collapse, and plethoric inferior vena cava) may be absent in patients with altered right ventricular compliance or pericardial mechanics, including pulmonary hypertension, constrictive pericarditis, and right ventricular hypertrophy.1,2 Our patient likely had decreased right ventricular compliance from long-standing pulmonary hypertension before transplant.

Donor-derived tuberculosis, although rare, has been documented in solid-organ transplant recipients, sometimes presenting months after transplantation, with reactivation often facilitated by immunosuppression.3, 4, 5
