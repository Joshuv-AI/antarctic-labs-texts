Targeting carbapenem-resistant and hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae: in vitro evaluation of cefepime/zidebactam, aztreonam/avibactam, imipenem/relebactam, and meropenem/vaborbactam
Authors: Yi Li, Yulian Li, Xun Jia, Huinan Mao, Dan Li, Jie Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe emergence of carbapenem-resistant hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae (CR-hvKp) is a growing concern because of the high mortality rates and limited treatment options. Few reports have examined the in vitro antibacterial activity of novel β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor drugs against CR-hvKp.AimsThis study investigated the in vitro activity of β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor including cefepime/zidebactam, aztreonam/avibactam, imipenem/relabactam and meropenem/vaborbactam toward CR-hvKP isolates. Molecular epidemiological characterization of CR-hvKP strains was performed.MethodsA total of 106 non-repetitive clinical CR-hvKP strains were collected from patients at Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital between August 2018 and December 2023. A VITEK-2 compact system (bioMérieux, France) was used for the preliminary identification of strains and drug susceptibility testing, and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry (Ci-phergen Biosystem, USA) was used to confirm the identity of all strains. CR-hvKP strains were screened using string tests and polymerase chain reaction. The E-test strip method was used to evaluate the in vitro antibacterial activity of novel antimicrobial drugs toward CR-hvKP stains. Molecular characterization of CR-hvKP strains was carried out using polymerase chain reaction amplification of resistance genes, virulence genes, housekeeping genes, and wzi genes. The virulence features of CR-hvKP strains were investigated using the Galleria mellonella infection model.ResultsThe susceptibility rates of CR-hvKP to cefepime/zidebactam and aztreonam/avibactam all exceeded 90%, with rates of 98.1% and 99.1%, respectively. CR-hvKP exhibited susceptibility rates of 57.5% and 65.1% to imipenem/relebactam and meropenem/vaborbactam, respectively. Sequencing identified ST11–KL64 as the predominant type in CR-hvKP strains. We identified three new ST subtypes, ST8115, ST8116 and ST8117. The most prevalent carbapenemase genes were blaKPC and blaNDM, and approximately 75.5% of CR-hvKP strains carried both blaKPC, blaSHV, and blaCTX-M.ConclusionsCefepime/zidebactam and aztreonam/avibactam hold great promise for the treatment of CR-hvKP infections. Our findings will not only effectively address the challenge of CR-hvKP resistance, but also provide evidence to support the optimization of clinical therapeutic strategies and further promote the development and application of novel antimicrobial drugs.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-12824-7.Keywords: Carbapenem-resistant hypervirulent Klebsiella pneumoniae, Molecular epidemiology, β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor (BL/BLI) combinations, In vitro activity, Antimicrobial resistance

Introduction

Klebsiella pneumoniae is a Gram-negative bacillus belonging to the family Enterobacteriaceae, and it is a globally significant pathogen that exhibits considerable genetic, phenotypic, and pathogenic diversity [1, 2]. Based on its virulence characteristics, K. pneumoniae can be categorized into classical K. pneumoniae and highly virulent K. pneumoniae (hvKP). Classical K. pneumoniae is typically less virulent and is primarily associated with hospital-acquired infections, particularly in immunocompromised individuals [3]. This strain is also known for its ability to acquire and disseminate antimicrobial resistance genes, including those encoding carbapenemases, leading to the emergence of carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP), which poses a significant threat to public health [4]. In contrast, hvKP is more virulent, invasive, and pathogenic than classical K. pneumoniae and can cause severe community-acquired infections, including in immunocompetent individuals [5]. The increasing prevalence of antimicrobial resistance in K. pneumoniae has further complicated the clinical management of infections caused by this pathogen. In the 2024 Priority Pathogens List for Bacteria, the World Health Organization designated CRKP as a critical priority pathogen [6]. Similarly, in March 2024, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention updated its 2019 Antibiotic Resistance Threat Report, continuing to classify carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae as an “urgent threat” to public health [7]. However, the increasing number of reports showing the emergence of strains with convergent multidrug resistance and high virulence profiles raises significant public health concerns [8]. A study reported a 30-day mortality rate of 20% following CR-hvKP infection, while bloodstream infections carried a mortality rate as high as 50%; 25.6% of these cases developed sepsis/septic shock [9].

In recent years, the continuous evolution of highly virulent or carbapenem-resistant plasmids has led to the emergence of highly virulent and carbapenem-resistant K. pneumonia (CR-hvKP) [10]. K. pneumoniae evolve into CR-hvKP through multiple evolutionary pathways [11, 12], with the acquisition of virulence plasmids by CRKP being the most common mechanism [13]. CR-hvKP has critical consequences on human health, as it is simultaneously multidrug-resistant, hypervirulent, and highly transmissible, which can lead to serious infectious diseases, making clinical treatment extremely difficult. Its spread has become a significant challenge to global public health [14]. In China, the alarming trend of CR-hvKP prevalence has garnered widespread concern [15]. To address this challenge, researchers have been actively seeking effective antibacterial strategies for CR-hvKP [16–18]. Several antimicrobial drugs have been developed in the last decade for Gram-negative infections, particularly β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, some of which are in clinical trials such as cefepime/zidebactam (CEFI/ZIDE), and some of which have already been approved for marketing in some countries, e.g., aztreonam/avibactam (AZA), imipenem/relebactam (IMR) and meropenem/vaborbactam (MEV), etc. To date, the in vitro antimicrobial activity of novel β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor against CR-hvKP has not been extensively reported, which may hamper effective intervention and control of lethal infections caused by these recalcitrant pathogens.

In this study, we explored the in vitro activities of 4 β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations antimicrobial drugs, including CEFI/ZIDE, AZA, IMR, and MEV, and other clinically used antimicrobials against CR-hvKP, with the hope of providing a scientific basis for clinical treatment. We collected 106 nonredundant CR-hvKP strains from 106 patients from the Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital in China from 2018 to 2023 and examined the in vitro antimicrobial activity of the antimicrobials against the 106 CR-hvKP strains. The findings of this study are of significant importance for guiding the rational use of antimicrobial agents in clinical practice and the development of new therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All operations were performed in the biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) laboratory of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital and the University of Electronic Science and Technology of China (Number: 2021-511).

Clinical samples and bacterial isolates

A total of 264 CRKP non-duplicate strains were collected continuously from K. pneumonia–infected patients at Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital between August 2018 and December 2023. 106 CR-hvKP strains were screened from 264 CRKP strains by string test and PCR (Polymerase Chain Reaction) analysis. The isolates were obtained from different samples, including sputum, bronchoalveolar lavage, urine, blood, abdominal drainage fluid, and pus. Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry was used to confirm the identity of all strains.

The K. pneumoniae strains resistant to at least one of the carbapenem antibiotics [ertapenem (ETP), imipenem (IPM), meropenem (MEM), or doripenem] were defined as CRKP. Strains were defined as hvKP by a positive string test ( > 5 mm) and detection of rmpA/rmpA2 and iucA genes [5].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was then performed. Susceptibility to conventional antimicrobials was tested using the VITEK 2 Compact system, and MICs for selected agents were additionally determined using MIC Test Strips (Liofilchem, Ascoli Piceno, Italy). Results were categorized as susceptible (S), intermediate (I), or resistant (R) according to the corresponding interpretive criteria. Unless otherwise specified, MICs were interpreted using breakpoints from the corresponding-year CLSI M100 edition (i.e., the most current CLSI M100 available in the year of isolate recovery/testing). The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of carbapenems (imipenem [IPM], ertapenem [ETP], meropenem [MEM]), cephalosporins (cefazolin, cefuroxime, ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, cefepime), aztreonam (ATM), aminoglycosides (amikacin [AMK], gentamicin), fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin [CIP], levofloxacin [LEF]), and the β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combination piperacillin/tazobactam (TZP) were interpreted according to breakpoints in the corresponding-year CLSI M100 edition. For cefepime/zidebactam (CEFI/ZIDE), because CLSI clinical breakpoints are not available for this combination, the proposed PK/PD cutoff (≤64 mg/L) was provided for reference only [19]. For categorical comparison, MICs were interpreted using the cefepime susceptible-dose-dependent (SDD) criterion (upper bound ≤8 mg/L) for Enterobacterales in the corresponding-year CLSI M100 edition [20]. MICs for meropenem/vaborbactam (MEV) and imipenem/relebactam (IMR) were interpreted according to the corresponding-year CLSI M100 edition (MEV: S ≤ 4/8 mg/L, R > 16/8 mg/L; IMR: S ≤ 1/4 mg/L, R > 4/4 mg/L). MICs of tigecycline (TGC) were interpreted using the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) breakpoints for Enterobacterales [21]. The European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) breakpoints were used to interpret MICs for polymyxin B (S ≤ 2 mg/L, R ≥ 4 mg/L) and AZA (S ≤ 4 mg/L, R > 4 mg/L) [22]. K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603, Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853 were used as quality control strains.

String tests

To determine whether K. pneumoniae isolates exhibited a hypermucoviscous phenotype, the string test was performed [5]. The isolates were inoculated on 5% sheep blood agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 16–18 h. A plastic inoculating loop was used to generate a viscous string. The string test was considered positive upon the formation of a viscous string greater than 5 mm in length.

PCR analysis

We used PCR analyses to detect carbapenemase genes (blaKPC, blaNDM, blaVIM, blaIMP, blaSME, blaGES, and blaOXA-48-like) and other resistance genes (blaCTX-M and blaSHV). Genomic DNA of CRKP was extracted using the boiling method. Detailed Steps: Pick 3 ~ 5 single colonies suspended in ultrapure water, heated at 100 °C for 10 min, cooled to room temperature and centrifuged at 13,000×g for 5 min; the supernatant was taken and stored at −20 °C for spare. The PCR reaction system and reaction conditions were set up according to the instructions of 2×Taq Master Mix kit from Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd. (Nanjing, China). The PCR products were visualized on a 1.5% agarose gel. Agarose was obtained from Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). PCR analysis was also used to detect virulence-associated genes(rmpA, rmpA2 and iucA) of CRKP. The PCR primers are listed in Table S1.

K-typing by wzi genotyping

To identify the K types of CR-hvKP, the wzi allele was amplified by PCR and aligned with the wzi sequences deposited in the database of Institut Pasteur (http://bigsdb.web.pasteur.fr) [23].

Multilocus sequence typing (MSLT)

All CR-hvKP strains were genotyped by MLST. Seven housekeeping genes (gapA, infB, mdh, phoE, pgi, rpoB, and tonB) were detected by PCR, followed by Sanger sequencing, in accordance with the protocol on the MLST website(https://pubmlst.org). The primers are listed in Table S1.

Galleria mellonella infection model

Among the 106 CR-hvKP strains, 10 strains were randomly selected to evaluate in vivo virulence in Galleria mellonella. Eight randomly selected G. mellonella larvae from Jiatongjia Co., Ltd. (Fuzhou, China), weighing approximately 300 mg, were selected for each isolate and maintained on woodchips in the dark at approximately 15 °C. The CR-hvKP concentration was adjusted to 1 × 107 CFU/mL in PBS, and a 10 µL aliquot was administered to infect the G. mellonella via the rear left proleg. The survival rates of G. mellonella were recorded every 12 h. Positive and negative control strains were K. pneumoniae, which was previously collected by our group. The hypervirulent strain SC43-25, isolated from blood samples of a 65-year-old patient admitted for liver abscess and subsequent bloodstream infection and endophthalmitis, was selected as the positive virulence control. Whole genome sequence analysis revealed that this strain (GenBank JAVKOU000000000) is highly homologous to the typical hypervirulent strain NTUH-K2044. The classic ST11 strain SC12-22 (GenBank JAVJNH000000000) (string test/iucA/rmpA/rmpA2 negative) was selected as the negative virulence control [24]. These experiments were conducted in duplicate. Larval survival was recorded at the indicated time points, and survival curves were generated. The p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.

Results

Identification of isolates

From August 2018 to December 2023, 264 hospitalized patients were diagnosed as CRKP infection. From these patients, 106 CRKP strains were further identified as CR-hvKP strains by positive string test and PCR amplification of the rmpA/rmpA2 and iucA genes. Thus, the isolation rate of CR-hvKP in this study was 40.2% (106/264).

The characteristics of the 106 patients are presented in Table 1. The mean patient age was 63 years, and more than half of the patients were male (79/106, 74.5%). Most patients were over 50 years old (84, 79.2%), with the highest proportions of patients in the 60–70 and > 80 years groups (24, 22.6%, and 23, 21.7%, respectively). Prior to admission, all patients had various underlying diseases (e.g., pulmonary disease, central nervous system disease, cardiovascular disease, cancer). The length of hospital stay varied from 1 to 307 d, and the mean stay was 39 d (Tables 1 and S2). The most common specimen sources were sputum (38/106, 35.8%), bronchoalveolar lavage (25/106, 23.60%), and tracheal aspirate (13/106, 12.30%). Most isolates (56; 52.8%) were collected from patients in medical intensive care units. The mortality rate of CR-hvKP-infected patients was as high as 31.3%.

Resistance genes

The presence of drug-resistance genes in the 106 CR-hvKP strains is summarized in Table 2 and Fig. 1. blaKPC was the carbapenemase gene detected in most strains (89/106, 84.0%), followed by blaNDM (7/106, 6.6%). The blaSME, blaVIM, blaIMP, blaGES, and blaOXA-48 genes were not detected in the CR-hvKP strains. blaSHV was present in 90.6% (96/106) of the CR-hvKP isolates, and blaCTX-M was detected in 84.0% (89/106). The PCR primers used for initial screening in this study were designed based on the classical blaSHV-1/11 sequence. Any point mutation, small deletion, or IS26 insertion in the target region can cause amplification failure due to primer-template mismatch, leading to misclassification as “gene deletion.” Therefore, the blaSHV-negative status observed in approximately 10% of CR-hvKP strains may result from mutations in their ancestral blaSHV. Five CR-hvKP strains carried both blaKPC and blaSHV, and five other strains carried both blaSHV and blaNDM. One strain carried blaNDM, blaSHV, and blaCTX-M, and four strains carried both blaKPC and blaCTX-M. Two strains did not carry any of the tested resistance genes.

MLST and capsular serotyping

Capsular serotyping of the CR-hvKP strains was determined using wzi genotyping (Table 3). PCR and Sanger sequencing revealed that the predominant capsular type of the CR-hvKP strains was KL64 (85.8%, 91/106), followed by capsular types KL1 (4/106, 3.8%), KL62 (2/106, 1.9%), and KL2 (1/106, 0.9%). Other capsular types included KL2, KL19, KL38, KL54, KL115, KL149, KL209, KL233, and KL323 (1 each, 0.9%). The distribution of MLST typing in the 106 CR-hvKP strains is summarized in Table 3. Most of the CR-hvKP isolates belong to ST11 (92/106, 86.8%), followed by ST23 (2/106, 1.9%) and ST29 (2/106, 1.9%). The other STs were ST65 (1/106, 0.9%), ST367 (1/106, 0.9%), ST726 (1/106, 0.9%), ST889 (1/106, 0.9%), ST2237 (1/106, 0.9%), ST2715 (1/106, 0.9%), and ST3176 (1/106, 0.9%). Most of the CR-hvKP isolates belong to ST11-KL64(90/106,84.9%). Additionally, three novel subtypes were identified, designated as ST8115-KL64, ST8116-KL1 and ST8117-KL233, respectively.

The antimicrobial susceptibility test results are summarized in Table 5 and Table S3. In an assessment of the in vitro activity of four novel β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combination antimicrobials, 106 CR-hvKP strains showed good susceptibility to all of them, all exceeding 55%. Antimicrobial susceptibility of CR-hvKP strains

We also examined the response of the CR-hvKP strains to the other common clinical antibiotics. The resistance rate of the CR-hvKP strains to AMK and gentamicin exceeded 80%, and the resistance rate to CIP and LEF exceeded 92%. The resistance rate to cephalosporins, including first to fourth generation cephalosporins, was over 98%. The sensitivity to polymyxin B, CZA, and TGC was high, at 87%, 91.7%, and 94.1%, respectively.

Discussion

This study describes the epidemiological characteristics, virulence features, and drug sensitivity of CR-hvKP strains obtained from patients at Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital. To the best of our knowledge, this is this is one of the few studies to report the in vitro activity of novel β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor, including CEFI/ZIDE, AZA, MEV and IMR toward CR-hvKP strains.

The CRKP strain was first reported in 1997 [25]. In 1986, Taiwanese researchers first reported hvKP, describing the characteristics of liver abscess caused by hvKP and the multiple metastatic infections, marking the initial understanding of highly virulent strains in humans [26]. With the acquisition of virulence plasmids by CRKP [27], the acquisition of carbapenem-resistance plasmids by hvKP [28], or the acquisition of virulence and resistance hybrid plasmids by KP [29, 30], CR-hvKP emerged and spread worldwide. As a result of its high virulence, high pathogenicity and multidrug resistance, CR-hvKP poses great challenges to the clinical treatment of CR-hvKP infection.

In our study, all CR-hvKP strains exhibited low susceptibility to cephalosporins, ATM, AMK, CIP, and LEF (Table 5 and Supplementary Table 3), which is consistent with previous studies [31, 32]. The common drugs tigecycline (TGC) and polymyxin B exhibited high antibacterial activity toward CR-hvKP, with susceptibility rates exceeding 87.0%, indicating that they may exhibit a high level of activity against CR-hvKP. TGC, a tetracycline derivative, and polymyxin B, a polymyxin, are the last line of treatment for infections caused by multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria [33, 34]. However, TGC is primarily influenced by hepatic function, while polymyxin B is mainly limited by nephrotoxicity and requires renal monitoring; moreover, resistance to these agents can further limit their clinical use [35, 36].

In recent years, the emergence of several new β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitors (BL/BLI) or β-lactam/β-lactam enhancers (BL/BLE) has brought hope for the clinical treatment of CRKP [16]. In this study, the sensitivity of CR-hvKP to CEFI/ZIDE, AZA, IMR, and MEV was detected using an E-test. AZA was approved by the European Commission for marketing in April 2024, and it is one of the last therapeutic options for treating infections caused by NDM-like-producing Enterobacterales [37]. Aztreonam is stable for hydrolysis by Metallo-β-Lactamases (MBLs) but not by most serine-lactamases. In some pathogens that produce both MBL and serine-β lactamases, aztreonam is susceptible to hydrolysis by serine-β lactamase, which inhibits aztreonam [38]. Avibactam is a non-β lactam BLI that inhibits class A, C, and some class D β lactamases (including ESBL, KPC-type carbapenemases, and OXA-48), protects aztreonam from hydrolysis by serine-β lactamases. The combination of AZA exhibits in vitro activity against Enterobacterales with class B, A, C, and some D-lactamases [16, 39]. A study by Echegorry et al., which examined the prevalence of carbapenemase-producing Enterobacteriaceae in Argentina in the post-Covid-19 era, reported co-production of carbapenemase combinations, particularly KPC and NDM, predominantly in K. pneumoniae, and AZA exhibited 96–98% sensitivity against all carbapenemase classes [40]. Liu et al. collected 204 strains of New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase (NMD)-producing Enterobacterales and concluded that AZA had the highest activity against NDM-producing Enterobacterales [41]. Similar to the previous study, our study showed that the 106 CR-hvKP strains showed 99.1% susceptibility to AZA. Although AZA shows high in vitro activity against enzyme-producing Enterobacterales, reduced susceptibility or resistance has been reported in some isolates. Xiang found that multiple copies of the KPC variant and mutations in OmpK36 and OmpK35 genes drive resistance to AZA in K. pneumoniae [42]. Le Terrier et al. reported that although aztreonam–avibactam (AZA) remains active against many NDM-producing E. coli, a subset of isolates with reduced susceptibility/resistance to AZA has been described, and sulbactam–durlobactam showed in vitro activity against these AZA-resistant isolates [43]. In addition to AZA, CEFI/ZIDE is another BL/BLI that inhibits class A and MBLs. CEFI/ZIDE, which is not yet approved for clinical use, has demonstrated promising in vitro activity against MBL-positive pathogens. Zidebactam is a non-β-lactam BLI that, in addition to inhibiting β-lactamases, exhibits antimicrobial activity by binding penicillin-binding protein 2 (PBP2) in gram-negative bacteria, including Enterobacterales and P. aeruginosa [38, 39]. In recent years, there have been some reports of CEFI/ZIDE activity against P. aeruginosa and Enterobacteriaceae. A recent study showed that CEFI/ZIDE exhibited highly effective in vitro activity and in vivo efficacy against multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa harbouring multiple resistance mechanisms compared with traditional β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, and CEFI/ZIDE is expected to be an important strategy for the treatment of multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa infection [44, 45]. Bakthavatchalam et al. found that CEFI/ZIDE inhibited more than 99% of strains at a concentration of ≤ 8 mg/L, regardless of the carbapenemase produced by K. pneumonia [46]. OmpK36 and OmpK35 gene mutations may lead to AZA resistance [42]. Notably, CEFI/ZIDE is active against K. pneumoniae that co-express serine and/or MBLs and are defective for OmpK35 and OmpK36 porins [47]. In this study, we found that strains that showed intermediate susceptibility to AZA were susceptible to CEFI/ZIDE. Overall, the susceptibility of CR-hvKP to CEFI/ZIDE was 98%, second only to AZA (99.1%). However, when AZA resistance is detected, CEFI/ZIDE could be considered as a therapeutic alternative.

In the past decade, there has been significant progress in the development and approval of third and fourth-generation antibiotics targeting cephalosporin-resistant and carbapenem-resistant Gram-negative bacilli (e.g. cefiderocol, ceftazidime/avibactam, IMR, and MEV). IMR and MEV are both carbapenem/β lactamase inhibitory combination antimicrobial drugs and were approved by the FDA in 2019 and 2017, respectively. IMR was approved for marketing in China in December 2024. Relebactam is a potent non-β-lactam, bicyclic diazabicyclooctane, β-lactamase inhibitor. It is structurally related to avibactam (a β-lactamase inhibitor currently approved for use in combination with ceftazidime), with the main distinguishing feature being the addition of a piperidine ring to the 2-position carbonyl group [48]. Vaborbactam, a non-antibiotic cyclic boronic acid inhibitor, binds reversibly with meropenem. The structure of vaborbactam is unlike any other currently marketed β-lactamase inhibitor. The structure and mechanism of action of vaborbactam not only improves the inhibition activity against β-lactamase, but also reduces the non-specific inhibition of mammalian proteases, thereby reducing the potential toxicity and side effects [39, 49]. Both inhibitors display activity against Ambler class A [including extended-spectrum β-lactamases, K. pneumoniae carbapenemases] and class C β-lactamases (AmpC); however, they are inactive against class B and D lactamases [16, 39]. The resistance mechanisms of IMR and MEV are as follows: 1) production of class B metallo-β-lactamases or partial OXA D-lactamases and overexpression of AmpC enzymes; 2) reduced expression or inactivation of OmpK; 3) overexpression of the efflux pump MexAB-OprM; and 4) other mechanisms, such as PBP3 mutations and KPC variants affecting the activity of MEV. Posteraro et al. conducted antimicrobial susceptibility testing on 13 clinical isolates of MBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae (including 9 K. pneumoniae, 3 Enterobacter cloacae, and 1 Providencia spp.). They found that all isolates showed sensitivity to AVA, while IMR and MEV were ineffective in 100% and 92.3% of the isolates, respectively [50]. In this study, all CR-hvKP strains resistant to IMR carried the class B metallo β lactamase gene blaNDM. Of the three newly discovered CR-hvKP strains, two strains (KL1–ST8116 and KL233–ST8117) were resistant to IMR. A study comparing MEV with ceftazidime-avibactam for carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae infections found that in patients with recurrent infections, resistance developed in three patients treated with ceftazidime-avibactam, compared with no resistance in the MEV group [51]. During the treatment of CRKP infections with the common agent ceftazidime/avibactam (CZA), an increase in resistance rates has been observed. This may be attributed to the emergence of blaKPC variants or overexpression of KPC [52]. While KPC variants exhibit CZA resistance, they show sensitivity to carbapenems. However, carbapenem monotherapy still has a high failure rate in treating infections caused by KPC variants. MEV and IMR demonstrate favourable in vitro activity against KPC variant strains. Therefore, when clinical isolates with CZA heteroresistance are detected, IMR and MEV can serve as alternative therapeutic options [53]. In this study, the resistance rates of CR-hvKP to IMR and MEV were 6.6% and 15.1%, respectively, making them potential treatment options for CR-hvKP infections.

Although numerous reports are available regarding the in vitro activity of novel antimicrobial agents against K. pneumoniae, CRKP, and hvKP, to the best of our knowledge, to the best of our knowledge, the in vitro activities of novel antimicrobial drugs, such as CEFI/ZIDE, AZA, MEV, and IMR, against CR-hvKP have been scarcely investigated only against highly virulent and highly resistant. We also combined the molecular epidemiological characteristics of CR-hvKP. In the Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital in China,KPC-producing ST11 KL64 is the most common type among CR-hvKP strains. ST11 KL64 is a common subtype in CRKP strains in China, and the amplification of capsular KL64 was accompanied by a decrease in the prevalence of KL47 [54]. However, the CR-hvKP we collected did not have the ST47 type, which may be due to localized regional differences and deserves further study. Therefore, the primary evolutionary pathway of CR-hvKP may involve the acquisition of a virulence plasmid by the classic ST11 KL64 CRKP strain producing KPC. Hong et al. demonstrated that ST11–KL64 is also the most prevalent CR-hvKP type in Jiangxi Province that primarily evolved from CRKP through the acquisition of a truncated IncHI1B/IncFIB virulence plasmid (lacking iroBCDN). The deficiency of this plasmid may facilitate the cross-regional dissemination of ST11–KL64 CR-hvKP [55]. As a highly virulent strain, ST11–KL64 CR-hvKP has maintained multidrug resistance, which may enhance its survival capability when confronted with antimicrobial treatment. By acquiring high-virulence plasmids and retaining multidrug resistance, ST11–KL64 CR-hvKP may have expanded its ecological niche, enabling it to adapt to a broader range of environments and hosts. This niche expansion may confer greater adaptability during its evolutionary process. Another study reported that nosocomial transmission and recombination of blaKPC-carrying plasmids and pLVPK-like virulence plasmids in ST11 K. pneumoniae enhances drug resistance and virulence at a lower adaptive cost, which may promote the spread and prevalence of CR-hvKP [56]. Additionally, we identified two relatively classic CR-hvKP strains, namely Sample 25 and Sample 94, both belonging to the ST23–KL1 type of CR-hvKP. Despite sharing the same serotype, they exhibited certain differences in their resistance profiles and the resistance genes they carried. Notably, the IMR resistance phenotype observed in Sample 94 may be associated with its carriage of the NDM resistance gene. Currently, research on the major circulating ST11–KL64 and classic ST23–KL1 CR-hvKP strains remains relatively limited regarding their resistance mechanisms, virulence characteristics, and co-evolutionary pathways between resistance and virulence. Further in-depth investigation is urgently needed. This study solely focused on the in vitro activity of novel antimicrobial agents against CR-hvKP. Although there have been reports from other regions on the in vitro activity of certain novel antimicrobial agents against CRKP, the significant differences in the resistance backgrounds of the strains isolated in this study compared to those from other regions preclude us from verifying this point. That is, whether high virulence affects antimicrobial susceptibility requires further validation. Additional research is needed to assess whether acquiring a virulent phenotype impacts the antimicrobial susceptibility of CRKP strains.

This study has several limitations. First, CR-hvKP strains were identified as those with resistance to any carbapenems, positive string tests, and rmpA/rmpA2 and iucA genes. However, not all hvKP isolates are string-test positive [5]. We, therefore, may have underestimated the incidence of CR-hvKP. Second, the classification of hvKP virulence genes remains incomplete, and the precise combination of virulence genes required for hvKP is still unclear [57]. Therefore, the high-virulence biomarkers (rmpA/rmpA2 and iucA genes) used in this study cannot ensure high virulence and additional animal infection models are required for confirmation. Third, this was a single-center retrospective study, and the results of the study may only apply to the patient population of this center. Therefore, additional studies with a broader patient group are required.

Conclusion

The results of this study demonstrate that several novel β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, including CEFI/ZIDE and MEV, exhibit significant antibacterial activity against CR-hvKP. The widespread presence of New Delhi metallo-β-lactamase (NDM)-related metalloproteases in CR-hvKP strains limits their susceptibility to IMR and MEV to only a moderate level. During the evolution of CRKP into CR-hvKP, changes in the genome and genetic background may significantly influence the susceptibility of these strains to β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations. Specifically, as blaNDM spreads, the resistance rates to IMR and MEV are likely to increase further. However, when KPC-2 undergoes mutation, these two antibiotics may still represent potential therapeutic options. In contrast, the novel β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations of CEFI/ZIDE and AZA show promising antibacterial potential against CRKP and CR-hvKP strains, particularly those of the ST11–KL64 type producing MBL. These combinations are thus considered promising candidates for treating infections caused by such strains and warrant further clinical evaluation.
