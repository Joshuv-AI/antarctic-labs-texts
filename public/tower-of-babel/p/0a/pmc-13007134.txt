Phase I trial of locoregional administration of autologous tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes in patients with uveal melanoma and liver metastases (the HAITILS trial)
Authors: Axel Nelson, Rebecca Riise, Samuel Alsén, Andrew Wong, Per Carlson, Sebastian Edman, Jan Holgersson, Roger Olofsson Bagge, Iva Johansson, May Sadik, Lars Edenbrandt, Lisa M Nilsson, Jonas A Nilsson, Lars Ny
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUveal melanoma is a rare melanoma subtype characterized by a liver-dominant pattern of metastasis which is associated with a lack of durable responses to immunotherapies. Adoptive cell transfer of autologous tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) has been shown to induce responses in a subset of patients with metastatic uveal melanoma. The safety and feasibility of locoregional administration of TIL therapy via hepatic arterial infusion (HAI) have not previously been evaluated.Patients and methodsThis prospective, open-label, phase I trial investigated the safety and feasibility of one-time TIL therapy administered via HAI in patients with liver metastases of uveal melanoma. Preconditioning chemotherapy with melphalan (1 mg/kg, intravenous) was administered on day −5. TILs were delivered via percutaneous catheterization of the hepatic artery according to a dose escalation scheme (0.1, 0.3, or 1×109 cells) on day 0. Patients received daily subcutaneous interleukin-2 (IL-2, 2 MIU) up to 14 days after TIL administration. The primary endpoint was the incidence and severity of adverse events (AEs). Secondary endpoints included clinical efficacy and the feasibility of TIL production using a semiautomated manufacturing system.ResultsSix patients received TIL therapy manufactured using the CliniMACS Prodigy platform; five were treated according to protocol, while one received a lower TIL dose than planned. All had received prior systemic treatment. No AEs related to the HAI procedure were observed. All patients experienced grade ≥3 hematologic AEs related to preconditioning chemotherapy, and two patients experienced grade ≥3 AEs attributed to TIL/IL-2. Best overall response was stable disease in all patients (100%). Median progression-free survival was 4 months, and median overall survival was 14 months.ConclusionsTILs can be manufactured using the CliniMACS Prodigy. Administration of TIL via HAI was safe and feasible in patients with liver-dominant metastatic uveal melanoma. The used regimen appears insufficient to achieve durable clinical efficacy and implies a need for further testing to obtain conclusive results.Trial registration numbersNCT04812470, EUCT number: 2024-512877-28-00, EudraCT number: 2020-006126-31.Keywords: Adoptive cell therapy - ACT, Tumor infiltrating lymphocyte - TIL

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy is an established and U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved treatment for advanced cutaneous melanoma, but its clinical utility in metastatic uveal melanoma is poorly defined. Patients with metastatic uveal melanoma rarely achieve durable responses to existing systemic therapies, underscoring the need for novel treatment strategies and optimized delivery approaches.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This phase I trial demonstrates that TILs can be successfully manufactured for uveal melanoma using a semiautomated, closed-system bioreactor and safely delivered via hepatic arterial infusion following melphalan preconditioning. Locoregional TIL administration was feasible without procedure-related toxicity, and the safety profile was manageable, although no objective responses were observed.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

These findings support continued investigation of locoregional TIL delivery, automated manufacturing, and tolerable preconditioning and postconditioning strategies in uveal melanoma and may guide the design of future early-phase trials aiming to develop more effective treatments for liver-dominant metastatic disease.

Introduction

Uveal melanoma (UM) is a rare malignancy accounting for approximately 3% of all cases of melanoma. Despite effective treatment of the primary tumor, ~50% of patients develop metastatic disease.1 The pattern of metastasis is strongly hepatotropic, with isolated liver involvement observed in over half of patients. Prognosis in this situation is poor, with a median overall survival (OS) of 4–10 months, and very few patients surviving more than 5 years.1 2

To date, the only approved treatment that has demonstrated an OS benefit in metastatic UM is tebentafusp, a bispecific fusion protein that directs T cells to melanoma cells. In HLA-A*02:01-positive patients, tebentafusp significantly improved OS compared with standard therapy (22 vs 16 months).3 However, long-term survival has generally not been observed, and treatment eligibility is restricted to ~50% of patients carrying the required HLA genotype. In contrast to cutaneous melanoma, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have demonstrated limited efficacy in UM, with ipilimumab plus nivolumab achieving objective response rates (ORRs) ranging from 10% to 18% and no documented OS benefits.4 5 While the use of PD-1 inhibitors in monotherapy is associated with minimal or absent activity, the combination of pembrolizumab and epigenetic therapy with a histone deacetylase inhibitor has resulted in durable responses in a subset of patients.6 Moreover, for patients with UM and isolated liver metastases, regional therapies such as isolated or percutaneous hepatic perfusion (IHP/PHP) with high-dose melphalan have yielded an ORR of approximately 40% and a median progression-free survival (PFS) gain of 4 months compared with standard care.7 Nonetheless, OS benefits have not yet been demonstrated, and durable responses are rare.8 Recently, however, the combination of PHP with ICIs has been shown to be superior to PHP alone, conferring a median OS benefit of 3.5 months.9 These data indicate that durable immunotherapy responses are possible, but since treatments are not curative, a high unmet clinical need remains.

Adoptive cell transfer using autologous tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) is an established immunotherapy in metastatic cutaneous melanoma that can induce durable and complete responses. The treatment involves harvesting tumor tissue, expanding TILs ex vivo, and reinfusing them following lymphodepleting chemotherapy. Interleukin-2 (IL-2) is administered after TIL infusion to support the function and survival of the TILs. A randomized phase III trial comparing TIL therapy to ipilimumab, including patients with PD-1-refractory melanoma, showed a significantly higher ORR of 49% with TIL therapy vs 21% with ipilimumab, alongside superior PFS.10 In a pooled analysis including 153 patients with advanced melanoma who had disease progression after PD-1 therapy, lifileucel, a commercially produced TIL product, demonstrated an ORR of 31% and durable responses. These pivotal data led to FDA approval11 and have established TIL therapy as a treatment option for advanced cutaneous melanoma. In metastatic UM, however, clinical experience with TIL therapy remains limited. The first, and so far only, trial of TIL therapy in this setting reported promising results with objective tumor regression in 35% of the 20 evaluable patients, including one complete response.12

Compared with cutaneous melanoma, UM is characterized by a lower tumor mutation burden, reduced TIL infiltration, and poor response to ICIs. This immunologically suppressed phenotype is partly attributed to the absence of a UV mutational signature, the predominance of GNAQ/GNA11 driver mutations, and the tolerogenic liver microenvironment. Nevertheless, both entities share melanoma-associated antigens, and tumor-reactive T-cell clones can be expanded from UM lesions,13 providing a solid rationale for further exploring TIL therapy. In addition, given that ~90% of patients with metastatic UM develop liver involvement, local administration of TILs may enhance their therapeutic potential. We have designed a prospective, open-label, phase I trial (HAITILS) to investigate the safety and feasibility of locoregional administration of TIL therapy via hepatic arterial infusion (HAI), combined with melphalan preconditioning chemotherapy and low-dose subcutaneous IL-2, in patients with liver-dominant metastatic UM.

Methods

Process development and preclinical evaluation

Tumor biopsies from UM patients were obtained from liver metastases and dissected into 1–2 mm fragments using scalpels. Two to three fragments per well in a 24-well plate were cultured in RMPI 1640 GlutaMAX (Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% human AB serum and 3000 IU / mL IL-2 (Miltenyi Biotec) with media exchange every second day and culture split on confluency for up to 16 days before being cryopreserved as young TILs (y-TILS).

Thawed yTILs were either expanded using a manual method in tissue culture flasks as described before14 15 or using case 3 of the tumor-reactive T cell (TRT) process on CliniMACS Prodigy as later described. TIL products were analyzed by flow cytometry (FACSLyric) for frequency of CD3+ T cells, CD4 and CD8 T cells as well as non-T cell impurities using BD Multitest CD3/CD8/CD45/CD4 (CD3 (clone SK7), CD8 (clone SK1), CD4 (clone SK3) and CD45 (clone 2D1)).

For analysis of T cell differentiation, exhaustion, and tumor reactivity, the following antibodies were used: CD3 (clone UCHT1), CD4 (clone A161A1), CD8 (clone HIT8a), CD45RO (clone UCHL1), CCR7 (clone G044H7), LAG-3 (clone 3DS223H), TIM-3 (clone A18087E), PD-1 (clone A17188A), TIGIT (clone A15153G), LIVE/DEAD Fixable Far Red Dead Cell Stain (ThermoFisher) and 4-1BB (clone 4B4-1), and analyses were performed on BD Accuri C6 Plus Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences).

All TIL products were polyclonally stimulated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA, Sigma Aldrich) and ionomycin (Sigma Aldrich) cell stimulation cocktail followed by brefeldin A (Invitrogen) treatment and staining of interferon gamma (IFNγ) (Miltenyi Biotec, clone 45-15) to assess responsiveness.

Whenever feasible, tumor specimens were transplanted onto non-obese, severe combined immunodeficient interleukin-2 gamma chain knockout (NOG) mice or NOG mice carrying a human IL-2 transgene (hIL2-NOG). Tumor volume was monitored by caliper measurement. Following confirmation of engraftment by two consecutively increasing measurements, autologous TILs were co-transplanted onto tumor-bearing mice, and tumor volume was monitored until sacrifice. TIL products were co-cultured with autologous tumor cells harvested from NOG mice in GMB-MG serum-free media (Cell Lines Service, Germany) for 48 hours and tumor reactivity by 4-1BB upregulation was analyzed by flow cytometry.

Patients and eligibility

Patients with histologically or cytologically confirmed liver metastases from UM were eligible if they had measurable disease according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) V.1.116 and liver-dominant disease as determined by the investigators. Any number of prior treatments was allowed. An Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status of 0–2 was required. Patients were excluded if they had a history of significant autoimmune disease or clinically significant cardiac, pulmonary, or renal dysfunction. Eligible patients were required to have a suitable liver lesion for resection or core biopsy for TIL production, and the disease had to remain evaluable by RECIST after the procedure.

Study design and treatment

This was a prospective, open-label, phase I trial. Patients underwent resection or core needle biopsy of a liver metastasis for TIL procurement. After TIL production, patients received outpatient preconditioning chemotherapy with melphalan 1 mg/kg 5 days prior to TIL infusion (day −5). Patients were hospitalized on day −1, and on day 0, they received TILs via HAI at escalating doses (0.1×109, 0.3×109, and 1×109 cells) followed by daily subcutaneous IL-2 (2 MIU) for 14 days (figure 1A). The selected TIL dose range was chosen based on safety considerations in this Phase I study of HAI of TILs. The protocol for this study can be found in online supplemental file 2.

TIL manufacturing

Tumor tissue was obtained by excision or core needle biopsy of liver metastases and transported to the Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) facility (Cell and Tissue Lab) at Sahlgrenska University Hospital (SU), Gothenburg, Sweden. Tumor samples were cut into 1–2 mm3 fragments and cultured in 24-well culture plates in RPMI 1640 GlutaMAX (Invitrogen) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated human AB serum (from Transfusion Medicine at SU), IL-2 (3000 IU/mL, Miltenyi Biotec), gentamicin (10 µg/mL, Invitrogen), and amphotericin B (0.5 µg/mL, Invitrogen). After 2 weeks, y-TILs were pooled and analyzed for cell number, viability, purity, identity, potency, sterility, and endotoxin presence before cryopreservation.

Further expansion of the y-TILs was performed by using a rapid expansion protocol (REP) in the CliniMACS Prodigy automated bioreactor (Miltenyi Biotec). y-TILs were co-cultured with allogeneic irradiated peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs; feeder cells, Transfusion Medicine, SU), an anti-CD3 antibody (OKT-3, Miltenyi Biotec), and IL-2 (3000 IU, Miltenyi Biotec) in TexMACS culture media (Miltenyi Biotec) supplemented with 2.5% human AB serum for the first 5 days. During the 14-day REP, multiple quality controls were performed, including assessments of cell number, viability, purity, identity, potency, sterility, and the presence of endotoxin and mycoplasma.

Final products were washed in Plasmalyte (Baxter) supplemented with 0.5% human serum albumin (Baxalta) and 1% glucose (Fresenius Kabi) and formulated as the final drug product ready for infusion. The manufacturing process of TIL is illustrated in figure 1B.

Hepatic arterial infusion

HAI was performed at Sahlgrenska University Hospital, Gothenburg, Sweden, under either sedation or general anesthesia. Vascular access was obtained via ultrasound-guided puncture in the right common femoral artery, followed by insertion of a 5-French vascular sheath. The celiac trunk was catheterized using a 4-French diagnostic catheter, and the hepatic arterial circulation was subsequently selectively catheterized by co-axial advancement of a 2.7-French microcatheter. Digital subtraction angiography was used to assess anatomical variation.

In patients with standard hepatic arterial anatomy, the microcatheter tip was positioned in the proper hepatic artery, and TILs were infused over 30 min via manual syringe administration. In patients with aberrant vessel anatomy, three-dimensional angiography with cone-beam CT and selective contrast injection was used to calculate perfusion of the hepatic parenchyma by each vessel, allowing a dose adjustment across vascular branches.17 After infusion, the catheter was removed, and the vessel was closed.

Assessments

Radiological screening procedures included CT and/or MRI of the abdomen and CT of the thorax. Baseline 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography-CT (18F-FDG-PET/CT) imaging was performed between days −14 and −5. Tumor evaluation was performed with 18F-FDG-PET/CT at week 6 and thereafter with CT every 3 months. CT was the modality used for RECIST-based response evaluation, whereas 18F-FDG-PET/CT was used for exploratory analyses. The 18F-FDG-PET data were analyzed using RECOMIA software, maximum intensity projection images, and a standardized uptake value (SUV) threshold of 4 for calculating metabolic tumor volume (MTV).18 Laboratory values and vital signs were assessed at regular intervals. During IL-2 treatment, blood counts and chemistry were monitored daily. Safety was evaluated via the collection of adverse events (AEs), graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the incidence and severity of AEs and serious AEs (SAEs). The secondary endpoints included ORR, clinical benefit rate, duration of response, PFS, hepatic PFS, OS, and feasibility of an automated production of TILs. AEs were captured from study inclusion, with attribution specified for biopsy/TIL harvest, HAI, melphalan, and TIL/IL-2. Tumor response was assessed according to RECIST V.1.1.16 Efficacy was evaluated in patients who completed the treatment schedule.

Statistical analysis

The study was designed to investigate the safety of TIL administration via HAI with melphalan preconditioning and low-dose IL-2. Six patients were treated in three dose cohorts (two per level). Analyses were descriptive and exploratory, with no formal hypothesis testing. Time-to-event endpoints were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method and reported as medians with 95% CIs. Missing data were not imputed.

Oversight

Safety was overseen by an independent data monitoring board with the authority to recommend study termination based on safety concerns. A predefined stopping rule was one event of clinically significant bleeding or thromboembolism attributable to HAI of TILs.

Results

Automated manufacturing of TILs using CliniMACS Prodigy

We developed and validated a semiautomated manufacturing process for TILs suitable for clinical GMP production. To this end, four tumor biopsies were split and processed in parallel using both a conventional manual method and the TRT process on the CliniMACS Prodigy bioreactor (figure 1B). Both manufacturing processes resulted in TIL products of high purity, consisting almost exclusively of T cells (CD3+ CD45+), with comparable proportions of CD4+ and CD8+ subsets and no non-T cell and non-hematopoietic impurities (online supplemental figure SF1A). The two methods also exhibited equal responsiveness to polyclonal stimulation, measured by intracellular staining IFNγ (online supplemental figure SF1B). From one of the four biopsies, a patient-derived xenograft model was successfully established. TILs manufactured by either method demonstrated equivalent tumor recognition as measured by 4-1BB upregulation after co-culture with autologous tumor cells (online supplemental figure SF1C), and similar ability to control tumor growth over time in hIL2-NOG mice (online supplemental figure SF1D).

During the technology transfer of the automated process into the GMP facility, three full-scale manufacturing runs were completed. All three development batches fulfilled predefined release specifications and cell yields were sufficient to support the intended dosing regimen for the clinical trial (online supplemental table S1).

Patients and treatments

Having established a reproducible and GMP-compliant manufacturing process, we proceeded to conduct a pilot clinical study to evaluate the feasibility, safety, and preliminary efficacy of HAI of autologous TILs in patients with metastatic melanoma. From February 2023 to August 2024, eight patients were screened and enrolled. Tumor tissue for TIL manufacturing was obtained from liver metastases by surgical biopsy in three patients and by core needle biopsy in five patients. In total, six patients proceeded to and completed the treatment schedule. At the time of patient enrollment, tebentafusp was not approved for clinical use in Sweden and was therefore not used as prior therapy.

HAI procedure and treatment delivery

The HAI procedure was performed under general anesthesia in two patients and under conscious sedation in four patients. The volume of TIL infusion ranged from 3.2 to 90 mL and was administered over approximately 30 min. In three patients, TILs were infused via the proper hepatic artery, while in three patients TILs were administered selectively to branches of the left and right hepatic arteries, as described in the Methods section. No technical complications were encountered during any of the six HAI procedures.

Five of the six patients (83%) received treatment per protocol, while one patient received a reduced TIL dose (4.4×108 cells) due to a bioreactor malfunction after lymphodepletion had been performed. Safety

Safety was the primary endpoint of the trial. Among the eight patients who underwent tumor harvest, four (50%) experienced grade ≥3 AEs related to the intervention (online supplemental table S2A). Among the six patients who received TILs via HAI, no AEs related to the infusion procedure were observed (0%).

All six treated patients (100%) experienced hematologic grade ≥3 AEs related to melphalan, while two patients (33%) experienced grade ≥3 AEs related to TIL or IL-2 therapy (table 2). The timing of treatment-related AEs is illustrated in online supplemental figure SF3. Hematologic toxicity was prominent: five patients (83%) required erythrocyte transfusions (median 2 units, range 1–6 units), five (83%) required platelet transfusions (median 4 units, range 3–4 units), all patients (100%) received granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, and four (67%) required broad-spectrum antibiotics due to neutropenia. Feasibility

Manufacturing feasibility was high throughout the trial. In the initial expansion phase, y-TIL products were successfully generated in 7 of 8 enrolled patients (88%). Final GMP-compliant products reached the target dose in 5 of 7 (71%). Six patients ultimately received TIL infusion: five at the planned target dose (0.1–1×10⁹ cells) and one at a reduced dose (4.4×10⁸ cells, 44% of target).

Two patients did not proceed to treatment, one due to the diagnosis of a second malignancy and one due to failure of y-TIL expansion. Microscopic analysis of tumor tissue from the latter patient demonstrated a very low proportion of lymphocyte content, with only 0.4% of the cells identified as lymphocytes.

The median manufacturing time from tumor harvest to infusion was 43.5 days (range 34–49). Serum IL-2 pharmacokinetics were consistent across patients, with a half-life of 2.9±0.5 hours (mean±SD, n=6) after subcutaneous administration of 2 MIU, and concentrations falling below the limit of quantification within 16 hours (online supplemental figure SF4).

Clinical efficacy

Although the sample size was small, efficacy evaluation was included as secondary endpoints. All six treated patients (100%) had stable disease (SD) as best overall response according to RECIST V.1.1 at the first evaluation (6 week). At the second evaluation (week 18), one patient (102) remained an SD, while the other five patients had progressive disease (PD). Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate for the first time that locoregional administration of autologous TIL therapy via HAI is safe and feasible in patients with liver-dominant metastatic UM. TIL products were generated using a semiautomated manufacturing system, and the planned target dose was achieved in five of six treated patients. No AEs were attributable to the HAI procedure, demonstrating that intra-arterial delivery is technically feasible and well tolerated with no observations of complications such as hepatitis, thrombosis, or bleeding. Previous clinical experience with intra-arterial delivery of cell therapies in cancer has been limited. A case was described by Rosenberg et al, suggesting that intra-arterial infusion of TILs into the head and neck region of a patient with cutaneous melanoma induced a stronger clinical response compared with intravenous delivery of the same product.19 A small study of six patients with colorectal cancer liver metastases also demonstrated that HAI administration of CAR-T cells could be performed safely.20 The present study is the first clinical trial to evaluate intra-arterial administration of TILs, supporting the concept that locoregional delivery of cell therapy, in a disease with strong hepatic tropism such as UM, is feasible in a clinical setting, opening a new avenue for the future clinical development of cell therapies.

The present trial differs from most previous TIL studies in its preconditioning regimen. Whereas cyclophosphamide and fludarabine (Cy/influenza) are typically used to induce lymphodepletion,21 22 we employed melphalan because of its established efficacy in UM when administered regionally at higher doses, as in IHP used by us and others for the treatment of UM with liver metastases.7 8 Cy/influenza and melphalan are alkylating agents, although their clinical profiles differ. The hematologic toxicities attributed to melphalan in this trial were broadly comparable to those reported with Cy/influenza, but melphalan required only a single administration, appeared tolerable, and allowed out-patient dosing with hospitalization starting at TIL infusion. In comparison, Cy/influenza usually requires 5 days of in-patient care. Notably, the depth of lymphodepletion with melphalan appeared somewhat less profound than that reported with Cy/influenza,22 yet the overall safety profile was favorable. Future work should assess whether melphalan provides sufficient lymphodepletion for optimal TIL engraftment, or whether the combined conventional regimen may be necessary. Alternative preconditioning strategies should therefore be explored in subsequent studies.

Another important difference from prior studies relates to IL-2 administration. High-dose intravenous IL-2 given for up to 5 days remains standard in many TIL protocols,10 11 21 but is associated with significant toxicity. We instead used low-dose subcutaneous IL-2 for 14 days, an approach supported by smaller studies14 and a randomized phase II trial,23 which found no major efficacy differences between subcutaneous and intravenous administration, though it was underpowered for definitive conclusions. Preclinical work from our group indicates that continuous IL-2 exposure is critical for in vivo TIL persistence and function.15 However, our pharmacokinetic analyses suggest that twice-daily dosing may have achieved more favorable systemic exposure. This provides a rationale for further optimization of IL-2 dose and schedule in subsequent trials.

The dose of TILs administered in this study was substantially lower than in most prior trials in cutaneous melanoma by a factor of 10–100.10 11 21 Prior studies have suggested a dose-response relationship between the number of TILs administered and clinical efficacy24; thus, the dose of TILs administered is a potential limitation of our trial. While Chandran et al observed an ORR of 35% in metastatic UM treated with TILs,12 we observed no objective responses, though all patients achieved SD. Nonetheless, signs of biological activity were present, including vitiligo in two patients, suggesting immune activation. Our study was not designed to evaluate whether HAI delivery provides added benefit. The absence of a tracer to assess intrahepatic distribution is a further limitation. Development of imaging biomarkers, such as those binding CD8,25 26 to confirm and quantify intratumoral delivery could strengthen future studies of locoregional TIL administration.

To our knowledge, this is the first clinical study using the CliniMACS Prodigy and TRT process for manufacturing of TILs. No notable differences between automated manufacturing and conventional manufacturing using cell culture flasks in terms of product quality were noted during process development. By utilizing functionally closed single-use systems, integrated final formulation, automated media exchanges, and reduced number of user interventions, the CliniMACS Prodigy can improve product quality and consistency as well as facilitate regulatory compliance. Although we only administered 109 TILs at the highest dose level, we acknowledge that the culture capacity of the CliniMACS Prodigy (approximately 5×109 cells) may be a limiting factor for its utility as a manufacturing platform for non-antigen selected TILs in the context of locoregional administration. However, a methodology developed after we validated our method suggests CD137 may be a potential marker for sorting in the Prodigy.27

Efficacy outcomes were modest, with median PFS and hepatic PFS of ~4–5 months and no objective responses. Median OS was 14.5 months, which compares favorably to historical expectations in metastatic UM2; however, further interpretation is limited by the small sample size and potential selection bias of the study population. Exploratory 18F-FDG -PET/CT analyses provided evidence of biological response in one patient, consistent with prior reports that UM-derived TILs can exert anti-tumor effects.12 28 Together, these findings underscore both the feasibility of the HAITILS approach and the challenges of achieving robust, durable clinical efficacy with the current regimen.
