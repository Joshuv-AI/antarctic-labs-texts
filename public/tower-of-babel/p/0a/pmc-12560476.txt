Introduction

Meckel’s diverticulum is a common congenital malformation of the gastrointestinal system that affects approximately 2% of the general population. The majority of individuals with this condition remain asymptomatic, and numerous diverticula are often discovered incidentally during surgical procedures or imaging examinations. Nevertheless, complications such as intestinal obstruction, hemorrhage, and inflammation can arise, leading to significant clinical implications [1]. Despite its established prevalence, the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying Meckel’s diverticulum and its associated complications remain inadequately understood, posing a diagnostic challenge for medical professionals [2]. Mesenteric volvulus and internal hernia represent significant surgical emergencies characterized by the twisting of the mesentery or the displacement of intestinal loops, respectively. Mesenteric volvulus occurs when a segment of the intestine rotates around its mesenteric connection, potentially compromising vascular supply and resulting in ischemia of the affected bowel section. Patients generally present with acute abdominal pain, along with nausea, vomiting, and indications of bowel obstruction, highlighting the necessity for prompt diagnosis and treatment to prevent severe complications, including bowel necrosis [3]. Conversely, internal hernias occur when a bowel segment protrudes through a defect in the peritoneal lining, which may also result in strangulation and ischemic conditions [4]. The diagnosis of these conditions typically necessitates imaging studies, notably computed tomography (CT), which can reveal specific characteristics such as whirl patterns or abnormal bowel positioning [3]. Existing literature emphasizes the importance of recognizing these conditions owing to their rapid deterioration if left untreated. Although mesenteric volvulus is relatively rare, its potential ramifications are grave, as diagnostic delays can lead to life-threatening complications. Internal hernias, while infrequent, also pose diagnostic challenges and frequently require a heightened index of suspicion along with appropriate imaging modalities to establish a definitive diagnosis [5]. This report describes a young man with acute abdominal pain diagnosed with mesenteric volvulus from a giant Meckel’s diverticulum, highlighting the rarity of this condition and the need for consideration in similar cases, especially with bowel obstruction signs. The case’s unique aspects enhance understanding of mesenteric volvulus and its causes.

Case presentation

History (subjective)

The male patient of Han Chinese ethnicity presented with persistent colicky pain in the lower abdomen that began 5 hours before admission, without any identifiable triggering factors. This discomfort was accompanied by a cessation of gas passage and bowel movements. The patient denied any additional symptoms, including chills, fever, nausea, vomiting, jaundice, acid reflux, hematemesis, hemoptysis, or a sensation of incomplete bowel evacuation.

Physical examination (objective)

Upon arrival at the emergency department, a thorough physical examination revealed significant tenderness in the lower abdomen, particularly in the right lower quadrant, though rebound tenderness was absent. Bowel sounds were noted as present and active.

Investigations/imaging (objective)

An enhanced CT scan of the abdomen was performed, which indicated a white blood cell count of 11.90 × 109/L, with a neutrophil percentage of 92% and a neutrophil count of 10.95 × 109/L. The CT findings demonstrated mesenteric congestion in the lower abdomen, presenting a localized whirlpool-like appearance, alongside adjacent small bowel distension and fluid accumulation. It also suggested the possibility of mesenteric volvulus or intra-abdominal hernia, a small volume of fluid in the pelvic cavity, and an elongated appendix measuring approximately 6 mm in diameter, accompanied by multiple lymph nodes in the ileocecal region (Fig. 1).

Assessment/diagnosis

The examination raised suspicion for mesenteric volvulus or an intra-abdominal hernia, leading to a preliminary diagnosis of mesenteric volvulus.

Plan

Owing to the worsening abdominal pain, a decision was made to perform laparoscopic exploration. During the operation, adhesions involving segments of the small intestine and omental tissue were discovered in the right pelvic cavity. In addition, necrotic portions of the small intestine were identified, along with approximately 200 ml of hemorrhagic ascites, necessitating a conversion to open surgery. Further exploration revealed a 25-cm-long oval-shaped pedunculated small bowel diverticulum located about 1.5 m from the ileocecal junction, with a distal diameter of approximately 3–4 cm and significant torsion at the proximal pedicle, exhibiting tight adhesion to the root of the small bowel mesentery (Fig. 2). Consequently, the decision was made to excise the small bowel diverticulum. The postoperative recovery was uneventful, and the patient was discharged 1 week later.

Discussion

This synthesis explores the clinical features and complications associated with Meckel’s diverticulum, as well as a rare case of mesenteric volvulus leading to its identification. Meckel’s diverticulum, the most common congenital anomaly of the gastrointestinal tract, arises from the incomplete obliteration of the vitelline duct, with an estimated prevalence of about 2% in the general population. While most cases are asymptomatic, they can result in serious complications such as gastrointestinal bleeding, obstruction, and inflammation, particularly in pediatric populations. Our study aims to illuminate the clinical implications of Meckel’s diverticulum by examining its presentations across various age groups, emphasizing the necessity for clinicians to maintain a high level of suspicion when diagnosing abdominal complaints.

In addition, we highlight a case involving a 30-year-old male of Han Chinese ethnicity who presented with a rare instance of mesenteric volvulus, which ultimately led to the identification and excision of a significant intestinal diverticulum. This case illustrates the importance of considering atypical presentations, as mesenteric volvulus can frequently result in bowel ischemia and necrosis if not diagnosed and managed promptly. The patient exhibited classic symptoms such as abdominal pain and signs of obstruction, aligning with typical presentations of both mesenteric volvulus and complications arising from diverticula.

The innovative aspect of this research lies in its comprehensive analysis of Meckel’s diverticulum cases, addressing a critical knowledge gap regarding its varied presentations and complications [6–8]. Previous studies have often concentrated on either incidence rates or pathological characteristics, failing to provide a multifaceted understanding of clinical manifestations [9–11]. By systematically comparing our findings with existing literature, we identified distinct patterns in symptomatology across different demographics. For instance, our results indicate that intestinal hemorrhage is more prevalent in younger patients, while the occurrence of diverticula as an underlying cause of volvulus remains relatively rare. This underscores the necessity for heightened awareness and prompt diagnosis to prevent severe complications, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes in affected individuals [12–14].

Conclusion

This case of mesenteric volvulus resulting from a diverticulum not only contributes to the current body of literature regarding abdominal emergencies but also serves as a crucial reminder of the intricacies involved in both diagnosing and managing such conditions [15]. Future clinical inquiries should prioritize elucidating the mechanisms underlying complications associated with diverticula, ultimately aiming to optimize treatment strategies to mitigate adverse outcomes.
