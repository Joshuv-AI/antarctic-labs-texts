Authors: G Sivasathiya, R Thiagarajan

Background

Dental treatment planning, especially when complex oral and maxillofacial operations are involved. It is one of the most demanding uses of medical imaging because of both the complexity of relationships between structures of vital interest and the strictness of the criteria of patient safety. Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) has developed into the gold standard of three-dimensional dental imaging with the capability to visualize hard tissues, root morphology, and vital anatomical structures including the inferior alveolar canal (IAC) [1, 2]. Nevertheless, the correct interpretation of CBCT images to guide treatment planning partly relies on the clinician’s skill and experience, as well as subjective evaluation, which contributes to high inter-observer variability and the possibility of medical errors that may lead to severe outcomes, such as nerve injury, infections, and treatment failures [3, 4]. Removal of mandibular third molars is one of the most complex clinical scenarios due to the importance of properly evaluating the spatial relationship between tooth roots and the inferior alveolar canal to plan surgery and assess risk. Research studies have shown that, in 2–8% of mandibular third molar extractions, iatrogenic damage of the inferior alveolar nerve takes place, and permanent sensory loss has been documented in 1–3% of the cases [5, 6]. Besides resulting in serious patient morbidity, these complications have been shown to pose considerable medico-legal risks to practitioners. Current clinical practice is based on subjective radiography with additional use of empirical risk assessment protocols, which cannot be as precise and consistent as needed to achieve the best treatment results in an era of evidence-based dentistry and personalized medicine [7, 8].

It has been demonstrated that the application of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning methods to dental imaging can potentially help resolve these clinical issues. Conventional deep learning methods, especially convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have shown to be successful in a number of dental imaging problems such as tooth segmentation, caries detection and anatomical structure recognition [9, 10]. The available methodologies, however, greatly limit their applicability to complex three-dimensional dental treatment planning situations. Traditional segmentation networks, including U-Net and V-Net, lack the complex feature integration ability required to develop comprehensive risk assessment and clinical decision support [11, 12]. New developments in medical image analysis have led to more advanced architectures that aim to overcome the shortcomings of their classical CNN-based counterparts. Swin Transformers and Vision Transformer (ViTs) are examples of models based on transformers, which have demonstrated better results in multiple medical imaging tasks by utilizing the idea of self-attention to learn long-range spatial interactions [13, 14]. On the same note, more advanced CNNs, such as nnU-Net, Attention U-Net, and nested U-Net, have demonstrated better segmentation results by providing enhanced feature representations and utilising skip connections [15].

In addition, existing methods often run independently of the richness of clinical domain knowledge and evidence-based guidelines that guide expert clinical decision-making. Most current AI systems lack built-in clinical reasoning functionality, which restricts their practical implementation in clinical settings that require treatment decisions to consider various factors, such as the patient’s anatomical peculiarities, clinical manifestations, treatment goals, and risk-benefit calculations [16, 17]. Also, the majority of current strategies address only the accuracy of segmentation and do not consider the key requirement of the quantification of uncertainty and risk, which are important in clinical translation and regulatory approval [18].

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have become an effective medical image synthesis and data augmentation paradigm and are applied in image-to-image translation, super-resolution, and domain adaptation [19]. Recent work has examined the applications of GAN in dental imaging, such as synthetic tooth generation and image enhancement, and demonstrated that adversarial training can enhance both the learning of features and the robustness of models under this strategy [20]. Nevertheless, current GAN-based solutions in the field of dental imaging have mainly been devoted to generating images and not utilizing the adversarial model to extract features and support clinical decisions.

Research gap identifcation

Several critical research gaps remain in dental treatment planning and risk assessment. First, current deep learning-based diagnosis methods provide insufficient multimodal feature integration and have limited capacity to encode and use clinical metadata, hindering comprehensive analysis of complex cases synthesized from heterogeneous sources for optimal planning [21, 22]. Second, most of the well-established deep learning models do not explicitly incorporate clinical domain knowledge and evidence-based guidelines into their analytical frameworks, leaving a persistent gap between technical performance and real-world clinical utility [23]. Third, AI-driven image analysis has largely emphasized classification and segmentation over probabilistic risk assessment and rigorous uncertainty quantification, both of which are essential for trustworthy clinical decision-support systems [24]. Lastly, the performance in terms of computational efficiency and real-time capabilities of clinical deployment are not well addressed. The deep learning models are accurate and efficient, they tend to be computationally intensive and hence cannot be used in practice in clinical settings where real-time processing is a crucial factor [25, 26]. Therefore, the required modification can be made in the well-established architectures to fulfil high performance support and satisfy clinical workflow requirements.

Proposed solution and innovation framework

The research gaps outlined above can be addressed by introducing structural modifications to a well-established GAN architecture, enabling the integration of multiple innovative components within a unified framework tailored to dental treatment planning and risk assessment. We refer to the resulting architecture as the modified GAN (mGAN) framework. It comprises three main modules, (i) Training Data module that handles the morphological features extraction such as textural, edge and contrast by incorporating U-Net architecture to fully enhance feature characterization; (ii) Generator module that handles the synthetic ground truth generation through the novel W-Net architecture with probabilistic risk distribution modeling; (iii) a Discriminator module that processes both real and synthetic samples to allow the accurate prediction of the surgical treatment necessity with the built-in uncertainty measurement. As a result, the proposed mGAN enables comprehensive analysis of both planar and volumetric anatomical relationships.

Research contributions

The proposed mGAN makes three principal contributions. (1) We introduce a W-Net backbone that combines U-Net and V-Net, yielding dual-path encoders and a skip-gated decoder to support robust multi-scale feature analysis tailored to complex dental anatomy. (2) We integrate a Large Language Model (LLM) to guide hyperparameter tuning and condition the generator with domain knowledge distilled from mandibular anatomy and prevalent dental pathology, thereby extending the system beyond purely data-driven pattern recognition. (3) We propose Probabilistic Feature Fusion (PFF), an adaptive multimodal integration mechanism that assigns dynamic weights to heterogeneous inputs based on local image content and LLM-provided clinical context, and that produces probabilistic maps of root–canal contact points to preserve interpretability and clinical relevance.

Methods

This study proposes a clinically grounded framework to decide whether surgical intervention is warranted by inferring the relationship between mandibular molar roots and the inferior alveolar canal, together with adjacent soft tissue/bony status, from routine dental imaging. Because ultrasound while theoretically suited to measuring soft-tissue thickness suffers from noise and lacks open datasets [27], we pivot to CT/CBCT and dental radiographs and explicitly encode radiographic primitives (tooth density, boundaries, curvature/angulation, radiolucencies, cortical thickness, enamel/dentin cracks, and mandibular–maxillary morphology). A training-data module built on a U-Net extractor derives texture, edge, contrast, and curvature maps from X-rays as “true” anatomical exemplars. A generator, our W-Net (a structurally modified U-/V-hybrid with probability feature fusion, as seen in Fig. 1), conditions on CBCT and weak priors distilled from large language models (LLMs) to synthesize risk-aware pseudo ground-truth maps that depict the likelihood and spatial extent of root–canal “extended contact points. A PatchGAN-style discriminator jointly enforces anatomical realism and outputs a calibrated probability that surgery is required for a given test case. Training couples Dice+cross-entropy with surface/curvature penalties, adversarial loss, and a light consistency term to align synthesized risk with LLM-derived priors, enabling robust learning from limited manual labels. LLM-derived conditioning (GPT-4 via Sentence-BERT encoding, c ∈ R¹²⁸) embeds structured clinical domain knowledge—including Winter’s classification labels, IAC proximity categories, and cortical plate status—into the generator and discriminator via conditional batch normalization (see Sect. 2, Module-II and Module-III for full details). The system generates anatomical masks, continuous risk maps and decision score at inference based on predicted minimal root-to-canal distance indicators and cortical thinning indicators. It is reported that performance is not only with Dice/HD95 but also with clinically aligned measures, distance error, contact classification and sensitivity at fixed specificity such that improvements are directly related to surgical decision-making.

Proposed mGAN architecture

Multimodal Input Design (2D OPG vs. 3D CBCT): To be fully methodologically transparent, the following statement explains that the proposed mGAN has dual-input architecture. The architecture deliberately takes two different imaging modalities as input using different pipeline stages: (1) The input of the architecture is 2D panoramic OPG X-ray images (public Kaggle dataset), which are only used in Module-I (Training Data Module) to obtain 2D anatomical feature maps (texture, edge, contrast, curvature), which are upsampled to 3D through trilinear interpolation and sent to the generator. (2) The main input of Module-II (W-Net Generator) and Module-III (Discriminator) is 3D CBCT volumes (1,500 scans of the institutional archive) to conduct volumetric segmentation, prediction of risk maps, and all quantitative evaluation. Thus, they are rightfully 2D data and do not actualise the eventual CBCT-based analysis. Predictive performance metrics in the results section are calculated. The rationale behind this multimodal design that is two-stage is due to the complementary nature of 2D OPGs that are ubiquitous to the bootstrapping of features and 3D CBCT that is essential to perform precise volumetric measurement.

Dataset, annotation protocol and experimental setup

Dataset composition and source

This research study is utilized 1,500 Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCTs) obtained in the institutional oral and maxillofacial surgery unit dental radiology archive between the years 2018 and 2023. The acquisition of each volume of the CBCT was performed with the help of a Planmeca ProMax 3D scanner (voxel resolution: 0.2 mm isotropic, field of view: 8x8 cm). A random sampling of the cases was made to have equal distribution of mandibular third molar impaction classes (Winter classification: vertical n=420, mesioangular n=385, horizontal n=340, distoangular n=355) with known surgical assessment recorded in the post-operative record.

Patient demographics

The sample size consisted of 847 women and 653 men (mean age was 28.412.6 years old, with a range of 18 to 64 years). The patients with the existing nerve conditions, previous surgery on the mandible (ipsilateral), or incomplete imaging facilities were excluded. Data on ethnicity was not undertaken because it was not a confounding factor in this imaging task.

Annotation protocol and inter-annotator agreement

Two board-certified oral and maxillofacial surgeons (Annotator-A: 12 years’ experience; Annotator-B: 9 years’ experience) annotated mandibular third molar root (M3M) and inferior alveolar canal (IAC) segmentation masks independently with ITK-SNAP version 3.8. A third senior radiologist resolved disputes that had a Dice of more than 0.85. Cohen kappa (= 0.87, strong agreement) and mean Dice agreement of 0.91=0.03 in all 1,500 cases was used to assess inter-annotator agreement. The evaluated annotations were the final ground truth upon which the training and the evaluation were done.

Train/validation/test split and class imbalance handling

The dataset has been divided into three distinct samples 70/15/15 split (train: n=1,050; validation: n=225; test: n=225), preserving the class distribution of contact (n=487, 32.5%) versus no-contact (n=1,013, 67.5%) cases across all splits. To address class imbalance in surgical necessity labels, we employed a combination of oversampling minority-class cases using CBCT-specific elastic deformation augmentation and a class-weighted loss function (α_contact = 2.07, inversely proportional to class frequency) during training. Data augmentation applied to training data included random rotation (±15°), random flipping (axial/sagittal), Gaussian noise (σ = 0.01–0.05), and intensity scaling (0.85–1.15) to improve generalization.

Training implementation details

The proposed mGAN framework was implemented in PyTorch 2.0 (CUDA 11.8) and trained on two NVIDIA A100 GPUs (80 GB VRAM each) for 200 epochs using an Adam optimizer (learning rate η = 1×10−4, β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999, weight decay = 1×10−4). The batch size was 4 for 3D volumes (patch size 128×128×64). A cosine annealing learning rate schedule with warm restarts (T_0 = 50 epochs) was applied. The composite loss function comprised adversarial loss (λ_adv = 1.0), Dice + cross-entropy segmentation loss (λ_seg = 5.0), surface distance penalty (λ_surf = 2.0), curvature penalty (λ_curv = 0.5), and LLM consistency term (λ_llm = 1.5). All baseline models were retrained under identical conditions on the same hardware, data splits, and augmentation pipeline to ensure a fair comparison. Cross-validation was performed using 5-fold stratified cross-validation on the training set, with the reported results corresponding to performance on the held-out test set.

Module-II: generator module

The Generator is designed as a W-Net, which is a hybrid architecture combining U-Net and V-Net, as seen in Fig. 2. The U-Net component ensures the use of 2D feature priors (such as the TDM features), while the V-Net component operates over 3D spatial data (i.e., CBCT volumes). The generator takes several inputs and conditions them through multiple stages, creating output images that simulate the “ground truth” image needed for training the discriminator. The key idea here is to generate fake samples with varying probabilities of root-canal contact points, allowing the network to learn anatomical boundaries, surface interactions, and risk distributions.

Module-III: discriminator module

The Discriminator Dφ in the proposed mGAN framework is designed as a PatchGAN-style architecture that jointly enforces anatomical realism and outputs a calibrated surgical-necessity probability. Unlike standard binary discriminators, the proposed discriminator operates at the patch level to assess local anatomical coherence, which is essential for capturing fine-grained root-canal boundary interactions. The discriminator receives two types of inputs: (i) real samples represented by the anatomically gated true-sample tensors Z_true(x) derived from the Training Data Module, and (ii) fake/synthetic samples G̃(s) produced by the W-Net Generator.

Architecture and forward pass

Uncertainty-aware surgical decision

The discriminator uses Monte Carlo Dropout to make inference to provide a calibrated uncertainty quantification. The system calculates the mean surgical score µ surg and epistemic uncertainty σ surg over N stochastic forward passes N = 20. The cases that exceed the uncertainty bound will be sent to the experts instead of automatically arriving at a decision, and patient safety is maintained in cases of uncertainties. The discriminator loss is a composite loss that sums adversarial loss, surface distance penalty, and a consistency loss. Hence, it ensures the output risk of the discriminator is consistent with the priors generated by the LLM using minimal manual annotating.

Results

In this section, covers detailed qualitative and quantitative comparison of the proposed mGAN (W-Net) with 12 state-of-the-art techniques (UNet, UNet++, ESPNetv2, KCNet, V-Net, nnU-Net, DeepLab v3+, Attention U-Net, 3D ResNet-UNet, UCTransNet, Swin and TSTNet) on evaluation metrics using a dataset of 1,500 CBCT scans. The major results include better performance using DSC of 0.925 + 0.019 (compared to best baseline TSTNet 0.911 + 0.023), contact detection F1-score 0.932 + 0.020 (1.6% improvement), and risk correlation 0.891 + 0.027 (5.2% improvement). The performance of distance measurement accelerates significantly with RMSE of 0.36 + 0.08 mm (20% lower than TSTNet, 46.3% lower than U-Net) and the network can also reduce computational cost by 5.1% relative to the Standard GAN baseline (847.3 GFLOPs versus 892.5 GFLOps), indicating that the proposed innovations do not produce a disproportionate lack of performance. Only cross-institutional validation shows better generalization with 2.3% performance degradation as compared to best baselines (3.2). Ablation experiments indicate that Probability Feature Fusion does not add significant improvement to the performance (1.8%), LLM integration means the risk correlation has the improvement by 6.4% and dual path encoding the improvement on all metrics are evenly spread. The remaining qualitative and quantitative findings are reported in the following subsections; their clinical interpretation, comparison with prior work, and the limitations of the study are addressed separately in the Discussion.

Local Contrast (Std, 15 × 15) Overlay image is the local contrast, the standard deviation of a 15 × 15 window (see: Figure 3(G)). Regions of high local variation in the image are emphasized, e.g. the interface of tooth enamel and dentin or a radiolucent bone. Local contrast helps in identifying slight tissue differences which might be cavities in the tissue or bone density problems. This is done by the unsharp mask method that helps to increase the contrast by subtracting the smoothed image of the original image. The process brings out tiny details including minor cracks or minor bone changes, and it is simpler to see the tooth fractures, enamel defects, or even the formations of cavity.

Ablation study: component contribution analysis

On the other hand, the ablation study of LLM Integration, it was found that Risk Correlation demonstrates the most significant drop to 0.834 ± 0.038 (-6.4%), whereas DSC and F1-score demonstrate more stable decreases to 0.913 ± 0.022 (-1.3%) and 0.921 ± 0.023 (-1.2%) respectively. Thus, it shows that the integration of LLM is almost entirely boosting the capacity of the system to integrate the clinical domain knowledge and increase the accuracy of risk predictions as opposed to elementary segmentation functionality. The decrease of Clinical Accuracy to 0.908 ± 0.024 (-1.6%) indicates that domain knowledge integration is important in clinical translation. The increase in the computational cost by 831.7 GFLOps (-1.8%) implies that the implementation of the LLM has almost no computational overheads and significant clinical gains. Deletion of Dual-Path Encoding reports equal effects on metrics with DSC dropping to 0.918 ± 0.021 (-0.8%), F1-score to 0.925 ± 0.022 (-0.8%), and Risk Correlation to 0.867 ± 0.031 (-2.7%). It is a substantial payoff in all the aspects considered where it shows significant drop in computational cost of 763.4 GFLOPs (-9.9). The Skip Gating ablation has the least performance relative, DSC reducing to 0.921 ± 0.020 (-0.4%) and F1-score to 0.928 ± 0.021 (-0.4%) and has comparable computational costs of 825.9 GFLOPs (-2.5%). The cumulative effect of all the innovative components can be seen by comparing it with the Standard GAN Baseline, but the performance difference of 4.0% in DSC, 3.4% in Contact F1, 11.7% in Risk Correlation, and 4.1% in Clinical Accuracy is achieved at the cost of 5.1% improvement in computational efficiency. This overall analysis of ablation substantiates the role played by each component of the architecture and shows that the proposed mGAN framework innovations offer significant performance advantages, sufficient to warrant the complexity of architecture, risk assessment strengths and clinical translation strengths. Regarding metric selection: DSC and HD95 are established standards for volumetric segmentation benchmarking; RMSE and CDE were chosen because sub-millimeter spatial accuracy directly determines whether a root-canal proximity is clinically actionable; RSAA captures the completeness of root surface reconstruction; CVP reflects canal morphology preservation; and Risk Correlation links computational outputs to the binary surgical decision outcome. We acknowledge that not all 23 metrics are equally independent or clinically motivated, and future work will focus on a reduced, clinically validated metric panel co-designed with oral and maxillofacial surgeons.

Discussion

This study set out to determine whether structural modifications to a generative adversarial network could support surgical decision-making for mandibular third molar (M3M) extraction by characterising the spatial relationship between the M3M roots and the inferior alveolar canal (IAC). Across a held-out test set drawn from 1,500 expert-annotated CBCT scans, the proposed mGAN framework outperformed all twelve baseline architectures on the segmentation, distance and classification metrics reported above, achieving a Dice Similarity Coefficient of 0.925, a root-mean-square distance error of 0.36 mm, and a sensitivity and specificity of 0.918 and 0.967, respectively, for surgical-necessity classification. The improvements over the strongest baseline (TSTNet) were accompanied by non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals on the primary measures and remained statistically significant after Bonferroni correction, with the exception of the contact-detection F1 score, which is therefore interpreted with caution pending external replication.

Clinical implications

From a clinical perspective, accurate sub-millimetre localisation of the root–canal interface is directly relevant to the planning of high-risk M3M extractions, in which inadvertent injury to the inferior alveolar nerve carries both patient morbidity and medico-legal consequences. The segmentation and distance-estimation accuracy reported here, combined with the uncertainty-aware decision mechanism that refers low-confidence cases for expert review, suggests that the framework is best positioned as a decision-support aid rather than an autonomous classifier, supporting evidence-based treatment planning where nerve-injury prevention is paramount. A processing time of approximately 2–2.2 min per case is compatible with integration into existing clinical workflows. These observations should nonetheless be read as indicators of potential clinical utility under controlled evaluation conditions, and not as evidence of improved patient outcomes.

Comparison with existing approaches

The performance gains relative to conventional segmentation networks such as U-Net and V-Net, and to more recent transformer-based and hybrid models, are consistent with the design rationale of the framework. The ablation analysis indicates that the integration of large language model (LLM)-derived clinical priors contributes most strongly to risk correlation, which showed the largest degradation (6.4%) when this component was removed, whereas Probabilistic Feature Fusion and dual-path encoding contributed more evenly across the segmentation and classification metrics. This pattern is consistent with the central hypothesis that encoding structured clinical domain knowledge, rather than relying on data-driven pattern recognition alone, is the principal source of the observed improvement in risk estimation, while the components of the W-Net backbone primarily improve boundary localisation and volumetric overlap.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. First, the reference standard was established by two oral and maxillofacial surgeons with adjudication by a senior radiologist on the same institutional data; the evaluation therefore represents internal validation rather than independent external clinical validation. Second, the qualitative analysis (Figs. 7 and 8) shows that the discriminator can fail to flag clinically relevant root–canal proximity when the segmented contact region falls below the pixel-count threshold, producing false-negative predictions in subtle cases, and the sensitivity of the model to such fine interactions requires further refinement. Third, the framework was developed and tested on retrospective CBCT volumes acquired with a single scanner at one institution, which may limit generalisability across imaging vendors, acquisition protocols and patient populations. Finally, performance was reported across a broad panel of metrics that are not all mutually independent or equally clinically motivated, so the reported effect sizes should be interpreted with this redundancy in mind.

Future directions

Future work will prioritise multi-centre external validation using data from different scanners and clinical sites, together with prospective studies that compare the recommendations of the framework against real patient outcomes and clinician performance before any clinical deployment is considered. We further plan to improve the sensitivity of the discriminator to subtle, low-pixel-count contacts, and to consolidate the evaluation around a reduced, clinically validated panel of metrics co-designed with oral and maxillofacial surgeons, so that reported performance maps more directly onto surgical decision-making.

Conclusion

This paper has introduced a modified Generative Adversarial Network (mGAN) with W-Net architecture for dental surgical decision support. A systematic comparison of the framework with 12 established methods on quantitative measures was conducted with a 1,500-scan CBCT dataset. According to the results, the proposed mGAN has consistent improvements over the assessed baselines with differences in performance based on evaluating metrics and baseline ranging between 1.3% and 46.3%, respectively. It is important to note that the accuracy of distance measurement went up to 46.3% over U-Net and risk correlation went up by 19.9%, which indicates that there is a beneficial effect of the integration of LLM-derived priors and Probabilistic Feature Fusion to anatomical precision. The ablation research testifies to the fact that all the proposed architectural elements lead to the positive influence on the overall performance. These findings are positive to the future of AI-assisted dental surgical planning systems but further clinical validation, external cohort studies, and comparison to clinician performance are required before any clinical implementation is advised. The existing results should be regarded as the evidence of a concept that proves the possibility of such a framework in the context of a computational test.
