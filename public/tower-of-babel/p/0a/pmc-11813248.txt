Occupational noise exposure and its effects among mill workers: A narrative review
Authors: Siti Najihah Abdul Salam, Nurul Farahana Kamaludin, Normah Awang, Anuar Ithnin, Dayana Hazwani Mohd Suadi Nata, Nur Zakiah Mohd Saat
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
There are many possibilities for noise exposure in industrial workplaces, including sectors that extensively use heavy machinery in processing each product. Various studies indicate a causal relationship between noise exposure and auditory/nonauditory effects among workers. Noise exposure poses risks to health and workers’ hearing acuity and safety. This review paper aims to identify and summarize occupational noise exposure by examining the auditory and nonauditory effects among mill workers. The mills included in the study are palm oil, lumber, textiles, rice, sugarcane, flour, paper, and sawmills. A two-step methodology was used to achieve the aims of this narrative review. The literature search was conducted by gathering PubMed and Google Scholar papers, focusing on occupational noise in different occupational mill environments. Grey’s literature was conducted by screening the worldwide national standard for the laws, guidelines, and other related information based on this topic. A total of 22 articles published have been included. The content of each publication was subsequently summarized. Most of the machine areas within the mills emitted high level of noise. The auditory effects of occupational noise exposure among mill workers were noise-induced hearing loss and hearing impairment. Hypertension, stress, communication disturbance, cardiovascular disease, and headache were the nonauditory effects experienced by workers. The recommendations provided in this review for mitigating noise exposure are derived from the hierarchy of control. Overall, most mill workers are exposed to a high noise level exceeding the standard noise exposure limit. Preventive actions should be taken to address and reduce work-related injuries among mill workers worldwide.

INTRODUCTION

Sound and noise play important roles in influencing our experiences and surroundings. Sound, defined as vibrations that flow through the air or another medium and can be heard by the human ear, has the ability to generate emotions, communicate information, and create an atmosphere. Sound has the capacity to alter our moods and perceptions, from relaxing musical melodies to the quiet rustling of leaves in the breeze. Noise, on the other hand, is frequently regarded as unwanted sound. “Unwanted sound” refers to any disturbance within a specified frequency band.[1] Decibels (dB) are units of sound pressure used to quantify noise. Human hearing normally varies between 0 dB, the threshold of hearing, and 140 dB, the threshold of pain.[2] The higher the decibel level, the more significant the impact, particularly on individuals’ health. High noise levels can cause stress, distraction, and other health problems.

Generally, noise is present in every human activity and can usually be classified as environmental and occupational noise. Environmental noise is defined as any undesired or detrimental sound that exists in the surrounding environment. This noise can come from various sources, including transportation, industry, building sites, and recreational activities. On the other hand, occupational noise in the workplace and work environment involves noisy tasks and machines. Not all noise disturbs the listener, depending on the high and low noise levels produced in decibels, especially if repeated overexposure occurs.[3] Noise can indeed be considered an occupational hazard in many workplaces, posing risks to the health and safety of employees.

According to the International Occupational Hygiene Association,[4] occupational hazards can be divided into several categories, such as physical, chemical, ergonomic, biological, and psychosocial hazards. Noise is categorized as a type of physical hazard and is recognized as a common hazard frequently found in the workplace. Noise exposure at the workplace is likely to occur, especially during product processing, which has various activities involving heavy machinery used throughout product production. Exposure to various occupational hazards may contribute to work-related injuries or occupational diseases.[5]

George[6] defines occupational disease as any illness associated with a particular occupation or industry. It can be classified into six groups: physical, chemical, biological, ergonomic, psychological agents, and accidents that are present in the work environment or are otherwise encountered during employment.[7] High levels of occupational noise exposure pose a risk to hearing acuity and employee safety.[8910] As for safety and precautions at the workplace, signals from sirens and alarms are vital to alert workers when an emergency occurs.

The occurrence of emergency cases at a workplace must be taken seriously as it could threaten the safety, health, and life of employees, thus eventually causing death. Based on statistics, a total of 2.78 million deaths are recorded each year as a result of occupational accidents or work-related illnesses.[11] Adverse health effects due to continuous exposure to high levels of occupational noise can be divided into auditory and nonauditory effects. Auditory effects are effects on hearing, such as hearing loss and hearing impairment, while nonauditory effects include impacts on the central and autonomic nervous system and the entire organism.[12] Most evidence suggests that these effects have a causal relationship with noise exposure. The statistic shows that an estimated 16 to 24% of hearing impairment worldwide is work-related, with an estimated health impact measurement of over 4 million disability-adjusted life-years.[13] The number of cases of hearing impairment related to occupational noise increased from 2013 until 2019.[14]

Occupations at high risk for noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL) include manufacturing, transportation, mining, construction, and agriculture.[10] This is because machine noise in various agro-based industries is a significant occupational hazard for workers.[15] Examples of mill industries exposed to high levels of occupational noise are palm oil, lumber, textiles, rice, sugarcane, flour, paper, and sawmills. Noise sources in mill industries include power transmission systems, seed cleaners, oil expellers, saw filling machines, weaving machines, and winding machines.[161718]

There are several types of noise patterns produced by the machine. It can be divided into three: steady, fluctuating, and impulse.[19] Most noisy machines generate noise patterns like these. Steady noise can be described as steady noise levels, with the meter reading on slow response fluctuating not more than 3 dB. Noise levels that are not steady, with meter readings on slow response varying over a 3 to 10 dB range, are known as fluctuating noise. Impulse or impact noise is a sudden, loud noise that significantly differs from the typical noise levels experienced in the workplace. Based on various evidence, the type of noise commonly found in mill industries is steady noise.[1618202122] This has caused workers in mill industries to experience effects such as NIHL, hearing impairment, hypertension, stress, communication disturbances, cardiovascular disease, and headaches.[15161820212324252627282930313233343536]

This review aims to analyze and consolidate data on occupational noise exposure, focusing on the adverse health effects, encompassing both auditory and nonauditory impacts, experienced by mill workers to disseminate knowledge, insights, and recommendations regarding occupational noise exposure. This review has the potential to provide valuable insights to society, particularly to industries, in addressing excessive noise levels that surpass regulatory thresholds.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A two-step methodology was used to achieve the aims of this narrative review. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram of the search procedure for narrative review is shown in Figure 1. The literature search involved collecting papers from sources such as PubMed and Google Scholar, with an emphasis on occupational noise in several occupational mill settings, and typically, the summarization of the content of each article was done. Titles and abstracts were screened. Data sources comprised the relevant information on noise exposure in palm oil mills, lumber mills, textile mills, rice mills, sugarcane mills, paper mills, sawmills, and flour mills. All those settings were included in the review. The literature search on the database used terms such as noise, auditory, nonauditory, mills, and other relevant terms from the retrieved papers. Next, grey literature was conducted by screening the worldwide national standard for the laws, guidelines, and other related information based on this topic. Full texts were obtained for those that met the criteria or where the abstract contained insufficient information to exclude. A total of 22 articles were published on the issue of noise exposure related to auditory and nonauditory health effects and recommended control measures were included, reviewed, and cited. Occupational Noise Exposure in Mills

Palm Oil and Oil Mills

Various processes are performed to produce oil, including cleaning and crushing seeds, expulsion of oil, and filtering. A study on noise surveys was conducted in the northeastern region of India and involved three well-known oil mills.[16] It revealed that 26% of workers were exposed to high noise levels that exceeded the US standard of noise exposure limit of 85 dB.[16] In another study, environmental noise from two selected palm oil mills was compared simultaneously,[23] showing that some areas of the two mills had excessive noise exceeding 90 dB. As this study was conducted in Malaysia, the cut-off point was based on the standard stated in the Factories and Machinery (Noise Exposure) Regulations 1989 (90 dB).[38] A study conducted at five palm oil mills in Sabah also reported that mean noise exposure was 96.1 [standard deviation (SD) 4.8] dB(A).[24] A retrospective longitudinal observational study was conducted at an olive oil mill in Spain involving 115 workers over a decade. Its findings revealed a significant increase in the noise level to which workers were exposed.[25] Areas with noisy environments are the nut plant, clarification area, engine room, and boiler houses.[26] The nut plant and clarification area are shown in Figure 2.

Noisy machinery at nut plant and clarification area.

Low-frequency noise dominated the oil mills.[16] The primary sources of high noise at oil mills included seed cleaners, power transmission systems, and oil expellers.[16] This is due to poor machine maintenance and the use of bamboo rods to prevent the belt from falling from inconsistent tackles.[16] Ghani machines were used for crushing seeds.[39] Linguistic evidence suggests that it is from these two crushing systems that press for oilseeds and sugarcane developed in the form of a mortar-and-pestle arrangement powered by animals.[40] This system is commonly called the Ghani.[40]

However, the noise levels at the three oil mills were in the range of 85 dB, which included the noise generated by Ghani machines, electric motors, and oil removers.[16] However, in some areas, namely workshops, general stores, and loading ramps, the noise did not exceed 90 dB, which was Malaysia’s cut-off point (90 dB).[26] The noise characteristics at oil mills are continuous noise type, with levels ranging from 78 to 92 dB (A), noise distribution with low frequency, and a duration of noise exposure of 3 hours.[16] Thus, it can be summarized that most of the machines used in these oil industries, whether modern or traditional, were the main contributors to the high level of noise generated.

Lumber Mills

A study on Canadian workers at a lumber mill found that exposure to occupational noise among workers was very high.[17] The mean equivalent continuous sound levels recorded was 91.7 dB (A), which is well above British Columbia’s standard exposure limit of 85 dB (A).[17] Although the prevalence of hearing protection is high among lumber mill workers, the study revealed that hearing protection devices do not provide complete protection from NIHL due to the high level noise exposure.[17] A cohort study among lumber mill workers found that the use of hearing protection devices was associated with noise exposure levels and age.[41] Observations of 52 jobs at the lumber mill found that only four jobs were below the noise exposure limit.[17] The mean noise levels obtained for 28 and 4 jobs were over 90 and 100 dB (A), respectively.[17] The areas with the highest exposure to high-frequency noise were the planning and saw-filing areas.[13] Areas for by-products and sawmills were dominated by high exposure to low-frequency noise.[17] Therefore, it can be concluded that the noise characteristics at lumber mills include a noise level of 91.7 dB (A) for an 8-hour duration of noise exposure, and the frequency distribution at these mills is dominated by both high- and low-frequency of noise.[17] This is very concerning because US statistics estimated that 22 million workers were exposed to dangerous noise levels.[424344]

Textile Mills

Noise in the work environment has become commonplace in this sophisticated modern age.[45464748] The use of machines in the textile industry has resulted in high noise exposure for their operators. A cross-sectional study conducted in Myanmar found that 66.4% of workers were exposed to noise levels exceeding 85 dB (A).[20] This means that only 33.6% of workers were exposed to noise levels below 85 dB (A).[20] A Thai study was conducted in a large-scale textile mill.[18] The findings revealed that weavers at the textile mills were exposed to severe noise levels.[18] Although efforts to increase worker productivity were made by reducing the distance between machines, this measure caused an increase in the noise level in the weaving section, recorded as 101.3 dB (A).[18] Similarly, a study conducted in a textile mill located in Tanzania showed that 80.3% of workers were exposed to noise levels exceeding 95 dB (A) in the noisiest loom shade department.[21] Previous studies were conducted in the cotton mill room of a modern textile mill to determine the type of noise occurring in the room based on the chronic effects of noise exposure among rats.[49] The results revealed damage to the ciliated cells.[49] This indirectly indicated that operators in the cotton mill room were exposed to noise levels that could cause adverse effects on health.[49]

Another study found that almost all textile mill areas (92.5%) exceeded China’s standard noise exposure limit.[27] The areas that recorded noise intensities above the standard 85 dB (A) included the winding, rotation 1, rotation 2, cleaning, weave 1, and weave 2 areas.[27] The type of noise in the mill was steady noise.[27] A total of 25% of sites recorded noise intensity levels over 100 dB (A).[27] The wear rate of earplugs was only 25.1%, despite workers being provided with earplugs to protect them from noise exceeding 85 dB (A).[27] A cross-sectional study conducted at a textile mill in Nagpur, India revealed that out of 228 selected employees, 47 workers were exposed to noise levels above 85 dB (A). In contrast, 88 and 93 workers were exposed to noise levels of 70 to 85 dB (A) and below 70 dB (A), respectively.[28] Various studies conducted at textile mills worldwide have revealed exposure to noise levels that exceed the national standard noise exposure limits. The characteristics of noise at textile mills are a steady noise type, ranging from 80.1 to 113.5 dB (A), with an 8-hour duration of noise exposure.[18202127] Prolonged exposure to noise can cause various harmful health effects that cannot be neglected.[50] Therefore, an effective prevention program should be implemented to reduce noise propagation, which will consequently result in fewer payments of compensation for occupational deafness because, in the United States, annual compensation is approximately 242.4 million dollars.[51]

Rice Mills

A study revealed that 5 out of 8 rice mills had a sound pressure level of more than 85 dB (A).[15] This study’s findings reflected those by Rinawati et al.,[22] , as the rice mills exceeded the threshold limit value (TLV), with the highest noise level intensity for the threshing area recorded at 115.39 dB (A). The noise sources in rice mills include paddy cleaners, electric motors without enclosures, rubber roll shellers, compartment separators, sieve shakers, and rice cleaners.[15] The type of noise is high-frequency. Meanwhile, the machinery that produces high-intensity noise at the rice mill includes thresher machines, skin crushing machines, bleaching machines, and sifting areas, with recorded noise intensities of 110.15, 109.39, 107.97, and 85.56 dB (A), respectively.[22] These findings are inconsistent with the TLV. In terms of personal noise exposure, 26% of workers were exposed to noise levels exceeding 85 dB(A).[15] Conversely, the drying area was the only site that remained within the TLV, recording a level of 80 dB(A).[22] Overall, the characteristics of noise at rice mills are of a steady noise type, with noise levels ranging from 80.0 to 110.15 dB (A) for an 8-hour duration of noise exposure.[22] All these values indicate that rice mill workers are exposed to high noise levels from various machines.

Sugarcane Mills

A descriptive evaluation study in a Guatemalan sugarcane mill found that noise doses ranged from 50.9% doses for the lowest to 25,174% doses for the highest, which exceeded the Guatemalan permissible exposure limit of 100% [85 dB (A) time-weighted average].[37] The highest mean noise doses observed were for evaporator cleaners (15,761%) and juice extractors (2047%).[37] While the dose for individuals revealed that 90% of the dosimeter samples ranged from 50 to 1200% of the dose.[37] Moreover, 48 out of 51 samples exceeded the prescribed noise exposure limit of 85 dB (A). Meanwhile, more than half of the total samples (66.6%) revealed daily noise exposure readings that exceeded 90 dB.[37] The noise characteristics at sugarcane mills range from 83 to 108.1 dB (A) for an 8-hour duration of noise exposure.[37] The primary noise sources were power generators, steam leaks, evaporator cleaning, steam ventilation, and shredders, with the dominant noise coming from vapor leakage and ventilation, which recorded the highest value against noise exposure.37 Thus, this study clearly showed that most workers in sugarcane mills were exposed to noise levels exceeding the limit recommended by the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health in the United States, which is 85 dB (A) with a 100% noise dose.

Paper Mills

A study was conducted in western and central Sweden involving 406 workers from four paper mills.[29] Inadequate safety practices have led to unsafe occupational noise exposure for workers in developing countries.[52] Workers in other occupational settings, such as manufacturing and mining, are also exposed to continuous high noise levels.[5354] Dosimetry equipment, area measurements, and perceived exposure survey measurements suggested that Swedish pulp mill workers were exposed to noise levels exceeding 85 dB (A), and dosimetry remained the preferred approach to measure the noise assessment.[30] Several other studies also stated that there were accurate readings of noise exposure when measured using dosimeters or sound level meters.[555657] Another study found that 406 paper mill workers were exposed to high daily noise levels ranging from 91 to 94 dB (A).[29] Overall, the noise characteristics at paper mills range from 80.1 to 94 dB (A) for an 8-hour duration of noise exposure, with the area monitoring levels between 80.0 and 89.1 dB (A).[2930] This indicates that paper mill workers are exposed to a high levels of noise. This is because the appropriate measurement for the time-weighted average to protect workers fully against NIHL is around 75 or 80 dB (A).[5859]

Saw Mills

A study conducted at a sawmill in Akluj town, Solapur district of Maharashtra, India, reported an average noise level of 79.3 dB (A).[31] The present research stated that the noise level at the sawmill location was 98.7 dB (A).[31] The sources of noise at sawmills were table saws, planning machines, Stenner 48 bandsaws, and sharpening machines.[60] The noise levels generated by these machines ranged from 89.76 ± 0.09 to 100.49 ± 0.20 dB(A), which are higher than the recommended value of 90 dB(A) set by the Federal Ministry of Environment and the Occupational Safety and Health Administration, except for sharpening machines.[6162] A study has also identified the issue of NIHL among sawmill workers. As a result, it is recommended that these workers use protective measures such as earplugs and earmuffs to mitigate the risk of NIHL.[31]

Flour Mills

The primary activities conducted at flour mills involve grinding wheat and spices, which are subjected to significant stress levels for approximately 6 to 8 hours per day. A comprehensive analysis was conducted on a sample of 62 flour mills located in Chandrapur City, situated in Central India.[32] The machine used in the process was the grinding machine.[32] The noise intensity at all flour mills ranged from 79.5 to 92 dB (A), averaging 85.02 dB (A) for 6 to 8 hours of noise exposure.[32] The source of noise was the vibrations from machines and electric motors.[32] The findings also revealed that operating the machines simultaneously increased the noise level.[32] In Nigeria, a study found that the highest noise exposure level at wheat processing was 99.4 dB (A).[33] Another study by Boateng and Amedofu,[63] found that the noise level at corn mills exceeded the 85 dB (A) limit recommended by the National Environmental Standards and Regulations Enforcement Agency.[64] Therefore, it can be summarized that workers in flour mills are also exposed to high noise levels. This problem should be taken seriously because acoustic overexposure among flour mill workers can significantly affect workers’ health.[65]

AUDITORY EFFECTS OF OCCUPATIONAL NOISE EXPOSURE

Noise-Induced Hearing Loss

NIHL is a condition characterized by a reduced auditory function caused by prolonged or excessive exposure to loud sounds. Workers may experience hearing loss when they cannot perceive at least 50% of the auditory stimuli presented to them, as evidenced by a hearing threshold over 25 dB for any frequency during an audiometric examination. Hearing loss can be categorized into four groups, namely mild, moderate, severe, and profound.[19] Mild hearing loss ranges from 26 to 40 dB, moderate hearing loss ranges from 41 to 70 dB, and severe hearing loss ranges from 71 to 91 dB.[19] If the mark equals or exceeds 91 dB, it reflects profound hearing loss.[19] NIHL has a few unique characteristics, including a condition wherein the inner ear’s cochlear hair cells are severely affected; therefore, NIHL is often sensorineural and, unfortunately, irreversible.[19] If there is a notch at a frequency of 3000, 4000, or 6000 Hz, the marked value subsequently recovering at 8000 Hz indicates the first sign of NIHL.[19] NIHL is a common work-related disease, especially among workers in the industrial sector.[66]

The organ within the inner ear, known as the cochlea, will be primarily affected when exposed to loud noises, damaging its cells and membrane.[67] Excessive noise exposure should be taken seriously because damage to auditory cells will eventually cause the workers to become deaf.[6869707172] Previous studies have also revealed a significant relationship between the occurrence of NIHL and noise intensity.[34] The intensity of noise affects the severity of damage to the inner ear’s hair cells. The cell damage will be more severe and permanent if the worker is exposed to higher noise levels. Palm oil mill workers exposed to a total noise intensity above 85 dB were 6.67 times more likely to experience NIHL than those exposed to a total noise intensity below 85 dB.[34] Similar findings were reported in a study of 146 textile mill employees, of whom 23.62% had high-frequency hearing loss.[27] No medical procedure can help restore the function of the damaged sensory cells because the cells cannot repair themselves naturally.[73] Another study found that 16% of oil mill workers complained that noise at their workplace adversely affected their hearing.[16] In India, 28% of sawmill workers had NIHL.[31]

Audiometric tests, as part of another study in Thailand, examined the fact that, in textile factories, weavers were the most likely to have hearing loss with some subjective symptoms.[18] Moreover, an extended work duration would result in a decrease in their hearing levels.[18] This observation aligns with the results of a previous investigation, wherein a significant correlation was established between the length of occupational engagement and the occurrence of NIHL.[34] Unexpectedly, the study’s results found that employees in their 20s or 30s suffered from NIHL.[18] Tinnitus was one of the symptoms often found in younger weavers.[18] Other often reported symptoms include earaches and distention of the ear.[18]

A palm oil mill study revealed that using personal protective equipment significantly impacted the occurrence of NIHL,34 with NIHL occurring 3.75 times more among employees who did not use hearing protective devices than those who did use them while working. However, another study revealed hearing loss in workers who used personal protective devices (PPDs).[18] The researcher attributes this to the PPDs not being well-suited to the workers.[18] Another possible reason could be that some workers only used cotton plugs.[18] A study suggested that efforts to control the risk of NIHL among mill workers should be implemented through global public health and prevention strategies.[46]

Another finding from the study conducted in Sabah mentioned that the prevalence of hearing loss among palm oil mill workers was 75%, with a mean duration of employment of 16.2 years (SD 9.7) among the workers.[24] Another study found that 76.6% of workers exposed to a noise level greater than 85 dB (A) were diagnosed with NIHL.[28] Most studies conducted in developing countries have found a high prevalence of NIHL among workers.[247475] Noisy machinery, extended overtime hours, advanced age, and marital status (P = 0.001) have been shown as essential variables contributing to hearing loss.[24] Some studies also revealed a significant relationship between hearing loss and smoking.[24767778] A longer duration of employment was also highly associated with hearing loss.[24747980] Therefore, it can be concluded that being exposed to noise levels above 85 dB (A) 8 hours a day can cause workers to develop NIHL.[81]

Hearing Impairment

Hearing impairment implies that workers’ average permanent hearing threshold level at 500, 1000, 2000, and 3000 Hz has shifted by 25 dB or more compared to the standard audiometric reference level.[19] Basically, the standard audiometric reference level is 0.[19] Results from a study found that five employees had language-frequency hearing loss, of which four had mild impairment, while one had a moderate impairment.[27] Most studies have indicated that those employed in mill occupations face an elevated likelihood of experiencing auditory impairment.

In Ibadan, Southwest Nigeria, 56% of mill workers exposed to noise levels ranging from 88 to 105 dB at their workstations had hearing impairment, with 0.6, 6.4, and 49% recording severe, moderate, and mild impairment, respectively.[35] None of the workers used hearing protection.[35] Moreover, 33% of workers from the control group were found to have mild (26%) or moderate (7%) hearing impairment, with no cases of severe impairment (P = 0.001).[35]

Hearing handicaps is another auditory effect among textile mill workers.[28] Hearing handicap means the disadvantage imposed by a hearing impairment on a person’s performance in daily activities.[28] Hearing handicap was rated in terms of hearing everyday speech in quiet environments. One-third of the textile mill workers in Nagpur were found to have hearing handicaps.[28] Exposure to a high noise level and having NIHL may increase the severity of hearing handicaps. The average hearing handicap was significantly higher (12.5%) among workers exposed to noise levels above 85 dB (A).[28] The risk of having a hearing handicap was 5.2 times higher in the exposed group compared to the non-exposed group.[28] Thus, it can be concluded that the high noise levels are associated with a high proportion of hearing handicaps and hearing impairment.

NONAUDITORY EFFECTS OF OCCUPATIONAL NOISE EXPOSURE

Hypertension

Hypertension can be defined as high blood pressure during the circulation of blood to the arterial walls of the body, where the average systolic blood pressure shows a reading equal to or above 140 mm Hg and the diastolic blood pressure is equal to or above 90 mm Hg.[82] Noise exposure can cause changes in various organ systems and other organs in the body.[83] An increase in blood pressure can occur when a person is exposed to noise, making it a risk factor contributing to cardiovascular disease.[7184] Several studies have revealed a dose–response relationship between noise intensity and hypertension among workers.[858687] This is further evidenced by a study in an oil mill that found the intensity of noise in the workplace to have a significant relationship with increased blood pressure.[34] These findings are similar to a study conducted on textile mill workers, which found that 7.93% of workers had hypertension after undergoing a physical examination.[27]

Variable comparisons between employees with and without hypertension revealed that age and high-frequency hearing threshold in the hypertensive group were high.[27] In total, 80 and 71.1% of workers exposed to noise levels above 85 dB showed an increase in systolic and diastolic blood pressure, respectively.[34] The results also showed significantly higher systolic and diastolic blood pressure in workers with high-frequency hearing loss.[27] Thus, it can be summarized that a high noise exposure levels are significantly associated with hypertension among workers.

Stress

A cross-sectional study that examined stress levels using O’Donnell’s inventory was conducted in seven sections across two mills.[23] The findings revealed that 21% of workers experienced stress in the workplace.[23] The study also stated that the prevalence of occupational stress was higher in areas exposed to a high levels of noise compared to those exposed to low levels.[23] However, most workers (79%) did not experience stress at work.[23] Another comparative study that assessed the level of occupational stress was conducted in five mills, involving seven sections, each using salivary alpha-amylase (sAA) as a biomarker indicator.[26] The results revealed that 100% of the workers in the group exposed to excessive noise had experienced occupational stress.[26] The average sAA activity showed a higher result in the stimulus condition for the group exposed to the high noise levels compared to the non-exposed group.[26] Although there were 11 risk factors, only personal noise exposure had a significant relationship with the stress levels.[26] Other findings revealed that workers with high noise sensitivity were 1.87 more likely to be stressed.[88] Thus, noise is a significant predictor of stress.[88]

Communication Disturbance

Several self-reported disabilities, deficiencies, and changes in behavior can occur if a person cannot understand speech due to noise exposure.[89] Exposure to noise potentially affects people working on tasks involving language, such as reading and cognitive processing, which demand attention, problem-solving, and remembering.[90] The most commonly reported symptom among employees is the need to speak loudly.[183033] Similar findings have also been reported from subjective responses, which revealed that 63% of oil mill workers experienced interference in their conversations.[16] At a rice mill, 49% of workers reported that noise interfered with their discussions.[15] This is because workers are required to raise their voices to be heard.[3091]

An increased risk of accidents also occurs when employees’ ability to hear and communicate is limited by loud noise.[92] This is because a noisy environment can pose an additional danger, as a sounding alarm may not be heard by workers. The efficiency of task execution is disrupted because difficulties in speech comprehension will distract the employees’ focus from completing the tasks entrusted to them. Approximately 16% of oil mill workers in India thought that noise interfered with their work.[16] Another study found that 26% of rice mill workers felt that noise interfered with their work.[15] A lower noise level ensures shorter time to complete a task compared to a high noise level.[93] Thus, to ensure work effectiveness among workers, management needs to take immediate action to reduce workplace noise.

Cardiovascular Disease

Awareness of noise exposure is vital even at moderate levels, as it can contribute to the identification of auditory and nonauditory effects that cannot be detected through hearing conservation programs.[94] Limitations of noise regulation may cause hearing loss, especially nonauditory health, to be overlooked.[82959697] The lack of consideration for comorbidities associated with NIHL in current rules is a significant factor. These comorbidities, such as cardiovascular disease, stress, melancholy, cognitive decline, and anxiety, are increasingly prevalent and should be taken into account.[9899100101] The cardiovascular, vestibular, and psychic systems and functions are all affected by noise.[102]

Long-term noise exposure has been associated with an increased risk of metabolic and cardiovascular diseases.[103] Risk factors identified as contributors to the development of cardiovascular disease are due to the chronic activation of the body’s stress response system.[104] A cohort study conducted in British Columbia involving 27,464 workers from 14 lumber mills with a mean noise exposure of 92 dB (A)[36] revealed that 2510 deaths occurred due to circulatory diseases during follow-up and deaths due to acute myocardial infarction increased in the entire cohort, with the subgroup without hearing protection showing a stronger association. Meanwhile, in Australia, workers who did not wear hearing protection devices were 53% more likely to have cardiovascular disease.[105] Exposure above 80 dB (A) to the impulsive noise type results in coronary heart disease, which may remain even after the workers retire.[106] Thus, it can be concluded that an increase in noise exposure at the workplace can increase the effect on the cardiovascular system; hence, the noise levels should be kept low.[107]

Headache

A study found that 23.07% of flour mill workers reported headaches at work, and 23.07% reported headaches that persisted even after work.[32] Noise exposure is associated with fatigue and headache.[108] In contrast to a study in Germany, the prevalence of headaches was not significantly associated with chronic noise exposure.[109] Meanwhile, another study confirmed that noise sensitivity is significantly associated with auditory effects such as disruption in daily rest, sleep quality, and night-sleep duration.[109]

Generally, noise may cause the most significant harmful effect on human health through auditory or nonauditory effects. Workers’ exposure to a combination of detrimental factors in the workplace is associated with worse health consequences, as shown in Table 2. Therefore, the noise source should be appropriately designed, and the appropriate recommendation should be implemented.

RECOMMENDATIONS

The recommendations outlined in Table 3 are categorized according to the hierarchy of control. The utilization of this hierarchical structure serves as a fundamental approach to regulating and managing the level of occupational hazards to protect the health and safety of workers.[110] Control systems that are both feasible and effective can be created by following a hierarchical approach. The hierarchy of control encompasses various strategies, including elimination, substitution, engineering control, administrative control, and personal protective equipment. The highest level of the hierarchy can be the most productive and safeguarding control measure compared to the lowest level. A hierarchical control system can effectively mitigate the risk of employee illness or injury in the workplace, enhancing overall safety.

CONCLUSION

In summary, it was observed that employees working in various industries such as palm oil, lumber, textile, rice, paper, flour, saw, and sugarcane mills were subjected to significant levels of occupational noise exposure. This review was capable of assessing the level of excessive noise exposure at those mills. Most of the machine areas within the mills emitted noise levels that were beyond those set by national standards. It can be concluded that the most critical combined effects of occupational exposure to noise and other factors are NIHL, hearing impairment, hypertension, disruptions in communication, cardiovascular ailments, stress, and headaches. Noise as an occupational factor contributes to high occupational stress levels. The matter at hand is of great concern due to its potential adverse effects on the general health of workers, particularly auditory issues, which may lead to nonauditory consequences. The results indicate a substantial correlation between noise level and the incidence of detrimental health outcomes.

If no consistent or effective countermeasures are implemented, the prevalence of noise-related illnesses will likely continue to escalate at an accelerated rate. It is imperative to promptly implement measures to mitigate NIHL, given its potential to manifest as a substantial occupational health concern. This condition can considerably diminish work efficacy and productivity inside the workplace. Controlling noise exposure in the workplace according to a hierarchy of control that begins with elimination, followed by substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls, and personal protective equipment aims to guide employers to overcome the occupational noise exposure problem. This is important in assisting employers to comply with and implement the legal requirements regarding noise exposure at the workplace.

LIMITATION

This review may not provide a comprehensive overview of the existing literature on this topic because it does not focus on specific research questions. However, this review may help identify literature gaps and offer valuable insights for future research directions.
