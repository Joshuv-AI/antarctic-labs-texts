Walk with the young

“One thing may save him (from becoming useless in his advancing years) … He must walk with the ‘boys,’ else he is lost; irrevocably lost; not all at once but by easy grades, … he must travel with the men who are doing the work of the world, ….”

From the Writings of William Osler, Counsels and Ideals (1905) & Selected Aphorisms, reproduced by Classics of Medicine Library, Birmingham, 1985.

Aristotle, the great philosopher and a pre-eminent biologist of his time, erred in his estimation of the role of the brain. Contrary to other beliefs at the time, he placed the heart ahead of the brain as the center of intelligence and originator of the nerves [1]. The term “intelligence” is hereby used in the broad sense as a system of cognitive function ranging from visual perception to social cognition. Whereas both the heart and brain have important functions, we now consider them to be an inseparable duo with separate but complementary functions [2]. Those who argue that the brain is the seat of intelligence and our inner selves have prevailed [1]. The brain is critical for our thoughts, actions, mental health, social interactions, and many other critical functions. In the current day, protection of the brain and brain health has become a worldwide priority [3]. Brain health and brain care programs are spreading globally and are dedicated to preventing brain injury and answering key questions about brain health and brain care. We now ponder such questions as: 1. What measures are necessary to maintain proper brain structure and function across the lifespan? 2. How may early life adverse events alter brain health later in life? 3. What preventatives administered in clinical practice may protect the brain, and what have clinical trials and observational studies taught us about avoiding brain injury? 4. What is the molecular underpinning of brain injury and brain health, and how may we enhance brain resistance and resilience? Furthermore, how do we assess cognitive fitness, and is there an economic argument to support brain health initiatives and programs?

With over 3.4 billion people affected worldwide by neurologic disorders (43.1 % of the population), there is much work ahead of us [3]. The brain may be viewed in economic terms as ‘Brain Capital’ whereby the brain substantially contributes to the betterment of society, making it a strategic economic resource deserving our full attention [4].

In this Special Issue of Cerebral Circulation Cognition & Behavior, the Editors have created a compendium of articles written by leading experts to answer important questions relating to brain health and brain care [5]. At the simplest level, brain health may be defined as maintenance of structure and function of the brain across the lifespan [6]. As our focus on brain health has sharpened, we now recognize the importance of adverse early life factors and social determinants of health on future brain function and structure in young adults and in midlife [[7], [8]]. From observational studies of cardiovascular risks to clinical trials, we have identified multi-dimensional intervention strategies that may curb the potential ravages of lifestyle and cardiovascular risks by preventing brain injury [[9], [10], [11]]. Brain health initiatives and programs have gained traction across the globe, but it will require a multi-level approach including but not limited to involvement of individual practitioners, healthcare systems, local and regional government and public health forces, and international cooperation, if we are to reach our goals [12]. We have the proper tools at our disposal [13], the momentum of national and international research collaborations [[12], [14]], and the promise of molecular discoveries to help us better understand and maintain brain resilience and resistance to injury [15]. Thus, we are advancing at a quick pace to close brain health knowledge gaps which will lead ultimately to a healthier society.

Osler, a remarkable and model physician, revealed in part the secret to longevity in his publications in the early 1900s. Specifically, Walk with the Young may be interpreted as a call-to-action for older people to remain vital by engaging with younger people (e.g., through social and other interactions), not becoming stale or stagnant in their pursuit of knowledge, and utilizing learning as a lifelong process. Some of the phraseology used by Osler was alleged to originate in an ancient text, Hermippus Redivivus, featuring a sage’s triumph over old age and a method to extend life and vigor [16]. Osler’s message persists. For example, in regions of possible (but controversial) unusual longevity referred to as ‘Blue Zones’, people are characterized as being faith-based, partaking in healthy diets, engaging in physical activity, and maintaining an intimate social circle [17]. To Osler’s main point, older people in these regions of longevity remain firmly entrenched within the local society as they are revered by the extended family unit and encouraged to share their wisdom and knowledge as it is sought after by younger people. Older people are apparently not shunned or prematurely ‘pushed off the cliff’ [17]. Osler emphasized remaining vital by engagement with younger people– a possible reference to avoidance of social isolation and maintenance of lifelong learning.

As for Aristotle, the great philosopher who among a multiplicity of achievements spanning many disciplines included the introduction of logic and deductive reasoning, he made the egregious error of contrarily attributing the control of sensation and movement to the heart instead of the brain [18]. Ironically, Aristotle, who was a leading biologist of his time, did not have a medical degree, never practiced formal clinical medicine, and did not partake in human dissection. His arguments about the role of the human brain were flawed [18], but he may be forgiven as he provided open dialogue about his position. What may not be forgivable, however, is if we do not seize the moment to advocate for brain health and brain care at the local, regional, and international levels. Look at the Special Issue of Cerebral Circulation Cognition & Behavior on Brain Health, and if in doubt, you will convince yourself about the importance of moving forward with brain health initiatives and programs across the globe [5].
