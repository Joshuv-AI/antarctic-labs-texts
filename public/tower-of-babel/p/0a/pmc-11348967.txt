Strategies for Identifying Biomarkers in Severe Asthma
Authors: Pierre-Olivier Girodet
Published: 2024

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disease of the airways characterized by clinical heterogeneity and a high degree of pathophysiological complexity (1). Multiple phenotypes and endotypes of asthma have been described on the basis of observable disease manifestations and inflammatory mechanisms (2). Since 2003, several therapies for severe asthma have been approved. These include monoclonal antibodies targeting a proinflammatory mediator, as well as cytokines and their receptors, such as IgE (omalizumab), IL-5 (mepolizumab, reslizumab), IL-5 receptor-α (benralizumab), IL-4/IL-13 receptor-α (dupilumab), and thymic stromal lymphopoietin (tezepelumab). In clinical practice, a limited number of biomarkers are available (3), such as serum total IgE, blood eosinophil counts, and levels of fractional exhaled nitric oxide (4), to differentiate type 2-high (allergic and/or eosinophilic) versus type 2-low (nonallergic and noneosinophilic) asthma. Thus, new biomarkers are needed to identify patients who are most likely to have a response to asthma biologics (5).

In a novel, proof-of-concept study (SoMOSA [Study of Mechanisms of Action of Omalizumab in Severe Asthma]) in this issue of the Journal, Djukanović and colleagues (pp. 288–297) assessed the usefulness of biomarker candidates for predicting the clinical response to the oldest biological therapy, omalizumab, in 191 subjects with severe asthma (6). Previous clinical studies on biomarkers as predictors of omalizumab efficacy have shown conflicting results (5). Djukanović and colleagues first focused their attention on urinary prostaglandin D2 (PGD2) metabolite 2,3-dinor-11β-PGF2α. The PGD2 pathway is of significant interest, partly because of its anti-IgE mechanism of action. Indeed, omalizumab recognizes IgE at the same site as the high-affinity IgE receptor (FcεRI binding site), which is mainly expressed on mast cells and basophils (7). By forming complexes with free IgE, omalizumab blocks the interaction between IgE and mast cells and basophils. PGD2 is the major mast cell–derived prostaglandin and is released in response to IgE-mediated degranulation (8). However, baseline concentrations of 2,3-dinor-11β-PGF2α (primary outcome) did not differentiate responders and nonresponders to omalizumab. Similar negative results were obtained with leukotriene E4, other eicosanoids, and standard biomarkers (blood and sputum eosinophils, exhaled nitric oxide, serum IgE).

Interestingly, this traditional hypothesis-driven strategy was associated with a hypothesis-free approach using omics technologies (co–primary outcomes). Several biomarkers that predict early or late clinical responses were identified, including five breath volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and five plasma lipids. In fact, five exhaled breath VOCs (benzothiazole, acetophenone, 2-pentyl-furan, methylene chloride, 2-methyl-butane) predicted early improvement assessed by global evaluation of treatment effectiveness score, whereas another set of VOCs (2-ethyl-1-hexanol, toluene, 2-pentene, nonanal, and an unidentified compound) predicted reduction in exacerbation greater than 50% over 12 months. Moreover, five plasma lipids, comprising four triglycerides and an unknown lipid, may predict both early and late efficacy of the anti-IgE omalizumab.

The discovery of new biomarkers in severe asthma is an exciting finding, but one needs to be careful in the interpretation of this retrospective study. First, the origin of exhaled breath VOCs may include the environment (exogenous), the host (endogenous), and the microbiome (9). Considering the nine VOCs mentioned above, their origins and their functions in asthma pathophysiology remain to be elucidated. It would be interesting to explore whether these VOCs regulate IgE function or influence IgE-dependent inflammation. Moreover, the mechanism by which those compounds may modify omalizumab pharmacokinetic and/or pharmacodynamic parameters in nonresponders is intriguing. Second, the same issues will arise on the expression and the role of triglycerides in asthma. Obesity is strongly associated to severe asthma, but increasing insulin resistance is more frequently observed than other components of the metabolic syndrome, such as hypertriglyceridemia (10). As stated by the authors, further elucidation of the detected lipid function would probably be complex. Mast cells contribute to the generation of lipid mediators, and triglycerides in mast cell lipid droplets are a substrate source for eicosanoid formation (11). Nevertheless, the clinical relevance of those fundamental studies remains to be determined.

Finally, hypothesis-free approaches have revealed the potential of omics methods (breathomics, lipidomics) for the discovery of asthma biomarkers. The traditional hypothesis-driven substudy was inconclusive, and further translational research is needed to explore the function of biomarker candidates and their putative role in biologic response. These results, although potentially important in the context of the lack of biomarkers in asthma, need replication on a larger scale before making more precise conclusions. I hope this study will stimulate future prospective studies to identify and validate diagnostic, prognostic, and predictive biomarkers to enhance the care of patients with severe asthma.
