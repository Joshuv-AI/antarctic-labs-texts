HNF1β Gene Mutation Leading to a MODY5 With Renal Dysplasia: A Case Report
Authors: Xingfa Han, Xueting Qiu, Rong Jiang, Nian Wang, Zheng Li, Zhimin Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis case report describes a 20‐year‐old female MODY5 patient. Genetic testing revealed a heterozygous missense mutation c.452C>G (p.R151G) in HNF1β, identical to the mutation carried by her diabetic mother. This case underscores the importance of genetic testing in young‐onset diabetes with renal anomalies, facilitating personalized care and family risk assessment.

Introduction

Background of HNF1β

Hepatocyte nuclear factor‐1β (HNF1β), a transcription factor belonging to the homeodomain family, plays a pivotal role in the development and function of multiple organs, especially the pancreas and kidney. In the pancreas, it is crucial for the differentiation and maturation of beta cells, regulating the expression of genes involved in insulin synthesis and secretion. Its absence or dysfunction can lead to a decline in insulin production, paving the way for diabetes. In the kidney, HNF1β is essential for the normal development of nephrons and the maintenance of renal function. It regulates the expression of genes related to renal structure and function, such as those involved in water and electrolyte transport. Any mutation in HNF1β can thus have profound impacts on both pancreatic and renal function, leading to disorders like diabetes and kidney dysplasia [1].

MODY5 (Maturity‐Onset Diabetes of the Young type 5) is an autosomal dominant disorder caused by mutations in the HNF1β gene, encoding a transcription factor essential for pancreatic β‐cell function and renal development, characterized by complete HNF‐1β deficiency in approximately 50% of cases [2]. This subtype is closely associated with the 17q12 microdeletion syndrome. Clinical manifestations often include diabetes mellitus, polycystic kidney disease, pancreatic hypertrophy, liver anomalies, and neuropsychiatric impairments. The primary clinical features are predominantly attributed to the deletion of the HNF‐1β gene, highlighting its central role in disease pathogenesis.

Epidemiology and Clinical Significance of HNF1β Mutations

HNF1β mutations are relatively rare, with an estimated prevalence of around 1 in 14,500 individuals. They are, however, of significant clinical importance as they are associated with early‐onset diabetes, typically diagnosed before the age of 35, and renal disorders, including renal dysplasia and cysts [3]. These mutations are inherited in an autosomal dominant manner, meaning that affected individuals have a 50% chance of passing them on to their offspring [4]. The clinical presentation can be highly variable, with symptoms ranging from mild diabetes to severe kidney dysfunction [5]. Early diagnosis and appropriate management are crucial to improve outcomes in individuals with HNF1β mutations.

Purpose of the Study

This case report aims to highlight the complex clinical manifestations of HNF1β mutations, particularly the association between early‐onset diabetes and renal dysplasia. By presenting this case, we hope to enhance clinicians' understanding of this condition and improve the accuracy of diagnosis and management. This will help in identifying affected individuals earlier, initiating appropriate treatment, and providing genetic counseling to families at risk, thereby improving patient outcomes and quality of life.

Case Report

Patient Presentation

A 20‐year‐old female presented with complaints of polyuria, polydipsia, and thirst for 2 years. She also reported fatigue and blurred vision. However, she didn't pay much attention to it. These symptoms prompted her to seek medical attention 2 years ago.

Family History

The patient's mother had diabetes since her early thirties and carried an HNF1β mutation. There was no history of kidney disease in the family.

Genetic Testing

Given the patient's clinical presentation and family history, genetic testing was performed for HNF1β mutations. DNA was extracted from the patient's blood sample and subjected to targeted next‐generation sequencing for the HNF1β gene. Genetic testing was performed at GrandOmics, which is an established and nationally recognized laboratory. The analysis identified a heterozygous missense mutation, c.452C>G, in exon 2 of the HNF1β gene (NM_000458.4 exon 2). This mutation results in the substitution of Glycine for Arginine at position 151 (p.R151G) of the protein. The same mutation was found in the patient's mother but not her father, confirming the autosomal dominant inheritance pattern. The c.452C>G mutation has been previously reported in individuals with MODY5, validating its pathogenicity in this case (Figures 2, 3, 4).

Diagnosis Summary

Maturity‐onset diabetes of the young type 5 (MODY5), Diabetic retinopathy (NPDR), Right renal dysplasia, Hepatic dysfunction, Hyperlipidemia, Hyperuricemia, Hypokalemia, Hypomagnesemia.

Therapy and Follow Up

After admission, the patient received intensified hypoglycemic treatment with an insulin pump. Once blood glucose levels were stably controlled, the antihyperglycemic regimen was adjusted to a combination of 0.25 mg semaglutide weekly and 22 units of insulin glargine daily. Additionally, fenofibrate was administered for lipid lowering, Magnesium Isoglycyrrhizinate Injection is used for liver protection treatment, electrolyte imbalances were corrected, and measures were taken to improve microcirculation. Two months after discharge, we followed up with the patient and found that their liver function and blood lipid levels had returned to normal. Serum uric acid levels were also within the normal range. Currently, ongoing monitoring indicates stable overall blood glucose levels.

Discussion

Pathogenesis of HNF1β Mutations

HNF1β mutations impact diabetes and renal dysplasia through intricate mechanisms. In the pancreas, HNF1β is vital for beta cell function. Mutations reduce its binding affinity to target DNA sequences, impairing transcriptional regulation of insulin and other critical genes. This leads to decreased insulin production, causing diabetes. In the kidney, HNF1β regulates nephron development and maintains renal function by modulating genes involved in water and electrolyte transport [6]. Mutations disrupt this process, resulting in abnormal kidney development, such as dysplasia and cyst formation [7]. The c.452C>G mutation in this case alters the protein structure, affecting its function and contributing to the observed clinical features.

Clinical Features of Early‐Onset Diabetes With HNF1β Mutations

Early‐onset diabetes associated with HNF1β mutations typically presents with polyuria, polydipsia, and weight loss, similar to type 1 diabetes. However, patients often have milder hyperglycemia and may not require insulin initially. They may also exhibit impaired glucose tolerance and progressive beta cell dysfunction over time. Unlike type 1 diabetes, these patients usually have a positive family history of diabetes and are less likely to have autoimmune markers. This case demonstrates these features, with the patient presenting at 20 years of age with classic diabetes symptoms and a family history.

Renal Dysplasia Associated With HNF1β Mutations

HNF1β mutations are closely linked to renal dysplasia, a condition characterized by abnormal kidney development. It can manifest as unilateral or bilateral renal dysplasia, with reduced kidney size and function [8]. Kidney dysplasia is also common, as seen in this case on the right side of the kidney. Clinically, patients may have hypertension, proteinuria, and progressive kidney dysfunction. Renal dysplasia can lead to chronic kidney disease, highlighting the importance of early diagnosis and management. The c.452C>G mutation is known to cause renal abnormalities, consistent with the findings in this patient.

Diagnosis and Treatment

Diagnosis of HNF1β‐related diabetes and renal dysplasia involves a combination of clinical features, family history, laboratory tests, and imaging studies. Polyuria, polydipsia, and early‐onset diabetes with a positive family history are key indicators. Elevated blood glucose, lipid levels, electrolyte abnormalities, and hepatic dysfunction support the diagnosis. Hepatic dysfunction is a significant feature of MODY5; the HNF1β gene plays a crucial role in regulating glucose metabolism and liver function, and mutations in this gene can lead to impaired liver function, including reduced glucose tolerance and increased liver enzyme levels. Patients with MODY5 may experience symptoms such as fatigue, abdominal discomfort, and jaundice, which are indicative of liver dysfunction. Renal ultrasonography and CT scans can detect kidney abnormalities. Differential diagnosis includes type 1 and type 2 diabetes, as well as other genetic forms of diabetes like MODY1 and MODY3. Renal dysplasia must be distinguished from polycystic kidney disease and other causes of kidney dysfunction. Treatment for diabetes in patients with HNF1β mutations focuses on glycemic control. Initially, oral hypoglycemic agents like sulfonylureas may be effective, but insulin may be required over time as beta cell function declines. Renal dysplasia management includes monitoring kidney function, controlling blood pressure, and managing electrolyte balance. Angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers can help control hypertension and protect the kidney. Regular follow‐ups and lifestyle modifications, such as a healthy diet and exercise, are also important. In severe cases of kidney dysfunction, renal replacement therapy may be necessary.

Prognosis and Genetic Counseling

The long‐term prognosis for individuals with HNF1β mutations depends on the severity of diabetes and kidney involvement [9]. Diabetes may progress, requiring more stringent glycemic control. Renal dysplasia can lead to chronic kidney disease, with a risk of end‐stage kidney disease. Complications include macrovascular and microvascular diseases, such as retinopathy, nephropathy, and cardiovascular disease. Regular monitoring and early intervention are crucial to manage these complications and improve outcomes. Genetic counseling is essential for family members of individuals with HNF1β mutations. As the condition is autosomal dominant, there is a 50% risk of inheritance for offspring. Counseling can provide information about the genetic transmission, potential health risks, and available management options. It enables family members to make informed decisions about reproductive planning and healthcare [10]. Early identification of affected individuals through genetic testing can lead to timely intervention and better outcomes.

Comparison With Previous Cases

This case shares similarities with previous reports of HNF1β mutations, including early‐onset diabetes, renal dysplasia, and the c.452C>G mutation. However, differences exist in the severity of diabetes and kidney involvement. Some cases report milder diabetes that remains manageable with oral medications, while others have more severe kidney dysfunction requiring dialysis. The presence of liver function impairment in this case is less commonly reported, highlighting the variable clinical manifestations associated with HNF1β mutations.

Conclusion

This case report presents a 20‐year‐old female with HNF1β mutation c.452C>G, showing early‐onset diabetes, right renal dysplasia, and multiple comorbidities. The mutation was inherited from her mother, highlighting autosomal dominance. It demonstrates the complex clinical spectrum associated with HNF1β mutations. Clinicians should consider HNF1β mutations in patients with early‐onset diabetes and kidney abnormalities. Future research should explore the long‐term outcomes and complications of HNF1β mutations, focusing on the progression of diabetes and kidney disease. Studies could evaluate novel therapeutic approaches, such as targeted therapies to improve beta cell function and kidney health. Clinical practice could benefit from standardized diagnostic protocols for HNF1β‐related disorders.
