Sestrins: a new therapeutic target for endothelial dysfunction and cardiovascular disease prevention
Authors: Cheng Li, Xu Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSestrins are a highly conserved family of proteins that have garnered significant attention for their critical roles in regulating cellular stress responses, antioxidative defense, energy metabolism, and cell survival. Endothelial dysfunction, a pivotal early event in the development of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), is strongly associated with oxidative stress, inflammation, and disruption of vascular homeostasis.FindingsRecent studies have demonstrated that Sestrins contribute to maintaining endothelial cell integrity by mitigating oxidative stress, modulating inflammatory signaling, and promoting cellular adaptation through the regulation of key pathways such as AMPK/mTOR and autophagy. Experimental evidence from cardiovascular disease models suggests that upregulation of Sestrins can improve endothelial function and attenuate vascular damage, highlighting their involvement in vascular protection and disease modulation.ConclusionsSestrins emerge as promising therapeutic targets for the prevention and treatment of endothelial dysfunction and cardiovascular diseases. This review underscores the potential of Sestrins in vascular biology and advocates for further research to elucidate their mechanisms and clinical applicability in cardiovascular medicine.Keywords: Sestrins, endothelial dysfunction, cardiovascular diseases, oxidative stress

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) represent the leading cause of mortality worldwide, posing significant health and economic burdens [1]. At the heart of CVD pathogenesis lies endothelial dysfunction, a pivotal event that precedes the development of more severe cardiovascular conditions, including atherosclerosis, hypertension, and heart failure. The endothelium, a monolayer of cells lining the interior surface of blood vessels, plays a critical role in maintaining vascular homeostasis by regulating blood flow, vascular tone, and blood coagulation. Disruption of endothelial function is characterized by reduced bioavailability of nitric oxide (NO), increased oxidative stress, and a pro-inflammatory state, leading to the progression of vascular diseases.

Sestrins, a family of highly conserved proteins encoded by the SESN genes, have emerged as crucial regulators of cellular homeostasis and survival under stress conditions. Originally identified as genes upregulated in response to genotoxic stress [2], subsequent studies have unveiled their roles in combating oxidative stress, regulating metabolism, and maintaining cellular integrity through the modulation of various signaling pathways, notably the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and the mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathways [3]. These pathways are essential for the regulation of cellular growth, metabolism, and survival, implicating Sestrins in a wide range of physiological and pathological processes.

Significant strides have been made in understanding the role of Sestrins in cardiovascular health over the past decade. Studies have shown that Sestrins can mitigate endothelial dysfunction by enhancing the production of nitric oxide (NO), reducing oxidative stress through the activation of antioxidant defenses, and modulating inflammatory responses [4]. These actions underscore the potential of Sestrins as a novel target for the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular diseases. This review aims to delve into the latest research findings on the biology of Sestrins, their protective roles in endothelial function, and the potential therapeutic implications for cardiovascular diseases. By exploring the molecular mechanisms underlying Sestrin-mediated protection against endothelial dysfunction, this review seeks to highlight the potential of Sestrins as a novel target for cardiovascular disease intervention.

Sestrins: isoform and biochemical roles

Sestrins constitute a stress-induced, highly conserved protein family that accumulates within stressed cells, enhancing AMPK activity while inhibiting mTOR activation [1,5]. While prevalent throughout the animal kingdom, no homologous Sestrin proteins have been identified in plants or fungi. Invertebrates possess a single orthologue, while vertebrates have three paralogues, known as Sestrin1-3 [6]. Sestrin1 was initially identified in 1999 as a gene activated by the tumor suppressor p53, with Sestrin2 and Sestrin3 discovered in 2002. Subsequent investigations have unveiled their involvement in diverse cellular processes such as autophagy, antioxidant defense, and mTORC1 signaling [7]. Sestrin1 is primarily expressed in response to genotoxic stress, playing a crucial role in activating the DNA damage response pathway, thereby facilitating cellular repair mechanisms and maintaining genomic stability [8]. Sestrin2, the most extensively studied isoform, is strongly induced by oxidative stress and nutrient scarcity. It serves as a key regulator of the mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signaling pathway, inhibiting mTORC1 activity under stressful conditions to modulate cellular growth, autophagy, and metabolism [1]. Although less characterized, Sestrin3 has been implicated in the regulation of oxidative stress and energy homeostasis, particularly in the context of mitochondrial function [9]. All members of the Sestrin family contribute to stress responses, enhancing organisms’ resilience to various stressors such as hypoxia, genotoxic stress, oxidative stress, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and metabolic stress [10]. Furthermore, Sestrin expression is governed by a variety of stress-responsive transcription factors, including p53, FoxO, Atf4, and Nrf2 [5,10]. While Sestrin1 and Sestrin2 primarily respond to p53, Sestrin3 is activated by FoxO transcription factors, which may also contribute to Sestrin1 induction [11]. In vitro, Sestrins demonstrate oxidoreductase activity and could potentially serve as antioxidants [12]. Independently of their redox activity, Sestrins trigger AMPK-dependent inhibition of mTOR signaling, thereby establishing a link between genotoxic stress and mTOR regulation [8].

Sestrins: the regulation and cellular effects

Sestrins signaling pathways

AMPK/mTOR signaling pathway

mTOR, a serine/threonine kinase, was first discovered in the early 1990s as the target of rapamycin [13]. This evolutionary conserved protein kinase plays a pivotal role in controlling cellular growth and metabolism, consisting of two distinct protein complexes known as mTORC1 and mTORC2. These complexes have different substrate specificities and regulate distinct yet overlapping processes [14]. Recent studies have shown that Sestrins can inhibit mTORC1 activity through two main pathways (Figure 1): i) By upregulating AMPK and promoting its phosphorylation, leading to the activation of tuberous sclerosis complex 2 (TSC2), which subsequently inhibits mTORC1. ii) By inhibiting the activity of the GATOR2 complex, which results in the inhibition of GATOR1 and subsequent suppression of mTORC1 [15–17]. In SESN-deficient mice, AMPK can inhibit mTORC1 activity through drug activation, highlighting AMPK as a crucial target for SESNs in maintaining metabolic equilibrium [18]. Furthermore, Sestrins can also promote mTORC2 activity through various mechanisms, thereby upregulating the mTORC2/AKT pathway level (Figure 1) [19,20]. Studies have reported that Sestrins activate the mTORC2/AKT pathway by stimulating the TSC1/TSC2 complex independently of mTORC1. This complex exerts a positive influence on mTORC2 activity [21]. Additionally, Sestrin2 and Sestrin3 can directly interact with the regulatory subunit Rictor of mTORC2, enhancing its catalytic activity and stimulating the phosphorylation of Ser473 on AKT [20]. The GATOR2 complex acts as a link between Sestrin2 and Rictor, facilitating the activation of mTORC2 and AKT mediated by Sestrin2 [22].

Sestrins are key regulators of the mTORC1 and mTORC2 kinases that play a critical role in many physiological and pathophysiological processes (By Figfraw). Under conditions of cellular stress, Sestrins are activated and inhibit mTORC1 via two distinct mechanisms: 1) Sestrins activate AMPK through phosphorylation, which subsequently promotes the tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC1/TSC2). TSC1/TSC2 inhibits Rheb, leading to mTORC1 suppression. 2) Sestrins inhibit GATOR2, resulting in GATOR1 activation. GATOR1 then suppresses the Rag GTPases (RagA/B-RagC/D), preventing mTORC1 recruitment to lysosomal membranes and its subsequent activation. On the other hand, mTORC1 suppression contributes to mTORC2 expression.

Nrf2/Keap1 pathway

Nrf2, a redox-sensitive factor, plays a crucial role in maintaining cell integrity and homeostasis by upregulating the expression of various antioxidant genes and eliminating reactive oxygen species (ROS). Normally found in the cytosol bound to its inhibitor Kelch-like epichlorohydrin-associated protein 1 (Keap1), Nrf2 is released from Keap1 during stress and translocates to the nucleus. There, it interacts with the antioxidant-response element (ARE) to regulate the transcription of genes involved in antioxidant defense [23]. A shared function of the Sestrins family is to activate the P62/sequestosome-1 (SQSTM1)-dependent pathway, leading to the degradation of Keap1 [24,25]. This process enhances the transcription of Nrf2-dependent antioxidant genes, inhibiting ROS and improving cell metabolism while preserving mitochondrial function [26] (Figure 2). Upregulation of Sestrin2 promotes Keap1 degradation and Nrf2 activation, whereas Sestrin2 deficiency hinders this function, increasing cell susceptibility to oxidative damage [26]. Oliveira et al. reported that Sestrin2 can modulate the Keap1/Nrf2 axis to control redox balance during liver regeneration and ischemia-reperfusion injury preceding liver regeneration [27]. Strengthening this protective mechanism could provide novel approaches for liver regeneration and enhance cellular resilience against oxidative stress.

Sestrins enhance antioxidant defense through Nrf2 activation (By Figfraw). ROS induces Sestrin expression via ROS response gene, while Sestrins initiate a cytoprotective feedback loop by promoting p62-dependent Keap1 degradation, enabling Nrf2 nuclear translocation and subsequent transcription of antioxidant genes and Sestrin family genes. This pathway crucially protects endothelial cells from oxidative damage in cardiovascular diseases.

Cellular effects

Oxidative stress

Sestrins have emerged as novel stress-responsive proteins crucial for mitigating oxidative damage during stressful conditions [28]. Under oxidative stress, all members of the Sestrins family, particularly Sestrin2, are activated, potentially playing a role in regulating ROS metabolism and associated compounds [7]. The overaccumulation of ROS and continuous mechanistic target of mTORC1 activation are recognized as catalysts for aging and age-related degenerative diseases [7]. Research indicates that suppressing Sestrin gene expression via shRNA leads to ROS buildup, DNA damage, genetic instability, or cell death across various cell types [12,29,30]. In Drosophila, inhibiting the Sestrin analog or dSesn similarly leads to ROS accumulation and oxidative damage in skeletal muscle [1], highlighting the evolutionary preservation of Sestrins’ anti-oxidative function.

Research indicates several roles for Sestrins in combating oxidative stress, particularly through their association with peroxiredoxins, enzymes crucial for breaking down harmful peroxides. Sestrins share structural features with bacterial AhpD and are involved in restoring overoxidized peroxiredoxins, acting differently from both AhpD and sulfiredoxin [28]. While Sestrins do not directly reduce peroxiredoxins like sulfiredoxin, they may indirectly promote their repair by activating the Nrf2 pathway, thus upregulating sulfiredoxin and enhancing cellular antioxidant defenses [26,31]. Moreover, Sestrins contribute to maintaining cellular redox balance through the AMPK/mTORC1 pathway, which regulates autophagy [32]. This process breaks down and recycles cellular components by transporting them to lysosomes. Recent findings indicate that Sestrin2 can trigger mitophagy and suppress NLR family pyrin domain-containing 3 (NLRP3) activation in macrophages, thereby increasing the efficiency of autophagy in removing damaged mitochondria to lysosomes for elimination [33]. Dysfunctional mitophagy can lead to the accumulation of defective mitochondria, resulting in increased ROS levels and subsequent damage to cellular structures [34–36]. Therefore, activating Sestrins offers an alternative approach to disposing of dysfunctional mitochondria through autophagy [37], contributing significantly to maintaining redox balance and cellular health.

Pro-autophagic and apoptotic

Autophagy, a dynamic and regulated metabolic process, plays a crucial role in breaking down denatured proteins and damaged organelles to maintain intracellular balance [38]. Sestrin2 is reported to combat stress by enhancing antioxidant levels, temporarily halting stressful anabolic reactions, promoting autophagy, and preserving growth factor and insulin signaling. In cases where stress and damage are irreparable, Sestrin2 can serve as a fail-safe mechanism by signaling apoptosis [39–41]. Upregulation of Sestrin2 contributes to autophagy induction, while downregulation suppresses autophagy activity induced by various stimuli [42]. Sestrin2 expression activates autophagy through AMPK activation and mTORC1 suppression, thereby ameliorating insulin resistance induced by palmitate [43]. Orientin induces autophagy and apoptosis by activating Sestrin1 expression, regulating AMPK/mTOR signaling in ox-LDL-induced HUVECs [44]. SIRT1 exerts cardiac protective effects by attenuating doxorubicin-induced oxidative damage and apoptosis in a Sestrin2-dependent manner [45]. Inhibition of Sestrin2 sensitizes cells to 2-deoxyglucose-induced apoptosis [46]. Sestrin2 overexpression may activate the AMPK pathway, alleviate high-glucose-induced eNOS dysfunction, and inhibit mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis in podocytes [47,48]. The p53-Sestrin2 pathway acts as a protective mechanism against acute kidney injury by controlling autophagy and mitophagy in renal tubules [37].

Mitochondria dysfunction

Every disease results from one or more stress signals converging at the mitochondria’s interface. After cellular injury, mitochondrial functionality is compromised, leading to increased ROS production. This disruption activates various proteins in response to mitochondrial stress [49,50]. Detailed studies on Sestrins in Drosophila have not only enhanced their conserved antioxidant function but also emphasized their importance in maintaining mitochondrial integrity and metabolism [1]. Deletion of the dSesn gene in Drosophila melanogaster led to structural changes in mitochondria, including altered shape, enlarged size with disorganized cristae, and organelle aggregation [1]. Thus, Sestrins are necessary for maintaining a healthy mitochondrial pool. Sestrin2, a highly conserved and stress-inducible protein, plays a significant role in mitochondrial function and autophagy in CVDs. Mitochondrial superoxide regulates Sestrin2 activation during mitochondrial quality control [51]. Inhibition of Sestrin2 was found to contribute significantly to doxorubicin-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis, cardiac mitophagy, mitochondrial dysfunction, and cardiotoxicity. Overexpression of Sestrin2 can protect against doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity by improving mitochondrial function and mitophagy [52]. Sestrin2, as a positive regulator of Parkin-mediated autophagy, is essential in mitochondrial homeostasis [53]. Sestrin2 is capable of regulating mitophagy by promoting the targeting of dysfunctional mitochondria for lysosomal breakdown and through the control of Parkin E3 ligase movement to the surface of damaged mitochondria [33,51]. Kumar et al. discovered that Sestrin2 facilitates cell death in response to prolonged mitochondrial damage rather than managing mitophagy under standard mitochondrial stress [54].

Recent studies have shown that Sestrin1 can inhibit oxidized LDL (oxLDL) production and subsequent NLRP3 inflammasome activation in macrophages within a mouse model of atherosclerosis [55]. This is critical because oxLDL acts as a ‘danger signal’ that triggers NLRP3 assembly via mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) overproduction and thioredoxin (Trx) system suppression, directly compromising endothelial integrity [56]. Given the link between oxLDL overproduction, mitochondrial respiratory chain impairment, and potential tissue damage [57], Sestrin1 may contribute to maintaining mitochondrial integrity by blocking NLRP3-dependent pyroptosis—an inflammatory cell death pathway that exacerbates vascular injury in atherosclerosis [58]. Additionally, Sestrin3 effectively inhibits FOXO3-mediated ROS overproduction and mitochondrial apoptosis [9], indicating its potential indirect role in preserving mitochondrial function. Notably, by attenuating mtROS accumulation (a key NLRP3 activator), both Sestrins could disrupt the ‘metaflammation’ cycle where metabolic stressors like hyperglycemia and AGEs perpetuate NLRP3 activation, endothelial dysfunction, and end-organ damage [56,59].

Ferroptosis

Ferroptosis is a recently identified form of cell death characterized by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, coupled with reduced activity of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), leading to oxidative stress and cell demise [60]. It distinguishes itself from other cell death mechanisms through specific features like mitochondrial shrinkage, increased membrane density, iron and lipid-based reactive oxygen species accumulation, and the activation of certain genes. Gao F. et al. reported that Sestrin1 overexpression can inhibit vascular endothelial ferroptosis by activating P21 in AS [61]. Yang et al. found that ferroptosis occurs during the myocardial reperfusion phase but not ischemia, with Sestrin1 involvement in this phase [62]. Zhang LL. et al. revealed an inverse relationship between Sestrin2 expression and ferroptosis, leading to reduced hypoxia-reoxygenation damage. Conversely, reducing Sestrin2 expression weakens its antioxidative abilities, resulting in higher ROS levels and further aggravating ferroptosis [63]. Additionally, decreased Sestrin2 expression in aged mouse hearts leads to intolerance to ischemia-reperfusion stress [64]. Moreover, decreased levels of Sestrin2 contribute to enhanced inflammatory responses and impaired barrier function in septic intestinal dysfunction by promoting ferroptosis, facilitated by the activation of the AMPK/Nrf2 signaling pathway [65]. A recent bioinformatic PPI network analysis predicted that Sestrin2 may be the key ferroptosis protein in the pathogenesis of diabetic peripheral neuropathy [66].

Sestrins and endothelial dysfunction

The vascular endothelium plays a crucial role in maintaining vascular homeostasis by regulating vascular tension and local cellular growth [67]. Endothelial dysfunction is widely recognized as an initiating factor in various cardiovascular and metabolic diseases [68,69]. Importantly, myoendothelial junction (MEJ) proteins—including TRPV4 and Piezo1 highlighted in recent research—serve as critical regulators of endothelial-dependent hyperpolarization (EDH) that maintain microvascular tone, where their dysfunction directly promotes cardiovascular pathogenesis [70]. While current evidence from mechanistic studies does not establish direct links between Sestrins and MEJ components, Sestrin1 upregulation by Orientin demonstrates endothelial-protective effects against oxidative stress and inflammation [44]. This suggests a potential, though unverified, therapeutic pathway where Sestrins might indirectly influence MEJ function through their antioxidant capacity—a hypothesis requiring validation in future MEJ-focused studies. Additionally, Sestrin1 shows lower expression in endothelial cells under low shear stress [71]. A major defense against vascular injury is endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS), which generates NO. When eNOS dysfunction occurs, not only does the bioavailability of NO decrease, but the generation of ROS also increases [47,72]. Silencing of Sestrin2 has been found to increase oxidative stress, reduce the viability of endothelial cells, and dysregulate the Nrf2, AMPK, and mTORC1 signaling pathways [73].

Angiotensin-II (Ang-II), a key component of the RAAS, not only elevates blood pressure but also enhances oxidative stress in endothelial cells, leading to hypertrophy and endothelial dysfunction. Sestrin2 has been shown to mitigate the harmful impacts of Ang-II on endothelial cells [74]. Studies have reported that Ang-II increases the expression of Sestrin2 in HUVECs in a time- and dose-dependent manner; moreover, Sestrin2 silencing can increase oxidative stress and reduce the viability of endothelial cells in response to Ang-II [74]. Ang-II can also promote the expression of Sestrin2 and Nrf2 proteins in endothelial progenitor cells to protect against apoptosis and dysfunction [75]. Additionally, Fang et al. have reported that patients with higher levels of Ang-II and carotid atherosclerotic plaque exhibit increased Sestrin2 levels compared to patients without these clinical characteristics [76].

Furthermore, Sestrin expression is tightly regulated by various stress-responsive transcription factors such as p53, FoxO, and Nrf2 [8,9,26], which are active in endothelial cells and respond to oxidative and mechanical stresses. Genetic and epigenetic modifications affecting SESN genes may alter their expression levels, thereby impacting endothelial redox balance, mitochondrial function, and nitric oxide bioavailability. This regulation underscores the importance of both genetic and environmental factors in modulating endothelial Sestrin function and vascular health, although further studies are needed to clarify the detailed mechanisms.

Hypertension

Hypertension serves as a precursor to several cardiovascular diseases, including atherosclerosis, stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure, and peripheral artery disease. Increased oxidative stress is recognized as a critical component in the progression of hypertension [85]. The Sestrins family of proteins regulates oxidative stress both in vivo and in vitro. Studies have reported significantly increased levels of Sestrin1, 2, and 3 in patients with hypertension compared to normotensive subjects. Further analysis revealed a positive correlation between plasma Sestrins levels and blood pressure values, including systolic and/or diastolic blood pressure [76]. Previous studies have indicated that Sestrins play a role in regulating hypertension, primarily through their antioxidant effects. Yang et al. reported that silencing Sestrin2 aggravated oxidative stress and elevated blood pressure in dopamine D2 receptor knockout-induced hypertension mice [86]. Moreover, silencing of Sestrin2 could further elevate blood pressure [86]. Angiotensin-II (Ang-II), a crucial component of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) pathway, not only raises blood pressure but also contributes to increased oxidative stress in endothelial cells. This leads to hypertrophy of these cells, exacerbating endothelial dysfunction. Sestrin2 has been found to counter the negative effects of Ang-II on endothelial health [74]. Proper management of the overactive Ang II exhibited in hypertension by Sestrin2 can decrease the ROS produced by NADPH oxidase [85]. Liu et al. observed elevated levels of Sestrin2 in the plasma and aortas of individuals with aortic dissection. They proposed that Sestrin2 might play a role in aortic dissection by decreasing apoptosis of vascular smooth muscle cells through activation of the Nrf2 pathway [87].

Atherosclerosis (AS)

AS is a chronic vascular inflammatory disease associated with oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction [88]. It is characterized by arterial stenosis due to a disorder in lipid metabolism and the formation of atherosclerotic plaques in the arterial wall [89], leading to cardiovascular disease and a high mortality rate in the population. Previous research indicates that during lipid peroxidation metabolism, the accumulation of ROS and lipid peroxides increases oxidative stress, leading to endothelial dysfunction and triggering an inflammatory response. This not only promotes ferroptosis but also contributes to the onset and progression of vascular diseases, including AS [90]. Rapamycin has been shown to mitigate stress-triggered apoptosis in endothelial cells by modulating redox regulation through the mTOR and Sestrin1 pathways [71]. Gao et al. reported that FOXP1 transcriptionally activates Sestrin1, alleviating ox-LDL-induced inflammation, lipid accumulation in macrophages, and the formation of aortic plaques, thereby inhibiting the development of AS [61,91]. Their research also indicated that Sestrin1 can be transcriptionally regulated by P53, offering protection against AS [61]. Mutant P53 has been associated with the occurrence of AS, while wild-type P53 can reduce the inflammatory damage of AS [92]. Furthermore, Sestrin1 shows high expression levels within aortic macrophages in atherosclerotic mice and prevents the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and NF-κB signaling pathways in macrophages induced by lipopolysaccharide [55]. Overexpression of Sestrin1 has been reported to significantly inhibit the formation of aortic plaques, inflammation, oxidative stress response, and improve the disease symptoms of AS [61].

Monocyte activation is considered as a crucial step in the progression of atherogenesis. Studies have shown that mRNA expression of all Sestrin isoforms (1, 2, 3) is significantly reduced in subjects with dyslipidemia, with Sestrin2 expression notably decreased in individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D) and dyslipidemia, with or without T2D [79]. This suggests that Sestrin2 plays a major role in monocyte activation in diabetes and dyslipidemia, making it a risk factor for atherogenesis. Jing et al. reported increased plasma Sestrin levels in patients with coronary artery disease, showing a positive correlation with the severity of coronary heart diseases [4]. Sun et al. found that aerobic exercise elevated Sestrin1 protein levels in aortal macrophages in atherosclerotic mice, contributing to alleviating inflammatory responses [93]. Another study suggested that Sestrin2 levels are increased in calcific aortic valve disease aortic valves and may be involved in the disease’s development by regulating oxidative stress via the Nrf2 pathway [94].

Ischemia reperfusion (I/R) injury

I/R injury refers to a cascade of pathophysiological events precipitated by temporary ischemia followed by reperfusion. This process involves the generation of oxygen-free radicals upon oxygen resupply, endothelial dysfunction, microvascular injury, changes in calcium regulation, and alterations in myocardial metabolism, all contributing to I/R injury. Studies have reported that Sestrin2 plays a crucial role in I/R reperfusion of various organs such as the heart, brain, liver, kidney, and intestines [5,37, 62,63,95]. Elevated Sestrin2 levels and heightened oxidative stress were observed in I/R hearts compared to those in normoxic conditions [96]. Knockdown of Sestrin2 exacerbated oxidative stress, increased infarct size, and resulted in cardiac dysfunction during I/R events [40]. Sestrin1, regulated by ferroptosis, also plays a significant role in myocardial I/R injury [62]. Additionally, luteolin has been shown to relieve diabetic myocardial I/R injury via positive interactions between Sestrin2 and Nrf2, promoting antioxidative actions [82]. In mouse hearts, the expression of Sestrin2 decreases with age, correlating with decreased activation of AMPK in response to ischemia [64,97]. Current evidence suggests that Sestrin2 can protect liver tissues from oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction caused by I/R injury [27]. Activating Sestrin2 can protect cerebral I/R injury via inhibits ferroptosis [95]. Sesrin2 can regulate autophagy and mitophagy to protect renal I/R injury [37]. Furthermore, Sestrin2 prevents intolerance to I/R injury, particularly in aging individuals [64].

Diabetes and obesity

Vascular injury plays a key role in various diabetic complications. Diabetes, along with associated vascular ailments, contributes to endothelial cell dysfunction due to hyperinflammatory states and metabolic disorders within endothelial cells [98]. In diabetic conditions, Sestrin2 is involved in regulating endothelial mitochondrial metabolism via the Nrf2/Keap1 pathway, which promotes angiogenesis and osseointegration [99]. Activation of protein kinase B (AKT) through mTOR2 activation by Sestrins leads to improved insulin sensitivity in obesity and diabetes [100,101]. Additionally, Sestrin2-mediated AMPK activation inhibits the induction of Nox4 and enhances ROS production caused by high glucose levels [47]. Sestrin2 also exhibits a protective effect against oxLDL-induced apoptosis and ROS production by activating the JNK/c-Jun pathway [102]. Another study suggests that the downregulation of AMPK and the overactivation of mTORC1 might be responsible for metabolic dysregulation when Sestrin2 levels are deficient [19]. Upregulation of Sestrin2 can reduce oxidative stress in mitochondria and promote angiogenesis under hyperglycemic conditions [99].

Overnutrition can result in persistent activation of mTORC1 and p70S6 kinase, leading to obesity and associated conditions such as fatty liver disease and insulin resistance. A previous study demonstrated a negative correlation between Sestrin2 and both fasting plasma glucose and postprandial plasma glucose. Additionally, Sestrin1 and Sestrin2 were negatively correlated with HbA1c levels [79]. Stimulation of Sestrin2 can inhibit the development of insulin resistance and fatty liver disease [19]. Furthermore, activating Sestrin2 triggers AMPK, inhibits mTORC1, and sustains elevated AKT activity, which helps reduce gluconeogenesis in the liver and lower blood sugar levels. Conversely, obese mice lacking Sestrin2 experience a significant decrease in AKT activity, leading to insulin resistance and excessive glucose production [19]. reported that the absence of Sestrin3 resulted in insulin resistance and glucose intolerance, while overexpression of Sestrin3 could prevent insulin resistance induced by a high-fat diet [20].

The role of sestrins in exercise adaptation and benefits

Exercise is acknowledged for inducing slight oxidative stress in skeletal muscle [103], which could be essential for experiencing the advantages of exercise [104] (Figure 4). Sestrins, as a group of evolutionarily conserved proteins induced by exercise, play a crucial role in mediating the benefits of physical activity. Given that Sestrins are finely regulated by cellular oxidative stress levels, it is plausible that exercise serves as an inducer of Sestrin expression. Specifically, dSesn and mammalian Sestrin1 are primarily expressed in skeletal and cardiac muscles [1,11]. Numerous studies have highlighted that Sestrins can indeed be induced by exercise training. Research utilizing both fly and mouse models has shown that genetically removing Sestrins impedes organisms from reaping metabolic benefits from exercise and improving their endurance through training. On the contrary, elevating Sestrin levels replicates both the molecular and physiological effects of exercise, suggesting that it could be a crucial regulator of exercise-related metabolism [105]. Long-term physical exercise in mice significantly elevated the basal level of muscle protein expression of Sestrin2 and Sestrin3, which are associated with its effector [106]. Furthermore, acute aerobic exercise led to an increase in Sestrin1 accumulation and a discrete augmentation of Sestrin2 protein content and AMPK threonine phosphorylation. However, chronic exercise resulted in reduced basal levels of Sestrin1 and Sestrin2, as well as AMPK threonine phosphorylation in the quadriceps muscles of C57BL/6J mice [107]. Additionally, exercise training further increased Sestrin expression in both humans and mice [106,108,109]. Human subjects also exhibited regulation of Sestrins in the context of exercise, with acute resistance exercise notably upregulating Sestrin1 expression in the vastus lateralis muscles, a regulation that was sustained after chronic exercise [108].

On contrast, muscle inactivity leads to reduced Sestrin expression. Deprivation of exercise in mice can contribute to significant muscle atrophy, a condition exacerbated by Sestrin loss; conversely, Sestrin overexpression can offer protection against this atrophy [110]. Studies have reported strong downregulation of Sestrin1 expression levels with aging, another condition associated with reduced activity and muscle wasting [110]. The current study demonstrated that Sestrin1 and Sestrin3 levels were markedly reduced in skeletal muscle tissues of elderly patients, with a corresponding decrease in Sestrin2 protein phosphorylation levels [111]. These findings suggest that muscle inactivity and aging may have contrasting effects compared to exercise on Sestrins. Overall, these observations indicate that Sestrins play a critical role in the physiology of exercise. The absence of Sestrin impedes exercise capacity and the metabolic benefits of exercise, while Sestrin overexpression enhances exercise capacity and its advantages. Therefore, Sestrins are essential for the adaptation to exercise and its associated benefits.

Prevention and treatment

Studies have shown that Sestrins could be novel preventive and therapeutic drug targets for conditions such as aortic dissection, ischemic heart disease, diabetes, insulin resistance, obesity, and hyperuricemia/gout [64,76, 87,97,112]. High blood pressure patients have exhibited increased levels of circulating Sestrins, providing insights into strategies for preventing clinical hypertension [76]. Additionally, reduced serum Sestrin2 levels were observed in patients with diabetic nephropathy, suggesting that assessing Sestrin2 levels could be an effective method for early identification and prevention of diabetic nephropathy [113]. Moreover, research indicates that Sestrins can mitigate the harmful effects of doxorubicin on cardiomyocytes without causing cardiotoxicity [114], potentially emerging as a significant preventive and therapeutic agent against doxorubicin-induced cardiotoxicity.

Numerous purified natural compounds from dietary sources have been investigated for their anti-oxidative activity through direct modulation of Sestrins expression, including quercetin [115–117], cucurbitacin [118], eupatilin [119], isorhapontigenin [120], and resveratrol [45,121,122]. Furthermore, cell sheets modified with Sestrins show promise as a potential preventive or therapeutic approach for aging-related conditions like sarcopenia [123,124], cancer [125,126], Alzheimer’s disease [127,128], Parkinson’s disease [129], and coronary artery disease [4,130]. However, a comprehensive understanding of the upstream and downstream pathways responsible for the biological effects of Sestrins is essential to fully realize their diagnostic and therapeutic advantages.

Conclusion and prospects

The Sestrins family acts as a crucial intracellular sensor, overseeing multiple processes such as autophagy, nutrient sensing, inflammation, metabolic balance, and oxidative stress. As a result, they play a significant role in various aspects of diseases associated with endothelial dysfunction. However, ongoing research has underscored the need for a more thorough exploration of the Sestrins expression pattern during endothelial dysfunction and the associated components that modulate both endothelial dysfunction and its related CVDs. Given the pivotal physiological roles of Sestrins in regulating metabolic homeostasis and mitigating pathologies related to endothelial dysfunction, further investigations into the biochemical functions of Sestrins could potentially drive the development of novel preventive therapies for endothelial dysfunction, harnessing the beneficial activities of Sestrins. Sestrins are poised to usher in a new approach to discovering medications for endothelial dysfunction and related CVD conditions.
