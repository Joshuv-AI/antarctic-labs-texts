Minimally Invasive Treatment Using Biportal Endoscopic Decompression with Vertebroplasty for Osteoporotic Vertebral Compression Fractures in Older Adult Patients
Authors: Sang-Min Park, Sang-Soo Na, Ho-Joong Kim, Jin S Yeom
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroudOsteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs) with neurological symptoms often require extensive fusion surgery, which carries significant risks for older adults. This study evaluated the clinical outcomes of biportal endoscopic lumbar decompression (BELD) combined with percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) as a minimally invasive alternative.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 21 older adults (mean age, 80.24 years) who underwent BELD with PVP for OVCFs between July 2017 and June 2022. Indications included neurological deficits or severe, intractable pain refractory to conservative treatment. Clinical outcomes were assessed using the visual analog scale (VAS), Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), European Quality of Life-5 Dimensions (EQ-5D), and modified MacNab criteria. Radiographic parameters included vertebral compression rate and wedge angle.ResultsThe mean operation time was 86.81 minutes, with an average blood loss of 136.19 mL. According to modified MacNab criteria, 76.2% of patients achieved excellent or good outcomes. Clinical parameters, including VAS, ODI, and EQ-5D, showed significant improvement over 24 months (p < 0.001). Neurological function improved in most patients, with the number of Frankel grade 5 cases increasing from 14 to 17. Radiographic parameters demonstrated significant initial improvement at 3 months (p < 0.001); however, changes were not statistically significant over time. Complications included cement leakage (4 cases), adjacent fracture (2 cases), and 1 revision surgery; no major perioperative complications or mortality occurred.ConclusionsBELD combined with PVP is a safe and effective, minimally invasive option for older adults with OVCFs and neurological symptoms. This technique provides adequate neural decompression and vertebral stabilization while minimizing the risks associated with extensive fusion surgery in this vulnerable population.Keywords: Biportal endoscopic decompression, Vertebroplasty, Osteoporotic vertebral fracture, Minimally invasive spine surgery, Older adult patients

Osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs) affect approximately 1.4 million people worldwide annually, posing a considerable healthcare challenge, particularly in aging populations.1,2) Although most OVCFs resolve with conservative treatment, studies indicate that approximately 30% experience progressive collapse, 13% develop nonunion, and 3% progress to delayed vertebral collapse (DVC) with neurologic deficits.3) Additionally, osteoporosis impairs vertebral fracture healing, leading to progressive vertebral body collapse, segmental kyphosis, and spinal instability.4)

Surgical intervention becomes necessary for patients with neurologic symptoms or severe lumbosacral radiculopathy to restore functionality and quality of life.5) Traditional approaches, such as posterior decompression with instrumented fusion and, in severe cases, anterior column reconstruction,3) are often unsuitable for older adult patients because of poor bone quality, sarcopenia, global spinal imbalance, and medical frailty.6,7) Percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) is widely used for simple OVCFs, providing rapid pain relief in most cases. However, its role in treating fractures with neurologic complications is limited8,9,10) owing to risks of cement extravasation and potential neurologic compromise.11) Recent advances in minimally invasive techniques have expanded treatment options for these complex cases.12)

Biportal endoscopic spine surgery has emerged as an effective, minimally invasive method for achieving neural decompression with minimal surgical damage.13) Benefits include reduced postoperative pain, minimal blood loss, lower infection rates, and preserved paravertebral muscle function.14,15) When combined with PVP, this approach may offer a safer alternative to traditional surgeries for older adults with OVCF-related neurological complications.12)

This study evaluates the clinical outcomes of combining biportal endoscopic lumbar decompression (BELD) with PVP for treating OVCFs complicated by neurologic symptoms or severe lumbosacral radiculopathy in older adult patients. We hypothesize that this minimally invasive technique provides effective neural decompression and vertebral stabilization while minimizing the risks associated with extensive fusion surgery.12,16)

METHODS

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (IRB No. B-2310-856-102) and conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments. The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Board.

Patient Selection

We reviewed the medical records of patients who underwent surgery between July 2017 and June 2022 at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital. The study included older adult patients (≥ 70 years) with OVCF complicated by neurological deficits or severe, intractable pain unresponsive to conservative management. Severe pain was defined as a persistent visual analog scale (VAS) score ≥ 7 despite 6 weeks of conservative treatment, including medication, physical therapy, and spinal nerve blocks. Osteoporosis was confirmed via dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) scanning (T-scores ≤ −2.5).

Of 45 patients who underwent BELD combined with PVP, 24 were excluded: 2 with pathological fractures secondary to malignancy, 4 with prior spinal surgery (except vertebroplasty) at the affected levels, 12 with follow-up less than 2 years, and 6 with inadequate documentation. The final analysis included 21 patients (5 men and 16 women) with a mean age of 80.2 years (range, 72–95 years).

Clinical Assessment

Demographic data included age, sex, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI). Medical status was assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index and the American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification. Bone mineral density (BMD) was evaluated via DXA (absolute BMD [g/cm2] and relative BMD [T-score]). Postoperative osteoporosis medication was also documented.

Clinical outcomes were evaluated using validated patient-reported outcome measures. Pain intensity was assessed with the VAS, which ranges from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst imaginable pain). VAS scores were recorded separately for lower back pain and lower extremity radiating pain to differentiate the effects of treatment on axial and radicular symptoms. Functional disability was measured using the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), a condition-specific outcome measure with 10 sections evaluating the impact of back pain on daily activities. ODI scores range from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater disability. Quality of life was assessed using the European Quality of Life-5 Dimensions (EQ-5D) questionnaire, which evaluates 5 dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. These assessments were performed preoperatively and at each follow-up visit.

Clinical outcomes were also evaluated using the modified MacNab criteria, which classifies results as excellent (complete relief of symptoms), good (occasional discomfort without impairment of daily activities), fair (improved functional capacity but handicapped by intermittent pain), or poor (symptoms unchanged or worse). For patients with neurological deficits, motor function was evaluated using the Frankel grade classification system. The reason for surgery was classified into the following 2 categories: motor weakness or unrelieved pain.

Surgical parameters recorded included operative time, estimated blood loss, length of hospital stay, and any surgery-related complications. For vertebroplasty procedures, the volume of cement injected was documented for both primary and redo procedures.

Radiographic Assessment

All patients underwent comprehensive radiographic evaluation, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT), and plain radiographs. Radiographic measurements were performed by a single orthopedic surgeon (SSN; a 3-year experienced orthopedic spine surgeon) who was blinded to the study outcomes, using a picture archiving and communication system (PACS; Infinitt).

Vertebral body measurements were obtained from lateral radiographs at 1.5 times magnification. To account for magnification bias, the mean height of adjacent vertebral bodies (superior [R1] and inferior [R2]) was used as a reference value (R = [R1 + R2] / 2). The vertebral compression rate (VCR) was calculated as a percentage using the formula VCR = (1 − A / R) × 100, where “A” represents the anterior vertebral body height.17) In cases where adjacent vertebrae were also fractured, measurements were taken from the nearest intact vertebra. Wedge angle (WA) was measured as the angle between the superior and inferior endplates of the fractured vertebra.17) These measurements were performed preoperatively and at postoperative intervals of 3, 12, and 24 months.

The degree of central and foraminal stenosis was graded according to established classification systems on MRI and/or CT images.18,19) The presence of an intravertebral cleft (IVC) was assessed on both plain radiographs and MRI. An IVC was identified as a linear or cystic area of low signal intensity on T1-weighted images and high signal intensity on T2-weighted images, indicating fluid collection within the vertebral body.20) In cases of failed vertebroplasty, the presence of an empty space around augmented cement in the vertebral body was confirmed either by IVCs on radiographs and/or fluid signal on MRI.17,20)

Surgical Procedure

All surgical procedures were performed by a single orthopedic surgeon (SMP) under general anesthesia. The patient was positioned prone on a radiolucent operating table with the abdomen free. Two standard cushions were placed beneath the chest and pelvis to maintain appropriate thoracolumbar curvature and facilitate clear fluoroscopic imaging.

Biportal endoscopic surgery

Two separate 1-cm skin incisions were made for the biportal endoscopic approach. For central decompression, the working portal was created approximately 1 cm lateral to the midline at the interlaminar space, and the viewing portal was placed vertically 1 cm proximal to the working portal with a 7-mm incision.14) For foraminal decompression, the viewing and working portals were created 2 cm lateral to the lateral pedicular margin, and each incision for the portals was 0.7 cm in length.21) A 30° endoscope was introduced through the viewing portal, and surgical instruments were manipulated through the working portal. Continuous saline irrigation was maintained at a pressure of 30–40 mmHg to ensure clear visualization and prevent soft tissue bleeding.

For central canal stenosis, the ipsilateral lower portion of the lamina was removed first using an endoscopic drill and Kerrison punches. The base of the spinous process was partially resected to access the contralateral side. The ligamentum flavum was carefully detached from its insertion on the superior lamina and removed to expose the dural sac. Decompression continued until complete mobilization of the dural sac was achieved, and pulsation was observed. For foraminal stenosis, the superior articular process was partially resected using an endoscopic drill and angled curettes. The extent of resection was determined by preoperative imaging and intraoperative findings. The hypertrophied ligamentum flavum was removed, and the superior portion of the caudal pedicle was undercut to ensure adequate decompression of the exiting nerve root. Careful hemostasis was maintained throughout the procedure using radiofrequency ablation.

Vertebroplasty procedure

Following neural decompression, PVP was performed under fluoroscopic guidance. An 11-gauge PVP needle was inserted via a transpedicular approach. For patients with severe vertebral collapse or sclerotic bone, a channel was created using a drill bit under fluoroscopic guidance. For redo PVP cases, preoperative imaging was carefully reviewed to identify areas of insufficient cement fill or new clefts. The needle trajectory was planned to access these specific areas while avoiding the existing cement mass.17) The PVP needle was positioned in the anterior third of the vertebral body. The polymethyl methacrylate cement was prepared to achieve appropriate viscosity. Under direct endoscopic visualization of the posterior vertebral wall, cement was injected slowly and continuously while monitoring for any leakage. The initial injection was directed anteriorly, and subsequent injections filled the middle and posterior portions of the vertebral body. Additional cement was carefully injected to achieve adequate vertebral body stability (Figs. 1 and 2).20)

Statistical Analysis

All variables were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The normal distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Statistical analysis was performed using generalized estimating equations (GEE) to analyze changes in clinical outcomes (VAS scores for back and leg pain, ODI scores, EQ-5D values) and radiographic outcomes (VCR and WA) during follow-up. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons between 0 and 3 months were performed within the GEE framework to determine specific time-dependent changes. Stata/MP 17.1 (Stata Corp.) was used for all analyses. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and results were considered statistically significant if p < 0.05.

RESULTS

A total of 21 patients (5 men and 16 women) with a mean age of 80.24 ± 5.15 years were included in this study. The mean BMI was 23.62 ± 4.44 kg/m2, and the mean BMD T-score was −3.13 ± 1.04. The most common fracture level was L3 (9 cases), followed by L2, L4, and L5 (3 cases each). The majority of decompressions were performed at L3–4 (7 cases) and L–3 (6 cases) (Table 1). Surgical Characteristics

The mean operation time was 86.81 ± 23.04 minutes, with average intraoperative blood loss of 136.19 ± 104.43 mL and postoperative drainage of 84.67 ± 55.97 mL. No patients required blood transfusion during or after surgery. The mean hospital stay was 6.43 ± 6.87 days. Twelve patients underwent central decompression, and 14 patients received foraminal decompression. The mean volume of injected cement was 11.36 ± 5.42 mL. Nine patients underwent surgery on both sides, while 8 had right-sided and 4 had left-sided procedures. Most patients (19 of 21) received postoperative osteoporosis medication, with teriparatide being the most common (10 patients) (Table 3).

Clinical Outcomes

Clinical outcome measures, including VAS for back pain, VAS for leg pain, ODI, and EQ-5D, showed significant improvements over the 24-month follow-up period (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3A-D). Of the 7 patients who presented with preoperative motor weakness, 6 underwent central decompression, and 1 patient required both central and foraminal decompression. Neurological status improved in most patients, with the number of Frankel grade 5 patients increasing from 14 preoperatively to 17 at the final follow-up. Based on the modified MacNab criteria, 16 patients (76.2%) achieved excellent or good outcomes, 4 patients had fair results, and 1 patient had a poor outcome (Table 3).

Radiographic Outcomes

Although the overall changes in VCR and WA during the follow-up period were not statistically significant (p > 0.05), post-hoc analysis revealed significant improvements between the preoperative and 3-month postoperative measurements (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3E and F).

Complications

Several complications were observed: cement leakage in 4 cases, adjacent-level fracture in 2 cases, cement breakage in 2 cases, and 1 case each of refracture, hematoma, and revision surgery requiring posterior decompression and fusion. No major perioperative complications, such as cardiovascular events, cerebrovascular events, pulmonary thromboembolism, or mortality, occurred during the follow-up period.

DISCUSSION

This study indicates that BELD combined with PVP may serve as a minimally invasive and effective treatment for older adults with OVCFs complicated by neurological symptoms or severe intractable pain. Our findings suggest that this approach achieves adequate neural decompression and vertebral stabilization while avoiding the risks associated with extensive fusion surgery in this vulnerable population. Significant improvements in clinical outcomes and radiographic outcomes were observed, with sustained benefits over the 2-year follow-up period. Although most patients experienced favorable outcomes, a small subset required additional interventions, such as nerve blocks or revision surgery. This underscores the importance of careful patient selection and thorough preoperative planning. Notably, this technique proved effective even in patients with severe osteoporosis (T-scores ≤ −3.5), a group that typically presents significant challenges for conventional surgical methods.

Traditionally, patients with OVCF experiencing neurological deficits or severe pain have been treated with posterior decompression and instrumented fusion, often requiring extensive surgical procedures.3) In cases of severe vertebral collapse or substantial canal compromise, anterior column reconstruction via corpectomy may be necessary. However, such approaches pose considerable challenges in older adults with severe osteoporosis.22) Poor bone quality increases the risk of instrumentation failure, including screw loosening and pullout, while medical comorbidities and frailty elevate perioperative risks.23) Complication rates for traditional fusion surgery in older adults have been reported to range from 20% to 30%, including hardware failure, adjacent segment problems, and wound complications.24) Furthermore, extensive soft tissue dissection during posterior fusion can lead to considerable blood loss, prolonged recovery, and increased surgical site infection risk in this population.24)

It is important to note that DVC presents differently depending on vertebral location. Nakajima et al.3) demonstrated that lower lumbar DVC often manifests as flat-type or concave-type fractures with predominantly foraminal stenosis, leading to lumbosacral radiculopathy, whereas thoracolumbar DVC typically presents with wedge-type fractures and kyphotic deformity.25) In our series, the surgical approach was tailored to each patient's specific pathology, with central decompression performed for central canal stenosis (12 patients) and foraminal decompression for foraminal stenosis (14 patients). This individualized approach acknowledges the heterogeneous nature of pain and neurological complications in OVCF and DVC.

Recent studies have explored less invasive alternatives for treating OVCFs. Singh et al.26) demonstrated successful outcomes with open vertebroplasty combined with lumbar decompression in older adults with thoracolumbar stenosis secondary to OVCFs. Similarly, Kang et al.12) reported favorable outcomes using BELD combined with PVP for older adults with lower lumbar DVC. These studies highlight that limited decompression combined with vertebral augmentation can provide effective neural decompression and pain relief while mitigating the risks of extensive fusion surgery. Our approach builds on these findings by incorporating biportal endoscopic techniques, which enhance visualization and minimize tissue trauma compared to traditional open decompression.

Our study demonstrated comparable clinical and radiographic outcomes to those reported in prior studies. Most patients (76.2%) achieved excellent or good results per the modified MacNab criteria, with significant improvements in VAS scores and functional outcomes over the 24-month follow-up period. Neurological improvement was also notable, with the number of Frankel grade 5 patients increasing from 13 preoperatively to 16 at the final follow-up. These outcomes are particularly noteworthy given the advanced age (mean, 80.24 years) and severe osteoporosis (mean T-score, −3.13) of our cohort compared to traditional fusion surgery, our approach had lower complication rates and shorter recovery times. Previous studies on conventional fusion have reported hardware-related complication rates of up to 15%.23) In contrast, our series had a low rate of major complications, with only 1 patient requiring revision surgery and no cases of mortality or severe medical complications.

The effectiveness of this technique can be attributed to several factors—the minimal tissue trauma associated with biportal endoscopy (mean blood loss: 136.19 mL; no transfusions required), direct visualization during decompression, and the stabilizing effect of vertebroplasty without instrumentation. This approach allows for neural decompression and vertebral stabilization through small incisions, reducing the physiological stress on older adult patients, as evidenced by the relatively short mean hospital stay (6.43 days).

This study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective design and absence of a control group limit direct comparisons with other surgical techniques. Second, the small sample size and single-center nature of the study may reduce the generalizability of the findings. Third, although substantial, the 2-year follow-up period may not capture long-term complications such as adjacent segment issues or recurrence of symptoms. Fourth, the learning curve associated with biportal endoscopic techniques may affect reproducibility in less experienced settings. Fifth, the inclusion of heterogeneous pathologies, such as both acute fractures and DVC, could have influenced the outcomes. Another limitation is the heterogeneity of osteoporosis treatments in our cohort while 19 of 21 patients received postoperative osteoporosis medication, the specific agents varied (teriparatide being most common in 10 patients), which could potentially influence healing rates and clinical outcomes. Standardizing osteoporosis treatment is challenging in clinical practice due to variations in patients' medical history, contraindications, insurance coverage, and prior treatment response. Future studies with more homogeneous osteoporosis management protocols would be valuable to control for this variable.

Our findings demonstrate that BELD combined with PVP is a viable, minimally invasive treatment for older adults with OVCFs complicated by neurological symptoms or severe intractable pain. This approach provides effective neural decompression and vertebral stabilization while minimizing the risks associated with extensive fusion surgery in this high-risk population. However, optimal outcomes require careful patient selection, meticulous preoperative planning, and adequate surgical expertise.
