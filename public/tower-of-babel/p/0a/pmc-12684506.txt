New‐Onset Atrial Fibrillation During ST‐Segment–Elevation Myocardial Infarction: Risk of Recurrence and Its Clinical Impact During 10 Years of Follow‐Up
Authors: Marina M Demidova, Maria A Baturova, David Erlinge, Pyotr G Platonov
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial fibrillation (AF) often complicates ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), but data on AF recurrence after first‐ever AF during STEMI are scarce. We aimed at assessing the risk of AF recurrence after first‐ever transient AF during admission for STEMI.MethodsWe performed a single‐center, register‐based cohort study. Patients with STEMI discharged alive after primary percutaneous coronary intervention during 2007 to 2010 were followed until 2018. Clinical characteristics, date of AF diagnosis, and mortality status were retrieved from Swedish national registries. All ECGs recorded before, during, or after STEMI were reviewed. The primary end point was the first documented AF episode after discharge, and the secondary end point was mortality. The risk of AF during follow‐up was calculated using the Fine‐Gray subdistributional hazard model for AF and total mortality, regressed on prespecified clinical and demographic covariates. In patients with first‐ever transient AF during STEMI, we looked for the factors associated with AF recurrence.ResultsOf 2120 patients discharged alive, 127 (5.9%) had AF during admission for STEMI. Patients with AF during STEMI were more likely to develop AF during follow‐up (hazard ratio [HR], 4.82 [95% CI, 3.86–6.02]; P<0.001), with 28% AF recurrence by 1 year. Compared with no AF, patients with AF during STEMI had a higher risk of death (HR, 1.17 [95% CI, 1.01–1.35]; P=0.033). No clinical or demographic characteristics predicted recurrent AF during follow‐up. Low P‐wave voltage in lead I at predischarge electrocardiography was independently associated with recurrent AF during follow‐up (HR, 2.39 [95% CI, 1.23–4.62]; P=0.010).ConclusionsTransient AF during STEMI is associated with an increased risk of AF after discharge and long‐term mortality.Keywords: atrial fibrillation, P wave, STEMISubject Categories: Atrial Fibrillation, Acute Coronary Syndromes

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

First‐ever transient atrial fibrillation (AF) during ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction admission is associated with a 4.8‐fold increased risk of AF after discharge, with recurrence rates reaching 28% by the end of the first year postdischarge.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Given the high recurrence rate and similar prognostic implications of new‐onset AF during ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction and preexisting AF, patients who experience a single transient AF episode during hospitalization for ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction should be managed in the same way as those with AF unrelated to ischemia.

Atrial fibrillation (AF) complicates the course of ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) in 5% to 14% of patients. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 AF during STEMI is associated with major in‐hospital complications, such as acute heart failure, pulmonary edema, cardiogenic shock, major bleeding, stroke, and increased in‐hospital mortality. 1 , 4 , 5 , 6 Even if successfully revascularized and discharged from the hospital, patients with STEMI with AF remain at risk of thrombotic and bleeding events, including stroke, coronary ischemic events due to stent thrombosis, and antithrombotic‐related bleeding. 7

AF in settings of acute myocardial ischemia and infarction can be precipitated by acute hypoxia, hypokalemia, endogenous or exogenous catecholamines, left ventricular dysfunction, and inflammation, actors that may superimpose on the underlying atrial substrate. 8 Whether first‐ever transient AF during STEMI, occurring under these specific clinical conditions, can be considered reversible with a low likelihood of recurrence remains uncertain. It is also unclear whether patients discharged in sinus rhythm after experiencing first‐ever AF during STEMI should be regarded differently from those with preexisting paroxysmal or persistent AF in terms of long‐term thromboembolic risk.

During the past decade, several indices and risk scores were proposed to predict AF in various clinical scenarios that include clinical characteristics, laboratory markers, echocardiographic and electrocardiographic indices, and combined scores. Several electrocardiographic‐based P‐wave measures were suggested as indices of atrial remodeling. Prolonged P‐wave duration was shown to predict AF occurrence in large community‐based cohorts. 9 , 10 Interatrial block (IAB) that reflects conduction delay between the right and left atria 11 was shown to predict AF in the general population 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 and in patients after non‐STEMI. 16 P‐wave terminal force in lead V1 (PTF‐V1) predicted AF in the general population 10 , 17 and was shown to strongly correlate with left atrial enlargement. 18 Reduced P‐wave voltage in lead I that reflects altered cardiac conductive properties, and therefore the extent of atrial fibrosis, was associated with AF both as a stand‐alone index 19 and as a component of recently proposed electrocardiographic‐based morphology‐voltage‐P‐wave duration score. 20 , 21

However, none of these electrocardiographic markers were tested to predict recurrence of AF in patients discharged in sinus rhythm after transient AF during STEMI. Our study was conducted to assess the risk of AF recurrence and to identify predictors of AF recurrence after first‐ever transient AF during admission for STEMI.

METHODS

Study Population

We performed a retrospective, single‐center, register‐based cohort observational study, and report the data in accordance with the Strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. 22 The study group consisted of consecutive patients with STEMI admitted to the Skåne University Hospital for primary percutaneous coronary interventions (PCI) during the 4‐year period from 2007 to 2010 (the study group was presented in detail previously). 23 The patients had an opportunity to opt out from inclusion in this register‐based analysis. If a patient was admitted more than once during the study period, only the first admission was accounted as the index admission.

Data Sources

Our study used the information from several Swedish nationwide and regional health care registries and databases. The SWEDEHEART (Swedish Web‐System for Enhancement and Development of Evidence‐Based Care in Heart Disease Evaluated According to Recommended Therapies) registry was used to assess demographic and clinical characteristics as well as angiography findings. The registry contains information on hospitalization for myocardial infarction and coronary interventions on the nationwide level. SWEDEHEART had been established in 2010 and covered former RIKS‐HIA (Register of Information and Knowledge About Swedish Heart Intensive Care Admissions) (formed in 1991), SCAAR (Swedish Coronary Angiography and Angioplasty Register) (formed in1998), and the Register of Heart Surgery (formed in 1992). For our study, we exported from the national SWEDEHEART registry the data for consecutive patients who were admitted to Skåne University Hospital for PCI for STEMI during 2007 to 2010. Because every Swedish citizen has a unique personal identification number, the data from different health‐related national registries can be easily merged. In our study, information on the first date of AF diagnosis (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision [ICD‐10] code I48), ischemic stroke (ICD‐10 code I63), and mortality outcomes was obtained from the SNPR (Swedish National Patient Register) and the SCDR (Swedish Cause of Death Register).

For every included patient, all available ECGs recorded at any time before, during, or at any time after STEMI (n=58 907) and stored in digital format were retrieved from either the GE Marquette MUSE system (GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, WI) or the Infinity MegaCare ECG Management System (Dräger, Lübeck, Germany) databases. The Skåne Region digital ECG archive contains all ECGs recorded in the Skåne Region during hospital admissions and in primary care facilities from 1988 onward.

Group Allocation and Exclusion Criteria

First‐ever transient AF, occurring after STEMI symptom onset during index hospitalization, was the arrhythmia of interest in our study. It was identified through: (1) the SWEDEHEART registry and SNPR, (2) the ECG digital database, and (3) medical histories, including heart rhythm surveillance telemetry records.

AF before STEMI (preexisting AF) was documented by record linkage with the SNPR and review of ECGs recorded before STEMI from the regional digital archive.

Patients with first‐ever AF discharged with ongoing AF and patients with AF first documented during or after an acute coronary artery bypass grafting procedure during index hospitalization for STEMI were excluded from analysis.

Study End Points

The primary end point was a first AF episode after discharge.

AF during follow‐up was assessed for the period starting after discharge and ending December 31, 2018, using the SNPR and the regional ECG archive. In patients who experienced their first‐ever transient AF during STEMI, we examined the ECG archive to identify the first ECG after discharge that showed AF. For patients who had no AF before or during STEMI, we reviewed the ECG archive and also checked the SNPR archive for the earliest recorded date of AF diagnosis.

Secondary end points were total mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and ischemic stroke during follow‐up.

ECG Analysis

The latest ECGs on sinus rhythm before discharge from hospital were analyzed to assess conventional ECG‐based P‐wave indexes calculated using the Glasgow algorithm. 24 P‐wave duration was obtained from a representative median beat based on all 12 leads as the interval between the earliest detection of atrial depolarization in any lead (P onset) and the latest detection of atrial depolarization in any lead (P offset), measured in milliseconds. A P‐wave axis <0° or >75° was considered abnormal. PTF‐V1, the depth of terminal negative component of a biphasic P wave, was calculated, and 2 cutoffs used to define abnormal PTF‐V1 were analyzed: >30 mm/ms and >40 mm/ms. 18 P‐wave voltage in lead I was assessed as the absolute maximum P‐wave amplitude in lead I. P‐wave morphology was automatically processed and coded as positive, negative, or biphasic. IAB was diagnosed in case of P‐wave duration ≥120 ms and was further classified as partial IAB if P waves were positive in inferior leads aVF and II, and as advanced IAB if P waves were biphasic (+/−) or negative in inferior leads III and aVF. P‐wave prolongation ≥120 ms with a biphasic (+/−) or negative P morphology in all inferior leads II, III, aVF was defined as typical advanced IAB. 11 , 21 The score comprising P‐wave morphology in inferior leads, P‐wave voltage in lead I, and P‐wave duration (morphology‐voltage‐P‐wave duration score) was calculated as previously described. 20

Statistical Analysis

To estimate the incidence of AF during follow‐up in the presence of competing risk of death during the long follow‐up period, a cumulative incidence competing‐risk method was used. The risk of AF during follow‐up was calculated using the Fine‐Gray subdistributional hazard model for AF and total mortality, regressed on prespecified clinical and demographic covariates, and presented as a subdistributional hazard ratio (SHR) and 95% CI. 25

Prespecified predictors of AF recurrence during follow‐up were tested in patients with AF during admission for STEMI in the Cox regression analysis adjusted for clinical and demographic covariates. The proportional hazards assumption for the Cox models was assessed using Schoenfeld residual plots. Based on prior studies, the following variables were identified as clinically relevant due to their association with the risk of atrial fibrillation: age, sex, diabetes, hypertension, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) <30%, and use of β‐blockers at discharge. These variables were included in the Cox model for predicting AF recurrence. For the secondary end points, the Cox models included age, sex, diabetes, hypertension, LVEF <30%, and the use of β‐blockers and anticoagulants at discharge. The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied for all covariates in the multivariable Cox regression models.

The functional form of continuous covariates was assessed using plots of martingale residuals against each variable. These residuals did not indicate any systematic deviation from linearity, supporting the use of linear terms for continuous variables in the Cox models. The Kaplan‐Meier method was used to generate survival curves indicating freedom from AF, stroke, as well as total and cardiovascular mortality during follow‐up.

Continuous variables are presented as mean±SD or median and 25% to 75% as appropriate, depending on distribution. Variables were compared across groups using χ2 or Fisher exact tests for categorical variables and the Student t test for continuous variables with an approximate normal distribution, or Mann‐Whitney U test, as appropriate, without imputation of missing data. P values <0.05 were considered significant.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY) and the R statistical programming language (version 4.2.3).

The study was approved by the regional ethics committee in Lund, Sweden (number 2010/585, 2010‐11‐29).

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

RESULTS

Patient Characteristics

Between January 1, 2007, and December 31, 2010, 2276 consecutive patients with STEMI were admitted to Lund University Hospital, Sweden, for primary PCI. The clinical characteristics of the study population have been described in detail previously. 23 , 26 Of the 2276 patients admitted with STEMI, 164 (7.2%) had new‐onset AF and 180 (7.9%) had a history of AF (Figure 1).

There were 2148 patients discharged from hospital. After exclusion of patients with AF after acute coronary artery bypass grafting for STEMI and patients with first‐ever AF discharged, the study group comprised 2120 patients; there were 127 (5.9%) patients with transient new‐onset AF during admission for STEMI, 164 (7.7%) patients with preexisting AF, and 1829 (86.3%) patients with no AF either before or during STEMI.

Of 127 patients with new‐onset AF during admission for STEMI, in 31 patients AF was observed before PCI (the time from STEMI symptom onset to AF detection 2 [1–6] hours), and in 96 patients after PCI (the time from STEMI symptom onset to AF detection 49 [28–85] hours).

As compared with patients with no AF, patients with new‐onset AF were older, had a greater incidence of low LVEF, and had a higher Killip class (see Table 1 for clinical characteristics). The localization of myocardial infarction was not associated with AF during STEMI. As compared with patients with preexisting AF, patients with new‐onset AF had a lower incidence of comorbidities such as hypertension, previous myocardial infarction, chronic heart failure, and stroke.

AF Recurrence (Primary End Point)

During the first year after STEMI, AF was observed in 36 of 127 (29.3%) patients with transient AF during admission for STEMI and in 40 (2.2%) of patients with no AF during STEMI. By the end of follow‐up, AF was observed in 74 of 127 (58.3%) patients with transient AF during admission for STEMI and in 56 of 1829 (3.1%) patients with no AF during STEMI (Figure 1). Median time to AF during follow‐up in patients with AF during admission for STEMI was 282 (46–1366) days. Median length of follow‐up for AF was 3432 (2986–3650) days. The risk of AF after discharge was greater in patients with AF during admission for STEMI (SHR, 4.82 [95% CI, 3.86–6.02]; P<0.001) than in patients with no AF and was not related to whether new AF occurred before or after PCI for STEMI (Figure 2).

In patients with transient AF during admission for STEMI, we looked for the factors associated with AF recurrence during follow‐up. Clinical and demographic characteristics were analyzed for all 127 patients, and ECG‐based variables were analyzed for 120 patients for whom ECGs before discharge recorded in sinus rhythm were available (Table 2). No clinical or demographic characteristics were associated with AF recurrence during follow‐up (Table S1). In patients who were discharged alive in sinus rhythm after their first‐ever transient AF during STEMI, age did not influence the risk of AF recurrence. The risk of AF recurrence during follow‐up did not differ with regard to the timing of AF during STEMI (before or after PCI). P‐wave characteristics associated with AF recurrence during follow‐up in the univariable analysis were P duration, increased PTF‐V1, and low P voltage in lead I (Table 3). P‐wave voltage in lead I <0.093 mV (75th percentile) (hazard ratio [HR], 2.39 [95% CI, 1.23–4.62]; P=0.010) remained independently associated with AF recurrence after adjusting for prespecified clinical and demographic covariates.

Mortality and Ischemic Stroke (Secondary End Point)

During the follow‐up period, 523 of 1829 patients died. Median length of follow‐up for total and cardiovascular death was 3365 (2935–3650) days. Both AF during admission for STEMI and preexisting AF were associated with long‐term total mortality and cardiovascular mortality after adjusting for clinical and demographic covariates (Table 4, Figure 3). Patients with AF during admission for STEMI did not differ from patients with preexisting AF with regard to either total or cardiovascular mortality.

During follow‐up, 159 patients had an ischemic stroke. Median length of follow‐up for stroke was 3325 (2658–3650) days. The unadjusted risk of stroke was greater in patients with AF during admission for STEMI than in patients without AF (HR, 1.38 [95% CI, 1.03–1.83]; P=0.028) but did not differ between patients with AF during admission for STEMI and patients with preexisting AF. After adjusting for clinical and demographic covariates, no significant association was found between AF during admission for STEMI and stroke (HR, 1.05 [95% CI, 0.76–1.44]; P=0.786) (Table 4). Preexisting AF was associated with an increased risk of stroke after adjusting for clinical and demographic covariates.

DISCUSSION

The main findings of the study were: (1) New‐onset transient AF in STEMI is relatively common and occurs in 6.5% of all STEMI admissions. (2) Transient AF during admission for STEMI is independently associated with increased long‐term mortality. (3) A >4.8‐fold increased risk of AF after discharge was shown in patients with transient AF during admission for STEMI as compared with patients with no AF during index STEMI admission. (4) The risk of AF recurrence after discharge is associated with low P‐wave voltage in lead I at predischarge electrocardiography.

The rate of new‐onset AF during STEMI in our study is similar to previous reports. 1 , 3 The reported occurrence rate depends on definitions and inclusion criteria, silent AF occurs more frequently than symptomatic AF (13% versus 4%), 27 and occurrence during the first 2 days of symptom onset is higher than occurrence during the subsequent 3 to 30 days after STEMI (7.5% versus 4%). 28 In contrast with declining incidence rates of ventricular arrhythmias complicating myocardial infarction, 29 there was no notable change in incidence of transient AF complicating acute myocardial infarction over the past 20 years. 1 , 3 , 30

Although AF in settings of acute STEMI can be considered secondary to reversible ischemic and metabolic substrate, AF often recurs after discharge despite successful revascularization. According to some previously published studies, AF recurrence by 1 year after STEMI ranged between 22% and 35%. 3 , 31 Guenancia et al reported AF recurrence in 24% of patients with symptomatic new‐onset AF and in 13% of patients with asymptomatic AF by year 3 of follow‐up. 27 In our study, the AF recurrence rate was 30% during the 1‐year follow‐up period and 58% during the 10‐year follow‐up period. The actual rate of AF recurrence in our study is likely even higher, because we predominantly captured symptomatic AF that led patients to seek medical care and undergo electrocardiography recording. Silent AF during follow‐up might have been underestimated, because routine ambulatory electrocardiography monitoring was not performed after discharge.

Most of the previous studies documented AF during follow‐up through the review of patient's medical records at the end of the study period. The strength of our study was the review of all ECGs recorded for each included patient through accessing the regional ECG database (nearly 60 000 ECGs were processed), which was essential to detect preexisting AF and thus minimize the number of undetected AF cases. Additionally, we retrieved the information on AF, stroke, and survival status from national databases, which allows collection of information from health care providers outside Skåne. We believe that working with electrocardiographic archive and administrative databases allows us to provide a higher level of detail concerning arrhythmic events and major adverse events.

The high AF recurrence rate after revascularized STEMI, when acute and reversible triggers are presumed to be resolved, indicates the presence of the atrial substrate, and AF predictors are expected to be related to structural or electrical atrial remodeling. Elevated ventricular filling pressure due to left ventricular myocardial dysfunction leads to increased atrial pressure, causing left atrial dilation. 32 , 33 If ischemic mitral regurgitation due to papillary muscle dysfunction is present, then the left atrium volume overload causes greater atrial remodeling. 33 In left circumflex or right coronary artery occlusions proximal to left atrial brunch, atrial myocardial ischemia can coexist with ischemia at the ventricular myocardium. 34 , 35 Severe atrial scarring and fibrosis was shown to be due to replacement of extensive areas of cardiomyocyte loss in left atrial infarctions. 33 Additionally, some genetic predisposition to recurrent AF cannot be ruled out. Recent results of a genom‐wide association studies meta‐analysis indicate that patients with recurrent AF seem to be at greater genetic risk for recurrent events. 36

Left atrial volume, age, and creatinine level were reported as risk factors for AF recurrence after myocardial infarction. 27 In our study, most patients had multiple comorbidities, but no demographic or clinical factors were associated with AF recurrence. Neither high CHA2DS2‐VASc score nor low LVEF were associated with AF recurrence during follow‐up. Although we were unable to review echocardiographic examinations made before discharge to assess atrial dimensions, we did analyze electrocardiographic markers of atrial abnormalities from ECGs recorded after revascularization and after elimination of acute ischemia, and we calculated P‐wave‐based indexes, and PTF‐V1 in particular, which was previously shown to be strongly correlated with left atrial enlargement. 18

Atrial electromechanical dysfunction is often associated with left atrial enlargement, although interatrial conduction delay can occur even without atrial enlargement due to fibrosis in the atrium. 11 In their study using electrophysiological mapping, Park et al showed that low P‐wave amplitude in lead I is associated with low left atrial voltage, slow intra‐left atrial conduction velocity, and displaced interatrial conduction pattern. 19 Alexander et al demonstrated significant colinear associations between reduced P‐wave voltage in lead I and presence and degree of IAB and its predictive value for new AF occurrence in non‐STEMI. 37 In our study, no type of IAB was associated with AF recurrence, and furthermore, typical advanced IAB was observed infrequently (3.1%) in patients with new AF. P‐wave duration, P‐wave amplitude in lead I, and PTF‐V1 were associated with AF recurrence after STEMI, and P‐wave amplitude in lead I remained independently associated with AF recurrence in multivariable analysis including clinical variables.

The results of previously published studies on the association of transient AF during STEMI and ischemic stroke are contradictory. 3 , 27 In a study performed during the pre‐PCI era, patients discharged after transient AF during STEMI on antiplatelet agents were shown to have increased risk of ischemic stroke. 3 In our study, in line with study by Guenancia et al, 27 transient AF during admission for STEMI was not associated with ischemic stroke during follow‐up after adjustment for clinical covariates.

Despite its transient character, new AF during admission for STEMI was associated with an increased risk of long‐term total and cardiovascular mortality. This finding is in line with the results of previous studies, 27 , 38 , 39 particularly in populations of patients with left ventricular dysfunction. 39 A recently published study reported that in patients with improved LVEF after myocardial infarction, new‐onset AF during STEMI is associated with increased incidence rate of all‐cause death, cardiovascular death, major adverse cardiac events, and hospitalization for heart failure during 5‐year follow‐up compared with patients without AF during STEMI. 40 In a study performed during the pre‐PCI era, long‐term survival was significantly lower in patients with transient AF regardless of whether AF occurred within the first 24 hours or after 24 hours from symptom onset; in the same study, the prognosis of patients with preexisting AF did not differ from the prognosis of patients on sinus rhythm. 35 In our study, the risk of mortality did not differ significantly between patients with new AF and patients with preexisting AF. Similar prognostic implications suggest similar pathophysiological mechanisms of new AF during STEMI and conventional nonvalvular AF.

Limitations

First, our ECG database is limited to the Skåne region and might have underreported episodes that occurred when patients traveled outside the region. Second, although the patients were continuously monitored during hospital admission, ambulatory electrocardiographic monitoring was not routinely performed after discharge, and silent AF during follow‐up may be underreported. Also, the patients with transient AF during admission for STEMI may have higher chance of capturing recurrent AF, because they might have been more vigorously monitored for recurrence than those without AF during STEMI. Finally, echocardiographic data at discharge were not routinely collected for this cohort of patients with STEMI. Consequently, data on left atrial size and volume, which have been previously shown to predict AF recurrence in various clinical settings, were not available.

CONCLUSIONS

New‐onset transient AF during admission for STEMI is associated with a 4.8‐fold increased risk of AF after discharge, reaching 28% AF recurrence by 1 year after discharge and 58% AF recurrence during 10 years of follow‐up. New‐onset AF during admission for STEMI is also associated with an increased risk of long‐term total and cardiovascular mortality. The prognostic implications of new‐onset and preexisting AF and electrocardiographic markers for AF recurrence after STEMI and conventional AF are similar, which suggests the evolution of similar pathophysiological mechanisms of atrial remodeling and atrial cardiomyopathy.
