Microbes first into the life rafts: preserving microbiomes to secure health in degrading ecosystems
Authors: Panuya Athithan, Suzanne L Ishaq, Emma Allen-Vercoe, Kieran C O’Doherty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAll organisms on the planet intrinsically rely on microbial ecosystems, and there are increasing calls from research communities to consider microbiota when administering personal or public health, ecosystem health, and the use of microbiota in personal or environmental health remediation, such as reducing the impacts of climate change, or protecting at-risk habitats, which host rare microbiota. Through our collective work on the integral nature of microbiomes to host and environmental health, on health policy, and on the development of research and policy agendas, we have previously developed the concept of “microbiome stewardship” and guidelines to promote consideration of microbial communities broadly or in specific scenarios. The practicality of stewarding one versus many microbiota is highly contextual, and will require different strategies for different scales of conservation. Here, we provide scientific arguments for the need for microbial stewardship, examples of possible solutions scaled to different ecological challenges or conservation goals, discourse on the logistical challenges that have been cited by research communities, and opportunities to use cutting-edge microbiome concepts and technology to implement large-scale interventions.

Sustainability statement

Microorganisms are responsible for environmental and organismal health, and the stewardship of microbiota has applications for human, plant, animal, and environmental health on local and global scales. The concepts described here are pertinent, in particular, to the United Nations’ Sustainable Development Goal (3) good health and well-being. Additionally, our paper is relevant to goals (6) clean water and sanitation, (9) industry, innovation, and infrastructure, (11) sustainable cities and communities, (12) responsible consumption and production, (13) climate action, (14) life below water, (15) life on land, and (17) partnerships for the goals.

Introduction

Historically, our understanding of, and relationship to, microorganisms has originated from the ways in which we most closely interact with them, even before we knew they existed. In this way, our concept of microorganisms/microbiota went from “a nuisance” to “a tool”: from food spoilers to fermentation chemists, from infectious agents to probiotics, from competitors for resources to partners for hosts. Environmental ecosystems, from soil to water to air, can only support complex forms of life because of the chemistry that microorganisms perform (Gilbert and Neufeld 2014) and many host ecosystems rely on facultative or obligate symbioses with microbiota to support specific biological functions. Given our intrinsic reliance on microbial ecosystems, research communities have been discussing the need to consider microbiota as a facet of planetary health for several decades. For example, as strong modifiers of environments, arguments have been made for using microbiota for environmental remediation and reducing the impacts of climate change (Bodelier 2011, Peixoto et al. 2022, Rappuoli et al. 2023), which recently culminated in a microbial specialist group for the International Union for the Conservation of Nature (IUCN) with the goal of developing a framework for conservation of biological and environmental entities (Gilbert et al. 2025). In parallel, public health efforts have promoted the integration of microbial ecology into personal and public medicine (Lowry et al. 2016, Ishaq et al. 2019, Rook 2022). Biological conservation efforts have also recognized the importance of specific microorganisms or collections thereof: microbial communities, in ensuring the health and repatriation of wildlife (Trevelline et al. 2019, Mueller et al. 2020). Guided by insights from our colleagues (e.g. Trevelline et al. 2019, Poyet and Groussin 2020, Ishaq et al. 2021, Rappuoli et al. 2023, Gilbert, 2025), we note a growing consensus on the importance of protecting key microbial communities, which are essential for environmental and host health.

Our work on the integral nature of microbiomes to host and environmental health, and the need for policy to promote best practices in microbiome research and industries, has led to the development of the concept of microbiome stewardship to promote consideration of the role of microbial communities in public and planetary health, agriculture and food systems, and ecosystem conservation and maintenance (Choudoir et al. 2025, O’Doherty et al. 2026). We define microbiome stewardship as “a set of insights, responsibilities, and practices aimed at maintaining microbial biodiversity and microbiome functioning across microbial habitats, which, in turn, supports the health and well-being of humans, animals, plants, and ecosystems” and we develop a more detailed framework for the implementation of microbiome stewardship elsewhere (Choudoir et al. 2025, O’Doherty et al. 2026). Our main premise is that there is a need to attend to conservation and stewardship of microbiota based upon the reliance that higher forms of life have on fundamental biochemical processing which microorganisms provide, and based on observations that human activities are disrupting, and even extinguishing, entire microbial communities. We recognize that there are also moral arguments to support protections of microbiota (Bossert and Höll 2025). Here, we do not take a position on the moral status of microorganisms or a responsibility to protect any life; instead, we focus on scientific arguments for their importance in sustaining life on our planet and achieving social goals (e.g. food security, population health).

In presenting our work to diverse audiences, we have found much agreement about the need to protect microbial communities. However, we have also encountered some disagreement: (i) microbiota are so numerous, diverse, and metabolically flexible that it is impossible for any specific microorganism to go extinct, and that even if one did, another would fill the niche; (ii) ecological functions are more important than the microbial lineage; (iii) the protection of environmental microbiota is actually a protection of a geographic location or ecosystem; and (iv) even if we need to protect microorganisms, it is not practically feasible to do so. The need, strategies, and practicality of stewarding one microorganism versus a community, or a specialist versus a generalist, is highly contextual and requires tailored implementation. In this paper, we outline those key points of disagreement, and offer a detailed rebuttal based on a review of studies across diverse hosts and environments, which demonstrate the need to protect and preserve microbial relationships, communities, and ecologies that are essential for overall health of specific organisms or ecosystems that are vulnerable to human activities. Highlighting a diverse range of vulnerable microbial relationships illustrates the need to employ a framework of stewardship towards microbiota that would promote ecosystem or host health. Our purpose is to definitively support the argument that there are microbial communities that are in need of protection in the face of human activities and natural factors. We also note that the practicality of stewarding microbiota is dependent on circumstances, ecology, and the goals of the intervening parties; thus, we outline examples of implemented or possible solutions at different ecological scales, as well as constraints or considerations.

Host specificity negates the argument of niche plasticity

Hosts require specific microbiota

Symbiosis or mutualism takes many forms in host-associated microbiomes. Obligate symbiosis, common in insects (Ferrari and Vavre 2011) or nematodes (Martinson et al. 2020), represents the clearest example of strain-specific microbiota which could be extirpated, and is a salient counter argument to the protestation that microorganisms are too flexible and ubiquitous to go extinct. While many microbiota are phenotypically plastic to some degree, host microbiota that have adapted over millennia often eschew genes for essential bioc hemical pathways which are performed by the host, and generally become adapted to the environmental conditions created by host anatomy and physiology, thus reducing their ability to survive in a new host species or ex vivo (Moran and Plague 2004). Further, removing the microbial symbiont can reduce host fitness (Fig. 1). Buchnera aphidicola is an obligate endosymbiotic bacterium, which must reside within aphids and provide essential nutrients and vitamins that the host cannot produce itself (family Aphidae; phylum Arthropoda; Heidari Latibari et al. 2025). When antibiotics are used to remove this bacterial endosymbiont from aphids as a means to test the insect’s reliance on their microbiota, the worst outcomes were observed in desert-dwelling aphids, which had no other facultative symbionts upon which to rely once B. aphidicola was removed (Heidari Latibari et al. 2025).

Preserving the functions of microbial communities in a host is not as simple as retaining genetic diversity or microbial products: there is a host specificity for the members of a microbial community that prevents full engraftment of a new microbiome, including anatomical, physiological, or immune-mediated hurdles. Cross-mammalian-species transfaunation experiments often fail to achieve engraftment of the new microbiome because of the host specificity, in particular, for enteric protozoa (Cairns 1953, Santos et al. 2021). Bacterial community transplants do not last forever even when the donor’s diet is fed to the recipient host to continue nurturing the community (Ruiz et al. 2024), and humanized lab mice do not retain the whole transplanted human microbiome (Zhou et al. 2019). Further, specific microbiota are often required by a host and cannot be interchanged without some loss of benefit to overall health. Mouse-associated microbiota are required to fully mature murine immune systems: conventionalization with human-associated microbiota does not supplant this function for mice (Chung et al. 2012). Microbiome science has long recognized the critical nature of host–microbial relationships in shaping host microbial communities, such as the effect of eons of co-evolution, termed phylosymbiosis (Kohl 2020, Lim and Bordenstein 2020) as well as the loss of these “Old Friends” on host health (Rook 2022). To save these host-reliant microbiota requires preservation of the host, which is a conservation challenge for many macrobiota. This highlights the need for preserving specific microorganisms not by creating breeding and repatriation programs for microbiota but by stewarding the circumstances by which they may be recruited by their host.

Moreover, niche plasticity is not as common as it is assumed to be, for multiple microorganisms may metabolize the same compound but they do not necessarily produce the same byproducts or, if they do, with the same efficiency (Quinn-Bohmann et al. 2024). One example is the metabolism of plant secondary compounds called glucosinolates found in the Brassica genus, which can be accomplished by some bacteria in the mammalian gut, in fermented cruciferous vegetables, or in compost. Depending on which bacteria are present and the pH of the local environment, bacteria will convert these glucosinolates with varying efficiency (Cheng et al. 2004, Yagishita et al. 2019), as well as into useful antiinflammatory isothiocyanates or their inactive nitrile versions (Cheng et al. 2004).

Numerous arthropods and mammals have been demonstrated to rely on gut microbiota to detoxify plant secondary compounds (Fig. 1), and while these microbial species might not be obligate symbionts, they represent critical partnerships as the ability to detoxify individual compounds can be niche. The bacterium Eubacterium rangiferina detoxifies usnic acid in lichen in the diet of reindeer (Rangifer tarandus, phylum Chordata) during rumen fermentation (Sundset et al. 2008), which is not found in other ruminants, and if reindeer lack this bacterium they cannot eat this diet. Specific strains of detoxifying bacteria are found only in subpopulations of woodrats (genus Neotoma, phylum Chordata), which consume geographic/population-specific toxic plant secondary compounds, such as creosote (Kohl and Dearing 2012). Koalas (Phascolarctos cinereus, phylum Chordata) are highly selective about consuming eucalyptus species to avoid toxins, and populations which are naive to certain eucalyptus will avoid toxic species even when starving. Gut bacteria allow koalas to detoxify eucalyptol and other compounds (Fig. 1): fecal microbial transplant between koalas induced animals to consume the toxic species of eucalyptus they had previously avoided, and in a dose-dependent manner (Blyton et al. 2019). This detoxifying microbial consortium is important enough to transfer from the maternal gut to immature joeys via a specific fecal excretion called pap (Blyton et al. 2022). While several bacteria of interest have been identified in the koala gut, one specific toxin-degrader has only been identified in the koala gut since its description in 1995, Lonepinella koalarum (Osawa et al. 1995), and represents unique ecosystem-specificity for a mammalian symbiont. The loss of this bacterium is implicated in worse survival in koalas following antibiotics to treat near-population-wide infections of chlamydia (Dahlhausen et al. 2018). Thus, this bacteria could be lost through the theoretical extinction of the animal host or through antibiotic use, but could be stewarded through protection of the environment and natural diets, as well as through biobanking and probiotics for captive-bred or relocated hosts.

While it might be considered that stewarding gut microbiota can largely be accomplished by curating the diet or nutrient substrate for a host, e.g. by simply replicating a “natural” diet, this may not be adequate, particularly for human gut microbiomes. For example, recreating a hunter-gatherer or paleolithic type diet in industrialized settings is unlikely to replicate the associated microbiome of the naturally sourced food substrates themselves, and it is unclear that how such food microbiomes may interact with or supplement the host’s autochthonous gut microbiota. This is especially true given that most food available in industrialized settings is deliberately depleted of microorganisms through pasteurization and/or amended with microbiome-damaging xenobiotics (Whelan et al. 2024) to extend shelf-life or reduce the likelihood of food-borne infection. It is also interesting to note that industrialization has rapidly broadened the range of dietary substrates available for consumption in comparison to paleolithic diets (Walter and Ley 2011, Pontzer and Wood 2021). Thus, this situation may have inadvertently reduced substrate availability over time for highly substrate-specialized host-associated microbial species, leading to their extirpation or extinction from the industrialized gut (e.g. if a substrate a specialist microbe is reliant upon is not consumed by the host often enough for microbial population sustainability). Thus, contrary to the idea that food diversity supports microbiome diversity, stewardship may require a more nuanced approach to dietary oversight.

Critical host microbiota linked to reproduction and are vertically transmitted

Reproductive fitness research has uncovered reliance on host microbial communities, although this is still under investigation to understand critical microbiota or function, reviewed elsewhere (Williams et al. 2019a, Comizzoli et al. 2021). For example, the same diet can affect gut microbiota differently, causing animal-species-specific reductions in the fertility of rhinoceros (genus Ceratotherium, phylum Chordata) related to gut microbiome processing of phytoestrogens (Williams et al. 2019b). Hydra viridis, small freshwater animals of the phylum Cnidaria, cannot bud and reproduce when they are raised as gnotobiotic (sterile) until bacteria are introduced in water or via feed (Rahat and Dimentman 1982). Many species of animals and plants exhibit vertical transmission of specific strains of obligate or facultative symbionts, or whole communities (Ballinger et al. 2018, Nyholm 2020, Fagan et al. 2024), and interrupting this cycle can reduce offspring fitness (Jašarević et al. 2017, Bunker et al. 2021) leading to extirpation of the symbiont (Sonnenburg et al. 2016, Jašarević et al. 2017). For example, cloacal bacteria, which are transferred onto the cuticle of eggs during laying protects lizard eggs (Fig. 1; order Squamata, phylum Chordata) from colonization by soil fungi and pathogens, improves hatchling survival, and results in larger offspring (Bunker et al. 2021).

Many host-associated microbiota are only vertically or horizontally transmitted as they have lost capacity for environmental survival over time (Moran and Plague 2004); thus, host microbiomes cannot be recapitulated from environmental exposure alone (Hascoët et al. 2011, Vecchi et al. 2018). Importantly, this means that host-associated strains are not interchangeable with non-host-associated strains (Vecchi et al. 2018, Ma et al. 2023, Chen-Liaw et al. 2024). Transmission occurs primarily from maternal-only sources, which is hypothesized to reduce the increased risk of pathogen transfer that could occur from two-parent vertical transmission (e.g. male lactation; Fagan et al. 2024), further limiting sources for these microorganisms. Problematically, a disruption to the gut microbiome, such as by the absence of fiber in an omnivorous mammalian diet (Sonnenburg et al. 2016), or the loss of microbiota which follows stress during pregnancy (Jašarević et al. 2015), can manifest as altered microbial communities with less genetic and phenotypic diversity in parents as well as offspring. Sustained microbiome deprivation compounds over generations, and cannot easily be remedied in offspring without sustained intervention to recruit new microbiota and their functional contributions (Sonnenburg et al. 2016). Stewardship in this scenario is not as simple as biobanking a symbiont and providing a probiotic; it requires long-term interventions such as ensuring plenty of parent-offspring contact, protection of nests and habitats, as well as fostering microbial communities in maternal sources.

Hosts’ selection of specific environmental microbiota

The adage “everything is everywhere” used to describe one microbial theory of dispersion does not apply to all host microbiota, which are obtained from the environment. Host-specific selective pressures and immune reactions to environmentally sourced microbiota determine their ability to engraft in new hosts (Beck et al. 2022), which is generally illustrated by the difficulty in achieving first-generation (food-sourced) probiotic engraftment into the gut. Hosts may create selective conditions to screen out unwanted strains: marine invertebrates are known to expel microbial communities from specialized tissues daily only to rescreen and select specific microbiota to re-colonize (McFall-Ngai and Ruby 1991, Chung et al. 2024), and the ability to select these symbionts is jeopardized by environmental pollution (Chung et al. 2024). Similarly, localized Helicobacter pylori strains can be commensal in people living within a geographic location for many generations. However, human migration fosters the trading of gut bacteria and the introduction of new strains between populations, which led to opportunism by these bacterial strains in certain populations, resulting in infections (Muñoz-Ramirez et al. 2021). The well-studied chytrid disease of amphibians (class Amphibia, phylum Chordata) caused by the fungal pathogen Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis can be treated or prevented via unique metabolites produced by bacteria living on amphibian skin, Janthinobacterium lividum and Pseudomonas fluorescens (Woodhams et al. 2016). Conservation of these specific microbiota requires dual strategies to conserve both the amphibian host and the environment: J. lividum colonizes new hosts more quickly and stably when it first passes through the environment instead of passing directly from host to host (Rebollar et al. 2016), precluding the use of this bacteria as a stable topical probiotics for hosts. Collectively, the examples provided thus far demonstrate that microbial conservation and intervention is highly nuanced and cannot be implemented using the same strategies as those for macrobiota, and negates the blanket statement that microbiota are too flexible and ubiquitous to be stewarded.

Ecology functions are inherent to microbial lineage

While host systems may require specific taxa or strains, microbial interventions that seek to achieve a specific goal, such as soil conservation or gut health, may be successful by nurturing the desired ecological function of a community rather than focusing on certain microbial lineages. We argue that microbial communities are not infinitely iterative: function is tied to microbial lineage, and co-habitation in a community long term is contingent upon the compatibility of microbial lineages (Stehr et al. 2009). Microbiota form complex relationships with each other, which can disintegrate under stressful environments. For example, the bacterium Alistipes onderdonkii is correlated with the ability to eliminate higher concentrations of mercury in mammals, yet it cannot improve elimination alone without the complex biochemistry afforded by a rich gut community (Coe et al. 2023). The fungal mutualist, Leucoagaricus gongylophorus, is cultivated by leaf-cutter ants (genus Atta, phylum Arthropoda) in fungal gardens where they deposit plant material for degradation, after which they consume the fungus and deteriorated detritus. L. gongylphorus is susceptible to most plant secondary compounds, and relies on the bacterial community sourced from the ant gut and soil to detoxify these plant compounds (Francoeur et al. 2020). Preserving these ecological functions relies on preserving some specific lineages. However, conserving all parties in these microbial partnerships using culture repositories can be difficult if neither partner nor the single microbe of interest can survive solitary culturing; though genome sequencing of both partners and identification of essential biochemical pathways found in one but not the other can elucidate the key to keeping each alive in isolation. Critically, recapitulating the conditions, which fosters all parties in a microbial partnership requires host- or ecosystem-level conservation, and therein lies the fundamental difficulty of preserving environmental microbiota when the environment is degrading and cannot be remedied.

Rare ecosystems exemplify highly specialized microbial communities in which specific members or the assembly itself might not exist in other locations and therefore, their survival is contingent on the reliability of conditions there. For example, the Cuatro Ciénegas basin in Coahuila, Mexico is a harsh and unique environment in which microbial and other diversity is reliant on periodic influxes of water, after which large microbial mats form and serve as the basis for biological life in the area (Souza et al. 2006, 2018). This fragile landscape is threatened by human activities, diversion of local waterways, which prevents the rehydration of the ecosystem and revival of the microbial mats, leaving the ecosystem non-functional (Rodriguez-Cruz et al. 2025). The specialized microbial communities and their capabilities, which can survive here, have not been found anywhere else on the planet, and could not be introduced into a new environment without very specific conditions.

Another example involves the genus Prochlorococcus, one of the most abundant photosynthetic cyanobacteria on the planet, playing a crucial role in the marine carbon cycle (Cai et al. 2024). It is typically found in surface waters of the tropics and subtropics living symbiotically with bacteria in the genus Alteromonas, which helps to detoxify reactive oxidative species (ROS) such as hydrogen peroxide that is an ubiquitous oxidant in surface water and causes harm to Prochlorococcus spp. as they lack catalase genes to detoxify ROS (Morris et al. 2011). Thus, a stable relationship between Alteromonas and Prochlorococcus is critical to maintaining marine biogeochemical cycles (Fig. 2); however, rising carbon dioxide levels, ocean stratification, and increasing surface water temperatures result in loss of critical functions required to maintain this partnership (Cai et al. 2024). There was an ∼50% decrease of gene expression associated with photorespiration, carbon fixation, and oxidative stress response in Prochlorococcus due to the increase in diffusive supply of carbon dioxide (Hennon et al. 2017). Furthermore, catalase genes in Alteromonas had significantly lower expression resulting in a six-fold decrease in the degradation rate of hydrogen peroxide; thus, increasing oxidative stress and higher mortality rates to Prochlorococcus under elevated CO2 levels (Hennon et al. 2017). Fundamental changes in the microbial partnership of Prochlorococcus and Alteromonas demonstrate shifts in Prochlorococcus functional capacity, which could, in turn, alter ecosystem dynamics critical for the base of the marine food web. In this example, conserving the ecosystem alone would not necessarily steward these microorganisms, while stewardship of this microbial partnership would, in of itself, help to protect this environment.

Microbial function in hosts is connected to host ecosystems

Animals and plants subjected to anthropogenic forces, including but not limited to domestication and captivity, undergo a drastic change from their lifestyle, which alters their exposure to environmental microbiota and relationships with their host-associated microbiota. Extensively manipulated diets, degraded natural environments, relocation to built environments, increased exposure to humans and urban populations of animals, and more, can strongly influence host–microbe relations removing the supportive functions the microbial community was providing to their host ecosystem, leading to adverse health consequences. For wild animals, this can manifest as colitis, shortened intestines, loss of reproductive fitness, susceptibility to disease, or malnutrition (e.g. Liukkonen-Anttila et al. 2000, Clayton et al. 2016, McKenzie et al. 2017, Williams et al. 2019a, Bornbusch et al. 2022). In most studies, captive animal populations demonstrate differing gut microbial composition compared to their wildlife counterparts. Captivity generally decreases microbial diversity due to the complexity of accurately replicating diets found in natural ecosystems (Fig. 2). Captive carnivores are a prime example as their diets mostly consist of domesticated animals, homogenized animals, and meat products, which are too lean contributing to losses in microbial diversity; as a result, there are increased rates of captivity-gastrointestinal diseases (Depauw et al. 2013, McKenzie et al. 2017, Wasimuddin et al. 2017). Captive nonhuman primates, such as doucs (genus Pygathrix, phylum Chordata), had a marked reduction in gut bacterial alpha diversity and more similar beta diversity to humans (Clayton et al. 2016). Similar to humans, doucs exhibited a gradient in diversity related to lifestyles: captive doucs at the bottom with the lowest, semi-wild doucs had intermediate, and wild doucs had the most bacterial diversity (Clayton et al. 2018). In addition, captivity is associated with trade-offs in microbial metabolic pathways capacities related to the exposure to antibiotics or xenobiotics, and altered balance of dietary nutrients, such as increased lipid metabolism in captive doucs compared to increased amino acids metabolism in the wild doucs (Clayton et al. 2018).

For wild animals, maintaining a set of ecological functions in their microbiomes is often tied to maintaining microbial lineages, and horizontal or environmental transmission of microbiota may be enough to restore host community and functionality. Despite these challenges, microbial community-based interventions have demonstrated their use in conservation already. Giving rescued lemurs (superfamily Lemuroidea, phylum Chordata) access to soil and the microbiota therein increased their gut bacterial diversity while in captivity, made it more similar to that of wild lemurs, and aided in maintaining health during captivity (Bornbusch et al. 2022). Similarly, a mixture of bacteria from healthy bat wings has shown potential as a treatment to rescue North American bats (order Chiroptera, phylum Chordata) from the fungal pathogen, which causes white nose syndrome (Wildlife Conservation Society 2019). Moreover, the release of captive Tasmanian devils (Sarcophilus harrisii, phylum Chordata) resulted in a reversal of the gut microbial diversity loss they had experienced in brief captivity during conservation-relocation events, and that captive-bred devils, which were released into the wild also normalized to wild gut microbiomes (Chong et al. 2019).

These health changes observed in wild animals with altered microbial function are even observed in livestock animals in industrialized production systems (i.e. factory farms) (Prabhu et al. 2020), and may be compounded by other stresses; for instance, corticosteroids generated from thermal or crowding stress in farmed fish reduce gut diversity and increase gill infections (Couch et al. 2023). In part, this is also due to the reduced exposure to environmental microbiota. In closed systems, host-microbial communities do not self-sustain indefinitely; e.g. lab mouse colonies experience microbial community drift when they have no incoming microbial exposures (Hildebrand et al. 2013), humans in research stations in Antarctica using recycled water systems experience increased pathogen load in the water over time (Barker et al. 2013), and wild-caught lobsters (Homarus americanus, phylum Arthropoda) maintained in filtered artificial seawater in tanks lose significant microbial diversity on their shells compared to when they were exposed to oceanic microbial diversity (Ishaq et al. 2023).

For domesticated agricultural plants, this manifests as an altered capacity to recruit soil microbiota to their roots (Soldan et al. 2021, Soldan et al. 2024), and indoor plants lose their tissue and soil-associated microbial diversity after just 6 months indoors if they lack regular inputs of water and soil laden with new microbiota (Mahnert et al. 2015). In agricultural animals and plants, stewardship of microbiota would suggest a return to wild microbiomes, yet the domestication of plants and animals, as well as the difference in lifestyle and environmental conditions, has led to a divergence of their microbiome (Prabhu et al. 2020, Reese et al. 2021), which would preclude recapitulation of the wild microbiome in the domesticated species. Further, the popularity and success of using low-dose antibiotics in livestock to increase weight gain (now banned in many countries) demonstrates that stewarding microbiomes can be in conflict with vested interests or profit motives of particular stakeholders. While this is an important challenge to recognize in realizing microbiome stewardship goals, it is critical to emphasize that relatively short-term goals of livestock growth are likely superseded by long-term consideration of sustainability and health of both human and livestock populations.

Protection of ecosystems supports global health

A potential argument against microbiome stewardship is that it is redundant because protection of environmental microbiota is actually a protection of a location or ecosystem and no additional efforts need to be taken. This argument is not inherently incorrect. If environmental microbiomes are the basis for biogeochemistry and plenary health, as well as act as sources for animal, human, and plant microbiomes, then logic dictates that these should be the focus of stewardship, conservation, and remediation efforts to solve as many microbial-mediated problems as possible (Fig. 2). Biological hotspots represent unique locations, which generate critical microbial communities on a broader scale, and which would be ideal for this type of protection. For example, marine environments, which act as breeding grounds for macrobiota, are also foundational for microbiota—not only is there a rich chemical diversity to support microbial life in these locations, but these locations also act as microbial sources for sinks in other ecosystems, especially marine hosts, which assemble from vertical or horizontal transmission from compatriots as well as from environmental microbiota (Boscaro et al. 2022, Busch et al. 2022). In these locations, stewardship would manifest as environmental protection and conservation of natural resources, which has successfully been employed to preserve microbiological diversity (Sathesh 2020).

However, we argue that additional efforts would support microbial conservation in degraded environments better than simply leaving the environment to manage itself, especially in light of ever-increasing impacts of anthropogenic activities. Environmental microbiota sustain all other life on Earth via chemical fixation and cycling, and especially increasing the bioavailability of elements (Gilbert and Neufeld 2014). Altering those fundamental processes can halt the growth or well-being of higher forms of life. For example, microbiota make soil what it is: filamentous microorganisms use hyphae networks to build clay particles, and other microorganisms produce exopolysaccharides that make clay particles compact, which yields aggregated soil (Gupta et al. 2017). Degradation of soil ecosystems disrupts the microbial partnership needed for bacterial nitrogen cycling, thus removing the ability from that location and resulting in accrual of nitrogen products to the point of toxicity, as well as greenhouse gas emissions (Carr et al. 2025). Remediation of chemical pollutants (Liao et al. 2019), as well as microbial inoculation (Bo et al. 2025), is demonstrated to improve soil properties and productivity. Thus, stewardship of degraded environments needs to involve pollutant remediation as well as biological restoration activities, some of which would center on restoring biological diversity within the constraints of a heavily urbanized environment (Mills et al. 2017) rather than a complete removal of human activities.

Degradation of ecosystems impacts microbial communities

Chemical and plastic pollution is globally pervasive, and will require tailored chemical and microbial remediation efforts, including those that help prevent the migration of chemical pollutant sources. Chemical pesticides are extensively employed to improve agricultural product quality and enhance crop yields; however, these pesticides are not limited to crops as they enter soil, water, insects, other animals and even humans, and cause off-target effects (Staley et al. 2015, Muturi et al. 2017). For example, honey bees (Apis mellifera, phylum Arthropoda) are often exposed to agrochemicals, especially herbicides and fungicides, used in agriculture through their foraging activities, which then get delivered back to the hive and can accumulate in food stores, thus exposing larvae and young bees. Glyphosate, the most used herbicide globally, can disrupt the core bee microbiota causing numerous changes such as significant decreases in the bacterial symbiont Snodgrasella alvi which is known to play pivotal roles in immune responses and clearing pathogenic bacteria from the host (Horak et al. 2020, Motta and Moran 2024). Furthermore, exposure to herbicides and fungicides reduces expression of host antimicrobial peptides, such as defensin, weakening the honey bee immune system, which leads to increased susceptibility to pathogenic bacteria such as Serratia marcescens or parasites. In addition, exposure to glyphosate promotes deformed wing virus replication and impacts bee physiology affecting learning, memory, and antioxidant and detoxification systems (Motta and Moran 2024).

Similarly, heavy metal contamination from unregulated mining and industrial solid and liquid waste has deteriorated host microbial communities and health (e.g. Gillingham et al. 2021), as well as collapsed whole ecosystems due to deterioration of microbial, insect, plant, animal, and human health, and creating conditions for infectious disease epidemics (Eagles-Smith et al. 2018). Lead is one of the most prominent toxic heavy metals to contaminate ecosystems due to its long-lasting nature and bio-and environmental accumulation. Toxic levels of lead impair microbial communities, decreasing microbial growth rates and cell size, inhibiting gene expression for stress response, and inhibiting gene expression and enzyme activity involved in microbial metabolisms (e.g. dehydrogenases, hydrolases) that are crucial for nutrient uptake and energy production (Abba et al. 2024). Furthermore, biomagnification of lead through trophic levels increases its destructive effects, demonstrated through contamination beginning in soil sites and moving to earthworms, songbirds, and large birds (Beyer et al. 2013, Sato et al. 2022). Similarly, air pollution has been demonstrated to exacerbate or contribute to the development of lung infections (McDuffie et al. 2021), and the ingestion of pollutants has been demonstrated to induce inflammation, alter microbial activity and survival, and lead to chronic inflammatory states and immune targeting of microbial commensals (Salim et al. 2014, Bailey et al. 2022).

Microplastics adhere to pollutants in the environment and bring them into the host, which causes chemical damage to host and microorganisms, physical damage to host tissues or forms blockages in the gastrointestinal tract that then affects microbiota, and forms attachment points for microbial biofilms. The accumulation of microplastics in animal hosts causes a loss of commensals (Adamovsky et al. 2021, Fackelmann et al. 2023). Microplastics impair amphibian health via decreases in microbial functional redundancy—similar biochemical functions performed by multiple taxa—specifically, those associated with glycan biosynthesis, chemotaxis, and flagella production (Zhang et al. 2023). Likewise, microplastic pollutants in European seabass (Dicentrarchus labrax, phylum Chordata) decreased lactic acid bacteria which are key contributors of short-chain fatty acids, and increased taxa which cause intestinal inflammation or infections (Montero et al. 2022). Lastly, immune system function can be compromised, leaving the host susceptible to infection and disease demonstrated through an increase in hemocytes (immune cells) in blue mussels (Mytilus edulis, phylum Mollusca), reactive lysosomes in the gill and digestive gland, and a loss of cell differentiation and specialization in digestive cells (Ferguson et al. 2022).

In addition to pollution, industrialization has caused deforestation on land and sea, the release of greenhouse gases, warming of many locations globally, and acidified oceans, all of which has negatively affected microbial communities in hosts and environments in myriad ways. Collectively, climate change is an existential threat to the survival of all levels of life by disrupting biogeochemistry, food webs, and host-microbial interactions (Choudoir and Eggleston 2022, Williams et al. 2023, Peixoto et al. 2025). For example, altering temperature and precipitation alters plant growth and reduces the plant root exudates which recruit a functional soil diversity as stressed plants can no longer afford to collaborate with soil microbiota for their minerals. Climate change leads to increased intensity of wildfires and storms that disrupt environmental microbiomes and challenge hosts with air pollution or intense precipitation. Moreover, drought (Buttimer et al. 2024), or rising temperatures and environmental degradation reduces the microbial diversity of amphibian skin and makes them susceptible to fungal (Greenspan et al. 2017) or viral (Harrison et al. 2019) infections.

Climate change has already destroyed many coral reefs, which are one of the most biodiverse ecosystems forming a series of mutualistic relationships, with many bacteria, algae, fungi, and archaea increasing corals’ resilience to anthropogenic effects and stressors such as warming temperatures (Robbins 2022). The bacteria Endozoicomonas are enriched in pathways for nutrient transport processes that aid with moving essential products—coral cannot synthesize their own amino acids and B-vitamins—to the coral for maintaining homeostasis, coral growth, and strengthening its immune responses (Neave et al. 2017, Robbins et al. 2019). One of the best-known essential relationships on the coral reef is between stony coral species (order Scleractinia, phylum Cnidaria) and photosynthetic algae, Symbiodinaceae zooxanthellae, which share critical metabolites. However, an important component to this relationship are bacterial associates that act as metabolic mediators, by increasing bioavailability or providing inorganic carbon for calcification (Sweet et al. 2021), to generate a successful coral- Symbiodinaceae-bacterial symbiosis (van Oppen and Medina 2020). Furthermore, certain coral-associated bacteria contain nitrogen (nifH and nirK) cycling genes which are critical for maintaining nitrogen-poor environments to promote coral growth (Sweet et al. 2021). Many bacterial symbionts such as Vibrio and Roseobacter contain sulphur cycling genes which are important for maintaining the antioxidant system, coral skeleton formation, and even regulating sea temperature through cloud formations. Together, these symbiotic relationships are used to promote and maintain host health and its coral- Symbiodinaceae–bacterial relationship while also preventing overgrowth of pathogens and other organisms such as algae. Environmental stressors (e.g. warming ocean temperatures, pH fluctuations, altered salinity and oxygen levels) can quickly disintegrate these symbiotic relationships (Voolstra et al. 2024). A loss of symbionts results in a loss of food sources/products and a weakened immune system leaving the coral susceptible to disease and death. For example, ocean acidification causes a reduction in carbonate ions, decreasing the rate at which calcareous skeletons are deposited on the corals; thus, coral growth and reproduction is stunted (Tan et al. 2023). Likewise, acidifying seawater decreases microbial diversity and rapidly shifts microbial communities. As members of the bacteria Endosoicomonadaceae family are lost through environmental stress, the relative abundance of Vibrionaceae and Rhodobacteraceae families increase and sequencing efforts have demonstrated that genomes from these families are closely related to those found in diseased corals, such as white syndrome and white plague disease (Meron et al. 2011). Further to this, coral hosts become more susceptible to infection and disease as elevated temperatures increase the virulence mechanisms of several coral pathogens such as the bacteria Vibrio coralliilyticus and can cause symbionts to potentially turn pathogenic—such as the green algae symbiont, Ostreobium (Kimes et al. 2012, Fordyce et al. 2021) .

Similarly, coastal wetlands are large, long-term, sustainable ecosystems for carbon fixation, organic matter decomposition, and maintaining biodiversity at the interface of land and sea (Liu et al. 2024). The intensification of human population density and land use on coastal and inland waterways, combined with warming coastal waters and lack of reinvestment into clean water infrastructure or poverty reduction, has compounded to fuel epidemics of water-borne illness. The genus Vibrio comprises marine bacteria which can form marine-species-specific symbioses in which commensal Vibrio assists the hosts in osmotic balance to prevent salt toxicity in the host. However, in terrestrial animals, this “assistance” causes an osmotic imbalance in the gut resulting in diarrhea and dehydration, leading to otherwise preventable deaths from lack of sufficient palliative care to patients. Vibrio cholerae epidemics can be tracked with weather data that predicts coastal warming and storm events, coupled with data on poverty or geopolitical events which would preclude medical care (Usmani et al. 2023). While most of the effects of climate change cannot be easily remedied, the combination of weather tracking and sociopolitical analysis is a clear example of using existing tools towards harm reduction, even if prevention cannot be achieved.

Environmental remediation and preservation

Remediation of the environment can be a daunting task, especially in heavily urbanized environments in which the idea of reforestation is dismissed, although adding indoor plants (Soininen et al. 2022, Soininen et al. 2022), redistributing outdoor green space into low-income areas (Jones et al. 2009, Rossen and Pollack 2012, Mears et al. 2019, Izcue et al. 2022), or increasing the biodiversity of existing greenspaces (Baruch et al. 2020, Robinson et al. 2020) are minor interventions with demonstrable benefits. Numerous studies cite the positive impact that exposure to environmental microbiomes can have on health during immersive daycares or vacations that foster skin-to-environmental-microbiome contact (Ruggles et al. 2018, Roslund et al. 2020, Scicchitano et al. 2024), short but intense exposure to soil and plant microbiomes via gardening (Mhuireach et al. 2023), and even low-level exposure using indoor plants (Soininen et al. 2022, Mhuireach et al. 2022). In addition to the general benefits of increased exposure to diverse microbiota and a short-term diversification of host microbiota (Grönroos et al. 2019), exposure to specific bacteria—or dust—from soil lowers stress in mice (Reber et al. 2016, Liddicoat et al. 2020), and increasing “greenwall” exposure in offices improved overall immune responses (Soininen et al. 2022). This effect was shown in greater detail in mouse studies, in which the immune system of gnotobiotic mice would not fully mature without conventionalization with diverse microbiota (Chen et al. 2025), and this was dependent on the succession from a diversified microbiome heavily influenced by environmental exposures to a mature and stabilized microbiome curated by host selection (Lubin et al. 2023). The impact of the microbiota manifested as increased and diversified immunoglobulins (IgG and IgM), increased B cell circulation, and overall increased tolerance for microbiota (Lubin et al. 2023, Chen et al. 2025), all of which are generalizable and provide systemic benefit.

Targeted interventions have been investigated using probiotics (West et al. 2019, Lorimer 2020, Mueller et al. 2020)—either in individuals, in built environments, or in natural environments—to replace specific lost species (Delgadillo-Ordoñez et al. 2024), combat pathogens which had caused microbial community collapse (Underwood 2018), or generally increase biodiversity and restore microbial community functionality (Calderón et al. 2017, Shade 2023, Pao et al. 2025). Probiotics have been used with variable success: restoration probiotics may fail to establish themselves in an already degraded ecosystem, except for short-term probiotics chosen for their ability to process specific pollutants are very successful until those pollutants are no longer present, and in one serendipitous case, a restoration probiotic which was a naturally occurring endophyte that promoted decontamination of chemicals (Doty et al. 2017). Both of these strategies employ a retroactive approach: trying to fix what was broken. Fostering best practices for probiotics to allow for their deployment while avoiding the pitfall that conservation has faced with animal or plant reintroduction.

On the other hand, stewardship would take a more holistic and proactive approach to also include, for example, human land use changes and limitations on industrialization to reduce pollution (Bello et al. 2018, Rook 2022). For instance, reducing the agricultural use of high-impact tillage which can erode soil in dry ecosystems to preserve long-term biodiversity of soils (Ishaq et al. 2020, Birnbaum et al. 2024), avoiding monocultured agriculture to diversify microbial communities (Ishaq et al. 2020), improving stormwater management infrastructure and sewage processing (Boehm et al. 2018) to reduce contamination of public water sources, and putting limits on industrial air pollution to prevent smog or acid rain. Stewardship will need to involve long-term changes to land use which maintain biological diversity and prevent environmental collapse (e.g. Robinson and Jorgensen 2020, Robinson et al. 2021). Many historic policy changes occurred in response to specific threats from pollution. The US instituted the Clean Air (42 USC ch 85, 1997) and Clean Water Acts (33 USC §1251, 1972). The United Nations Resolutions include the Right to Clean Water (United Nations 2010), and Right to Clean Environment (United Nations, 2019, The Human Right to a Clean et al. 2022), with which dozens of countries have ostensibly agreed to comply. More recently, revisions to constitutions and reorganizations of policy, such as the revision of the Chilean Constitution, have been suggested as opportunities to instill environmental justice as a means to support long-term equitization of health (Izcue et al. 2022), or to consider how structural barriers and inequality also impoverish the microbiome (Prescott et al. 2017, Harrison and Taren 2018, Rook 2022).

Practicality is a matter of perspective

One common argument against stewardship occurs when the assumption is made that all microbiota would be conserved, even microorganisms which cause mass mortality or morbidity such as the infectious agents carried by mosquitoes and other insect or arthropod vectors which are being systematically eliminated to improve health. One can debate the moral right of humanity to select microbiota, or any form of life, for extinction, and from a scientific basis even pathogens provide an ecosystem service; however, we would be hard-pressed to find anyone who mourns the passing of smallpox and it would not be possible or effective to conserve every microorganism. Thus, it is important to avoid broad and vague notions to “protect all microbiota” that are easily shown to be logically impossible. Conversely, the technical difficulty of tagging and surveilling microbiota should not disqualify them from being stewarded and protected by policy. Practicality is a matter of perspective: whether the cost of the effort was worth the return in outcomes, and often ascribing the value of “practicality” to a monumental effort is tied to technological advancement. For example, sequencing the human genome, studying microbiota during space travel, and treating cancer using CRISPR-edited bacteria were once all too expensive or logistically difficult to achieve until advancements in knowledge and technology made them a reality. The field of microbial ecology has made incredible strides in the last few decades, and it stands to reason that in another few, we could easily monitor certain microbiota.

We acknowledge that some practical considerations of stewardship have not been addressed here. It could be futile to try to track a microorganism to assess population and extinction risk in the way that macrobiota are tracked. We would argue that much of conservation now relies on environmental DNA signatures to track rare or cryptic macrobiota as other means of tracking are not practical. Moreover, conservation of microbiota would not follow the same guidelines as macrobiota, as extinction of microbiota would be rare. However, there are clear examples of microbial extirpation from hosts resulting in a loss of fiber fermentation (Sonnenburg and Sonnenburg 2014) or from environments causing the inhibition of dark carbon fixation and sequestration due to climate warming affecting the biodiversity of chemoautotrophs and the carbon fixation pathways (Liu et al. 2024). Stewarding microbiota to prevent extirpation, or to facilitate repatriation, can restore microbiomes (Chong et al. 2019, Bornbusch et al. 2022) but only if interventions are promptly provided after microbial extirpation (Sonnenburg and Sonnenburg 2014).

Further, stewarding microbial communities may involve fostering ecological dynamics rather than specific community members. There are other dynamics which act on microbial communities which may be important to preserving them yet may be counterintuitive. For example, in marine environments, phages (viruses) can reduce the biomass and diversity of a microbial community by half in just a day or two (Breitbart et al. 2018), but this viral predation keeps microbial communities dynamic and in constant flux so there can be no stagnation. Similarly, the ecological succession of microbial communities are critical to their functions, such as the assemblage of gut microbiota and immune development (Lubin et al. 2023), the succession in a swamp and the transition from greenhouse gas production to sequestration (Hu et al. 2020), and the succession of microbial communities on dying hydrothermal vents into a community which can remain in a stable state for thousands of years and act as sources for communities on new vents (Hou et al. 2020).

Conclusion

In conclusion, we observe that there is increasing recognition of the importance of microbiomes for host and ecosystem health. However, there have been some objections to calls for conservation or stewardship of microbiomes. Our aim has been to rebut these objections using evidence from published scientific studies. In particular:

To the objection that microbiota are so numerous, diverse, and metabolically flexible that it is impossible for any specific microorganism to go extinct, and that even if one did, another would fill the niche, we respond that it may or may not be possible for individual microorganisms to go extinct. However, there is strong empirical evidence across many domains of life, that microbial communities are essential to host organisms, larger ecosystems, and/or biogeochemical planetary processes. In addition, it is often not the metabolic potentials of individual microorganisms, but rather that of entire microbial ecologies that are relevant to consider. In short, while not all microbiota need to be protected, microbial communities that provide essential functions must be protected to avoid potentially catastrophic outcomes.

However, current efforts toward conservation and environmental stewardship tend to neglect attention to microbial communities. As we have demonstrated, microbial communities are not only essential parts of larger ecosystems, they are often foundational. In this paper, we hope to have provided a further pillar to existing calls for microbiome conservation and stewardship. However, we have here only selectively attended to how microbiome stewardship might be implemented in practice in different contexts. Next steps, therefore, will require developing principles for the implementation of microbiome stewardship, developing metrics for measuring and evaluating stewardship goals, and case studies across diverse domains such as biomedicine, agriculture, and natural environments.
