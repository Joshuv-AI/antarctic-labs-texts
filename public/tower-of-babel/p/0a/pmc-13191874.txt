Novel NDUFV1 variant in progressive cavitating leukodystrophy with microcephaly: a case report
Authors: Lu Xiaowei, Wang Hong, Li Hong, Wu Bo, Yu Xiaoli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProgressive cavitating leukoencephalopathy (PCL) is a rare mitochondrial neurodegenerative disorder primarily caused by mitochondrial respiratory chain complex Ⅰ deficiency, with NDUFV1 identified as a major pathogenic gene. Typical phenotypes associated with NDUFV1-related PCL include motor regression, dystonia, cognitive impairment, and limb weakness, while microcephaly is extremely rare. The c.749T > A (p.Val250Glu) variant in NDUFV1 has not been documented previously, and its clinical significance remains undetermined. This report describes a case of PCL associated with this novel variant and microcephaly, aiming to investigate the correlation between genotype and phenotype.Case presentationA 17-month-old male infant of Chinese ethnicity was admitted to the hospital for assessment of motor skill regression. Physical examination revealed a head circumference of 45.5 cm (below the 3rd percentile for age and sex), hypertonia, and positive pyramidal tract signs. Laboratory tests showed elevated blood lactate levels. Neuroimaging revealed widespread, symmetrical, patchy abnormal signals accompanied by cavitation in the bilateral cerebral white matter, extending to involve the corpus callosum, brainstem, cerebellum, and upper cervical spinal cord. Genetic testing identified compound heterozygous NDUFV1 variants: c.749T>A (p.Val250Glu, paternal, previously unreported) and c.365C>T (p.Pro122Leu, maternal). The analysis of mitochondrial respiratory chain enzyme activity in skin fibroblasts revealed a deficiency in complex I. The patient received oral cocktail therapy combined with rehabilitation training and was followed up for over two years, showing improvements in motor, language, and cognitive functions, normalization of blood lactate levels, and reduction of abnormal MRI signals post-treatment.ConclusionOur study describes a case of NDUFV1-associated PCL caused by a novel compound heterozygous variant. This finding expands both the mutational spectrum of NDUFV1 and the phenotypic spectrum of PCL. It thus contributes significantly to the understanding of genotype-phenotype correlations in this disorder. Importantly, it highlights the need for early recognition of clinical features such as developmental delay, motor regression, and microcephaly, followed by diagnostic and therapeutic steps including prompt genetic testing, timely diagnosis, and appropriate intervention, all essential to improve patient outcomes and slow disease progression.Keywords: Progressive cavitating leukoencephalopathy, NDUFV1, Mitochondrial complex I deficiency, Case report

Background

Progressive cavitating leukoencephalopathy (PCL) encompasses a group of rare mitochondrial-associated disorders characterized by vacuolar degeneration of the cerebral white matter and progressive neurodegeneration, primarily affecting the central nervous system in children. Clinically, affected children typically present with psychomotor developmental delays, progressive motor regression, dystonia, cognitive impairments, and limb weakness. To date, the clinical prognosis for pediatric PCL remains poor, as no effective curative treatments are currently available [1, 2]. Regarding its pathogenesis, PCL is strongly associated with dysfunction of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I. As a central component of the mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation machinery, complex I facilitates electron transfer and adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production; compromised complex I activity directly disrupts cellular energy metabolic homeostasis [3]. Given its high energy demands, cerebral white matter is particularly susceptible to such metabolic disturbances, which lead to pathological damage and subsequent clinical manifestations related to PCL [4].

The NDUFV1 gene is located on chromosome 11q13.1 and encodes NADH dehydrogenase (ubiquinone) flavoprotein 1, a crucial component of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I [5]. Dysfunction of NDUFV1 compromises the structural integrity and functional stability of complex I, thereby disrupting mitochondrial energy metabolism and leading to multisystem involvement, including cerebral white matter injury in pediatric patients [6]. Previous research has shown that variants in the NDUFV1 gene are primarily associated with a range of mitochondrial disorders, such as Leigh syndrome, mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, severe infantile lactic acidosis, and progressive optic atrophy [7–10]. Although a limited number of studies have linked NDUFV1 variants to pediatric PCL [6], relevant case reports remain scarce, and the genotype-phenotype correlation between these variants and the clinical manifestations of PCL in children has yet to be fully elucidated. Existing evidence suggests that the main clinical phenotypes of NDUFV1-associated PCL in pediatric patients include motor regression, dystonia, cognitive impairment, and ataxia; some cases may also present with abnormal eye movements and epileptic seizures [11]. However, microcephaly as a phenotypic feature of pediatric NDUFV1-associated PCL is extremely rarely reported in the literature, highlighting a significant gap in the current understanding of the phenotypic spectrum of this disorder in children.

Given the limited research on NDUFV1 variant-associated pediatric primary mitochondrial disease (PCL), we present a case of pediatric PCL caused by compound heterozygous NDUFV1 variants. The patient exhibited microcephaly, a rare phenotypic feature in this population. Notably, one of the variants, c.749T > A (p.Val250Glu), has not been previously documented in the literature. This case report broadens the variant spectrum of NDUFV1 and enhances our understanding of the phenotypic diversity associated with pediatric PCL. Furthermore, it offers novel theoretical and practical insights that may facilitate early and accurate clinical diagnosis, targeted genetic testing, and evidence-based prognostic assessments of pediatric PCL. This work also establishes a foundation for future in-depth investigations into the mechanisms linking NDUFV1 variants with clinical phenotypes of PCL in children.

Case presentation

A 17-month-old male infant was admitted to our hospital in February 2023 with a 2-month history of motor regression, complicated by psychomotor developmental delay since infancy. He demonstrated head control by three months, achieved the ability to roll over by six months, and attained independent sitting by nine months. At 10 months of age, he could stand with support, maintain independent standing for several seconds, and take two assisted steps; however, no further motor progression was observed until 15 months, after which motor regression occurred. This regression was characterized by unstable independent sitting, loss of the ability to stand independently, and unsteady supported standing. The infant was the first-born child (G1P1), delivered at full term via spontaneous vaginal delivery with a head circumference within the normal range at birth; no complications were recorded during the perinatal period. Both parents exhibited normal phenotypes and reported no history of consanguineous marriage. No family history of inherited metabolic disorders was reported.

Physical examination revealed a head circumference of 45.5 cm, which is below the 3rd percentile for the patient’s age and gender. Additional findings included hypertonia, hyperactive deep tendon reflexes, and positive pyramidal tract signs. Laboratory analysis indicated an elevated serum lactic acid concentration of 6.18 mmol/L (reference range: 0.5–2.2 mmol/L), while chromosomal karyotyping showed no abnormalities. Cranial CT demonstrated reduced white matter density, and cranial MRI (T2WI/FLAIR) revealed multiple hyperintense lesions in the bilateral cerebral white matter (periventricular, subcortical, and deep white matter), corpus callosum, brainstem, cerebellar hemispheres, and upper cervical spinal cord, with relative sparing of the basal ganglia. Cystic lesions were also noted in the corpus callosum, periventricular regions, and centrum semiovale. Additional imaging features included thinning of the corpus callosum, ventricular dilatation, and widened extracerebral spaces (Fig. 1). Given the clinical and radiological findings suggestive of leukoencephalopathy with vanishing white matter and mitochondrial disease, whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed.

WES was conducted on DNA extracted from peripheral blood samples obtained from the proband and both of his parents (Fig. 2). Compound heterozygous variants were identified in NDUFV1 (NM_007103.4): c.749T > A (p.Val250Glu) and c.365 C > T (p.Pro122Leu), with each variant inherited from one parent. According to the 2015 ACMG guidelines, the c.749T > A (p.Val250Glu) variant is a novel variant of uncertain significance (VUS), supported by criteria PM2, PM3, and PP3, and has not been previously reported. The c.365 C > T (p.Pro122Leu) variant was classified as likely pathogenic (LP) based on criteria PM3_Strong, PM2, and PP3; this variant is classified as likely pathogenic in ClinVar and documented as a disease-causing mutation in HGMD. The infant was diagnosed with progressive cavitating leukoencephalopathy (PCL) based on clinical manifestations, which included psychomotor developmental delay, motor regression, microcephaly, increased muscle tone, and positive pyramidal tract signs. Cranial MRI findings revealed extensive symmetric white matter abnormalities with cavitation affecting the corpus callosum, brainstem, cerebellum, and upper cervical spinal cord. The identification of compound heterozygous variants in the NDUFV1 gene further confirmed this diagnosis. Currently, there is no established targeted treatment approach for PCL. Consequently, a comprehensive symptomatic treatment regimen was initiated, which consisted of oral combination therapy (including B vitamins, vitamins C and E, L-carnitine, coenzyme Q10, and idebenone) along with systemic rehabilitation training, aimed at improving energy metabolism and alleviating clinical symptoms.

After being discharged, the infant continued to receive oral medication and rehabilitation therapy. Following three months of treatment, slight enhancements in psychomotor development were noted. He was able to maintain stable independent sitting and standing, walk with support, utter reduplicative words (e.g.,“mama,” “baba,” “nainai”), and perform simple instructed movements. His blood lactic acid level normalized to 1.2 mmol/L. Cranial MRI at seven months of treatment showed a significant reduction in white matter lesions compared to baseline; At 13 months, the lesion demonstrated mild progression but was still less extensive than at baseline, (Fig. 1) with no clinical deterioration observed. At eighteen months, the head circumference measured 47 cm. Mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I deficiency was verified via an enzyme activity assay conducted on skin fibroblasts at an external medical facility.The methodology references published literature (Kirby et al., 1999; Murayama et al., 2009) [12, 13]. Quantitative results showed that the activity of complex I in the patient’s skin fibroblasts was 24.4% of the normal control, with ratios to citrate synthase (CS) and complex II (Co II) being 21.0% and 30.8%, respectively, both markedly below the normal reference range (> 40%). In contrast, the activities of complexes II–IV and citrate synthase were within normal limits, consistent with the enzymatic profile of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I deficiency. After more than two years of follow-up, with the last telephone check-in in December 2025, the patient was four years old. He demonstrated the ability to walk independently, exhibiting a slightly wide gait, and was also able to run, jump, and climb stairs. However, he was unable to stand on one leg or perform single-leg jumps. Fine motor skills were mildly impaired, as evidenced by difficulty stringing beads. He was able to look at pictures, narrate simple stories, and communicate normally with peers, indicating sustained improvement in psychomotor development.

Discussion

PCL is a rare autosomal recessive genetic condition characterized by the presence of vacuolar lesions in the cerebral white matter, accompanied by progressive neurological degeneration. The diagnosis of this disorder is primarily based on distinctive radiological features, which are confirmed through genetic analysis [2, 5, 14]. The disorder exhibits significant clinical phenotypic heterogeneity, most commonly presenting with motor developmental regression, dystonia, and cognitive impairment. Cranial imaging reveals abnormal signals in the cerebral white matter that progress to vacuolar lesions, with changes in the corpus callosum being the most specific. Previous autopsy studies have confirmed that PCL is primarily characterized by confluent vacuolar degeneration in the subcortical and periventricular white matter, myelin destruction, and macrophage infiltration, while exhibiting relative axonal preservation and frequent sparing of subcortical U-fibers [2]. The clinical and radiological presentation of the pediatric patient in this case aligns closely with these characteristic features. However, cases of PCL complicated by microcephaly are rarely reported in the literature, suggesting that microcephaly may represent a novel phenotype of NDUFV1-associated PCL, thus expanding the phenotypic spectrum of this disorder.

PCL is a mitochondrial disorder caused by mutations in approximately 20 different genes. The most commonly mutated pathogenic gene is IBA57, followed by NDUFS1, NDUFV2, and NDUFV1, while mutations in NDUFAF5, LYRM7, and other genes are relatively rare [14, 15]. All these genes are involved in the assembly of mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I or in the biosynthesis of iron-sulfur clusters, indicating that mitochondrial dysfunction is the central pathological mechanism underlying PCL [14]. Impairments in energy metabolism lead to oligodendrocyte apoptosis and myelin degradation, ultimately resulting in vacuolar degeneration of white matter [16].

To characterize the genotype-phenotype correlations between NDUFV1 and progressive cavitating leukoencephalopathy (PCL), we conducted a comprehensive search of the PubMed, CNKI, and Wanfang databases. Using the keywords ‘NDUFV1’、’complex I deficiency’ and ‘cavitating leukoencephalopathy’, we reviewed all publications from the inception of these databases up to January 2026. We excluded cases exhibiting phenotypes of Leigh syndrome, optic atrophy, and other unrelated manifestations, resulting in a total of 32 cases associated with NDUFV1 and PCL [11, 14, 17–22]. Including one pediatric patient from the present study, our total cohort comprised 33 cases, with core phenotypes predominantly characterized by motor developmental regression (17 cases) and dystonia (23 cases) (Table 1). Only one case in the previous literature mentioned microcephaly. It involved a previously healthy 1-year-old male child who developed neurodegeneration following minor head trauma. Clinical examination revealed marasmus and a small head circumference (“His head circumference corresponded to − 2 to − 3 Z-scores”) [17]. No specific head circumference value or genotype-phenotype correlation was reported.

The male infant, aged 17 months, presented with a head circumference of 45.5 cm, placing him below the 3rd percentile for his age and gender, thereby fulfilling the diagnostic criteria for microcephaly. Investigations, including cranial CT, chromosomal karyotype analysis, and mitochondrial-related genetic testing, were performed, ruling out other potential etiologies of microcephaly, such as congenital brain malformations, chromosomal abnormalities, and other mitochondrial disorders. In light of the patient’s classic PCL clinical manifestations, characteristic neuroimaging findings, and the identification of compound heterozygous variants in NDUFV1, it was concluded that the microcephaly is associated with these genetic variants rather than representing a coincidental comorbidity.

Pathogenic variants of NDUFV1 impair mitochondrial complex I function, leading to severe ATP deficiency and excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species. This disruption hinders the proliferation and accelerates the apoptosis of energy-dependent neural precursor cells, ultimately reducing both the number and volume of cerebral neurons [23], resulting in microcephaly. Cranial MRI of the infant demonstrated widespread vacuolar degeneration of the white matter, cerebral atrophy, and thinning of the corpus callosum. Symptomatic improvement was observed following treatment targeting energy metabolism, further supporting this pathogenic mechanism.

Previous studies have predominantly focused on NDUFV1 variant-induced white matter injury, with core phenotypes primarily limited to neurological dysfunctions such as psychomotor developmental delay, motor regression, dystonia, and positive pyramidal tract signs [17, 20]. In contrast, cranial morphological anomalies have been largely overlooked. Here, we report for the first time a case of NDUFV1-associated PCL complicated by microcephaly, which extends beyond the classic core manifestations. This finding broadens the phenotypic spectrum of the disorder and suggests that microcephaly may represent a potential phenotype of severe complex I dysfunction caused by compound heterozygous variants of NDUFV1, providing valuable clinical diagnostic clues. Specifically, in pediatric patients presenting with progressive psychomotor developmental delay, motor regression, and concurrent microcephaly, routine investigations for chromosomal abnormalities and other brain development-related disorders should be supplemented with consideration of NDUFV1-associated PCL. Timely genetic testing and mitochondrial function assessment are essential to prevent misdiagnosis or missed diagnosis.

According to the 2015 ACMG standards and guidelines for the interpretation of sequence variants [24], the NDUFV1 c.749T > A (p.Val250Glu) variant identified in the proband in this study was initially classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) based on the criteria PM2 + PM3 + PP3. Following updates and refinements to the latest ACMG/AMP sequence variant interpretation guidelines (https://www.clinicalgenome.org/working-groups/sequence-variant-interpretation/), re-evaluation of the pathogenicity of this variant resulted in a revised classification as Likely Pathogenic, supported by the criteria PM2_Supporting + PM3 + PP3_Strong. Specifically, PM2_Supporting indicates that the variant was absent from normal control populations in the ESP, 1000 Genomes, and ExAC databases (or represents an extremely low-frequency allele in recessive disorders); PM3 denotes the detection of a pathogenic variant in trans in the setting of an autosomal recessive disorder; and PP3_Strong is justified by multiple in silico algorithms predicting a deleterious effect on the gene or its product, with the integrative protein function prediction tool REVEL yielding a score of 0.995, which meets the threshold for PP3_Strong. The infant’s clinical phenotype, elevated blood lactate levels, and white matter vacuolar changes align with NDUFV1-associated PCL. Symptomatic improvement following mitochondrial-targeted treatment and a significant reduction in complex I activity in skin fibroblasts provide indirect functional evidence. A limitation of this investigation is the absence of direct functional verification. Future in vitro cell experiments and animal models may elucidate the pathogenic mechanism and refine the variant classification. In summary, this variant is likely pathogenic and, in conjunction with c.365 C > T (p.Pro122Leu), contributes to PCL in this infant.

Currently, there is no targeted therapy available for NDUFV1-associated PCL [20]. Clinical management primarily emphasizes comprehensive symptomatic therapy aimed at enhancing mitochondrial energy metabolism, alleviating symptoms, and promoting neurological recovery [25]. The widely utilized “cocktail therapy” typically involves the administration of B vitamins, vitamins C and E, L-carnitine, coenzyme Q10, Idebenone, and various other compounds designed to enhance mitochondrial metabolic efficiency and mitigate neuronal injury. When combined with systemic rehabilitation training, this regimen can improve muscle tone, motor skills, and cognitive function in pediatric patients, thereby supporting their psychomotor development.

The patient in this study was monitored for over two years following the initiation of combined treatment. Blood lactate levels normalized, white matter lesions exhibited significant improvement, and psychomotor development advanced markedly, including independent walking and normal communication, without any severe complications. These findings align with previous reports, reinforcing the notion that standardized comprehensive treatment can delay disease progression and enhance prognosis, thereby providing clinical evidence for the long-term management of PCL. Notably, a cranial MRI conducted 13 months post-treatment indicated a slight increase in the extent of white matter lesions compared to the scan performed at 7 months, suggesting a potential recurrence of the disease associated with persistent mitochondrial dysfunction and irreversible neuronal damage. Consequently, long-term treatment and rehabilitation are crucial, necessitating regular re-evaluations of blood lactate levels, cranial MRI, and neurological function to facilitate timely adjustments to the treatment plan.

This study has several limitations. It is a single-case report with a small sample size, which leaves the long-term efficacy, optimal dosage, and treatment duration unclear. The correlation between symptomatic relief and the inherent variability of the disease course requires additional exploration. Direct functional verification of the NDUFV1 c.749T > A (p.Val250Glu) mutation—such as through in vitro cell experiments or animal models—was not performed. In addition, the patient harbored both a known likely pathogenic variant (p.Pro122Leu) and a novel missense variant (p.Val250Glu); Therefore, the complex I deficiency observed in this study could not be definitively attributed to a single allelic variant, which constitutes another major limitation of this investigation. Future studies should increase the sample size to include more cases carrying this mutation, thereby verifying its pathogenicity and genotype-phenotype correlation. Concurrently, in vitro and in vivo experiments can clarify the molecular mechanisms underlying complex I dysfunction, providing a theoretical foundation for targeted therapies. Furthermore, it is essential to investigate optimized treatment protocols to enhance clinical efficacy.

Herein, we report a pediatric case of PCL harboring compound heterozygous NDUFV1 variants: the novel, previously unreported potentially pathogenic variant c.749T > A (p.Val250Glu) and the likely pathogenic variant c.365 C > T (p.Pro122Leu). To our knowledge, this is a report confirming microcephaly as a rare or underreported phenotype of NDUFV1-associated PCL. Mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I dysfunction and abnormal energy metabolism induced by NDUFV1 defects are the core pathogenic mechanisms underlying microcephaly in this patient. Supported by multidimensional evidence, the NDUFV1 c.749T > A (p.Val250Glu) variant is likely pathogenic, and its synergy with the c.365 C > T (p.Pro122Leu) variant drives disease onset. Cocktail therapy, in conjunction with rehabilitation, significantly enhances patients’ energy metabolism and alleviates clinical symptoms. Furthermore, long-term standardized treatment and follow-up effectively delay disease progression. This study broadens the genotypic and phenotypic spectra associated with NDUFV1-related PCL, offering essential insights for early clinical diagnosis, genetic testing, counseling, treatment, and follow-up.
