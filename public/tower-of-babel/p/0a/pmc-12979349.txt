Impact of albumin infusion on 30-day mortality in ICU patients with acute-on-chronic liver failure: a retrospective cohort using MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Sheng Lu, Xiaxia Weng, Jian Cao, Junchao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman serum albumin (HSA) possesses oncotic, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory properties. Although recent studies suggest that albumin may promote resolution of acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) and reduce the incidence of infection, its impact on overall prognosis with daily administration remains unestablished. Patients meeting the Asian Pacific Association for the Study of the Liver (APASL) criteria for ACLF were extracted from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC-IV 2.2) database. The primary outcome was 30-day mortality. To balance baseline characteristics between the albumin and non-albumin groups, we applied stabilized inverse probability of treatment weighting (SIPTW). The association between daily albumin infusion and 30-day mortality was subsequently assessed using Cox regression analyses. A total of 505 patients were enrolled in the study, of whom 325 received albumin therapy within the first 24 h of ICU admission. After applying SIPTW, the cohort comprised 169 patients in the non-albumin group and 319 in the albumin group. Overall, albumin administration was not significantly associated with 30-day mortality in patients with ACLF. However, subgroup analyses revealed that albumin infusion conferred the most substantial survival benefit in specific patient populations. These included individuals with spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, a Model for End-Stage Liver Disease score of ≥ 30.1, a mean arterial pressure below 73 mmHg, a daily albumin dosage of ≤ 1.0 g/kg, or those receiving a combination of 5% and 25% albumin concentrations. Conversely, a dosage exceeding 1.0 g/kg/day was associated with inferior 30-day survival. Albumin administration is associated with reduced mortality in specific subpopulations of patients with ACLF. Key clinical parameters—including serum albumin concentration, SBP, MELD score, and MAP—were identified as significant modifiers of treatment efficacy and should be incorporated into clinical decision-making when initiating albumin therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10238-025-01998-0.Keywords: Acute-on-chronic liver failure, Albumin, Stabilized inverse probability of treatment weighting

Introduction

Acute-on-chronic liver failure (ACLF) is a distinct clinical syndrome marked by acute decompensation of cirrhosis, identifiable precipitating events, systemic inflammation, and high short-term mortality. A hallmark of its pathophysiology is profound splanchnic and systemic vasodilation, which reduces systemic vascular resistance and triggers a compensatory rise in cardiac output. This hemodynamic dysregulation results in a mismatch between splanchnic and systemic perfusion, culminating in effective hypovolemia [1]. In recent decades, substantial progress has been made in elucidating the complex mechanisms of ACLF-particularly the roles of systemic inflammation, immune dysregulation, and oxidative stress [2]. Against this pathophysiological backdrop, early targeted interventions—such as volume expansion, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant agents, and immune modulation—may offer therapeutic benefits.

Human serum albumin (HSA) represents a uniquely promising candidate in this context, given its diverse physiological roles, including oncotic, antioxidant, immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory effects [3]. A randomized trial in patients with advanced cirrhosis and non-spontaneous bacterial peritonitis bacterial infections demonstrated that adding albumin to antibiotic therapy improved ACLF resolution rates and reduced nosocomial infections compared with antibiotics alone [4]. In recent, an observational study of 109 ACLF patients treated with HSA reported encouraging outcomes: 86% achieved ACLF resolution, alongside a 72.7% rate of infection clearance [5]. Despite these promising findings, a critical knowledge gap remains: no study has yet specifically evaluated whether albumin administration reduces mortality in an exclusively ACLF-defined population. Therefore, the present study focuses specifically on patients with ACLF, with the primary aim of determining the effect of albumin infusion on mortality.

Patients and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective study enrolled patients diagnosed with ACLF form the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) 2.2 database, which contains extensive clinical records of patients admitted to the intensive care units (ICUs) at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) between 2008 and 2019 [6]. The study was exempt from ethical approval by the Institutional Review Board of BIDMC, which also waived the requirement for informed consent due to the de-identified nature of the data. Shen Lu was authorized to access the database and performed the data extraction. (certification number: 13228859).

The study enrolled patients diagnosed with ACLF upon ICU admission. For patients with multiple ICU admissions, only the first ICU stay was included. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) liver malignancy (n = 80); (2) chronic kidney disease (n = 94); (3) hospital stay of less than 24 h (n = 18); (4) initiation of albumin therapy 24 h or more after ICU admission (n = 222). Data collection and definitions

Clinical data for patients with ACLF were obtained from the MIMIC-IV 2.2 database. The extracted variables encompassed demographic details, laboratory parameters, clinical severity scores and vital signs from the first day of ICU admission, along with complications, comorbidities, and prognostic information. Data extraction was performed using Structured Query Language via Navicat Premium software (version 16.2.7).

Patients who received albumin infusion within the first 24 h of ICU admission were classified into the albumin group. Cirrhosis diagnoses, etiologies, and ACLF-related complications were comprehensively identified using ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM codes recorded at discharge (Supplementary Table S1). ACLF was defined according to established criteria as acute liver failure (bilirubin > 5 mg/dl and INR ≥ 1.5) in patients with pre-existing cirrhosis [7]. The albumin dosage was calculated as: Albumin dosage = [volume A (mL) × concentration A + volume B (mL) × concentration B + …]/weight (kg)/duration of albumin treatment(days).

Our primary outcome focused on the 30-day mortality.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean (standard deviation) or median (interquartile range) where appropriate, and categorical variables as frequencies (percentage). For intergroup comparisons, the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables, and student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, depending on the data characteristics. Variables with ≥ 20% missingness were retained and imputed only if clinically essential. In terms of data preprocessing, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) to handle missing data, with the imputation performed 5 times. The remaining missing data were filled in using the median interpolation method, with detailed information on the missingness patterns of each variable provided in Table S2.

Stabilized inverse probability of treatment weighting (SIPTW) was employed to balance baseline characteristics between the albumin group and the non-albumin group. Propensity scores were estimated via multiple logistic regression, with adjustments made for age, gender, cirrhosis etiology, total bilirubin, transaminases, creatinine, sodium, albumin, international normalized ratio (INR), routine blood tests, lactate, partial pressure of oxygen (PO2), mean arterial pressure (MAP) and baseline complications. Variables included in the propensity score (PS) models to calculate SIPTW weights for risk adjustment were based on descriptive covariate analysis. The PS model generated predicted probability of being to the albumin group, and SIPTW weights were subsequently derived by multiplying crude inverse probability weights by the corresponding marginal probability of being in the albumin group. To mitigate the influence of extreme weights, the stabilized weights were trimmed at the 1 st and 99th percentiles. Covariate balance prior to and following SIPTW application was evaluated using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with a variable considered balanced when its SMD was < 0.2.

Overall survival were calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Multivariate cox regression analyses were performed to identify risk factors associated with prognosis, adjusting for age, gender, etiology, ascites, HE, EVB, SBP, HRS, sepsis, WBC, hemoglobin, PLT, sodium, Cr, Tbil, ALT, AST, INR, MAP, lactate, SpO2.A two-sided P-value of < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.3).

Result

Albumin administration in the study

From the MIMIC-IV database, 4156 patients with cirrhosis were identified, of whom 919 were diagnosed with ACLF within 24 h of ICU admission. After applying the exclusion criteria,505 patients were ultimately included. Among these ACLF patients, 325 (64.4%) received albumin therapy within the first 24 h, while 180 (35.6%) did not.

Albumin administration was initiated at a median of 6.9 h (interquartile range: 3.0–13.6). All patients in the albumin group received more than one administration. The minimum and maximum daily albumin doses were 0.28 g/kg (interquartile range:0.17–0.45) and 0.83 g/kg (interquartile range:0.48–1.04), respectively. Among the 325 patients treated with albumin, the average cumulative dose was 3.66 g/kg over a median treatment duration of 6 days (interquartile range: 2–14 days). Additionally, 182 patients received 25% albumin solution, 22 patients received 5% albumin solution, and 121 received a combination of both concentrations.

Baseline characteristics of patients with and without albumin infusion before and after SIPTW

The albumin group exhibited more severe illness and more complications compared to the non-albumin group (Table 1, p < 0.05). Hepatic dysfunction was more pronounced in the albumin group, as reflected by significantly higher total bilirubin and INR values (Table 1, p < 0.05). Additionally, the albumin group displayed more severe renal injury, as characterized by elevated levels of blood urea nitrogen and creatinine. (Table 1, p < 0.05). SIPTW was implemented to mitigate discrepancies between the two groups. Following SIPTW, the non-albumin group consisted of 169 patients and the albumin group included 319 patients. In the SIPTW cohort, all baseline characteristics were comparable between the two groups, as indicated by a SMD of < 0.2 and all p-values of < 0.05 (Table 1).

The influence of albumin administration on the risk of 30-day mortality

A total of 161 (31.8%) deaths were recorded in the cohort, including 111 (34.2%) in the Alb group and 50 (27.8%) in the non-Alb group. The median hospital length of stay was 11(6–20) days overall, with a notable disparity between the groups: 13 days (IQR: 7–23) in the Alb group versus 7 days (IQR: 4–12) in the non-Alb group. Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in 30-day survival between the Alb and non-Alb groups (72.2% vs. 65.8%, log-rank p = 0.334; Fig. 2a).Multivariate cox regression further confirmed that albumin infusion was not independently associated with 30-day mortality (HR: 0.73, 95% CI: 0.50–1.06, p = 0.100; Fig. 3a).

To address potential confounding due to baseline imbalances, we applied SIPTW. In the weighted cohorts, the 30-day survival rates remained comparable between groups (62.8% vs. 67.9%, log-rank p = 0.152; Fig. 2b). Consistent with the primary analysis, SIPTW-adjusted Cox regression demonstrated no significant association between albumin administration and mortality (HR: 0.75, 95% CI: 0.55–1.03, p = 0.152; Fig. 3b).

Indications for albumin administration

Subgroup analyses demonstrated that albumin administration significantly improved 30-day survival in patients with SBP (p < 0.05). In contrast, no significant mortality benefit associated with albumin therapy was observed in subgroups of patients with other complications (p > 0.05).Importantly, these results were consistently supported by both multivariable Cox regression (Fig. 3a) and SIPTW-adjusted Cox regression analyses (Fig. 3b).

Further stratification by albumin dosage and concentration revealed distinct associations with 30-day survival (Table 2). Albumin administration at a dosage of ≤ 1 g/kg/day was associated with significantly improved survival (HR 0.62; 95%CI 0.42–0.91; p = 0.014). In contrast, a higher albumin dosage (> 1 g/kg/day) was linked to a significant deterioration in 30-day survival (HR 3.44; 95%CI 1.92–6.16; p < 0.001). Regarding albumin concentration, neither 5% albumin (HR 0.38; 95%CI 0.14–1.05; p = 0.061) nor 25% albumin (HR 0.90; 95% CI 0.60–1.34; p = 0.601) showed a significant mortality benefit compared with the non-albumin group. However, combined administration of 5% and 25% concentration was associated with significantly reduced mortality (HR 0.59; 95% CI 0.36–0.96; p = 0.034).

The impact of meld and MAP on the effectiveness of albumin therapy

We next assessed how baseline MELD scores modified the treatment effect of albumin. In the albumin group, baseline MELD scores exhibited a linear relationship with 30-day mortality, where higher scores were associated with increased risk (Fig. 4a). In contrast, the non-albumin group showed a non-linear (U-shaped) association between MELD scores and mortality (Fig. 4b, with p < 0.05 for nonlinearity). To identify the MELD score threshold above which albumin infusion remained beneficial, we performed further analysis. This showed that albumin treatment was associated with significantly lower 30-day mortality in patients with a baseline MELD score ≥ 30.1 compared to those not receiving albumin (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

Our study of cirrhotic patients with ACLF yields several noteworthy findings. Firstly, although albumin administration was not associated with reduced 30-day mortality in the overall cohort, it demonstrated a significant survival benefit in patients with with SBP. Furthermore, albumin infusion was effective when administered at a dosage ≤ 1 g/kg/day, in patients with MAP < 73 mmHg, or in those with a MELD score ≥ 30.1.

Beyond its well-established oncotic properties, HSA exerts crucial non-oncotic physiological effects, including potent antioxidant activity, toxin binding and transport, and immunomodulatory functions [3]. These pleiotropic properties suggest that HSA administration may offer benefits beyond volume expansion. This hypothesis is supported by a meta-analysis demonstrating significant mortality reduction in cirrhotic patients receiving albumin therapy [8]. However, our study revealed no significant therapeutic benefit in overall cohort, even after comprehensive adjustment for confounding factors through both SIPTW and multivariate COX regression analyses. This discrepancy may be attributable to differences in ACLF severity, complication, and albumin dosage and concentration.

Impaired hepatic and hemodynamic function may predispose patients to organ failure following acute decompensation. Previous research has demonstrated that both lower MAP and higher MELD score are significant risk factors for ACLF development within one year [9]. These observations align with the findings of Zhu et al. [10] and Chen et al. [11], who identified MAP and MELD score as robust prognostic indicators in ACLF patients. Consequently, the cutoff values of MELD score and MAP for initiating human albumin therapy appear to influence its efficacy. Our subgroup analyses confirmed that albumin treatment reduces mortality in patients with MAP < 73mmHg or Meld score ≥ 30.1.

Systemic inflammation induces vasodilation, thereby diminishing effective arterial blood volume (EABV). This hemodynamic disturbance promotes acute kidney injury development in patients with SBP. Similarly, vasodilation—accompanied by reduced EABV and renal perfusion pressure—represents the primary pathogenic mechanism in HRS and post-paracentesis circulatory dysfunction [12]. Human albumin exhibits the capacity to bind and transport water-in-soluble substances and inflammatory mediators [13]. Consequently, albumin administration has been recommended for large volume paracentesis, SBP and HRS [14]. Similarly, systemic inflammation plays a pivotal role in ACLF, with significantly higher inflammatory markers compared to acute decompensated cirrhosis [15, 16]. This heightened inflammatory state induces more pronounced vasodilation, predisposing patients to severe circulatory dysfunction [16]. Our study further corroborates these findings, demonstrating that albumin administration improves survival in ACLF patients with SBP. However, this beneficial effect was not observed in ACLF patients with HRS, possibly due to the more rapid progression of acute kidney injury in ACLF [17]. Another potential explanation is that ACLF patients present with significant hyperbilirubinemia, substantially increasing their risk of bile cast nephropathy compared to those with decompensated cirrhosis [18]. A recent Post hoc analysis of North American research revealed that HRS patients with lower serum creatinine levels exhibited higher rates of HRS resolution and better survival outcomes [19]. Such profound pathophysiological disturbances may compromise the therapeutic efficacy of albumin in ACLF patients with HRS. Similarly, ACLF patients with ascites derive no benefit from albumin administration, consistent with a meta-analysis indicating that albumin infusion does not significantly reduce mortality in cirrhotic patients with ascites [8].

Albumin also fails to improve survival in patients with sepsis and shock, which may be attributed to the predominance of non-SBP infections in this subgroup. A meta-analysis established that intravenous albumin administration does not reduce mortality risk, while significantly increasing the incidence of pulmonary edema in cirrhotic patients with non-SBP infections [20]. Clinical studies have shown that although 20% albumin administration in cirrhotic patients with sepsis-induced hypotension improves hemodynamic parameters and ameliorates hyperlactatemia, these benefits do not translate into improved 28-day survival and may exacerbate pulmonary edema [21]. This therapeutic paradox was most evident in pneumonia patients, who constituted the majority of the study cohort. The underlying pathophysiology—impaired alveolocapillary barrier function and increased vascular permeability—may explain albumin infusion intolerance in pneumonia patients [21] Our study confirms that intravenous albumin administration provides no benefit in ACLF complicated by pneumonia.

Furthermore, in cirrhotic patients with sepsis, impaired lymphatic circulation exacerbates capillary permeability, potentially increasing the extravasation of albumin from the vascular compartment. This process can lead to albumin accumulation in the extravascular space, contributing to fluid overload. Hu et al. [22] proposed that the therapeutic effects of albumin infusion are significantly attenuated in septic patients with cirrhosis when the administered dosage exceeded 1.0 g/kg/day. Our study further confirms these observations, demonstrating that albumin administration at dosage below 1.0 g/kg/day improves 30-day survival in ACLF patients.

In our study, 25% albumin was administered in the majority of our cases in our study. Notably, 5% albumin expands plasma volume by approximately 0.18 times the infused volume, whereas 20% albumin expands plasma volume by approximately 2.09 times [23]. Kamal et al. [1] report that cirrhotic cardiomyopathy is present up to 58.3% of ACLF patients with severe sepsis, primarily manifesting as left ventricular systolic dysfunction(LVDD), and further noted that 20% albumin not only fails to improve circulatory function but may induce cardiac decompensation in these patients. In contrast, Philips et al. [24] demonstrated that 5% albumin infusion significantly outperforms normal saline in improving short-term in-hospital survival among cirrhotic patients with sepsis-induced hypotension. Similarly, Li et al. [25] showed 5% albumin is associated with a reduction in 28-day mortality in ICU patients with cirrhosis and acute kidney injury, whereas 25% albumin has no association with mortality outcomes. Some experts recommend avoiding the rapid infusion of 20%, instead advocating for a judicious fluid management strategy guided by assessment of inferior vena cava diameter, collapsibility index, and point-of-care ultrasound evaluation to determine optimal fluid volume in patients [21]. Additionally, 4–5% albumin is recommended for initial rapid volume expansion; once a MAP ≥ 65 mmHg is achieved, transition to daily administration of 20% albumin (40–60 g) may be considered to optimize and stabilize hemodynamics [26]. Consistent with these prior findings, our study revealed that monotherapy with 25% albumin failed to significantly improve patient prognosis. However, combination therapy with 25% albumin and 5% albumin demonstrated clinical benefit. Although the 5% albumin monotherapy group did not reach statistical significance, this may stem from the limited sample size (n = 22), necessitating validation through multicenter randomized controlled trials.

This study has some limitations. First, as a retrospective analysis using data from the single-center MIMIC-IV database, it is subject to inherent selection bias. Albumin administration was not standardized and likely varied among physicians. Although we applied SIPTW methods to mitigate bias, unmeasured confounders such as nutritional status and concomitant medications could not be fully accounted. Secondly, the half-life of albumin is approximately 15 days [27], the duration of albumin use in our study was relatively short (IQR: 2–14 days), which may have influenced the outcomes. Additionally, ACLF not only reduces albumin quantity but also induces molecular structural alterations in albumin, significantly compromising its physiological functions including ligand binding, detoxification efficiency, and antioxidant activity. Baldassarre et al. proposed the concept of effective albumin concentration, which serves as an independent predictor of 90-day mortality, whereas total albumin concentration shows no significant association with mortality [28]. Consistent with this, our study confirmed that conventional serum albumin measurements lack clinical utility for guiding albumin therapy in ACLF patients. The absence of effective albumin concentration measurements represents an important limitation that warrants future investigation. Fourth, current definitions of ACLF vary considerably [7]. Our study used the APASL definition, thus our findings may not be generalizable to other ACLF criteria. Finally, although we performed comprehensive subgroup and sensitivity analyses, the limited sample size in certain subgroups may affect the reliability of these findings.

In conclusion, albumin administration provided a significant 30-day survival benefit in specific subgroups of ACLF patients. SBP, MELD score and MAP were identified as modifiers of treatment efficacy and should be considered when initiating albumin infusion. Additionally, high concentration and large-volume albumin infusion should be used cautiously in patients with altered pulmonary capillary permeability. Well-designed prospective interventional trials with adequate sample sizes are needed to confirm these findings.
