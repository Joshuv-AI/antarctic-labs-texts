A bispecific-aptamer TSHR-CD80-1 treats thyroid eye disease by inhibiting CD8+ T cell-fibroblast interactions
Authors: Jiamin Cao, Zhaochangci Chen, Lemeng Feng, Bingyu Xie, Yao Tan, Wei Xiong, Feng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThyroid eye disease (TED), an autoimmune disorder, involves critical interactions among immune cells that drive disease progression. However, the underlying mechanisms and their therapeutic potential remain unclear. This study profiled leukocyte composition in the peripheral blood of TED patients, revealing an elevated proportion of CD8+ T cells and distinct T-cell receptor (TCR) characteristics. Using a TSHR-targeting nucleic acid aptamer (TSHR-21-42), this study demonstrated a positive correlation between fibroblast proportion and TED activity. Histological examination further confirmed the interaction between CD8+ T cells and orbital fibroblasts. To inhibit this interaction, this study designed TSHR-CD80-1, a bispecific aptamer that simultaneously targets TSHR and CD80. In vitro experiments showed that TSHR-CD80-1 effectively interfered with the crosstalk between orbital fibroblasts and T cells while retaining the ability of TSHR-21-42 to suppress orbital fibroblast activation, adipogenic differentiation, hyaluronic acid secretion and fibrotic responses. In a TED mouse model, peri-ocular injection of TSHR-CD80-1 significantly alleviated systemic inflammation and reduced intra-orbital infiltration of CD8+ T cells, with no obvious adverse effects. Background

Thyroid Eye Disease (TED), significantly impacts patients due to its alterations in ocular appearance and visual function [1]. As the most frequent extrathyroidal manifestation of Graves’ disease (GD), TED is believed to share common autoantigens with GD. The thyrotropin receptor (TSHR) is among the earliest identified antigens implicated in TED pathogenesis and is highly expressed in orbital fibroblasts, particularly in CD34+ orbital fibroblasts [2, 3]. TSHR contributes to disease progression through multiple mechanisms. On one hand, TSHR interacts with the insulin-like growth factor-1 receptor (IGF-1R), jointly promoting the proliferation and differentiation of orbital fibroblasts. This leads to adipogenic and fibrotic changes, as well as hyaluronic acid secretion. On the other hand, TSHR can be presented by orbital fibroblasts as an antigen to T cells, serving as a primary signal for T cell activation. Subsequent binding of T cells to secondary signals, such as CD80 on antigen-presenting cells, results in full T cell activation and downstream inflammatory cascades [4–6]. Therefore, inhibiting TSHR function represents a potential therapeutic strategy for TED. Currently, several monoclonal antibodies targeting TSHR have been developed and are undergoing clinical trials [7, 8]. However, these monoclonal antibodies share common limitations with other biologics, including high cost, limited programmability, and single-target specificity.

Nucleic acid aptamers are single-stranded DNA or RNA oligonucleotides [9]. Since their discovery in the late 20th century, the methods for aptamer selection have diversified, and the range of selectable targets has expanded to include proteins, cells, and tissues [10]. In previous work, our group selected a high-affinity nucleic acid aptamer targeting TSHR, designated TSHR-21-42, and utilized it for profiling leukocytes in TED patients [11]. However, whether this aptamer possesses pharmacological activity and could be applied therapeutically in TED remains unknown. Furthermore, compared to monoclonal antibodies, aptamers offer superior programmability and can be conjugated with various molecules to form structures with complex functionalities [12]. Given that orbital fibroblasts perform diverse functions and interact with other cell types within the orbit [13], it is unclear whether conjugating additional functional modules could enhance the therapeutic efficacy of an aptamer. CD80, a key secondary signal for T cell activation, is itself a target for which specific aptamers have been developed [14, 15]. Nevertheless, it remains to be determined whether conjugating an anti-CD80 aptamer to TSHR-21-42 can simultaneously inhibit both TSHR and CD80 function, block the interaction between orbital fibroblasts and T cells, and thereby improve the therapeutic outcome against TED.

This study aims to investigate the interaction between CD8+ T cells and orbital fibroblasts in TED orbital tissues. This study will construct a bispecific aptamer targeting both TSHR and CD80 to elucidate its therapeutic potential and underlying mechanisms in TED.

Methods

Flow cytometry

Peripheral blood (1 mL) was collected from TED patients and mixed with 9 mL of red blood cell lysis buffer (Solarbio, China). After gentle mixing, the sample was incubated at room temperature for 10 min and centrifuged at 1800 rpm for 5 min. The pellet was washed twice with 1 mL of PBS buffer (Solarbio, China). Antibodies against CD3, CD4, CD8, CD19, and CD56 (BD Biosciences, USA) were added to a final concentration of 5%, or the TSHR-21-42 aptamer was added to a final concentration of 500 nM. The samples were incubated at room temperature for 30 min, washed twice with PBS, resuspended, and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Isolation and culture of primary orbital fibroblasts

Orbital adipose tissue was obtained from TED patients undergoing orbital decompression surgery. Within 2 h, the tissue was washed twice with PBS containing 10% penicillin-streptomycin (Procell, China), minced into 1–2 mm3 fragments, and digested with 1 mL of serum-free high-glucose DMEM (Procell, China) containing 5 mg of type II collagenase (Worthington, USA) at 37℃ for 1 h. Digestion was stopped by adding 1 mL of fetal bovine serum (Procell, China). The cell suspension was filtered through a cell strainer and centrifuged at 100 rpm for 5 min. The pellet was washed twice with 1 mL of PBS and then cultured in high-glucose complete medium (10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin-streptomycin) at 37℃ with 5% CO2.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were seeded on coverslips. When orbital fibroblasts reached 80% confluence, the culture medium was removed, and cells were washed twice with 1 mL of PBS. Cells were fixed with 1 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min, washed with PBS for 10 min (repeated twice), and permeabilized with 1 mL of 1% Triton X-100 (Thermo Fisher, USA) for 5 min. After three PBS washes, cells were blocked with 1 mL of 5% BSA (Thermo Fisher, USA) for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies (vimentin, fibronectin, collagen I, CD34, TSHR, α-SMA) (Proteintech, China) diluted to 5% were applied to the coverslips, which were then incubated overnight at 4℃. After three PBS washes, FITC-conjugated secondary antibody (Proteintech, China) diluted to 5% was applied and incubated for 1 h. Following washes, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime, China), and the coverslips were mounted with glycerol for fluorescence microscopy.

Secondary structure prediction

The secondary structure of TSHR-21-42 and CD80 was predicted using RNAfold (http://rna.tbi.univie.ac.at/cgi-bin/RNAWebSuite/RNAfold.cgi) with default parameters.

CCK-8 assay

Orbital fibroblasts were seeded in a 96-well plate. When cells reached 50–70% confluence, different concentrations of TSHR-CD80-1 (0, 125, 250, 500, 750 nM) were added. After 24 h, the medium was removed, cells were washed with 200 µL of PBS, and 100 µL of CCK-8 solution (Beyotime, China) was added. After incubation at 37℃ for 2 h, absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Oil red o staining

Orbital fibroblasts were seeded in a 6-well plate and cultured through human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells adipogenic differentiation kit (Hycyte, China) with TSHR-CD80-1 treatment. During the 2 week differentiation period, cells were cultured alternately with adipogenic induction medium (basal medium, fetal bovine serum, penicillin-streptomycin, L-glutamine, insulin, 3-isobutyl-1-methylxanthine, rosiglitazone, dexamethasone) for 3 days and adipogenic maintenance medium (basal medium, fetal bovine serum, penicillin-streptomycin, L-glutamine, insulin) for another 3 days, and this cycle was repeated until the completion of the experiment. After 2 weeks, the medium was removed, and cells were washed with PBS. Cells were fixed with 1 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature, washed twice with PBS, and rinsed with 60% isopropanol for 30 s. Cells were then stained with Oil Red O working solution (isopropanol: saturated Oil Red O solution = 2:3) (Hycyte, China) for 30 min. The staining solution was removed, cells were rinsed with 60% isopropanol, washed with PBS, counterstained with hematoxylin for 3 min, washed, and observed under a light microscope.

Western blot

Proteins were extracted from orbital fibroblasts using RIPA lysis buffer. Proteins were separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was blocked with skim milk, washed, and incubated with primary antibodies (GAPDH, PPARγ, C/EBPα, FABP4, TSHR, CD80) (Proteintech, China) at a 1:2000 dilution for 2 h. After three 10-min washes with TBST, the membrane was incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at a 1:2000 dilution for 1 h. Following three washes, the blot was imaged.

Quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from orbital fibroblasts using an RNA extraction kit and reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit. The qPCR reaction mixture consisted of 5 µL of 2× SYBR Green qPCR master mix, 0.5 µL each of 10 µM forward and reverse primers, 1 µL of cDNA template, and 3 µL of water. The cycle threshold (Ct) values were determined using a qPCR instrument. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

ELISA

Orbital fibroblasts were cultured in adipogenic induction medium for 3 days, and the supernatant was collected. Hyaluronic acid concentration was measured using a hyaluronic acid ELISA kit (Zike, China). Standards and samples (50 µL) were added to the plate wells, followed by 100 µL of HRP-conjugated detection antibody. After incubation at 37℃ for 60 min, the plate was washed five times. Substrates A and B (50 µL each) were added, and the plate was incubated at 37℃ in the dark for 15 min. The reaction was stopped with 50 µL of stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm.

TCR Sequencing

Peripheral blood (3 mL) was collected from TED patients and healthy controls. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated using human mononuclear cell separation medium, and total RNA was extracted. A TCR library was constructed using a TCR library preparation kit, and cDNA was obtained by reverse transcription. TCR sequencing and raw data quality control were performed by Kindstar Global. TCR diversity was analyzed using two metrics: the sum of the top 100 TCR sequence frequencies and D50. D50 represents the number of unique TCR rearrangements that account for 50% of the cumulative frequency in a sample, with a higher D50 indicating greater diversity. To reduce computational complexity, TCR sequences were included in the comparative analysis between TED and control groups only if they appeared in at least two samples.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Adipose tissue was paraffin-embedded and sectioned. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene (20 min, 20 min, 15 min), rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 100%, 85%, 75%; 5 min each), and rinsed with distilled water. Sections were stained with hematoxylin for 10 min, rinsed with tap water, differentiated in 1% acid alcohol, rinsed again, and blued in bluing solution for 5 min. After rinsing, sections were stained with eosin (Servicebio, China) for 3 min, rinsed, dehydrated through an ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral resin.

Tissue oil red o staining

Adipose tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in OCT compound, and sectioned. Sections were rinsed with 60% isopropanol for 20 s and stained with 60% Oil Red O working solution. After staining, sections were rinsed with 60% isopropanol, washed with PBS, counterstained with hematoxylin, washed again, mounted, and observed under a microscope.

Tissue immunofluorescence staining

Tissue immunofluorescence staining was performed by Servicebio. Briefly, adipose tissue was paraffin-embedded, sectioned, deparaffinized, and subjected to antigen retrieval. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked with hydrogen peroxide, and sections were washed with PBS. After serum blocking, sections were incubated with CD34 primary antibody at 4℃ overnight. Following three washes, HRP-conjugated secondary antibody was applied and incubated at room temperature for 50 min. After washing, iF555-TSA was added and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 10 min. Sections were washed with TBST, and antigen retrieval was repeated with citrate buffer. The above steps were repeated sequentially for CD8 primary antibody with iF440-Tyramide and α-SMA primary antibody with iF647-Tyramide. CD4, CD19 and CD56 were incubated with secondary antibodies labeled with iF555-Tyramide, iF488-Tyramide and iF647-Tyramide, respectively. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and autofluorescence quenching reagent was applied for 5 min. Sections were mounted and examined under a fluorescence microscope.

Degradation assay

The nucleic acid aptamer was diluted to 1 M in PBS containing 10% serum and incubated at 37℃ for 0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 24, 36, and 72 h. After incubation, samples were analyzed by 3% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the gel was imaged.

Molecular docking

The secondary structure of TSHR-CD80-1 was obtained using RNAfold. The three-dimensional (3D) RNA structure was generated using RNAComposer (http://rnacomposer.ibch.poznan.pl). Using Avogadro 2, the 2’-hydroxyl groups on the ribose sugar were replaced with hydrogen atoms, and the 5-carbon of uracil was modified with a methyl group to generate the final 3D structure of TSHR-CD80-1. Protein structures of TSHR (PDB: 7XW6) and CD80 (PDB: 1DR9) were obtained from the Protein Data Bank. Molecular docking simulations between TSHR-CD80-1 and TSHR or CD80 were performed using HDOCK (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/). Results were visualized using Chimera 1.17.3.

Aptamer-antibody competitive binding assay

Five groups were established in this experiment: PBS group, TSHR-CD80-1 group, TSHR antibody+TSHR-CD80-1 group, CD80 antibody+TSHR-CD80-1 group, and TSHR antibody+CD80 antibody+TSHR-CD80-1 group. Orbital fibroblasts in the PBS group and TSHR-CD80-1 group were incubated with PBS and 500 nM TSHR-CD80-1(FITC) for 30 min, respectively. For the TSHR antibody+TSHR-CD80-1 group, CD80 antibody+TSHR-CD80-1 group and combined antibody group, orbital fibroblasts were first incubated with TSHR antibody and/or CD80 antibody for 30 min, followed by the addition of 500 nM TSHR-CD80-1(FITC) and another 30-minute incubation. After two washes, samples were analyzed by flow cytometry.

T Cell isolation and activation

Peripheral blood (3 mL) from TED patients was diluted with an equal volume of PBS. The diluted blood was carefully layered onto 12 mL of human mononuclear cell separation medium in a 50 mL centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 1800 rpm for 25 min. The mononuclear cell layer was collected, washed twice with 5 mL of PBS, and stained with APC-conjugated anti-CD3 and PE-conjugated anti-CD8 antibodies (BD Biosciences, USA) at room temperature for 30 min. After washing with 1 mL of PBS, CD3⁺CD8⁺ T cells were sorted using a BD flow cytometer. Sorted T cells were cultured in high-glucose complete medium and activated with CD3/CD28 magnetic beads (BioLegend, USA) at a 1:1 bead-to-cell ratio.

Co-culture of orbital fibroblasts and T cells

Orbital fibroblasts were seeded in a 6-well plate. When cells reached 80% confluence, activated T cells were added and co-cultured for 24 h. T cells were then removed. Orbital fibroblasts were washed twice with PBS, and the number of T cells bound to fibroblasts was counted under a light microscope.

TED mouse model

Thirty-six 6-8-week-old female Balb/c mice were randomly divided into six groups. At weeks 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14, mice received intramuscular injections in the left thigh with 50 µL of PBS (n = 6), empty adenovirus (n = 6) (OBiO, China), or adenovirus expressing the TSHR A-subunit (n = 24) (OBiO, China). The viral titer was 1 × 10⁹ PFU/mL for weeks 0–8 and 1.5 × 10⁹ PFU/mL for weeks 10–14. At weeks 12, 13, 14, and 15, mice in the treatment groups received peri-ocular injections of 10 µL of PBS, CD80-targeting aptamer, TSHR-21-42, or TSHR-CD80-1 (all at 333 nM) into the right orbit. Mice were sacrificed at week 16 for subsequent analyses.

Immunohistochemical staining

Mouse orbital tissues were paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and deparaffinized. Antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer. After washing with PBS, endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Sections were blocked with serum and incubated with primary antibodies (CD34, CD8) at 4℃ overnight. After washing, secondary antibody was applied and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Following PBS washes, sections were developed with DAB, counterstained, and observed under a microscope.

Statistical analysis

Data were processed and visualized using GraphPad Prism 9. For in vitro and clinical data following a normal distribution, comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed clinical data were analyzed using the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test. Linear regression analysis and correlation analysis were adopted to establish the relationships among the data. TCR sequencing and transcriptome sequencing data were analyzed using R. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Elevated proportions of CD8+ T cells and orbital fibroblasts in the peripheral blood of TED patients

To investigate the composition of leukocyte subsets in TED patients, this study performed flow cytometry analysis on samples from 6 TED patients and 5 healthy controls (clinical characteristics in Supplementary Table 2). The results showed that the proportion of total T cells among leukocytes was lower in the TED group than in the healthy control group. In contrast, the proportions of CD8+ T cells, B cells, and NK cells were significantly higher in TED patients (Fig. 1A). No significant differences were observed in the proportions of total T cells, CD8⁺ T cells, B cells, and NK cells between active (n = 3) and inactive TED patients (n = 3) (Fig. 1B).

Subsequently, TCR sequencing was performed on peripheral blood from 6 TED patients and 5 healthy controls, and sequence information is presented in Supplementary Table 3. Analysis of TCR functional categories revealed no significant differences between the TED and control groups (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Statistical analysis of TCR diversity, including the cumulative frequency of the top 100 TRB rearranged sequences and the D50 index, also showed no significant intergroup differences (Supplementary Fig. 1B and C). Comparison of J gene and V gene usage indicated no significant difference in J gene segments (Supplementary Fig. 1D). However, the frequency of the TRBV7-6 segment was significantly lower in the TED group (Fig. 1C). Analysis of V-J gene pairs identified 11 pairs that differed significantly between the two groups (Fig. 1D). From a total of 71,678 TCR sequences obtained, 46,051 unique sequences were present in at least one sample from each group. There were 16,994 sequences unique to the TED group and 8,633 unique to the control group. Among these, 1,124 and 403 sequences showed significantly higher or lower frequencies, respectively, in the TED group compared to controls (Fig. 1E). The top 10 sequences with frequency differences are displayed in Supplementary Fig. 1E and F.

In our previous work, an aptamer that specifically binds to TSHR was identified. To characterize its binding capacity in comparison with TSHR antibodies, this study collected peripheral blood and corresponding clinical data from 30 TED patients and 20 healthy control participants (clinical characteristics are shown in Supplementary Table 4). First, using lymphocyte subset data from routine clinical examinations, this study compared peripheral blood immune cells between active and inactive TED patients (active: n = 15; inactive: n = 8; missing data: 2 active and 5 inactive cases). No significant differences were observed in the percentages of total T cells, CD4+T cells, CD8+T cells, B cells, or NK cells between the two groups, which is consistent with our flow cytometric analysis of lymphocyte subsets (Supplementary Fig. 1G). Then this study evaluated the binding capacity of the TSHR-targeting aptamer and compared it with the initial library and TSHR antibodies. The results confirmed that the TSHR-targeting aptamer exhibits TSHR-binding capacity comparable to that of TSHR antibodies (Supplementary Fig. 1H-J).

Subsequently, the TSHR-targeting aptamer was used to probe peripheral blood samples from TED patients and healthy controls. The result showed that the proportion of TSHR+ fibroblasts among total fibroblasts was significantly higher in the TED group (Fig. 1F). Linear regression analysis confirmed a statistically significant linear relationship between the proportion of TSHR+ fibroblasts and proptosis., whereas no significant linear relationship was found between TSHR antibody levels (active: n = 15; inactive: n = 11; missing data: 2 active and 2 inactive cases) and proptosis (Fig. 1G and H). Furthermore, the proportion of TSHR+ fibroblasts was significantly higher in active TED patients (n = 17) compared to inactive patients (n = 13) and showed a positive correlation with the Clinical Activity Score (CAS) (Fig. 1I and J). In contrast, serum TSHR antibody levels did not correlate with disease activity (Fig. 1K and L). These results indicate that the proportions of CD8+ T cells and orbital fibroblasts are elevated in the peripheral blood of TED patients, and the proportion of TSHR+ fibroblasts is associated with key clinical features of TED.

CD8+ T cells approximate orbital fibroblasts and promote tissue inflammation

Prior studies suggest that TSHR+ fibroblasts can migrate from peripheral blood into orbital tissue and differentiate into CD34+ orbital fibroblasts. To investigate the distribution and interaction between CD8+ T cells and CD34+ orbital fibroblasts in TED, this study analyzed orbital adipose tissue from 12 TED patients (6 active, 6 inactive) and 2 healthy control (Supplementary Table 5). H&E staining revealed greater inflammatory infiltration and fibrotic changes in TED orbital tissue compared to controls (Fig. 2A). Inactive TED tissues exhibited more collagen deposition in adipose septa, while active TED tissues showed more severe inflammatory infiltration and disorganized cellular architecture. Oil Red O staining indicated more positively stained cells in the interstitial spaces of TED orbital fat compared to controls, with active disease demonstrating the highest level (Fig. 2B).

Immunofluorescence staining for α-SMA (a fibroblast marker) and CD34 showed higher expression of all three markers in active TED patients compared to inactive patients and controls (Fig. 2C). Quantitative analysis confirmed that the proportion and area of α-SMA+ and CD34+ cells were significantly higher in active TED tissues (Fig. 2D and E). The proportion of CD34+ orbital fibroblasts was higher in active than in inactive TED (Fig. 2F).

To explore whether the infiltration of CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, B cells and NK cells in orbital adipose tissue correlates with their corresponding peripheral blood subsets, immunohistochemical staining for CD8, CD4, CD19 and CD56 was performed on orbital adipose tissue sections (Fig. 2B; Supplementary Fig. 2A and 2B). No significant differences in the positive areas of CD8, CD4, CD19 and CD56 were observed between active TED patients (n = 5) and inactive TED patients (n = 3) (Supplementary Fig. 2C), consistent with the findings of peripheral blood lymphocyte subset analysis. Subsequent correlation analyses were conducted between the immunohistochemically positive areas of CD8, CD4, CD19, CD56 in orbital tissue and the proportions of corresponding lymphocyte subsets in peripheral blood. Positive correlations were identified between CD8+ positive area and peripheral CD8+ T cell proportion, as well as between CD19 positive area and peripheral B-cell proportion (Supplementary Fig. 2D). By contrast, no significant correlations existed between CD4 positive area and peripheral CD4+ T cells or between CD56 positive area and peripheral NK cells. Collectively, these data indicate that peripheral blood lymphocyte subsets partially reflect the inflammatory infiltration status within orbital adipose tissue.

This study further focused on the tissue infiltration of CD8+ T cells. The results demonstrated that The distribution of CD8+ T cells varied within the tissue: their density within fat lobules was significantly higher in active TED, whereas no significant difference was found in interstitial or perivascular areas (Fig. 2G). Furthermore, the numbers of CD34+ and CD8+ cells showed a significant positive linear correlation (Fig. 2H). Measurement of the distance between CD34+ orbital fibroblasts and CD8+ T cells revealed that these cells were located significantly closer to each other in tissues from active TED patients (Fig. 2I and J). Thus, CD8+ T cells and CD34+ orbital fibroblasts are in closer spatial proximity in active TED orbital tissue, suggesting their interaction modulates disease activity.

Construction and characterization of a bispecific TSHR/CD80 aptamer

T cell activation requires engagement of the T cell receptor (TCR; signal 1) and co-stimulatory molecules (signal 2). Orbital fibroblasts in TED can function as antigen-presenting cells. TSHR, highly expressed on these cells, can provide signal 1, while co-stimulatory factors like CD80 provide signal 2. To simultaneously block both signals and disrupt the interaction between CD8+ T cells and orbital fibroblasts, this study designed a bispecific aptamer targeting TSHR and CD80. This was based on our previously selected TSHR-targeting aptamer (TSHR-21-42) and a published CD80-targeting aptamer. First, this study isolated and characterized primary orbital fibroblasts (Supplementary Fig. 3) and confirmed that TSHR-21-42 could inhibit adipogenesis, fibrosis, and hyaluronic acid secretion in preliminary experiments (Supplementary Fig. 4).

This study designed three bispecific aptamer constructs (TSHR-CD80-1, -2, -3) using different linkers (Fig. 3A). Binding assays showed that TSHR-CD80-1 had a higher binding affinity for orbital fibroblasts than TSHR-CD80-2 or TSHR-CD80-3 (Fig. 3B and C). Serum stability tests indicated that TSHR-CD80-1 and TSHR-CD80-2 degraded at similar rates, both significantly slower than TSHR-CD80-3 (Fig. 3D). To confirm dual target engagement, this study performed a pull-down assay using the aptamers and orbital fibroblast lysates, followed by western blot for TSHR and CD80. All three bispecific aptamers bound both targets. The binding efficacy of TSHR-CD80-1 and TSHR-CD80-2 was comparable to that of the individual TSHR-21-42 and CD80-specific aptamers (Fig. 3E). Based on these results, TSHR-CD80-1 was selected for further study. Furthermore, TSHR-CD80-1 was capable of competing with anti-TSHR and anti-CD80 antibodies for binding sites. After orbital fibroblasts were incubated with anti-TSHR and/or anti-CD80 antibodies and subsequently treated with fluorescently labeled TSHR-CD80-1, no significant reduction in fluorescence intensity was observed compared with the control group (Fig. 3F). Its equilibrium dissociation constant (Kd) for binding orbital fibroblasts was determined to be 78.56 nM (Fig. 3G). Molecular docking simulations predicted that TSHR-CD80-1 could form hydrogen bonds with both TSHR and CD80. The TSHR-21-42 moiety was primarily responsible for TSHR binding, while the CD80-specific aptamer moiety bound CD80 (Fig. 3H). This study also confirmed that TSHR-CD80-1 could bind TSHR and CD80 in mouse tissues (Fig. 3I). In summary, TSHR-CD80-1, constructed by direct phosphodiester linkage of the two aptamer moieties, is a stable bispecific aptamer that retains the ability to bind both TSHR and CD80 on orbital fibroblasts.

TSHR-CD80-1 inhibits the interaction between CD8+T cells and orbital fibroblasts

This study investigated whether TSHR-CD80-1 could inhibit the binding of CD8+ T cells to orbital fibroblasts. Non-activated or pre-activated CD8+ T cells were co-cultured with orbital fibroblasts in the presence or absence of TSHR-CD80-1 (Fig. 4A). TSHR-CD80-1 significantly inhibited the binding of non-activated CD8+ T cells to fibroblasts but had no effect on the binding of pre-activated cells (Fig. 4B). CCK-8 assays showed that TSHR-CD80-1 alone did not significantly affect the proliferation of either non-activated or activated CD8+ T cells (Fig. 4C). Orbital fibroblasts promoted the proliferation of non-activated CD8+ T cells, an effect that was suppressed by TSHR-CD80-1. Fibroblasts did not promote the proliferation of pre-activated T cells (Fig. 4D). Conversely, non-activated T cells did not affect fibroblast proliferation, whereas activated T cells significantly promoted it. This pro-proliferative effect of activated T cells on fibroblasts was inhibited by TSHR-CD80-1 (Fig. 4E and F). Furthermore, neither non-activated nor activated CD8+ T cells, regardless of TSHR-CD80-1 treatment, induced CD34 expression in orbital fibroblasts (Fig. 4G). Therefore, TSHR-CD80-1 inhibits the binding of non-activated CD8+ T cells to orbital fibroblasts and disrupts the mutually promotive interaction between activated CD8+ T cells and orbital fibroblasts.

TSHR-CD80-1 retains the ability of TSHR-21-42 to suppress pathological phenotypes in orbital fibroblasts

Given the structural differences between TSHR-21-42 and the bispecific TSHR-CD80-1, this study compared their functional effects on orbital fibroblasts. A CCK-8 assay established 500 nM as the effective concentration for both aptamers in subsequent experiments (Fig. 5A). Immunofluorescence staining and qPCR showed that TSHR-CD80-1, similar to TSHR-21-42, inhibited the expression of vimentin, indicating suppression of fibroblast activation (Fig. 5B and C). During adipogenic induction, both aptamers effectively inhibited lipid droplet formation (Fig. 5D), reducing both the size of lipid droplets and the percentage of Oil Red O+ area to a similar extent (Fig. 5E and F). At the mRNA level, both aptamers suppressed C/EBPα but not PPARγ or FABP4 (Fig. 5G). However, at the protein level, both significantly inhibited the expression of PPARγ, C/EBPα, and FABP4 (Fig. 5H and I). TSHR-CD80-1 also inhibited hyaluronic acid secretion (Fig. 5J) and downregulated the mRNA levels of HAS1 and HAS3, comparable to TSHR-21-42 (Fig. 5K). Furthermore, TSHR-CD80-1 suppressed fibrosis, as evidenced by reduced protein levels of collagen I and α-SMA (Fig. 5L), although their mRNA levels were not significantly altered (Fig. 5M). In summary, TSHR-CD80-1 retains the capacity of TSHR-21-42 to inhibit adipogenic differentiation, hyaluronic acid secretion, and collagen production in orbital fibroblasts.

In vivo efficacy of TSHR-CD80-1 in a TED mouse model

To evaluate the therapeutic potential in vivo, this study established a TED mouse model via intramuscular injections of an adenovirus expressing the TSHR A-subunit. After eight injections, ocular symptoms developed. Mice then received four peri-ocular injections of the aptamer therapeutics (Fig. 6A). Body weight increased over time in all groups without significant intergroup differences (Fig. 6B and C). At the endpoint, flow cytometry using TSHR-CD80-1 as a probe revealed a significantly higher number of bound leukocytes in the TED (PBS) group compared to the PBS control and empty virus groups. Treatment with the CD80-specific aptamer or TSHR-21-42 did not alter this number, whereas treatment with TSHR-CD80-1 significantly reduced it to a level comparable to controls (Fig. 6D). Thyroid size was significantly larger in the TED (PBS) group but normalized after TSHR-CD80-1 treatment (Fig. 6E and F). Macroscopic examination showed that TED (PBS) mice exhibited eyelid congestion, edema, reduced palpebral fissure height, and disheveled periorbital hair. These symptoms were markedly alleviated by TSHR-CD80-1 treatment (Fig. 6G). Histologically, TSHR-CD80-1 treatment reduced the expression of CD34 and CD8 in orbital tissues (Fig. 6H). H&E and Masson’s trichrome staining further demonstrated that TSHR-CD80-1 treatment attenuated retro-orbital tissue fibrosis, necrosis, and inflammatory changes (Fig. 6I). No significant pathological changes were observed in major visceral organs following TSHR-CD80-1 treatment (Fig. 6H). In conclusion, TSHR-CD80-1 controls systemic inflammation and improves thyroid pathology in TED mice. Locally, it reduces the infiltration of CD8+ T cells into the orbit, diminishes their interaction with CD34+ orbital fibroblasts, and thereby alleviates orbital tissue fibrosis and inflammation.

Discussion

Thyroid eye disease (TED) is an autoimmune disorder characterized by multiple pathological alterations in orbital tissues, with adipogenesis, excessive hyaluronic acid secretion, and fibrosis being the most prominent and extensively studied phenotypes [16, 17]. Given the complexity of TED pathogenesis, orbital fibroblasts play multifaceted roles within the orbital microenvironment and engage in intricate communication with neighboring cells, particularly T cells [18]. Therefore, this study began by profiling T cell characteristics in TED patients, including peripheral T cell subset distribution and T cell receptor (TCR) repertoire features, confirmed that TRBV7-6 is downregulated in TED. However, few studies have explored its exact mechanisms underlying TED pathogenesis. Referring to the reported roles of other TRBV genes in autoimmune diseases, this study speculate that the altered expression of TRBV7-6 is part of the changes in the TCR repertoire during TED progression and may be involved in the recognition of autoantigens by T cells. In addition, this analysis confirmed a significant elevation of CD8⁺ T cells in the peripheral blood of TED patients.

Our previous work identified a TSHR-targeting nucleic acid aptamer, TSHR-21-42, and characterized its binding affinity. However, its therapeutic potential remained unexplored. In the current study, this study employed TSHR-21-42 to probe peripheral blood from TED patients and demonstrated that TSHR levels detected by the aptamer correlate with clinical disease activity. This finding suggests that TSHR-21-42, by targeting TSHR, holds therapeutic promise for TED. Indeed, prior studies have highlighted the role of TSHR in evaluating TED manifestations, but they primarily utilized TSHR antibodies for detection—a method associated with high cost and batch-to-batch variability [19, 20]. Our aptamer-based approach offers a significant economic advantage, eliminates batch effects, and reduces the potential cost for clinical application.

This study further investigated the therapeutic effects of aptamers on TED. This study found that TSHR-CD80-1 could not only inhibit adipogenesis of orbital fibroblasts, but also alleviate fibrotic alterations. Nevertheless, the trends of changes in mRNA transcription levels were not fully consistent with those of protein levels for PPARγ, FABP4, Collagen I and α-SMA. This discrepancy may be attributed to the mismatched time windows for the expression peaks of mRNA and protein (temporal lag). Furthermore, post-transcriptional regulation of RNA and post-translational regulation of proteins may also lead to such expression changes. Furthermore, this study also examined the infiltration of CD8-positive T cells in orbital tissues and their spatial distribution relative to orbital fibroblasts, especially the CD34+ subset. Previous research indicates that CD34+ orbital fibroblasts are a key pathogenic subpopulation, originating from bone marrow-derived fibrocytes that migrate via the bloodstream to orbital tissue, expressing high levels of TSHR on their surface [21, 22].

Existing literature demonstrates that orbital fibroblasts can function as antigen-presenting cells. Following T cell receptor (TCR) engagement with presented antigen, full T cell activation requires a second signal, such as CD80 or CD86 [23, 24]. To disrupt the interaction between T cells and orbital fibroblasts, this study designed a bispecific aptamer by conjugating a CD80-targeting aptamer with TSHR-21-42. This TSHR/CD80 bispecific aptamer, TSHR-CD80-1, was shown not only to retain the functional properties of TSHR-21-42 but also to effectively block the binding between orbital fibroblasts and T cells, particularly naive CD8-positive T cells. These findings suggest that TSHR-CD80-1 may exert its effects at the early stage of TED, prior to robust T cell infiltration and the onset of orbital inflammation. Accordingly, future studies may focus on early administration or prophylactic application of TSHR-CD80-1 for TED treatment. Initially, this study considered the possibility that structural changes upon conjugation might alter target binding. However, our results indicate that the bispecific aptamer successfully preserves the functions of its individual components. This programmability—the ability to be edited or conjugated with other molecules to achieve complex functionalities—represents a key advantage of aptamers over monoclonal antibodies, as supported by other studies [25, 26].

To validate the therapeutic efficacy of TSHR-CD80-1, this study employed a TED mouse model. Common methods for inducing TED in animals involve injection of plasmids or adenoviruses encoding the TSHR A-subunit [27, 28]. Our model utilized intramuscular injections of a TSHR A-subunit-encoding adenovirus. However, unlike many protocols, our injections were administered biweekly at doses of 5 × 10⁷ or 7.5 × 10⁷ PFU, which are lower than those typically reported [29]. This adjustment was made to balance experimental costs and to mitigate potential mortality associated with high viral loads. This study compared the severity of ocular symptoms in mice with relevant TED mouse models reported in other literature. No reduction was observed in either the severity of ocular manifestations or the proportion of mice presenting typical symptoms. These results indicate that the viral dose and injection frequency adopted in this study are sufficient for establishing the TED mouse model. The aptamer was delivered via peri-ocular injection, chosen to minimize systemic degradation, target the orbital site of pathology, and for its relative technical ease compared to tail vein injection. Subsequent experiments showed similar disease severity in both eyes of individual mice, and differences in thyroid size and color among treatment groups suggested that the peri-ocularly administered aptamer could enter the systemic circulation to exert effects at distant sites.

Our study has several limitations. First, while this study constructed TSHR-CD80-1 to block the interaction between orbital fibroblasts and T cells, constraints in CD8⁺ T cell availability and research costs limited our functional assays primarily to proliferation. This study did not assess changes in the expression profiles of key inflammatory cytokines from either cell type. Second, the animal experiments evaluated the therapeutic effect of TSHR-CD80-1 only after the onset of ocular signs, thus not addressing its potential in disease prevention. Furthermore, the efficacy of TSHR-CD80-1 was not directly compared with first-line TED therapy (e.g., glucocorticoids). Third, due to constraints of cost and time, this study evaluated only TSHR-21-42 for assessing patient status and did not test TSHR-CD80-1 on peripheral blood from TED patients to explore its potential as a clinical detection tool.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that an elevated proportion of CD8⁺ T cells and their interaction with orbital fibroblasts contribute to TED pathogenesis. The bispecific aptamer TSHR-CD80-1, developed by conjugating TSHR- and CD80-targeting aptamers, effectively inhibits CD8⁺ T cell infiltration into orbital tissue and their crosstalk with orbital fibroblasts. Simultaneously, it suppresses pathological phenotypic changes in orbital fibroblasts, thereby ameliorating TED.
