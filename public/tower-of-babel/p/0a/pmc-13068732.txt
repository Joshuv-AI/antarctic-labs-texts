Emergence and Genomic Profiling of Multidrug-Resistant Candida auris Isolated from Intensive Care Unit Patients in a Malaysian Teaching Hospital
Authors: Nishanthinie Thatchanamoorthy, Rukumani Devi Velayuthan, Chandramathi Samudi Raju, Subha Bhassu, Alexandria Sonia Karajacob, Sasheela Ponnampalavanar, Mohan Rao, Sun Tee Tay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCandida auris, a significant pathogen causing invasive candidiasis with high mortality rates and frequent hospital outbreaks, is understudied in Malaysia. This study retrospectively examined invasive candidiasis cases at Universiti Malaya Medical Centre from 2018 to 2021. Four C. auris strains were isolated from blood and peritoneal samples of intensive care unit patients and identified using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). Antifungal susceptibility testing using Sensititre™ YeastOne YO10 plate showed resistance to amphotericin B (2–4 µg/ml) and fluconazole (256 µg/ml) but susceptibility to echinocandins. Whole-genome sequencing revealed a 12.31 Mb genome, 45.59% G + C content, 99.95–99.99% average nucleotide identity between the isolates, and 190–225 single nucleotide polymorphisms. The Malaysian strains were phylogenetically related to the South Asian Clade (I) and harbored mutations associated with resistance to fluconazole (ERG11 gene: Y132F; CDR1 gene: E709D) and amphotericin B (ERG2 gene: E39D). The first genomic characterization of multidrug-resistant C. auris in Malaysian ICU patients highlights the urgent need for enhanced infection control measures in healthcare settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11046-026-01059-4.Keywords: Candida auris, Whole genome sequencing, Antifungal resistance, Malaysia

Introduction

Candida auris is an emerging yeast pathogen known for its multidrug resistance and potential to cause nosocomial outbreaks. It was first identified in 2009 in Japan [1]. C. auris infections are associated with high mortality rates, particularly among critically ill intensive care unit (ICU) patients, who were exposed to broad-spectrum antibiotics and/or antifungals, had multiple comorbidities, and underwent invasive medical procedures [2, 3]. Given these clinical challenges, understanding the genetic diversity and transmission patterns of C. auris has become increasingly important.

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) is the gold standard for assessing the clonality of fungal isolates, providing detailed genetic relationships among strains from epidemiologically linked cases [4]. WGS studies have identified four major C. auris clades (South Asian, East Asian, African, and South American) each separated by thousands of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). In addition, the recently reported fifth clade in Iran differs from the other clades by approximately 200,000 SNPs, while a sixth clade in the Indomalayan zone shows at least 37,000 SNP differences [5–8]. C. auris also exhibits high levels of resistance to antifungal agents, including fluconazole and amphotericin B, with resistance mechanisms involving mutations in antifungal resistance target genes and increased drug efflux [9, 10]. Common resistance mutations include Y132F and K143R in the ERG11 gene, associated with azole resistance and E39D in the ERG2 gene, associated with amphotericin B resistance [11, 12]. As WGS also enables the detection of resistance-associated mutations, integrating genomic and susceptibility data is crucial for understanding local resistance patterns.

Despite these global insights into resistance mechanisms, population structure, and genetic characteristics, data on C. auris in Malaysia remain limited. The first reported case was from a hospital in northern Malaysia, where an elderly patient with neutropenia was diagnosed with a C. auris infection [9]. The ITS sequence of this strain (GenBank accession no. KP326583) closely resembled Brazilian and South African strains, sharing 99–100% sequence identity with those reported by Prakash et al. [13]. This study addresses significant gaps in understanding the population structure, genetic characteristics, and antifungal susceptibility of C. auris strains in Malaysia, particularly at the Universiti Malaya Medical Centre (UMMC). By providing in-depth epidemiological data, genomic insights, and antifungal susceptibility profile, the study aims to enhance infection prevention and control measures and guide treatment strategies for C. auris infections. The accurate detection and comprehensive analysis of C. auris are vital for effective patient management and guiding therapeutic decisions.

This study reveals a 4.9% isolation rate of C. auris among Candida species from sterile sterile site specimens samples, with C. auris constituting 3.7% of candidemia cases at UMMC from 2018–2021. Genetic analysis through WGS revealed that Malaysian C. auris strains are closely related to the strains from South Asian clade I, suggesting geographic influences on pathogen evolution. Despite high nucleotide identity, inter-strain variability is evident, with at least 190 SNPs detected among the strains. Antifungal susceptibility testing revealed elevated minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for fluconazole and amphotericin B, with resistance-associated mutations identified in key antifungal resistance target genes.

Materials and Methods

Retrospective Analysis of Invasive C. auris Infection Among Intensive Care Patients

A retrospective observational study on invasive infections, particularly those caused by C. auris, among ICU patients was conducted at UMMC over a four-year period (January 2018–December 2021). Inclusion criteria included ICU patients with at least one positive Candida culture from sterile sites, such as blood, peritoneal fluid, pleural fluid, vitreous fluid, cerebrospinal fluid, bile fluid, joint fluid, and pericardial fluid. Patients with positive Candida cultures from non-sterile sites were excluded. All patient and laboratory data were collected from the hospital's electronic information system. The recorded data included patient age, sample collection date, specimen type, and isolated organism. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Medical Research Ethics Committee, UMMC (MRECID no: 2021512-10132).

Identification of Clinical C. auris

C. auris identification was performed using the VITEK® MS system with IVD library version 3.2 (bioMérieux, France). All C. auris strains designated as C021–C024 were subcultured on Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) (Difco, USA) for 24 h at 37 °C prior to identification. Briefly, a single C. auris colony was smeared onto a VITEK MS target plate and overlaid with 0.5 µL of formic acid (FA) (bioMérieux, France). After air drying at 28 °C, 1 µL of a matrix solution containing α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid (CHCA) (bioMérieux, France) was applied and left to dry. The plate was then inserted into the Vitek MS system, with Escherichia coli ATCC 8739 serving as a control strain. Identification was based on comparison of the colony spectra with the reference spectra stored in the database.

DNA Extraction and Sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted using the MasterPure™ Yeast DNA Purification Kit (Lucigen, USA) for downstream analysis. A 30 µL aliquot of each DNA sample was sent to a commercial facility (Novogene, China) for Illumina sequencing. The draft genome sequences of the strains were deposited in the GenBank database under BioProject ID: PRJNA882827.

Whole Genome Analysis

The quality of raw reads was assessed using FastQC version 0.11.5. Adapter trimming and removal of reads shorter than 50 base pairs were performed using Fastp version 0.23.0 with default parameters. K-mer length estimation was performed using Kmergenie version 1.7051 with default parameters. De novo assembly into scaffolds was performed using SPAdes version 3.15.3 with default parameters, and assembly gaps were closed using GapFiller version 1.10 [14]. Repetitive sequences were masked using RepeatMasker version 4.0.7 with fungal specific parameters, followed by gene prediction using Augustus version 3.4.0, with Candida albicans as the reference species. Functional annotation was conducted using eggNOG-mapper version 2 based on the Cluster of Orthologous Groups of proteins (COGs) database with default parameters [15]. The average nucleotide identity (ANI), a quantitative metric for genome similarity, was computed using the “Create Average Nucleotide Identity” tool within CLC Genomics Workbench version 22 (Qiagen, Germany) with default settings [16]. SNP variants were annotated using SnpEff version 5.1d with default parameters. The identified SNPs were filtered using the following criteria: minimum depth of 10, minimum relative depth of 10, minimum SNP quality of 30, and a Z-score exceeding 1.96 [17]. Comparative genomic analysis was performed using OrthoFinder version 2.3.1 [18] and RAxML version 8.2.12 for phylogenetic inference [19] with default parameters. Representative isolates from each clade were included in the comparative genomic analysis, as shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Antifungal Susceptibility Testing

Antifungal susceptibility testing was conducted using the Sensititre™ YeastOne YO10 plate method (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) against nine antifungal agents: fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole, amphotericin B, 5-flucytosine, anidulafungin, micafungin, and caspofungin. Single colonies from a 24-h C. auris pure culture were emulsified in demineralized water. The turbidity of the suspension was adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland standard at a wavelength of 530 nm. Twenty microliters of the suspension were added to 11 mL of Sensititre™ YeastOne broth (Fisher Scientific, USA) to achieve a final inoculum concentration of 1.5–8 × 103 CFU/mL. The Sensititre™ YeastOne YO10 plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was inoculated by transferring 100 µL of the final yeast suspension into each well. The inoculated plate was incubated at 37 °C, and the MIC readings were recorded after 24 h. Antifungal resistance was interpreted using tentative MIC breakpoints recommended by the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC): fluconazole (≥ 32 µg/mL), amphotericin B (≥ 2 µg/mL), micafungin (≥ 4 µg/mL), caspofungin (≥ 2 µg/mL), and anidulafungin (≥ 4 µg/mL) [20]. MIC breakpoints for itraconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole, and 5-flucytosine have not yet been established.

Resistance Mutation Analysis

Mutation analysis of antifungal resistance genes associated with fluconazole and amphotericin B resistance (ERG11, CDR1, ERG2, ERG3, and FLO8) was performed in CLC Genomics Workbench version 22 (Qiagen, Germany). Reference sequences of resistance-associated genes were retrieved from the NCBI protein database using free-text queries such as “(fungi[filter] AND Candida[All Fields]) OR (txid498019[Organism:noexp]) AND ‘Gene Name’[all fields]”, where 'Gene Name' was replaced with each target gene. The resulting protein sequences were downloaded, and incomplete or unrelated entries were excluded. The selected protein sequences were used as queries in tBLASTn to identify the closest homolog in each strain. Protein sequences from each tBLASTn result were extracted, and aligned using multiple sequence alignment for each resistance gene family. Non-synonymous mutations were identified using BioEdit version 7.2.

Functional Analysis

Functional analysis of the Malaysian C. auris genomes identified proteins categorized into three primary functional groups: information storage and processing, cellular processes and signaling, and metabolism (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, a substantial proportion of proteins (22.94%) remain poorly characterized, and their roles in C. auris are not yet fully understood.

Comparative Genomic Analysis

Comparative genomic analysis based on concatenated alignment of 3,384 orthologous genes from four Malaysian strains and 24 reference strains indicated that the Malaysian C. auris strains are phylogenetically related to South Asian clade I (Fig. 1). All four Malaysian strains closely clustered with Clade I strains from Germany, Italy, Brazil, India, Pakistan, and Lebanon (Fig. 1).

Antifungal Resistance Mutation Analysis

Mutation analysis of resistance genes confirmed the antifungal resistance profile of the Malaysian C. auris strains (Table 4). Fluconazole resistance was associated with mutations in the ERG11 gene (Y132F) and the CDR1 gene (E709D) (Fig. 2). Additionally, a novel amino acid substitution, G1414E, was identified in the CDR1 gene (Fig. 2). Amphotericin B resistance was linked to a mutation in the ERG2 gene (E39D) (Fig. 2). However, no mutations were detected in the ERG3 and FLO8 genes, which are also associated with amphotericin B resistance in C. auris strains.

Discussion

C. auris has recently been recognized as a significant global cause of invasive fungal infections [21]. This study documented the occurrence of 82 invasive Candida infections among Malaysian ICU patients over a 4-year period (January 2018 - December 2021), including four cases of C. auris infection. Notably, all C. auris cases occurred in 2018, with no further isolates detected in subsequent years. This suggests that C. auris was present only during that period and did not persist in the ICU setting. The continued detection of other Candida species throughout the study period indicates that the absence of C. auris in later years was not due to limitations in surveillance or diagnostic practices. The absence of C. auris in subsequent years is likely the result of enhanced infection control measures, which prevented further transmission.

In this study, C. auris infection predominantly affected elderly individuals (64–81 years old). This may be attributed to the presence of multiple risk factors and underlying debilitating illnesses in older patients compared to younger patients, as reported in previous studies [22–24]. All four patients in this study were immunocompromised due to prolonged ICU stays and chronic underlying diseases. A retrospective study conducted in Indian healthcare facilities identified ICU stays and central venous catheterization as significant risk factors for C. auris candidemia [23]. Patients undergoing invasive medical interventions, such as placement of ventilators and catheters, are at increased risk of developing C. auris candidemia. These catheters can provide a pathway for the pathogen to infiltrate the bloodstream, facilitating the development of candidemia [25].

This is the first study to use WGS to provide insights into the genomes of C. auris strains in Malaysia. The assembled genomes had an average size of 12.31 Mb, with a mean G + C content of 45.59%. On average, 5,018 genes and 4,304 functional proteins were identified across the strains. These genomic details are consistent with previous findings by Munoz et al. [26].

The functional analysis of proteins provided insights into the roles of various proteins within the Malaysian C. auris strains. The distribution of proteins across functional categories highlights how these strains regulate cellular activities and respond to their environment. A significant proportion (15.86%) of the C. auris genome remains unannotated. However, 30.34% of the genome was successfully annotated to encode proteins involved in cellular processes and signaling pathways. This was followed by the annotation of proteins involved in metabolic processes (28.04%). The high similarity in protein profiles between the Malaysian strains and the reference strain B11220 highlights the conservation of essential cellular functions across different isolates. This shared core of cellular machinery suggests that despite genetic variations, the fundamental biological processes remain largely consistent. These conserved functions may serve as promising targets for therapeutic intervention, enabling the development of more effective and broadly applicable therapeutic strategies against C. auris infection.

WGS is widely recognized as the gold standard for determining the clonality of fungal strains due to its robustness and high discriminatory power [4]. As the first WGS based molecular investigation of C. auris in Malaysia, this study provides valuable insights into the population structure of the strains in the region. The comparative genomic analysis demonstrated that the Malaysian C. auris strains clustered within Clade I, also known as the South Asian clade, one of the six globally recognized clades. Notably, Malaysian strains formed a distinct subclade closely related to Clade I reference strains from other countries, including Germany, Italy, Brazil, India, Pakistan, and Lebanon. This clustering pattern suggests a possible South Asian origin for the Malaysian strains, given their close phylogenetic relationship to Clade I strains. While the genomic similarity may reflect a single introduction followed by local transmission, the presence of closely related but non-identical strains also suggest the possibility of multiple introductions. However, due to the lack of epidemiological data, this study could not determine the exact transmission dynamics or routes of introduction of C. auris strains in Malaysia. In contrast, the Malaysian strains were phylogenetically distinct from Clades II to VI, including the recently described Clade VI, which comprises strains from Singapore and Bangladesh. Despite the geographical proximity of Clade VI strains, the Malaysian strains did not cluster within this clade, suggesting they belong to a distinct lineage. The clade distribution pattern observed in this study is consistent with the findings from a previous WGS analysis conducted by Lockhart et al. [27], which identified four major distinct clades, associated with different geographic regions: South Asia (India/Pakistan), South Africa, South America (Venezuela), and East Asia (Japan). The geographical clustering of C. auris strains in these distinct clades, separated by tens of thousands of SNPs, supports the idea that the distribution of clades is influenced by geographical factors. The genetic relatedness among strains from different regions may reflect independent introductions and regional transmission patterns of C. auris lineages across different parts of the world.

The high ANI of over 99% among the Malaysian C. auris strains indicated a strong genetic similarity among the strains. However, despite this high ANI, the presence of SNP differences ranging from 190 – 225 among the strains suggested the presence of inter-strain variability from a single tertiary hospital in Malaysia. This level of divergence may reflect microevolution within the ICU environment, where minor genetic changes accumulate over time due to selective pressures such as antifungal exposure, host immune responses, and environmental persistence. These microevolutionary processes can result in genetic divergence even within a localized outbreak, as previously reported in global C. auris outbreaks. Whole-genome studies have documented SNP level evolution among clonally related isolates, supporting the possibility that the strains in the present study may have originated from a common source but subsequently diverged under ICU specific selective pressures.

In a study conducted by Lockhart et al. [27], two strains from Pakistan displayed over 600 SNP differences compared to other South Asian clade strains, despite belonging to the same clade. Similarly, Hamprecht et al. [28] reported that strains from Germany, although clustered within the same South Asian clade, exhibited SNP differences ranging from 30 to 800. These findings emphasize that C. auris strains within the same clade can still exhibit significant genetic variability. This variability is important to recognize as it may have implications for the virulence, drug resistance, and other phenotypic characteristics of C. auris. Even within the same clade, strains may display diverse genomic characteristics that influence their behavior and response to treatment.

The resistance of C. auris to clinically available antifungal drugs remain a major concern in healthcare settings. In this study, all Malaysian strains exhibited elevated MICs for fluconazole and amphotericin B. However, there was a notable exception of a single Malaysian strain isolated in 2017 and tested using E-test method, which exhibited multidrug resistance, including caspofungin, itraconazole, voriconazole, amphotericin B, and fluconazole [9]. Overall, C. auris strains, including Malaysian strains in this study, consistently displayed high levels of fluconazole resistance. However, their susceptibility to echinocandins and amphotericin B varies, posing significant challenges in managing C. auris infections. Notably, strains of C. auris from South Asian countries such as India [22, 29–31], Pakistan [32, 33], and Bangladesh [34] frequently demonstrated resistance to amphotericin B and fluconazole. Susceptibility to echinocandins, however, appears to differ by geographic regions [35]. For instance, approximately 5% of USA strains exhibit echinocandin resistance, whereas this resistance is relatively rare among Indian strains, with fewer than 2% of strains demonstrating resistance [11].

The resistance mutation analysis provided a genetic basis for the observed antifungal resistance patterns of the Malaysian strains. These strains exhibited resistance to fluconazole and amphotericin B, which was confirmed by the presence of specific mutations in the ERG11, CDR1, and ERG2 genes. The Y132F mutation in the ERG11 gene has been widely associated with fluconazole resistance in C. auris strains from South Asian regions [21]. Similarly, the E709D mutation in the CDR1 gene has been previously reported in azole-resistant strains, including those within the South Asian clade [36]. For amphotericin B resistance, a point mutation (E39D) in the ERG2 gene was observed in all amphotericin B-resistant Malaysian strains. This mutation has also been identified in amphotericin B-resistant strains from India [12]. Additionally, a novel amino acid substitution, G1414E, was detected in the CDR1 gene. While this mutation may have potential implications for antifungal resistance or other cellular functions, its functional relevance remains uncertain without experimental validation. Therefore, further studies are needed to determine its biological significance.

In conclusion, this 4-year retrospective study at a Malaysian teaching hospital identified a 4.9% incidence of invasive C. auris infections. The Malaysian strains were classified within the South Asian clade (I) and exhibited resistance to fluconazole and amphotericin B, while remaining susceptible to echinocandins. This study offers valuable insights into the genomics of Malaysian C. auris strains and highlights their antifungal resistance profile. However, as the findings are based on specimens from a single hospital, a larger multi-center study could offer a broader perspective on C. auris epidemiology in Malaysia. Despite the limited sample size, our study provides important clinical and genetic insights into C. auris and its treatment in Malaysia, addressing a critical research gap. Since C. auris is an emerging hospital-acquired pathogen, it is crucial to investigate the factors driving its emergence and to implement stricter infection control measures in Malaysian healthcare facilities to prevent future outbreaks.
