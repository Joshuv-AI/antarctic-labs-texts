Activation of the Snail transcription factor induces Mdm2 gene expression
Authors: Alexander R. Mabry, James Gorman, Juan S. Delvasto, Andrew R. Lavik, Justin H. Layer, Lindsey D. Mayo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Epithelial-like tumor cells can become metastatic by undergoing molecular and phenotypic reprogramming in a process referred to as epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT). In response to EMT genes that promote migration and condition the tumor microenvironment to permit intravasation into the bloodstream, dissemination and extravasation into new organs are induced. While the mutant p53 has been implicated in extravasation, one negative regulator of p53, the oncogene murine double minute-2 gene (Mdm2), is required in the early stages of metastasis and the driver of EMT. This activity is independent of Mdm2’s role in the p53-Mdm2 autoregulatory feedback loop. Herein, we examine the EMT transcription factor Snail as a downstream effector of kinase signaling pathways. We show that the activation of upstream receptors and KRas signaling increase Snail levels. Snail binds to Ebox DNA motifs, and Mdm2 has two Ebox DNA-binding domains in the second promoter. Snail binds to the second Ebox and induces Mdm2 gene expression. Knockdown of endogenous Snail by shRNA shows a decrease in Mdm2 and is associated with reduced migration. The reintroduction of Mdm2 in shSnail cells restores cellular migration. These data integrate upstream pathways that induce Snail-Mdm2 to promote the metastasis of tumor cells.

The murine double minute-2 gene (Mdm2) was discovered on a double minute chromosome in murine cells, and mouse cells were transformed in combination with HaRas (1). Mdm2 can transform primary human cells with additional genetic manipulations (2). These data categorize Mdm2 as an oncogene. In human cancer, Mdm2 is associated with regulating DNA repair and replication, gene expression, cell proliferation, survival, metabolism, and metastasis (3, 4, 5, 6). Its most reported function is the interplay with the tumor suppressor p53. However, these functions of Mdm2 may be associated with a response to a specific stimulus. For example, at the conclusion of DNA repair, p53 induces the Mdm2 gene, and the subsequent increase in Mdm2 protein levels is associated with the conjugation of ubiquitin to p53 and export out of the nucleus (7, 8, 9). Cytoplasmic p53 is polyubiquitinated by an E4 ligase and destroyed by the proteasome (10). This interplay is specific to DNA damage, yet in response to growth conditions or cytokines, Mdm2 gene expression is induced by other transcription factors independent of p53 (11, 12, 13, 14). Increased Mdm2 protein can be in a complex with many proteins. Depending on the specific signal transduction pathway, Mdm2 can function to recruit the ubiquitin complex, neddylation complex, or sumoylation complex (15, 16, 17), which can regulate protein stability or cellular compartmentalization. Mdm2 gene expression and protein regulation are more complex than the canonical p53-Mdm2 interplay.

There are specific stages of metastasis; invasion, intravasation, extravasation, metastatic foci formation. Mdm2 is required for invasion, induction of angiogenesis, and intravasation (4, 11, 18). Epithelial cells may reprogram molecularly to transition to mesenchymal-like cells to become metastatic. Mdm2 is integrated into the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) in response to several EMT-inducing stimuli (19). These data were further supported in vivo by xenograft models. Several key extrinsic factors (cytokines or growth factors, oxygen levels, and vasculature) are necessary for programming cells to become metastatic. Mdm2 is required for the early stages of metastasis, yet how the intrinsic signaling pathways activate Mdm2 gene expression is poorly defined. While migration and EMT programs can be activated in the presence of WT or mutant p53, suggesting the induction of Mdm2 gene expression is not integrated into the p53 pathway.

Several transcription factors are involved in EMT by regulating the genes that code for gap-junction proteins necessary for cell–cell contacts (20). Herein, we provide a pathway whereby the transcription factor, Snail, induces Mdm2 gene expression. Snail binds to an E-box DNA-binding element in the second promoter of Mdm2. Several upstream kinases can activate Snail, and we show that TGFβ1 and K-Ras/Snail pathway signals for Mdm2 gene induction. For EMT programming, our data show that cells can use different pathways to induce a common oncogene, Mdm2, which is necessary for cell migration.

Results

Mdm2 increases tumor cell migration

We have reported that migration in vitro (via different assays) and in vivo metastasis (in two fat pads to lung metastasis models) were disrupted with the loss of Mdm2 (4). To provide additional experimental evidence for the role of Mdm2 in migration and metastasis, we elevated Mdm2 in three cell lines (TMD231, H226, and H3255) via viral transduction. All three cell lines are mutant for the tumor suppressor p53 and show that increasing Mdm2 levels increased the migration of cells (Fig. 1, A–C) relative to viral transduced controls. These data confirm that cell migration is increased with elevated Mdm2, and our previous work shows that diminished Mdm2 decreases migration (4). In addition, TMD231 control and Mdm2 were injected into the fat pad of female NSG mice, and tumor volume (over time) and tumor weights were determined (Fig. 1, D and E) with no significant differences in the tumor growth. However, examination of metastasis showed a significant increase in lung metastasis in tumors with elevated Mdm2 (Fig. 1F). Induction of the Mdm2 promoter by K-Ras signaling pathways

Considering that artificially elevating Mdm2 can promote migration, we investigated if there were endogenous factors that could elevate Mdm2 that are associated with migration and metastasis. It has been reported that roughly 5% of triple negative breast cancer have K-Ras mutation, and K-Ras is associated with EMT (21). Ha-Ras and N-Ras can induce Mdm2 gene expression by activating distinct transcription factors, Ets2-AP1 and N-Myc, respectively (12, 14). It is unclear if K-Ras could regulate Mdm2 gene expression. Interestingly, the TMD231 cells used in our previous study (4) and in Figure 1 have K-Ras mutation and maintain a mesenchymal-like phenotype permissive of metastasis (22, 23). Furthermore, these mesenchymal-like cells have no E-Cadherin expression due to promoter methylation (24). To determine if K-Ras could induce Mdm2 gene expression, we use the P2 promoter linked to luciferase with Rous sarcoma virus promoter-βgal transiently transfected with either control, Ha-Ras, K-Ras, or N-Ras in H1299 cells (devoid of p53). We observed that N-Ras did not induce the Mdm2 P2 promoter (Fig. 2A), which is unsurprising as this pathway required N-Myc to activate the Mdm2 promoter, which was was reported in neuronal cells (25). Ha-Ras and K-Ras activated the P2 promoter (Fig. 2A). Ha-Ras was reported to induce the Mdm2 P2 promoter by activating Ets2 and AP1 (12). It could be assumed that K-Ras and Ha-Ras use the same pathway to induce Mdm2 gene expression as indicated by the activation of mitogen activated protein kinase (MAPK) (phosphor-extracellular signal-regulated kinase) (Fig. S1B). However, using the P2 promoter with deletions in the Ets factor binding elements in conjunction with KRas, the P2 promoter was still induced (Fig. 2B). These data suggest that the Ras–MAPK–Ets factor pathway was not responsible for Mdm2 gene expression. To understand how the K-Ras pathway was involved, murine embryo fibroblasts (MEF) Ha-Ras, N-Ras, or HA-Ras−/− N-Ras−/− with endogenous K-Ras or mutants of KRas reconstituted were used to determine Mdm2 levels. By Western blot, Mdm2 levels in KRas or mutant KRas Mefs were increased relative to N-Ras or HA-Ras Mefs (Fig. 2C). Interestingly, the Ras–MEK1/2–MAPK pathway can induce the Snai1 gene, and its protein product, Snail, which is a transcription factor associated with mesenchymal cells. Snail was elevated in KRas or mutant KRas cells and low in HA-Ras and N-Ras (Fig. 2C). Snail binds to E-box DNA-binding elements in promoters, which can either repress or activate gene expression associated with EMT (20). Examining the P2 promoter, we found two E-box DNA-binding elements. Since we identified that the Ebox2 DNA-binding site was responsive to Snail in transient assays, we examined if Snail bound to the P2 promoter. We initiated our study by using a DNA pull-down assay. The P2 promoter was amplified by PCR with the 5′oligo tagged with biotin. The product was bound to streptavidin beads. To understand if Snail was binding specifically to the Mdm2 promoter, we used His-tagged Snail (rSnail), produced in bacteria and purified by chromatography. rSnail was added to the P2 promoter-streptavidin beads. Western blot shows Snail bound to the Mdm2 P2 promoter and the binding was absent in the ΔEbox2 (Fig. 3E). We also use rp53 as a positive control for DNA binding to both the WT and mutant EBox2 in a DNA pulldown (Fig. S1D) To verify endogenous Snail bound to the genomic Mdm2 promoter, a chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) was used. In response to TGFβ1 MCF10a, cells will increase Snail levels. To examine if Snail bound to the P2 promoter, mock, or TGFβ1 was added to the media of MCF10a cells. ChIP assay was performed using a Snail antibody and showed endogenous Snail bound to the Mdm2 promoter (Fig. 3F). These data indicate that activated Snail will bind to the Ebox2 DNA-binding element in the P2 promoter and induce Mdm2 gene expression.

To determine if Mdm2 was downstream of Snail for activation of migration, we knocked down endogenous Snail with shRNA in MDA468 (Fig. 4A) and TMD231 (Fig. 4B) cells and verified by Western blot. Mdm2 levels were decreased in shSnail compared to the control (Fig. 4 and Fig. S4E). Using a scratch assay (wound healing assay), we analyzed shControl, and shSnail, MDA468, and TMD231 cells. Not surprisingly, shSnail cells were ineffective at closing the gap compared to shControl cells (Fig. 4, A and B). These data are consistent with a previous report showing that Snail was important for metastasis (26). To test if Mdm2 could restore migration in cells knocked down for Snail, we transduced cells with the Mdm2 virus, which slightly elevated Mdm2 in shSnail cells (Fig. 4, A and B). The Mdm2 transduced shSnail cells migration was similar to shControl (Fig. 4), correcting the shSnail migration defect. Discussion

The tumor microenvironment has a multitude of growth factors, cytokines, nutrients, and altered oxygen tension, which can influence a small fraction of tumor cells to reprogram and become invasive. The genotypic or phenotypic changes can lead to an EMT or a non-EMT process. Ultimately, this leads to intracellular programming that activates or represses genes to promote migration. Cell surface receptor activation by supraligand levels, elevated oncogenes, or hyperactivation mutations in the receptor is one modality in activating downstream kinases such as Ras. However, Ras also has a gain of function mutations that bypass cell surface receptors for downstream activation. These pathways lead to Ras signaling downstream for the activation of transcription factors. Interestingly, N-Ras, K-Ras, and Ha-Ras signal many transcription factors; some are common to these kinases, and some appear distinct.

We reported that the Mdm2 oncogene was associated with EMT (11). We also found that Mdm2 was required for the early stages of metastasis, promoting angiogenesis (4, 27) and migration/intravasation (4). Our initial observations showed that Mdm2 was elevated as cells transitioned into mesenchymal cells (11). Key EMT signaling pathways utilized Ras, epidermal growth factor receptor, and transforming growth factor beta receptor to signal downstream transcription factors to induce gene expression. Snail, Slug, and Twist are transcription factors associated with regulating EMT genes (20). To address which factors may play a role in regulating Mdm2 gene expression, we focused on Snail as it has been reported that Slug can repress Mdm2 gene expression (28). Analysis of Snail activation showed that KRas and KRas mutants increased Snail, and Mdm2 was also increased. As it has been reported that HA-Ras induced Mdm2 gene expression through the Ets DNA-binding elements (12, 29), we examined if these were required. We found that the Ets-binding elements were dispensable for K-Ras induction of Mdm2. Since Snail binds to EBoxes, we identified two and found that Snail bound the 3′ EBox proximal to the P2 TATA box. Loss of Snail by shRNA resulted in decreased Mdm2 levels. We use several models to examine the role of Snail in the induction of Mdm2. We focused on MDA468 cells as these cells were amplified with epidermal growth factor receptor, which would increase KRas activation, showing that TGFβ1 is not the only pathway that regulates Snail/Mdm2. This might be predicted as both factors have been independently reported to be important for migration (7, 30, 31, 32). Ras family members can activate specific pathways to induce to induce Mdm2 by other transcription factors that maybe specific to external stimuli or cell lineage.

Interestingly, when the shSnail cells were viral transduced with Mdm2 to increase expression, migration was restored equal to shControl. It should be noted that it is difficult to elevate Mdm2; as shown here, Mdm2 gene expression and protein are tightly regulated. This tightly regulated balance also reflects the cellular response to environmental cues to trigger an elevation in Mdm2, which is a proportional response to the activation of specific signaling pathways. Snail is upstream of Mdm2 which is a key driver of migration mediated by upstream signaling pathways to program a metastatic phenotype. However, our previous work and herein support a feed-forward pathway in between Mdm2 and Snail (4). This pathway may be countered by the induction of Slug, which has been shown to repress Mdm2 gene expression (28), raising the possibility that these two transcription factors play opposing roles in regulating Mdm2. It is unclear if the transcription factor Twist1, a factor known to also play a role in migration but also in other pathways will regulate Mdm2 similar to Slug or Snail. The regulation of Mdm2 gene expression may be dependent on the extracellular stimulus to activate specific pathways. Additional investigation will be needed to understand other factors and pathways that activate Mdm2 oncogene in the context of specific microenvironment stimuli.

Currently, there are several clinical trials using small molecules to target the amino terminus of Mdm2 in the hopes of activating p53. While targeting Mdm2 seems rational, to prevent invasive/metastatic programs may not have therapeutic efficacy for several reasons. Targeting the amino terminus to prevent the binding and alleviate the repression of the tumor suppressor p53 will not be effective. Late-stage metastatic tumors have a high frequency of p53 mutations. Mechanistically, this is supported by a study showing that Mdm2 could drive metastasis in cancer cells with mutant p53 (4). Thus, using therapies to target Mdm2 in the amino terminus would not be efficacious in a mutant p53 background. There are other complicating factors to consider when targeting the amino terminus of Mdm2, as other domains seem more important for docking many proteins and alternative start sites or splice variants of Mdm2 delete the amino terminus. These truncated forms of Mdm2 are present in tumor cells and have been reported to transform primary cells (33), yet specific functions have not been clearly defined. Understanding how Mdm2 is regulated (gene and protein), leading to a metastatic phenotype in tumors devoid of functional p53, is essential to developing new strategies to target metastatic disease, the driver of patient death.

Experimental procedure

Cell culture

The cell lines in this study were obtained from American Type Culture Collection or Frederick National Laboratories for Cancer Research National Cancer Institute (U2OS, MDA468, TMD231, H226, H3255, H1299, and Ras MEFs, respectively). The cells were grown in Roswel Park Memorial Institute medium, or Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium, with 8% fetal calf serum, penicillin, and streptomycin (11). MCF10a cells were grown Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium/F12 supplemented with 5% horse serum, 20 ng/ml epidermal growth factor, 0.5 mg/ml hydrocortisone, 100 ng/ml cholera toxin, 10 ug/ml insulin, penicillin, and streptomycin. All cell lines were grown in a 5% CO2, 37 °C humidified incubator. shSnail constructs were purchased from Open Biosystems (two shSnail were use per cell line: ccaatcggaagcctaactaca and ccactcagaatgtcaagaagta), and Mdm2 was generated by subcloning Mdm2 cDNA into the pLNCX vector. The virus was produced in 293T cells, harvested, filtered, polybrene added, and then the media was incubated with cells (4, 16, 34). After 48 h incubation with the virus, cells were selected with puromycin for shcontrol and shSnail or G418 resistance (Mdm2). Migration assays were performed as previously described (4). Data from migration assays were generated and repeated at least twice, and statistical analyses were performed to determine standard deviation and p values. For animal work, the protocol was approved by the Indiana University School of Medicine Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Mdm2 promoter analysis

ChIP assay, DNA pulldown, and reporter assay were performed as previously described (11). Oligos used for PCR of the P2 promoter were ggctgcgggcccctgcggcg and aatctggaatctgtggggtgg. DNA pull-down assay used recombinant Snail produced in bacteria and purified by Ni (nickel) nitriloiacetic acid (NTA) agarose column on Akta chromatography system. The Mdm2 promoter was amplified by PCR using the P2 oligo corresponding to the TATA box was labeled with biotin, and the PCR product was confirmed on an agarose gel The PCR product was purified from the gel and incubated with streptavidin Sepharose beads. Snail bound to the promoter was detected by Western blot. Reporter assays were transient transfected in cells using PEI of Mdm2 P2 reporter upstream of luciferase and beta-galactosidase plasmid. Three independent transfections were used to determine the relative luciferase activity as a ratio of luciferase activity to beta-galactosidase activity. The data was repeated at least twice. SD was determined from the mean and p values were determined.

Western blot analysis

Whole-cell lysates were isolated by urea lysis buffer, prepared for an SDS-PAGE gel, and transferred to the polyvinylidene fluoride membrane as previously described (27). Mdm2 (2A10, 4B2, and 2A9: Calbiochem), β-actin (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), Snail antibody (Proteintech) with secondary HRP antibody, and chemiluminescence were used to detect the respective protein by Bio-Rad imaginer.
