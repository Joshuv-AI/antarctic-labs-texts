Cardioplegia practices in pediatric cardiovascular surgery: Survey across Asia, Europe, and North America
Authors: Robert X. Lao, Cristina Salvo, Anamaria Stanisic, Osami Honjo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardioplegia and myocardial protection strategies continue to evolve, with wide variation in reported practices. However, there is no contemporary report of these practices in pediatrics. We conducted a survey of pediatric cardioplegia practice among major institutions across Asia, Europe, and North America.MethodsA survey was distributed to members of the Congenital Heart Surgeons Society, European Congenital Heart Surgeons Association, and Asian Association for Pediatric and Congenital Heart Surgery. Responses were collected from March to September 2024. Questions focused on cardioplegia type, delivery, adjunct protection, del Nido usage, and differences in practices based on patient age or complexity.ResultsComplete responses were obtained from 71 surgeons and 7 perfusionists representing 70 institutions. Although the types of solutions used vary across Asia and Europe, >80% of North America respondents reported using del Nido. Cold cardioplegia (<10 °C) was the predominant strategy (92%; n = 72 of 78 respondents). The most frequent dosing was induction between 20 and 30 mL/kg (76%; n = 59) and redosing between 10 and 20 mL/kg (63%; n = 49), with a wide range of redose timing (10-120 minutes). Notably, 28% of respondents (n = 22) reported giving only single-dose cardioplegia, and 56% (n = 44) reported using topical cooling, with only 8% (n = 6) using “hot shot” cardioplegia. Among respondents using del Nido, 91% (n = 43 of 47) reported using it exclusively for all cases.ConclusionsInstitutions in North America are using primarily del Nido, while heterogeneity remains in cardioplegia use across Asia and Europe. There also remains significant variation in practices, highlighting the need for clinical outcomes studies to determine an optimal cardioplegia strategy.

Cardioplegia use in pediatric cardiac surgery across Asia, Europe, and North America.

Central Message

del Nido is the predominant choice for cardioplegia in pediatric cardiovascular surgery across North America; however, considerable variation remains in cardioplegia strategy across Asia and Europe.

Perspective

Cardioplegia is fundamental to myocardial protection in pediatric cardiovascular surgery. Yet despite its importance, there is no consensus on guidelines, and worldwide clinical practices are rarely reported. This study provides a contemporary profile of cardioplegia practices across major institutions in Asia, Europe, and North America in 2024.

Myocardial protection (MP) is critical to the success of open-heart surgery, especially in pediatric cardiovascular surgery when repairing complex congenital heart disease (CHD). MP is achieved through cardioplegia solutions.1 These solutions are delivered at the initiation of cardiopulmonary bypass when an aortic cross-clamp is applied and act to induce controlled cardiac arrest to enable surgeons to operate on a still and bloodless heart.2 Surveys of pediatric cardioplegia practices and perfusion practices performed within the last 15 years have highlighted significant variability in strategies across North America,3 the United Kingdom & Ireland,4 Japan5 and globally.6 This may underlie why many clinical reports in pediatric cardiovascular surgery point to inadequate MP and perioperative myocardial damage as a leading cause of postoperative morbidity and death following technically successful operations.7, 8, 9 There is no consensus on best practices; strategies continue to evolve worldwide, many of which are not widely reported. However, there has been growing interest in del Nido cardioplegia, which was formulated to optimize the protection of neonatal and infant myocardium,10 with the most recent survey across North America showing its emergence as a popular cardioplegia option3 and growing interest beyond North America for adopting del Nido in clinical practice.4 Thus, here we sought to survey major institutions across Asia, Europe, and North America to establish up-to-date pediatric cardioplegia practices and assess the state of either utilization or hesitancies surrounding del Nido cardioplegia in clinical practice.

Material and Methods

Study Population

Ethical approval for this study was provided by The Hospital for Sick Children’s Research Ethics Board (Protocol 1000080347; approved August 17, 2023). An online survey was distributed via email to 182 surgeon members of the Congenital Heart Surgeons Society, 250 members of the European Congenital Heart Surgeons Association, and 11 executive directors of the Asian Association for Pediatric and Congenital Heart Surgery. Additionally, in the email invitation we encouraged surgeons to forward the survey to lead perfusionists within their institution. Nonresponders were followed up via e-mail during the study data collection period from March 2024 to September 2024. All participants provided consent for publication of their study data.

Survey Design

The survey design was informed by 2 previous pediatric cardioplegia practice surveys across North America3 and the United Kingdom and Ireland4 with a mix of quantitative and qualitative questions. The full survey is provided in Figure E1. The survey was administered, and data were collected through the REDCap platform managed by The Hospital for Sick Children under license from Vanderbilt University.11

In brief, our survey focused on questions regarding cardioplegia composition and delivery, the use of adjunct MP strategies, and the use or hesitancy regarding del Nido cardioplegia. Respondents were asked to complete the survey based on their approach when operating on infants (age 1-12 months) undergoing a complex repair (eg, tetralogy of Fallot). A final section at the end of the survey allowed respondents to identify all changes to their practice based on patient age and/or complexity of the operation.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel. Responses were stratified into 3 geographic regions: Asia, Europe, and North America. Categorical data are presented as percentage and their respective proportion. Continuous data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation unless specified otherwise.

Regardless of cardioplegia composition, cold temperature delivery (<10 °C) remains the predominant strategy, used by 92% of respondents (n = 72/78) (Table E4). Adjusting Practices

The vast majority of respondents (90%; n = 70 of 78) reported not adjusting their practice based on patient age or complexity of the operation (Table E8). Respondents who adjust their practice based on age reported using a higher cardioplegia induction dose and longer redose intervals for surgery in neonates (0-1 months), and the converse—lower cardioplegia induction dose and shorter redose intervals for surgery in children and adolescents (1-19 years). Respondents who adjust practice based on complexity of the operation use lower systemic cooling temperatures, and higher induction and maintenance cardioplegia doses.

del Nido Cardioplegia

del Nido cardioplegia was used exclusively by 77% of respondents across North America (n = 36 of 47) but by only a small fraction of respondents across Asia and Europe (Table E9). Among those respondents using del Nido as part of their practice in any setting, 91% reported using del Nido exclusively (n = 43 of 47) (Tables E1 and E9). Additionally, when asked about when their institution first used del Nido, the responses showed a clear trend of del Nido being more widely first adopted across North America (Table E9). Respondents who do not use del Nido exclusively reported instead using blood cardioplegia in shorter or simpler cases (Table E10). Three key themes emerged when respondents who do not use del Nido were asked about their reasoning: (1) inability to use del Nido due to lack of regulatory approval in their region, (2) challenges in procuring del Nido components (eg, Plasmalyte-A), and (3) lack of robust clinical evidence demonstrating the superiority of del Nido.

Discussion

Our survey of current pediatric cardioplegia practices has identified great variations in clinical practices. By comparing surveys performed over the past decade, we also identify trends and shifts toward a majority opinion, especially in North America. The reasons why surgeons and perfusionists tend toward one strategy over another remain elusive. Although clear advances in cardioplegia have paved the way for open heart surgery and increasing complex repairs of CHD,12, 13, 14 the optimal cardioplegia strategy remains to be determined.

Trends in Cardioplegia Practice Across North America

Kotani and colleagues, in a survey of cardioplegia practices across North America in April 2012 with data from 56 surgeons, found great variation in the types of cardioplegia solution used and overall strategy. Notably, while 38% of their respondents (n = 21 of 56) at that time used del Nido cardioplegia, our data show a clear shift in practice across North America, with 81% of respondents (n = 38 of 47) reporting using del Nido in 2024. While the previous survey had a large proportion of respondents who used customized solutions (34%; n = 19 of 56), our survey reveals that very few respondents opt for customized solutions today. This is further supported by the fact that only a small proportion of respondents in our current survey reported using additives.

Looking at del Nido cardioplegia strategy in North America reveals some interesting shifts. Previously, the predominant blood-to-crystalloid delivery ratio for del Nido was 1:4, used by more than 90% of respondents (n = 19 of 21). Although this remains the most popular strategy, our current survey reveals a shift toward a new practice, with a 4:1 delivery ratio used by 21% of respondents (n = 8 of 38). This is a deviation from the originally devised protocol for del Nido delivery, which recommended 20% by volume of fully oxygenated patient blood.10 Perhaps this change can be attributed to studies that demonstrate benefits of blood-based cardioplegia solutions versus crystalloid solutions.15, 16, 17 This “reverse ratio” has been described in an adult aortic surgery study, with the aim of reducing lidocaine and crystalloid load during prolonged operations.18 However, the reasons for this del Nido cardioplegia delivery strategy in pediatrics remain unsupported by strong clinical evidence. The most common dosing strategy remains the same as induction with 20 to 30 mL/kg and redoses of 10 to 20 mL/kg. There has been a clear increase in the time between maintenance doses for del Nido across North America, from an average of 45 minutes in 2012 to 74 minutes in 2024, with an accompanying increase in the number of respondents who deliver del Nido as a single dose. One of the key advantages for del Nido has always been the increased time interval required between doses, which allows for continuous operation. This trend in increased maintenance dosing time intervals may reflect something that surgeons continue to push the limits on to minimize disruptions during long and complex CHD repairs.

In addition to cardioplegia strategy, various adjunct practices available to enhance MP. While the proportion of respondents who continue to use topical cooling in their practice has remained unchanged from 2012 (64%; n = 36 of 56) to 2024 (62%; n = 29 of 47), there has been a drastic decrease in the use of “hot shot” cardioplegia, from 21% of respondents (n = 12 of 56) in 2012 to only 4% (n = 2 of 47) in 2024. Our survey was limited in exploring the full scope of adjunct practices, but a coordinated MP strategy, including but not limited to cardioplegia and the use of cardiopulmonary bypass–related practices (eg, modified ultrafiltration19,20) and transfusion practices,21,22 are required to achieve optimal protection.

The Need for Rigorous Clinical Evidence

Variation in cardioplegia strategies is stark and starts with something as fundamental as the type of cardioplegia solution. We found that variation can be attributed to regulatory restrictions or procurement challenges for proprietary components in del Nido and HTK/Custodiol. While the cost of using del Nido versus St. Thomas No. 2/Plegisol appears to be comparable,23 Talwar and colleagues reported that a 1-L preparation of del Nido costs $4.61 USD, whereas a 1-L solution of HTK/Custodiol costs $184 USD.24 Therefore, financial burden also may be an important consideration in their adoption in clinical practice.

Although it is possible that different strategies may offer comparable MP, there is a paucity of evidence to support this claim. Clinical trials of cardioplegia in pediatric cardiovascular surgery have been reviewed,25 but the evidence remains insufficient. As highlighted previously,4 one major deficiency is that the high-risk pediatric cohorts in which complex CHD repair must be accompanied with equally excellent MP are not represented in these trials. Therefore, there is a pressing need for rigorous, multicenter randomized clinical trials, which will need to include well-validated early and late endpoints, such as the ongoing DESTINY trial, the first-of-its-kind phase II/III multicenter patient-blinded and assessor-blinded, parallel-group, individually randomized controlled trial conducted across the United Kingdom with head-to-head testing of del Nido versus St. Thomas blood cardioplegia for MP in pediatric cardiovascular surgery.26 Moreover, most existing clinical studies on this topic have been conducted primarily by European institutions.25 There is impetus to perform trials in North America and globally, where enrollment rates may be higher to enable large randomized multicenter trials that pave the way for regulatory approval of the best cardioplegia solution(s) for use in pediatric cardiovascular surgery.

Study Limitations

This survey includes only 78 responses capturing 70 institutions with low response rates in Europe and Asia, and thus our findings might not reflect the entire pediatric cardiovascular surgical community across Asia, Europe, and North America. Based on the institutions that responded, our data are biased toward reporting practices at major cardiovascular surgery centers. We note that our respondents were mainly senior clinicians, which, although potentially a strength regarding the quality of data, might not adequately reflect the perspectives of all clinicians. The survey did not measure the impacts of any of the reported cardioplegia strategies on patient outcomes, and thus any conclusions based on trends in cardioplegia strategy are limited by the assumption that respondents are obtaining equivalent and satisfactory MP with the strategy they indicated using and/or are allowed to use at their institutions. Although there were variations in responses from respondents within the same institution, the small sample size did not permit us to perform an analysis of intrainstitutional variations in practice, which remains a potential topic of further investigation.

Conclusions

Our survey of current cardioplegia strategies across Asia, Europe, and North America highlights continued variability in clinical practices provided to enable MP during pediatric cardiovascular surgery. Ironically, variation in practices remains the only constant. While there have been noticeable shifts toward predominant strategies across North America, especially with del Nido, great variation still exists across Asia and Europe. The degree of variation in clinical practices seen in this study, along with previous studies on pediatric cardioplegia practices that represent a subgroup of the geographic regions that we surveyed, support the need for clinical evidence to appropriately define optimal pediatric cardioplegia strategy.
