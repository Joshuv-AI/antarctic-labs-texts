Protein Biomarkers of Infertility Implicate Inflammation and Cell Growth/Remodeling Pathways
Authors: Micayla N Flores, Yunong Zhao, Linke Li, Wesley A Spacht, Charles Kooperberg, JoAnn E Manson, Charles B Eaton, Alexander P Reiner, Michael C Honigberg, Emily S Lau
Published: 2026

What is the clinical question being addressed?

Among women with a history of infertility, are there measurable differences in circulating cardiovascular disease–related protein biomarkers that could help explain the observed association between infertility and later-life CVD risk?

What is the main finding?

Infertility was associated with protein biomarkers representing immune/inflammatory signaling, cell remodeling, thrombosis, and metabolism pathways, with replication for inflammatory and intracellular signaling proteins NEMO and SRC in a second, independent cohort.

Emerging data link infertility to a greater risk of coronary heart disease, heart failure (HF), and overall cardiovascular disease (CVD).1,2 In a Swedish registry study of 863,324 women, those experiencing infertility for ≥5 years had a 19% higher incident CVD risk than women without infertility.1 We previously demonstrated that among 38,521 women enrolled in the Women’s Health Initiative (WHI), infertility was associated with greater HF risk.2 Despite consistent epidemiologic evidence demonstrating an association between infertility and CVD, the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms remain unclear. Analysis of circulating protein biomarkers offers a complementary approach to identify candidate biological pathways that may link infertility with later-life CVD in women. Prior work in polycystic ovary syndrome showed higher circulating levels of C-reactive protein and homocysteine, suggesting a potential role for systemic inflammation in infertility-related CVD.3 However, comprehensive proteomic studies of infertility are lacking. We examined associations between infertility and CVD-related protein biomarkers among postmenopausal participants enrolled in the WHI and hypothesized that infertility would be associated with higher levels of inflammatory, endothelial, and atherosclerotic protein biomarkers.

Methods

We analyzed 366 unique CVD-related proteins in 1,093 WHI Long Life Study (WHI-LLS) participants. WHI-LLS is an ancillary study of 7,875 individuals from the original WHI cohort (n = 161,808) who completed an in-person visit between 2012 and 2013 and were drawn from a predefined cohort with available baseline genetic and cardiovascular biomarker data. Among participants with available plasma proteomic data (n = 1,317), we excluded those with HF (n = 26), prevalent myocardial infarction (n = 66), and missing key covariates (n = 132), yielding a sample of 1,093 participants. Infertility was self-reported at WHI baseline and defined as inability to conceive after ≥1 year of trying to become pregnant, independent of pregnancy outcome. Circulating CVD-related protein biomarkers were measured using Olink Cardiometabolic, Inflammation, and CVD II/III panels. In the WHI-LLS discovery cohort, associations between infertility and log-transformed standardized biomarkers were tested using multivariable linear regression, adjusting for age, race/ethnicity, systolic blood pressure, hypertension treatment, body mass index, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, smoking, and cohort. We evaluated model assumptions and found no major violations.

Associations identified in WHI (false discovery rate q-value <0.05 considered statistically significant after multiple hypothesis testing correction; uncorrected P < 0.05 considered suggestive) were validated in the UK Biobank (UKBB) Pharma Proteomics Project, a prospective cohort of 54,306 healthy volunteers aged 40 to 69 years, using the same Olink panels. The UKBB cohort included women with infertility (n = 359), identified by International Classification of Diseases code N97, and a 1:5 propensity score-matched control group (n = 1,795) matched on the same covariates listed above (standardized mean difference <0.05 for all variables). All participants provided informed consent, and study protocols were approved by appropriate Institutional Review Boards. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.3.

Results

Among 1,093 WHI participants, 180 (17%) reported a history of infertility. The mean age was 64 ± 6 years at WHI enrollment and 80 ± 6 years at biomarker assessment. Clinical characteristics were similar by infertility status. The UKBB replication cohort included 2,154 women (mean age 52 ± 8 years).

In multivariable-adjusted models, infertility was nominally associated with 13 distinct CVD-related proteins, although none met experiment-wide significance (P < 0.05, false discovery rate q > 0.05) (Figure 1). Compared with women without infertility, those with infertility had higher levels of interleukin (IL)-17A, nuclear factor-kappa B essential modulator (NEMO), tumor necrosis factor superfamily member 13b, hydroxyacid oxidase 1 (HAOX1), cluster of differentiation 40 (CD40), cluster of differentiation 6, proto-oncogene tyrosine-protein kinase src (SRC), cathepsin Z, integrin beta-1 binding protein 2, and fibroblast growth factor 5 (FGF-5) (β range 0.06-0.23), and lower levels of tissue plasminogen activator (tPA), IL-18, and matrix metallopeptidase 3 (MMP-3) (β −0.12 to −0.15). These biomarkers reflect biological pathways of immune and inflammatory regulation (CD40, IL18, NEMO), cell growth and remodeling (FGF, SRC, MMP-3), thrombosis (t-PA), and glyoxylate metabolism (HAOX1).

Of the 13 biomarkers identified in the WHI discovery cohort, NEMO, SRC, and IL-17A were also associated with infertility in UKBB. NEMO and SRC showed directionally consistent associations across cohorts (NEMO: β = 0.16, P = 0.048 [WHI] vs β = 0.13, P = 0.027 [UKBB]; SRC: β = 0.12, P = 0.039 [WHI] vs β = 0.12, P = 0.045 [UKBB]), representing replication in an independent cohort. IL-17 A was also associated with infertility in both cohorts but showed directionally discordant effects (β = 0.15, P = 0.016 [WHI] vs β = −0.16, P = 0.005 in [UKBB]).

Discussion

In this large-scale proteomic analysis, infertility was associated with distinct CVD-related protein biomarkers spanning immune and inflammatory regulation, cell growth and remodeling, thrombosis, and glyoxylate metabolism, with replication for inflammatory signaling protein NEMO and angiogenesis and cell signaling molecule SRC across 2, independent cohorts.

NEMO governs nuclear factor kappa B-mediated immune responses vital for host defense and tissue homeostasis, and its activation drives chronic inflammation and downstream vascular dysfunction.4 SRC, a nonreceptor tyrosine kinase, regulates cellular migration, adhesion, proliferation, and survival and has been implicated in early atherosclerosis.5 Although these proteins are not established clinical risk markers for incident CVD in women, their known biological functions support the possibility that infertility is associated with dysregulated inflammatory and intracellular signaling pathways relevant to later-life CVD risk. Notably, infertility was associated with higher IL-17A levels in WHI but lower levels in UKBB. Although lower levels of inflammatory proteins were unexpected in infertility, this may reflect heterogeneity in infertility phenotypes due to differences in infertility ascertainment.

Our study has several limitations. First, infertility was self-reported in WHI and ascertained by International Classification of Diseases code in UKBB, potentially capturing different infertility phenotypes. Self-report may reflect a broader range of women with infertility, including those who did not seek specialty evaluation, whereas International Classification of Diseases coding likely identifies a narrower subset with clinically recognized infertility. Self-report has shown moderate-to-high validity for infertility ascertainment, whereas the validity of code-based ascertainment is less well established. These differences may have introduced heterogeneity and contributed to replication differences. Second, ascertainment of biomarkers and covariates was not contemporaneous with the infertility event in both cohorts, limiting causal inference due to confounding and temporal separation of analyses. Third, the UKBB cohort was younger and healthier than the WHI cohort and was not restricted to postmenopausal women, which may explain why several thrombosis- and metabolism-related proteins did not validate in the UKBB. Fourth, sample size precluded stratification by infertility subtype, and subtype data were not reliably available in UKBB. Finally, infertility was nominally associated with 13 distinct CVD-related proteins, but effect sizes were modest, and none met experiment-wide significance; findings should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis generating. Nonetheless, this study represents the most comprehensive proteomic analysis of infertility to date, revealing potential molecular links between infertility and elevated later-life CVD risk.

In conclusion, our broad proteomic investigation characterized associations between infertility and CVD-related protein biomarkers involved in immune and inflammatory regulation, cell growth and remodeling, thrombosis, and glyoxylate metabolism, with reproducible signals for NEMO and SRC in a second, independent cohort. Future studies should determine whether targeting inflammatory and cell remodeling pathways can mitigate infertility-associated cardiovascular risk.
