Intravenous lacosamide for acute repetitive seizures and convulsive status epilepticus in critically ill children
Authors: Man‐Ru Hsiao, Tzu‐Cheng Tsai, Shao‐Hsuan Hsia, Oi‐Wa Chan, En‐Pei Lee, Jainn‐Jim Lin, Kuang‐Lin Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

Acute repetitive seizures and convulsive status epilepticus are common neurological emergencies in pediatric intensive care units (PICUs). 1 Pediatric seizures can be generally categorized into two types, namely, provoked and unprovoked seizures. Provoked seizures primarily arise from identifiable acute conditions, including febrile seizures, hypoglycemia, central nervous system infections, head trauma, congenital metabolic disorders, electrolyte imbalance, hypoxia, cerebrovascular diseases, and tumors. Unprovoked seizures do not arise from acute conditions but are rather associated with pre‐existing brain abnormalities or genetic factors. According to US statistics, approximately 25 000 to 40 000 previously healthy children experience their first episode of acute unprovoked seizures annually. 1 , 2 Seizures can further be classified based on their onset features into focal onset, generalized onset, and unknown onset, with further subtypes based on seizure characteristics. 3 According to the latest definition by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE), status epilepticus is defined as generalized tonic–clonic seizures lasting 5 min or more or focal seizures lasting 10 min or more with impairment of consciousness. When acute seizures progress to status epilepticus and remain uncontrolled, long‐term adverse effects such as neuronal death, neuronal injury, and alterations in neuronal networks may occur. 4 Status epilepticus is more common in children with a history of epilepsy (with an incidence of 9.1–27%), and its incidence decreases with increasing age. According to US statistics, the incidence among children aged 1–19 is 10–58 cases per 100 000 population, while among children under 1 year old, it is 135.2–156 cases per 100 000 population. 5

The pharmacological treatment of acute seizures or status epilepticus in clinical practice primarily involves the early use of intravenous antiseizure medications. According to treatment guidelines from the American Epilepsy Society (AES) and Neurocritical Care Society (NCS), this typically includes (1) first‐line benzodiazepines such as midazolam (intravenous or intramuscular), lorazepam (intravenous), or diazepam (intravenous or rectal administration); (2) second‐line antiseizure medications including intravenous fosphenytoin (not available in Taiwan, but phenytoin is available), valproic acid, levetiracetam, or phenobarbital; and (3) third‐line options may involve repeating second‐line treatments or using anesthetic doses of thiopental, midazolam, pentobarbital (not available in Taiwan), or propofol. For children with refractory status epilepticus, in addition to third‐line treatments, other options may include lacosamide and topiramate. 6

Lacosamide was approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2008 for treating partial onset seizures in adults with epilepsy, and in 2017, it was approved for oral use as monotherapy or adjunctive therapy for partial‐onset seizures in children aged 4 and above. In 2021, both intravenous and oral formulations of lacosamide were approved for monotherapy or adjunctive therapy for partial‐onset seizures in children aged 1 month to 4 years. 7 The mechanism of action of lacosamide involves selectively enhancing the slow inactivation of voltage‐gated sodium channels rather than fast inactivation, thereby stabilizing overexcited neuronal membranes, blocking sodium channels, and preventing the propagation of action potentials, ultimately suppressing neuronal repetitive firing. 8 , 9

Lacosamide has not been associated with severe interactions when used concomitantly with other commonly used drugs. Common adverse reactions in children include dizziness, drowsiness, and headache; however, some individuals may experience bradycardia, prolongation of P‐R interval on electrocardiogram, and first‐degree atrioventricular block. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 In addition, skin‐related adverse reactions may include rash and pruritus, and clinicians should be aware of Stevens‐Johnson syndrome. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19

Data regarding the use of intravenous lacosamide for critically ill children with acute repetitive seizures and convulsive status epilepticus in Taiwan, especially in young infants and children, are lacking. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of intravenous lacosamide for acute repetitive seizures and convulsive status epilepticus in critically ill children at a medical center in northern Taiwan.

METHODS

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chang Gung Medical Foundation (Approval No. 202300576B0 and 202300903B0C502). We conducted a retrospective chart review of pediatric patients aged 18 years and younger admitted to the PICU at Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, Linkou Branch. Patients who were newly started on intravenous lacosamide were included and divided into two groups: (a) previously healthy, and (b) those with a history of epilepsy receiving other antiseizure medications or oral lacosamide. Patients who were older than 18 years, those who received only one dose of lacosamide, and those who had the route of administration of lacosamide changed from oral to intravenous without a change in dosage due to fasting were excluded. However, patients who had previously received oral lacosamide with or without intravenous loading and those in whom the dosage had been adjusted for acute seizures were included. Data were collected from October 2017 to September 2022, including the electronic medical records of the eligible patients.

After screening, we recorded the patients' basic information (age and sex), etiologies and types of seizure, duration of lacosamide use during hospitalization, dosage, treatment outcomes (cessation of seizures or not), concomitant use of other antiseizure medications during hospitalization, medications prescribed at discharge, and the final administration of lacosamide after discharge.

The protocol for administering intravenous lacosamide for acute seizures at our hospital included a loading dose of 10 mg/kg followed by a maintenance dose of 4 mg/kg every 8 h. The etiologies of seizures were categorized as infection, structural, genetic, traumatic brain injury, and other causes. In addition, based on the timing and types of antiseizure medications used before and after intravenous lacosamide administration, the order of the selection of second‐line antiseizure medications was recorded.

According to pharmacokinetic studies, lacosamide has good bioavailability (~100%) and low protein binding (<15%). Intravenous lacosamide reaches peak plasma concentrations at the end of the infusion. Therefore, we categorized treatment outcomes into early response (within 4 h without additional antiseizure medications) and no late breakthrough seizures (within 24, 48, and 72 h without additional antiseizure medications). 20

Steady‐state concentrations of drugs are typically achieved after approximately 3 to 5 half‐lives. Considering the elimination half‐life of lacosamide varies slightly based on weight, the estimated elimination half‐lives were approximately 7.4 h for children with an average weight of 11 kg, 10.6 h for children with an average weight of 28.9 kg, and 14.8 h for children with an average weight of 70 kg. Based on these estimates, we evaluated whether additional antiseizure medications were added within 48 and 72 h after lacosamide administration to assess seizure cessation under steady‐state concentrations.

Adverse reactions related to lacosamide were categorized based on definitions from the International Council for Harmonization of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH) and the World Health Organization (WHO), taking into account national circumstances. We recorded all adverse reactions occurring within 7 days after intravenous lacosamide administration, particularly changes in heart rate, rhythm, and electrocardiogram parameters, such as prolongation of the P‐R interval. Bradycardia was defined according to age as follows: (1) 1 month to 1 year: less than 90 beats per minute, and (2) 2 to 18 years: less than 60 beats per minute. All patients receiving intravenous lacosamide in the PICU were monitored with physiological monitors, and further, 12‐lead electrocardiograms were arranged if the aforementioned cardiac changes occurred. 21

STATISTICAL METHODS

Descriptive statistics were used to present percentages for each category, including sex, age distribution, and etiologies of acute repetitive seizures and convulsive status epilepticus. The chi‐square test was used to compare categorical data among groups (e.g., sex, age, cause of occurrence), Wilcoxon's signed rank test was used for ordinal data, and t‐tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare means among groups.

RESULTS

Characteristics of the study subjects

During the study period, a total of 123 pediatric patients were admitted to the PICU due to acute seizures and were newly treated with intravenous lacosamide to control seizures. After applying the exclusion criteria, 67 patients were finally included, all of whom were under 18 years old and had been admitted to the PICU after inadequate seizure control with first‐line benzodiazepines and subsequently received intravenous lacosamide as second‐line antiseizure treatment. Among them, 32 were previously healthy, while 35 had a history of epilepsy and received antiseizure medications. Among the 67 patients, there were 42 boys (62.7%) and 25 girls (37.3%), with a mean age of 7.20 ± 5.66 years (age range: 1 month to 17.9 years). The distribution of age groups was as follows: ≤1 year (10 patients, 14.9%), 2–4 years (23 patients, 34.3%), 5–8 years (7 patients, 10.4%), 9–12 years (13 patients, 19.4%), and 13–18 years (14 patients, 20.9%). Around half of the participants were less than 4 years old. The etiologies of seizures were as follows: infection (13 patients, 19.4%), structural (11 patients, 16.4%), genetic and head trauma (10 patients each, 14.9%), hypertensive encephalopathy (6 patients, 9.0%), hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy (4 patients, 6.0%), postcardiac arrest syndrome (3 patients, 4.5%), mitochondrial encephalopathy, autoimmune disorders and hematology/oncology disorders (2 patients each, 3.0%), metabolic abnormalities (1 patient, 1.5%), and unknown causes (3 patients, 4.5%). The types of seizures included focal‐onset seizures in 34 patients (50.7%), generalized‐onset seizures in 27 patients (40.3%), and mixed‐type seizures in 6 patients (9%). Of the 67 patients, 37 (55.2%) had convulsive status epilepticus or super‐refractory status epilepticus (Table 1).

Treatment outcomes according to the type of medication used

Among the 32 previously healthy children treated with lacosamide, 9 used lacosamide alone during hospitalization, 13 used it in combination with 2 other antiseizure medications, 6 used it in combination with 3 other antiseizure medications, and 4 used it in combination with 4 other antiseizure medications. The rates of seizure cessation when lacosamide was used as the first to fourth choice of antiseizure medication were 100.0%, 85.7%, 40.0%, and 50.0%, respectively.

Among the 35 patients with a history of epilepsy treated with lacosamide, 19 used lacosamide alone during hospitalization, 12 used it in combination with 2 other antiseizure medications, 2 used it in combination with 3 other antiseizure medications, and 2 used it in combination with 4 other antiseizure medications. The rates of seizure cessation when lacosamide was used as the first to fourth choice of antiseizure medication were 73.7%, 54.5%, 100.0%, and 0.0%, respectively. Treatment outcomes according to the etiology

In the overall cohort, the main etiologies of seizures were infection in 13 patients, structural factors in 11 patients, and genetic factors or head trauma in 10 patients each (the other etiologies are detailed in the Characteristics of the Study Subjects section). Among the 67 patients, a total of 27 developed bradycardia. After excluding other interventions (e.g., hypothermia therapy) and potential causes (pre‐existing bradycardia), 16 patients (23.9%) with bradycardia were suspected to have adverse reactions related to lacosamide use. In patients who experienced bradycardia, the heart rate decreased by approximately 15.1% to 48.4% compared to baseline following lacosamide administration. All patients' blood pressures remained within normal ranges. The occurrence of bradycardia did not lead to discontinuation or modification of lacosamide therapy. Therefore, bradycardia was mostly transient and reversible in all cases. Besides, among these 16 patients, 4 underwent complete 12 lead electrocardiography, and no prolongation of the P‐R interval was observed. One patient (1.5%), who received 4 antiseizure medications during hospitalization, developed a rash during treatment, which resolved after discontinuation of the drug. Further examination using the lymphocyte transformation test showed positive lymphocyte activation for lacosamide, suggesting the rash had been caused by lacosamide. This adverse reaction was recorded in the hospital's adverse reaction system.

DISCUSSION

There are limited clinical data on the early use of intravenous lacosamide for treating critically ill children with acute repetitive seizures and convulsive status epilepticus, especially in young infants and children age less than 4 years. In this study, we investigated 67 patients (under 18 years old) who received a loading dose of 10 mg/kg and a maintenance dose of 4 mg/kg of lacosamide every 8 h. Among them, 29 and 25 patients, respectively, used intravenous lacosamide as the first and second‐choice antiseizure medication, and the results showed good seizure control (82.8% and 72.0%). At 72 h after treatment, intravenous lacosamide demonstrated good efficacy for seizures caused by infection, structural factors, genetic factors, and traumatic brain injury. Although 16 (23.9%) patients developed bradycardia as an adverse effect, which is relatively higher compared to the literature, no prolongation of the P‐R interval was observed on electrocardiography. One patient (1.5%) was suspected of having developed a rash due to lacosamide use. Overall, adverse reactions to lacosamide were low and reversible.

As lacosamide has only recently been approved for use in children, past experience with lacosamide is relatively limited. We reviewed 4 previous studies (2 in adults and 2 in children) on the use of lacosamide in treating seizures (Table 5). In the first adult study, d'Orsi et al. prospectively analyzed 38 patients (mean age 44.5 years), including 23 with status epilepticus and 15 with repetitive seizures. After intravenous lacosamide use, 80% achieved seizure cessation in the status epilepticus group, compared to 86% in the repetitive seizures group. Adverse reaction analysis showed dizziness in 2 patients (5.3%). 20 In the second adult study, Newey et al. retrospectively analyzed 84 patients with status epilepticus (mean age 59.6 years) who received intravenous lacosamide as the second or third‐choice antiseizure medication, and assessed the efficacy from 4 h to 48 h after use. The results showed that the treatment was effective in 82.4% of the patients, and adverse reactions included hypotension in 8 patients, transient elevation in liver function tests in 4 patients, and acute renal failure in 1 patient. 22

In the first pediatric study, Poddar et al. retrospectively analyzed 9 patients (mean age 5.7 years) who received intravenous lacosamide as the second‐line or later antiseizure medication, with mean loading and maintenance doses of 8.7 mg/kg and 13.8 mg/kg/day, respectively. Four patients (44.4%) achieved complete seizure cessation, and 3 patients (33.3%) had a more than 50% reduction in seizure frequency. Adverse reactions included bradycardia in 1 patient (11.1%). 8 In the second pediatric study, Welsh et al. retrospectively analyzed the safety of intravenous lacosamide in 51 patients, including 22 aged 0–4 years, 17 aged 4–12 years, and 12 aged over 12 years. The mean loading dose was 2 mg/kg, and the maintenance dose ranged from 2 to 13 mg/kg/day. Among them, 29 patients were newly treated with intravenous lacosamide, while 22 had previously used oral lacosamide, usually as the third or fourth‐choice antiseizure medication. Safety analysis after use showed bradycardia in 1 patients (2.0%), which was considered to be related to lacosamide but without P‐R interval prolongation, and rash in 1 patient (2.0%). 23 In these previous studies, when benzodiazepines failed as first‐line treatment and a second‐line antiseizure medication was required, lacosamide was less often chosen as the first or second choice.

Recent literature also suggests that early treatment with lacosamide may result in better outcomes, and when given at sufficient doses, early treatment appears to be effective and safe. 8 , 23 This study provides insights into the use of lacosamide in the PICU for critically ill children with acute repetitive seizures and convulsive status epilepticus. The early initiation of intravenous lacosamide as a second‐line antiseizure medication after failure of benzodiazepines appears to be effective and safe in children. 8 , 22

LIMITATIONS

This study has several limitations. First, some patients received immunomodulatory/immunosuppressive therapy, hypothermia therapy, or a ketogenic diet along with antiseizure medications, making it difficult to assess the efficacy of antiseizure medications alone. Second, the number of patients was relatively small, especially those who received lacosamide as a later‐line treatment. With the increase in lacosamide usage, further studies with larger sample sizes would allow for a more comprehensive analysis of efficacy.

CONCLUSION

This study showed that the early use of intravenous lacosamide was effective in treating acute repetitive seizures and convulsive status epilepticus in critically ill children, including young infants and children less than 4 years old. At 72 h after intravenous lacosamide use, lacosamide demonstrated good efficacy for seizures caused by infection, structural factors, genetic factors, and traumatic brain injury. Overall, adverse reactions to lacosamide were low and reversible.
