Study on Thermal Risk of Coal-Based Activated Carbon after Adsorbing Acetone, Cyclohexane, and Butyl Acetate
Authors: Lanyun Wang, Qinghui Fan, Yan Wang, Yongliang Xu, Yao Li, Yanmei Zheng, Xiaodong Feng, Kun Zhang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Combustion and explosion accidents of the mixture may occur after the adsorption of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) by coal-based activated carbon (CBAC). It is of great significance to explore the oxidation and combustion performance of CBAC before and after adsorbing VOCs in order to prevent the reoccurrence of fire and explosion. Based on the CBAC sample commonly used in industrial production, three types of CBAC samples after adsorbing VOCs, i.e., acetone, cyclohexane, and butyl acetate, were prepared. The oxidation and combustion characteristics of the samples before and after adsorbing VOCs are measured and analyzed by thermal analyzer and cone calorimeter. Thermal analysis results indicate that during the oxidation process, the VOCs in the adsorbed samples will burn in the early stage, generating amounts of heat which may accelerate the oxidation and combustion of CBAC. According to the combustion performance experiments by cone calorimeter, it is also found that the combustion rate of CBAC after adsorbing VOCs is significantly enhanced. The time to ignition is shortened, the heat release rate becomes larger, and the time to reach the peak of heat release rate is significantly moved forward. In addition, the CO yield of the adsorbed sample is significantly improved. In general, VOC adsorption in CBAC can promote oxidation reactions and may result in an enhanced combustibility of CBAC.

Introduction

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) are important precursors to PM2.5 and ozone.1,2 Therefore, reducing the emission of VOCs is an important way to solve environmental problems. Activated carbon is the most widely used adsorption materials due to their large specific surface area, well-developed pore structures, abundant functional groups, and preferential affinity for some chemicals in the gas or liquid phase.3,4 As a kind of economical and efficient adsorbent, activated carbon is widely used in the adsorption and removal of environmental pollutants in recent years, especially for the adsorption and treatment process of VOCs.5 However, activated carbon will lose its adsorption properties due to saturability after a period of application. As a carrier of enriched pollutants, the activated carbon and its attached VOCs molecules will become hazardous waste, and there will exist a risk of combustion and explosion. When the temperature of activated carbon is increased and the heat dissipation rate is lower than its heat generating rate, heat accumulation will occur inside the activated carbon, approaching its igniting point and resulting in spontaneous combustion.6,7 According to thermogravimetric and differential scanning calorimetry (TG-DSC) investigations, Jayabalan et al.8,9 found that increasing the surface oxygenated groups of activated carbons can enhance the reactivity with air. In addition, it was reported that the increase of the number of oxygen-containing functional groups also has a promoting impact on the spontaneous combustion properties of activated carbon.10 van der Merwe et al.11 reported that a higher potassium content made the activated carbon more active and easier to combust. It has also been reported12 that activated carbon particles with smaller particle size are more likely to undergo oxidation reactions. Suzin et al.13 pointed out that the temperature point of the initial oxidation reaction of activated carbon is very important for the experimental operation and production safety in the process of temperature rise. Xiao et al.14 analyzed the oxidation process and oxidation mechanism of three coal-based activated carbons (CBACs) and determined the kinetic characteristics of the samples during the combustion process using the Kissinger-Akahira-Sunose (KAS) method. Li et al.15 studied the kinetic parameters and kinetic models of the desorption process of polycyclic aromatics in CBAC by TG-DTG analysis and KAS method.

The current study mainly focuses on the oxidative combustion of original activated carbon or CBAC, while there are fewer studies on the combustion characteristics and the mechanism of activated carbon or CBAC after adsorbing VOCs. And the CBAC prepared from coal is more abundant, lower in price, and has a wide range of industrial production applications compared with some other activated carbons.14,16 Therefore, three samples after adsorbing VOCs, i.e., acetone (AC), cyclohexane (CYH), and butyl acetate (BAC), were prepared here. The characteristic temperatures and mass loss during the oxidation of CBAC samples before and after adsorbing VOCs were studied by TG-DSC, and the gas products generated were analyzed by TG-FTIR system. In addition, a cone calorimeter was used to analyze the combustion performance characteristics, i.e., the time to ignition (TTI), heat release rate (HRR), and CO yield (COY), of the four samples.

Preparation

Firstly, the CBAC was filtered by a sieve with a size of 6 mesh. Secondly, the obtained samples were rinsed for three times with flowing deionized water to remove surface suspended solids and excess impurities. Then, they were dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 24 h. Next, the samples were weighed and adsorbed by organic solvents using an adsorbing device shown in Figure 1. After the adsorption of the CBAC reached saturation until the mass no longer increased, the samples were weighed again.

Diagram of the adsorption device for samples.

Experimental Methods

Structure Measurements

Firstly, the KBr reagent and the samples before and after adsorption were dried and ground, and then the mixed samples after grinding were pressed by a tablet press at a pressure of 20 MPa for 20 s. After that, the pressed samples were put into the infrared analyzer for testing. The scanning range of the samples was 400–4000 cm–1 with a resolution of 8 cm–1, and the scanning times were 64. The background should be deducted before the experiments.

Thermal Measurements

Simultaneous TG-DSC of the samples were performed using a NETZSCH STA 449C thermal analysis system under an air atmosphere. The samples to be tested weighed about 10 mg and placed in an Al2O3 crucible. The temperature was increased from 30 to 800 °C with a heating rate of 10 K/min and an air flow rate of 15 mL/min.

The TG-FTIR system connecting the simultaneous thermal analyzer and the Fourier transform infrared spectrometer can simultaneously analyze the gas products generated from the samples in the TG testing. In this study, the products generated from the samples before and after adsorption were analyzed by TG-FTIR as well.

Combustion Performance Measurements

Figure 2 shows a structural schematic diagram of the cone calorimeter (FTT) test device. The FTT used in the experiment selected ISO5660 (GB/T16172-2007) as the test standard, and the radiant heat flux was set to be 35 kW/m2. Each sample of 100 g was laid a layer of 20 mm in a tin foil inside a box with a size of 100 × 100 mm. During the experiment, the TTI, HRR, and COY were obtained.

Results and Discussion

The ratio of the mass increase of the CBAC after adsorption to the mass before adsorption is called saturation adsorption rate. According to the adsorption experiments, the mass fraction of adsorption of AC, CYH, and BAC by the CBAC is 40.6, 27.1, and 50.8% respectively, indicating an order of adsorption capacity of BAC > AC > CYH. As the existing research experiments show,17 the higher the relative molecular weight of VOCs, the greater the polarity, and the easier it is to be adsorbed by polar groups on the surface of activated carbon. In addition, the higher the boiling point of the adsorbent, the lower the saturated vapor pressure, and the greater the equivalent force between molecules, resulting in increased adsorption capacity.

Functional Groups Analysis

Figure 3 shows the infrared spectra curves of the four samples. The vibrations of C=O18,19 located at 1640 cm–1, as well as C–O–C20 at 1160, 1110, 1030, and 1058 cm–1, of the AC and BAC samples are significantly larger than those of the ROW sample. This results are reasonable because there exist O-containing groups in AC and BAC structures, while CYH does not have O-groups. Meanwhile, the absorbance of −OH peak (3670–3200 cm–1)21 of the AC and BAC samples increases greatly, but the CYH sample decreases. It is possibly due to the different water content in the samples. CYH has no hydrophilic groups (C=O) compared to AC and BAC, so the AC and BAC samples can attract more H2O than the CYH sample.

TG-DSC-FTIR Analysis

The TG experiments were carried out with an air flow rate of 15 mL/min and a heating rate of 10 K/min, and the obtained TG-DTG-DSC curves of CBAC before and after adsorption are illustrated in Figures 4 and 5.

TG-DTG-DSC curve of the ROW sample.

TG-DTG-DSC curve of the three VOCs-adsorbed samples. (a) AC sample, (b) CYH sample, and (c) BAC sample.

According to the division method for oxidation processes in coal, the oxidation-heating curve of the ROW sample can be divided into four characteristic temperatures point.22−25 Point T1 is called dry temperature, at which the DTG curve first reaches zero. Point T2 is the thermal decomposition temperature, at which the sample reaches its maximum weight due to oxygen absorption and is about to start pyrolysis. Ignition point T3 is determined using the tangent method, and T4 is the maximum mass loss rate point temperature, which is the valley point on the DTG curve of the combustion process. On the basis of these characteristic temperature points, the oxidation and combustion process of the ROW sample can be divided into four stages,22 i.e., water evaporation and desorption stage (Stage I), oxygen-absorption and mass-gain stage (Stage II), the thermal decomposition stage (Stage III), and the combustion stage (Stage IV), which is shown in Figure 4.

The first stage is the water evaporation and desorption stage, which ranges from the beginning to the T1. The mass loss of the ROW sample and endothermic DSC at this stage is mainly due to the water evaporation from the surface and inside the pores, as well as the gas desorption.

With the continuous increase in temperature, the oxidation of ROW sample enters into the oxygen-absorption and mass-gain stage, which ranges from T1 to T2. In this stage, the amount of oxygen chemically adsorbed by the ROW sample gradually increases, and much heat is released corresponding to a slowly exothermic trend in DSC curves.

Thereafter, the sample starts to enter the thermal decomposition stage, ranging from T2 to T3. During this stage, some oxygen-containing functional groups on the surface of CBAC and a large number of internal structures such as aromatic rings are broken, and the mass loss rate gradually increases. Also, the exothermic rate of DSC curve continues to increase.

When the temperature reaches the ignition point T3, the ROW sample starts to burn and enters the combustion stage with a rapid loss rate of the mass and a large amount of heat being released.

However, compared to the ROW sample, the oxidation process of the three adsorbed samples does not undergo the oxygen-absorption and mass-gain stage (Stage II), but rather a VOC desorption and combustion stage (Stage II), which is shown in Figure 5. At this time, the VOCs adsorbed in the pores of CBAC start to gradually escape from the constraint of van der Waals forces of CBAC molecules, and the VOCs escaped in gaseous form may combust due to the high temperature. The DSC curve at the later stage shows an exothermic phenomenon as the temperature increases because the exothermic heat from the VOCs combustion is gradually larger than the endothermic heat from the VOCs desorption.

From the TG curves of the samples before and after adsorption, the mass loss of the samples can be calculated with the decreasing order of BAC (59.59 wt %) > AC (45.53 wt %) > CYH (44.36 wt %) > ROW (44.24 wt %). Generally, the mass loss of the adsorbed sample is larger than that of the ROW sample, indicating that the adsorption of VOCs can make the oxidation and combustion process more complete.

It can be seen that the characteristic temperatures of the oxidation process of the CBAC after adsorbing VOCs are all significantly reduced, and the decrease in T2–T4 is mainly due to the large amount of heat released from the combustion of VOCs, thereby promoting the oxidation and combustion of CBAC.

Combustion Performance

The Time to Ignition

Figure 8 illustrates the initial combustion fire and the TTI of four samples. It can be seen that the TTI of the four samples increases in an order of CYH<BAC<AC<ROW. Clearly, the samples after adsorbing VOCs is more combustible, and the CYH sample is the most easily ignited one. From the flame scale, the flame heights are larger and combustion intensities are stronger for the adsorbed samples. In addition, the flame color of the ROW sample is light blue, indicating that the surface temperature of the ROW sample is lower than that of the other three adsorbed samples during combustion.

Initial combustion images and the TTI of CBAC before and after adsorbing VOCs.

Heat Release Rate

HRR refers to the heat released from per unit area of material combustion, reflecting the heat releasing rate of materials during combustion.26Figure 9a shows the HRR of the four samples. It is observed that the HRR of the ROW sample shows a slowly increasing trend, but the HRR curve of the VOCs-adsorbed samples shows a rapid increase to the peak and then a rapid decrease. This is owing to a fact that the low boiling point of VOCs enables it to volatilize with a combustible gas mixture being created which will burn instantly encountering an igniting source releasing large amounts of heat. With the VOCs being consumed gradually, the CBAC starts to burn and release heat.

The (a) HRR curve and (b) PkHRR of CBAC before and after adsorbing VOCs.

Figure 9b demonstrates the maximum heat releasing rate (PkHRR) for the four samples, with an order of CYH (663.41 kW/m2) > BAC (421.4 kW/m2) > AC (219.49 kW/m2) > ROW (71.4 kW/m2). It is observed that the PkHRR of three VOCs-adsorbed samples is much higher than that of ROW, where the PkHRR of the CYH sample is the highest, indicating that CYH sample has the most serious combustion hazard.

CO Yield

CO is a common gaseous product during combustion. Higher CO yield indicates higher toxicity and risk of the material in actual fires. The COY curves of the four samples tested by FTT are illustrated in Figure 10, exhibiting a decreased order of COY as CYH > BAC > AC > ROW. The COY of the VOCs-adsorbed samples is higher than ROW sample at the same time, indicating that the toxicity and risk of adsorbed samples are greater.

Conclusions

In this paper, the oxidation and combustion characteristics of CBAC before and after adsorbing VOCs, i.e., AC, CYH, and BAC, were studied by the thermal analyzer and cone calorimeter.

According to the TG-DSC and TG-FTIR results, it is found that the VOCs-adsorbed samples have an additional organic solvent desorption process, and the escaping VOCs will burn in the early stage, which increases the heat accumulation and promotes the oxidation and decomposition of CBAC.

The cone calorimeter experiments also show that the adsorbed VOCs in CBAC greatly improves the combustion performance in the early stage and enhances the fire hazard due to their shorter TTI and stronger flame intensity. And the HRR of the VOCs-adsorbed samples is all enhanced in a short period of time. Meanwhile, the COY of the VOCs-adsorbed samples shows a significant increase. In short, the fire hazards of the three adsorbed samples follow an order of CYH > BAC > AC.

In general, adsorbing VOCs in CBAC can push forward the combustion and enhance the combustion intensity. It is thus strongly recommended that before the application of CBAC in gas adsorption under high temperatures, the decomposition and combustion behaviors should be measured and analyzed in detail to prevent the fire accidents in process.
