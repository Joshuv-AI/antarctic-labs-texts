Emerging perspectives on leveraging physiologically based biopharmaceutics modeling (PBBM) for BCS class III biowaivers: a webinar summary
Authors: Lanyan (Lucy) Fang, Shereeni Veerasingham, Yunming Xu, Alfredo Garcia-Arieta, Sivacharan Kollipara, Yuvaneshwari Kanagasabapathy, Tausif Ahmed, Frederico Severino Martins, Sandra Suarez-Sharp
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The regulatory framework for Biopharmaceutics Classification System (BCS) class III drug products provides a pathway for streamlined biowaivers in drug development, eliminating the need for expensive and time-consuming bioavailability and bioequivalence studies while maintaining quality standards. To qualify, the Test must align with the excipients of the Reference product, while variations in flavor, color, and preservatives are allowed. Quantities of excipients, including changes in grade and percentage, must remain comparable, with cumulative differences not exceeding 10%. However, excipient modifications may affect drug release, potentially necessitating further evaluation for equivalence. Additionally, in cases where very rapid dissolution is not met, it limits the use of a BCS class III-based biowaiver for demonstrating bioequivalence. This article examines the regulatory landscape across regulatory agencies surrounding the application of physiologically based biopharmaceutics modeling (PBBM) to support BCS class III biowaivers, providing insights into the current level of acceptance and expectations. Additionally, it addresses the scientific and regulatory challenges associated with implementing PBBM, highlighting knowledge gaps and obstacles that could hinder adoption in regulatory decision-making. The article also presents case studies demonstrating practical approaches to leveraging PBBM and risk assessment for BCS class III biowaivers, offering valuable insights into successful applications and potential future directions.

Introduction

The drug development process is designed to meet regulatory requirements, ensuring product quality, efficacy, and safety. To facilitate regulatory approval while minimizing unnecessary burdens throughout the lifecycle of a drug product, the Biopharmaceutics Classification System (BCS) provides a critical framework in pharmaceutical sciences. This system plays a pivotal role in guiding the development and regulatory evaluation of immediate-release (IR) solid oral dosage forms or suspensions.

The International Council for Harmonization of Technical Requirements for Pharmaceuticals for Human Use (ICH) Guideline M91 has standardized recommendations for waiving the requirement to demonstrate in vivo bioequivalence (BE) for IR products, based on the BCS.2 This waiver is only applicable to products containing highly soluble drugs, provided certain conditions are met. For products with highly soluble, low permeable drugs (i.e., BCS class III), the waiver is recommended if the dissolution rate of both the Test and Reference products is very rapid (i.e., ≥ 85% within 15 minutes) within the physiological pH range (1.2-6.8). Importantly, drugs that completely dissolve within 15 minutes in the stomach and remain in solution after leaving the stomach are typically exempted from BE testing. This exemption applies regardless of permeability, as the rapid and complete release is considered self-evident.3 Because the absorption of BCS Class III drugs may involve uptake and efflux transporters, as well as paracellular pathways, excipients can potentially influence these mechanisms or alter the residence time within the absorption window.1 Therefore, it is recommended that excipients in the Test product be of similar grade and quantity to those in the Reference product to minimize the risk of differential effects on transporter activity or paracellular absorption.

The degree of similarity in excipient composition depends on the type of excipients used. For excipients that may impact absorption, the difference in quantity should not exceed 10% of the amount used in the Reference product, both individually and in total across all excipients. For other excipients, the difference is based on the percentage of each excipient in the core (excluding film-coating or capsule shells), with different acceptance limits for each excipient type. For example, for stearates, the percent difference relative to core weight should not exceed 0.5%. Additionally, the total allowable change in excipient percentages (including those that may affect absorption) is capped at 10%.1

If the drug substance degrades by more than 10% during the solubility assessment, it cannot be reliably assessed for solubility or classified. Additional data will be needed to support solubility determinations. For BCS class I and III drugs, stability in the gastrointestinal (GI) tract should be assessed using compendial or simulated gastric and intestinal fluids, with the drug solution incubated at 37°C for a duration reflective of in vivo conditions, one hour in gastric fluid, and three hours in intestinal fluid. If significant degradation (> 10%) occurs during solubility assessment, solubility cannot be reliably determined, and therefore, the drug substance cannot be classified.1

While these conditions are designed to ensure BE, modeling-and-simulation approaches may justify BCS Class III biowaivers beyond current limits on formulation composition, provided the analyses explicitly address uptake and efflux transporters and the potential impact of excipients, including excipient–transporter interactions.4

Recent advancements in physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) absorption modeling and physiologically based biopharmaceutics modeling (PBBM) provide an opportunity to refine and expand the criteria for BCS class III biowaivers. PBBM incorporates both the PK behavior and biopharmaceutic factors of a drug, including the effects of excipients and formulation characteristics on absorption. It accounts for key processes, such as dissolution, GI transit, drug permeation, and site-specific versus site-independent absorption, offering a comprehensive representation of drug behavior in the body. This enhanced understanding can support the granting of biowaivers for formulations containing BCS class III drugs.

Once a PBBM/PBPK model is developed and validated, it can be used to assess how in vitro or in vivo dissolution differences affect bioavailability and define the maximum allowable difference that still ensures BE. These models could help conclude that BE is achievable even when Test and Reference products do not exhibit very rapid or similar dissolution profiles. In one such case, a validated PBBM supported the similarity testing (despite f2 mismatch) and enabled a biowaiver by demonstrating BE for both strengths via virtual trials. A dissolution safe space (> 85% in 60 minutes) was defined, thereby supporting relaxed dissolution criteria for BCS class III drugs using PBBM.5 Note that if the reference listed product (RLD) does not dissolved very rapidly, which is often the case, the BCS biowaiver for BCS class III drugs is not acceptable, even if the Test product exhibits similar dissolution profiles.

This manuscript explores the evolving regulatory landscape for BCS class III biowaivers, addressing critical challenges faced by the scientific community, industry, and regulatory agencies. It emphasizes the potential of PBPK/PBBM modeling methodologies to enhance drug development efficiency, reduce regulatory burden, and facilitate patient access to safe, affordable generic medicines, especially for low-risk applications, such as the impact of drug substance degradation on BE. Furthermore, this paper summarizes key discussions from the recent webinar, Leveraging PBBM/PBPK in Support of BCS Class III Biowaivers.

Webinar presentations

On September 24, 2024, Simulations Plus, Inc., hosted a webinar titled "Leveraging PBBM/PBPK modeling in Support of BCS Class III Biowaivers." The session featured a distinguished group of experts, including Drs. Kollipara, Ahmed, Kanagasabapathy, Veerasingham, Fang, Xu, Martins, Garcia-Arieta, and Suarez-Sharp. They explored the application of PBBM/PBPK absorption modeling to support biowaivers for BCS class III drugs. A summary of the presentations and discussions is presented below.

Leveraging PBBM beyond ICH M9: the crucial role of risk evaluation

While PBBM/PBPK absorption modeling can support biowaivers beyond the traditional criteria for BCS class III drugs, further risk classification within BCS class III molecules can help facilitate waivers. Dr. Sivacharan proposed that low-risk category molecules are those where only passive transport is involved and the excipients are identical to those in the Reference product. In such cases, changes in manufacturing processes resulting in dissolution profiles not meeting the acceptance criteria could still be justified through PBBM. The medium-risk category includes molecules where both passive and active transport are involved, and differences in excipients between the Reference and Test products exist, but the absorption-related excipients remain similar. For high-risk category products, absorption is entirely governed by active transporters, and the composition of the Test products differs significantly from the Reference, including the excipients that impact absorption. Since the absorption of these drugs is limited by permeability, even minor modifications in excipient composition or formulation can lead to significant changes in their performance. Therefore, a deep understanding of excipient interactions and their potential influence on permeability is critical for ensuring BE and justifying biowaivers for such formulations.

This risk categorization further aids in utilizing modeling approaches to obtain waivers for BCS class III molecules when certain criteria in ICH M9 are not met. In the case of BCS class I drugs, the risk associated with implementing modeling in lieu of a PK study to determine the impact of not meeting dissolution requirements is relatively low, if there is sufficient justification for such changes. This is due to their inherently high permeability, which reduces the likelihood that variations in excipients will significantly affect absorption.

Applying PBBM/PBPK modeling for deviations from ICH M9 recommendations—low/medium risk cases

Using PBBM/PBPK modeling and simulation can be valuable when dissolution profiles differ from the recommended rates for BCS class III drugs. During Dr. Xu’s presentation, he noted that PBBM provides an effective way to address this challenge by simulating how different dissolution profiles affect bioavailability. Mechanistic models, such as the Z-factor model, product particle size distribution (P-PSD) model, and extended Wang-Flanagan model, help define a safe space for dissolution. This allows for greater flexibility in, e.g., manufacturing process while maintaining BE. He added that, when dissolution criteria are challenging to meet, modeling software like GastroPlus® or Simcyp™ may be used to simulate the GI environments and predict in vivo outcomes, supporting the biowaiver by demonstrating that the dissolution rate variations do not significantly impact bioavailability.

It has been suggested that the dissolution rate of these drugs could be slow enough to make dissolution, rather than permeability, the controlling factor in absorption.6 Simulations accounting for a “worst-case scenario”, where absorption occurs within the absorption window of the proximal intestine (duodenum and jejunum), demonstrated that the bioavailability differences in drugs (like amoxicillin, atenolol, and cimetidine) measured by maximum plasma observed drug concentration (Cmax) and area under the concentration-time curve (AUC) would be less than 10%, even if the Test product dissolves more slowly (i.e., 85% dissolution in 60 minutes compared to the 15 minutes of the Reference). This was experimentally confirmed, as delaying the dissolution of cimetidine up to 120 minutes did not reduce its absorption in humans.7 Similarly, BE was observed between an IR tablet and an oral solution of metformin,8 as well as between an IR tablet and a prolonged-release metformin tablet that released its content over 3 hours.9 Even when dissolution profiles differed, 14 famotidine IR products were considered bioequivalent, with BE ensured as long as complete dissolution occurred within 99 minutes.10

BCS biowaivers typically rely on similar in vitro dissolution (IVD) profiles for Test and Reference products: within 30 minutes (≥ 85% for the mean percent dissolved in ≤ 30 minutes) for BCS class I drugs (using the f2 similarity factor) and within 15 minutes (≥ 85% for the mean percent dissolved in ≤ 15 minutes) for BCS class III drugs, which does not require f2 calculation. This assumption is based on the belief that if IVD is similar under specific pH conditions, in vivo dissolution will also be similar. However, this assumption is not always true.11 Therefore, a PBBM/PBPK absorption model that predicts in vivo dissolution and absorption would be more reliable than relying on this assumption, especially in those cases where dissolution requirements (i.e., acceptance criterion and similarity testing) are not met.

To demonstrate the application of PBBM for low-risk scenarios in support of BCS class III biowaivers, Drs. Kollipara and Ahmed presented a case study in which PBBM was utilized to obtain a waiver for a BCS class III drug product, despite a dissolution dissimilarity. The presentation highlighted how PBBM plays a crucial role in supporting BCS class III biowaivers, particularly when the dissolution criteria specified in ICH M9 are not met. It also emphasized the importance of categorizing risk within BCS class III drugs, which can guide the selection of the most appropriate strategies for obtaining biowaivers. Furthermore, the case study underscored the potential of PBBM to go beyond traditional BCS class III biowaiver criteria, presenting an attractive option for regulatory approvals and facilitating the possibility of more approvals in the near future. Specifically, the drug product in question is available in two strengths: 50 mg and 100 mg. A fasting BE study was conducted for the 100-mg strength in Market-A, and these data were leveraged to support the BE application for Market-B. However, the f2 value at pH 6.8 was found to be below 50 due to the slower release of the Reference product from Market-B, whereas, at other pH conditions, pH 1.2 and 4.5, dissolution similarity was seen between pivotal Test and Reference products between Market-A and Market-B.

The PBBM was developed and rigorously validated using both in-house BE study data for a Market-A 100-mg product and available literature. By incorporating the dissolution data for the Reference product from Market-B into the model, it was shown that BE could still be achieved, even with similarity f2 value below 50. Additionally, a dissolution safe space was established, indicating that a drug release of 85% within 60 minutes would not significantly impact BE. This assessment was performed for both 50-mg and 100-mg strengths, thereby enabling fasting BE study waiver for both strengths for Market-B. Importantly, they concluded that PBBM/PBPK absorption modeling could be a valid alternative to in vivo BE studies for BCS class III drugs with varying dissolution rates or non-rapid dissolution. However, it is crucial that the model be thoroughly verified and validated, as any incorrect decision based on this model could have significant regulatory consequences. This example highlights the importance of using PBBM/PBPK for regulatory support to get waivers for time-consuming and costly human BE studies. It also demonstrates how this approach can help avoid unnecessary human BE studies altogether, especially in cases where the same product is leveraged to different countries, where only the RLD is different. Further, this presentation also proposed further sub-categories within BCS III classes, to enable the use of modeling approaches for waivers in cases where conventional BCS biowaiver criteria are not met (Fig. 1). For generic drug product development, risk categorization is based on permeability mechanisms, availability of sufficient clinical information in literature, and composition.

The presentation by Dr. Lanyan (Lucy) Fang explored the application of PBPK absorption modeling to support BE assessments for BCS class III drugs. Dr. Fang referred to the ICH M9 guidance and product specific guidance (PSG) recommendations for the application of BCS-based biowaivers for generics containing BCS class III drugs. PBPK modeling was introduced as a promising tool to assess scenarios which deviate from the conditions specified in the ICH M9 guidance, such as non-Q1/Q2 (qualitatively and quantitative similar) formulations and active pharmaceutic ingredient degradation, supporting BCS-based biowaiver for generic drugs.

Dr. Fang presented two relevant case studies which illustrated the practical application of PBPK modeling in this area. In the first case, nadolol oral tablet is a nonselective β-blocker and approved for managing hypertension and angina. With a molecular weight of 309.42 g/mol and a logP value of 0.38, it has low lipid solubility, limiting its ability to cross the blood-brain barrier and reducing central nervous system effects. Its oral bioavailability is approximately 35%, primarily binding to alpha-1 acid glycoprotein with an unbound fraction of 0.7. Nadolol has a low to moderate volume of distribution (0.3 to 0.4 L/kg), indicating limited extravascular distribution. It has a long plasma half-life of 17 to 23 hours and is not hepatically metabolized. Instead, around 75% is excreted unchanged via the kidneys, with the remainder eliminated through non-renal routes, such as biliary excretion. Its renal clearance is approximately 0.101 L/h/kg.12 Notably, nadolol is a substrate of the cation transporters OCT1, OCT2, MATE1, MATE2-K, and of P-glycoprotein (P-gp).13 This case includes molecules when both passive and active transport are involved, and differences in excipients between the Reference and Test products exist, but based on risk assessment, the impact of excipients differences (Q1) was not considered clinically relevant. The PSG recommends a fasting PK BE study. PBPK modeling was used to support expanding BCS-based biowaiver for a non-Q1 generic nadolol (BCS class III) drug product. A PBPK model for nadolol was developed and validated using intravenous and oral PK,14,15,16 which well predicted the PK profiles of different strengths (i.e., 10, 30, and 80 mg) with prediction errors < 20%. In the PBPK model for oral nadolol tablets, dissolution data were incorporated using Z-factor dissolution model and parameter sensitivity analysis (PSA) showed that PK parameters (Cmax and AUCs) are sensitive to Z-factor values (Fig. 2). For the model application, virtual bioequivalence (VBE) simulations were conducted by incorporating different theoretical dissolution profiles with different release rates (i.e., different Z-factor values) to determine dissolution safe space. The PBPK modeling and VBE simulations demonstrated that non-Q1/Q2 nadolol IR tablets with dissimilar dissolution profiles are of low risk for non-BE when the dissolution profiles are within the suggested dissolution safe space (when the Z-factor for the generic product is decreased by 10% to 60% compared to that of the RLD). The presented PBPK modeling framework could help expand biowaiver to non-Q1/Q2 and non-very rapidly dissolved BCS class III drug products.

The second case study focused on cladribine tablets, a BCS class III drug that degrades significantly (> 10%) in acidic environments, raising challenges for a BCS-based biowaiver for this drug product as per ICH M9 guidance. Cladribine is a highly soluble drug, with oral bioavailability of approximately 40%17,17 with limited absorption due to transporter-mediated efflux.17 Cladribine is categorized as a BCS class III drug and is eligible for a BCS-based biowaiver.20,20 Cladribine is susceptible to acidic degradation in vitro, while no change to cladribine bioavailability was reported in clinical studies with proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), as well as in a food effect study.21

This case constitutes a low-risk category where the excipients are identical to those in the Reference product. Although the Test product matches the RLD in composition (quantitively and quantitatively), substantial degradation during solubility testing (> 10%) in a BCS class III drug may necessitate additional studies to confirm true solubility.

A PBPK model was developed and used to evaluate whether BCS waiver is applicable for cladribine tablets with degradation (Fig. 3). The cladribine PBPK disposition and absorption model was developed using intravenous and oral PK data with the incorporation of cladribine IVD data with degradation in the model. The IVD data using a fasted state simulated gastric fluid medium was found to be biopredictive for fasted conditions. The model was validated by well predicting the cladribine PK with and without the administration of Proton Pump Inhibitors (PPIs). This also demonstrated that the degradation of cladribine at lower pH does not have a significant impact on PK for both generic and RLD products, as evidenced by the similar PK in the absence and presence of PPIs. Using VBE simulations, the model also demonstrated BE between Test cladribine 10 mg tablets and RLD by incorporating similar biopredictive total cladribine dissolution data with similar degradation (even > 10%) profiles for generic and RLD products. This modeling approach showed that comparable biopredictive dissolution and degradation data for generic and RLD products, in combination with other information (e.g., solubility, formulation comparison as per ICH M9 guidance), provide evidence for low risk of non-BE for the generic BCS class III drug product and support a BCS class III-based biowaiver for this case. Hence, the PSG for cladribine oral tablets was updated to include a BCS class III-based biowaiver option.

Dr. Fang noted that while PBPK modeling holds great potential, challenges remain, including the limited availability of biopredictive dissolution and PK data for model validation. Future efforts should focus on collaborative initiatives to generate high-quality in vitro and dissolution data, extending validated methodologies to broader applications, and developing standardized workflows. Dr. Fang emphasized that PBPK modeling can facilitate the expansion of BCS class III biowaivers for IR products, particularly for those with non-Q1/Q2 formulation differences, slower dissolution rates, or stability concerns.

The cladribine case was also presented by Dr. Shereeni Veerasingham. In the Canadian market, the Reference product, Mavenclad® (EMD Serono, Canada; approved 2017,17), includes cladribine complexed with hydroxypropyl betadex which may enhance the stability and solubility of the drug substance. The challenges in obtaining a biowaiver for a subsequent-entry (generic) cladribine product are underscored by the multiple requests for guidance in demonstrating high solubility of the drug and initial deficiency notices issued for submissions.

A recent application included a PBBM-informed risk assessment of the potential impact of cladribine degradation in acidic conditions on in vivo performance of a cladribine tablet in support of a BCS-based biowaiver per the ICH M9 guideline. The systemic disposition parameters for the PBBM were obtained by fitting data for a 3 mg intravenous infusion over 1 hour.18 The simulated plasma concentration profile matched that for the observed data, confirming an appropriate disposition model. The oral absorption model employed data for the Test cladribine tablet and the Reference product, Mavenclad® tablets. Input for the model included in vitro cladribine degradation rates in acidic media and dissolution profiles in phosphate buffer, pH 6.8. The model was validated with single profile simulations for oral administration of Mavenclad® tablets under fasting conditions, with and without a PPI.18 Simulated plasma profiles and PK parameters matched the data observed in the clinical study. A PSA indicated that changes in gastric pH over a range that reflects normal variability, or the presence of a PPI, had a minor impact on the Cmax parameter. This suggests that the degradation of cladribine in an acidic pH environment (range 1 to 3) likely does not lead to a notable difference in bioavailability. Further, model-based single profile simulations for oral administration of the Test tablet were compared to simulated and observed profiles for the Mavenclad® tablet. The predicted ratios (Test: Reference) were close to unity for the Cmax and AUC parameters, supporting the conclusion that cladribine degradation in acidic conditions did not impact the bioavailability of the Test cladribine tablet relative to the Mavenclad® tablet. The regulatory impact of the modeling was considered to be low as other information was available to support the risk assessment. Overall, the PBBM-informed risk assessment supported a BCS-based biowaiver for a subsequent-entry cladribine tablet.

Dr. Veerasingham noted that this case study illustrates the utility of a PBBM-informed risk assessment in supporting a biowaiver request, despite substantial drug degradation in acidic media. The approach employed PSA to evaluate if changes in gastric pH impact cladribine PK parameters, and model-based simulations to assess relative bioavailability of the Test and CRP formulations. A carefully considered modeling strategy and application is helpful in mitigating risk of biowaivers.

Applying PBBM/PBPK for deviations from ICH M9 recommendations- high risk cases

The waiver for in vivo BE studies in humans is recommended for BCS class III drugs when the Test and Reference products contain qualitatively and quantitatively similar excipients. Deviations from these recommendations constitute a higher risk requiring clinical BE studies. Minor differences in colorants and flavoring agents may be acceptable if they are present in very small amounts. Although some dyes and flavoring agents have been shown to influence intestinal transporters in experimental settings, 22 only large amounts (25 mg/kg) of United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic (FD&C) Red 40 or E129, a potent organic anion transporting polypeptide 2B1 (OATP2B1) inhibitor, reduced the bioavailability of the OATP2B1 substrate fexofenadine in P-gp-deficient mice. This highlights the potential role of animal and in vitro permeability studies in supporting exceptions outlined in ICH guidelines.

Maintaining identical excipients in similar amounts may not always be feasible due to patent protections, manufacturing constraints, or cost considerations. Excipients can influence drug absorption by modifying GI transit time, altering luminal volumes, impacting permeability through transporter interactions, or affecting metabolism within the GI tract.233,24,245 While the approval of generics with different excipients suggests that they do not significantly affect permeability, the absence of evidence does not necessarily confirm a lack of effect.6 Some excipients, like sorbitol and mannitol, may exhibit similar impacts on intestinal absorption, yet their effects could be masked or counteracted by other formulation components. Beyond these factors, considerations such as drug “speciation” may also be relevant as part of biopharmaceutics risk assessment. PBBM can also address speciation when appropriate due to its mechanistic approach, though this is generally not expected to represent a significant risk for highly soluble drugs.

Currently, in vitro and in vivo tools for evaluating excipient interactions remain underdeveloped,266 though efforts are underway to compile a knowledge base for better understanding excipient disposition and interaction potential.27 In vitro models do not fully replicate the complexity of the small intestine, and PBPK modeling has some limitations in translating in vitro and physiological data into predictions of human absorption and systemic exposure. The ICH M9 Questions and Answers document288 acknowledges that while PBPK absorption modeling aids in assessing formulation risks, it cannot fully account for all excipient-related absorption differences due to gaps in mechanistic understanding and high-quality in vivo data. Consequently, model-based risk assessments alone cannot justify excipient changes beyond recommended limits, though well-validated PBPK models can support risk assessments in specific cases where excipient effects are well understood. Therefore, presently, using PBPK absorption modeling systematically to justify a qualitative and/or a quantitative change in excipients appears to be a challenge.

PBBM/PBPK absorption modeling has been used to assess the impact that different factors (e.g., altered solubility, dissolution, effective permeability, intestinal metabolism and transport, GI fluid volumes or motility) may have on the bioavailability of a given drug by means of sensitivity analysis and, subsequently, assess whether excipient effects need to be considered to grant a biowaiver.29 A deviation beyond the established limits of a well-characterized excipient may be acceptable if it is known, or can be demonstrated, that the excipient does not influence physiological pathways, such as GI transit time or osmotic effects, that could affect the absorption of the drug, particularly when the drug is not a substrate for intestinal transporters or metabolic enzymes. The existence of validated PBPK absorption models for the drug of interest is useful to give reassurance that all of the relevant pathways (e.g., transporters/enzymes) involved in the PK of the drugs are known and taken into account by the models, but experimental data to validate the model performance when all these factors are affected may not be available.

Recent research by the FDA and the University of Maryland has demonstrated that excipients can influence drug absorption of many BCS class III drugs. For example, hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) and microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) have been shown to reduce the Cmax and AUC of cimetidine and acyclovir.30

A published study from the collaboration of the FDA and Pharmaron examined the impact of permeability from four excipients used as antioxidants: ascorbic acid, cysteine, propyl gallate, and alpha tocopherol, using the in vitro dissolution absorption system (IDAS). The IDAS combines dissolution testing with Caco-2 assays to provide permeability data for four BCS class III drug substances (acyclovir, atenolol, ranitidine, and cimetidine) in the presence of these excipients. The results showed that these excipients, across a wide range of concentration levels, do not impact the permeability of the tested BCS class III drug substances.31 This experiment could potentially be used to quantify the correlation between drug permeability and excipient concentrations, offering input for excipient-permeability models in PBBM. Subsequent internal studies from the FDA explored the BE limit based on the percent change in human effective permeability and demonstrated that the permeability range for the Test drug product to be bioequivalent to the Reference product is broad, indicating the potential of using excipient-permeability models to support the biowaiver decisions. To address these challenges, PBBM is frequently employed to simulate how excipients influence dissolution and absorption. However, none of the available software platforms currently includes a generalizable excipient-permeability model that provides reliable prediction for all excipients. The flexibility of these models supports the exploration of various formulation strategies, reduces reliance on very rapid dissolution criteria, and promotes a science-based evaluation of biowaiver eligibility, though they have yet to fully capture all aspects of excipient-related impacts. Dr. Xu highlighted that PBBM provides a science-driven approach to addressing the challenges associated with BCS class III biowaivers. By modeling the excipients, PBBM helps to provide justifications even when very rapid dissolution or Q1/Q2 criteria are not fully met. PBBM is expected to play an increasingly important role in expanding biowaiver applicability, facilitating faster and more efficient drug development.

Yuvaneshwari Kanagasabapathy presented a case study showcasing the application of PBBM in generic drug product development. The study focused on biopharmaceutics risk assessment for excipients added or deleted in BCS class III Fixed-Dose Combination (FDC) drug products. A PBBM/PBPK absorption model (Fig. 4) was developed to demonstrate BE between the RLD and the proposed FDC product. After validating standalone drug models, the FDC model was further validated.

The robustness of the model was confirmed through VBE trials, including an evaluation of food effect, reflecting the edge of failure (Fig. 5). The tablet core of the Reference product utilizes well-established excipients, while the Test product features qualitatively and quantitatively different disintegrants, stabilizers, and diluents. A comprehensive biopharmaceutics risk assessment was conducted, focusing on the impact on membrane permeation, as the drug substances are highly soluble, making solubility impact clinically insignificant. The key assessment aspects for evaluating the impact of excipient changes on BE included 1) literature evidence on potential impact from excipients listed in regulatory safety databases, 2) comparison of maximum daily exposure of excipients with the proposed daily dose, 3) literature reports on potential effects of excipients on drug metabolism or transporters, and 4) evaluation of appropriate ex-vivo models to assess the permeability impact of critical excipients.

Virtual population PK trials (n = 10 runs) were conducted for Drugs A and B using their individually developed models. The study design, including the number of subjects and variability, was aligned with the description of the comparator study.

The validated model was used to achieve several objectives: 1) virtual population-based BE between the FDC Test and Reference products with Q1/Q2 non-similarity (for both higher and lower strengths), where a quality control dissolution medium was fitted into a Z-factor model, 2) relaxing dissolution specification limits, and 3) exploring the establishment of permeability safe space.

This modeling exercise suggests the potential for relaxing dissolution criteria for BCS class III drugs, shifting from very rapid release to rapid release, is contingent on a biopharmaceutics risk assessment of newly added excipients. The presentation concluded with a call for the generic drug industry to collaborate in building data, particularly on excipient impact on permeability, to support expanding the scope of the ICH M9 guidance to include PBBM for drug products currently ineligible for waiver under BCS class III.

Summary of session discussions

A panel discussion followed the presentation, where the experts addressed several questions raised. The valuable insights from this discussion are summarized below.

Question 1 - What has been your experience using PBBM to justify BCS class III biowaivers when Q1/Q2 conditions are not met? Is the existing knowledge on excipients adequate to quantify their effect on bioavailability using PBBM/PBPK absorption modeling?

The panelists highlighted that PBBM has shown biowaivers for BCS class III drugs can be justified under certain conditions, even in the absence of Q1/Q2 compliance. However, a key challenge lies in accurately quantifying the impact of excipients on drug solubility, permeability, and dissolution, as these factors can significantly influence bioavailability and BE. Many excipients have been well studied and have shown to have no impact on bioavailability, in such case, dissolution data can be incorporated into the model and predict PK. If an excipient or multiple excipients are found to potentially affect permeability and consequently, bioavailability, an empirical correlation, such as the relationship between the concentration of the excipient and effective permeability, could be utilized to build an excipient-permeability model. This model could then be integrated into the PBBM to evaluate the effect of the excipient on PK. If the effect of the excipient is clearly understood and validated through clinical studies, it is possible to use this piece of information to support the proposed objective of the PBBM.

Limitations in excipients knowledge and impact on PBBM predictions

A recurring concern among panelists is the limited mechanistic understanding of excipients in influencing drug absorption. While extensive physicochemical data exist for excipients, their effects on bioavailability remain difficult to generalize. Excipients are often studied within specific formulations, making it challenging to extrapolate their impact across different drug products. Certain classes, such as surfactants and solubilizers, have been investigated in more detail, but broader, systematic studies are needed to strengthen PBBM/PBBM model predictions.

One case highlighted a successful biowaiver justification for FDC of two BCS class III drugs, despite significant qualitative and quantitative differences in excipients. However, the absence of in vivo data for those specific excipient changes introduced uncertainty, as there was no literature evidence to support their precise impact on bioavailability. This underscores the current gap in excipients data and its implications for PBBM reliability.

Challenges in PBBM/PBPK absorption model development

Panelists acknowledge that PBBM/PBPK absorption models hold promise as alternatives to clinical BE studies for BCS class III drugs, especially when Q1/Q2 criteria are not met. However, ensuring the robustness of these models requires addressing key challenges:

Quantifying Excipients Influence: The differences in physiochemical properties between excipients in Test and Reference formulations must be well understood to assess risk and ensure regulatory acceptance.

Question 4 - What are the regulatory expectations for PBBM development and validation in support of BCS class III based biowaivers when Q1/Q2 conditions are not met? What are the data requirements?

Clinical PK data remain valuable for model validation, but when such data are not available, well-designed in vitro permeability and dissolution experiments may serve as credible alternatives. To ensure regulatory acceptance, comprehensive parameter optimization, rigorous validation, and sensitivity analyses are crucial components of the model credibility. These steps help establish the reliability of the model and its ability to predict the biopharmaceutical performance of the Test formulation under non-Q1/Q2 conditions.

Concluding remarks

Despite harmonized guidelines, challenges persist in the effective implementation of biowaivers for BCS class III drug products. Compositional restrictions for excipients, concerns over absorption variability, and the need for robust BE assessments often hinder regulatory acceptance of the biowaiver approach. Additionally, factors such as transporter interactions, GI stability, and dissolution kinetics further complicate the evaluation process, underscoring the importance of a mechanistic and evidence-based approach to excipient risk assessment. PBBM/PBPK absorption modeling is a powerful tool for supporting BCS class III biowaivers, particularly in cases where deviations from ICH M9 guidance occur. Traditional biowaiver assessments rely on dissolution and formulation similarity; however, real-world challenges such as dissolution dissimilarity, degradation concerns, and excipient differences often make adherence to these criteria impractical. PBBM provides a mechanistic approach to evaluating BE by integrating key physiological, physicochemical, and formulation-dependent factors into predictive models, allowing for a more flexible and scientifically robust assessment.

PBBM can separate and quantify the individual contributions of dissolution and degradation to the observed PK of a drug. For instance, a PBPK/PBBM model using the P-PSD approach allows dissolution and degradation to be modeled as independent processes. This method enables dynamic tracking of drug dissolution rates based on real-time changes in particle size, solubility, and bulk concentration, while simultaneously accounting for drug degradation using independently measured first-order kinetics. Such an approach provides a deeper understanding of the interplay between formulation differences and in vivo drug behavior, thereby helping to justify biowaivers even when dissolution criteria outlined in ICH M9 are not fully met.

A major advantage of PBBM is its ability to define a "dissolution safe space," which identifies conditions under which BE can be maintained despite variations in dissolution profiles. This is particularly valuable when the f2 similarity factor falls below the regulatory threshold or when excipient differences extend beyond the recommended range. By demonstrating that such deviations do not impact drug absorption in a clinically meaningful way, PBBM enhances regulatory confidence in biowaiver decisions and reduces the need for unnecessary human BE studies. Furthermore, PBBM supports a risk-based evaluation of excipients, helping to assess whether formulation differences influence drug permeability, intestinal transit time, or degradation pathways. It enables a systematic sensitivity analysis, providing scientific justification for deviations from ICH M9 requirements when sufficient mechanistic understanding is available.

Although traditionally considered inert, excipients can significantly influence the oral bioavailability of BCS Class III drugs through various mechanisms, including changes in dissolution and solubility, modulation of gastrointestinal transit and luminal fluid volumes, alterations in intestinal permeability, and interactions with transporters or metabolic enzymes. Building on recent PBBM advancements, meaningful progress requires systematic evaluation of these effects using complementary experimental and modeling approaches. Greater success in applying PBBM to support approval of BCS Class III drugs will depend on integrating evidence across in vitro, in situ, and in vivo studies. In vitro assays, such as dissolution-permeation systems and transporter-specific models (e.g., Caco-2), can quantify permeability shifts and transporter interactions. In situ techniques, including intestinal perfusion or closed-loop models, can validate these mechanisms under physiological conditions and generate quantitative data for model development. Clinical pharmacokinetic studies, such as those reporting PEG-400 or mannitol effects on ranitidine absorption and transit, further illustrate that excipient impacts can be dose-dependent. Incorporating such data into PBBM enables sensitivity analyses that predict absorption outcomes under realistic scenarios. Ultimately, collaborative efforts among regulators, industry, and academia to align mechanistic insights with real bioequivalence outcomes will be essential to strengthening the evidence base and expanding the regulatory acceptance of PBBM.

As regulatory agencies increasingly recognize the value of modeling and simulation in BE assessments, PBBM is expected to play an integral role in expanding the applicability of BCS class III biowaivers, facilitating more efficient drug development and regulatory approval pathways.
