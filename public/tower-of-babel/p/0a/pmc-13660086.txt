Context-dependent reconfiguration of functional connectivity in posterior (VWFA-1) and anterior (VWFA-2) visual word form area under attentional and linguistic demands
Authors: Joonwoo Kim, Solbin Lee, Jeahong Kim, Kichun Nam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe visual word form area (VWFA) has been proposed to function as a multiplex hub integrating both attentional and linguistic processing, yet how its functionally distinct subregions dynamically reconfigure their network connectivity across cognitive contexts remains unclear. This study examined resting-state and task-evoked functional connectivity of two VWFA subregions—posterior VWFA-1 and anterior VWFA-2—with predefined attention and language network regions of interest in 20 healthy adults performing a visuospatial flanker task and a Korean lexical decision task. We hypothesized that the posterior-to-anterior anatomical gradient corresponds to an attention-to-language functional role differentiation, observable both in intrinsic connectivity and in task-dependent reconfiguration. At rest, the VWFA exhibited a posterior-to-anterior intrinsic gradient, with VWFA-1 preferentially coupled to the attention network and VWFA-2 to language regions. Across both tasks, task context differentially shifted attention-network connectivity between VWFA-1 and VWFA-2, broadly following the intrinsic gradient. The alignment was only partial, however, as during lexical decision it was the posterior VWFA-1 rather than the anterior VWFA-2 that increased its coupling with the language network. Behaviorally, attention connectivity predicted accuracy across tasks from both subregions equivalently rather than from the gradient itself, suggesting a shared attention-based mechanism rather than gradient-specific contributions. Together, these findings provide preliminary support for the view that intrinsic VWFA organization shapes task-specific routing of attention network interactions, while behavioral performance converges on a shared attention-based mechanism.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12993-026-00356-8.Keywords: Visual word form area, Functional connectivity, Resting-state fMRI, Dorsal attention network, Language network

Introduction

Understanding how the visual word form area (VWFA) interfaces with broader cortical systems and how this interface shifts with task demands is essential for elucidating the neural architecture of reading. This is because reading is not a unitary process localized to a single region but rather emerges from distributed interactions across multiple brain networks. The left ventral occipitotemporal cortex (vOT) serves as a critical interface for visual word recognition.

The VWFA supports the transformation of visual input into abstract orthographic representations that enable efficient mapping from print to higher-level linguistic representation. Specifically, it is proposed to coordinate with language networks for phonological and semantic processing [8, 10], attention networks for feature selection and spatial orienting [15, 21], and visual networks for perceptual encoding [34, 35]—suggesting that its functional contribution depends not only on its local computations but critically on its pattern of connectivity with these distributed cortical systems. Accordingly, characterizing the VWFA’s functional connectivity architecture—both its stable intrinsic organization and its dynamic task-dependent reconfiguration—is therefore fundamental to understanding how the brain supports fluent reading.

Early characterizations portrayed the VWFA as a domain-specific module selectively tuned to letter strings through reading experience [10, 24]. This view was supported by converging evidence from both resting-state and task-based functional connectivity studies suggesting that the VWFA maintains privileged coupling with perisylvian language regions. Resting-state functional connectivity (RSFC) studies demonstrate that the VWFA maintains stronger intrinsic coupling with inferior frontal (Broca’s area) and posterior superior temporal (Wernicke’s area) regions relative to neighboring ventral visual regions such as the fusiform face area [32, 36]. However, the extent of this preferential connectivity has been debated and depends on methodological choices and control comparisons. This connectivity pattern emerges early in development: connectivity patterns at age 5, before literacy acquisition, predict the future location of word-selective responses at age 8 [30]. Individual differences in VWFA-language connectivity at rest predict word recognition ability [16, 32]. Task-based studies further reveal that reading intensifies VWFA engagement with language networks, with stronger connectivity during word processing predicting faster lexical access [28, 31] and better reading outcomes [14]. These converging lines of evidence supported the hypothesis that the VWFA operates as an integrated component of the language network, with intrinsic coupling scaffolding functional specialization for print.

However, emerging evidence challenges the purely linguistic account by demonstrating significant VWFA engagement with dorsal attention systems. Under a purely linguistic account, VWFA connectivity should be dominated by coupling with language regions; yet connectivity analyses reveal substantial coupling with attention networks as well. RSFC analyses reveal that the VWFA exhibits functional coupling with the intraparietal sulcus (IPS), frontal eye fields (FEF), and middle temporal area (MT+)—core regions of the dorsal attention network [7, 36]. Task-based studies support this multiplex perspective: during non-linguistic visual attention tasks, the VWFA increases connectivity with dorsal frontoparietal regions, notably the FEF and IPS, while decreasing coupling with language areas such as the IFG, AG, and SMG [22, 36]. Even during reading, attentional modulation correlates with task demands, with spatial attention to word location enhancing VWFA-IPS connectivity while attention to word identity strengthens VWFA-language coupling [15, 21]. Individual differences in VWFA-attention network connectivity have been linked to performance on visual search tasks involving letter strings [13]. These findings align with the multiplex hub model [7], suggesting that rather than functioning as an isolated language module, the VWFA integrates linguistic and attentional information streams within a heterogeneous functional architecture [6, 7, 26].

Recent high-resolution neuroimaging reveals that the VWFA is functionally heterogeneous along the posterior-anterior axis of the fusiform gyrus [20, 38] a gradient that may underlie its proposed multiplex hub function. Anatomically, both subregions lie along a continuous strip of left ventral occipitotemporal cortex spanning the fusiform gyrus and the adjoining occipitotemporal sulcus, with VWFA-1 in posterior fusiform/lateral occipitotemporal cortex and VWFA-2 in mid-fusiform cortex, separated by roughly 1.5–2 cm along the posterior-anterior axis. The VWFA-1/VWFA-2 distinction thus reflects a functional and connectional gradient along a continuous cortical substrate rather than a partition by a sharp sulcal boundary.

As the ventral visual stream is hierarchically organized from perceptual to conceptual processing, a posterior-anterior gradient within the VWFA would be expected to support division of labor across subregions, enabling differential coupling with domain-general control systems (e.g., attention) versus language circuits. Posterior subregions (VWFA-1) respond preferentially to sublexical visual features—letter combinations, orthographic regularity, and word length—whereas anterior subregions (VWFA-2) are more sensitive to lexical-semantic content [35, 38]. Structural connectivity studies using diffusion tractography corroborate this dissociation: VWFA-1 maintains stronger white matter connections with occipital and posterior parietal cortices, while VWFA-2 shows enhanced connectivity with anterior temporal and inferior frontal language regions [4, 41]. RSFC analyses confirm these patterns: VWFA-1 exhibits stronger intrinsic coupling with bilateral visual areas and dorsal attention regions, whereas VWFA-2 shows enhanced connectivity with ventral language networks [40]. Task-based studies demonstrate dynamic modulation of these connectivity patterns. Li et al., [22] showed that verbal tasks enhanced connections from both subregions to ventral language areas, while non-verbal visuospatial tasks enhanced connections to dorsal attention regions. VWFA-2 showed greater activation for verbal versus non-verbal tasks. Together, these findings support a hierarchical model in which VWFA-1 serves as a visual-attentional interface and VWFA-2 supports lexical-semantic integration [40].

Despite these advances, critical gaps remain in understanding how VWFA subregions dynamically reconfigure their network affiliations across different cognitive contexts. Most studies have examined either resting-state connectivity or single-task conditions, making it difficult to distinguish stable intrinsic architecture from transient task-driven modulation. Few studies have directly compared VWFA-1 and VWFA-2 within the same participants across multiple cognitive contexts that differentially recruit attention and language systems. A particularly important gap concerns whether VWFA connectivity reorganizes systematically when perceptual input is held constant but cognitive demands shift between attentional and linguistic processing. Existing research often confounds sensory input with cognitive demands by comparing reading tasks to non-orthographic control conditions, leaving this question unresolved. Additionally, while Li et al. [22] demonstrated task-dependent connectivity changes, their study did not examine resting-state baseline connectivity or test whether task-evoked reconfiguration persists after controlling for intrinsic architecture through baseline-adjusted connectivity measures (i.e., task–rest difference scores). Establishing both baseline connectivity profiles and their task-dependent reconfiguration within the same participants is essential for determining whether the VWFA operates as a flexible interface that dynamically coordinates with large-scale cortical networks depending on cognitive demands.

This study addressed these limitations by examining both intrinsic and task-evoked VWFA connectivity within the same participants across three conditions: resting-state, a non-linguistic attentional task, and a linguistic lexical decision task. By comparing resting-state baseline with two tasks that systematically vary cognitive demands while controlling for visual input, we can distinguish stable intrinsic architecture from genuine task-driven reconfiguration and characterize how VWFA-1 and VWFA-2 flexibly engage with different cortical networks. We adopted a network-based seed-to-target approach with a priori defined regions based on previous research [7, 38]. Two seed regions were defined within the left fusiform gyrus based on previously reported MNI coordinates (VWFA-1: -43, -61, -10; VWFA-2: -46, -45, -18; [38]). Target regions were selected from three functional networks identified in previous research [7]: the Attention Network (FEF, MT+, aIPS, pIPS), Language Network 1 (ITG, AG, IFG-oper, preCG, SMG), and Language Network 2 (aSTS, mSTS, pSTS). Participants completed a Flanker task requiring selective visuospatial filtering and conflict monitoring without linguistic processing, and a lexical decision task requiring lexical-semantic discrimination of visually presented words and nonwords. Critically, both tasks employed visual stimuli, allowing us to attribute connectivity differences to top-down cognitive demands rather than sensory input. To our knowledge, this design provides the first within-subject comparison of VWFA-1 and VWFA-2 connectivity across resting-state and two tasks that systematically isolate attentional versus linguistic demands.

Based on the posterior-anterior anatomical organization and prior evidence of functional specialization, we formulated three specific hypotheses. If the posterior-anterior (P-A) anatomical gradient corresponds to an attention-to-language (A-L) functional role differentiation, this should be observable both in intrinsic connectivity at rest and in task-dependent reconfiguration patterns. First, we hypothesized that at rest, VWFA-1 would exhibit stronger intrinsic coupling with the Attention Network than with Language Network 1, while VWFA-2 would show higher Language Network 1 connectivity compared to VWFA-1, reflecting a visual-to-linguistic gradient in baseline connectivity. Second, we predicted that these connectivity profiles would be modulated by task demands in a manner consistent with attentional versus linguistic engagement: VWFA-1 should show greater attention network coupling during the Flanker task, while both subregions should increase language network connectivity during lexical decision. Third, we expected that this task-dependent modulation would parallel the posterior-anterior functional specialization documented in prior work, with differential reconfiguration persisting even after controlling for baseline resting-state connectivity through baseline-adjusted (task–rest) connectivity measures. By testing these hypotheses, we aimed to clarify how the VWFA flexibly coordinates with large-scale cortical networks to transform visual input into linguistic meaning.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-six healthy, right-handed native speakers of Korean participated in the study. All reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological or psychiatric disorders. Handedness was assessed using the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory ([25]; mean score = 7 ± 4). Data from one participant were excluded due to discontinuation during scanning, and four were excluded for not meeting inclusion criteria across the resting-state, Flanker, and lexical decision sessions. One additional participant was excluded for poor behavioral performance (accuracy < 60%) in both the Flanker and lexical decision tasks, resulting in a final sample of 20 participants (11 male, 9 female; mean age = 24 years, SD = 3). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Korea University (KUIRB-2019-0305-02).

fMRI tasks

Participants completed three MRI sessions in the following fixed order: resting-state fMRI, Flanker task, and lexical decision task (LDT) (see Fig. 1). The resting-state scan was acquired first to obtain a baseline measure of intrinsic connectivity, during which participants were instructed to keep their eyes closed, remain still, stay awake, and let their minds wander without focusing on any specific thought or stimulus.

Schematic illustration of fMRI tasks: (A) the Flanker task and (B) lexical decision task (LDT)

The Flanker task (Fig. 1A) consisted of three conditions—neutral, congruent, and incongruent. Participants responded to the direction of a central arrow while ignoring flanking arrows, pressing either left or right button. All the stimuli were presented as an image with the resolution of 1280 × 720. The experimental procedure is as follows. A fixation point (“+”) appeared in the center of the screen for 500 ms, followed by a target stimulus presented for 2500 ms at the center of the screen. After responding to the target by pressing a designated key in the response box, an inter-trial interval (ITI) blank was displayed with a jittered duration of 6000–8000 ms. After completing the practice session, which consisted of 12 trials, participants were instructed to proceed to the main session. In two functional runs, each run included 45 trials, which consisted of 15 trials per condition.

Next, participants performed a rapid event-related lexical decision task on Korean nouns (Fig. 1B). In each trial, the target stimulus was presented at the center of the screen for 500 ms. Target stimuli consisted of real Korean nouns, phonotactically legal pseudowords, and fixation (“+”) trials that served as baseline. Following each target presentation, a blank screen with a jittered interstimulus interval ranging from 1500 to 7500 ms served as the null condition. The duration of this blank interval was pseudo-randomized to reduce temporal predictability and to optimize event-related design efficiency using optseq2 (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/optseq/). In both tasks, all participants achieved accuracies above 80% during the practice session. Behavioral accuracy (ACC) and reaction times (RTs) were recorded simultaneously during all task fMRI sessions.

We chose a centrally presented flanker task rather than a peripheral orienting paradigm, such as the Attention Network Test [11, 23], for two reasons. First, the goal of this task was to engage selective spatial filtering and response-conflict resolution, rather than exogenous orienting. Although the stimuli were presented centrally, participants had to select the central target while suppressing spatially adjacent distractors. Second, keeping stimulus eccentricity constant across tasks was important for the present design. As the lexical decision task presented words foveally, a centrally presented flanker task allowed us to compare task-related connectivity under similar retinotopic input conditions. This design therefore helped minimize potential confounds related to stimulus location or peripheral orienting demands, while preserving the intended contrast in top-down selection, distractor suppression, and conflict processing.

MRI data acquisition

MRI data were collected using a Siemens Magnetom Trio 3T scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 12-channel head coil at the Korea University Brain Imaging Center.

For all functional runs—including resting-state (RS-fMRI), Flanker, and a lexical decision task (LDT)—T2*-weighted gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequences were used to measure blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signals. Acquisition parameters were identical across tasks: repetition time (TR) = 2000 ms, echo time (TE) = 20 ms, flip angle = 90°, field of view (FOV) = 240 mm, matrix size = 80 × 80, slice thickness = 3 mm, no gap, yielding isotropic voxels of 3 × 3 × 3 mm³. Each volume comprised 42 interleaved axial slices acquired in the ascending order, with a GRAPPA acceleration factor of 2. For the resting-state scan, 150 volumes were acquired over 5 min and 6 s. The Flanker task comprised two sessions, each lasting 7 min and 44 s (229 volumes per session). The noun lexical decision task consisted of a single run of 8 min and 34 s (254 volumes). High-resolution T1-weighted structural images were also acquired for anatomical reference using a 3D magnetization-prepared rapid gradient-echo (MP-RAGE) sequence (TR = 1900 ms, TE = 2.52 ms, flip angle = 90°, FOV = 256 mm, matrix size = 256 × 256, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm³) covering the whole head.

Region of interest selection

Two seed regions of interest (ROIs) were defined within the left ventral occipitotemporal cortex corresponding to the visual word form area (VWFA; see Fig. 2A). The posterior seed (VWFA-1) was centered at MNI coordinates [–43, − 61, − 10], and the mid-fusiform seed (VWFA-2) at [–46, − 45, − 18] [38]. Target ROIs were selected from three functional networks following the Chen et al. [7], in which the authors drew the attention and core reading-network nodes from Vogel et al. [36] and the superior temporal sulcus nodes from the NeuroSynth automated meta-analytic map for the term “reading.” Here, the two language networks were defined separately to capture functionally distinct contributions to reading: Language Network 1 indexes the classic left-hemisphere fronto-parieto-temporal reading network (orthographic, lexical-semantic, and phonological-articulatory components), whereas Language Network 2 indexes phonological and speech-related processing along the left superior temporal sulcus. The Attention Network (Fig. 2B) included the frontal eye field (FEF; [–26, − 5, 50]), middle temporal area (MT+; [–45, − 71, − 1]), anterior intraparietal sulcus (aIPS; [–25, − 62, 51]), and posterior intraparietal sulcus (pIPS; [–25, − 69, 34]). The Language Network 1 (Fig. 2C) comprised the angular gyrus (AG; [–49, − 57, 28]), inferior temporal gyrus (ITG; [–61, − 33, − 15]), inferior frontal gyrus pars opercularis (IFG-oper; [–45, 14, 21]), inferior parietal lobule (IPL-oper; [–49, − 40, 26]), precentral gyrus (preCG; [–57, − 11, 45]), and supramarginal gyrus (SMG; [–56, − 43, 31]). The Language Network 2 (Fig. 2D) included the anterior, middle, and posterior superior temporal sulci (aSTS [–54, 9, − 20]; mSTS [–53, − 18, − 10]; pSTS [–52, − 40, 5]). All ROIs were defined as 10 mm-radius spheres in MNI space and extracted using MarsBaR [5]. The two VWFA seeds were spatially near-independent despite their close proximity along the posterior-anterior axis: their centers were 18.14 mm apart and the spheres overlapped by only 7 voxels (Dice = 0.014, Jaccard = 0.007), corresponding to 1.3% of VWFA-1 and 1.4% of VWFA-21.

Data preprocessing and denoising

All MRI data were preprocessed using the CONN functional connectivity toolbox (version 23b; [39]) implemented in SPM12 (Wellcome Department of Imaging Neuroscience, London, UK) running on MATLAB R2025a. Functional images were realigned and unwarped to correct for motion artifacts, slice-timing corrected, and inspected for outliers using the Artifact Detection Toolbox (ART) with thresholds of 2 mm framewise displacement or a 3 SD global signal change. Structural and functional images were segmented and normalized to MNI space using the unified segmentation procedure, and spatially smoothed with a 6 mm full-width at half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian kernel. Physiological and movement-related confounds were removed using the aCompCor approach, with principal components derived from white matter and cerebrospinal fluid signals, as well as six motion parameters and their first-order derivatives. The resulting residual time series were linearly detrended and temporally band-pass filtered (0.008–0.09 Hz) to isolate low-frequency BOLD fluctuations associated with functional connectivity. For task runs (Flanker and lexical decision), task-evoked effects were additionally regressed out as part of the same denoising step prior to functional-connectivity estimation, using each task’s regressors convolved with a canonical hemodynamic response function and their first-order temporal derivatives, following CONN’s recommended denoising pipeline for task-based functional connectivity. This step explicitly removes shared task-evoked activation from the time series and thereby mitigates inflation of task-state functional-connectivity estimates by co-activation [9].

Statistical analysis

Functional connectivity was estimated at the ROI-to-ROI level using the visual word form area (VWFA) as the seed region and predefined target ROIs belonging to three networks of interest. The first language network included the inferior temporal gyrus, angular gyrus, left inferior frontal gyrus, and left precentral gyrus; the second language network included the anterior, middle, and posterior superior temporal sulci; and the attention network included the frontal eye fields, middle temporal area (MT+), and anterior and posterior intraparietal sulcus. Connectivity strength was computed as Fisher’s Z-transformed correlation coefficients between the VWFA and each target ROI for each task (resting-state, Flanker, and lexical decision) and for each participant.

All statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.1; [29]) using the lme4 [2], lmerTest [18], and emmeans [19] packages. Connectivity effects were examined using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) implemented with random intercepts for Subject and ROI to account for repeated measures and ROI-level variance. This model structure was applied consistently across all analyses—including task-based FC, baseline-adjusted task FC (ΔZ), and resting-state FC (RSFC)—to ensure comparable estimation of variance components. Degrees of freedom (df) for fixed effects were estimated using the Satterthwaite approximation in lmerTest. Complete fixed- and random-effects parameter estimates for all three models are reported in Supplementary Tables S1–S3, and the full post hoc pairwise contrasts (FDR-corrected) are provided in Supplementary Tables S4–S6.

Post-hoc pairwise contrasts were estimated with the emmeans package and corrected using the false-discovery-rate (FDR; Benjamini–Hochberg) method. The correction was applied separately for each model (RSFC, task-based FC, and ΔFC) and, within each model, separately within each contrast family—that is, the complete set of pairwise comparisons decomposing a single omnibus effect (e.g., the main effect of Network, or the Seed × Network × Task interaction); p-values were not pooled across families or across models. Finally, to examine the behavioral relevance of intrinsic VWFA connectivity, RSFC values were correlated with behavioral indices, including both accuracy (ACC) and reaction time (RT; correct trials only), derived from each task. For the Flanker task, interference was computed as the difference between incongruent and congruent conditions (ACCIncongruent − ACCCongruent; RTIncongruent − RTCongruent), and for the lexical decision task (LDT) as differences between word and nonword trials (ACCPseudoword − ACCWord; RTPseudoword − RTWord). Positive ΔACC values indicate greater interference, whereas smaller or negative values reflect higher efficiency; ΔRT was interpreted analogously, with larger values indexing greater interference. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated separately for each VWFA seed (VWFA-1, VWFA-2) and network (Attention, Language 1, Language 2) to assess condition-specific brain-behavior relationships. In addition, overall task accuracy and overall RT (each collapsed across conditions, with RT log-transformed) were correlated with RSFC values to evaluate whether global performance was associated with intrinsic VWFA coupling strength. All correlations were two-tailed, and resulting r and p values were interpreted as indices of the strength and direction of brain–behavior relationships at rest. For each correlation, a 95% confidence interval (CI) was estimated using Fisher’s r-to-z transformation. The same correlational approach was applied to reaction time (RT; correct trials, log-transformed) to evaluate potential speed–accuracy trade-offs.

Results

Resting-state connectivity (RSFC)

The results on resting-state functional connectivity (RSFC) analysis are summarized in Fig. 2 (see Supplementary Tables S1 and S4 for full model estimates and post-hoc contrasts). A linear mixed effect model (LMM) was conducted with fixed factors Seed (VWFA-1 vs. VWFA-2) and Network (Attention, Language 1, Language 2) and random intercepts for Participant and ROI. Type III Wald χ² tests revealed a main effect of Network [χ²(2) = 14.25, p = .001]. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons indicated that connectivity between VWFA and the Attention network was significantly elevated relative to the Language 1 network [Attention > Language 1: b = 0.22, t(7) = 3.75, SE = 0.06, pFDR = 0.021]. No reliable difference was observed between the two VWFA subregions [VWFA-1 vs. VWFA-2: b = −0.01, t(368) = −0.29, SE = 0.02, pFDR = 0.77].

A significant interaction of Seed and Network was also observed [χ²(2) = 10.9, p = .004]. Follow-up tests showed that VWFA-1 exhibited stronger intrinsic coupling with the Attention network compared with the Language 1 [Attention > Language 1: b = 0.29, t(9.23) = 4.65, SE = 0.06, pFDR = 0.017]. Furthermore, VWFA-2 demonstrated enhanced coupling with the Language 1 network relative to VWFA-1 [VWFA-1 < VWFA-2: b = −0.09, t(368) = −2.90, SE = 0.03, pFDR = 0.02].

These findings indicate that, at rest, VWFA-1 maintained stronger intrinsic connectivity with the dorsal attention network, whereas VWFA-2 showed relatively stronger baseline connectivity with Language 1 regions, consistent with a posterior-to-anterior gradient from attentional to language-related coupling within the VWFA.

Task-based connectivity

Task-based functional connectivity results are presented in Fig. 3 (see Supplementary Tables S2 and S5 for complete model estimates and post-hoc contrasts). A similar LMM was implemented with fixed factors Seed (VWFA-1 vs. VWFA-2), Network (Attention, Language 1, Language 2), and Task (Flanker vs. LDT), with random intercepts for Participant and ROI. Type III Wald χ² tests revealed a significant main effect of Network [χ²(2) = 17.87, p < .001]. Pairwise comparisons showed connectivity with the Language 1 network was attenuated relative to the Attention network [Language 1 < Attention: b = −0.22, t(9.97) = −4.1, SE = 0.05, pFDR = 0.006], regardless of Seed and Task.

Connectivity strength is presented for three target networks: Attention (red), Language 1 (blue), and Language 2 (green). A significant Seed × Task interaction emerged [χ²(1) = 26.40, p < .001]. During the Flanker task, VWFA-1 exhibited stronger overall connectivity than VWFA-2 [VWFA-1 > VWFA-2: b = 0.05, t(3170.96) = 4.51, SE = 0.01, pFDR < 0.001], whereas the pattern reversed during the LDT [VWFA-1 < VWFA-2: b = −0.03, t(3170.96) = −2.76, SE = 0.01, pFDR = 0.006].

A Task × Network interaction was also significant [χ²(2) = 81.18, p < .001], reflecting greater connectivity with the Attention network than with Language 1 for both tasks [LDT: b = −0.22, t(10.40) = −4.07, SE = 0.05, pFDR = 0.006; Flanker: b = −0.22, t(10.40) = −4.05, SE = 0.05, pFDR = 0.006].

Critically, the Seed × Task × Network was significant [χ²(2) = 159.93, p < .001]. During the Flanker task, VWFA-1 showed enhanced coupling with the Attention network [b = 0.13, t(3170.96) = 8.64, SE = 0.02, pFDR < 0.001], and reduced connectivity with Language 1 [b = −0.06, t(3170.96) = −3.62, SE = 0.02, pFDR = 0.002] relative to LDT. In contrast, during the LDT, VWFA-2 exhibited greater coupling with the Attention network relative to Flanker [b = −0.17, t(3170.96) = −10.66, SE = 0.02, pFDR < 0.001]. For completeness, VWFA-2’s coupling with Language Network 1 was numerically lower during the LDT than during the Flanker task, although this difference was not significant [Flanker > LDT: b = 0.03, SE = 0.02, t(3170.96) = 1.71, pFDR = 0.151], consistent with Fig. 3B.

Taken together, this pattern reveals a double dissociation: VWFA-1 preferentially engages attention-related regions during the Flanker task and language-related regions during the LDT (see Fig. 3A), whereas VWFA-2 shows the complementary pattern, with enhanced attentional coupling specifically during the lexical task (see Fig. 3B).

Collapsing across networks, ΔFC was lower in Flanker than LDT [Flanker − LDT: b = − 0.02, SE = 0.01, t(3329.84) = − 2.28, pFDR = 0.023]. For the Seed × Task effect, VWFA-1 showed higher ΔFC than VWFA-2 during Flanker [b = 0.05, SE = 0.01, t(3329.84) = 3.75, pFDR = 0.001], whereas VWFA-2 showed lower ΔFC in Flanker than in LDT [b = − 0.06, SE = 0.01, t(3329.84) = − 4.22, pFDR < 0.001]. For the Seed × Network effect, VWFA-1 exhibited lower ΔFC to Attention than to Language 1 [b = − 0.06, SE = 0.02, t(15.43) = − 2.81, pFDR = 0.049], whereas VWFA-2 showed the opposite pattern [b = 0.06, SE = 0.02, t(15.43) = 2.92, pFDR = 0.049]. Within Language 1, VWFA-1 showed higher ΔFC than VWFA-2 [b = 0.09, SE = 0.01, t(3329.84) = 6.57, pFDR < 0.001]; within VWFA-2, ΔFC was lower for Language 1 than Language 2 [b = − 0.08, SE = 0.03, t(15.43) = − 3.09, pFDR = 0.049].

Critically, the Seed × Network × Task interaction localized the reconfiguration. During Flanker, VWFA-1 showed greater ΔFC to Attention than VWFA-2 [VWFA-1 Attn Flanker − VWFA-2 Attn Flanker: b = 0.11, SE = 0.02, t(3329.84) = 5.67, pFDR < 0.001] and higher ΔFC to Attention than to VWFA-2’s Language 1 in Flanker [b = 0.08, SE = 0.03, t(31.57) = 3.09, pFDR = 0.013]. Relative to LDT, VWFA-1 increased ΔFC to Attention in Flanker [b = 0.12, SE = 0.02, t(3329.84) = 5.98, pFDR < 0.001]. In contrast, VWFA-2 showed markedly lower ΔFC to Attention in Flanker than in LDT [b = − 0.17, SE = 0.02, t(3329.84) = − 8.37, pFDR < 0.001], and lower ΔFC in Flanker than LDT for Language 1 as well [b = − 0.13, SE = 0.03, t(31.57) = − 5.19, pFDR < 0.001]. Additional contrasts were consistent with this pattern (e.g., VWFA-1 Lang1 Flanker > VWFA-1 Lang1 LDT: b = − 0.06, SE = 0.02, t(3329.84) = − 2.87, pFDR = 0.013; VWFA-1 Attn LDT < VWFA-2 Lang1 LDT: b = − 0.18, SE = 0.02, t(3329.84) = − 8.69, pFDR < 0.001).

In summary, even after controlling for baseline resting-state connectivity, VWFA-1 selectively increased coupling with attention-related regions under Flanker demands (see Fig. 4A), whereas VWFA-2 preferentially strengthened coupling with language-related regions during the lexical decision task (see Fig. 4B). The Seed × Task × Network dissociation thus reflects genuine task-evoked reconfiguration rather than residual variance inherited from intrinsic architecture.

Relationship between resting-state connectivity and behavior

The relationship between intrinsic VWFA connectivity and behavioral performance is summarized in Fig. 5 (see Supplementary Tables S3 and S6 for complete model estimates and post-hoc contrasts). For each participant, RSFC values were correlated with accuracy indices derived from the Flanker and lexical decision tasks. Pearson’s correlation coefficients (R) and p-values are indicated within each panel

Across both tasks, attention-related connectivity consistently predicted higher accuracy. For the Flanker task (see Fig. 5A–B), VWFA-1–Attention (r = .45, 95% CI [0.01, 0.75], p = .045) and VWFA-2–Attention (r = .50, 95% CI [0.07, 0.77], p = .026) connectivity were positively correlated with overall performance. Similarly, for the LDT (see Fig. 5C–D), stronger VWFA-1–Attention connectivity predicted higher lexical accuracy (r = .52, 95% CI [0.10, 0.78], p = .019), with a comparable but nonsignificant trend for VWFA-2 (r = .38, 95% CI [−0.08, 0.70], p = .10). No reliable associations were observed between VWFA–Language 1 or VWFA–Language 2 connectivity and overall accuracy in either task (all ps > 0.27).

Parallel analyses examined reaction time (RT; correct trials, log-transformed) to assess potential speed–accuracy trade-offs. For the Flanker task, stronger VWFA-2–Attention connectivity predicted faster responses (r = −.55, 95% CI [−0.80, −0.14], p = .013), paralleling the accuracy effect and thus arguing against a speed–accuracy trade-off; the VWFA-1–Attention association was directionally consistent but nonsignificant (r = -.28, 95% CI [−0.65, 0.18], p = .23). No other RSFC–RT correlations were significant in either task (all remaining |r| < 0.44, ps > 0.05).

These findings indicate that intrinsic VWFA-attention connectivity predicts global task performance across both attentional and linguistic domains, whereas language network connectivity showed no reliable relationship with overall accuracy.

Discussion

The present study examined how the visual word form area (VWFA) dynamically reconfigures its functional connectivity across resting, attentional, and linguistic contexts. By contrasting two functionally distinct subregions along the posterior-anterior axis—VWFA-1 and VWFA-2—we characterized both stable intrinsic organization and flexible task-driven modulation. We hypothesized that this posterior-to-anterior (P-A) anatomical organization would correspond to attention-to-language (A-L) functional role differentiation, observable both in intrinsic connectivity at rest and in task-dependent reconfiguration. Three key findings emerged: (1) at rest, VWFA-1 demonstrates stronger intrinsic coupling with dorsal attention regions relative to Language Network 1, while VWFA-2 shows enhanced Language Network 1 connectivity compared to VWFA-1, confirming the predicted P-A/A-L gradient; (2) VWFA-1 and VWFA-2 exhibit dissociable task-dependent connectivity patterns with the attention network consistent with this gradient, though language network connectivity showed unexpected reconfiguration; and (3) intrinsic VWFA-attention connectivity predicts global task performance across both attentional and linguistic domains, supporting the functional significance of attention network coupling. Together, these results demonstrate that the VWFA operates as a flexible interface that dynamically coordinates with attention and language networks depending on cognitive demands.

Intrinsic VWFA Organization: a posterior-anterior gradient

At rest, VWFA-1 exhibited stronger intrinsic coupling with the dorsal attention network, while VWFA-2 showed enhanced connectivity with Language Network 1, including the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG), angular gyrus (AG), and supramarginal gyrus (SMG). This posterior-anterior gradient replicates and extends prior findings from both RSFC [7, 40] and structural connectivity studies [4, 41], confirming that the VWFA’s internal functional organization mirrors the broader hierarchical structure of the ventral visual stream.

The presence of dissociable intrinsic connectivity patterns supports the hypothesis that the VWFA’s functional specialization for reading emerges from pre-existing network architecture [30, 32]. Saygin et al., [30] demonstrated that connectivity patterns at age 5—before literacy acquisition—predict the future location of word-selective responses at age 8, indicating that functional specialization is constrained and scaffolded by intrinsic connectivity. Our findings extend this developmental framework to the subregional level: VWFA-1 appears anchored within a visual-attentional network that gates perceptual input and supports sublexical feature detection, whereas VWFA-2 is embedded within a language-dominant network that facilitates orthographic-to-semantic mapping. These complementary baseline architectures provide the neural substrate for the hierarchical processing cascade required for fluent reading [35, 38].

Task-dependent reconfiguration of VWFA connectivity

Task-based functional connectivity analyses revealed a double dissociation between VWFA subregions, meaning that the two subregions showed opposite patterns of connectivity change across tasks: VWFA-1 and VWFA-2 each preferentially coupled with different networks depending on whether the task emphasized attentional or linguistic processing demands. This dissociation was most clearly observed in attention network connectivity and provided mixed evidence in language network connectivity.

For the attention network, task-dependent modulation aligned with the predicted P-A/A-L functional gradient. During the Flanker task, which required selective visuospatial filtering and conflict monitoring without linguistic processing, VWFA-1 exhibited enhanced coupling with the dorsal attention network—including the frontal eye fields (FEF), intraparietal sulcus (IPS), and middle temporal visual area (MT+). In contrast, during the lexical decision task (LDT), which required lexical-semantic discrimination, VWFA-2 showed enhanced coupling with the attention network. This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that VWFA-1, positioned more posteriorly, serves as an attentional gating mechanism during non-linguistic visual tasks, while VWFA-2, positioned more anteriorly, recruits attentional resources specifically when lexical processing demands are high. These findings replicate and extend previous work showing that visuospatial tasks enhance VWFA connections to dorsal attentional regions [22] and support the hierarchical gradient model in which posterior VWFA maintains closer functional ties with visual-attentional systems [40].

For the language network, connectivity departed from the strict gradient prediction. During the Flanker task, VWFA-1 reduced its coupling with Language Network 1, consistent with diminished linguistic demand; during the LDT, however, it was VWFA-1, not VWFA-2, that increased coupling with perisylvian language regions, while VWFA-2 concurrently strengthened attention-network connectivity. Although unexpected under a purely lexical-semantic reading of the gradient, this pattern is consistent with the established sublexical tuning of posterior ventral occipitotemporal cortex [35] and with evidence that, along the posterior-to-anterior axis, the more posterior VWFA preferentially connects with regions supporting grapheme-phoneme conversion rather than lexical access [4, 28]. Our lexical decision required word–pseudoword discrimination, which preferentially taxes sublexical, grapheme-to-phoneme analysis rather than whole-word semantic retrieval [34]; this demand plausibly recruited the posterior VWFA-1 and increased its coupling with Language Network 1 regions implicated in orthographic-phonological mapping, notably the inferior frontal pars opercularis and supramarginal gyrus. On this view, the finding does not contradict the posterior-anterior gradient but indicates that the gradient is engaged according to the level of linguistic representation a task emphasizes, here sublexical orthography rather than lexical semantics, while VWFA-2’s concurrent attention coupling likely reflects the top-down selection and response-conflict demands intrinsic to binary lexical decision. Further studies parametrically manipulating pseudoword word-likeness or decoding load could test whether such demands scale VWFA-1–language coupling, an inference the present design cannot adjudicate.

Critically, baseline-adjusted analyses (ΔZ = Z_Task − Z_RS) confirmed that these task-dependent patterns—both the attention network modulation consistent with our hypothesis and the unexpected language network configuration—reflect genuine task-evoked changes rather than residual variance from intrinsic architecture. After controlling for resting-state connectivity, the dissociation persisted: VWFA-1 showed selective increases in coupling with attention regions during the Flanker task and enhanced connectivity with language regions during the LDT, while VWFA-2 maintained enhanced attention network coupling during the LDT. This persistence demonstrates active, context-dependent reorganization of VWFA networks consistent with the dynamic connectivity framework proposed by [22] and the connectivity-biased account of cortical function [30]. Rather than serving as a fixed “word-form” module, each VWFA subregion functions as an adaptive hub that flexibly aligns with domain-relevant systems according to cognitive demands. This flexibility may be a defining feature of the VWFA’s integrative role in reading: the region must coordinate visual feature extraction, spatial attention, phonological access, and semantic retrieval in rapid succession during normal text processing [6, 26].

Behavioral relevance of intrinsic VWFA connectivity

Brain-behavior analyses revealed that intrinsic VWFA-attention connectivity was the most robust predictor of task performance. Across both the Flanker and lexical decision tasks, participants with stronger resting-state coupling between VWFA (particularly VWFA-1) and the dorsal attention network achieved higher performance regardless of whether the task required visuospatial conflict resolution or lexical-semantic discrimination. Extending the task-dependent reconfiguration results, which highlighted changes in VWFA-attention coupling as the dominant axis of task-related modulation across both tasks, the brain-behavior analyses suggest that baseline attention network connectivity supports domain-general visual processing efficiency that benefits both attentional and linguistic tasks.

The brain–behavior correlations thus complement the task-based findings by implicating the same VWFA–attention pathway in individual differences in performance, even though task-evoked connectivity did not directly predict accuracy. Considered alongside these behavioral results, the task-based reconfiguration of VWFA–attention coupling and its intrinsic association with performance indicate that this pathway constitutes a principal route for allocating attentional resources, whereas VWFA–language connectivity plays a more limited role in predicting global task performance. The task-based analyses showed that attention-network coupling was the primary locus of VWFA reconfiguration: during the Flanker task, VWFA-1 displayed the strongest coupling with dorsal attention regions, whereas during the LDT, VWFA-2 exhibited enhanced attention network connectivity. Critically, although these task-evoked connectivity measures themselves did not show reliable correlations with accuracy, intrinsic VWFA–attention connectivity at rest—particularly between VWFA-1 and dorsal attention regions—predicted performance in both the Flanker and lexical decision tasks, whereas neither intrinsic nor task-evoked VWFA–language connectivity was associated with accuracy. Both tasks required rapid visual feature extraction, spatial selection, and sustained attention to centrally presented stimuli—processes that depend on coordination between ventral visual cortex and dorsal attention systems [13]. Together, these task-based connectivity patterns and behavioral correlations provide converging evidence that baseline VWFA–attention coupling supports domain-general visual processing efficiency across both visuospatial and lexical tasks.

The dissociation between intrinsic and task-evoked predictors of performance is itself informative, and we interpret it in terms of a trait–state distinction. Resting-state connectivity indexes stable, individual-specific network architecture—the baseline efficiency with which ventral visual and dorsal attention systems are coupled—which scaffolds and constrains functional capacity across contexts [30] and reliably indexes individual differences in reading competence [16]. Such intrinsic organization therefore behaves as a trait-like predictor of performance irrespective of the specific task. Task-evoked connectivity, by contrast, reflects the state-dependent configuration a task imposes; as this reconfiguration is largely shared across participants, indexing what the task demands rather than how efficiently a given individual meets them, it carries comparatively little of the between-participant variance that tracks performance. This asymmetry was likely accentuated by our design: task-based connectivity was estimated over shorter runs than resting state, and hence less reliably, and accuracy was relatively high, leaving limited variance for task-state coupling to explain. Intrinsic VWFA–attention coupling thus provides a stable scaffold for performance, whereas its task-driven reconfiguration supports the computation at hand without itself indexing individual ability.

In contrast to VWFA–attention connectivity, VWFA–language connectivity showed no reliable relationship with either accuracy or response time in the Flanker or lexical decision tasks, indicating that it does not provide a domain-general scaffold for performance even when the task involves lexical processing. Although the LDT is linguistically oriented, both the Flanker and lexical decision paradigms place broad demands on visual selection, conflict monitoring, and decision making, such that individual differences in overall performance are likely to be driven primarily by domain-general visual–attentional efficiency rather than by subtle variation in orthographic–phonological or semantic processing. From this perspective, the absence of brain–behavior correlations for VWFA–language connectivity in the LDT could be interpreted as indicating that language network coupling supports more fine-grained linguistic operations—such as detailed orthographic–phonological mapping or semantic integration [32]—that were not the primary limiting factor for successful performance in lexical decision [42]. Future research employing more sensitive behavioral metrics—such as lexicality effects, phonological priming, or reading comprehension measures—could better characterize the functional significance of VWFA-language connectivity and clarify how linguistic and attentional pathways jointly support skilled reading.

Integration with theoretical models of VWFA function

Our findings challenge purely modular accounts of the VWFA [10] and instead support network-based frameworks emphasizing distributed, dynamic coordination [7, 37]. The task-dependent reconfiguration we observed indicates that the VWFA’s functional contribution to reading and attention arises not from local specialization alone but from its pattern of connectivity with broader cortical systems. This perspective aligns with accumulating evidence that cortical regions are best understood as nodes within large-scale networks whose functions emerge through interaction rather than isolation [1].

The posterior-anterior gradient we documented—from VWFA-1’s attention-dominant connectivity to VWFA-2’s language-dominant connectivity—instantiates the hierarchical progression from perceptual to conceptual processing proposed by multiple reading models [34, 35, 40]. Our task-based findings reveal that this hierarchy is not rigid but dynamically modulated: when attentional demands are high, even VWFA-1 increases its coupling with attention regions; when linguistic processing is required, VWFA-1 shifts toward language networks while VWFA-2 maintains strong attention connectivity. This flexible hierarchical organization may enable fluent reading by allowing rapid transitions between perceptual feature extraction and higher-level linguistic interpretation as task context changes.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while the fixed task order (resting-state, Flanker, and LDT) represents a methodological limitation, its potential to induce severe, compounding carry-over effects was likely mitigated by our blocked design and structural control. The presence of a 1-to-2-minute physical break, the introduction of entirely new explicit instructions, and the inclusion of a dedicated LDT practice session between the sessions provided a robust cognitive boundary that facilitates task-set reconfiguration, allowing participants to successfully disengage from the prior Flanker rules. Nonetheless, as recent behavioral literature elegantly demonstrates [17, 27], preceding attentional engagement can directly modulate subsequent lexical processing efficiency due to the inherently shared neural infrastructure between visuospatial attention and language systems. In particular, single-session paradigms utilizing highly valid, predictive visual location cues with minimal inter-stimulus intervals (e.g., Kress et al., [17] have been shown to induce immediate priming and attentional orienting effects on reading performance. Although the robust double dissociation observed in our task-evoked connectivity (ΔZ) is consistent with the VWFA’s proposed role as an adaptive hub at the dynamic nexus of these overlapping networks rather than a passive reflection of residual variance, our current design cannot fully separate such sequence-dependent, top-down interactions from intrinsic task demands. Moreover, as the Flanker task preceded the LDT, a practice-related benefit carrying over to lexical decision (cf. Franceschini and Bertoni [12], could have selectively inflated the apparent attention–LDT association while masking a language–LDT relationship, so the LDT brain–behavior correlations warrant particular caution. Therefore, future neuroimaging studies must employ a fully counterbalanced session sequence to conclusively disentangle these structural priming effects from context-dependent reconfigurations.

Additionally, although our seed coordinates were taken from White et al. [38], we used 10-mm spherical ROIs rather than the individually defined VWFA-1 and VWFA-2 parcellations used in that study; this choice favors replicability but may have reduced sensitivity to individual variability in VWFA location. More fine-grained ROI definitions—for example, smaller spherical radii (e.g., the 6-mm radius used by [7]), individually defined functional parcellations, or updated meta-analytic masks such as those provided by Bedini et al. [3] for the FEF and inferior frontal junction—could be employed in future work to improve anatomical specificity. Subject-specific localizers or gradient mapping could reveal finer subregional specialization. Moreover, our ROI-based approach captures large-scale network organization but not representational content. Multivariate methods (e.g., representational similarity analysis) combined with effective connectivity modeling would clarify whether connectivity changes reflect shifts in representational geometry and whether reconfiguration is driven bottom-up or top-down.

A further limitation concerns statistical power. With a final sample of N = 20, a sensitivity analysis indicated that the brain–behavior correlations were adequately powered (80%, two-tailed α = 0.05) only to detect large effects (|r| ≥ 0.57); power to detect smaller associations was correspondingly limited. The significant correlations we observed (|r| ≈ 0.45–0.55) therefore lie near this sensitivity threshold, and their 95% confidence intervals are accordingly wide. These brain–behavior associations should therefore be regarded as preliminary and interpreted with caution, warranting replication in larger, adequately powered samples. By contrast, the within-participant connectivity analyses, estimated from trial- and ROI-level data with linear mixed-effects models, are considerably better powered; it is the between-subject brain–behavior correlations that constitute the primary power limitation.

A related limitation concerns task design: our tasks differed in stimulus complexity and response requirements beyond their cognitive demands. The unexpected pattern of VWFA-1 language network connectivity during lexical decision, combined with the absence of VWFA-language correlations with overall accuracy, highlights the need for more controlled paradigms with parametric manipulations on various linguistic variables. Future research should systematically vary orthographic regularity, lexical frequency, and attentional load within a single task framework to better capture the functional contributions of VWFA connectivity patterns. Such studies could test whether VWFA-1’s enhanced language connectivity during LDT reflects a general principle—that lexical tasks recruit posterior VWFA for orthographic-phonological processing—or whether it is specific to word-pseudoword discrimination demands.

Finally, longitudinal studies tracking connectivity across reading acquisition could test whether VWFA network flexibility predicts literacy outcomes and whether intervention strengthens specific pathways [33], directly testing whether experience-dependent connectivity modulation underlies functional specialization.

Conclusion

By separately examining VWFA-1 and VWFA-2 connectivity across resting-state, Flanker, and lexical-decision contexts, this study makes two main contributions. First, it provides evidence that the VWFA’s role in reading reflects context-dependent reconfiguration of functional connectivity rather than static domain specialization. Resting-state analyses were consistent with a posterior-to-anterior intrinsic gradient, with VWFA-1 more strongly coupled with attention networks and VWFA-2 with language networks; task-based analyses further suggested that this organization was flexibly reconfigured by cognitive demands, with VWFA-1 engaging dorsal attention regions during the Flanker task and strengthening coupling with perisylvian language regions during lexical decision, whereas VWFA-2 showed increased attention-network coupling during lexical decision. Second, brain-behavior analyses suggested that intrinsic VWFA–attention connectivity, particularly for VWFA-1, predicted performance across both attentional and linguistic tasks, whereas VWFA–language connectivity was not reliably associated with performance. Together, these findings support a view of the VWFA as a context-sensitive hub whose coupling with distributed attention and language systems is dynamically tuned to cognitive demands, providing a network-level account of the flexibility of skilled reading.
