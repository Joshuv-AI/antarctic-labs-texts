What factors influence contraceptive counselling readiness among Vietnamese medical students? A nationwide study protocol applying the extended theory of planned behaviour (the V-COC study)
Authors: Nguyen Dinh Ky, Pham The Lam, Nguyen Dang Linh Chi, Nguyen Quynh Anh, Nguyen Mai Linh, Tran Ngoc Tam Phuc, Khuc Thi Ngoc Anh, Nguyen-Tien Thang, Nguyen Manh Thang, Nguyen Thi Huyen Anh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionContraceptive counselling is a key preventive intervention for reducing unintended pregnancies and unsafe abortions. In a setting such as Vietnam, where contraceptive use is prevalent, the quality and consistency of counselling provided by future physicians are critical. Although this is a core competency in medical education, evidence suggests that medical students demonstrate deficits in applied knowledge, skills and confidence regarding counselling. Evidence regarding medical students’ intentions to provide contraceptive counselling in Vietnam remains limited and has not been systematically examined.Methods and analysisA nationwide cross-sectional study will be conducted from October 2025 to October 2026 at 12 medical universities representing three distinct regions. Self-administered online questionnaires (via KoboToolbox) will be distributed to first-year to sixth-year medical students. This protocol describes a two-phase study. Phase 1 (scale development and validation; n=600, including pilot and psychometric evaluation) aims to develop and validate the Extended Theory of Planned Behaviour (E-TPB) scales (Knowledge, Attitude, Subjective Norms, Perceived Behavioural Control, Intention). This phase uses the Content Validity Index, Classical Test Theory and Item Response Theory Two-Parameter Logistic to generate latent trait scores for Knowledge, followed by exploratory factor analysis, confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and assessment of convergent and discriminant validity. Phase 2 (main survey; n=1300) describes the current status by academic year, gender and institution. Simultaneously, the E-TPB model will be tested using Covariance-Based Structural Equation Modelling to estimate standardised path coefficients and mediation effects (5000 bootstrap samples). A multigroup SEM will be conducted to test measurement/structural invariance and to compare key groups (gender; preclinical vs clinical). Missing-data handling will prioritise full information maximum likelihood for CFA/SEM, with strict adherence to predefined quality control criteria.Ethics and disseminationThe study protocol was formally approved by the Institutional Ethical Review Board for Biomedical Research at Hanoi Medical University on 3 March 2026 (Approval No. 120/GCN-KHCN). Results will be published in international peer-reviewed journals and sent to participating medical universities.

Introduction

According to the Programme of Action of the International Conference on Population and Development in 1994, access to contraceptive services and family planning education is a fundamental human right.1 However, current research evidence suggests that there are still approximately 160–270 million women of reproductive age with unmet needs.2 3 The significant gap between recognised rights and actual access to services has led to serious consequences, evidenced by alarming global abortion rates: 61% of unintended pregnancies and 29% of all pregnancies.4 Notably, Vietnam has the second-highest abortion rate globally and ranks first in Asia.5 In this context, contraceptive counselling serves as a core intervention. Research evidence confirms that high-quality counselling significantly improves compliance and reduces unintended pregnancy rates.6 7 In Vietnam, this skill is taught in modules on reproductive health, family planning and is a mandatory competency for medical students on graduation.8 This underscores the importance of healthcare workers, including medical students, mastering the knowledge and skills to provide optimal care.

However, international studies from India and Malaysia to Egypt highlight a notable reality: although medical students have positive attitudes and a desire to counsel, they often lack practical knowledge and necessary communication skills, leading to a lack of confidence when facing patients.911 In Vietnam, a recent survey of health science students in Ho Chi Minh City showed that knowledge of contraceptive methods was only average, and positive attitudes towards service provision were inconsistent (47.3%).12 Therefore, identifying the factors affecting medical students’ counselling readiness and capacity is essential. These results will serve as a basis for proposing effective interventions to improve this situation. However, to date, no study has systematically focused on the contraceptive counselling behaviour of healthcare workers or medical students. Globally, although studies on counselling readiness exist, the measurement tools used are often unstandardised and lack a foundational theoretical framework.

To address these gaps, a behavioural theory capable of explaining intentions is required. This protocol therefore adopts the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB) proposed by Ajzen as the theoretical framework to conceptualise contraceptive counselling as a deliberate professional behaviour. In TPB, behavioural intention is considered the most proximal predictor of behaviour and is shaped by Attitudes (A), Subjective Norms (SN) and Perceived Behavioural Control (PBC).13 A large meta-analysis by McEachan and colleagues (2011), synthesising 237 studies, reported that TPB explains on average approximately 44% of the variance in health-related intentions, supporting its utility for studying intentional health and professional behaviours.14

In medical education, professional knowledge is commonly viewed as a foundational prerequisite for confidence and perceived control in clinical practice. Empirical work using structural equation modelling (SEM) in related health-professional contexts has suggested that knowledge is positively associated with PBC and attitudes (standardised coefficients typically reported in the range of 0.18–0.30), while associations with SN are often weaker or non-significant.15 16 On this basis, the present study extends the TPB framework by modelling Knowledge as an antecedent construct, forming an Extended TPB (E-TPB) framework that may better reflect competency-based training contexts and clarify how knowledge may directly and indirectly influence counselling intention through core psychosocial components.

Based on the above theoretical framework, this study is designed to establish an appropriate measurement foundation for the components of the E-TPB in Vietnamese medical students and to examine the hypothesised relationships between professional knowledge, psychosocial constructs, and contraceptive counselling intention. Specifically, the study focuses on the following objectives:

Objective 1. To describe the current status of contraceptive counselling knowledge and the core components of the TPB, including Attitude, SN, PBC and Counselling Intention, among Vietnamese medical students, based on standardised measurement instruments.

Objective 1. To describe the current status of contraceptive counselling knowledge and the core components of the TPB, including Attitude, SN, PBC and Counselling Intention, among Vietnamese medical students, based on standardised measurement instruments.

Objective 2. To evaluate the measurement validity and structural stability of the Knowledge scale and TPB-based constructs in the context of Vietnamese medical students, providing a methodological basis for subsequent behavioural model testing.

Sample size

This study is based on a single nationwide cross-sectional survey, from which two independent analytical datasets will be derived a priori to address distinct research objectives specified in the protocol.

A total sample of approximately 1900 medical students is planned. Of these, approximately 600 participants will be allocated to the scale development and validation phase, and approximately 1300 to the main survey phase for descriptive analyses and SEM.

Phase 1: scale development and validation

The scale development phase will include approximately 600 medical students, comprising approximately 100 for pilot testing and approximately 500 for formal psychometric evaluation using classical test theory (CTT), item response theory (IRT), exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA).

The sample was split into two independent subsamples of 300 each for EFA and CFA, respectively, meeting commonly recommended sample size requirements for stable factor extraction and factor loading estimation. In addition, the full validation sample (n=600) was used for IRT analyses, a sample size that exceeds established thresholds for reliable parameter estimation in IRT models for multi-item instruments.17 18

Phase 2: main survey and SEM

The main survey will include approximately 1300 medical students recruited from 12 medical training institutions across Vietnam. For hypothesis testing using covariance-based SEM (CB-SEM), approximately 880–900 students in years 3–6 will be included. In the structural model, the psychosocial components of the E-TPB (Attitude, SN, PBC and Counselling Intention) will be modelled as latent variables indicated by their respective Likert-scale items, while professional knowledge will be represented by an IRT-derived latent ability score (θ) rather than individual knowledge items, in order to minimise measurement error. The target sample size is intended to ensure stable parameter estimation, adequate statistical power for testing direct and mediated pathways using bootstrap resampling, and sufficient precision for model fit evaluation. In addition, the planned sample size allows for MGSEM analyses to assess measurement and structural invariance across key subgroups (eg, by sex and training stage), which typically require larger samples to achieve reliable estimation and valid group comparisons. Simulation studies have suggested that complex SEM models with mediation and multi-group comparisons generally require sample sizes of 800 or more to achieve acceptable power (≥0.80) and to reduce SE bias in parameter estimates.19 Approximately 400 first-year and second-year students (Y1-Y2) will be included exclusively for descriptive analyses of knowledge and TPB constructs across stages of medical training. These participants will not be included in SEM analyses, as they have not yet completed core coursework in obstetrics and gynaecology or reproductive health, have limited exposure to WHO Medical Eligibility Criteria (MEC) and have not received foundational clinical counselling skills training. Inclusion of this group in SEM analyses may therefore compromise the validity of latent construct estimation.

Allowance for non-response

Target sample sizes in both phases have been inflated by approximately 10% to account for potential non-response and incomplete questionnaires.

Sampling strategy and recruitment

A multistage sampling strategy combining purposive selection of clusters (universities and classes) and convenience sampling of students within those clusters. This approach aims to ensure implementation feasibility and comparability between study groups.

Medical schools were purposively selected to represent diverse geographical regions and training models in Vietnam. All participating institutions offer a full-time MD programme.

Within each institution, the student population is stratified by academic year (Y1–Y6). For analytical purposes, these strata may be aggregated into preclinical (Y1–Y3) and clinical (Y4–Y6) groups to facilitate comparisons across training stages.

Participant recruitment follows a two-stage cluster sampling process. First, active classes or course modules within each academic year stratum are identified as cluster units. From these clusters, target classes are selected for student access. Within the chosen classes, eligible students are invited to participate using convenience sampling until the allocated sample quota for each institution and academic year is fulfilled.

Students are approached via internal academic channels at each institution, including responsible lecturers, class monitors or research focal points. Participation is strictly voluntary, and all students who meet the inclusion criteria are invited to join the study.

Development of the knowledge scale

Concept and measurement scope

In this study, knowledge (K) is defined as the integration of foundational and applied knowledge related to contraceptive counselling in clinical practice. The WHO MEC are used as a core content domain for applied knowledge but do not represent the entire Knowledge concept; therefore, the scale is generally designated as ‘K’ (knowledge) rather than ‘K_MEC’.20

Item format and content

The K scale consists of 25 objective multiple-choice questions in a true/false/don’t know format; the ‘don’t know’ option is included to minimise guessing bias in the self-administered survey. The items were systematically developed by the core research team’s three primary investigators under the strict supervision and content validation of leading Obstetrics, Gynaecology and Reproductive Health experts at Hanoi Medical University.

Conceptual framework and study hypotheses

The theoretical framework of this study is grounded in the E-TPB. According to this framework, behavioural intention is the most proximal predictor of actual behaviour and is influenced by three core psychosocial components: A, SN and PBC.22

Within the context of medical education, the formation of intention to practise contraceptive counselling depends not only on psychosocial factors but is also closely linked to foundational professional competence.23 24 Therefore, this study extends the traditional TPB framework by integrating knowledge (K) as a key antecedent variable. Knowledge is seen as a necessary condition that enables medical students to evaluate the value of behaviour appropriately, perceive control over counselling practice, and form behavioural intentions in clinical contexts, rather than being treated as a peripheral or external factor.

As illustrated in figure 2, knowledge is modelled as an exogenous variable that may influence contraceptive counselling intention through both direct and indirect pathways mediated by the core components of the E-TPB.

Statistical analysis

All analyses will be conducted according to an a priori analysis plan. After data cleaning, analyses will be performed using R (V.4.4.1). The analytical workflow will follow sequential phases aligned with the study objectives: (1) scale standardisation; (2) descriptive and exploratory analyses and (3) testing of the theoretical model using SEM and MGSEM.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethics approval

The CVI assessment conducted by subject-matter experts and the cognitive pilot study involving a small group of medical students were undertaken solely for instrument development and refinement. These preparatory phases did not constitute data collection for the main study, and findings derived from these activities were not included in any primary analyses.

The study protocol was formally approved by the Institutional Ethical Review Board for Biomedical Research at Hanoi Medical University on 3 March 2026 (Approval No. 120/GCN-KHCN).

Following approval, the study was implemented across 12 participating medical training institutions, representing a range of geographical regions and educational models nationwide. Data collection procedures at each site will comply with local scientific governance requirements and applicable ethical regulations.
