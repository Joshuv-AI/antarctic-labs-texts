Supervising trainee research projects: an interview study of nephrology supervisors in Aotearoa New Zealand
Authors: Natasha Houghton‐Jones, Katherine G Richards, Kalpa Jayanatha, Suetonia C Green
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResearch projects are a mandatory requirement for advanced training in Aotearoa New Zealand, yet trainees and supervisors report challenges in the research process. While barriers and enablers of research have been described internationally, the experience within physician training in New Zealand has not been formally explored.AimsWe aimed to identify barriers and enablers of research conducted by nephrology trainees in New Zealand from the perspective of supervising nephrologists and to inform evidence‐based recommendations for addressing challenges.MethodsWe carried out semi‐structured interviews with nephrologists who had supervised at least one advanced training research project in New Zealand until theoretical sufficiency occurred. Reflexive thematic analysis was conducted on transcribed interviews.ResultsEighteen nephrologists from eight training centres were interviewed. Five themes were identified: (1) perceived value motivates supervisors; (2) research constrained by feasibility, including competing clinical demands with insufficient research time, limited access to specialised research support and bureaucratic delays; (3) trainee engagement and ownership facilitated success; (4) overcoming trainee inexperience with supervisor guidance; and (5) the importance of research infrastructure.ConclusionPerceived value, trainee ownership, adequate supervision and supportive infrastructure were critical to successful research projects.Keywords: research supervision, research activities, graduate medical education

Introduction

Research projects are mandatory in many undergraduate and postgraduate medical education programmes. 1 , 2 Research is prioritised in medical training to enhance critical appraisal skills, inform evidence‐based practice and develop research competencies. 1 , 3 , 4 , 5 Medical practitioners are uniquely positioned to identify and translate clinically relevant research. 5 In recent years, there has been growing concern about the declining number of clinician‐academics globally and in New Zealand. 6 , 7 Factors contributing to declining numbers may include increased pressures on health services, lack of flexible training positions, decreased interest in academic medicine and fewer clinical academic positions. 6 , 8 , 9 Studies also suggest that some health practitioners view research as less of a priority compared to clinical care. 8 Junior doctors in particular struggle to balance their clinical and academic responsibilities. 10 Positive research training experiences during postgraduate medical specialisation may increase the numbers of clinicians seeking academic involvement. 7 , 9 The Royal Australasian College of Physicians (RACP) requires advanced trainees to complete a research project with an estimated time commitment of 200 h. Acceptable projects include original research or audits of sufficient size and quality. 2 Projects are mandatory for Fellowship eligibility and are typically supervised by physicians or academics on a voluntary basis.

In the 2024 RACP Medical Training Survey, only 65% of trainees reported they had sufficient opportunity to develop knowledge and skills in research, which was lower than all other curriculum domains surveyed (range 83%–97%). 11 Barriers to research in specialist medical training have been reported and include inexperience with research, delays in accessing timely ethics approval, limited time for research with competing responsibilities and a lack of funding for research activities. 10 Factors facilitating successful research included the selection of appropriately sized projects that aligned with the trainees' genuine interests, dedicated research time and a responsive approach from supervisors. 10 Similar factors have been identified in undergraduate medical research training. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 Supervision quality and the supervisor‐student relationship have also been recognised as crucial to research success in higher research degrees. 12 , 16 , 17 , 18

The barriers and enablers of research in the context of New Zealand physician training have not been formally explored. We aimed to identify barriers and enablers of research conducted by nephrology trainees in New Zealand from the perspective of supervising nephrologists and to inform evidence‐based recommendations for addressing challenges.

Methods

This semi‐structured interview study was conducted and reported according to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines. 19 Institutional ethical approval was provided by the University of Otago Human Ethics Committee (reference number 24/020).

Participant selection and recruitment

Vocationally registered nephrologists holding Fellowship with RACP who had supervised the advanced training research project of at least one advanced trainee in New Zealand were eligible to participate. Participants were recruited via email through the authors' professional networks. Purposive sampling was used to target participants with the expertise to meaningfully explore the research phenomenon. Participants from a range of geographic regions (tertiary/regional centres), levels of research and supervision experience, years post qualification, ethnicity and gender were deliberately selected. A supervisor was considered experienced if they held an academic position or higher research degree and had supervised five or more research projects. Ethnicity data were used to contextualise analysis but are not reported due to the small participant pool and risk of identification.

Study design

Semi‐structured interviews were conducted with participants via digital video technology (Zoom) or face to face. Interviews occurred between April 2024 and December 2024 with two authors (N. Houghton‐Jones and K. G. Richards). N. Houghton‐Jones is a female Pākehā (non‐indigenous) nephrology trainee, and K. G. Richards is a female Pākehā early career nephrologist and PhD candidate. Both have previous experience in qualitative research interviewing. N. Houghton‐Jones undertook this project in the context of a trainee research project. K. G. Richards had not previously supported trainee research and had experience of her own trainee research projects. Participants were reassured of confidentiality and anonymity to reduce the influence of social desirability bias. In view of N. Houghton‐Jones's position as a trainee and consequent power dynamics, none of her direct clinical supervisors were interviewed. All interviews were recorded and transcribed by N. Houghton‐Jones or a voluntary research assistant with a confidentiality agreement. All participants were offered the opportunity to review their transcript. Theoretical sufficiency was defined as no new themes emerging. 20

Data analysis

Analysis was conducted using a latent inductive approach following the framework for reflexive thematic analysis described by Braun and Clarke. 21 Deidentified transcripts were uploaded into qualitative analysis software (Dedoose version 9.2). N. Houghton‐Jones coded transcripts line by line. K. G. Richards and S. C. Green participated in two collaborative coding sessions with N. Houghton‐Jones to enhance reflexivity and reliability. Provisional themes created by N. Houghton‐Jones were reviewed by K. G. Richards for investigator triangulation prior to meeting with all authors to establish themes and subthemes. Results were circulated to participants for feedback.

Results

Eighteen participants were interviewed from eight of the 10 training centres (six North Island Te Ika‐a‐Māui, two South Island Te Waipounamu centres, including six tertiary (n = 15) and two secondary/regional hospitals (n = 3)). One supervisor declined to participate due to capacity constraints; four did not respond to recruitment emails. Seven were experienced research supervisors. Nine of the participants were female and nine were male. Interviews ranged from 23 to 53 min (median 36 min). Theoretical sufficiency was reached after 18 interviews. We identified five themes and 14 subthemes. These are summarised in Figure 1. Supporting participant quotes are presented in Table S1.

Themes and subthemes.

Theme 1: perceived value motivates supervisors

Desiring value

Supervisors were motivated to support research projects they perceived to be valuable to the individual trainee, the department or nephrology practice. Projects were considered valuable to the trainee if they developed useful skills or resulted in publication. Some participants noted that doing research enhanced trainees' clinical reasoning, allowing them to better interpret and apply evidence. These skills were thought useful even for trainees without academic aspirations. Projects were considered valuable to the department when they investigated locally important clinical questions. Some participants described trainees as uniquely placed to investigate practical local questions not likely to be addressed by external funding or more experienced researchers. Participants reported they were less eager to support trainee research if they could not see value in the project or if they believed the trainee was putting in the minimum effort needed to meet mandatory research requirements.

Developing relationships

Several participants reported that projects provided an early opportunity for trainees to connect with other researchers, attend conferences and build research networks. This was particularly valuable for those wishing to pursue further academic training. Some participants described research supervision developing into a broader mentorship of junior colleagues; they valued this and reported it motivated them to supervise research.

Prioritising research in proportion to training value

Many participants expressed that time dedicated to research should be proportional to its value within speciality training. Views differed on the degree of this value. Some participants believed research should be a secondary consideration after developing clinical competence. These participants emphasised the importance of research not competing or interfering with clinical exposure or detracting from clinical skill competency development. Some questioned whether research should still be required. Conversely, other participants believed the current level of research exposure was inadequate and that training would be improved by having more stringent research requirements. However, there was broad consensus among participants that providing safe clinical care takes precedence over developing research competencies.

Theme 2: constrained by feasibility

Limited time and competing demands

Several participants noted that producing high‐quality research was time‐intensive and highlighted how challenging it was to find this time when working full time (or more) clinically. All participants noted that trainees, and often supervising nephrologists, are constrained by competing clinical demands and urgency and limited research time. Participants reported that time constraints had been exacerbated in recent years by escalating clinical workloads and staffing shortages. Some participants reported difficulty maintaining the interest and curiosity needed for research in this context. Many participants supported protected research time but recognised it was hard to maintain. Several participants highlighted that it was unrealistic for a project to be completed within working hours and emphasised that trainees are required to put in substantial amounts of their own time.

Challenges accessing specialised skills

Limited access to specialised research support was a major obstacle identified by participants. For example, a paucity of qualified biostatisticians often caused substantial delays. Another commonly cited challenge was insufficient guidance on cultural consultation with tāngata whenua (indigenous peoples), as is usually required for research approvals. Many participants lacked training and confidence in this area. Participants in several regions reported that formal processes for engaging with iwi and hapū (indigenous tribal structures) were absent, inactive or inaccessible. They described difficulty and delays meeting mandatory cultural consultation requirements for ethics approval in this context. Some participants relied on personal connections with research‐trained Māori colleagues, reporting these were often informal, unrecognised and unpaid. Participants felt this placed an unfair burden on Māori colleagues.

Delayed by multilevel bureaucracy

Participants in some regions reported low risk ethics application pathways had been abolished. Lengthy ethics review processes caused delays and were seen as a barrier to timely project completion. Participants also noted significant duplication of effort between the ethics approval process (regional or national) and localities approval (required by individual hospitals). Multiple applications were often needed, causing additional delays. Lack of reciprocal recognition of approval between hospitals was reported to be particularly challenging for trainees conducting multi‐site research or moving for clinical placements. A few participants described this process as so disheartening it deterred trainees (and colleagues) from undertaking research. Some participants supported re‐instituting a separate ethics pathway for low‐risk studies (audits, retrospective cohort and qualitative research) and exploring reciprocal recognition between regions.

Theme 3: trainee engagement and ownership facilitate research

Importance of project management

Participants identified trainee attributes which facilitated successful research. These included organisation, time management, self‐motivation, initiative, being proactive and the ability to delegate and collaborate. Research projects were recognised by participants as an opportunity for trainees to develop these general management skills and considered these skills valuable for future leadership as consultants.

Expecting ownership

Participants expected trainees to take ownership of projects and responsibility for their progress. Some expressed frustration when this did not happen. Supervisors found they often had to drive progress by chasing trainees or working on projects themselves. This was described as particularly disappointing when projects of local importance were abandoned or delayed to the extent that results were obsolete. Some supervisors mitigated this by scheduling regular progress meetings or explicitly setting expectations, which proved effective with some trainees. Participants viewed their role as supportive: to provide guidance. However, for projects to be successful, participants considered that trainees needed to take responsibility and actively drive them forward.

Valuing enthusiasm

When trainees were engaged and enthusiastic, participants reported positive and mutually beneficial experiences. Some supervisors enjoyed working on research with trainees, feeling energised and motivated by it. Others found it rewarding to see trainees develop new research skills. One supervisor found it particularly satisfying to co‐publish research in quality journals. Recognition by senior colleagues was identified by participants as a motivating factor for trainees. Some participants hoped their own enthusiasm for projects would inspire and motivate trainees.

Theme 4: overcoming inexperience with guidance

Initial inexperience

Several participants highlighted that most trainees had little prior research experience, making projects a steep learning curve associated with stress. Some participants noted trainees' research inexperience contrasted sharply with their comparatively developed clinical skills. They recognised this was uncomfortable for those accustomed to professional competence. However, most participants did not see inexperience as insurmountable, reporting that trainees were able to acquire the necessary skills while working on projects provided supervisor support was adequate. Some participants suggested that introducing research projects earlier in training could ease this transition. Early initiation of projects was also suggested as a way of reducing time pressure at the end of training.

Supervisors as guides

Supervisors described their role as that of a guide, providing advice, support, connections and feedback. This intellectual input occurred at all stages of the project, from research question development, protocol writing, methodology selection and ethics application to data analysis and critical review of manuscripts. Participants also connected trainees with others who could assist, such as biostatisticians. A few participants noted that it would have been faster and easier to do the project themselves than to guide a trainee. However, participants recognised the importance of the learning process and sought to minimise the extent to which they undertook the work themselves.

Several participants highlighted project selection as an area where guidance was most needed and most useful. Developing a good research question was recognised as an advanced skill that many trainees lacked. Some participants reported their departments kept a list of topics for trainees to choose from. Others encouraged trainee‐driven project selection where supervisors worked closely with them to develop the research question. Helping anticipate and avoid major pitfalls was also recognised as crucial. A common pitfall was taking on projects that were unrealistically large or unfeasible with existing resources. Participants tried to guide trainees towards projects that were well scoped and realistic in the timeframe. Adequate supervision with timely and responsive guidance was identified as key to project success by several participants.

Theme 5: the importance of infrastructure

Limited infrastructure and inequitable access

Many participants highlighted the absence of research infrastructure – such as dedicated research time, funding, research methods training or biostatistics support – within Health New Zealand Te Whatu Ora. This was perceived to be a barrier for trainees and non‐university‐affiliated clinicians conducting research. Some participants contrasted this with their international experience in centres with greater research infrastructure, such as in the United Kingdom or Australia. They noted that environments which supported and rewarded research were more likely to foster successful clinical academics. A few participants suggested creating a small number of competitive publicly funded research fellow positions each year for trainees interested in pursuing a clinical academic pathway. However, others felt change was unrealistic with current health system funding constraints. Several participants suggested that taking time out of training for a higher research degree or fellowship was the only viable pathway for those trainees wishing to pursue an academic career in the current New Zealand training model.

Desiring college guidance

Some participants expressed a desire for clearer expectations, guidance and more research support from the RACP. Supervisors reported difficulty guiding trainees towards successful project outcomes when requirements were unclear. Some participants reported insufficient support to develop the skills they needed to supervise research. They expressed a desire for more formal research training resources from the college or mentoring from experienced research nephrologists.

The importance of informal networks

Several participants described relying on informal networks and personal connections to compensate for the lack of formal research infrastructure. This approach was often reported to be effective. Participants noted the small nephrology community in New Zealand fostered personal connections and a spirit of generosity towards supporting fellow researchers and trainees – even unremunerated. However, some reported that the benefits of informal networks were not evenly distributed. Supervisors with less research experience found it more difficult to access expertise on behalf of their trainees.

Several participants suggested establishing a formalised research network at a regional or national level. They believed this could better leverage existing expertise and harness trainee time to address high‐priority questions for the nephrology community. Other participants were opposed to the idea, fearing it might introduce additional bureaucracy, reduce departmental autonomy, centralise research priorities among a few individuals and discourage researchers from pursuing original ideas. One participant emphasised that membership in any formalised research network should remain voluntary.

Recommendations for optimising projects

Based on the above themes, seven recommendations were identified from the data, including selection of feasible projects, early start of research project, access to biostatistics and cultural consultation, streamlined ethics pathways, dedicated research time, clarity around RACP expectations and research methodology training. Discussion

Our study determined that, from a supervisor perspective, research projects thrived where motivated trainees with strong management skills selected feasible projects. Supervisor guidance was crucial to success. Supervisors were motivated to invest in projects they believed valuable. Challenges include limited time, competing clinical demands, unclear research requirements and a lack of research infrastructure.

Our findings are consistent with existing literature on the barriers and enablers of research among medical students and trainees. 4 , 10 , 12 , 14 , 15 A 2022 mixed‐methods study of 130 Australian medical student research supervisors identified student inexperience, lack of dedicated time, competing academic demands, ethics approval delays and insufficient student motivation as barriers. 12 Interviews with 12 family medicine registrars in South Africa reported similar challenges. 10 Of note, factors enabling research in that study included the selection of appropriately scoped projects, responsive and collaborative supervision, genuine registrar interest and protected research time. We did not identify reports describing research experiences for Australian trainees, but these would be valuable to explore and compare given the shared RACP framework.

Our study expands on the existing research by emphasising the importance of perceived value of research for supervisor and trainee engagement. We also provide insights into trainee research experiences specific to New Zealand, including gaps in support for Māori cultural consultation and a desire for increased guidance from the RACP on research requirements. The absence of local research infrastructure was notable in contrast to jurisdictions with comparable postgraduate specialist training (particularly Australia or the United Kingdom). We identified a diversity of views within the nephrology community regarding the place of research in training, in terms of the importance of research and approaches to addressing barriers. This lack of consensus highlights areas to be explored in future initiatives to improve research within training.

While this study focused on nephrology supervisors, there are implications for research training across RACP specialties. All specialties require research projects as a component of training. A recently published survey of new fellows of the RACP reported that of all practice domains, respondents felt least prepared for research at training completion. 22 Our findings may support efforts to enhance training experiences and research competence.

This study provides insights into the barriers and enablers of research in New Zealand and identifies practical data‐driven recommendations for optimising trainee research. We chose to interview nephrologists who supervised research as their longer experience enables recognition of common patterns and recurring challenges. However, this study does not capture trainee perspectives. Further work to triangulate findings with the experiences of trainees would be beneficial. The use of purposive sampling may introduce selection bias by preferentially including participants with greater interest in research compared with the wider nephrology workforce. However, purposive sampling also maximised heterogeneity and facilitated inclusion of participants with diverse perspectives. The investigators in this work ranged in position from a senior academic nephrologist to a nephrology trainee, providing a range of perspectives to inform data collection and analysis. Potential risks of bias were moderated through data triangulation across all investigators. The roles that investigators had in clinical research and trainee development were a potential source of bias and may have influenced interpretation of data, but it was mitigated through team‐based analysis.

Conclusion

Perceived value, trainee ownership, adequate supervision and supportive infrastructure were critical to successful and satisfying research projects. This study determined that targeted departmental strategies such as feasible project selection, timely initiation of projects, developing supportive research environments and clarifying college research expectations could enhance project quality and trainee experiences.
