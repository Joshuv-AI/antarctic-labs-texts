CCR7-PERK-CREB3L1 signaling mediates tumor-associated macrophages-derived SPP1 promotion of oral squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Shanfeng Lin, Tian Li, Weiqi Li, Cong Yan, Wanhang Zhou, Haoxuan Wu, Zengxu Wang, Zhenning Li, Changfu Sun, Fayu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChemokine receptor type 7 (CCR7) is extensively implicated in the regulation of inflammatory signaling. Although CCR7 is frequently overexpressed in malignant epithelial cells, its immunomodulatory role in tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and its contribution to oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) progression remain poorly defined. To investigate the functional relevance of CCR7 in TAMs, we establish a CCR7-deficient (Ccr7-/-) mouse model of OSCC. We find that loss of CCR7 in TAMs suppresses the malignant phenotype of OSCC cells and inhibits OSCC progression. We perform single-cell RNA sequencing to profile CCR7-dependent transcriptional changes in TAMs. This analysis identifies SPP1 as a key CCR7-regulated effector that promotes the malignant phenotype of OSCC cells. Consistent with this finding, in vitro assays confirm that TAM-derived SPP1 promotes the malignant phenotype of OSCC cells, whereas neutralization of SPP1 reverses OSCC progression. Mechanistically, CCR7 in TAMs regulates the transcription factor CREB3L1 via the PERK pathway, thus enhancing the expression of its downstream target gene SPP1. We further show that inhibition of the CCR7-CREB3L1 axis in TAMs significantly suppresses OSCC progression in vivo. These findings suggest that targeting the CCR7–CREB3L1 axis in TAMs may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for OSCC.Subject terms: Cell growth, Oral cancer

Introduction

Oral cancer is one of the most prevalent malignancies of the head and neck, with oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) accounting for ~90% of all oral cancer cases1. The incidence of OSCC has been steadily escalating in recent years2. Despite substantial advancements in surgical techniques, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy over the past three decades, the 5-year overall survival rate for OSCC patients has remained below 50%, primarily attributed to the high proportion of cases diagnosed at advanced stages3–6. Consequently, there is an urgent imperative to establish novel and more effective therapeutic strategies. Recently, immunotherapy and targeted therapy have been extensively explored as treatment options for patients with solid tumors, yielding promising results. In 2016, the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) granted approval for immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), thereby broadening the therapeutic options available for the management of recurrent and metastatic head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC)7. ICIs targeting PD-1 have shown notable efficacy in HNSCC, but only a limited proportion of patients (<20%) exhibit a response to mono-immunotherapy7. Additionally, recent clinical trials investigating neoadjuvant mono- or dual-immunotherapy in advanced OSCC have demonstrated only modest clinical benefits8,9. Hence, it is imperative to identify novel therapeutic targets and develop alternative strategies to improve the efficacy of ICIs in the larger cohort of OSCC patients.

Tumor microenvironment (TME) encompasses a diverse array of immune and stromal cells, which are integral to tumor progression and the response to therapeutic interventions10. It has been well-established that the degree of immune cell infiltration correlates directly with the clinical prognosis of cancer patients11. Consequently, a more profound understanding of the immunological landscape within the OSCC microenvironment is essential for identifying novel strategies to enhance the therapeutic efficacy of OSCC immunotherapy. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) represent a pivotal constituent of the tumor microenvironment and are integral to various aspects of malignancy, significantly influencing the clinical prognosis of OSCC12–14. It has been well-established that TAMs and tumor cells exhibit close spatial interactions and engage in intricate, multidirectional intercellular signaling15. Commonly, TAMs secrete a broad array of cytokines and chemokines that facilitate tumor progression, metastatic dissemination, and immune evasion16,17. As extensively documented, TAMs exhibit notable phenotypic and functional plasticity, with a common classification into two subsets: M1 and M2, associated with tumor suppression and promotion, respectively18. However, emerging evidence has highlighted that TAMs with tumor-promoting functions display highly heterogeneous and multifaceted phenotypic profiles in vivo, which cannot be adequately explained by the conventional M1/M2 dichotomy14,19,20. This underscores the critical need to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the establishment of such heterogeneous TAM populations. Recently, Mikael J. Pittet et al. systematically established a cohort of 51 patients diagnosed with HNSCC, identifying that the mutually exclusive expression of CXCL9 and SPP1 in TAMs serves as a critical factor in determining whether these TAMs exhibit an antitumor or pro-tumor phenotype14. Consequently, targeting SPP1+TAMs may represent a promising therapeutic approach for OSCC.

CC-chemokine receptor 7 (CCR7) is a G protein-coupled, seven-transmembrane chemokine receptor expressed on a wide range of immune cells, including macrophages, dendritic cells, and both B and T lymphocytes21,22. In concert with its ligands, CCL19 and CCL21, CCR7 regulates a broad range of migratory processes in both health and disease23. In light of this, CCR7 signaling has gained attention as a promising target for antitumor therapies22. Activation of CCR7 signaling has been implicated in cancer cell invasion, metastasis, and lymphangiogenesis across various cancer types, including head and neck, breast, prostate, and colon cancers, as well as B cell malignancies24–28. A proposed metastatic mechanism suggests that CCR7-expressing tumor cells can ‘home’ to lymph nodes, mimicking the behavior of immune cells29. Our prior work also revealed that the upregulation of CCR7 in OSCC cancer cells is linked to macrophage recruitment and M2 polarization during tumor progression30. While earlier studies have focused on the consequences of CCR7 overexpression in cancer cells, research on its role within other components of the TME during OSCC progression remains limited. To further elucidate the precise role of CCR7 in the OSCC TME, our recent study revealed that OSCC in Ccr7-/- C57BL/6 mice exhibited significant attenuation compared to wild-type (WT) mice, with notable alterations in the microenvironment, particularly a reduction in the infiltration of tumor-associated macrophages, as assessed by single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq)31. However, the underlying mechanisms by which CCR7 influences TAMs in OSCC remain unclear, especially in relation to the pathogenesis, progression, and/or invasion of OSCC.

Here, we aimed to investigate the role and mechanism of CCR7 in tumor-associated macrophages in OSCC. Further analysis of the single-cell RNA sequencing data from our previously published work revealed a crosstalk between TAMs and OSCC cells, in which CCR7 modulates the secretion of SPP1 by TAMs. This secreted factor influences OSCC cell proliferation, invasion, cell cycle progression, and partial epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (pEMT). Mechanistic studies revealed that CCR7 in macrophages upregulates the expression of the transcription factor cAMP-responsive element-binding protein 3-like 1 (CREB3L1) via the PERK pathway, which subsequently promotes the transcription of SPP1 as its downstream target gene. Furthermore, we identified that inhibition of the CCR7-CREB3L1 axis in TAMs significantly suppresses OSCC progression in vivo, suggesting that targeting this pathway could provide novel therapeutic strategies to improve the efficacy of OSCC treatment.

Results

cNMF revealed CCR7-mediated functionally heterogeneous gene expression programs in tumour cells

The Cancer Genome Atlas dataset indicates overall CCR7 upregulation in tumors tissues (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Thus, we generated CCR7-deficient C57BL/6 mice (Supplementary Fig. 1B, C) to investigate its role in OSCC. CCR7 depletion did not induce any apparent morphological or physiological abnormalities, including no significant changes in survival rates. Subsequently, the flanks of both Ccr7−/− and wild-type mice were injected with murine syngeneic OSCC cells. Notably, tumor growth was markedly delayed in Ccr7−/− mice compared with wild-type controls, as demonstrated in our prior publication31.

Transcriptional heterogeneity in cancer is increasingly recognized as a key driver of tumor progression, metastasis and treatment resistance32. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has enabled unbiased transcriptomic profiling of individual tumor cells, revealing significant heterogeneity even within cells from the same tumor11,33,34. Our prior work employed scRNA-seq to analyze the TME changes mediated by CCR7 in tumor-bearing wild-type and Ccr7−/− mice31. To further investigate the heterogeneity of tumor cells in the tumor microenvironment mediated by CCR7, we extracted tumor cell data (Supplementary Fig. 1D) and employed cNMF to identify gene modules representing co-expressed gene sets. Using K-selection plots, we determined the optimal value for K (Fig. 1A). Lastly, the cNMF analysis revealed that tumor cells could be classified into four distinct meta-programs (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. 1E), reflecting the complex phenotypes of functionally heterogeneous tumor cells within the tumor microenvironment.

To further investigate the functional implications of each meta-program, enrichment analysis was performed on the top 50 genes with the highest weight in each meta-program (Supplementary Table S1). Relevant pathways were identified, revealing significant variations in the transcriptomic profiles across the meta-programs. These were functionally categorized as the inflammation meta-program, cell cycle meta-program, epithelial cell differentiation (Epi-diff) meta-program, and pEMT meta-program (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. 1F). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis of their specific characteristic gene expression signatures confirmed these functional classifications. The inflammation meta program was mainly enriched in inflammatory responses, regulation of cytokine production, regulation of interleukin-1 production, and IL-17 signaling pathway (Supplementary Fig. 1G). The cell cycle meta program was significantly enriched in mitotic cell cycle, cell division, and cell cycle process (Supplementary Fig. 1H). The Epi-diff meta program was significantly enriched in epithelial cell differentiation, cell-cell junction organization, and protein localization to cell–cell junction, while pEMT meta program was highly enriched in ECM-receptor interaction, focal adhesion, protein digestion and absorption, and PI3K-Akt signaling pathway (Supplementary Fig. 1I, J). Similarly, according to the heatmap (Fig. 1D), there were significant differences in the activity of the cells in these different expression programs. The annotated genes have a greater weight in the corresponding expression programs, which indicates that these genes may play a crucial role in defining these expression programs. The pEMT meta-program constituted the largest proportion of tumor cells, indicating that OSCC is a hyper-metastasis disease (Fig. 1E). Previous studies have shown that the pEMT program may facilitate local invasion and lymph node metastasis in head and neck cancers. Furthermore, the proportion of tumor cells in the cell cycle meta-program was significantly lower in the Ccr7−/− group compared to the wild-type group (Fig. 1E). Thus, pEMT and cell cycle meta-program tumors cells may be a crucial process in CCR7-mediated OSCC development.

Overall, by integrating cNMF with prior research, we have further refined the TME of OSCC into 11 distinct subpopulations: macrophages, monocytes, granulocytes, fibroblasts, T cells, B cells, endothelial cells, cell cycle-associated tumor cells, pEMT-associated tumor cells, epithelial cell differentiation-associated tumor cells, and inflammation-associated tumor cells (Fig. 1F). This refined classification provides a more comprehensive understanding of the heterogeneous cellular landscape within the TME of OSCC.

CCR7 deficiency in TAMs represses the malignant phenotype of OSCC cells

To further investigate the interactions between various cell identities and tumor cells within the tumor microenvironment of OSCC, we conducted correlation analyses to assess the associations between different cell types. Our findings revealed that TAMs showed the strongest correlation with tumor cells and exhibited significantly higher CCR7 expression compared to other cell types (Fig. 2A). Additionally, a significant positive correlation was observed between CCR7 expression and both macrophages and MRC1 in the TCGA-HNSCC datasets (Fig. 2B, C; R = 0.392, P < 0.001 for macrophages; R = 0.366, P < 0.001 for MRC1). These results sparked our interest in further exploring the role of CCR7 in TAMs.

Building on these findings, we next isolated BMDMs from wild-type and CCR7-deficient C57BL/6 mice (Fig. 2D), confirming their identity through cell morphology (Supplementary Fig. 2A) and the expression of CD11b and F4/80 (Supplementary Fig. 2B). To mimic the tumor microenvironment of TAMs in vitro, we utilized a Transwell-based co-culture system to co-culture BMDMs with MOC2 cells (Fig. 2D), or treated BMDMs with conditioned medium from MOC2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 2C). Subsequently, CCK-8, cell cycle analysis by flow cytometry and Transwell assays were employed to assess the proliferation, cell cycle, migration and invasion of MOC2 cells. The results demonstrated that the co-culture of MOC2 cells with KO-TAMs led to a significant inhibition of this process, compared to MOC2 cells co-cultured with WT-TAMs. This highlights the suppressive effect of CCR7 deficiency in TAMs on the malignant phenotype of OSCC cells (Fig. 2E–G).

Overall, these findings indicate that CCR7 deficiency in TAMs significantly reduces the malignant phenotype of OSCC cells.

Construction of intercellular communication networks between diverse cell types and heterogeneous cancer cells in the CCR7-mediated TME

Secretion of soluble factors by tumor-associated macrophages is a major contributor to the malignant phenotypes observed in OSCC cells35. Given that the co-culture system permits only the transfer of soluble factors via Transwell pores, it suggests that the promotion of OSCC progression by WT-TAMs is likely mediated through the secretion of such factors. To identify the specific soluble factors involved in OSCC progression, we utilized CellChat analysis to investigate the distinct intercellular signaling interactions between different cell types, specifically TAMs, and the functionally heterogeneous tumor cells present in both the WT and KO groups. Analysis of interaction strengths in the two-dimensional space indicates that macrophages are the primary source of both outgoing and incoming signals in both WT and KO groups (Fig. 3A, B), further confirming the pivotal role of macrophages in the CCR7-mediated tumor microenvironment. Additionally, our findings show a significant reduction in SPP1 and MIF signaling in the KO group compared to the WT group, suggesting a possible contribution to the observed phenotypic differences (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Notably, macrophages are the main source of SPP1 and MIF within the tumor microenvironment, while tumor cells demonstrate negligible output of these signals (Supplementary Fig. 3B).

Intercellular communication analysis revealed that CCR7 deficiency led to a reduction in interactions between TAMs and cell cycle-related tumor cells, particularly those displaying signaling pairs such as Vegfα-Vegfr1, Spp1-Itgαβ, Lgals9-Havcr2, and Cxcl16-Cxcr6 signaling pairs (Fig. 3C). Extending this investigation, we further examined the communication patterns between TAMs and pEMT tumor cells. Our findings indicated that CCR7 deficiency also diminished interactions between TAMs and pEMT tumors, specifically impacting the Vegfα-Vegfr1, Spp1-Itgαβ, and Il1a-Il1ar1 signaling pairs (Fig. 3D). Additionally, the patterns of intercellular communication between TAMs and both Epi-diff and Inflammation tumor cells are detailed in Supplementary Fig. S3C, D. It is worth highlighting that WT macrophages interacted with cell cycle and pEMT tumor cells via the Spp1-Itgαβ3 ligand-receptor pair, exhibiting notable upregulation compared to KO macrophages (Fig. 3E, F). This finding is supported by evidence indicating that CCR7 in TAMs may orchestrate tumor cell pEMT and proliferation via the Spp1-Itgαβ3 axis.

CCR7 deficiency reshapes the heterogeneity of TAMs in the OSCC TME

To further elucidate the impact of CCR7 on the heterogeneity of TAMs within the tumor microenvironment, we initially conducted a differential gene expression analysis comparing macrophages from KO and WT samples. Our investigation revealed that 37 genes were significantly upregulated (e.g., Fos, Btg2, Dusp1), while 66 genes were downregulated (e.g., SPP1, Slc7a11, Cxcl1) in the KO group compared to the WT group (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, similar to our aforementioned findings from CellChat analysis, the expression of SPP1 in macrophages from the KO group was significantly diminished. Importantly, SPP1 secreted by TAMs plays a crucial role in tumor infiltration and immune suppression. Moreover, recent research has indicated that SPP1+ macrophages significantly impact patient clinical outcomes. TAMs are known to differentiate into functionally distinct subsets, thereby altering the TME. Subsequently, to gain deeper insight into how CCR7 influences the functional subsets of macrophages, we conducted a specific analysis of myeloid cells, focusing on distinct myeloid cell clusters. UMAP analysis revealed seven distinct cell clusters (Srgn_Mono, Cxcl2_Macro, Ccr2_Macro, Cst3_Macro, Egr1_Macro, Spp1_Macro, Rsad2_Macro) based on marker genes and merged them (Fig. 4B). For instance, the Cxcl2_Macro cluster mainly expressed Cxcl2, Cxcl3, and Cle4ce, while the Ccr2_Macro cluster primarily expressed Ccr2, Atf3, and Fos, and the Cst3_Macro cluster predominantly expressed Cst3, C1qb, and Stmn1 (Fig. 4C, Supplementary Fig. 4A). Interestingly, following CCR7 depletion, the proportion of the Spp1_Macro cluster among the identified cell types significantly decreased (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Furthermore, we performed pathway analysis on the macrophage subclusters using gene set variation analysis (GSVA). Based on the results from GSVA and subclustering, the Spp1_Macro cluster was enriched in the angiogenesis, unfolded protein response, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition pathways (Fig. 4D), which is consistent with previous studies.

To gain a more thorough comprehension of the involvement of macrophages subtype contribute to the tumor immune heterogeneity of OSCC, we applied cytoTRACE and Monocle 3 to reconstruct the pseudotemporal trajectory inference of all acquired myeloid cells. As Monocle3 does not directly identify the origin of cell differentiation, we initially applied cytoTRACE to determine the differentiation starting point. After extracting myeloid cell data from scRNA-seq and performing dimensionality reduction, we analyzed the differentiation trajectory of myeloid cells. CytoTRACE analysis revealed that Srgn_Mono possessed the highest differentiation potential, consistent with established developmental hierarchies, while Spp1_Macro occupied the terminal state of the differentiation trajectory (Fig. 4E, F). Building on cytoTRACE results, we used Srgn_Mono as the differentiation origin for Monocle3 analysis, which yielded results consistent with cytoTRACE (Supplementary Fig. 4C). As the pseudotime trajectory progressed, we observed dynamic expression changes in markers specific to macrophage subtypes. The trajectory analysis of key genes (C1qa, Mrc1, S100a6, etc.) revealed that C1qa, Mrc1, and Spp1 were predominantly expressed during the mid-stages of differentiation. In contrast, S100a6 expression remained stable throughout the process, while Plac8 was upregulated in the early stages, gradually decreased, and then increased again in the final stages of differentiation (Supplementary Fig. 4D).

Collectively, SPP1+ TAMs constitute the ultimate differentiated form of TAMs and are involved in the later phases of tumor progression. Notably, CCR7-deficient TAMs exhibit a reduced proportion of SPP1+ TAMs.

CCR7 deficiency in OSCC TAMs decreased SPP1 secretion and inhibited OSCC cell malignant phenotypes

To verify our hypothesis, we initially performed several bioinformatics analyses, revealing a notable increase in SPP1 expression within HNSCC tissues compared to both normal and adjacent tissues (Supplementary Fig. 5A). Moreover, Kaplan-Meier plotter analysis indicated that higher SPP1 expression is associated with a reduced relapse-free survival in HNSCC patients (Supplementary Fig. 5B). These findings suggest that SPP1 overexpression is prevalent in HNSCC and is associated with a poor prognosis. Additionally, a significant positive correlation between the expression of SPP1 and enrichment of macrophages was observed in the TCGA-HNSCC datasets (R = 0.510, P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 5C).

Next, we isolated TAMs from the WT and KO groups with distinct CCR7 expression levels. Both RT-qPCR and western blot analyses confirmed the effective knockdown of CCR7 in BMDMs. Consistently, the expression of SPP1 was also decreased when CCR7 expression was suppressed (Fig. 5A, B). Additionally, we observed a substantial reduction in the secretory level of OPN in the supernatant of KO-TAMs relative to the WT-TAMs group, as assessed by ELISA (Fig. 5C). Together, these findings provide evidence to support the hypothesis that SPP1 functions as a downstream regulatory factor of CCR7 in TAMs.

Given that CCR7 in TAMs regulates SPP1 expression, we investigated whether CCR7 affects OSCC progression through SPP1. Initially, we suppressed OPN levels in the supernatant of WT-TAMs using OPN neutralizing antibodies. This treatment was followed by an assessment of the malignant phenotypes of OSCC cells. As shown in Fig. 5D, transwell migration and invasion assays indicated fewer migratory and invasive cells in the anti-OPN-treated WT-TAMs compared to the untreated WT-TAMs. Additionally, EdU analysis demonstrated that pretreatment of WT-TAMs with anti-OPN significantly reduced the proliferative ability of co-cultured MOC2 cells (Fig. 5E). Similarly, flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that anti-OPN treatment inhibited cell cycle progression, resulting in an increase of cells in the G1 phase and a reduction in the S phase population (Fig. 5F). Given that pEMT is a key process enabling cancer cells to acquire the capacities of invasion and metastasis, we attempted to investigate whether CCR7 in TAMs regulates the pEMT process of OSCC cells via SPP1. RT-qPCR and WB analyses revealed a significant downregulation of pEMT markers, including Pdpn, Lamc2, and Lamb3, along with classic EMT markers such as N-cadherin and Vimentin in MOC2 cells following stimulation with anti-OPN protein in the WT-TAMs group (Fig. 5G–J). Conversely, E-cadherin expression was upregulated (Fig. 5G–J). Subsequently, we further examined the impact of exogenous SPP1 on MOC2 cells by adding rmOPN protein to MOC2 medium for treated 48 h. As anticipated, CCK-8 assay revealed a significant dose-dependent increase in MOC2 cell proliferation following rmOPN treatment (Supplementary Fig. 5D). Similarly, this intervention significantly enhanced the ability of WT-TAMs to promote the malignant phenotype of MOC2 cells (Supplementary Fig. 5E–G).

Collectively, these findings indicate that the deficiency of CCR7 in TAMs inhibits the malignant phenotype of OSCC cells by reducing the secretion of SPP1 derived from TAMs.

CREB3L1 mediates CCR7-dependent SPP1 transcription in TAMs to drive tumor cell malignant phenotypes

Due to the presence of multiple post-translational modifications and the heterogeneity of binding receptors, therapeutic targeting of SPP1 activity remains a complex challenge36. To further explore the mechanism through which CCR7 regulates SPP1 expression in TAMs, we utilized pySCENIC, a bioinformatics pipeline specifically designed for the identification of regulons, to identify the key transcription factors involved in regulating SPP1. In the identified TAMs subpopulations, we identified several transcription factors that regulate the expression of SPP1, including CREB3L1, TWIST1, HMG20A, and NR3C1 (Fig. 6A). Additionally, analysis of the TCGA database revealed a substantial correlation between key transcription factors and SPP1 expression, as well as macrophage enrichment (Fig. 6B, C; Supplementary Fig. 6A–D). Notably, CREB3L1 was found to be the most significant transcription factor involved in the regulation of SPP1. Based on these findings, we hypothesize that CREB3L1 may serve as a critical transcriptional regulator of SPP1 expression in TAMs, thereby influencing the cellular response to CCR7 signaling.

Next, RT-qPCR and WB analyses revealed that CCR7 knockdown resulted in a decrease in CREB3L1 expression levels in BMDMs (Fig. 6D, E). Fluorescence staining further indicated that the fluorescence intensity of CREB3L1 and SPP1 was significantly higher in the WT-TAMs group compared to the KO-TAMs group (Fig. 6F). To further investigate the regulatory relationship between CREB3L1 and SPP1 expression, we transfected macrophages with either a CREB3L1 overexpression vector (CREB3L1-OE) or CREB3L1-specific siRNA (si-CREB3L1), followed by co-culturing with MOC2 cells. After verifying the knockdown efficacy (Supplementary Fig. 6E, F), we observed that the changes in SPP1 expression mirrored the alterations in CREB3L1 expression, as confirmed by Western blot analysis (Fig. 6G–J).

We next performed rescue experiments to determine whether CREB3L1 mediates CCR7-dependent tumor-promoting effects in TAMs. Western blot analysis showed that MOC2 cells co-cultured with CREB3L1-overexpressing KO-TAMs exhibited increased expression of EMT-associated and pEMT-associated markers, compared with cells co-cultured with control KO-TAMs (Supplementary Fig. 6G, H). In contrast, CREB3L1 knockdown in WT-TAMs led to reduced expression of these markers in MOC2 cells relative to control WT-TAMs (Supplementary Fig. 6I, J). Similarly, transwell assays demonstrated that CREB3L1 overexpression in KO-TAMs significantly increased migration and invasion relative to control KO-TAMs (Supplementary Fig. 6K). Conversely, CREB3L1 silencing in WT-TAMs significantly decreased MOC2 cell migration and invasion compared with control WT-TAMs (Supplementary Fig. 6L). Cell cycle analysis further showed that CREB3L1 overexpression in KO-TAMs increased the S-phase population relative to control KO-TAMs (Supplementary Fig. 6M. In contrast, CREB3L1 knockdown in WT-TAMs reduced the S-phase fraction compared with control WT-TAMs, indicating cell cycle arrest (Supplementary Fig. 6N).

Furthermore, we utilized the ChIP assay to explore whether CREB3L1 can bind to the SPP1 transcriptional start site, influencing SPP1 expression as a transcription factor. Utilizing the JASPAR database, we identified a potential CREB3L1 DNA-binding motif within the SPP1 promoter’s C-terminal domain (−1922 to −1945), which was subsequently confirmed as a CREB3L1 binding site (Fig. 6K, L). ChIP RT-qPCR results showed an increase in binding activity at this site (Supplementary Fig. 6O). Parallel ChIP assays in murine BMDMs demonstrated significant enrichment of CREB3L1 at the corresponding Spp1 promoter region, indicating conservation of this transcriptional regulation in the murine system (Supplementary Fig. 6P, Q). Consistent with these findings, dual-luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that mutation of this motif abolished the transcriptional enhancement induced by CREB3L1 overexpression (Fig. 6M).

Collectively, these results suggest that CCR7 modulates the transcription of SPP1 from TAMs through the transcription factor CREB3L1.

CCR7 regulates CREB3L1 expression via PERK/EIF2α signaling pathway

Based on the above results, we furthermore investigated the mechanism by which CCR7 regulates CREB3L1 transcription in macrophages. By conducting GSVA on scRNA-seq data, we observed notable activation of the downstream unfolded protein response (UPR) pathway in SPP1+TAMs (Fig. 4D). GSEA further confirmed that WT-TAMs were more associated with the UPR pathways (Fig. 7A). Emerging studies have implicated PERK signaling in tumor malignancy and have associated its activation with enhanced infiltration of M2-like macrophages37. Moreover, CREB3L1 has been identified as a key downstream mediator of PERK-driven metastasis in breast cancer38. Together with our data, these findings suggest that CCR7 regulates CREB3L1 expression in TAMs via the PERK signaling pathway.

We initially treated WT-TAMs with the PERK pathway inhibitor GSK2656157, and observed a significant downregulation in the expression levels of CREB3L1 and SPP1 (Fig. 7B). Additionally, western blot analysis revealed that the deletion of CCR7 significantly inhibited the phosphorylation levels of PERK and EIF2α proteins in TAMs, while the total protein expression remained unchanged (Fig. 7C). Collectively, these data suggest that CCR7 expression in TAMs contributed to the upregulation of CREB3L1 through the PERK signaling pathway.

CCL19 and CCL21 are the only known ligands of CCR7. To examine whether activation of the CCR7–PERK–CREB3L1–SPP1 signaling axis in TAMs is driven by CCL19/21 within the OSCC tumor microenvironment, WT-TAMs and KO-TAMs were stimulated with recombinant CCL19 and CCL21 (100 ng/mL), with PBS-treated cells serving as controls. Western blot analysis revealed that CCL19/21 stimulation markedly increased PERK and EIF2α phosphorylation in WT-TAMs compared with PBS-treated WT controls (Fig. 7D). This was accompanied by increased CREB3L1 and SPP1 expression, indicative of downstream pathway activation (Fig. 7D). Although CCL19/21 treatment produced modest increases in KO-TAMs, the extent of pathway activation was significantly lower than that observed in WT-TAMs. Collectively, these results indicate that CCR7-dependent signaling in TAMs is responsive to CCL19/21 stimulation, implying that ligand availability in the tumor microenvironment may contribute to activation of this pathway.

In vivo validation of the CCR7–PERK–CREB3L1–SPP1 signaling axis

To investigate whether CCR7-CREB3L1 axis in TAMs could impact the development of OSCC in vivo, we utilized the immunodeficient nude mice as an in vivo model. Specifically, we conducted adoptive transfers of WT-TAMs with shRNA-induced CREB3L1 knockdown (WT-TAMs+sh-CREB3L1; Supplementary Fig. 7A) or nonsense-treated controls (WT-TAMs+sh-CREB3L1#NC, KO-TAMs+sh-CREB3L1#NC), co-injected with MOC2 cells at a 1:1 ratio into the nude mice (Fig. 8A). Encouragingly, we observed that nude mice in the KO-TAMs+sh-CREB3L1#NC and WT-TAMs+sh-CREB3L1 groups showed an apparent reduction in the volume of OSCC tumors (Fig. 8B, C), when compared to WT-TAMs+sh-CREB3L1#NC group. We further performed multiplex immunofluorescence to detect the expression of CREB3L1 and SPP1 in tumor tissues from different groups. The data presented in this study showed that no significance difference in the number of infiltrated macrophages among the three groups, but the proportion of cells with low expression of CREB3L1 and SPP1 was higher in the KO-TAMs+sh-CREB3L1#NC and WT-TAMs+sh-CREB3L1 groups compared to the WT-TAMs+sh-CREB3L1#NC group (Fig. 8D). Additionally, IHC analysis of tumor tissues revealed a marked reduction in the percentage of SPP1-positive cells in the KO-TAMs+shCREB3L1#NC and WT-TAMs+shCREB3L1#NC groups compared to the WT-TAMs+shCREB3L1 control group (Supplementary Fig. 7B). Furthermore, to evaluate the role of the CCR7–CREB3L1–SPP1 axis within a fully immunocompetent tumor microenvironment, we conducted parallel in vivo experiments in C57BL/6 mice. Consistent with the findings in nude mice, disruption of CCR7-CREB3L1 in TAMs significantly attenuated OSCC tumor growth (Supplementary Fig. 7C–E). This growth inhibition was accompanied by a corresponding reduction in CREB3L1 and SPP1 expression in tumor tissues (Supplementary Fig. 7F, G). Taken together, these findings imply that disrupting the CCR7-CREB3L1 axis reduces SPP1 expression and inhibits OSCC progression in vivo,

Building on the findings in the mouse model, we further investigated the clinical relevance of the CCR7–PERK–CREB3L1–SPP1 signaling axis in human OSCC tissues. Multiplex immunofluorescence was performed on human OSCC specimens, revealing that CCR7 is localized to a substantial proportion of CD68+ macrophages. Notably, higher CCR7 expression in CD68+ macrophages was associated with elevated levels of pPERK and SPP1, whereas lower CCR7 expression corresponded with reduced levels of pPERK and SPP1 (Fig. 8E). These findings are consistent with our previous results, further supporting the involvement of the CCR7–PERK–CREB3L1–SPP1 signaling axis in OSCC progression (Fig. 8F).

Bioinformatics-based screening for potential therapeutic agents suitable for tumors with high CCR7 expression

A recent report detailing the crystal structure of CCR7 opens new avenues for targeting this axis in the development of novel cancer therapies39. Further, we utilized cMap web tool (https://clue.io/) to screen for anti-CCR7 chemical compounds. We identified Calyculin, Bisbenzimide, Hydrocortisone, THM-I-94, Vindesine, Halcinonide, Topotecan, Fluticasone, JNJ-7706621 and PP-30 as Top10 potential inhibitors of CCR7-mediated pro-oncogenic effects (Supplementary Table S2). To investigate protein-compound interactions, we performed docking simulations of CCR7 with compounds using Discovery Studio software (version 4.5). Among the candidates, Calyculin, THM-I-94 and Halcinonide failed to dock with CCR7, whereas Bisbenzimide, Hydrocortisone, Vindesine, Topotecan, Fluticasone, JNJ-7706621 and PP-30 successfully docked. Detailed 3D structures of the docking pockets and a 2D graph illustrating interaction forces and distances are showcased in Supplementary Fig. 8A–G. These compounds engage with CCR7 residues through various types of interactions, suggesting their potential as CCR7-targeted drugs. In summary, Bisbenzimide, Hydrocortisone, Vindesine, Topotecan, Fluticasone, JNJ-7706621, and PP-30 emerge as promising alternatives for OSCC therapy.

Discussion

To date, OSCC represents a poor-prognosis malignancy, with the prevalence of therapeutic resistance, and new therapeutic strategies for OSCC are urgently needed40,41. Thus, identifying effective therapeutic targets remains a major challenge in the treatment of OSCC. Our previous work highlighted that CCR7 dysregulation plays a pivotal role in regulating tumor growth and metastasis in OSCC31. However, the role of CCR7 in TAMs in promoting OSCC remains poorly understood. In this study, we observed that CCR7 deficiency in TAMs represses the malignant phenotype of OSCC cells. We further investigated the potential mechanism by which CCR7 in TAMs contributes to the progression of OSCC, thereby filling the existing gap in knowledge. By directly stimulating with recombinant OPN and depleting antibodies from macrophage-conditioned medium, OPN was identified as the key mediator through which CCR7+TAMs contribute to the progression of OSCC. Furthermore, our findings herein reveal a novel mechanism in which CCR7 signaling in TAMs amplifies the expression of CREB3L1 through the PERK pathway, subsequently enhancing the transcription of TAMs-derived SPP1, which promotes the progression of OSCC. Together, we found that CCR7 regulates CREB3L1 expression via PERK, which subsequently promotes SPP1 transcription, highlighting the CCR7-CREB3L1 axis as a potential target for OSCC.

Inflammation plays a multifaceted role in tumorigenesis and tumor progression42, with evidence suggesting that it facilitates the transformation to malignancy43,44. Additionally, inflammation is a key component of the tumor microenvironment, driving multiple hallmarks of cancer and influencing various aspects of tumor biology44. Given the pivotal role of CCR7 in mediating inflammatory and immune responses, targeting CCR7 presents a promising therapeutic strategy for cancer treatment39. Our prior research has demonstrated that CCR7 significantly modulates the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages, T-cell differentiation, and the function of cancer-associated fibroblasts in OSCC, thereby playing a crucial role in shaping the inflammatory microenvironment31,45,46. Notably, macrophages are crucial drivers of tumor-promoting inflammation12,47. Studies have demonstrated that tumor-associated macrophages are instrumental in driving tumor growth and invasiveness, orchestrating intricate interactions with multiple components of the tumor microenvironment to promote immune evasion48,49. This has highlighted TAMs as pivotal candidates for targeted therapeutic approaches. In this study, we utilized an in vitro co-culture system of macrophages and tumor cells, demonstrating that CCR7 deficiency in TAMs significantly suppressed the malignant phenotypes of OSCC cells. Thus, inhibiting CCR7 in TAMs to prevent OSCC progression may represent a promising strategy. Currently, this work provides further insights into the mechanism by which CCR7 in TAMs contributes to OSCC progression.

Given that macrophages predominantly exert their biological functions through the secretion of cytokines, alterations in macrophage-derived signaling may critically influence the tumor microenvironment. In the current study, cell-cell interaction analysis revealed a significant reduction in SPP1 signaling in TAMs derived from CCR7-deficient mice SPP1, also known as OPN, serves as a key adhesion protein and plays a pivotal role in various types of tumors14. Accumulating evidence suggests that OPN is aberrantly expressed in malignant cells and contributes to aggressive tumor behaviors35. Additionally, OPN is expressed in multiple cell types within the tumor microenvironment, including macrophages36. Extensive SPP1-mediated interactions between TAMs and tumor cells further highlight the critical role of this signaling axis in tumor progression50–52. Consistently, our results revealed that CCR7-mediated regulation of TAM-derived OPN also contributes to the progression and EMT process of OSCC cells. Emerging evidence suggests that SPP1+ macrophages (SPP1+Macs) represent a prevalent TAM subset across multiple cancer types14,19,36,53. Recent studies have identified SPP1+ Macs as either Angio-TAMs or Inflam-TAMs, both of which are associated with adverse prognostic outcomes54,55. Notably, a recent study in HNSCC reported that CXCL9–SPP1 macrophage polarization marker, was inversely correlated with patient prognosis, and it was proposed as a potential biomarker for both protumorigenic and antitumorigenic signaling pathways within the TME14. Similarly, another study reported that SPP1+ Macs secreted cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, which facilitated the progression of HNSCC53. Interestingly, our previous work demonstrated that CCR7 promotes OSCC progression by facilitating macrophage polarization toward an M2-like phenotype30. In the present study, immunofluorescence co-staining of SPP1 with the traditional M2 marker MRC1 in OSCC tumor sections revealed significant spatial overlap between SPP1+Macs and MRC1+ Macs. Additionally, scRNA-seq analysis revealed that CCR7 deficiency markedly reduced the abundance of SPP1+Macs subset. These findings suggest that SPP1+ Macs identified here are closely related to the conventional M2 macrophage population and thereby provide supportive evidence for our prior findings. Similarly, a pan-cancer single-cell transcriptomic analysis by Cheng et al. demonstrated that SPP1+TAMs exhibited higher M2-associated gene signatures across multiple tumor types56. Furthermore, SPP1 exerts its regulatory effects on tumor growth, invasion, metastasis and angiogenesis by interacting with integrin receptors or CD44 on the tumor cell surface57–59. Our scRNA-seq data further showed that CCR7 deficiency in TAMs disrupted SPP1–integrin αvβ3 signaling between partially epithelial–mesenchymal transition cells and cell cycle-related tumor cells. These findings suggest that TAM-derived SPP1 may contribute to the malignant phenotype of tumor cells by interacting with integrins on their surface. However, further experimental validation is required to confirm this mechanism.

CREB3L1, a transcription factor belonging to the CREB3 family, is implicated in crucial biological processes, including metabolism, cell differentiation, and protein secretion60,61. Dysregulation of CREB3L1 expression has been observed in a variety of malignancies, with growing evidence supporting its involvement in the regulation of tumorigenesis38,60,62,63. Elevated CREB3L1 expression has been reported to indicate a higher risk of papillary thyroid carcinoma recurrence and may contribute to thyroid cancer dedifferentiation60,63. Additional studies have concluded that the EWSR1–CREB3L1 gene fusion drives the initiation and progression of sclerosing epithelioid fibrosarcoma64,65. In this study, we identified CREB3L1 as a key mediator of CCR7, regulating SPP1 expression in TAMs and driving macrophage-mediated tumor phenotypes. Consistent with our in vitro findings, the subcutaneous co-injection tumor model further demonstrated the contribution of the macrophage CREB3L1–SPP1 axis to tumor development and confirmed the tumor-suppressive effect of CCR7 depletion in macrophages. These findings suggest that the CREB3L1-SPP1 axis may serve as a potential therapeutic target for OSCC. However, the precise mechanism by which CREB3L1 exerts its effects in OSCC requires further investigation. Recently, Xu et al. demonstrated that CREB3L1 functions as a novel UPR sensor, promoting the progression of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma by upregulating COL3A1 and reprogramming the TME through M2 polarization of TAMs62. Interestingly, SPP1+ Macs also exhibit characteristics of M2 macrophages, and SPP1 can similarly remodel the extracellular matrix55. Notably, CREB3L1 primarily functions as a transcription factor, orchestrating the expression of downstream target genes at the transcriptional level to regulate their biological functions66. Murakami et al. demonstrated that CREB3L1-deficient mice developed severe osteopenia due to decreased collagen I, which was associated with the transcriptional activation of COL1A1 by CREB3L167. In our study, both scRNA-seq data and in vitro experiments indicated that CREB3L1 regulates the expression of SPP1. We further characterized and validated its potential regulatory binding sites, although the contribution of other transcription factors remains to be elucidated.

Unfolded protein response sensors are crucial in carcinogenesis, tumor microenvironment remodeling, and the facilitation of tumor progression37,68. As a major downstream branch of the UPR, PERK signaling has been implicated in multiple malignant processes, including tumor cell invasion and metastasis38,69. Previous studies have shown that PERK activation fosters an immunosuppressive M2 macrophage phenotype, with the loss of PERK not only inhibiting this phenotype but also restoring antitumor immunity37. In our prior research, we observed that CCR7 in TAMs promotes M2 macrophage polarization through the inhibition of DUSP131. Moreover, PERK is essential for the metastatic dissemination of cancer cells that have undergone an epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition70,71. In this study, we found that CCR7 promotes the epithelial-mesenchymal transition process in OSCC cells by regulating the expression of SPP1 derived from TAMs. Additionally, previous studies have shown that CREB3L1 functions as a downstream effector of PERK, modulating the expression of collagen I and FN1 to facilitate invasion and metastasis in breast cancer38. In the present study, we similarly observed that the expression of CREB3L1 in TAMs is regulated through the PERK signaling pathway.

CC-chemokine ligand 19 (CCL19) and CCL21 are the only known ligands for CCR7 and play crucial roles in regulating immune cell chemotaxis, homeostasis, and tolerance mechanisms39. Several studies have highlighted a significant association between CCL19/21 and cancer progression. For instance, Chen et al. demonstrated that CCL21 secreted by tumor-associated lymphatics interacts with CCR7 on OSCC cells, inducing EMT and promoting tumor cell stemness via the JAK2/STAT3 signaling pathway72. Similarly, Zhao et al. showed that disruption of CCL21–CCR7 paracrine signaling between lymphatic endothelial-like cells and glioblastoma stem cells inhibited glioblastoma proliferation and growth73. Furthermore, Geraldo et al. emphasized the importance of CCL21/CCR7 signaling in TAMs for driving their tumor-supportive polarization within the glioblastoma TME22. Previous studies have also emphasized the critical roles of CCL19 and CCL21 in recruiting TAMs. Specifically, Ato et al. demonstrated that CCL19 and CCL21 stimulate macrophage migration in vitro74. Moreover, our prior work showed that recombinant CCL19 and CCL21 significantly reprogrammed M0 macrophages to an M2 phenotype in vitro30. Building on this, the present study explores whether the activation of the CCR7–PERK–CREB3L1 signaling axis in TAMs is driven by CCL19/21 within the OSCC TME. After treatment with recombinant CCL19/21 proteins, we observed a significant upregulation of pPERK, CREB3L1, and SPP1 expression, indicating that the CCR7–PERK–CREB3L1 pathway is indeed responsive to CCL19/21 signaling in TAMs. These findings imply that the CCL19/21 levels in the TME may activate this signaling axis, thereby promoting OSCC progression through TAMs. Collectively, these results provide new insights into the role of CCL19/21-CCR7 signaling in the TME and its influence on the immune-modulatory functions of TAMs in OSCC.

CCR7 has been increasingly implicated in various cancers, suggesting that small molecule ligands designed to inhibit CCR7 may offer significant potential for suppressing tumor progression. Recently, Kathrin et al. presented the 2.1 Å resolution crystal structure of human CCR7 fused with sialidase NanA, which provides a theoretical foundation for the development of small molecule compounds targeting CCR739. We screened potential therapeutics that can regulate CCR7 to influence the pathophysiology of OSCC. Using molecular docking, we identified Bisbenzimide, Hydrocortisone, Vindesine, Topotecan, Fluticasone, JNJ-7706621 and PP-30. Bisbenzimides, a class of fluorescent dyes, bind preferentially within the minor groove of double-stranded DNA, particularly to AT-rich regions75. Previous studies have demonstrated that Bisbenzimides induce proapoptotic and cytostatic effects in cancer cells76,77. Similarly, other compounds have been shown to inhibit tumor progression through various mechanisms78–82. However, these findings from molecular docking and network analysis require further validation through in vivo and in vitro experimentation.

In this study, we present a novel pathway by which CCR7 in TAMs regulates the progression of OSCC, providing a rationale for manipulating macrophages to enhance antitumor responses through targeting macrophage CCR7. However, several potential limitations must be considered when interpreting our findings. First, our study primarily focuses on the role of CCR7 in TAMs. Nevertheless, the use of a global CCR7 knockout model does not allow the complete exclusion of contributions from other cellular compartments. Although epithelial-derived confounding effects were minimized through the use of a subcutaneous transplantation model, a potential impact of CCR7 deficiency in host epithelial cells on the observed phenotypes cannot be entirely ruled out. Therefore, the cell type-specific roles of CCR7 in OSCC remain to be further defined. Beyond macrophages, CCR7 also plays important roles in multiple immune cell populations. To date, most studies examining the biological relationship between CCR7 and T cells have concentrated on its role in regulating T-cell homing, localization within secondary lymphoid organs, and egress from these sites. In contrast, the impact of CCR7 signaling in T cells on antitumor immunity within the tumor microenvironment remains poorly understood. In support of a clinically relevant role, Xu et al. reported that the CCL19/CCR7 axis drives regulatory T-cell migration and is associated with poor prognosis in gastric cancer83. Moreover, our prior work demonstrated that CCR7 modulates the tumor microenvironment by regulating the activation of naive CD8 + T cells, thereby promoting OSCC proliferation. In parallel, tumor-infiltrating B cells constitute an important component of antitumor immune regulation. Consistently, Zhang et al. identified a B cell-specific gene signature that includes CCR7 (ZNF439, KMO, KDM5D, IFT57, HDAC9, GSAP, and CCR7) with prognostic value in head and neck cancer84. Together, these observations highlight that CCR7 may influence OSCC biology through multiple immune lineages beyond TAMs. Second, although human OSCC tissue samples were included in our study, the limited availability of sufficient human samples restricts the ability to fully validate the clinical relevance of our findings. This will be addressed in our future studies, which will continue to be accomplished in our further studies. Third, due to experimental constraints, only six samples were used for single-cell RNA sequencing analysis. While this reduced the number of animals used, it also introduces a potential risk of bias.

In conclusion, our current findings reveal an important mechanism that discloses the role of TAMs on OSCC cells through the CCR7-PERK-CREB3L1-SPP1 signaling axis in the TME. This mechanism is crucial for OSCC development, progression, and metastasis. Our study highlights that the CCR7-PERK-CREB3L1-SPP1 signaling in TAMs may be a potential therapeutic target against OSCC.

Methods

Human OSCC specimens

From December 2023 to January 2025, tumor specimens were collected from 48 patients with OSCC who underwent surgical resection at the Stomatology Hospital of China Medical University. Eligible patients met the following inclusion criteria: (1) OSCC diagnosis confirmed by histopathological examination; (2) no prior radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, or other antitumor treatments before surgery. Tumor tissues were obtained at the time of surgical resection and were subsequently processed for histological and multiplex immunofluorescence analyses. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Stomatology Hospital of China Medical University (Approval No. K2023029). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed. Human OSCC tissue sections were subjected to multiplex immunofluorescence staining to assess the expression and spatial distribution of CCR7, CD68, p-PERK, CREB3L1, and SPP1 within the tumor microenvironment.

Mouse studies

Six- to eight-week-old male C57BL/6 wild-type and Ccr7−/− (KO) mice were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences (Suzhou, China). All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and the Ethics Committee of China Medical University (Approval No. K2022028). We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use. Animal experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. The experimental unit was an individual mouse. All mice were housed in pathogen-free conditions at the animal experimentation center, with a 14 h light/10 h dark cycle, maintained at 22 °C and 60% humidity. The C57-CCR7 knockout (Ccr7−/−) mice were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 technique and validated through agarose gel electrophoresis and Western blotting (Figs.S1C and 5A, B). The CCR7 guide (g)RNA was designed to target exon 3 of CCR7, with the corresponding sequences listed in Supplementary Table S3. The primer sequences used for genotyping are provided in Supplementary Table S4. PCR amplicons were electrophoresed on a 1% agarose gel in TAE buffer. SolarRed Nucleic Acid Dye was incorporated into the agarose, and electrophoresis was carried out at 100 V for 45–60 minutes. The gels were visualized using the ChemiDoc Imaging System.

We opted for subcutaneous implantation of the mouse oral cancer cell line (MOC-2), as studies on murine orthotopic OSCC models are often constrained by the morbidity associated with oral tumors, which impairs the mouse’s ability to feed and creates a limited window for evaluating therapeutic outcomes. Moreover, prior research has demonstrated comparable immune infiltration kinetics in both subcutaneous and orthotopic immunocompetent oral cancer models85,86. For subcutaneous tumor formation experiments, 6–8 week-old BALB/c-nu mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 5 per group) using a computer-generated randomization sequence. The sample size was based on previous similar mouse tumor studies and preliminary experiments. To minimize potential confounders, all tumor measurements were performed using the same procedure across groups. A 200 μL mixture of TAM cells (5 × 10⁵) and MOC2 cells (5 × 10⁴) was injected into the flank of each mouse. Tumor size was measured every 2 days starting 4 days after injection, and the volume was calculated using the formula: volume = (length × width²)/2. The maximum tumor volume permitted under the approved animal protocol was 1.5 cm³, and this limit was not exceeded in any experiment. Mice were euthanized when tumor volumes approached the approved endpoint, and tumor tissues were collected for subsequent analyses, including immunofluorescence (IF) and immunohistochemistry (IHC). No animals or data points were excluded from the analysis. Investigators who performed tumor measurements and data quantification were blinded to group allocation. Group information was revealed only after outcome assessment and data analysis were completed.

Cell lines and primary cell culture

C57BL/6-derived MOC-2 cells (RRID: CVCL_ZD33) were purchased from Kerafast (USA). Human leukemia monocytic cell line (THP-1) (RRID: CVCL_0006) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). MOC2 cells were cultured in a 2:1 mixture of HyClone Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium/HyClone Ham’s Nutrient Mixture F12 (IMDM/F12). THP-1 cells were maintained in Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium with 10% FBS, 1% PS, and 0.05 mM β-mercaptoethanol. All cells were incubated in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 and routinely tested for Mycoplasma Detection Kit (LT07-318, Lonza).

Ccr7+/+ and Ccr7−/− mice were sacrificed, and bone marrow cells were isolated by flushing femoral marrow cavities with DMEM, followed by filtration and centrifugation. The cells were treated with red blood cell lysis solution, cultured in DMEM with FBS, penicillin-streptomycin, and 20 ng/mL recombinant MCSF (315-02-1MG, PeproTech) for 6 days. Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were identified by flow cytometry after staining with anti-CD11b-FITC and anti-F4/80-APC antibodies. The cells were then analyzed using a BD FACSCanto™ II (BD, USA) flow cytometer and FlowJo v10 software.

Recombinant mouse osteopontin (OPN) treatment in MOC2 cells

For stimulation with recombinant mouse OPN (rmOPN, 499260, Sigma, St. Louis), MOC2 cells were plated in six-well plates and cultured to confluence. The cells were then treated overnight in serum-free IMDM-F12 medium supplemented with 1% BSA. The cells were either stimulated with rmOPN or left untreated for 2 days.

Conditional medium (CM) preparation

Cells were plated at a density optimized to reach around 70% confluence within 24 h. Afterward, they were washed twice with PBS and cultured for another 48 h in medium containing 2% low serum. The resulting supernatant was collected, clarified by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 10 minutes, and either used directly or preserved at −80 °C for later experiments.

Indirect co-culture assays

The indirect co-culture assays were conducted using a six-well Transwell insert with a 0.4 μm pore size. Briefly, BMDMs (1 × 10⁶) were seeded in the lower chamber, while MOC2 cells (5 × 10⁵) were seeded in the upper chamber. After 48 h of incubation, either BMDMs or MOC2 cells were harvested for subsequent assays.

Macrophage-conditioned medium treatment with neutralizing anti-osteopontin antibody

To neutralize OPN in macrophage-conditioned medium, a mouse-derived anti-osteopontin neutralizing antibody (AF808, R&D Systems) was applied at a final concentration of 2 μg/ml and incubated on ice for 1 hour. As a control, CM was treated under the same conditions using normal IgG.

Cell cycle assays

Cell cycle assays were conducted to investigate cellular processes associated with cell division. For the assay, MOC2 cells were stained with propidium iodide (PI) in the dark for 30 minutes. Flow cytometry analysis was subsequently performed to determine the distribution of cells across different phases of the cell cycle. The obtained data were analyzed using ModFit LT5 and FlowJo software.

Cell proliferation analysis

Tumor cells were pre-treated with recombinant OPN for 24 h. Following this, the cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1000 cells per well in triplicate. Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CK04, Dojindo) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

EdU assay

After treatment of MOC2 cells with anti-OPN or control treatment, the cells were plated into 24-well plates (2 × 10⁴ cells/well) and incubated for three days. Following the incubation period, the cells were exposed to EdU reagent for 2 h. The cells were then fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde and 0.5% Triton X-100. Nuclear staining was performed using Hoechst stain. The EdU incorporation rate was quantified using ImageJ software.

Transwell migration and invasion assay

Migration and invasion assays were conducted using 24-well Transwell plates equipped with 8-μm pore inserts. MOC2 cells (3 × 10⁵ per well) were resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 0.2% FBS and added to the upper chambers. The lower chambers were filled with BMDMs conditioned medium from C57BL/6 mice, either untreated or supplemented with recombinant mouse OPN protein. After a 24-hour incubation period, membranes were fixed using 100% methanol and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Cells that had migrated to the underside of the membrane were counted under a light microscope across five randomly selected fields per sample. Quantification was performed using ImageJ software. For invasion assays, 100 μL of Matrigel solution was pre-coated on the upper side of the insert and allowed to solidify prior to cell seeding. Cells were co-cultured under the same conditions for 48 h. Subsequent processing steps, including fixation, staining, and quantification, were carried out identically to the migration assay.

Measurement of OPN levels

Following the collection of cell culture supernatants, mouse osteopontin concentrations were determined using a mouse-specific enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (EK0483, Boster), in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol.

qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted using the RNA-Quick Purification Kit (ES Science) and reverse-transcribed using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (RR037A, Takara). mRNA expression was quantified using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II kits (DRR081A, Takara). The expression levels of the indicated genes were calculated using the ΔΔCt method. The primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Supplementary Table S5.

Western blotting

Cell lysates were prepared for total protein extraction, and concentrations were measured using the BCA assay. Samples containing 10–20 μg of protein were resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 2% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies including OPN (ab283656, Abcam, 1:1000), CCR7 (86180-5-RR, Proteintech, 1:2000), CREB3L1 (11235-2-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000), PERK (68482-1-Ig, Proteintech, 1:5000), psPERK (82534-1-RR, Proteintech, 1:5000), EIF2α (5324, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), psEIF2α (3398, Cell Signaling Technology, 1:1000), E-cadherin (20874-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:5000), N-cadherin (22018-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:10000), Vimentin (10366-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:10000), PDPN (33099-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:2000), LAMC2 (19698-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000), LAMB3 (ab150385, Abcam, 1:1000), and β-actin (AF7018, Affinity, 1:10000). Following incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies, protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays were performed in macrophages cells using the ChIP assay kit, following the manufacturer’s protocol. After antibody binding and pull-down, the target DNA fragment was amplified and analyzed by PCR. The sequences used for the SPP1 ChIP assay are as follows: Forward: 5’-AAGAGTCCTGTAAAGGGTCGTA-3’; Reverse: 5’-CCCGCATAAGCGTGTCA-3’.

Dual luciferase assay for promoter activity

The luciferase reporter assay was conducted according to the protocol provided in the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit from Promega. Briefly, HEK 293 T cells were co-transfected with various plasmids, including the CREB3L1 vector, wild-type (WT) SPP1 promoter vector, mutant 1 (MUT1) SPP1 promoter vector, mutant 2 (MUT2) SPP1 promoter vector, mutant 3 (MUT3) SPP1 promoter vector, and negative control plasmids. The SPP1 promoter activity was determined by measuring the ratio of luminescence from the firefly luciferase reporter to the Renilla luciferase control, using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System and a Synergy H1 Light Luminescence Reader.

Immunofluorescence

Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were first deparaffinized and rehydrated through a graded xylene and ethanol series, followed by heat-mediated antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer (P0081, Beyotime) using a microwave oven. For frozen samples, slides were brought to room temperature in PBS prior to staining. All tissue sections were blocked with 5% donkey serum and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The next day, fluorophore-labeled secondary antibodies were applied for 1 hour at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Fluorescence signals were captured using either an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope or an Olympus BX63 epifluorescence microscope.

Immune infiltration analysis

The correlation between SPP1 expression and immune cells in HNSCC was analyzed using TCGA data through the Assistant for Clinical Bioinformatics platform (https://www.aclbi.com/static/index.html#/). The TPM RNA-seq data for SPP1 in HNSCC was sourced from UCSC Xena (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/). The CIBERSORT algorithm was utilized to quantify the immune cell scores, and the correlation between SPP1 and macrophage markers was assessed using the Spearman correlation test.

Molecular docking

The crystal structures of CCR7 were obtained from the RCSB PDB protein data bank (http://www.rcsb.org/pdb/). The acquired protein crystals underwent processing using PyMOL (https://pymol.org/), involving various treatments such as protein pre-processing and removal of water molecules. The “query” tool of cMap (https://clue.io/) was utilized to screen for potential anti-CCR7 chemical compounds. After automatic preparation of CCR7 and ligand preparation for the compounds, the binding sites and all compound conformations were identified. Docking simulations were performed using Discovery Studio software (version 4.5). A lower free energy of binding between the ligand and the receptor indicated greater binding stability. The 3D view of the binding pocket and the 2D view of the intermolecular forces were displayed.

Single cell RNA-seq

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the GSEA software (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp).

Consensus non‑negative matrix factorization analysis

Gene expression profiles from tumor cells were deconvoluted using consensus non-negative matrix factorization (cNMF) to infer underlying transcriptional programs. Pairwise Pearson correlation coefficients between programs were computed to identify shared patterns, which were subsequently grouped into meta-programs. Manual curation followed by enrichment analysis of the top 50 most variable genes per program revealed four distinct meta-programs, each capturing a recurrent axis of transcriptional heterogeneity across samples.

Gene-regulatory networks

Gene regulatory network inference was performed using the pySCENIC package (v0.11.2), a Python-based implementation of the SCENIC pipeline. An in-house workflow integrating GRNBoost2 with default parameters was applied. The mouse mm10 genome served as the reference, and motif enrichment was assessed using the v9 non-redundant MGI motif annotation. Regulon specificity scores were calculated for all MGI-annotated transcription factors to identify cluster-enriched regulators.

Analysis of cell–cell communications

We utilized CellChat 1.1.0 (https://github.com/sqjin/CellChat) to infer direct interactions between immune cells and tumor cells, as well as between stromal cells and tumor cells, and to identify distinct intercellular interactions between wild-type and knockout groups.

Trajectory and gene regulatory network analysis

Cellular differentiation states of macrophages were inferred using CytoTRACE (v0.1.0), an R package designed to reconstruct trajectories from single-cell RNA-seq data based on gene counts and expression diversity. A random subsample of 1000 cells was analyzed following the standard protocol (https://cytotrace.stanford.edu/). The resulting differentiation scores were projected onto UMAP embeddings, with cells colored by inferred differentiation potential. To further delineate differentiation trajectories, Monocle 3 and CytoTRACE were jointly applied. Both algorithms reconstruct lineage progression under an unsupervised framework by ordering cells along a pseudotemporal axis, thereby capturing the continuum of macrophage maturation.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were conducted using R 4.2.0 and GraphPad Prism 9. Quantitative results are presented as mean ± SD. Three independent biological replicates were included for all cell experiments. Animal experiments were performed with five mice in each group, and all animals were included in the statistical analyses. Differences between two groups were assessed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test. Statistical comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA. Growth curves of tumor volume were analyzed using two-way ANOVA. The assumption of homogeneity of variance was assessed prior to parametric statistical analyses. When this assumption was violated, statistical approaches appropriate for unequal variances were applied. Statistical significance was defined as a p value of p < 0.05. The following significance levels were applied: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001.
