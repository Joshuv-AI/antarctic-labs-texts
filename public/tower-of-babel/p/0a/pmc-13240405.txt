Simulated Y‐Site Compatibility of Calcium Gluconate and Benzylpenicillin With Maintenance Fluid Used in Neonatal Intensive Care Unit
Authors: Ayman Allahham, Robert Pulbrook, Billy Lo, Stephen Halliwell, Nhan Le, Janice Wong, Viena Habbabe, Vivek Babu Nooney, Vincent Chan, Victoria Letts, Thilini Thrimawithana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMaintenance fluids for neonates, which are commonly composed of glucose 10%, sodium chloride (NaCl) 0.225% w/v with potassium chloride (KCl) 20 mmol/L (GSP), are administered as a continuous infusion, and prescribed medications are required to be co‐administered frequently through a Y‐site. There are limited studies on the compatibility of commonly used medicines with this maintenance fluid. Therefore, the objective of this study is to study the compatibility of commonly used medicines in the neonatal intensive care unit with maintenance fluid (GSP). An audit at the RCH Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) was performed to determine the average doses, durations of infusion, and volumes of maintenance fluid, benzylpenicillin, and calcium gluconate administered. The Y‐site chemical compatibility of calcium gluconate with the GSP was determined using a titration method. The Y‐site physical compatibility for both calcium gluconate and benzylpenicillin with the GSP was determined on the basis of visual appearance, turbidity, and pH. There was no clinically significant change in pH, turbidity, or concentration of the calcium gluconate at a 1:1, 1:2, 1:4, or 2:1 ratio with GSP with a contact time of up to 4 hours. Both physical and chemical analyses demonstrated the stability of calcium gluconate with GSP at a range of concentrations and time points, indicating Y‐site compatibility. Physical changes were detected in the samples containing benzylpenicillin and GSP following the longer interaction time, thus indicating potential Y‐site incompatibility. This has been confirmed by both the turbidity and the Z‐average tests.

Introduction

Medication administration in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) is complex as many neonates receive multiple medicines, often via the parenteral route. The number of medications used often exceeds the number of available intravenous (IV) lines due to the difficulties and risks associated with the insertion of additional lines in an acutely unwell neonate [1]. Therefore, Y‐site administration is frequently employed in a NICU to simultaneously administer multiple medicines via the same infusion line. However, this mode of medication administration can lead to interaction of medicines at the point of intersection known as the Y‐site [2]. These drug–drug or drug‐diluent interactions can lead to complications such as catheter occlusion, embolism, or therapeutic failure due to degradation of the medicine [2, 3, 4].

At the Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne (RCH), Australia, the standard maintenance fluid recommended for neonates over the age of 24 h is a solution of glucose (10%), sodium chloride (0.225%) and potassium chloride (20 mmol/L) [5]. There is currently a dearth of data on Y‐site compatibility of many commonly used medications when mixed with this glucose, sodium chloride and potassium chloride (GSP) fluid. An audit undertaken by the team identified benzylpenicillin and calcium gluconate as two of the frequently administered medicines at the RCH NICU. Benzylpenicillin is used to treat gram‐positive bacterial infection, and calcium gluconate is administered as a treatment for neonatal hypocalcaemia [6, 7]. However, there is no data on the Y‐site compatibility of these two medicines when mixed with GSP [7]. Therefore, current clinical practice includes the cessation of maintenance fluid infusion prior to the drug administration, followed by flushing of the line with NaCl 0.9%. The medication is then infused as per local guidelines and the IV line is flushed again with NaCl 0.9%, prior to recommencement of the maintenance fluid. This procedure can increase the risk of hypervolaemia and the risk of infections in the neonate [8]. In addition, this procedure is time consuming and adds to the workload of nursing staff. Thus, the aims of this study were to conduct a one‐year audit to select the possible volume(s) and concentrations of both drugs (calcium gluconate and benzylpenicillin) in Y‐site in accordance with the guidelines followed by the RCH and then to determine the Y‐site compatibility of calcium gluconate and benzylpenicillin when mixed with GSP at clinically relevant doses and infusion durations.

Methods

Audit of RCH Medications Administrations

The NICU at RCH in Melbourne, Australia, provides care for acutely unwell babies and infants from Victoria, interstate, and overseas. There are typically over 800 admissions each year for a broad range of complex medical and surgical problems. An audit of NICU drug administrations for the calendar year 2023 was performed in August 2024. The audit was used to select the possible volume(s) and concentrations of both drugs (calcium gluconate and benzylpenicillin) in Y‐site and in accordance with the guidelines followed by the RCH. Records that contain any of these two medicines (calcium gluconate and benzylpenicillin) were included in the audit. In total, there were 1502 individual administrations of benzylpenicillin and 169 calcium gluconate infusions. This informed the need for further studies of these agents with GSP due to the frequency with which maintenance fluid was interrupted for these therapeutic infusions.

The range for Benzylpenicillin dosing based on 1502 orders was 32.16–336 mg. The volumes varied depending on the concentration used. Two concentrations were used for benzylpenicillin: 60 or 90 mg/mL. Based on this, the volume would convert to 0.536–5.60 mL using a 60 mg/mL or 0.357–3.73 mL using a 90 mg/mL. For calcium gluconate, the dose was 0.31–1.41 mmol, which would convert to a volume of 1.312–5.967 mL. This range was based on 169 orders for Calcium Gluconate. The calcium gluconate was diluted to 0.11 mmol/mL before administration of the respective dose.

GSP Infusion Preparation

The standard maintenance fluid used in the NICU was a pre‐prepared solution of GSP, supplied in 500 mL bags by Baxter. Due to limited supply, the GSP solution was prepared in our labs. GSP (which is 10% w/v glucose with 20 mmol/L potassium chloride (KCl) and 0.225% w/v sodium chloride (NaCl)) was created by combining 20 g of D‐glucose (BDH Chemicals, analytical grade, Kilsyth VIC, Australia), 450 mg of NaCl (Chem‐Supply, analytical Gillman South, Australia), and 4 mL of 10 mmol/10 mL KCl (Pfizer) with Milli‐Q water (Milli‐Q EQUATION 7008 ultrapure water system, Molsheim, France) subjected to autoclaving (121°C, 122–125 kPa pressure, 20 min holding time, Atherton Cyber Series autoclave, Australia) to make a total volume of 200 mL under laboratory conditions. A magnetic stirrer (CS76083V, Industrial Equipment & Controls Pty Ltd., Australia) and Ultrasonic cleaner FXP8 (Unisonics, Australia) were employed for 20 min to achieve a homogeneous solution.

Calcium Gluconate Stability Evaluation Method Design

According to the Pediatric Injectable Guidelines [7], calcium gluconate (93.1 mg/mL, Phebra calcium gluconate 2.2 mmol/10 mL injection, supplied by RCH) is diluted to ≤ 0.11 mmol/mL with NaCl 0.9%, infused over 10–60 min; therefore, a concentration of 0.11 mmol/mL was selected for this study. Under the intensive care setting, the infusion could also be given undiluted over 2–5 min as a slow push [7]. The only Y‐site compatible IV fluids known were KCl 20 mmol/L and Plasma‐Lyte 148 [7].

The Y‐site co‐administration was done through simple mixing following standard practice in the literature [9]. The mixing volumes and ratio of infusion were determined based on the birth weights reported by Emmerson et al. [10], ranging from 0.5–4.5 kg. The minimum and maximum daily IV fluid volumes required were 60 and 120 mL/kg/day, respectively, according to The RCH Protocol Neonatal Intravenous Fluid Management [5]. Consequently, the minimum IV fluid volume for a 0.5 kg infant was calculated as 60 mL/kg/day × 0.5 kg = 30 mL/24 h (1.25 mL/h), while the maximum for a 4.5 kg infant was 120 mL/kg/day × 4.5 kg = 540 mL/24 h (22.5 mL/h). To ensure the total interaction rates between GSP and the drug were covered, infusion rates of 1, 10, and 24 mL/h (for birth weights of 0.5–4.5 kg) were considered. Table S1 shows the volumes of drug and GSP calculated according to the infusion rates and mixing ratios determined.

The prepared tested ratios of calcium gluconate in NaCl, 0.9% to GSP, are 1:1, 1:2, 1:4, and 2:1. The mid‐volumes (10 mL/h) were examined. According to the Pediatric Injectable Guidelines, calcium gluconate and benzylpenicillin are infused over a maximum of 60 and 30 min respectively [7], therefore, the 4 h duration is beyond the time frame of infusing these two products in standard clinical practice. To ensure reliability and reproducibility, three replicates of each calcium gluconate and GSP mixing ratio were prepared and analyzed at immediate 0‐min, 30‐min, 60‐min, 120‐min, and 240‐min intervals. Additionally, three replicates of the blank, where calcium gluconate was replaced by water for injection, were also prepared and tested at each time point.

Benzylpenicillin Stability Evaluation Method Design

According to the Pediatric Injectable Guidelines, benzylpenicillin (Seqirus BenPen Powder for Injection 600 mg) was diluted to 60 mg/mL or weaker with water for injection and infused over at least 15 min [7]. However, concentrations of up to 90 mg/mL have been used [7], therefore, a high concentration of 90 mg/mL was selected for this study. The only Y‐site compatible IV fluids known were KCl 20 mmol/L and for concentrations of < 48 mg/mL Plasma‐Lyte 148 [7].

The tested ratios of benzylpenicillin in water for injection to GSP were 1:1, 1:4 and 2:1, each prepared to a volume of 12 mL. To ensure reliability and reproducibility, three replicates of each mixture were prepared and analyzed at baseline (0‐min), 30‐min, 60‐min, 120‐min and 240‐min intervals. Additionally, three replicates of the blank were also prepared and tested at each time point. The change in pH, visual appearance and turbidity as well as mean droplet size measured by dynamic light scattering and reported as Z‐average diameter are standard methods to detect the physical stability of injectables as reported in the literature [11] and are explained in the following methods.

Samples pH Assessment

The pH of each mixture was measured to determine if any chemical reaction may be involved and to ensure that the pH remained physiologically compatible following the mixing of medication (calcium gluconate or Benzylpenicillin) with GSP. The pH of each solution was measured using a pH meter (SevenDirect SD23 with pH Sensor InLab Expert Pro‐ISM, Mettler‐Toledo Limited, USA) that was calibrated daily using standard buffer solutions at pH 4, 7, and 10. A total volume of 10 mL of the medication and GSP mixture was tested in triplicate to ensure reproducibility.

Visual Inspection and Turbidity Assessment

For each concentration ratio and time point of calcium gluconate or benzylpenicillin with GSP, physical compatibility was assessed using a visual inspection. This was performed with the naked eye against a dark background in the laboratory. The color, opacity, and presence of any visualized particulate matter were documented. Following the visual inspection, the solution turbidity was tested using a portable microprocessor turbidity meter (HI 93703 Portable Microprocessor Turbidity Meter; Hanna Instruments, Woonsocket, RI, USA). The reading in the device will be checked using both milli‐Q water and an AMCO‐AEPA‐1 @10 FTU calibration solution (Hanna Instruments, Woonsocket, RI, USA). A 10 mL sample of the test mixture was added to the proprietary borosilicate testing vial, with the turbidity reading taken in triplicate. A change in turbidity of greater than 0.5 formazin turbidity units (FTU) and any noted particulates, haziness, or color change would be flagged as physical incompatibility [9, 12].

Zetasizer Analysis of Samples

A zetasizer (Zetasizer Nano Series; Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK) was used to assess the zeta potential of the prepared GSP solution across different time points (from 0 min up to 4 h). This was to ensure that the GSP itself remained stable for the length of the experimental period. A refractive index of 1.347 and 1.348 was used for calcium gluconate‐GSP and benzylpenicillin‐GSP mixtures respectively in the zetasizer measurement. Particle sizing (n = 3) was also performed to rule out subvisual precipitation by measuring the Z‐average of different ratios of benzylpenicillin in water for injection to GSP (1:1, 1:4 and 1:4) at different time points (0, 30, 60, 120 and 240 min). Both zeta potential and particle size samples were measured three times, in which 9 readings were recorded for each measurement for particle size, and 100 runs were recorded for each measurement for zeta potential.

Calcium Gluconate Assay Method

A titration was conducted according to the USP monograph for calcium gluconate [13]. A volume of 2 mL aliquot of 3 M HCl (Merck, analytical grade, Baywater, Australia) was pipetted into a conical flask containing 5.22 mL of calcium gluconate injection (Phebra calcium gluconate 2.2 mmol/10 mL injection, supplied by RCH and stored at room temperature) using a 5 mL micropipette. Milli‐Q water (with resistivity 18.2 MΩ cm at 25°C, obtained using a Milli‐Q EQUATION 7008 ultrapure water system, Molsheim, France) was then added to reach a total volume of 150 mL. While stirring with a magnetic stirrer, 20 mL of the titrant (disodium edetate, Analytical grade, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) was introduced from the titration burette. A volume of 15 mL of 1 M NaOH (prepared from NaOH pellets, Analytical grade, ChemSupply, Australia) and a weight of 300 mg of hydroxy naphthol blue disodium salt (Analytical grade, Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) were added to the same flask, and the titration continued until a blue endpoint was reached. A blank titration was also conducted for comparison.

Following the USP method [10], the percentage of the labeled amount of total calcium in the sample taken was calculated using the formula: Result=VS−VB×M×FW×100 where VS= Titrant volume (mL) consumed by the Sample, VB= Titrant volume (mL) consumed by the Blank, M= Titrant molarity (mmol/mL), F= equivalency factor (40.08 mg/mmol). W= weight of calcium in the Sample (mg). The acceptance range was 95.0%–105.0%.

Statistical Analysis of Results

Standard deviation (SD) and average were calculated for all results. Statistical analysis was performed to compare means of turbidity and assay at different conditions using SPSS and one‐way ANOVA to compare the mean values of all results using the post Hoc Tukey test (α=0.05).

Results

All pH values recorded at each time point and concentration fell within a range of 6.39 (±0.27) to 6.67 (±0.05), thus indicating calcium gluconate‐GSP sample stability. When comparing the pH over time, the results did not indicate a clear trend of reduction or increase over time; reporting only a drop of about 0.1 in pH value (for the ratio of 1:1) and a slight rise of 0.1 in pH value (for ratios 1:2, 1:4 and 2:1) which was not significant (p > 0.05) confirming a stable pH with the interaction between calcium gluconate and GSP in all ratios and across different time points.

Visual Inspection and Turbidity Assessment for Drug‐GSP Samples

The turbidity meter was checked with both Milli‐Q water and an AMCO‐AEPA‐1 @10 FTU calibration solution resulting in average FTU readings of 0.42 (±0.06) and 9.78 (±0.07), respectively, indicating valid measurements.

The visual inspection of calcium gluconate‐GSP solutions at every ratio and time point was unremarkable and reported with the same result “clear solution, no particles”. The turbidity was tested for all ratios and time points, with results ranging from 0.10 (±0.02) to 0.59 (±0.05), with no mean difference found to be greater than 0.5 FTU (Table 3). Statistical analysis with one‐way ANOVA was performed for all ratios and time points, indicating a statistically significant difference between different time points in the 1:2 ratio samples (p = 0.001) and the 2:1 ratio reading at 0‐min and 240‐min (p = 0.031). However, the turbidity reading recorded did not indicate a change in FTU ≥ 0.5. Given the disparity between the reading at 0 min in the 1:2 ratio and the other samples, it is a probable error. There was no change in the turbidity of greater than 0.5 FTU consistent with guidelines, and no visualized particulate matter or change in color or opacity of any sample at any concentration or time point; therefore indicating the stability of the combinations.

Zetasizer Analysis for Drug‐GSP Samples

To test the apparent physical instability for benzylpenicillin‐GSP solutions, these solutions were tested using a zetasizer. The GSP zeta potential for the prepared GSP was tested and ranged from −14.35 mV (±1.07 mV) to −15.97 mV (±2.21 mV), indicating no significant difference in the means for the zeta potential over time (p = 0.145). The laboratory‐produced GSP was considered to remain stable and therefore it can be considered equivalent to the Baxter GSP infusion bags that are used in the NICU. The Z‐average for the individual components was measured and was found to be 401.68 nm (±59.62 nm) and 562.38 nm (±92.87 nm) for benzylpenicillin and GSP respectively.

The Z‐average of the benzylpenicillin‐GSP solutions for all ratios and at different time points was measured and is shown in Figure 1. The measurement of Z‐average gave a polydispersity range of 0.45–0.91 with the mean count rate ranging from 159.8 to 353.4 indicating valid measurements. The results for the 1:1 ratio increased from 35.95 nm (±14.88 nm) at time zero to 91.26 nm (±88.34 nm) at time 240 min indicating a significant increase in the particle size (~60% increase in particle size) (p = 0.006). A similar trend was observed for the 1:4 ratio where the Z‐average increased from 14.88 nm (±2.69 nm) at time zero to 88.34 nm (±59.93 nm) at time 240 min which is an 83% increase in particle size (p = 0.008). The most dramatic change in the Z‐average was seen in the 2:1 ratio of benzylpenicillin‐GSP solution where the particle size increased to 341.48 nm (±93.91 nm) at 240 min and was significantly larger than the average at all other points (~95% increase in size) (p < 0.001). Despite the large variation in the Z‐value readings, these results demonstrate possible agglomeration of particles detected in the solution at these ratios due to possible interaction between benzylpenicillin and GSP. The results of the zetasizer confirm the possible physical instability shown in the turbidity.

Discussion

This study evaluated the stability of calcium gluconate and benzylpenicillin when combined with the routine maintenance fluid GSP in the context of neonatal intensive care setting to determine the Y‐site compatibility. The findings suggest a significant contrast in compatibility between these drugs and GSP, with calcium gluconate demonstrating stability across all tested concentration ratios and time points, whereas benzylpenicillin exhibited signs of instability within 30 min.

The results establish the compatibility of calcium gluconate with GSP across all tested concentration ratios (1:1, 1:2, 1:4, 2:1) and time points up to 240 min. pH levels remained stable, reducing the likelihood of losing efficacy and minimizing the chance of irritation. A stable pH, along with visual inspection results, indicated no particulate formation and turbidity values. There was no more than 0.5 FTU change with regard to turbidity between different dilutions and time points spanning 240 min, which indicates stability.

Calcium gluconate, an organic salt, has become more widely used in clinical practice due to its reduced risk of incompatibility with parenteral nutrition products and lesser likelihood of venous irritation [15]. Osmolarity is a key consideration when administering solutions through the intravenous route (IV) [16]. The stability of calcium gluconate concentrations observed in this study suggests that its interaction with GSP is unlikely to alter osmolarity beyond expected levels of the combination, thereby minimizing the risk of additional discomfort or adverse effects in neonates associated with a lack of stability. This is supported by the lack of significant changes in pH, turbidity, or concentration, further validating the physical and chemical compatibility of calcium gluconate with GSP. These findings provide evidence to support the co‐administration of calcium gluconate and GSP via a Y‐site in NICU settings.

In contrast, benzylpenicillin exhibited significant instability when combined with GSP and stored at room temperature (20°C–25°C) for 4 h. Turbidity measurements showed a time‐dependent increase, rising from 0 FTU at 0 min to 1.32 FTU at 240 min for the benzylpenicillin to GSP 2:1 mixture. The particle size analysis experiments suggest precipitation in all mixing ratios at longer durations, and the precipitation was considerably greater with higher proportions of benzylpenicillin (mixing ratio of 2:1).

Benzylpenicillin is a weak acid with a pKa of 2.65–2.70 and it is insoluble in water [17]. Benzylpenicillin solutions are mostly stable in the pH range 6–7 and optimally at pH 6.8 [18, 19]. Therefore, it is likely that the lower pH of the benzylpenicillin and GSP mixture (pH 5.41–5.82) has led to precipitation of benzylpenicillin in a time and concentration‐dependent manner. The pH of the benzylpenicillin solution was 5.99 (SD 0.06), and the observed reduction in pH for all mixing ratios is likely due to degradation of benzylpenicillin at room temperature. Previous studies have identified the two major degradation products of benzylpenicillin to be penicilloic acid and penillic acid, which have two carboxyl groups [20]. These additional carboxyl groups, which increase the concentration of H+ ions, are likely to have resulted in the observed reduction in pH over the study duration.

Hossain et al. [21] reported that penicillin G sodium diluted in 5% dextrose solution and stored at 5°C was chemically stable for up to 21 days. However, the authors reported a gradual reduction in drug concentration over time from 100% to ~95% by day 21 and a reduction in pH of the solution from 6.70 to 6.06 for 50,000 units/mL solution stored in PVC bags, indicating hydrolysis of the drug in solution [22]. It is likely that samples tested in this study also underwent hydrolysis at a higher rate due to storage at room temperature (20°C–25°C) for 4 h. In this study, additional testing on the chemical stability of benzylpenicillin was not undertaken due to changes observed in particle size measurements; therefore, physical instability was demonstrated. The variations in the room temperature range used in the study (20°C–25°C) from those in the clinical setting were not reported in this study. These small variations may have a small effect on the compatibility of the selected drugs with the maintenance fluid.

The strength of this study lies in its realistic experimental design assessing real‐world clinical practice, informed by an audit conducted at the Royal Children's Hospital (RCH) Melbourne. By analyzing common dosing practices for calcium gluconate, benzylpenicillin, and GSP, the experimental design simulated clinically relevant ratios and interaction volumes, enhancing the applicability and generalizability of its findings. As RCH Melbourne is the largest tertiary pediatric hospital in Victoria, the results are likely representative of broader practice in neonatal populations in Australia and other countries.

The study also used validated analytical methods, including pH measurement, turbidity assessment, visual inspection, and chemical assay (as per USP), to evaluate drug stability. This study has limitations that should be acknowledged. Testing was conducted in vitro without using Y‐sites or a syringe driver, which may limit the accuracy of the results in replicating NICU conditions. Clinical parameters such as pressure, flow rate, and the composition of the Y‐site materials were not replicated, and in vitro conditions do not always account for in vivo interactions, where variables such as blood pH and protein binding could affect drug stability. However, in vivo testing raises ethical concerns; therefore, simulating a Y‐site in vitro is more appropriate. Furthermore, the GSP solution, prepared from raw materials rather than clinical formulations, and the substitution of autoclaved Milli‐Q water for injection water could contribute to subtle deviations from clinical conditions. However, this may be insignificant as the GSP has been proven to be stable through zetasizer testing. Although no clear degradation trend was observed, sample degradation may have occurred if not promptly analyzed or properly stored [23]. Finally, while turbidity measurements and visual inspections were employed to assess physical stability, they lacked the sensitivity to detect sub‐visible particles [24]. Advanced particle analysis techniques, such as dynamic light scattering, could provide more detailed insights into particulate formation and enhance the robustness of these findings [25].

Clinical Implications and Future Directions

The compatibility of calcium gluconate with GSP has significant implications for NICU practices. Incompatibility between medicines and IV fluids can increase the risk of particulate matter occluding the line or increase the risk of emboli in the neonate. By enabling concurrent Y‐site administration, the findings reduce the need for interrupting maintenance fluid infusions to administer calcium gluconate, improving both patient safety and clinical efficiency. Conversely, the demonstrated instability of benzylpenicillin with GSP reinforces the need for separation and especially for interaction time above 10 min. These findings provide guidance for clinicians, supporting evidence‐based decisions to enhance the safety and efficacy of drug administration in neonates. Additionally, testing under simulated clinical conditions, including the use of Y‐site connectors and varying infusion rates, would enhance the applicability of these results to real‐world practice.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides robust evidence for the compatibility of calcium gluconate with GSP, demonstrating its physical and chemical stability when combined. These findings support its concurrent administration via a Y‐site, offering significant benefits for patient safety and clinical efficiency in NICU settings. Conversely, the demonstrated instability of benzylpenicillin with GSP highlights the importance of separate administration to prevent adverse outcomes.
