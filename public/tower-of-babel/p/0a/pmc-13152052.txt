Performance evaluation of Stago sthemO 301 hemostasis analyzer for routine testing, coagulation factors, and inhibitors
Authors: Marjorie A Goujon, Nicole Pesnel, Joffrey Feriel, Véronique Le Cam Duchez, Frank Mentz, Françoise Le Boulaire, Romain Duriez, Bas De Laat, Emmanuel de Maistre, Aurélien Lebreton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe sthemO 301 is a new hemostasis analyzer developed by Diagnostica Stago, combining simultaneously three different methodologies (i.e. clotting, chromogenic and immunological assays) on a single platform. The objective of this evaluation was to assess the analytical performances of the sthemO 301 and to compare it with the STA R Max on a selection of 20 hemostasis parameters (PT, aPTT with two reagents, Clauss fibrinogen, thrombin time, D-dimer, anti-Xa, factors II, V, VII, X, VIII, IX and XI, antithrombin, protein C and protein S). Within-run and inter-laboratory precisions have been evaluated using quality controls. Method comparisons were performed using a minimum of 100 plasma samples, collected across multiple sites, and compared to the STA R Max analyzer. Reference intervals were determined or verified based on published data. Within-run and inter-laboratory precisions were respectively below 5.2 and 7.6% whatever the parameter and the sample used. Pearson correlation coefficient was above 0.976 for all assays and slopes ranged from 0.94 to 1.07 for all but thrombin time, which was at 0.86. Bias at levels of interest were less than 6% for most routine coagulation parameters, coagulation factors and inhibitors. We reported the precision, accuracy and reference intervals of 20 parameters on the sthemO 301, half of these being reported for the first time, which include anti-Xa, factors II, V, VII, X, VIII, IX, XI, protein S free antigen and activity. The sthemO 301 analyzer offers satisfactory analytical performance and a good comparability with the STA R Max for all parameters.Keywords: coagulation analyzer, method comparison, precision, reference intervals, repeatability, reproducibility

Introduction

Hemostasis is a complex, dynamic system, making its laboratory assessment challenging [1]. Advances in automation, digital technologies, and novel biomarkers have expanded testing capacity and reliability. These innovations are transforming hemostasis diagnostics and improving care for bleeding and thrombotic disorders [2–4]. Hemostasis instrumentation has evolved from semi-automated systems to fully integrated platforms. Modern analyzers combine technologies like mechanical or optical clot detection, latex immunoassays, chromogenic assays, and chemiluminescence within one system, allowing better analytical performances and improving the throughput up to hundreds of tests per hour in some cases [5,6].

The sthemO family (Diagnostica Stago, Asnières-sur-Seine, France) are fully automated hemostasis analyzers for qualitative and quantitative coagulation testing by clotting (chronometric), chromogenic, or immunological methods in citrated plasma. Paired with dedicated reagents, they ensure enhanced traceability via unique reagent vial identification and optimized performance through tailored pipetting and mixing protocols. Workflow improvements – such as streamlined handling of samples, disposables, and reagent management, along with an optimized measuring cell and higher reagent capacity – boost throughput and reduce turnaround and hands-on time. The family includes sthemO 301, for medium-to-high volume labs with track compatibility, and sthemO 201, a benchtop model for medium-sized labs.

Limited data have been published on analytical performances of the sthemO 301 due to its recent market release, although the first studies suggest satisfactory performances under both controlled and real-world conditions [7,8].

The objective of this study was to evaluate 20 first-line and second-line coagulation assays on the sthemO 301 at seven core lab sites, covering clotting [prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) with silica or kaolin, fibrinogen, thrombin time (TT), factors II, V, VII, X, VIII, IX, XI, protein C activity (PC), protein S activity (PS)], chromogenic [anti-Xa unfractionated heparin (UFH)/low molecular weight heparin (LMWH)], antithrombin activity (AT, PC), and immunoturbidimetric (D-dimer, protein S free antigen) methods. For each assay, we assessed within-run and inter-laboratory precisions, verified or determined reference intervals, and compare the results with Stago's STA R Max analyzer.

Methods

Study design

This prospective multicenter study was conducted between January 2021 and July 2023 in several core lab sites: Rouen University Hospital (France), MedibioLab (Melun, France), Cerba Xpert (Lille, France), Synapse Research Institute (Maastricht, the Netherlands), GHBS (South Brittany Hospital Group), Lorient (France), Clermont-Ferrand University Hospital (France), and Dijon University Hospital (France).

Leftover citrated plasmas were used for precision assessments and method comparisons, whereas an additional sample was requested for reference intervals studies. Artificial samples were limited to a maximum of 10% of inclusions for all parameters to ensure adequate coverage of the measurement range. The only exception was TT, where they reached 13.9%, still within the 20% limit set by the Clinical & Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines [9]. Fresh or frozen samples were used, centrifuged for 15 min at 2000–2500g at room temperature or double centrifuged for frozen and heparinized samples to obtain platelet poor plasmas [10]. Samples were assessed following each site's standard procedures. HIL (refers to Hemolysis, Icteric, Lipemic) indices considered in the study were derived from screening results recorded on the site of the evaluation [11].

The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and French ethical regulations. For the reference intervals study at Synapse, the need for an additional sample was required informed consent from all participants and received ethical committee approval on 21 October 2020.

Reagents

A single lot of reagents and quality controls (QC) was used for all analyzers. Fresh reagents were prepared for each run per manufacturer instructions. PT, fibrinogen, D-Dimer, and protein S free antigen were factory-calibrated. All other specialized assays, except Staclot PS, were calibrated at study start and recalibrated only if QCs were out of range; PS Clot was calibrated at each run. For PT, STA-NeoPTimal and sthemO PT M reagents provide thromboplastin from rabbit brain extract. The international sensitivity index (ISI) value was determined by the manufacturer and close to 1.0 in both cases. The mean normal prothrombin time (MNPT) was calculated on each site and implemented in the PT calibration, with values ranging from 13.2 to 13.6 s for the STA-NeoPTimal reagent and from 11.8 to 12.6 s for the sthemO PT M reagent. A mean normal time was established for both aPTT reagents. The sthemO CK Prest reagent, used for preoperative screening and intrinsic factors, showed reference clotting times of 28.1–30.4 s across sites (site values: 28.1, 28, 28, 30.4 s). The sthemO PTTA reagent, a general screening assay, had a mean normal time of 32.7–34.2 s (site values: 32.7, 33.8, 34.2, 32.8 s). The two TT reagents, STA-Thrombin and sthemO Thrombin M, contain human calcium thrombin at a concentration of approximately 1.5 NIH unit/ml. Hybrid UFH-LMWH calibration was performed on day one for the reagent lot and remained valid throughout the study. For some reagents, they were agitated without magnetic stir bars, using the sthemO 301's integrated agitation system to reduce handling and contamination risk. Reagents are detailed in Table 1.

Evaluation procedure

The sthemO 301 evaluation followed CLSI EP28-A3c [12] and CLSI EP09c [13] guidelines, covering reference intervals and method comparison. Tests were run in singlicate on sthemO 301 and in duplicate on the predicate STA R Max device, reducing imprecision in reference values and ensuring the comparison focused on sthemO 301 result accuracy.

Precision was assessed for clotting assays (PT, aPTT with silica or kaolin, Clauss fibrinogen, TT, factors II, V, VII, X, VIII, IX, XI, PC, and PS), chromogenic assays (anti-Xa activity UFH/LMWH, AT, PC) and immuno-turbidimetric assays (D-Dimer, protein S free antigen). Within-run precision was evaluated by testing normal and abnormal QC plasma samples 25 times in a single run. Inter-laboratory precision was assessed across two to four sites over 16–72 assay runs, each with duplicate analyses of the same reconstituted lyophilized normal and abnormal QC materials. Precision was expressed as standard deviation (SD) and coefficient of variation (CV).

Reference intervals for assays on the new analyzer were determined or verified using published data. Only fresh samples were used for PT, aPTT, Clauss fibrinogen, TT, and D-dimer, as these are routinely tested on fresh specimens. For anti-Xa, fresh samples from individuals not receiving anticoagulant therapy were tested to verify anti-Xa activity remained below 0.1 IU/ml, corresponding to the assay's limit of detection, rather than to define a formal reference intervals. PT, aPTT, and TT reference intervals were determined from at least 120 samples across two to three sites; reference intervals of D-dimer was verified using 63 samples. All participants were healthy individuals aged 18–65 years with similar numbers of women and men.

For comparability studies, a minimum of 100 plasma samples, fresh or frozen leftover samples, including plasma samples from patients with different clinical settings, were tested first on STA R Max and then on sthemO 301, within maximum 1 h between the two measurements. Method comparison was performed on the following assays: PT, aPTT (with two reagents, silica and kaolin), TT, Clauss fibrinogen, anti-Xa activity UFH/LMWH, D-dimer, factors II, V, VII, X, VIII, IX, XI, AT, PC, protein S activity, and free antigen. Following the In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (IVDR) and CLSI EP09c, class C reagents (PT, aPTT, D-dimer, anti-Xa, factors II, V, VII, X, VIII, IX, XI, AT, PC, protein S activity, and free antigen) were assessed at three sites on with a minimum of 100 samples each, totaling at least 300 samples per reagent. Class B reagents (TT, Clauss fibrinogen) were assessed at one site on at least 100 patient samples [13,14].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed in Excel using Analyse-it. Analyses used seconds, % and international normalized ratio (INR) for PT; seconds and ratio for aPTT; g/l for Clauss fibrinogen; ng/ml (or μg/ml on STA R Max) for D-dimer; IU/ml for heparin anti-Xa; and IU/dl for factors II, V, VII, X, VIII, IX, XI, AT, PC, protein S activity, and free antigen. Biases were calculated with the Predict-Act function in Analyse-it, estimating mean differences at specific regression values. Method agreement was assessed via Passing–Bablok regression and Spearman rank correlation.

The evaluation was conducted over an 18-month period.

For routine coagulation parameters, PT in % showed excellent within-run precision, with CVs of 1% for both levels, and inter-laboratory CVs of 3.5 and 3.1%, respectively. The aPTT silica-based assay demonstrated low variability (CVs: 0.8–0.9% within-run; 1.6–2% inter-laboratory), while the aPTT kaolin assay showed slightly better within-run CVs (0.6–0.7%) and similar inter-laboratory CVs (1.2–1.5%). For anti-Xa activity, the UFH assay presented the highest within-run CV (5.2% at low concentration), with inter-laboratory CVs of 4.4–7.1%, while LMWH testing remained more stable (CVs: 1.4–2.6% within-run; 3.1–4.4% inter-laboratory). TT was evaluated at only one level and exhibited excellent within-run precision (0.7%) and acceptable reproducibility (2.7%). Fibrinogen assays also showed good precision, with CVs of 1.2–3.6% within-run and 2.2–3.3% inter-laboratory. D-dimer assays demonstrated excellent precision at both levels, with within-run CVs of 0.9–1.2% and inter-laboratory CVs of 1.1–3.5%.

For coagulation factors assays (FII, FV, FVII, FX, FVIII, FIX, and FXI) across two concentration levels, within-run CVs ranged from 1.2 to 3.2%, with slightly higher values observed at low concentrations, particularly for FVIII (2.4%), FIX (2.2%), and FXI (3.2%). Inter-laboratory precision was acceptable across all factors, with CVs ranging from 5 to 7.6%, consistent with expected variability for factor assays. The highest inter-laboratory CVs were observed for FVII (7.6%), FVIII (7.4%), and FII (7.1%) at low activity levels, which may reflect greater sensitivity to reagent or matrix effects in this range.

Precision was also evaluated for coagulation inhibitor assays. Within-run CVs ranged from 0.8 to 3.2%, with slightly higher values observed for clot-based assays (up to 3.2% for PC Clot and 3.1% for PS Clot). Inter-laboratory precision remained within acceptable limits, though increased variability was noted at low levels, particularly in PC Chrom (6.6%) and PS Clot (6.5%).

Reference intervals

Table 3 compiled the reference intervals of the 20 coagulation parameters. Reference intervals verification on at least 60 samples was considered valid if at least 90% of values fell within limits. If not, new reference intervals were determined: phase 1 used 120–145 samples to estimate 95% reference intervals limits with 90% confidence intervals (CIs), acceptable if the 90% CI width was less than 20% of the interval width. Otherwise, phase 2 required at least 146 samples for 95% CIs. Lower and upper limits were the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. Anti-Xa normal values (<0.10 IU/ml) were verified in 20 patients, all less than 0.10 IU/ml. For D-dimer, 97% of results were less than 0.500 μg/ml, the threshold used in the traditional approach to exclude venous thromboembolism in patients with a low or moderate clinical pretest probability [15]. For patients with values more than 0.500 μg/ml, alternative causes unknown by the participants such as inflammatory diseases, acute or chronic infections or cardiovascular diseases may be considered.

Method comparison

Results for comparison studies between the sthemO 301 and the STA-R Max are presented in Table 4 and Fig. 1 . Method comparison covered the analytical measuring interval for all parameters and showed good agreement between sthemO 301 and STA R Max. Pearson correlation coefficient was above 0.976 for all assays and slopes ranged from 0.94 to 1.07 for all but thrombin time, which was at 0.86. Bias at levels of interest were less than 6% for most routine coagulation parameters, coagulation factors and inhibitors.

For PT in % (n = 333), the slope was exactly unit (1), with no constant bias (intercept = 0.00) indicating excellent agreement. For PT in seconds, the slope was lower (0.95) with a constant negative bias (intercept at −0.55) reflecting a slight underestimation across the range, reaching −9.7% at 12 s. INR values showed a similar trend (slope = 0.94) but with a positive intercept (+0.06) and a slight underestimation increasing at higher INR values (bias, −3.4% at INR 2.5). Regarding aPTT, the silica-based assay (n = 351) displayed a proportional positive bias (slope = 1.06) and a constant negative bias (intercept at −1.56), resulting in a negligible overestimation (+0.1% at 29 s). The silica ratio showed also a proportional positive bias (slope = 1.05) with a small negative intercept (−0.05). The kaolin-based aPTT (n = 330) demonstrated excellent agreement (slope = 0.99) with a slight constant negative bias (intercept = −0.72) leading to a slight underestimation (−3.5% at 25 s). The kaolin ratio showed a similar trend (slope = 1.02, intercept −0.03) with a slight bias of −0.3% at 1.2. For anti-Xa activity, both UFH and LMWH assays (n = 316) showed high linearity (slope = 0.96) and minimal bias, ranging from −0.7 to 3.1%, indicating good comparability between methods. TT (n = 101) showed the largest proportional deviation (slope = 0.86) and a positive intercept (+1.12 s), leading to an underestimation (−7.9% at 21 s). The proportional bias appears unaffected by the cause of the prolonged TT, as the same trend is observed with heparinized and coagulopathic samples. As the nature and concentration of thrombin are similar for STA-Thrombin and sthemO Thrombin M reagents, we hypothesize that the observed difference could be due to the analyzer. Lastly, fibrinogen (n = 98) displayed a proportional negative bias (slope = 0.95), with a slight positive intercept (+0.07), resulting in a bias of −2.2% at 1 g/l. In particular, the D-dimer assay (n = 318) demonstrated excellent analytical performance, with a slope of 1.03 and negligible bias across the measurement range (e.g. a minimal deviation of −0.7% at 0.500 μg/ml).

Among the coagulation factor assays evaluated, several showed minor to moderate biases across the analytical range despite excellent correlation (r ≥ 0.986). Factor II (n = 115) and factor X (n = 114) both demonstrated a slope of 1 and a constant negative bias (intercept −4 and −3, respectively), with underestimations reaching −5.1% at 78 IU/dl activity for FII and −3.7% at 82 IU/dl for FX. Factor IX (n = 104) exhibited proportional underestimation (slope = 0.95) and negative intercepts (−1.04), with notable deviations, particularly at low concentrations with a bias of −8.4% at 30 IU/dl. In contrast, factor V (n = 105) and factor XI (n = 106) showed mild positive proportional bias (slope = 1.05 and 1.04, respectively) and positive intercepts (−2.90 and +1.81, respectively) with negligeable bias at decision threshold (1.2% at 77 IU/dl for FV and 6.1% at 91 IU/dl for FXI). Factor VII (n = 109) showed the highest slope (1.07), indicating a consistent proportional positive bias with values up to +3.8% at 68 IU/dl. Factor VIII (n = 106) showed minimal differences, with a perfect slope (1.00) and negligible bias (0.3% at 30 IU/dl). For factors II, V, and VII, the consistent negative bias may reflect differences in clot detection methodology between the two analyzers.

For coagulation inhibitor assays, some differences were observed despite excellent correlation (r ≥ 0.983). AT (n = 110) demonstrated a slope of 1.07 and a constant negative bias (intercept −4.26), with bias reaching 1.7% at 88 IU/dl activity. For PC clot (n = 114), a slight proportional underestimation was observed (slope = 0.97), with a small negative intercept (−1.70) leading to a progressively lower bias at lower levels (−5.3% at 65 IU/dl). Similarly, the PC Chrom (n = 106) exhibited an ideal slope (1), but with a consistent negative intercept (−2), leading to a mild underestimation at lower activities (bias, −2.5% at 80 IU/dl). For protein S, both clot-based and free antigen assays showed slight overestimations, with positive intercepts (+4.00 and +5.34, respectively), and proportional biases reaching +9.5% at 65 IU/dl for the free PS assay.

Discussion

We reported the precision, accuracy, and reference intervals of 20 parameters on the sthemO 301, half of these being reported for the first time, which include anti-Xa, factors II, V, VII, X, VIII, IX, XI, protein S-free antigen, and activity. We found satisfactory within-run and inter-laboratory precisions using QCs as well as a good agreement for all parameters between sthemO 301 and STA R Max. This performance evaluation complements and expands on previously published data on this instrument [7,8]. The reference intervals provided for the routine coagulation parameters, coagulation factors, and inhibitors facilitate their implementation in clinical laboratories.

Within-run and inter-laboratory precisions for routine assays are in line with the literature. CVs were low and similar or better to those reported by Pontis et al.[7], Nguyen et al.[16], and Gardiner et al.[17]. For D-dimer, our CVs consistently outperformed Gardiner's and Nguyen's findings. For coagulation factors, within-run CVs ranged from 1.2 to 3.2%, comparable to published data and occasionally slightly better, particularly for factors VII and X, where we achieved lower CVs than Nguyen et al. Inter-laboratory CVs ranged from 5 to 7.6%, slightly higher than those reported by Nguyen's and Gardiner's results but still within acceptable limits without clinical significance. For coagulation inhibitors, the only differences with already published data are lower within-run CVs for chromogenic and clot-based PC assays and slightly higher inter-laboratory CVs for AT.

Similarly, results of the method comparison corroborate those of the authors mentioned above with overall good agreement with the predicate device. Some points should be highlighted. Consistent with Pontis's findings, we consistently found shorter TT on the sthemO 301. This underestimation may stem from analytical variability inherent to TT assays, including high sensitivity to fibrinogen levels, interference by inhibitors such as heparin, differences in detection systems or reagents, pathological samples, or variations in testing conditions. We found lower biases for fibrinogen measurement, aPTT with both activators and D-dimer assays. For the latter, the negligeable bias of −0.7% at 0.500 μg/ml is in strong disagreement with that of Denitto's study which peaks at −31% bias. This difference may be explained by fact that the use of the ACL TOP 750 (Werfen) as predicate device and the lack of standardization for D-dimer [8,15]. FVIII results showed minimal bias (0.3%) at low levels, consistent with Gardiner's findings and supporting its reliability for the management of patients with hemophilia A. FIX exhibited a greater negative bias at low levels (−8.4%), reflecting known challenges, but remained within acceptable limits.

Analytical performances of the sthemO 301 reported in this study are similar or superior than those reported recently for other coagulation analyzers such as CS-5100 (Sysmex) [18], CN-3000 (Sysmex) [19], CN-6000 (Sysmex) [17,20–22], STA R Max 2 (Diagnostica Stago) [23], ACL TOP 750 (Werfen) [24,25], and Atellica COAG 360 (Siemens Healthineers) [26,27].

Strengths of the study include the multicentric design, a relatively large number of samples analyzed, and adherence to CLSI guidelines. This is the first evaluation to report precision and accuracy on the sthemO 301 of anti-Xa activity UFH/LMWH, seven coagulation factor assays (FII, FV, FVII, FX, FVIII, FIX, and FXI), and two coagulation inhibitor assays (PS free antigen and activity). Finally, reference intervals were verified or defined for all parameters, facilitating their implementation in routine coagulation laboratories.

Our study has several limitations. First, no comparison was performed between capped and uncapped samples, though the manufacturer lists validated tubes for cap piercing. Second, as a single lot of reagents and QC was used throughout the study, lot-to-lot reagent variability was not examined. Third, while samples were screened according to each site's routine, including HIL assessment on STA R Max or sthemO 301, the HIL function itself was not specifically evaluated; further studies are needed to assess interference from hemolysis, icterus, or lipemia. Finally, instrument throughput was not evaluated here; Pontis et al. reported consistent throughput across 10 runs despite organizational constraints.

To conclude, we report the precision, accuracy, and reference intervals of 20 parameters on the sthemO 301, half of these being reported for the first time which include anti-Xa, factors II, V, VII, X, VIII, IX, XI, protein S-free antigen, and activity. The sthemO 301 analyzer offers satisfactory analytical performance and a good comparability with the predicate device for all parameters, meeting expectations for implementing a new coagulation analyzer in the laboratory.
