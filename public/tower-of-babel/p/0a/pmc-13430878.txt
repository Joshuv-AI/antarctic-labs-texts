Deciphering the mechanism of selenium-induced resistance to TR4 in bananas via proteomic profiling
Authors: Lina Liu, Yue Ding, Kesuo Yin, Guodong Wang, Ying Luo, Qiuyun Xiao, Chengye Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelenium (Se), an essential trace element for both animals and plants, plays a crucial role in suppressing TR4 (Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense tropical race 4) infection in bananas. Proteins are central to plant disease resistance and defense responses; however, their functions in Se-enhanced disease resistance in bananas remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the regulatory effects of Se on banana resistance to TR4 from a proteomic perspective.ResultsIn this study, we detected the changes in Se content and quantitative traits of banana tissue-cultured seedlings across different generations treated with Se in vitro. Additionally, we investigated the alterations in TR4 infection progression and the responses of antioxidant enzyme system at different time points after pathogen inoculation in the 7th-generation treated seedlings, as well as the changes in protein expression profiles of banana tissue-cultured seedlings at key time points. From the host perspective, this study further clarifies that Se elicits plant innate immunity and enhances banana defense response against TR4 invasion. The protein expression profiles identified that banana glutathione S-transferase, cysteine-related proteins, peroxidase, ABC transporter, and transcription factors (bZIP and MYBS1) are involved in host resistance to TR4 under Se treatment. Furthermore, the function of mitochondrial NADH dehydrogenase in the oxidative phosphorylation pathway (ko00190) during Se-induced disease resistance responses in bananas was further characterized. This suggests that one of the mechanisms by which Se enhances plant disease resistance is maintaining the structural integrity and functional stability of mitochondria.ConclusionThis study highlights the significant impact of Se in promoting the expression of disease resistance-related proteins in bananas, and identifies the critical function of banana mitochondrial proteins in resistance to TR4. Therefore, the present study provides valuable insight and practical basis for the application of Se fertilizers as an alternative to fungicides, thereby facilitating the cultivation of disease-resistant crops in agricultural production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09121-5.Keywords: Selenium, Banana, Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense tropical race 4, Protein expression profile, Mitochondrial proteins

Background

As an important trace element, selenium (Se) is not essential for plant growth and development, but it plays an irreplaceable role in enhancing plant resistance to both biotic and abiotic stresses, including insect pests, fungal diseases, viruses, drought, salinity, heavy metals, and temperatures [1–4]. In animals, Se is involved in the formation of selenoproteins and is closely associated with health; its significance in anti-aging and disease prevention has been fully verified [5–8]. In the plant system, research on Se-mediated disease resistance has gradually become a hot topic. Recent studies have further indicated that Se can improve plant disease resistance through multiple pathways: (1) Strengthening the plant’s innate defense systems, such as the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway and the salicylic acid (SA) pathway [9–14], to prevent pathogen invasion; (2) Directly damaging the cell membranes and cell extension structures of pathogens after they invade plant tissues [15, 16]; (3) Regulating the structure of soil microbial communities to construct a plant defense barrier [17–20]. These studies provide a valuable insight for regulating plant disease resistance using Se, however, the specific mechanisms vary significantly depending on factors such as plant species, cultivars, Se dosage, and pathogen types [21]. The regulatory mechanisms remain to be further explored, especially the disease-resistant response mediated by Se through protein regulatory pathways, which has not been clearly elucidated.

Banana (Musa spp.) is widely cultivated in more than 135 countries and regions around the world, serving as an important cash and food crop in tropical and subtropical zones [22, 23]. However, Fusarium wilt of bananas caused by Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense tropical race 4 (TR4) has long restricted the sustainable development of the global banana industry [24, 25]. TR4 exhibits strong host adaptability, as it not only infects Cavendish banana, the dominant commercial cultivar worldwide, but also poses significant threat to various local varieties. The pathogen is challenging to control because it can survive long-term in soil and organic debris, as its chlamydospores can persist in the soil for decades. It enhances pathogenicity through multiple mechanisms, such as altering the composition of rhizosphere microorganisms and inducing nitric oxide bursts. Once the disease occurs in the field, it is difficult to eradicate. Severely infected fields often must be abandoned or undergo crop rotation, resulting in enormous economic losses to the industry [26–29]. At present, research on TR4 control has covered multiple fields, including the breeding of disease-resistant varieties, soil conditioning, the application of beneficial microorganisms, and conservation tillage cultivation technologies. Among these, the Technical Code for the Prevention and Control of Banana Fusarium Wilt has achieved a control effect that reduces the disease incidence to below 10% in severely affected areas [30, 31]. Nevertheless, existing control measures still have obvious limitations, including insufficient variety diversity, unstable performance of resistant varieties, inconsistency between laboratory experimental effects and field production requirements, and difficulties in popularizing high-efficiency cultivation techniques [31]. Our previous studies have found that Se contributes to the control of TR4 in the cultivated banana variety [22], in greenhouse and field trials, Se treatments (20–160 mg/L) reduced disease symptoms by 42.4% to 65.7%, and the disease index was significantly negatively correlated with the total selenium content in roots. Indeed, after field application of Se at 100 or 200 mg/plant, the incidence of TR4 decreased significantly. Further elucidation of its underlying regulatory mechanisms will help explore safe, efficient, and highly adaptable Se-based new approaches for TR4 control, thereby providing novel strategies to safeguard the healthy development of the banana industry.

As the primary executors of life activities, proteins play a central role in the plant defense responses, participating in multiple key processes such as pathogen recognition, signal transduction, defense substance synthesis, and redox homeostasis regulation [32–34]. Studies on the interaction system between banana and TR4 have found that the high expression of proteins and protein-protein interactions can directly regulate the resistance of banana [35–37], and proteins in different tissue parts exert distinct functions. For instance, the MaKAN4 protein in the root endodermis can interact with the promoters of autoinhibited Ca2+-ATPase 7 (MaACA7) and Alcohol Dehydrogenase 3 (MaADH3) from Musa acuminata and promote their expression, revealing that MaKAN4 plays a critical role in banana disease resistance by regulating the Zn2+-dependent MaACA7/MaADH3 module [38]. Notably, recent studies on multiple plants such as Arabidopsis thaliana, rice, tomato, castor, broccoli, and barley have indicated that the physiological functions of Se are mostly achieved through regulating the expression and activity of proteins [39–43]. Existing studies have confirmed that Se enhances the activity of antioxidant enzymes (e.g., glutathione peroxidase [GSH-Px], peroxidase [POD], and polyphenol oxidase [PPO]) and reduces the disease index of banana Fusarium wilt [21, 22]. However, the specific molecular mechanisms by which Se induce disease resistance by regulating the protein expression profiles, post-translational modifications, and interaction networks in banana remain unclear.

Consequently, current research on host-protein reprogramming has several clear limitations. First, the association between Se-mediated protein regulatory pathways and known disease resistance mechanisms in the banana-TR4 interaction system has not been fully established. Second, due to a lack of systematic proteomic analysis, the protein targets of Se in regulating plant resistance remain unidentified. Third, there is insufficient research on the correlation between the differences in Se absorption and accumulation among banana germplasms with their distinct protein regulatory patterns. Based on this, the present study focuses on the core mechanism of Se-regulated proteins inducing banana resistance to TR4, aiming to identify key Se-regulated proteins and pathways in banana, and provide a theoretical basis for novel control strategies against Fusarium wilt.

Results

Dynamic changes in Se accumulation, growth traits and TR4 infection of banana seedlings under Se treatment

To clarify the Se accumulation status in banana seedlings during Se treatment, this study determined the total selenium content (TSC) in various tissues of seedlings from the Se-treated groups and the control group, which had undergone successively subculture to the 7th generation (Fig. 1A). The results showed that the TSC in the fibrous roots of 1st generation under Se treatment significantly increased, reaching 28.23 mg/kg, and peaked at the 5th generation (31.06 mg/kg), with no significant difference observed among the generations. The TSC in the corms significantly increased as early as the 1st generation, and reached the maximum value of 20.76 mg/kg at the 5th generation, which was significantly higher than that of the 1st, 3rd and 7th generations, respectively (Fig. 1A). The TSC in the leaves was the highest at the 5th generation (6.99 mg/kg), as compared to 1st and 3rd generations, while no significant difference was found compared with the 7th generation. Overall, the TSC in different tissues exhibited the order of fibrous roots > corms > leaves. Taking the 7th generation as an example, the TSC in the fibrous roots, corms, and leaves of the Se-treated group were 26.76 ± 1.49, 16.36 ± 0.99, and 6.50 ± 0.50 mg·kg-1, and all of which were significantly higher than those of the control group (Fig. 1A). The translocation factor of Se in banana seedlings was 0.243, and the bioconcentration factors of various tissues were in the order of fibrous roots (5.35) > corms (3.27) > leaves (1.30). These results indicated that Se treatment significantly increased Se accumulation in banana plants, with a tendency of Se translocation from roots to leaves.

To analyze the effects of Se treatment on the quantitative traits of banana seedlings, this study determined the main growth parameters of seedlings across successive generations (Fig. 1B–F). The results revealed that Se treatment significantly reduced both the fresh weight and plant height of banana seedlings, with the lowest values recorded at the 3rd generation, representing a decrease of 66.90% and 52.15% (Fig. 1B and C). Thereafter, both indicators gradually recovered, showing an increase of 26.09% and 18.62% at the 7th generation compared with those at the 3rd generation. The number of fibrous roots and leaves increased by 4.56% and 15.66% at the 5th generation, respectively, but the differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). The length of the longest fibrous root increased significantly across different generations, with an increment ranging from 53.50% to 93.51% (Fig. 1D-F). These findings indicated that Se treatment exerted a distinct inhibitory effect on the quantitative traits of banana seedlings (e.g., fresh weight and plant height). However, this growth inhibitory effect attenuated with increasing subculture generations.

The results of confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) observation on fibrous roots of bananas infected by TR4 at different time points are shown in Fig. 1G. At 1-day post-inoculation (dpi) with TR4, hyphal aggregation was observed on the surface of fibrous roots, and no significant difference was noted between the Se-treated group (7th generation seedlings) and the control group at this time point. By 3 dpi, only a small number of hyphae had invaded the root tissues and extended to the cortical sclerenchyma in the control group, whereas no hyphal penetration into the roots was detected in the Se-treated group. At 5 dpi, the hyphae in the control group further invaded the cortical parenchyma and gradually approached the endodermis (Fig. 1G). In contrast, the hyphae in the Se-treated group remain primarily confined to the root surface, with a significant difference between the two groups was observed at this time point, suggesting that Se treatment might enhance the host resistance, thereby improving tolerance to TR4 in banana. Meanwhile, 5 dpi was identified as the critical time point for differentiating Se-treated and control seedlings. Based on this, samples from 5 dpi were selected for subsequent protein analysis.

Responses of banana seedlings at the proteomic level to Se treatment and TR4 infection

We performed proteomic analysis on four groups of banana seedling samples, including TR4 treatment (BT), Se treatment (BS), combined Se+TR4 treatment (BST), and control (BC). A total of 51,796 peptides and 7,303 proteins were identified from four sample groups (Table S1). As shown in Fig. 2A, the inter-sample correlation analysis revealed high correlation among samples within each treatment group, with the intra-group correlation coefficients greater than 0.967. In contrast, the inter-group correlation coefficients were relatively lower (ranging from 0.509 to 0.690). These results demonstrate strong experimental reproducibility, significant differences in protein expression between different treatments, and high data reliability.

The results of functional annotation showed that these proteins were mainly enriched in multiple biological processes (BP), including metabolic process, cellular process and response to stimulus. In the cellular components (CC) groups, these proteins were significantly involved in cellular anatomical entity, and protein-containing complex. In terms of molecular function (MF), they were mainly involved in catalytic activity, binding, antioxidant activity, and transcription regulation activity (Fig. 2B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis revealed that these proteins were significantly involved in metabolic pathway, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, oxidative phosphorylation, and glutathione metabolism (Fig. 2C). Protein families (PFAM) domain analysis further identified multiple conserved domains, including the previously reported pathogen recognition and perception proteins resistance to Pseudomonas syringae pv. maculicola 1 (RPM1) and leucine-rich repeat (LRR) [44, 45]; EF-hand involved in calcium signaling [46] mitochondrial carrier motif (mito_carr) responsible for substrate translocation between the cytoplasm and mitochondrial matrix [47]; as well as Se‑induced peroxidase and glutathione S-transferase (GST) [22, 48] (Fig. 2D). Subcellular localization prediction revealed that these proteins were primarily distributed in the cytoplasm, chloroplast, and mitochondrion (Fig. 2E). Based on the functional classification of the identified proteins, we found that the distribution of proteins across different functional categories was generally consistent with the functions reported in previous studies [49, 50] as well as with those anticipated under our treatment conditions (Se and TR4), further validating the reliability of our data.

Protein changes and functional analysis of banana seedlings under different treatments

Analysis of differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) between multiple groups showed that the number of DEPs were higher in BST vs. BC (963 up-regulated, 559 down-regulated) and BST vs. BT (969 up-regulated, 586 down-regulated), respectively (Table S2). In contrast, the number of DEPs in BT vs. BC was relatively lower (285 up-regulated, 211 down-regulated) (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B; Supplementary Fig. 2). The proteins with significantly increased expression in the BST vs. BT comparison group included 7 GST proteins, 35 ATPase-related proteins, 26 NADH dehydrogenase-related proteins, 10 ATP sulfurylase and iron-sulfur proteins, 12 cysteine-related proteins, 11 peroxidase proteins, 6 ABC transporters, and different transcription factors, including (transcription initiation factor TFIID subunit 9, transcription termination factor MTERF15, bZIP transcription factor 1-B isoform X1, transcription factor MYBS1). To evaluate the reliability of our protein expression data, we performed reverse transcription quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) to analyze the expression of genes encoding 19 DEPs (Table S2). The expression trends of 78.9% of these genes were consistent with the corresponding protein abundances across different treatments, further confirming the reliability of the data and supporting its suitability for further analysis.

The results of KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated that the DEPs in different comparison groups were distributed across distinct metabolic and signaling pathways (Supplementary Fig. 1C, Table S4). The DEPs in the BST vs. BC group were significantly enriched in the pathways of oxidative phosphorylation (ko00190), nitrogen metabolism (ko00910), porphyrin metabolism (ko00860) and spliceosome (ko03040). In contrast, the DEPs in the BST vs. BT group were significantly enriched in oxidative phosphorylation (ko00190) and spliceosome (ko03040) pathways (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Notably, within the commonly and significantly enriched oxidative phosphorylation pathway, both NADH dehydrogenase- and ATPase-associated proteins significantly exhibited upregulated expression (Supplementary Fig. 1D; Supplementary Fig. 3). These data suggested that Se modulates DEPs in key pathways such as oxidative phosphorylation, thereby altering the resistance of banana seedlings to TR4.

GO enrichment analysis indicated that the DEPs of different comparison groups were enriched in distinct GO terms (Supplementary Fig. 1E, Table S5). As shown in Fig. 3E, the BP terms commonly enriched among the four comparison groups included monocarboxylic acid biosynthetic process (GO:0072330) and fatty acid biosynthetic process (GO:0006633). Meanwhile, the comparison groups involving Se shared the enriched BP terms such as tetrapyrrole biosynthetic process (GO:0033014), quinone biosynthetic process (GO:1901663) and response to stress (GO:0006950). The BP terms uniquely enriched in the BST vs. BT group comprised regulation of DNA-templated transcription (GO:0006355), regulation of nitrogen compound metabolic process (GO:0051171), regulation of response to biotic stimulus (GO:0002831), sulfur amino acid biosynthetic process (GO:0000097), and others. The DEPs were commonly enriched in different MF terms, including oxidoreductase activity, acting on superoxide radicals as acceptor (GO:0016721) and superoxide dismutase activity (GO:0004784), respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1E). The MF terms uniquely enriched in the BST vs. BT group were metal ion binding (GO:0046872), calcium-dependent phospholipid binding (GO:0005544) and transferase activity, transferring alkyl or aryl (other than methyl) groups (GO:0016765). In the term of CC group, the commonly enriched terms were mitochondrial protein-containing complex (GO:0098798), NADH dehydrogenase complex (GO:0030964) and extrinsic component of organelle membrane (GO:0031312). In contrast, the CC terms uniquely enriched in the BST vs. BT group included nucleus (GO:0005634), nuclear pore (GO:0005643) and organelle inner membrane (GO:0019866) (Supplementary Fig. 1E). This indicates that Se altered the DEPs of banana seedlings, thereby participated in the defense response against TR4 with a particular involvement of mitochondrial proteins.

Given the important role of MF terms about oxidoreductase activity in Se and plant disease resistance, we further examined the antioxidant enzyme system in banana seedlings under different treatments, including the activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), POD, PPO, GSH-Px and the content of reduced glutathione (GSH) (Fig. 3A-F). Banana seedlings treated with Se and inoculated with TR4 mainly exhibited altered SOD and CAT activities as well as GSH-Px activity and GSH content. The activities of SOD and CAT showed a downward trend, reaching a significant level on the 5th dpi, with reductions of 48.07% and 42.44%, respectively (Fig. 3A and B). Notably, GSH-Px activity remained at a consistently low level, whereas GSH content was significantly elevated, being 2.43-, 1.57-, 1.57- and 2.33-fold higher than that of the control group at different time points. The GSH content peaked on the 5th dpi, reaching 8.58 µmol·L-1 (Fig. 3F). These data suggested that Se primarily modulates GSH-Px and GSH contents at the early stage of TR4 infection, thereby enhancing the resistance of banana seedlings against the pathogen.

The role of mitochondrial proteins in Se-induced resistance of banana seedlings to TR4

We performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) to systematically evaluate different comparison groups, aiming to comprehensively identify protein sets with potential biological functions at the global protein expression level (Fig. 4). The GSEA results showed that among the positively enriched GO terms, the protein sets commonly involved in all comparison groups with Se treatment were mainly associated with mitochondrial structure, including mitochondrial part, mitochondrial membrane, inner mitochondrial membrane protein complex, mitochondrial protein complex and mitochondrial membrane part (Fig. 4A-D). These proteins might regulate the resistance of banana to TR4 through molecular interaction networks. For instance, in the BST vs. BT group, the proteins with a weight value greater than 10 included 8 annotated mitochondrial proteins and 9 mitochondrial-related proteins. In addition, the uniquely enriched protein sets in the BST vs. BT group were also related to functions such as sequence-specific DNA binding, double-stranded DNA binding, transcription regulator activity, protein heterodimerization activity and mRNA processing. In contrast, no differentially expressed proteins annotated as or related to mitochondria were identified in the BT vs. BC group (Fig. 4D). Instead, the enriched protein sets were associated with processes including enzyme inhibitor activity, macromolecule catabolic process, hydrolase activity, hydrolyzing O-glycosyl compounds, hydrolase activity, acting on glycosyl bonds, extracellular region, exopeptidase activity, endopeptidase regulator activity, polysaccharide catabolic process and peptidase inhibitor activity (Fig. 4E). We suggested that Se mediates the resistance response of banana seedlings to TR4 through regulating the functions of mitochondrial proteins.

We further analyzed the expression abundance of mitochondrial proteins in banana seedlings under different treatment conditions (Table 1). The results showed that the abundance of mitochondrially annotated proteins exhibited a trend of BST > BS > BC or BT across the four groups, including 5 proteins of NADH dehydrogenase [ubiquinone] iron-sulfur protein, 4 proteins of cytochrome b-c1 complex subunit, and 3 proteins of NADH dehydrogenase [ubiquinone] flavoprotein. Among these groups, the abundance of such proteins was the highest in the BST group, being 1.5–4.0 folds that of the BT group. For instance, the expression abundances of XP_009401101.1 (cytochrome b-c1 complex subunit 9, mitochondrial) in the BST and BT groups were 345.56 and 86.94, respectively (Table 1). The abundances of XP_009407142.2 (NADH dehydrogenase [ubiquinone] flavoprotein 1, mitochondrial isoform X2) were 6045.27 and 2806.56 (Table 1). These findings showed that Se regulates the expression of mitochondrial proteins, thereby forming a protein interaction network system and further enhancing the resistance to TR4.

Discussion

Se alters the protein expression profile of banana to induce host resistance against Fusarium wilt

Under Se-stress, plants may reallocate resources toward defense pathways, as evidenced by the downregulation of differential proteins involved in photosynthesis, carbon fixation, and glutathione metabolic pathways [51]. Se treatment can alter the levels of storage proteins such as albumin and gluten in barley grains [43], while Se fortification upregulates proteins including GST, HSP70, and F₁ ATP synthase in broccoli florets [52]. These studies suggest that the regulation of proteome by Se exhibits species-specificity and tissue-specificity, and its disease-resistant effects and application dosage need to be analyzed in combination with specific crops and environmental conditions. In this study, we investigated the response of banana protein expression profile to TR4 under 5 ppm Se treatment in a tissue culture environment, which minimized the interference of external environmental factors on the research outcomes. This work represents the first study to explore the protein expression dynamics underlying Se-induced resistance to Fusarium wilt of banana, and found that the DEPs enriched in key functional groups, including oxidative phosphorylation, regulation of nitrogen compound metabolic process, regulation of response to biotic stimulus, sulfur amino acid biosynthetic process, and mitochondrial protein-containing complex, are involved in banana disease resistance.

Key proteins such as ATP sulfurylase (APS), GSTs, SODs, and the ABC transporter family are implicated in Se accumulation and detoxification in wheat and castor [42, 53]. Recent studies have also demonstrated that Se induces stress resistance in crops by regulating protein expression. For instance, in Se-treated rice seedlings, proteins related to the photosynthetic system, energy metabolism, transport, and signal transduction were significantly upregulated. This achieves the coordinated enhancement of antioxidant stress tolerance and photosynthesis, thereby providing material and energy support for resistance to cadmium and arsenic stress [41, 54]. Functional verification further confirmed that the As-tolerant mutant astol1 enhances the physical interaction between chloroplast-localized O-acetylserine (thiol) lyase and serine acetyltransferase within the cysteine synthase complex. This interaction activates the sulfate and Se uptake pathways, promotes the synthesis of cysteine, glutathione, and phytochelatins, and ultimately improves arsenic tolerance [55]. Previously, it has been reported that the tomato protein Q954YH4 was involved in the response to low-temperature stress under 24-epibrassinolide + Se treatment [40]. Our study further revealed that proteins including GST, APS, cysteine, POD, ABC transporter, as well as transcription factors (bZIP and MYBS1), are induced under Se treatment and participate in banana resistance to TR4. Previous gene functional validation has confirmed that these proteins or their encoding genes play a positive regulatory role in plant disease resistance [56–59]. Moreover, transcriptomic analyses of transgenic banana lines demonstrated strong induction of basal immunity regulators (e.g., ABC transporter G5, LRR receptor-STK, pathogen-related protein-1, thaumatin, MaNAC105, and MaNAC46) to enhance resistance, corroborating our proteomic results [60, 61].

Our results indicate that GSH and its transferase (GST) play key roles in Se-induced resistance to TR4 in banana. GSH acts as a non-enzymatic antioxidant that directly scavenges pathogen-induced reactive oxygen species (ROS) and, through GST-catalyzed conjugation, detoxifies harmful substrates, thereby alleviating oxidative damage [62]. Furthermore, GSH participates in the SA signaling pathway; its increased levels and subsequent conversion to oxidized glutathione activate downstream defense genes [62]. In addition, GSH is essential for the early induction of camalexin biosynthesis pathways during pathogen infection, and GSH deficiency suppresses the activation of defense genes [63]. In the tobacco mosaic virus (TMV) system, GSH is crucial for both local resistance and systemic acquired resistance against TMV [64]. In summary, GSH plays a central role in plant disease resistance by modulating ROS scavenging and the SA signaling pathway, providing strong support for its function in Se-induced resistance to TR4 in banana.

Mitochondrial proteins are involved in disease resistance of banana

Although cytoplasmic and chloroplast proteins were the most abundant in our proteomic analysis, only mitochondria‑associated proteins were significantly enriched in defense‑related functional categories. This observation suggests that under Se treatment, plants may prioritize mitochondrial stress signaling over general cellular maintenance. The ROS generated in this process acts as key signaling molecules that initiate and regulate the multi-level stress defense responses of plants [65]. Mitochondria are recognized as central hubs for ROS production and retrograde signaling during environmental stress, where intra‑mitochondrial ROS formation serves as a key trigger for downstream protective responses [66]. Indeed, disruption of mitochondrial function, such as reduced ATP content and energy charge following pathogen infection, accelerates disease progression [67], whereas impairment of mitochondrial gene editing attenuates resistance to pathogens [68]. Collectively, these findings confirm that mitochondrial functional integrity and its associated regulatory networks play an indispensable role in plant disease resistance. Consistent with this view, Se has been shown to restore mitochondrial dysfunction and regulate programmed cell death under chromium stress, positioning mitochondria as a key hub for Se action during stress alleviation [69]. In contrast, the high abundance of cytoplasmic and chloroplast proteins likely reflects their housekeeping roles in maintaining cellular homeostasis rather than direct involvement in defense signaling. Therefore, the preferential enrichment of mitochondrial proteins in our dataset further supports the central role of mitochondria as both initiators and amplifiers of stress signals in Se‑induced defense responses. Future work employing subcellular proteomics and functional validation will be necessary to dissect the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this compartment‑specific response.

This study is the first to reveal that mitochondrial NADH dehydrogenase plays a key role in Se-induced resistance of banana to TR4. A finding consistent with the conclusions of multiple studies on Se-mediated regulation of plant mitochondrial function. Se acts on mitochondria through various mechanisms to improve crop resistance to heavy metal and salt stress, including enhancing the structural integrity of mitochondria, reducing metal content in mitochondria, increasing mitochondrial membrane potential, and inhibiting the expression of mitochondrially localized selenium transporter genes [69–72].

The role of NADH dehydrogenase in plant stress resistance has been partially reported, which supports the conclusions of this study. Notably, low-concentration Se can significantly enhance the photosynthetic efficiency of roses, among which the genes encoding the ND6 and NDUFAB1 subunits in photosynthetic complex I (homologous to mitochondrial NADH dehydrogenase) were significantly upregulated [73]. Mitochondrial NADH dehydrogenase AtNDB2 and alternative oxidase AtAOX1A in A. thaliana together form a complete non-phosphorylating electron transport pathway. Activation of this pathway can markedly improve plant tolerance to various environmental stresses [74]. Under salt stress, the expression levels of NADH dehydrogenase-related proteins in plants also increase significantly, implying their positive role in stress adaptation [75]. Strawberry mitochondrial NADH dehydrogenase (ubiquinone) 1β subcomplex subunit 9 (FaNDUFB9) interacts with the RNA silencing suppressor Pro2Glu protein of strawberry mottle virus (SMoV). By inhibiting the expression and silencing activity of Pro2Glu, it impairs viral infectivity, indicating that mitochondrial proteins regulate the function of viral pathogenic factors to participate in plant immunity [76], a mechanism analogous to the role of NADH dehydrogenase in enhancing banana resistance to TR4 identified in this study. Although the central role of mitochondria in sensing cellular stress signals and coordinating defense responses has only garnered widespread attention in recent years [77]. The aforementioned studies collectively indicate that mitochondrial NADH dehydrogenase exerts a positive regulatory effect on plant resistance.

However, inconsistencies exist between the results of this study and some previous reports. For example, wheat mitochondrial NADH dehydrogenase (ubiquinone) 1α subcomplex subunit 9 (NDUFA9) inhibits SA synthesis, thereby attenuating wheat resistance to Fusarium head blight [14]. The maize gene GRMZM2G318346, which encodes cytochrome b-c1 complex subunit VII (ZmQCR7), a component of the mitochondrial electron transport chain, and it’s A. thaliana homolog both suppress NLR protein-induced hypersensitive response [78]. Wheat WRKY74 can reduce the activity of NADH dehydrogenase complex (NDH) by downregulating TaCRR6 expression, thereby triggering ROS burst and enhancing resistance to Candidatus Phytoplasma tritici, suggesting a negative correlation between NDH activity and disease resistance [79]. In addition, after knocking out partial homologous copies of the rapeseed cytochrome b-c1 complex subunit BnQCR8 using CRISPR/Cas9 technology, the resistance of plants to Sclerotinia sclerotiorum and Botrytis cinerea were significantly enhanced [80]. The discrepancies in results may arise from differences in NADH dehydrogenase protein subtypes, plant species, and disease resistance modes. Future studies should employ approaches such as in vitro protein interaction assays, gene editing, and subcellular localization verification to further clarify the functional targets and regulatory networks of mitochondrial proteins (including NADH dehydrogenase) in Se-induced banana resistance to TR4, thereby providing more sufficient data support for elucidating the mitochondrial-mediated disease resistance mechanism.

Conclusions

In this study, we detected the variations in Se content and quantitative traits of banana tissue culture seedlings across different generations treated with Se in vitro. Further, we monitored the changes in TR4 infection progress and the responses of antioxidant enzyme systems and analyzed the alterations in protein expression of banana tissue culture seedlings. From the perspective of the host plant, these measurements further clarified that Se activates the plant’s innate immunity and enhances banana defense response against TR4 invasion. In addition, the protein expression profile confirmed that banana proteins including GST, cysteine, peroxidase, ABC transporter, as well as transcription factors (bZIP and MYBS1), are involved in host resistance to TR4 under Se treatment. The role of mitochondrial NADH dehydrogenase in oxidative phosphorylation (ko00190) suggesting that Se-mediated maintenance of mitochondrial structural integrity and function enhances plant disease resistance. This study highlights the role of Se in plant-pathogen interactions, further clarifies the functions of mitochondrial proteins in pathogen reduction, and provides important practical basis for the substitution of fungicides with Se fertilizers and the cultivation of disease-resistant crops in agricultural production.

Materials and methods

Culture of banana tissue-cultured seedlings and determination of quantitative traits

The Cavendish subgroup banana cultivar used in this study was the commercially grown variety Brazilian banana (Musa spp.), which is widely cultivated globally. Tissue-cultured plantlets have been maintained long-term in the Agricultural Environment and Resource Research Institute, Yunnan Academy of Agricultural Sciences for research use, with the accession number “Baxijiao” [81–83]. This variety is highly susceptible to TR4. The materials were propagated and rooted via tissue culture. The formulation of the propagation medium was as follows, MS medium 4.4 g/L + NAA 0.2 mg/L + 6-BA 3.5 mg/L + agar 4.8 g/L + sucrose 30 g/L + distilled water. The formulation of the rooting medium was MS medium 4.4 g/L + NAA 0.5–0.6 mg/L + activated carbon 0.3–0.5 g/L + agar 4.8 g/L + sucrose 30 g/L + distilled water. The culture environmental conditions were set as follows, temperature 28 ± 2 °C, photoperiod 12 h/d, and light intensity 1600–2000 lx. For Se treatment, the medium was supplemented with Na₂SeO3 at a final concentration of 5.0 mg/L. The subculture materials were tissue-cultured seedlings from propagation culture, and all experimental materials were rooted banana plantlets. The rooted seedlings were cultured for approximately 30 days and then used for quantitative trait measurement, TSC determination, and TR4 inoculation assays.

Determination of TSC in banana tissue-cultured seedlings

Samples of leaves, corms and fibrous roots were collected from rooted banana tissue-cultured seedlings, rinsed thoroughly with distilled water and then oven-dried. An aliquot of 0.5–3.0 g of the dried solid samples was weighed accurately to the nearest 0.001 g. The determination of TSC in the samples was performed in accordance with the National Food Safety Standard of the People’s Republic of China for the determination of Selenium in foods (GB 5009.93–2017), using the hydride generation atomic fluorescence spectrometry method. For detailed procedures, reference was made to the published literature [21, 22]. The translocation factor (TF) and bioconcentration factor (BCF) were calculated using the following formulas.

All results were retained to two decimal places.

Method for inoculating banana tissue-cultured seedlings with GFP-TR4

The GFP-TR4 preserved at -80 °C were revived and streaked onto PDA (potato dextrose agar) solid medium [84]. To facilitate their robust growth, the inoculated plates were incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 5–7 days until the medium was fully covered with mycelia. After the mycelia reached the desired growth stage, vigorously growing mycelial plugs were picked and transferred onto the fresh PDA plates for further propagation. The sterilized filter paper discs (approximately 0.5 cm in size) were placed around the mycelia and incubated until the discs were completely overgrown with mycelia, which were then ready for pathogen inoculation assays.

Sample collection method for laser confocal microscopy and detection of TR4 infection

Uniformly developed rooted banana plantlets with roots extending approximately 5 cm beyond the medium were selected. The root surfaces were slightly pricked with a syringe needle to create wounds, and the mycelium-covered filter paper discs were immediately attached tightly to the wounded sites. Each treatment included 3 biological replicates. The inoculated plantlets were transferred back to the tissue culture room and cultured continuously under the original conditions for subsequent experimental sampling.

At three different time points (1 dpi, 3 dpi and 5 dpi), root tissues were collected from banana plants treated with distilled water and Se, respectively, for sectioning and observation, to monitor the dynamic changes in the infection response process of plantlets to GFP-TR4 under different treatments. For root tissue sectioning, root segments of approximately 1 cm in length were taken from between the inoculation sites. The selected tissues were rinsed thrice with sterile water to ensure thorough cleaning. Using an ultrafine blade, the tissues were sliced longitudinally into sections with a thickness of 60–70 μm and immediately placed onto glass slides. An appropriate amount of sterile water was added dropwise, followed by careful placement of coverslips. The slides were then stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C for subsequent use. For each treatment and time point, root systems from three GFP-TR4-inoculated banana plants were selected as samples. Observations were performed using a laser confocal microscope manufactured by Leica (Germany). The green fluorescence of GFP-TR4 was visualized under excitation light at 488 nm, while the autofluorescence of plant cell walls was observed under excitation light at 633 nm.

Determination of physiological and biochemical indices of banana tissue-cultured seedlings at different time points after TR4 inoculation

Plants from different treatment groups were sampled at 1, 3, and 5 dpi with GFP-TR4. The activities of SOD, POD, CAT, PPO and GSH-Px, and the content of GSH were determined separately. The dynamic response trends of antioxidant enzyme activities, glutathione content, and glutathione peroxidase activity during TR4 infection were monitored to clarify the correlation between these physiological responses and Se-induced disease resistance in banana tissue-cultured seedlings. Each treatment group included three biological replicates, and the detection and calculation methods were performed according to the references [21, 22].

Proteomic detection and analysis of banana tissue-cultured seedlings

First, proteins were extracted from rooted banana seedlings at 5 dpi in four groups, such as control (BC), Se-treated (BS), TR4-inoculated (BT), and Se+TR4-inoculated (BST). Subsequently, protein concentration determination and sample purification were performed to remove interfering substances. Then, peptide samples were prepared, followed by peptide desalting and quantification. The above protein experimental procedures were performed as described in Ming et al. (2022) [49]. Finally, equal amounts of peptides were dissolved in mass spectrometry loading buffer and analyzed using DIA. Peptide separation was performed by nano-flow liquid chromatography on an µPAC High Throughput column (75 μm × 5.5 cm, Fisher Scientific, USA) using a Vanquish Neo system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with an 8 min gradient. Mobile phase A consisted of 2% acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid), and mobile phase B consisted of 80% acetonitrile (containing 0.1% formic acid). The gradient program was as follows: 0–0.1 min, 4–8% B; 0.1–1 min, 8–12.5% B; 1–1.1 min, 12.5–12.6% B; 1.1–3.1 min, 12.6–22.5% B; 3.1–4.6 min, 22.5–45% B; 4.6–5 min, 45–99% B; 5–6.5 min, 99% B. The eluted peptides were analyzed by an Astral mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) operated in positive ion mode. The ion source voltage was set to 1.5 kV, and data were acquired in DIA mode over a scan range of 100–1700 m/z. Data acquisition was controlled by Thermo Xcalibur 4.7 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Using protein abundance information obtained from database searching against the Musa acuminata AAA Group genome in the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/?bioproject=PRJNA957115), statistical test analysis of DEPs were carried out. Student’s t-test was used to calculate the statistical significance (adjusted p-value) of differences between samples. Proteins with adjusted p-value < 0.05 and a fold change > 1.5 were identified as DEPs.

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) functional annotation

All DEPs were functionally annotated using gene ontology (GO) (http://www.blast2go.com/b2ghome; http://geneontology.org/) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway annotation. The metabolic pathways involving DEPs were analyzed using the KEGG pathways database (http://www.genome.jp/kegg//). GO terms and KEGG pathways with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed to systematically evaluate different comparison groups, aiming to comprehensively identify protein sets with potential biological functions at the global protein expression level. A network modeling approach was used to construct protein-protein interaction (PPI) network. Furthermore, a topological property analysis of the network was conducted, and PPI clusters with a composite score > 200 were selected for visualization using Gephi software.

RNA extraction and gene expression analysis by RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from frozen banana seedlings using the RNAprep Pure Plant Plus Kit (Polysaccharides & Polyphenolics-rich) (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Qualified RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the FastKing RT Kit (with gDNase) (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from 2 µg of DNase-treated total RNA in a final reaction volume of 20 µL. qPCR was performed using the SuperReal PreMix Plus (SYBR Green) kit (Tiangen Biotech Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) with three biological replicates. The banana qTUB gene was used as an internal reference for data normalization [85]. Target genes were amplified using the primer pairs listed in Table S3. The relative transcript abundance of each gene was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method [86].

Data analysis

Data regarding quantitative traits, enzyme activities, TSC and relative expression levels of mRNAs were statistically analyzed using Microsoft Excel software. Student’s t-test was applied to examine the significance of differences among various treatment groups, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. Finally, graphs were plotted using Origin 2021 and GraphPad software.
