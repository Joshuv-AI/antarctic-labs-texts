Mortality After Cardiovascular Surgery and Procedures During the COVID-19 Pandemic in Ontario, Canada: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Yuchen Dai, Anna Chu, J. Michael Paterson, Xuesong Wang, Maneesh Sud, Stephen Fremes, Joshua G. Lee, Louise Y. Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCOVID-19 disrupted global healthcare delivery, but its impact on cardiovascular procedure volumes and outcomes remains unclear. We compared rates and outcomes of coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), isolated open valve (iValve) surgery, combined CABG and valve, other cardiac surgeries, transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) before and during the pandemic.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study used ICES health administrative data to identify all adults (aged ≥ 18 years) with Ontario Health Insurance Plan coverage who underwent cardiac surgery, TAVI, or PCI between April 2018 and March 2022. The primary outcomes—procedure rates and 30-day all-cause mortality—were compared between the pre-COVID period (April 1, 2018-March 31, 2020) and the COVID period (April 1, 2020-March 31, 2022), the latter using multivariable logistic regression. Secondary outcomes included hospital length of stay and 30-day readmission.ResultsA total of 41,511 cardiac surgeries, 7138 TAVIs, and 86,549 PCIs were analyzed. During the COVID period, procedure rates declined for CABG (−18.8%), iValve (−19.3%), and PCI (−14.7%), whereas TAVI rates increased by 21.4%. Adjusted 30-day mortality increased for CABG (1.92 [95% confidence interval [CI] 1.69-2.15] to 2.25 [95% CI 2.01-2.49] per 100 procedures; P = 0.043), iValve (2.25 [95% CI 1.78-2.71] to 3.29 [95% CI 2.76-3.82]; P = 0.016), and PCI (2.41 [95% CI 2.28-2.54] to 2.74 [95% CI 2.60-2.88]; P < 0.001), but remained stable for TAVI. One excess death during the COVID period occurred per 303 CABGs, 96 iValves, and 303 PCIs.ConclusionsAlthough CABG, iValve, and PCI volumes declined and 30-day mortality increased during the pandemic, the absolute number of additional deaths remained small.

The COVID-19 pandemic disrupted the delivery of healthcare services globally, as resources and hospital priorities shifted toward preserving capacity for managing patients with severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) infection and minimizing transmission.1 Several Canadian provinces, including Ontario, implemented health policy countermeasures in March 2020 to halt elective procedures.2 For patients awaiting cardiac care, this change resulted in delays in diagnosis3 and a subsequent decline in referrals for cardiac surgeries and other commonly performed cardiovascular procedures.4 Similar reductions were reported globally, including a 38% decrease in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction activations in the US5 and a 40% reduction in Spain.6 However, the specific impact of these disruptions on cardiovascular surgeries and procedures remains unclear and may provide valuable insights into resource prioritization during future healthcare system interruptions. This study aimed to compare the rates of commonly performed cardiovascular surgeries and procedures, as well as 30-day all-cause mortality and readmission, before vs during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of adult patients in Ontario, Canada using health administrative data from ICES (www.ices.on.ca). Under Ontario’s health information privacy law, ICES is authorized to collect and analyze health and demographic data without consent for health system evaluation and improvement. Ontario is Canada’s most populous province with a population of 15 million, with all residents covered by a universal healthcare system.

Eligible patients were identified from the CorHealth Ontario Registry as having undergone cardiac surgery, transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI), or percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) between April 1, 2018 and March 31, 2022. CorHealth is an advisory body to the Ministry of Health (MOH) that maintains a centralized registry with demographic, clinical, and perioperative data on all Ontarians who undergo cardiovascular procedures.7 Registry data have been validated through selected chart audits.8,9 Included are eligible Ontario residents aged ≥ 18 years. For patients who underwent multiple eligible cardiac procedures within a fiscal year, only the first procedure was included, to avoid double-counting staged or repeat interventions and better reflect patient-level incidence rather than procedural frequency.

The exposure was the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, officially declared by the World Health Organization on March 11, 2020, and coinciding with the MOH’s directive to postpone elective surgeries and procedures on March 15, 2020.2 We defined the “pre-COVID period” as April 1, 2018 to March 31, 2020, and the “COVID period” as April 1, 2020 to March 31, 2022, based on fiscal quarters. Procedures were categorized as isolated coronary artery bypass graft surgery (iCABG), isolated open valve surgery (iValve), combined CABG with open valve surgery, other cardiac surgery (eg, aortic surgery, pericardiectomy, septal defect closure, arrhythmia surgery), TAVI, and PCI. Procedure classification was confirmed through linkage with the Canadian Institute for Health Information (CIHI) Discharge Abstract Database (DAD), which contains discharge records for all acute care hospitalizations in Ontario. If a procedure could not be confirmed, it was excluded from the analyses.

Baseline characteristics not captured in the CorHealth Ontario Registry were supplemented by ICES data,10 including the Registered Persons Database for sociodemographic and vital status information, the CIHI DAD, and the National Ambulatory Care Reporting System (NACRS) for clinical history (eg, stroke, transient ischemic attack, PCI, CABG, liver disease, peripheral vascular and/or artery disease, cardiac arrhythmias, frailty, and preoperative interventions), and the Ontario Laboratories Information System (OLIS) for laboratory values (eg, hematocrit, platelets, and leukocytes). The NACRS captures data from emergency and institution-based ambulatory care visits, whereas OLIS compiles lab results from hospital and community laboratories across Ontario.

Baseline comorbidities were assessed using Deyo–Charlson Comorbidity Index scores derived from hospital and emergency visit records in the 5 years preceding the index procedure.11 Wait times were calculated as the number of days between the procedure date and the most recent cardiologist visit, based on Ontario Health Insurance Plan (OHIP) physician claims (within 365 days). If this number was unavailable, the date of the most recent angiogram (from CorHealth) or internist visit (within 365 days) was used.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were procedure rates before vs during the pandemic and 30-day all-cause postoperative mortality. Deaths were ascertained using the Registered Persons Database. Secondary outcomes included hospital length of stay and 30-day all-cause readmission, identified through the DAD.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics between periods were compared using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with values < 0.10 considered negligible. Overall, sex- and age-stratified procedure rates were calculated quarterly as the number of procedures per 100,000 population. Population sizes were obtained from Statistics Canada’s 2016 and 2021 census data and from intercensal and postcensal population estimates determined by the MOH and the Ministry of Long-Term Care: IntelliHealth. Quarterly population denominators were derived by interpolation. To account for temporal changes in population characteristics, we also directly age- and sex-standardized rates to the April-June 2018 population (the first study quarter), and the change in rates, pre- vs during COVID, was calculated as the difference in the average rates during the 2 periods.

Mortality and readmission rates were reported as crude and adjusted per 100 people. For adjusted rates, we used model-based risk-adjustment, a commonly performed method to adjust for differences in patient characteristics when studying healthcare outcomes across populations (Supplemental Appendix S1).12 Specifically, we performed logistic regression for each period separately, adjusting for sociodemographic and clinical covariates predictive of mortality and readmission (Supplemental Appendix S2).10,13 Based on the model, the expected number of patients with the outcome was calculated as the sum of the predicted probabilities for all patients. The adjusted rate was then calculated as the ratio of the observed to expected number of patients, with the outcome multiplied by the combined pre-COVID and during-COVID outcome rate.12 Mean length of stay was calculated quarterly and annually. In instances in which rates during COVID were higher than those during pre-COVID, the number needed to harm was calculated as the inverse of the absolute difference in mortality between periods. All datasets were linked using unique encoded identifiers and were analyzed at ICES using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). A 2-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Study reporting

The study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Supplemental Table S1).14

Results

In the pre-COVID and COVID periods, 41,511 patients underwent cardiac surgery, 7138 underwent TAVI, and 86,549 underwent PCI. Baseline characteristics were similar between the pre-COVID and COVID periods in terms of age, sex, and comorbidities (Table 1), with SMD < 0.10 for all variables except TAVI wait time, which decreased during the COVID period (SMD = 0.29). Fewer than 0.6% of patients were COVID-positive during their admission. The urgency of admission, and clinical acuity at presentation, did not differ between groups. The relative proportions of urgent and/or emergent vs elective procedures were also similar between periods for all procedures except TAVI (SMD < 0.10), indicating that no meaningful shift occurred in Cardiac surgery rates declined by 17.4% during the COVID period compared to the pre-COVID period, including decreases of 18.8% for iCABG, 19.3% for iValve, 21.5% for combined CABG with open valve surgery, and 4.1% for other cardiac surgeries (Figs. 1 and 2). PCI rates fell by 14.7%, driven by declines in both urgent (11.9%) and elective (29.4%) cases (Fig. 3). Among aortic valve interventions, the TAVI rate increased by 21.4%, whereas surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) and combined CABG with aortic valve replacement (AVR) rates declined by 31.7% and 25.8%, respectively (Fig. 4). By the final fiscal quarter of the study (January-March 2022), procedure rates had not returned to pre-pandemic levels. Compared to the same quarter pre-COVID (January-March 2020), cardiac surgery rates per 100,000 population were still down by 24.8%, including 23.9% for iCABG, 30.2% for iValve, 39.6% for SAVR, 20.0% for combined CABG and open valve, 21.2% for combined CABG with AVR, and 24.3% for other cardiac surgeries. Crude 30-day all-cause mortality rates were significantly higher during the COVID period for most procedures, except SAVR (P = 0.18), combined CABG and open valve surgery (P = 0.78), and TAVI (P = 0.89; Table 2). No differences were observed for combined CABG and AVR (P = 0.41) or other cardiac surgeries (P = 0.41). These findings remained consistent after adjusting for sociodemographic and clinical factors. Among aortic valve interventions, the adjusted 30-day mortality rates per 100 persons during COVID were 2.26 (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.57-2.95) for SAVR, 3.19 (95% CI 2.12-4.26) for combined CABG and AVR, and 1.82 (95% CI 1.42-2.22) for TAVI. Quarterly age- and sex-standardized cardiac procedure rates declined sharply at the onset of the COVID-19 period, with the largest reductions occurring among older patients and for surgical interventions (Supplemental Figs. S1-S5). In patients aged < 50 years, declines were modest and were limited to elective PCI, whereas those aged 50-74 years experienced substantial drops in CABG and elective PCI, with only partial recovery. Patients aged ≥ 75 years had persistent reductions in the number of surgical procedures, whereas TAVI rates increased above pre-pandemic levels. Overall, pandemic-related disruptions disproportionately affected older adults, and surgical interventions with less-invasive procedures showed better recovery.

Across the study period, male patients underwent substantially more cardiac surgeries than did female patients for all procedure types. Isolated CABG was the most common surgery in male patients, whereas female patients had lower rates across all categories. Both genders showed an increasing trend in TAVI procedures, reflecting broader adoption over time.

Discussion

In Ontario, Canada’s most populous province, rates of cardiac surgery and PCI declined during the COVID-19 pandemic and remained below pre-pandemic levels 2 years later. These system-wide reductions should be interpreted within the broader disruption of the cardiovascular care pathway during this period. Because PCI, TAVI, and cardiac surgery were all subject to the provincial suspension of elective procedures, we evaluated them within a shared framework while analyzing outcomes separately by procedure type. Although reduced diagnostic testing may have contributed to fewer elective interventions being performed, declines were also observed in urgent PCI, whereas TAVI volumes increased, suggesting that procedural prioritization and resource constraints—rather than diagnostic disruption alone—shaped procedural trends. Despite similar patient characteristics, crude and adjusted 30-day all-cause mortality rates following iCABG, iValve, and PCI were significantly higher during the pandemic. However, the absolute increase in deaths was small for iCABG and PCI, and modest for iValve (1.0%). In contrast, TAVI volumes continued to rise in line with pre-pandemic trends, with no significant change in 30-day mortality. Length of stay and 30-day readmission rates were unchanged, and fewer than 0.6% of patients were COVID-positive during admission. Given the very small number of infected patients— ≤ 12 in most procedural subgroups—we were unable to meaningfully evaluate outcomes in this subset, suggesting that direct infection was unlikely to account for the observed mortality differences. These findings suggest that factors beyond patient acuity—such as delays in diagnosis before waitlisting, or broader systemic disruptions— may be associated with the observed increase in mortality. Unlike prior studies that linked a higher mortality rate to greater acuity or longer wait times,4,15 our analysis found no significant differences in baseline risk, including comorbidities, wait times, or urgency of admission. Consistent with this finding, the distribution of procedural urgency remained largely unchanged between periods, suggesting that differences in short-term mortality were unlikely to be explained by a shift toward a higher-acuity operative case mix. The slight rise in mortality rate despite comparable clinical profiles underscores the wider impact of the pandemic on timely cardiac care delivery. Given that this study is observational, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of a causal relationship between pandemic-related healthcare disruptions and postoperative mortality.

Age- and sex-stratified analyses provided additional context for procedural trends during the pandemic. TAVI utilization increased with advancing age, whereas SAVR and combined CABG with AVR declined among older patients, consistent with a shift toward less-invasive therapies in populations at higher procedural risk.16 Across both revascularization and aortic valve interventions, procedural rates were consistently lower in female patients than in male patients, reflecting well-described sex differences in cardiovascular care utilization.9 Despite these baseline differences, pandemic-related trends appeared directionally similar between women and men, suggesting that system-level disruptions affected procedural access broadly across sexes. These findings highlight the importance of continued monitoring to ensure that existing disparities in cardiac care are not amplified during periods of healthcare system strain.

Upstream delays in cardiovascular disease diagnosis during the COVID-19 pandemic have been well-documented in both Canadian and international cohorts.3,17 Government restrictions on elective procedures disrupted referral pathways, leading to reduced outpatient testing and risk stratification, particularly for coronary artery disease.18 These disruptions contributed to delayed diagnoses and, as our data show, reduced cardiac surgery and procedural volumes. A global survey of 909 centres, including Canadian sites, reported a 64% overall decline in cardiac diagnostic procedures from March 2019 to April 2020, including a 78% drop in noninvasive stress testing and a 55% decrease in coronary angiography.18 Consequently, patients referred for cardiac interventions during the pandemic may have presented later in their disease course, which may be associated with a higher observed mortality rate.

A higher postprocedural mortality rate during COVID-19 may reflect logistical barriers to timely care. A study from the University of Ottawa Heart Institute reported a 44-minute increase in median symptom-to-balloon time for patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction undergoing primary PCI during lockdown, raising concerns, as delayed presentation is associated with larger infarcts, higher rates of heart failure, and worse long-term outcomes.19, 20, 21 Contributing factors included limited emergency medical services, delays from personal protective equipment protocols, slower bed turnover due to isolation measures, and reduced capacity at PCI centres overwhelmed with COVID-19 cases.22 Additionally, the sharp decline in acute coronary syndrome hospitalizations across Canada likely underestimates the pandemic's true impact, as many affected persons—referred to as “vanishing patients” by Tam et al.4—avoided seeking care due to fear of contracting COVID-19 and stay-at-home orders. These systemic pressures likely contributed to the observed trends. Both PCI and iCABG volumes declined during the pandemic (by 14.7% and 18.8%, respectively), each with a 0.3% rise in 30-day mortality. However, PCI was associated with a significantly shorter hospital stay (3.8 vs 12.7 days), which may have made it the preferred revascularization option when feasible, to conserve resources and minimize inpatient time.

TAVI rates continued to rise during the pandemic, maintaining their pre-pandemic trajectory in contrast to declines in SAVR and combined CABG with AVR. Like PCI, TAVI was associated with a shorter hospital stay (6.3 days) compared with SAVR (10.9 days) and combined CABG with AVR (13.7 days), along with reduced wait times and a higher proportion of urgent admissions and cardiogenic shock presentations. Despite these shifts, adjusted 30-day mortality for TAVI remained stable, supporting its ongoing safety and efficacy. Several factors may explain these trends. Procedure volumes observed in early 2022 may not reflect a complete postpandemic recovery, as COVID-19-related disruptions were still ongoing. In addition, the observed increase in TAVI utilization likely reflects expanded access and evolving clinical practice, including indications extending to lower surgical risk patients,16 rather than a direct pandemic effect. The shorter length of stay associated with TAVI may have further favoured its selection when both TAVI and SAVR were clinically appropriate, easing pressure on hospital resources and minimizing infection risk. Moreover, several Canadian centres adopted streamlined TAVI pathways—incorporating remote assessments, virtual multidisciplinary discussions, rapid computed tomography screening, expedited implantation, and early discharge—that optimized care delivery during the pandemic.23,24 Collectively, these shifts toward percutaneous therapies may have contributed to a concomitant decline in isolated surgical valve procedures independent of COVID-19-related factors, and procedure patterns may continue to evolve beyond the study period. Further research is warranted to assess the impact and scalability of these models beyond the pandemic.

The 30-day all-cause readmission rates declined across all procedures during COVID-19, although only PCI showed a statistically significant reduction. This finding aligns with reports of a higher proportion of same-sitting PCI procedures being performed during the pandemic in Ontario—likely to shorten hospital stays and reduce the need for repeat visits.4 Similar trends were observed for TAVI.23,24 Additionally, the expanded use of virtual care—including remote assessments, follow-ups, rehabilitation, and monitoring—may have contributed to reduced readmissions and warrants continued support and evaluation.25

Beyond explaining these trends, our findings offer insight into how cardiovascular care delivery may be better preserved during future system disruptions. The increase in mortality despite similar patient risk profiles suggests that delays earlier in the care pathway—particularly in diagnosis and referral—can have important downstream consequences. Crisis planning should therefore prioritize preservation of diagnostic capacity and timely evaluation for high-risk cardiovascular conditions, even when elective procedural volumes must be reduced. The stability of outcomes for shorter-stay, less-invasive procedures, such as TAVI and PCI, further highlights the value of streamlined care pathways, virtual assessment models, and procedural prioritization strategies that maintain access while minimizing resource use. Together, these approaches may help reduce unintended inequities in access to time-sensitive cardiac care during periods of constrained health-system capacity.

These findings should be interpreted in the context of limitations. Although consistent with other Canadian and provincial registries,26 the data reflect Ontario’s single-payer system, encompassing both urban and rural centres, which may limit generalizability to other health systems. Second, this study captures only periprocedural mortality, likely underestimating the true impact. The so-called “vanishing patients”—those who may have died before receiving intervention or who were outside the perioperative window were not captured in our analysis. Postprocedural mortality data lacked cause-of-death information, and we were unable to assess patients’ COVID-19 infection history or vaccination status—factors known to affect cardiovascular outcomes.27 Notably, fewer than 0.6% of patients were COVID-positive at the time of their procedure. We were unable to fully account for heterogeneity in pandemic-related disruptions across individual surgical centres, which may have varied according to regional COVID-19 burden, local resource constraints, and institutional policies. Although we were able to examine differences by age and sex, we could not assess variation by socioeconomic status or urban vs rural residence because reliable yearly population denominators at these levels were not available, precluding comprehensive rate-based comparisons over time. Changes in short-term mortality during the pandemic may in part reflect unmeasured differences in clinical severity at presentation; although cardiogenic shock was captured, it likely represents only the most extreme acuity, and more granular indicators of illness severity were not available in the data sources used. Finally, as the study is observational, causality between pandemic-related changes and trends in procedure rates or outcomes cannot be determined.

In summary, CABG, iValve, and PCI volumes declined during the COVID-19 pandemic in Ontario and remained below pre-pandemic levels 2 years later. Although the 30-day mortality rate rose, the absolute increase was modest—0.3% for iCABG and PCI, and 1.0% for iValve. These trends, along with emerging evidence on the long-term cardiovascular effects of COVID-19, highlight the need for ongoing monitoring of cardiac procedure outcomes. Future research should also examine nonmortality measures, including hospitalizations, emergency visits, and procedural ineligibility due to delayed care.
