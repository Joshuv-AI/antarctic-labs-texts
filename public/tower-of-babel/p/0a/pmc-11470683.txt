Spinster homolog 2 (SPNS2) deficiency drives endothelial cell senescence and vascular aging via promoting pyruvate metabolism mediated mitochondrial dysfunction
Authors: Haojun Tang, Pan Gao, Weng Peng, Xiaodan Wang, Zhenbo Wang, Weiqian Deng, Kai Yin, Xiao Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Endothelial cell (EC) senescence and vascular aging are important hallmarks of chronic metabolic diseases. An improved understanding of the precise regulation of EC senescence may provide novel therapeutic strategies for EC and vascular aging-related diseases. This study examined the potential functions of Spinster homolog 2 (SPNS2) in EC senescence and vascular aging. We discovered that the expression of SPNS2 was significantly lower in older adults, aged mice, hydrogen peroxide-induced EC senescence models and EC replicative senescence model, and was correlated with the expression of aging-related factors. in vivo experiments showed that the EC-specific knockout of SPNS2 markedly aggravated vascular aging by substantially, impairing vascular structure and function, as evidenced by the abnormal expression of aging factors, increased inflammation, reduced blood flow, pathological vessel dilation, and elevated collagen levels in a naturally aging mouse model. Moreover, RNA sequencing and molecular biology analyses revealed that the loss of SPNS2 in ECs increased cellular senescence biomarkers, aggravated the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), and inhibited cell proliferation. Mechanistically, silencing SPNS2 disrupts pyruvate metabolism homeostasis via pyruvate kinase M (PKM), resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction and EC senescence. Overall, SPNS2 expression and its functions in the mitochondria are crucial regulators of EC senescence and vascular aging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-024-01859-5.

Introduction

Aging-related vascular dysfunction plays a crucial role in the progression of aging-related diseases, such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, Alzheimer’s disease, and chronic kidney disease. With an increasing in aging population and life expectancy, these aging-related diseases contribute significantly to the global healthcare burden [1–3]. Although drugs such as rapamycin, senolytics, and metformin treat aging-related vascular diseases by preventing or slowing vascular senescence, the existing literature differs in terms of adverse effects and clinical efficacy [4]. Endothelial cells (ECs) are important components of blood vessels and are susceptible to pathogenic stimuli that lead to cellular senescence. Senescent ECs exhibit an aberrant morphology with a highly active, secretory, and pro-inflammatory phenotype that impairs the integrity of the vascular endothelium and contributes to vascular aging and aging-related diseases [5]. Therefore, it is important to elucidate the mechanisms underlying EC and vascular aging in order to prevent and alleviate senescence.

Spinster homologue 2 (SPNS2) is a member of the major facilitator superfamily of transporters and is mainly expressed in ECs (vascular, lymphatic, and pulmonary ECs). It is responsible for the export of sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P) and non-natural S1P analogues, such as the clinical drug FTY720. Research over the past decade has uncovered the function of SPNS2 in regulating postnatal neuroinflammation [6], postnatal retinal morphogenesis [7], inflammatory and autoimmune diseases [8, 9], cancer metastasis [10, 11], vascular development and maturation [12], and hearing loss [13, 14]. Moreover, the discovery of the structure of SPNS2 protein in multiple functionally relevant states has increased our understanding of its function and may aid in the development of SPNS2 inhibitors and drugs [15]. However, the effect of SPNS2 on EC senescence has not yet been examined.

Mitochondria are the primary energy-generating and metabolic systems in most cells. As highly sensitive subcellular organelles, mitochondria constantly undergo remodelling via fusion, fission, and metabolic changes in response to cellular and environmental cues [16]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that abnormal mitochondrial dynamics, characterised by respiratory dysfunction, metabolic disorders, disrupted membrane potential, and dynamic dysregulation, contribute significantly to EC senescence, eventually leading to aging-related diseases [17–19]. The metabolism of pyruvate, an important intermediate that drives mitochondrial adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production via oxidative phosphorylation and citrate cycling, is controlled by several enzymes including pyruvate kinase, mitochondrial pyruvate carrier, pyruvate dehydrogenase, and pyruvate carboxylase. Mutations in the genes encoding proteins that regulate pyruvate metabolism can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction [20]. Based on these findings, we hypothesised that SPNS2 mediates EC senescence, partly via pyruvate metabolism-mediated mitochondrial homeostasis. To test this hypothesis, we first confirmed a negative correlation between SPNS2 and vascular aging in various aging models, including a population with an aging-related disease (atherosclerosis), aging mice, and an H2O2-induced human umbilical vein endothelial cell (HUVEC) senescence model. Transcriptional RNAseq was performed to assess the function of SPNS2 in HUVECs. The results revealed that cellular senescence, cell cycle, tumour protein 53 (P53), and other senescence-related pathways were enriched in the absence of SPNS2. These results were further validated using molecular biological approaches. Furthermore, we aimed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying SPNS2 deficiency-mediated HUVEC senescence and found that pyruvate kinase M-mediated pyruvate metabolism and mitochondrial dysfunction were major contributors of this pathological process. Finally, we assessed the role of SPNS2 in vascular aging using EC-specific SPNS2-knockout mice. Our results highlight the role of SPNS2 in aggravating EC senescence and vascular aging and provide a novel target for treating aging-related diseases.

Results

Reduced SPNS2 expression in aging vasculature and senescent ECs and its negative correlation with aging

To investigate the role of SPNS2 in vascular aging, we first searched the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database for SPNS2 expression in populations with and without atherosclerosis (an aging-related disease). We found lower SPNS2 mRNA expression in the vasculature of the population with atherosclerosis than in the population without atherosclerosis (Fig. 1A). Next, we examined the relationship between SPNS2 expression and blood vessel aging in naturally aged mice (aged 4, 10, 16, and 22 months). The results showed that as the mouse age (in months) increased, the expression of the aging marker genes including the cell proliferation antigen Ki-67 (KI67) and nuclear membrane protein B1(Lamin B1) was decreased at mRNA and protein level. We then performed immunofluorescence detection of Lamin B1 and endothelial cell marker Platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (CD31) in mouse aortic sections, and the decrease of LaminB1 was confirmed by immunofluorescence, indicating successful construction of the aging model (Fig. 1B and D). Meanwhile, the level of SPNS2 gradually decreased in vascular ECs with age (Fig. 1C and D). Notably, negative correlations were observed between SPNS2, KI67 and Lamin B1 (Fig. 1E). Moreover, an association between SPNS2 and aging has been confirmed at the cellular level. We created two cellular senescence models: one model induced by hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 200 μmol/L) for 48 h, and the other is a replicative senescence model (with passage 4 as young HUVECs and passage 13 as old HUVECs). The β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) assay showed that the number of senescence-positive HUVECs increased markedly after H2O2 treatment and in the replicative senescence model (Fig. 1F, S1A). Western blotting revealed that the expression of P53 increased, whereas that of Lamin B1 decreased significantly after H2O2 treatment and in the replicative senescence model (Fig. 1G, S1B), indicating the HUVEC senescence model was successfully established. Notably, lower protein and mRNA levels of SPNS2 were observed in the two HUVEC senescence model (Fig. 1H-I, S1C-D). Collectively, these results indicated the potential role of SPNS2 in vascular aging and aging-related diseases.

SPNS2 knockout in mice ECs aggravates vascular dysfunction and collagen deposition

To further examine the function of SPNS2 in endothelial senescence and vascular aging, we generated SPNS2TEK−/− mice, in which SPNS2 was selectively ablated in ECs by crossing TEK-Cre mice with SPNS2flox/flox mice; their littermate controls were referred to as SPNS2flox/flox (Figure S2A). Immunofluorescence and qRT-PCR shown that the expression of SPNS2 in the vasculature was decreased in SPNS2TEK−/− mice, indicating that an endothelial SPNS2 deletion mouse model was successfully constructed (Fig. 2A-B). These SPNS2TEK−/− and SPNS2flox/flox mice were fed with regular diets for 22 weeks to establish ageing model. Meanwhile, immunofluorescence detected a decrease tendency of Lamin B1 in mouse aortic slices after SPNS2 knockout (Fig. 2A). By qRT-PCR, we also found that the mRNA level of KI67 and Lamin B1 decreased, whereas the mRNA level of p21 increased in the SPNS2TEK−/− mice (Figure S2B). Along with these changes in the aging markers, the mRNA level of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors including interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8), and matrix metalloproteinase 3 (MMP3) was higher in SPNS2TEK−/− mice vasculature (Fig. 2C). In addition, structural and functional changes in the aorta, referred to as aging-related vascular remodelling and vascular dysfunction, respectively, have also been observed. We used a small-animal diagnostic ultrasound system to measure the abdominal aortic structure and found that the inner and outer diameters increased in SPNS2TEK−/− mice (Fig. 2D). Consistent with this, blood flow velocity was reduced in SPNS2TEK−/− mice (Fig. 2E). These results indicated that the absence of SPNS2 may lead to vascular dilation and dysfunction. Masson and Sirius red staining revealed elevated collagen content in the abdominal aorta of SPNS2TEK−/− mice (Fig. 2F–G). Collectively, these results suggest that SPNS2 deficiency is closely associated with endothelial senescence during aging, which further aggravates abnormal vascular structure and function, ultimately resulting in vascular damage.

SPNS2 knockdown aggravates HUVEC senescence

To further investigate the potential role of SPNS2 in HUVEC senescence, we transfected HUVECs with Sh-SPNS2 and generated a stable SPNS2-knockdown model. Western blotting was performed to assess the effect of SPNS2 knockdown on HUVECs and revealed a significant reduction in SPNS2 protein expression (Fig. 3A). The HUVECs with SPNS2 knockdown exhibited a markedly altered morphology, as evidenced by their larger size and extensive protrusive structures (Fig. 3B). To determine the effect of SPNS2 knockdown on HUVECs, we performed a comparative transcriptome analysis of Sh-NC and Sh-SPNS2 HUVECs (Fig. 3C). The volcano plot shows that the expression of 3934 genes was upregulated and that of 3743 was downregulated following SPNS2 knockdown (Fig. 3D). Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis indicated that these genes were mainly enriched in cellular senescence, the cell cycle, P53, and DNA replication in the absence of SPNS2 (Fig. 3E), indicating that SPNS2 may play an important role in HUVEC senescence. To further validate this result, we performed a β-galactosidase assay and detected a significant increase in the number of SA-β-gal positive cells following SPNS2 knockdown compared with that in the control group (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, methylthiazolyldiphenyl-tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay revealed that the viability of HUVECs decreased within 48 h of SPNS2 knockdown (Fig. 3G). Immunofluorescence results confirmed that the expression of KI67, a marker of HUVEC proliferation, was markedly reduced following SPNS2 knockdown (Fig. 3H). Western blotting revealed reduced protein expression of Lamin B1 and P53 following SPNS2 knockdown (Fig. 3I). Even the low expression of P53 in senescent cells is inconsistent with most studies, it was recently reported by another group [21]. As senescence cells are often accompanied by a SASP [22], we aimed to investigate whether the loss of SPNS2 contributed to the increased SASP of HUVECs. The results showed that following SPNS2 knockdown, the expression of SASP factors including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and MMP3 increased markedly (Fig. 3J). Moreover, we also used an SPNS2 inhibitor (SLF1081851, 200 μm, 24 h) to decrease the level of SPNS2 in HUVECs. Western blot analysis indicated a significant reduction in the protein levels of SPNS2, Lamin B1, and P53, similar to the results obtained using lentivirus. (Figure S3A). Additionally, the mRNA level of SASP makers including Il-1β, Il-6, Il-8, and MMP3 was increased in SLF1081851 group (Figure S3B). And the number of SA-β-gal positive cells exhibited a significant increase after SPNS2 inhibitor treatment (Figure S3C). In conclusion, these results suggest that low SPNS2 expression aggravates senescence in HUVECs.

Subsequently, we further investigated whether overexpression of SPNS2 could improve the senescence status of HUVECs. We created SPNS2-overexpressing HUVECs with an SPNS2-overexpressing lentivirus. Notably, accompanied by the increased expression of SPNS2, the protein levels of Lamin B1 and P53 also elevated (Fig. 3K). And the mRNA levels of Il-6, Il-1β, Il-8, and MMP3 of SASP is markedly inhibited after SPNS2-overexpressing (Figure L). These results indicate that the overexpression of SPNS2 is beneficial for improving HUVECs senescence.

SPNS2 knockdown induces HUVEC senescence by affecting mitochondrial function

Mitochondria, the energy centres of cells, play crucial roles in various cellular processes, particularly senescence [23]. We observed significant changes in mitochondrial structure and number following SPNS2 knockdown using electron microscopy and Mito-red staining on HUVECs. Cells with SPNS2 knockdown showed a significant increase in mitochondrial volume and length and a decrease in mitochondrial number, consistent with the mitochondrial characteristics in aging cells (Fig. 4A-B). Consistent with this result, the protein and mRNA level of the outer mitochondrial membrane 20 (TOM20) and voltage-dependent anion channel 1 (VDAC1), which are located in the mitochondrial outer membrane and primarily determine the integrity of the mitochondrial structure, was significantly decreased after SPNS2 knockout. Moreover, the expression of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator-1α (PGC-1α), a transcriptional coregulator that mainly regulates mitochondrial biogenesis to control mitochondrial quantity and function, was significantly reduced in the SPNS2 knockdown group (Fig. 4C, S4A). The mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ether (TMRE) fluorescence, which showed a lower membrane potential in the SPNS2 knockdown group (Fig. 4D). In addition, the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in HUVECs was markedly increased in the absence of SPNS2 (Fig. 4E). Similarly, the protein and mRNA level of heat shock protein-60 (HSP60), a signature protein that regulates mitochondrial respiratory capacity, was decreased after SPNS2 silencing (Fig. 4C, S4B). These data implied that decreased SPNS2 expression impaired mitochondrial structure and function in cells.

To further determine whether SPNS2 mediates HUVEC senescence by affecting mitochondrial function, we treated SPNS2-lacking HUVECs with N-acetylcysteine (NAC), an ROS scavenger. TMRE analysis confirmed that SPNS2-lacking HUVECs treated with NAC exhibited a higher membrane potential (Fig. 4F). Moreover, ROS production was significantly reduced following NAC treatment (Fig. 4G). NAC effectively salvaged the protein and mRNA level of TOM20, VDAC1, HSP60, and PGC-1α in HUVECs with SPNS2-knockdown (Fig. 4I, S4C). In summary, our results revealed that NAC partially restored the mitochondrial integrity and function, which were compromised following the SPNS2 knockdown. In addition, the β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) assay showed that the number of senescence-positive HUVECs that induced by Sh-SPNS2 was markedly decreased after NAC treatment (Fig. 4I). In addition, the mRNA level of senescence markers, such as KI67 and Lamin B1, and the protein level of Lamin B1 showed a decreasing trend in HUVECs after NAC treatment compared to those in HUVECs without NAC treatment (Figure S4D, 4 J). Notably, ELISA showed that the levels of IL-6, IL-1β and IL-8 in the cell culture medium were also significantly reduced after NAC treated (Fig. 4K). At the same time, qRT-PCR analysis confirmed that the expression of SASP factors, including MMP3, IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-8 also decreased markedly after NAC treatment (Figure S4E). Together, these findings demonstrate that the integrity of mitochondrial structure and function are crucial for SPNS2 silencing-mediated HUVEC senescence.

SPNS2 knockdown impairs mitochondrial function by promoting PKM-mediated dysregulation of pyruvate metabolism

Next, we examined the mechanisms underlying SPNS2-deficiency-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction. The RNA-seq data showed that SPNS2 knockdown significantly affected pyruvate metabolism (Fig. 5A). The hypoxia inducible factor-1α (HIF-1 A) pathway also exhibited marked changes, including an increase in the protein and mRNA levels of HIF-1 A with SPNS2 knockdown (Fig. 5B–C, S5A). These results suggest that SPNS2 knockdown induces hypoxia in HUVECs. Numerous studies have confirmed that pyruvate enters the mitochondria to produce acetyl-CoA under aerobic conditions, whereas it accumulates in the cytoplasm during hypoxia and is converted into ethanol or lactate [24]. Furthermore, we assessed the final changes in pyruvate metabolites and found that pyruvate levels in cells and secretion of lactic acid and ethanol into the cell culture medium increased significantly following SPNS2 knockdown (Fig. 5D–F). In contrast, the levels of ATP and acetyl-CoA were significantly decreased in HUVECs with SPNS2 knockdown (Fig. 5G–H). These results suggest that SPNS2 knockdown triggers the abnormal accumulation of pyruvate, predominantly promoting its conversion into lactate/ethanol, rather than acetyl-CoA, under hypoxic conditions. Pyruvate is a key substrate for mitochondrial respiration, and disorders in pyruvate metabolism result in a failure to maintain normal mitochondrial function. Both ethanol and lactate exhibit certain levels of toxicity, and prolonged exposure may damage mitochondrial structure and function [25]. Therefore, identifying specific molecules that regulate pyruvate metabolism following SPNS2 knockdown is crucial.

To this end, we analysed the changes expression about pyruvate metabolism-related molecules from RNA-seq date with or without SPNS2 (Fig. 5A) and validated them using qRT-PCR. The key molecules with altered expression included lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), phosphoglucomutase 1 (PGM1), mitochondrial pyruvate carrier 2 (MPC2), glycolytic enzyme enolase 2 (ENO2), hexokinase 1 (HK1), pyruvate dehydrogenase phosphatase 2 (PDP2), hexokinase 2 (HK2), phosphofructokinase-1 liver type (PFKL), and PKM. Among these, PKM, a subtype of pyruvate kinase that promotes pyruvate generation in the cytoplasm, was markedly increased in the absence of SPNS2 (Figure S5B). Therefore, we aimed to determine whether the loss of SPNS2 affects pyruvate metabolism and whether it partly relies on PKM-mediated abnormal accumulation of pyruvate. To address this, we used a PKM siRNA to inhibit PKM activity in SPNS2-deficient HUVECs (Fig. 5I). We found that, compared to SPNS2-deficient cells transfected with negative control siRNA, pyruvate levels in cells and ethanol and lactic acid released into the culture medium decreased after PKM siRNA treatment (Fig. 5J–L). These results demonstrate that the knockdown of PKM expression improves pyruvate metabolism dysfunction induced by SPNS2 knockdown. Moreover, the structure and function of mitochondria were restored in SPNS2 knockdown cells following PKM inhibition, as evidenced by the increased mRNA and protein level of TOM20, VDAC1, HSP60, and PGC-1α (Fig. 5M, S5C). Notably, ATP and acetyl-CoA levels in SPNS2 knockdown cells showed an increasing trend following PKM siRNA treatment (Fig. 5N–Q), which may partly be because of the restoration of mitochondrial function. These findings indicate that PKM is an important mediator of SPNS2 knockdown-mediated dysregulation of pyruvate metabolism and mitochondrial dysfunction.

Discussion

Aging-associated processes occur at various stages of life in organisms. Aging manifests not only at the cellular level but also at the organ system level, where the vasculature performs major functions [26, 27]. The key role of endothelial senescence in vascular aging has been demonstrated previously [28], and is strongly supported by the findings of the present study. SPNS2 is expressed at low levels in the aging vasculature and senescent ECs, and its knockdown further aggravates the hallmarks of endothelial senescence and vascular aging-associated pathologies. Our findings indicate the following sequence of SPNS2-related events: insufficient SPNS2 signalling disturbs pyruvate metabolic homeostasis and fails to maintain mitochondrial structure and function, ultimately exacerbating endothelial senescence. Endothelial dysfunction further damages the vascular elasticity and promotes collagen deposition and fibrosis, thereby leading to vascular aging. This suggests that maintaining optimal levels of SPNS2 expression in ECs during aging may have a protective effect against vascular function and age-related diseases such as atherosclerosis, diabetes, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, and osteoporosis. In addition to the role of endothelial SPNS2 in vascular aging, its function in other aging-related organs such as adipose tissue, skeletal muscles, and the liver needs to be further explored. With the identification of the cryo-EM structure of human SPNS2, further studies should focus on discovering SPNS2 agonists and inhibitors that may ameliorate vascular dysfunction-related diseases.

Cellular senescence causes irreversible cell cycle arrest in response to various cellular stress stimuli through complex interactions [29]. Numerous studies have confirmed that the activation of p53 and subsequent induction of p21 are early events in response to various cellular stress stimuli and are crucial for driving cells into a senescent state [30, 31]. We observed an expected expression pattern of some of the senescence markers including SA-β-gal, Lamin B1, and KI67 in SPNS2-deficient senescent cells. However, the expression of p53 decreased with cell senescence. This unexpected pattern of p53 expression is consistent with the results of a recent study showing that senescent cells limit p53 activity via multiple mechanisms and remain viable [21]. These findings supported the hypothesis that senescent cells actively combat apoptosis in several ways.

Mice with SPNS2 knockout in ECs exhibit impaired vasodilation to flow, dipping blood pressure, and higher blood pressure, which are related to worsened cardiac hypertrophy [32]. Consistently, we observed increased inner and outer diameters during the systolic and diastolic periods, decreased mean velocity (Vm) of the abdominal aorta, and increased wall thickness, fibrosis, and collagen deposition in naturally aging mice with SPNS2-deficient ECs. Several studies have shown that global deletion of SPNS2 impairs blood vessel integrity, indicating that SPNS2 is essential for vascular development and maturation [12]. This suggests that SPNS2 function in vascular aging may be partly attributed to this factor. Notably, SPNS2 in ECs is critical for supporting immune system function by targeting normal lymphocyte survival, development, circulation, and localisation [9, 33, 34]. Given the important regulatory role of immune system homeostasis in delaying aging [35–37], further exploration is needed to determine whether SPNS2 affects vascular aging and related diseases by affecting the immune system.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is typically accompanied by decreased respiratory capacity and membrane potential. Together with the increased production of free oxygen radicals, which are a cause and consequence of cellular senescence, mitochondrial dysfunction plays a major role in multiple feedback loops that induce and preserve the senescent phenotype [38]. Previous studies have shown that S1P, transported by SPNS2, regulates multiple mitochondrial functions, including survival [34], biogenesis [39], fission [40], assembly [41], and respiration [42]. These studies prompted us to consider whether SPNS2 affects EC senescence partly through the mitochondria. Our results demonstrated that SPNS2 silencing in vascular ECs damaged mitochondrial structure and function by increasing mitochondrial membrane potential and ROS production. Furthermore, improving mitochondrial function using ROS scavengers effectively inhibited EC senescence induced by SPNS2 knockdown. Given the dual role of SPNS2, further investigations are needed to determine whether its regulatory effect on the mitochondria depends on its S1P transport function or its function as a second messenger within the cell.

KEGG enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes revealed that the pyruvate metabolism signalling pathway was significantly enriched following SPNS2 knockdown. Additionally, the HIF-α signalling pathway was also clearly enriched in cells lacking SPNS2. Numerous studies have confirmed that pyruvate, which accumulates in the cytoplasm, is converted to ethanol or lactic acid via fermentation during hypoxia. Consistent with these findings, we observed a series of indications suggestive of anaerobic metabolism of pyruvate, such as an increase in pyruvate and lactate levels and a decrease in acetyl-CoA levels. Among the changes in the expression of the pyruvate metabolism-related genes mentioned above, the expression of PKM, the final rate-limiting enzyme in glycolysis that transfers the high-energy phosphate bonds of phosphoenolpyruvate to ADP-producing pyruvate and ATP, was markedly elevated during SPNS2 deficiency. Notably, decreased PKM activity in SPNS2 knockdown cells effectively restored pyruvate metabolism disorders and mitochondrial damage. Notably, the regulatory function of pyruvate in the mitochondria is influenced by two major factors: the cytoplasmic concentration of pyruvate [43, 44] and the levels of mitochondrial pyruvate carrier (MPC) proteins [45]. Although PKM plays a crucial role in pyruvate metabolism-mediated mitochondrial damage observed in this study, it is conceivable that changes in MPC-mediated facilitated diffusion also play a key role in this process. In addition, whether the regulatory effect of SPNS2 in PKM rely on its regulating role in the concentration of S1P inside and outside the cell or its directly acts as a second messenger to perform function will be the primary focus of our future research.

In conclusion, our study highlights that the perturbation of vascular EC homeostasis induced by the downregulation of SPNS2 is a critical driving force in vascular aging. Our data suggest that harnessing the SPNS2-aided restoration of vascular homeostasis and function may serve as a novel treatment strategy for inducing a healthier and youthful state that actively slows the progression of aging.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and reagents

HUVECs used in the experiment were purchased from Shanghai Bioleaf Biotech Co., Ltd. The cells were maintained in DMEM with 10% heat-inactivated foetal bovine serum (Sangon Biotech Shanghai Co., Ltd.) with 1% penicillin and streptomycin procured from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA) in a humidified environment with 5% CO2 at a constant temperature of 37 °C.

Mouse experiments

C57BL/6 mice used in the experiment were purchased from Jiangsu Wukong Biotechnology Co., Ltd., weighing 25–40 g. Approximately, six to seven mice were used for each experiment. Randomisation and blinding were performed in the animal studies. The expression level of SPNS2 in vascular tissues was detected in mice aged 4, 10, and 16 months All mice were housed in a pathogen-free and climate-controlled environment (22–25 °C, 40–60% humidity) with a 12 h light-dark cycle that provided free access to food and water unless stated otherwise, and all the experimental mice grew well without any underlying diseases. SPNS2flox/flox and Tek-Cre mice were purchased from Cyagen Biosciences (Wuhan, China). SPNS2flox/flox mice were crossed with Tek-Cre mice to produce the EC-specific SPNS2 knockout mice (SPNS2TEK−/−). The genotypes of the mice were determined using PCR, and all primers used for PCR are listed in Table 1. After the mice were sacrificed, their tissues were collected and immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen or soaked in a 4% fixative solution (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).

Primers for qPCR

HUVEC aging model

HUVECs were cultured in DMEM containing 10% foetal bovine serum (Sangon Biotech Shanghai Co., Ltd.), H2O2 with different concentration gradients detected using MTT toxicity test, a temperature of 37 °C, and 5% CO2 for 48 h. According to the instructions of the β-GAL detection kit (Shanghai Liquid Quality Testing Technology Co., Ltd.), we selected the concentration with the highest positive rate. And the final results showed that the concentration of HUVECs at 200 μmol/L had the highest positive rate, best senescence inducing effect and least cytotoxicity.

Cellular senescence detection by β-galactosidase assay

HUVECs were detected using a β-galactosidase kit (Shanghai Liquid Quality Testing Technology Co., Ltd.). HUVECs were washed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with a special fixative for 15 min. After washing, approximately 500 μL of staining working solution was added to each well. After incubation at 37 °C for 8 h, the cells were observed under an ordinary microscope.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from HUVECs using TRI reagent (Invitrogen Corporation) according to the instructions of the manufacturer. cDNA was synthesised via reverse transcription, and mRNA levels were examined using 2xTaq PCR Master Mix (Wuhan Huaruikang Biologic Technology Co., Ltd.). The results were normalised with β-actin, and qRT-PCR was performed using real-time fluorescent quantitative PCR (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from the HUVECs using precooled RIPA buffer (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd.). After protein quantification, an equivalent protein sample was added, separated using 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and transferred to 0.45 μL polyvinylidene fluoride (Merck Millipore, Germany). After sealing with 5% skim milk from TBST for 1.5 h, the membrane was incubated at 4 °C 10–12 h with the corresponding primary antibody SPNS2 (1:2000, GeneTex, USA), P53 (1;5000, Proteintech, UK), Lamin B1 (1:5000, Proteintech), PKM (1:1000, Proteintech), and β-actin (1:5000, Proteintech). After 12 h, the polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes were washed thrice with TBST and incubated for 2 h at 4 °C with their respective secondary antibodies: horseradish peroxidase-labelled goat anti-mouse and goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:4000, Sam Golden Bridge Biotechnology Co., Ltd, CHN). Finally, the PVDF membranes were imaged using a Molecular Imager ChemiDoc XRS + system (BioRAD, USA). The protein expressions were quantified using ImageJ software and normalised using β-actin as an internal reference.

Cell transfection and viral infection

Cells were transfected with PKM siRNA or PKM negative control siRNA (Shanghai GeneChem Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) at a final concentration of 250 nmol/L using lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (Shanghai GeneChem Co., Ltd.) according to the instructions of the manufacturer. For lentivirus infection of HUVECs, 60% confluent cells were incubated with Sh-SPNS2 and negative control lentiviruses (Shanghai GeneChem Co., Ltd.) in growth medium, followed by replacement with fresh media containing puromycin (5 μg/mL) for selection over 2 days.

MTT detection of cell viability

HUVECs were incubated with 5 mg/mL MTT solution (MedchemExpress, HY-15924) for 4 h. Dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) was added to each well, and the plate was shaken for 10 min to completely dissolve the crystals. The absorbance of each well was measured using a microplate reader (optical density measured at 490 nm). Finally, cell viability was calculated.

Immunofluorescence

HUVECs were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilised with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS, followed by overnight blocking with 2% PBA (PBS containing 2% bovine serum albumin). The cells were then incubated with the primary antibody KI67 (1:200, Affinity Biosciences Cat. No. AF0198) for 1 h at room temperature. A fluorescent-labelled secondary antibody (1:4000, Proteintech, UK) was then added and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. After washing with PBS, the nuclei were stained with an antifade mounting medium containing DAPI (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). Finally, immunofluorescence was examined using confocal microscopy.

Mouse aorta samples were fixed in a 4% formaldehyde solution, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100, sealed with 5% BSA for 1 h, and incubated overnight with the appropriate primary antibody SPNS2 (1:500, GeneTex, USA), Lamin B1 (1:500, Proteintech) and CD31(1:2000, Proteintech). Then incubation with fluorescent-labelled secondary antibodies were performed as described previously. DAPI was used to counter-stain the nuclei and images were collected by fluorescence microscope.

Electron microscopy

After collecting HUVECs, 2.5–3% glutaraldehyde fixative solution five to ten times the sample volume was added, followed by fixing with 1% osmium tetrachloride after 1 h. After rinsing five times, acetone was used for sequential dehydration, followed by infiltration and embedding with a pure embedding agent according to the concentration gradient, and Epon812 resin (curing at: 37℃, 12 h; 45℃, 12 h; and 60℃, 48 h) was injected. The sections were sliced with an ultramicrotome, stained in dark for 10–30 min and washed with double-distilled water. A transmission electron microscope (TEM; JEM-1400FLASH, JEOL) was used to collect images of the copper mesh. Each copper mesh was observed 6000 times.

Staining of organelles

To label organelles, we used MitoRed (KeyGEN BioTECH, Nanjing, China) to visualize the mitochondria and DAPI (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Shanghai, China) to stain the nuclei. Fluorescent images were taken with a Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope (plate number 1, Zeiss, Germany). The images were created using ImageJ version 1.32j software.

Tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ether (TMRE)

HUVECs were incubated with 30 nM mitochondrial membrane potential assay kit with TMRE (Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Shanghai, China) for 10 min, followed by centrifugation (300 xg). The cells were washed once with PBS, re-suspended in 400 μL PBS, and transferred to a microcentrifuge tube for analysis using flow cytometry.

Reactive oxygen

HUVECs were incubated with the ROS assay kit (Beyotime Biotech Inc), followed by centrifugation. The cells were washed once with PBS, re-suspended in 400 μL PBS, and transferred to a microcentrifuge tube for analysis using flow cytometry.

ELISA for IL-6, IL-1β and IL-8

The concentrations of IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-8 in the cell culture supernatants of HUVECs alone and HUVECs supplemented with NAC were measured using IL-6, IL-1β, and IL-8 ELISA kits (UpingBio Technology Co., Ltd, cn). The ELISA was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the sample readings were obtained at 450 nm using a microplate reader. All samples were assayed in triplicate.

Cell culture medium and HUVEC index determination

The cell culture medium and HUVECs were used to determine the activities or contents of ethanol and acetoacetyl coenzyme A (Acetoacetyl-CoA; Beijing Biosynthesis Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) according to the instructions of the manufacturer. In addition, the activities or contents of lactic acid, pyruvate and ATP in the cell culture medium and HUVECs were measured according to previously described methods (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China).

HyperDoppler image acquisition

Animals were anaesthetised with 1–2% pentobarbital sodium and placed on a temperature-controlled heating pad to maintain core body temperature. The MyLab X8 echo scanner (Esaote, Firenze, Italy) was used to measure the internal and external diameters, wall thickness, blood flow rate, and heart rate of the mouse abdominal aorta.

Histological analysis of the aorta

Abdominal aorta was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 48 h, followed by cryo-embedding. Subsequently, 6 μm aorta cryosections were stained with MASSON and Sirius Red (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) according to the instructions of the manufacturer, followed by sealing with a neutral resin. Histopathological changes were observed and photographed using an MDX10 light microscope (Guangzhou Microshot Technology Co. Ltd., Guangzhou, China).

RNA sequencing analysis

Six HUVEC samples (three samples from each group) from the Sh-NC group and Sh-SPNS2 group were sequenced using Shanghai Jikai Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The basic process is as follows: mRNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligonucleotide-attached magnetic beads. Cleavage was performed using divalent cations in the NEBNext First-Strand Synthesis Reaction Buffer (5X) at elevated temperatures. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers and M-MuLV reverse transcriptase (RNase H −). This was followed by synthesis of second-strand cDNA using DNA polymerase I and RNase H. After the 3’ end of the DNA fragment was adenylated, the NEBNext adapter with a hairpin loop structure was connected to prepare for hybridisation. Library fragments were purified using the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, MA, USA). Three microliters of USER Enzyme (NEB, USA) was then used with size-selected, adapter-ligated cDNA at 37 °C for 15 min and then at 95 °C for 5 min prior to PCR. PCR was performed using Phusion High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase, universal PCR primers, and index (X) primers. Finally, the PCR products were purified (AMPure XP System), and library quality was assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Index-encoded samples were clustered on a cBot cluster generation system using TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot HS (Illumina). After cluster generation, the library preparations were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq platform.

Statistical analyses

All experimental data in this experiment are represented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Data analysis was performed using GraphPad software, and p < 0.05 was considered as a significant difference.
