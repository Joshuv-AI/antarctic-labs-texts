Long‐Term Health and Economic Impacts of COVID‐19 Disruptions on Bowel Cancer Inequalities in Victoria, Australia: A Modelling Study
Authors: Anita Lal, Eliza Mudford, Bedasa Taye Merga, Lan Gao, Craig Sinclair, Kate Broun, Justin M. Yeung, Nikki McCaffrey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe COVID‐19 pandemic significantly disrupted colorectal cancer (CRC) diagnosis and treatment globally. Most research on long‐term impacts has focused on population‐level outcomes, overlooking disparities in under‐screened groups. This study examines the long‐term effects of COVID‐19 disruptions on health inequalities and healthcare costs in Victoria, Australia.MethodsA Markov microsimulation model (PRISM‐CRC), containing socioeconomic quintiles in Victoria, estimated health outcomes in a population aged 50–74 years from 2023–2037. The model simulated the development of CRC, considering the expected population increase in CRC cases projected by the Victorian Cancer Registry (VCR) due to COVID‐19 disruptions and counterfactual scenarios with no disruptions. The model estimated CRC case numbers, CRC deaths, and total costs of CRC screening and treatment by quintile. Screened status, stage of diagnosis, and survival were obtained using a dataset linked to patients diagnosed with CRC in 2009–2021 from the VCR.ResultsPost COVID‐19, CRC cases and mortality increased across all quintiles, with the greatest impacts in the most disadvantaged groups. Quintile 2 showed the largest relative cancer cases increase (21.7%), followed by quintile 1 (16.3%), whilst quintile 5 increased only 1.2%. CRC deaths rose 30.1% in quintile 1 and 28% in quintile 2, compared to 1.6% in quintile 5. Combined screening, diagnostic testing and treatment costs increased 21.4% in quintiles 1 and 24.8% in quintiles 2, versus 3.4% in quintile 5. Overall, the post‐COVID‐19 period intensifies the social gradient in CRC incidence, mortality, and healthcare costs.ConclusionOur study underscores the significant impact of COVID‐19 disruptions on worsening cancer inequalities. Targeted CRC screening interventions are essential to protect vulnerable populations. Enhanced demographic data from screening programs will help better understand non‐participants and groups with low participation rates.

Introduction

The impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic continues to have lasting impacts on healthcare systems worldwide due to the disruptions to routine medical services [1]. The COVID‐19 pandemic led to a substantial disruption to colorectal cancer screening and diagnosis by colonoscopy, and modification of treatment schedules in many countries [2, 3, 4, 5]. Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the world's second‐largest cause of cancer death and the third most common malignancy [6].

The reductions in bowel cancer services and hesitancy in seeking healthcare raised significant concerns about delayed diagnoses, higher rates of advanced‐stage cancers, and increased mortality [7, 8, 9]. Worthington and colleagues estimate that COVID‐related screening service decreases led to 7.4 million fewer faecal occult screens performed in 2020 globally, and without catch‐up screening, this could lead to an estimated 13,000 additional CRC cases and 7900 deaths from 2020 to 2050 [8]. A 6‐month disruption to screening was predicted to result in a relative increase in additional deaths of 1% in Australia and 0.4% in the Netherlands and Canada between 2020 and 2050, compared to undisrupted screening [10]. In the US, excess CRC‐related deaths due to COVID disruptions are estimated at 4000–7000, with 6–12 month recoveries in CRC healthcare services [11] and in the UK a 15.3%–16.6% increase in deaths is expected [9].

Early detection using faecal occult blood test (FOBT) screening significantly improves CRC survival outcomes [12]. National screening programs are available in many jurisdictions, mostly in Western countries where CRC incidence is higher, and more resources are available [13]. In Australia, the National Bowel Cancer Screening Program (NBCSP) plays an important role in reducing CRC mortality by facilitating early diagnosis and treatment. However, screening participation rates are low (40%) compared to countries with similar programs, such as the Netherlands, Denmark, Finland and Scotland that report screening rates over 50% [14].

Disparities in bowel cancer screening and subsequent health impacts were evident worldwide in low socioeconomic position (SEP) groups prior to the COVID‐19 pandemic [15, 16]. Further inequalities in re‐uptake of bowel cancer screening after the COVID‐19 pandemic have been reported for those living in the most deprived areas in countries such as England [4] and the United States [17]. However, the research to date on longer‐term impacts of missed bowel cancer screenings due to COVID‐19 has focused on population‐level impacts without considering the disparities in already under‐screened, disadvantaged groups [8, 10, 18]. The highest number of missed cancer diagnoses in 2020–2021 in Victoria is estimated by the Victorian Cancer Registry to have occurred in the most disadvantaged socioeconomic quintile due to lower participation in bowel cancer screening by more disadvantaged groups, which was worsened during the COVID‐19 pandemic [19]. However, the study does not examine the longer‐term health and economic impacts of these missed diagnoses.

Victoria was the hardest hit state of Australia by COVID‐19 in 2020 and 2021. In response, the Victorian Government implemented several policies aimed at reversing rising case numbers, including restrictions on travel to a five‐kilometre radius [20] for 7 months in 2020 and 6 months in 2021. Compared to the rest of Australia, Victorians had a higher proportion of people who delayed seeking medical help [21] [22]. Following the disruptions associated with the COVID‐19 pandemic in Victoria, there were 3732 fewer bowel cancer cases than expected in the period 2020–2021, an 11.5% decrease compared with historical trends between 2001–2019 [23]. Disruptions to routine cancer screening programs may result in missed detection of pre‐cancerous lesions, allowing progression to malignant disease over time. Diagnostic delays may also contribute to cancers being identified at more advanced stages, which are typically associated with poorer prognoses and increased mortality risk [24].

This study aims to explore the subsequent long‐term burden of bowel cancer‐related morbidity and mortality of missed and delayed screening and diagnostic testing due to COVID‐19 disruptions in Victoria on health inequalities and healthcare costs. The Victorian Cancer Registry predicts that delays in diagnosis due to COVID‐19 disruptions will lead to an overall increase in CRC cancer cases of 10% over the 15 years from 2023 to 2037 [23]. These projections were used to model the impact of interrupted bowel cancer screening and treatment, focusing on health inequalities. Specifically, we will investigate the impact on CRC cases, deaths and healthcare costs across socioeconomic groups due to later detection and more intensive treatment needs. By analysing the longer‐term health impacts and healthcare costs, this research seeks to provide insights into the consequences of pandemic‐related healthcare disruptions on underserved populations to inform future healthcare planning and policy.

Methods

Analytic Approach

We used a previously developed microsimulation model, the Priority Population Microsimulation Colorectal Cancer (PRISM‐CRC) which consists of five identically structured Markov nodes to estimate healthcare costs, CRC cases and CRC deaths across socioeconomic quintiles [25]. (See Colorectal Cancer Model section). A healthcare system perspective was used to evaluate relevant screening, diagnostic and treatment costs for CRC cancer. Cost and health outcomes were discounted at a compounding rate of 5% per annum according to guidelines for submissions of economic evaluations of pharmaceuticals in Australia [26], with a reference year of 2023 in line with the start of the modelled time‐period, over 15‐years to replicate the time period of predicted increases in cancer cases from the Victorian Cancer Registry [23].

Study Population

We used the population of adults aged 50–74 in Victoria in 2023 as a closed cohort; the age group eligible for the National Bowel Cancer Screening Program. Population data from the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) Census indicates 1,804,342 people in this age group resided in Victoria in 2021 [27]. The Index of Relative Socio‐economic Advantage and Disadvantage (IRSAD) from the Socio‐Economic Indexes for Areas (SEIFA) was used as an indicator of socioeconomic position [28]. IRSAD quintiles represent groups of individuals who live in similarly ranked areas, and depend on the socio‐economic conditions of a community or neighbourhood, such as income, education, employment, occupation and housing [28]. Each quintile is approximately 20% of the population aged 50–74, or 360,868 people. We compared quintiles without COVID‐19 disruptions to quintiles in the presence of COVID‐19 disruptions.

PRISM‐CRC Model

Overview

The PRISM‐CRC model was used to estimate the health outcomes and healthcare costs for 10,000 individuals living in Victoria for a 15‐year period from 2023–2037 compared to a scenario without the impact of COVID‐19 disruptions on bowel cancer screening and treatment [25]. This model simulates the progression of CRC by accounting for participation rates in the NBCSP and estimates health outcomes, such as CRC cases and deaths, for the impact of COVID‐19 versus a counterfactual scenario. A key distinction between NBCSP participation and non‐participation lies in the stage at which CRC is diagnosed. The simulated cohort moves through the Markov model in annual cycles until death. At the end of each cycle, individuals either transition to a different health state or remain in their current state. The model comprises three main components: (1) natural cancer development, (2) screening and (3) colonoscopy surveillance. Individuals enter the model through either the screening pathway or natural cancer development. The model uses a closed cohort.

Colorectal Cancer Model

The model assumes that CRC develops through the adenoma‐carcinoma pathway, with natural disease progression determining movement between health states. The seven health states include: disease‐free, low‐risk adenoma, high‐risk adenoma, and four CRC stages (A to D) based on the Australian Clinico‐Pathological Staging (ACPS) system, reflecting the cancer's spread [29]. (Figure S1). Like other CRC models, the model distinguishes between non‐symptomatic and symptomatically diagnosed cancers, with undiagnosed cancers progressing through stages A to D. Survival depends on the stage at diagnosis and time since diagnosis. The model also assumes that patients surviving more than 5 years are considered cancer‐free with standard mortality rates [30]. Increased risk due to family history is not included (Figure 1).

Modelled colorectal cancer screening pathway.

CRC Screening

The model simulates the patient pathway, where the NBCSP sent screening kits to individuals aged 50 to 74 years every 2 years [31] (Figure 2). A proportion of the population enters the screening pathway based on participation rates, while the remainder follows the non‐screening pathway. It is assumed participants correctly completed the FOBT kits at home. Those with negative results re‐enter the screening program and receive another kit in 2 years. Positive results lead to a GP visit, typically followed by a colonoscopy, which could result in adenoma detection, cancer detection, or no disease. For those who undergo a colonoscopy, the rates of detection are detailed in Table 1. No adverse events from colonoscopy were assumed. If an individual declines a colonoscopy, they move to the natural cancer development pathway, with symptomatic detection if cancer is present.

Screening Participation Rates

Participation rates by socioeconomic position (SEP) were calculated by matching Victorian residents' participation rates, grouped by statistical area level 2 (SA2) in 2019–2020 and 2020–2021 [37], with the ABS Index of Relative Socioeconomic Disadvantage (IRSD) decile postcodes [38].

Colonoscopy Surveillance

If an adenoma is detected during colonoscopy, the individual moves to the colonoscopy surveillance section of the model, with follow‐up colonoscopies every 5 years via GP referral. Detected adenomas are removed, and surveillance continues until age 75.

Clinical Variables

The prevalence of adenoma and CRC in the unscreened population was estimated using Victorian CRC incidence rates [40]. Adenoma and progressive adenoma incidences were back‐calculated from age‐specific CRC rates, assuming a 21‐year progression from adenoma to cancer, as derived from previous models [41, 42] and the annual transition probability of progressive adenomas developing into malignancies is 0.05 [32]. The incidence of low‐risk adenomas was calculated by subtracting progressive adenoma cases from the total adenoma incidence [43]. Since age‐specific IRSAD quintile incidence rates were unavailable, incidence rate ratios from the VCR were used to calculate CRC rates [44]. For the screened population, adenoma and CRC prevalence data were taken from Australia Institute for Health and Welfare diagnostic outcomes [33, 34, 35, 36].

Upon entering the model, participants transition between health states annually based on progression probabilities. For the unscreened population, the natural history of adenomas was modelled with three key transition probabilities: adenoma incidence, transition from low‐ to high‐risk adenomas, and from high‐risk adenomas to CRC. These transition probabilities and the CRC stage distributions were calibrated using U.S. data from Frazier et al. [32]. CRC stage diagnosis probabilities were based on the distribution of unscreened individuals, as reported in a previous bowel cancer modelling study [45]. IRSAD quintile‐specific background mortality rates were calculated by subtracting CRC mortality rates [23] from averages of male and female, index of relative socio‐economic advantage and disadvantage, and mortality rates (deciles combined into quintiles) [46]. Background mortality data can be found in the Table S1.

The model was built using TreeAge Pro software [47]. Key input parameters for the PRISM‐CRC model are summarised in Table 1.

Survival

Life expectancy was calculated based on 5‐year bowel cancer survival rates using Kaplan Meier analyses by stage and IRSAD quintile from the VCR‐linked dataset. If a participant survived more than 5 years, they are assumed to have the same mortality as the average population. Non‐CRC mortality by age was calculated by subtracting Victorian CRC mortality rates from Victorian life expectancy [23, 48]. Stage‐specific survival for 1–5 years by IRSAD quintile is detailed in Table S2.

Data Sources

A linked dataset was used for all Victorian patients diagnosed with CRC (ICD10 18–20), as recorded by the VCR. These cancer records were linked to the NBCSP and the National Death Index. The dataset contains patient records from 1 January 2009 to 31 December 2021 and groups individuals by IRSAD quintile.

Healthcare Costs

Healthcare costs were calculated by multiplying the number of CRC cases by the treatment costs, based on CRC stage from an Australian study [49]. For Stage D, where half the patients receiving chemotherapy also received bevacizumab (costing approximately $12,375), the average cost of treatment with and without bevacizumab was used. Bevacizumab dosage and duration were based on Ananda et al., with a weekly 200 mg dose over 28 weeks [49]. Detailed costs are provided in Table 2.

COVID‐19 Modelled Scenario

We used projected increases in bowel cancer rates over the period 2023–2037 from the Victorian Cancer Registry (VCR) to estimate the predicted inequalities in morbidity, mortality and healthcare costs [23] (Table 3). The VCR estimated the direction and magnitude of trends in bowel cancer incidence by age group and sex, by applying Poisson regression to pre‐COVID monthly data up to 2019 where month, sex and age group were categorical, and year was included as a continuous variable [23].

Based on Victorian Cancer Registry estimates there were 3797 missed bowel cancer diagnoses in 2020–2021, with the largest number of missing cases occurring in the most disadvantaged group (Table 3) [23]. This is equivalent to 1 year of missed incident cases for Victoria. As the most disadvantaged quintiles had the most missed cases, we proportioned a 20% increase in cases over 5 years using the percentages of missed cases by quintile in Table 3. Based on stage at diagnosis from the VCR in 2022 [23], compared to 2019, the percentage of stage A cancers decreased by 4.7%, stage B decreased by 9.5%, stage C decreased by 7.2%, and stage D increased by 1.4%. We assumed these stage shifts for the first 5 years. Using the increase in cases from Table 3, we also applied increases of 8.2% (0%–17.5%) to 20.9% (7.5%–34.2%) to cancer and adenoma incidence in the last 10 years. Using the proportions of screened and not screened by stage from 2019 we estimated the stage proportions by screening status and the resulting stage shifts (Table 1).

Counterfactual Scenario

In the counterfactual scenario, there are no COVID‐19 disruptions. CRC cases do not increase because of reductions in screening participation and delays in diagnostic follow‐ups or treatment due to pandemic‐related constraints. Individuals are assumed to have continued attending appointments without hesitation or fear of exposure to COVID‐19, and those with positive FOBT results are not expected to experience delays in follow‐up colonoscopies. Diagnostic services are assumed to operate at full capacity, ensuring the timely identification of cancers or pre‐cancerous lesions. Colorectal Cancer and adenoma incidence from 2019 were assumed for the 15‐year model period.

Uncertainty and Sensitivity Analyses

Uncertainty analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the results to key assumptions. A probabilistic multivariate uncertainty analysis, using Monte Carlo simulation, was conducted with 2000 trials of 10,000 patients each in TreeAge software [55]. Means and 95% uncertainty intervals for incremental costs are reported. Uncertainty parameters are detailed in Table 1. The model was validated against 2022 CRC statistics from the Victorian Cancer Registry [23].

Results

Colorectal Cancer Incidence

Due to COVID‐19 disruptions to bowel cancer diagnosis and treatment, the two most deprived groups of the Victorian population, IRSAD Quintile 1 and Quintile 2, are projected to have the highest increase in cases post‐COVID compared to the counterfactual. Quintile 2 is expected to have the highest increase of 21.7% more CRC cases (12,558 (95% CI: 11,262 to 13,854)) than the counterfactual (10,321; 95% CI: (11,262 to 13,854)), followed by Quintile 1 with 16.3% more cancer cases (13,388 (95% CI: 12,051 to 14,725)) than the counterfactual (11,512 (95% CI: 10,269 to 12,755)) (Table 4).

In the post‐COVID scenario relative to Quintile 1, Quintile 3 is predicted to have 17.5% fewer cases, while Quintile 5 records 32.9% fewer cases, demonstrating a marked socioeconomic gradient in incidence. Mortality

Higher increases are predicted for deaths in the most deprived groups: quintile 1 is predicted to have 30.1% more CRC deaths (4367 (95% CI: 3593 to 5140)) than quintile 1 counterfactual (3356 (2677 to 4035)). This contrasts with the increases below 4% predicted for the two higher quintiles in CRC cases and deaths. Post‐COVID, Quintile 3 has 25.6% fewer deaths than Quintile 1, and Quintile 5 has 47.1% fewer deaths. In the counterfactual scenario, Quintile 3 is predicted to have 16.1% fewer deaths and Quintile 5 32.3% fewer, again showing a consistent but less pronounced social gradient.

Mean Costs Per Person

The increased cases of CRC in quintile 1 and quintile 2 are reflected in the projected higher overall costs of $1932 M (95% CI: $1701 M to $2164 M) and $1832 (95% CI: $1605 M to $2058 M), 21.4% and 24.8% higher than the counterfactual predictions, respectively. Increases of only 4.1% and 3.4% were predicted in the two highest quintiles.

In the post‐COVID scenario, relative to Q1, Q3 has 22.9% lower mean costs, and Q5 has 45.9% lower costs, paralleling the incidence and mortality gradients. The counterfactual scenario shows a consistent but less pronounced gradient of 10.6% lower mean costs in Quintile 3 relative to Q1 and 24.3% lower costs in Quintile 5.

Model Validation

The model uses a fixed cohort of adults aged 50–74 and has been calibrated considering case numbers from published Victorian incidence and mortality data. Quintile 3 in the counterfactual scenario can be considered to reflect the average population group if COVID‐19 disruptions did not occur. The average number of new diagnoses in the 50–74 age group from 2010–2019 was 3488 cases from VCR data [44]. Quintile 3 in the counterfactual scenario predicts 50,822 CRC cases over 15 years, averaging 3392 cases per year. Our cases are lower and fall in the range of the expected number of cases, as we used a closed cohort, so no new members were added to the group.

The average number of CRC deaths in the 50–74 age group from 2010–2019 reported by the VCR was 1280 cases [44]. The quintile 3 counterfactual scenario in the model predicts 14,074 CRC deaths over 15 years, an average of 938 deaths per year. This equates to a survival rate of 72%. The Australian survival rate in 2016–2020 was 71% [56]. As we used a closed cohort, we expect the number of deaths to be lower than the number reported by the VCR because the population is smaller, as no new participants entered the model.

The average total projected cases, including COVID‐19 disruptions, is approximately 4153 per year from the Victorian Cancer Registry [44]. When subtracting the average cases in the under 50 age group (approximately 7% of total cases) the average cases per year is approximately 3862. The modelled projection for the COVID‐19 population average is 3681 cases per year.

Discussion

Our study predicted that COVID‐19 disruptions to healthcare services among the Victorian population aged 50–74 from 2023–2037 will lead to significant socioeconomic disparities in bowel cancer outcomes due to the missed diagnoses, which will further widen health inequalities. Under the post‐COVID scenario, the two most deprived groups are predicted to experience the most pronounced increases. Quintiles 1 and 2 are predicted to experience 16%–22% more CRC cases and 28%–30% more deaths, in contrast to Quintiles 4 and 5 with only 1% to 4% increases in cases and deaths. Similar disproportionate impacts are reflected in the healthcare costs. The results indicate a more intense social gradient in incidence, deaths, and mean costs in the post‐COVID period. While more cancers are being detected overall, delays in screening lead to pre‐cancerous adenomas not being detected early. Consequently, cases are diagnosed at more advanced stages, requiring more intensive and costly treatments. These results highlight the vulnerability of these populations to healthcare disruptions, exacerbating pre‐existing disparities and emphasising the need for robust screening and early detection programs for underserved populations.

Consistent bowel cancer screening and diagnostic follow‐up during the pandemic might have reduced the health inequities. But our findings indicate the inequalities in health outcomes became more pronounced in the post‐COVID period, reinforcing that catch‐up screening is necessary to prevent the gap from widening. In 2019, before the pandemic, there was a 7.7% difference in participation between IRSAD quintile 1 and quintile 5 in Victoria (42.5% to 50.2%), and this gap increased to 8.5% in 2020 (39.7% to 48.2%) [37]. Our findings align with inequalities in the re‐uptake of bowel cancer screening after the COVID‐19 pandemic reported in England for those living in the most deprived areas, including ethnic minorities, who exhibited lower awareness and participation in bowel cancer screening initiatives [4].

Previous studies that have modelled the long‐term health outcomes due to COVID‐19 disruptions of CRC have not considered socioeconomic differences. Our results predict the population average quintile CRC cases to increase by 8.5% overall, resulting in a 15.4% increase in CRC deaths, close to the UK estimated increases in deaths at 15.3%–16.6% for England [9] that have a similar national bowel screening program. The projected proportions of total CRC cases in each quintile are similar to the proportions of missing cases reported by the VCR in 2020–21 [19].

In Australia, lower participation rates and uptake of colonoscopy have been linked to higher proportions of those with limited English proficiency, Aboriginal populations and individuals with chronic health conditions [57]. Despite the extensive geographic distribution of the Australian population, it is the variation in behavioural factors, rather than geographic differences alone, that is linked to differences in screening participation and diagnostic testing. However, some populations face unique barriers due to at‐home kits being temperature‐sensitive, necessitating a ‘hot zone policy’ whereby participants can only receive kits during cooler months [58]. These areas are more likely to be remote/rural, have lower socio‐economic status and have higher Indigenous populations [58]. Addressing these disparities requires a multifaceted approach [59]. Investment in evidence‐based campaigns and public education, tailored to meet the knowledge needs of under‐screened groups that have shown to be effective in promoting the uptake of screening programs, is required to achieve sustained increases in screening participation [60, 61].

A major strength of this study is the use of data extracted from a Victorian linked dataset containing all patients diagnosed with CRC in 2009–2021. Where Victorian data were unavailable, Australian data were used whenever possible. However, as with all modelling studies, the assumptions may not fully capture future changes in healthcare practices or population behaviour. For the counterfactual scenarios, we assumed a constant incidence rate based on 2019 levels. Taking this conservative approach, the counterfactual results may be overestimated. Variables such as the IRSAD‐specific 5‐year mortality by cancer stage and stage of diagnosis based on screened status were included in the model and are also assumed to remain constant. We used the diagnostic results to determine the probabilities of adenoma, cancer, and no disease in the model for screened participants [33, 34, 35, 36]. Nevertheless, data from the 2006–2010 NBCSP participants indicated that less than 0.1% of individuals with a negative screen were diagnosed with bowel cancer within 2 years [62]. Some higher risk polyps (depending on number, size and type) may have a more frequent colonoscopy surveillance than 5 years. The inclusion of the potential impact on productivity would allow a more complete picture of the economic impacts.

Improved demographic data is essential for a comprehensive understanding of participation in the National Bowel Cancer Screening program in Australia. Currently, key demographics, including language spoken, country of birth, disability status, LGBTIQ identity, and Aboriginal status, are based on estimates of census data by the Australian Institute for Health and Welfare. There is a growing call for linking screening programs and the National Cancer Screening Register to the Person Level Integrated Data Asset [63] containing census data to obtain a clearer picture of groups with low participation rates.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study highlights the significant impact of COVID‐19 disruptions on the exacerbation of health disparities among the most disadvantaged socioeconomic groups. The need for targeted interventions to enhance screening efforts and protect vulnerable populations is clear. Improved demographic data of screening programs will enable a better understanding of the traits of non‐participants and groups with low participation rates.
