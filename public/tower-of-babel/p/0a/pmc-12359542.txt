Optical correction of hyperopia in school-aged children: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Gayathri Srinivasan, Kristen L Kerber, Su-Hsun Liu, Vivian M Manh, Kristine Huang, Alexandra Williamson, Soumen Sadhu, Morgan C Ollinger, Zahra Tajbakhsh, Jennifer H Fisher, Nathan L Cheung, Jasmine Junge, Kevin C H Chan, Jameel Rizwana Hussaindeen, Patrick Simard, Kelsey R Trast, Christina E Morettin, Samantha Krueger, Augustine N Nti, Debora M Lee Chen, Tawna L Roberts
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPrescribing patterns for hyperopia in children vary widely among eye care providers worldwide. This scoping review aims to identify and map the current literature on optical correction and catalogue outcomes reported, particularly in the domains of vision, vision-related functional outcomes and quality of life (QoL) in school-aged children with hyperopia.Methods and analysisThis protocol was developed in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute’s Manual for Evidence Synthesis. We will include studies involving school-aged children with hyperopia without restrictions on sex, gender, race, ethnicity, type of optical correction, length of intervention, publication date or country of origin. We will include studies with internal or external comparison groups. We will exclude studies associated with myopia control treatments, ocular and visual pathway pathologies affecting vision or visual function. We will search Cochrane CENTRAL, Embase.com and PubMed. Examples of data to be extracted include population demographics, visual acuity, study-specific definitions for refractive error, treatment regimens for optical correction, vision and vision-related functional outcomes and QoL (general or vision-related) as quantified by validated instruments.Ethics and disseminationInformed consent and Institutional Review Board approval will not be required, as this scoping review will only use published data. The results from the scoping review will be disseminated by publication in a peer-reviewed scientific journal and at professional conferences.

Introduction

Hyperopia is a common childhood refractive error. Hyperopia of ≥+2.00 dioptre (D) spherical equivalent has a prevalence of 14.6%–27.2% in children between ages 4 and 6 years in the USA.1 2 The prevalence varies by race, ethnicity1 2 and geographic location,3 4 with Hispanic white and non-Hispanic populations associated with a greater risk for moderate hyperopia (≥+4.00 D).4 Unlike myopia that causes reduced distance vision, only 10% of children with moderate bilateral hyperopia have reduced distance vision.5 Thus, prescribing for hyperopia is often made based on other associated factors and has garnered diverse perspectives among paediatric eye care providers.612

Moderate hyperopia is a known risk factor for the development of amblyopia5 and strabismus13 in early childhood. Although hyperopia as low as +2.00 D is associated with a sixfold increased risk of esotropia, <10% of children develop esotropia with <+4.00 D, at which point the incidence of esotropia increases exponentially.13 While optical correction is considered the first line of treatment for uncorrected hyperopia in the presence of amblyopia or strabismus,6814 15 the benefit of the intervention in young children without amblyopia or strabismus is less clear.

Uncorrected hyperopia, without amblyopia and strabismus, has been shown to negatively impact a number of different visual functions despite normal distance visual acuity.1620 A child with uncorrected hyperopia must exert more accommodation than a child without hyperopia to achieve clear vision as the accommodative demand increases with the magnitude of hyperopia and closer viewing distances. Previous studies have shown that some young children with moderate hyperopia (>+3.00 D) have reduced accommodative accuracy, reduced binocular near acuity and diminished stereopsis compared with children with emmetropia or low hyperopia.18 20 21

Beyond the impact on visual function, uncorrected hyperopia has also been linked to challenges with early literacy skills22 and academic performance2326 in young school-aged children. Studies have shown that emergent literacy skills, such as letter and word recognition and receptive vocabulary, were found to be reduced in children 4–7 years with hyperopia ≥+2.00 D compared with those with ≤+1.50 D of hyperopia.26 In a larger study of preschool children in the US (ages 4–5 years), the Print Knowledge subtest of the Test of Preschool Early Literacy was significantly reduced in children with hyperopia ≥+4.00 D compared with those with ≤+1.00 D of hyperopia.22 In a US study of elementary school children (first to fifth grade), academic achievement scores were found to be reduced in children with uncorrected hyperopia >+1.25 D.27

Past studies have documented the benefits of hyperopic correction on vision,28 visual function29 and academic performance in older school-aged children.29 The Collaborative Longitudinal Evaluation of Ethnicity and Refractive Error study showed a dose-dependent impact of uncorrected hyperopia on visual acuity in children aged 6–14 years and found better distance vision in children wearing correction compared with those without.28 In the Baltimore Reading and Eye Disease study (ages 7–10 years), near visual acuity improved after correction of hyperopia ≥+2.50 D compared with baseline.30 In a recent study in children aged 5–10 years with low to moderate hyperopia (+1.00 D to +5.00 D SE), wearing full cycloplegic correction for 10 minutes improved accommodative performance and reading speed compared with some children without correction.29

Despite some studies indicating that uncorrected hyperopia can have negative effects on visual function and academic performance, not all children with uncorrected hyperopia experience these negative effects. Consequently, for those with uncorrected hyperopia who show no signs or symptoms, identifying appropriate management strategies remains elusive, particularly because there are no evidence-based prescribing guidelines worldwide. Several professional societies3133 and researchers3436 have published prescribing guidelines for refractive errors in children, but it is important to note that the recommendations are mostly based on clinical experience or consensus among eye care professionals. A recent study evaluated the quality of these guidelines37 using the Appraisal of Guidelines for REsearch and Evaluation II (AGREE II) tool and found that, of the 65 prescribing guidelines identified, only 5 guidelines met study-specific inclusion criteria to be evaluated by the AGREE II tool. Of the five guidelines, only one, The American Academy of Ophthalmology Paediatric Eye Evaluations Preferred Practice Pattern (AAO PPP),38 was identified as high-quality. Furthermore, of the 168 prescribing statements reviewed by the authors, only 95 statements were deemed unanimously appropriate by an expert panel. Reasons for non-consensus were consideration for family history, levels of vision, parental preferences and impact of socioeconomic factors.

Given the lack of evidence-based guidelines, the decision to prescribe for hyperopia in school-aged children will vary depending on the understanding of the research evidence by eye care providers. Several survey-based studies have reported the prescribing patterns for childhood hyperopia among eye care providers.912 For example, a recent survey found that while approximately 60% of ophthalmologists considered stereoacuity an important factor when prescribing, nearly 40% did not. Similar discrepancies were noted within optometrists when considering factors such as family history, where only 65% of providers considered it relevant. Not only are there discrepancies in which factors are important when considering when to prescribe for uncorrected hyperopia, but the magnitude for which to prescribe was also inconsistent. The trend of a wide range of prescribing thresholds persisted when considering associated clinical factors such as esophoria, high accommodative lag, reduced near vision or reduced stereopsis.

This scoping review aims to identify and map the current literature on optical correction and catalogue outcomes reported, particularly in the domains of vision, vision-related functional outcomes and quality of life (QoL) in school-aged children with hyperopia. The overarching goal is to provide the paediatric eye care community with a consolidated source of reference related to this topic, identify evidence gaps and provide guidance for future research.

Methods and analysis

We will use the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI)’s Manual for Evidence Synthesis and Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews39 and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) methodologies40 to perform and report this scoping review, respectively. We will perform the literature search without limitation on the publication date or geographic location. Database searches will be completed by August 2025, with the goal of publishing the resulting manuscript within 1 year of the search.

Eligibility criteria

Participants

Studies included in this review will involve school-aged children with hyperopia (with or without astigmatism), children with amblyopia (refractive, strabismic or combination) and strabismus. Studies involving participants with mild speech delay, reading and/or learning disabilities or attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder will also be included. We will exclude studies in children with deprivation amblyopia, paralytic strabismus, genetic syndromes known to be associated with high levels of refractive error, severe developmental delay or those with diagnosed ocular (cornea, lenticular, retinal and optic nerve diseases) or neurological conditions known to affect vision and/or visual function. Studies that enrolled participants with hyperopia and ocular pathologies that do not have an impact on vision or visual function (eg, peripheral retinal degenerations) will be considered if they reported intervention results by refractive errors, separately.

Interventions

We will include studies that examined prescribing any optical correction (glasses or contact lenses) with each other or with no optical correction. There will be no limit on the duration of intervention.

Outcomes

We will document all outcomes, particularly in the domains of vision (eg, visual acuity, amblyopia), vision-related functions (eg, stereopsis, fusion, accommodative function, ocular alignment, academic performance, visual processing function) and QoL.

Study design

Eligible studies will include randomised controlled trials, cohort studies, case-control studies, case series or cross-sectional studies. In the instances of case series, a comparison before and after the intervention is required. We will exclude case reports. There will be no restrictions on the country of origin of the study. We will use Google Translate for an initial assessment of articles not published in English. If further translation is needed, we will request assistance from Cochrane Eyes and Vision staff and Cochrane Engage (https://engage.cochrane.org/). If no translation is available, we will classify the study as ‘awaiting classification’ for reporting purposes.

Search strategy

Working with an experienced information specialist, we will design and implement electronic searches in Cochrane CENTRAL, Embase.com and PubMed for relevant studies (online supplemental appendix I). We will also screen reference lists of included studies for additional relevant studies.

Study selection

We will upload the search results into Covidence, automatically removing duplicates. Two independent reviewers will screen the titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. We will then retrieve the full-text publications of relevant records for additional review by two independent reviewers for final disposition. Any disagreements that arise between the paired reviewers during the initial screening and full-text review will be resolved by consulting a third reviewer. We will report the number of articles included and the reasons for exclusion in a PRISMA-ScR flow diagram40 in the review manuscript.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers will extract data using structured data extraction forms built in the Covidence tool. The extracted data regarding study and population characteristics will include first author, year of publication, study location, study design, sample size, age of participants, sex, if social determinants of health41 were considered, participant refractive error, and if children with strabismus and/or amblyopia were included (online supplemental table 1). We will extract how hyperopic refractive error is defined and assessed, level of hyperopia correction, type and duration of intervention, adherence and follow-up (online supplemental table 2). Lastly, we will extract the study outcomes and conclusions as reported by the study (online supplemental table 3). Any disagreements that arise between reviewers at any stage will be resolved through discussion and/or with an additional reviewer(s). If needed, authors of included studies will be contacted to request missing information or additional data. At least two attempts will be made to contact the study investigators within 2 weeks. When no responses or adequate data are obtained, the review authors will use existing data for evidence synthesis.

Data analysis and presentation

We will present data in summary tables or graphics regarding patient demographics, diagnostic criteria for the level of hyperopia with or without astigmatism, type of interventions, type of outcomes and conclusions related to effectiveness. When applicable, quantitative evidence will be combined using summary statistics as suggested by the JBI Manual.39

Patient and public involvement

None.

Ethics and dissemination

We will only include published data in this scoping review; therefore, informed consent and Institutional Review Board approval are not required for this study. We will disseminate the results of this scoping review at national conferences and publish them in a peer-reviewed journal
