Influence of endovascular surgery on abdominal aortic aneurysm management strategies from a national health insurance database survey
Authors: Wei-Ling Chen, Chung-Dann Kan, Yi-Ting Huang, Hsin-I Shih
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is a significant global health concern, yet comprehensive population-based studies remain limited. This study aimed to evaluate the hospitalization rates, surgical trends, mortality, and reintervention rates for ruptured (r-AAA) and nonruptured (nr-AAA) AAA using data from a national health insurance database.Methods:A population-based retrospective cohort study was conducted utilizing data from the Taiwanese National Health Insurance Research Database from 2007 to 2018. The study included individuals aged 20 years and older with a newly diagnosed AAA.Results:Among 70 457 patients diagnosed with aortic aneurysm or dissection, 22 538 (32%) adult patients (≥20 years) were identified with AAA. The annual incidence of AAA ranged from 7.7 to 10.3 per 100 000 population, with r-AAA decreasing from 1.3 to 0.8 per 100 000 and nr-AAA from 9.0 to 6.8 per 100 000. Most patients with AAA were older adults (85%), with 15 392 (68%) hospitalized and 4885 (32%) undergoing surgery within 14 days of diagnosis. The use of endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) significantly increased from 28% to 96% over the study period. Long-term survival was higher in patients who underwent open surgical repair (OSR) compared to those who received EVAR or conservative management, irrespective of whether they had r-AAA or nr-AAA.Conclusion:AAA predominantly affects older individuals, and the annual incidence shows a declining trend. Since the introduction of EVAR, its use has steadily increased while OSR rates have decreased. Although both EVAR and OSR are associated with reduced mortality in patients with r-AAA, OSR is linked to superior long-term survival outcomes.

1. INTRODUCTION

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is defined as enlargement of the aorta to 1.5 times its normal diameter. This condition is common and potentially fatal, especially if a rupture occurs, and therefore, it must be promptly diagnosed and treated. Most patients are asymptomatic before an AAA rupture. In addition, only 50% of patients with ruptured AAA (r-AAA) are admitted to the hospital promptly after rupture, and 30% to 50% of these patients die in the hospital.1 AAA is often associated with a familial predisposition, smoking history, and high blood pressure. Research on screening reported that the AAA prevalence ranges from 4% to 8%, and it generally affects men more than women.2 Screening programs are crucial because they enable early detection of enlarged AAAs, which allows for interventions to be initiated before emergency conditions develop and thereby enables timely and potentially life-saving management.

Traditionally, open surgical repair (OSR) has been the recommended management for aneurysms exceeding 5 cm in diameter. It involves either a midline or retroperitoneal incision followed by replacement of the diseased aorta with a prosthetic graft. However, treatment preferences have shifted considerably since the introduction of minimally invasive endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) in the 1990s. Compared with OSR, EVAR, which involves sealing the aneurysm through an inguinal approach using self-expanding stent grafts, has dramatically reduced operative mortality and early postoperative morbidity. Therefore, EVAR has become the predominant procedure for AAA in the United States, and it is used in more than 80% of cases.3 Advances in endovascular technology, including fenestrated and branched stents, have broadened the application of EVAR to other complex conditions such as r-AAA and pararenal as well as thoracoabdominal aneurysms,4,5 with more than 95% of elective AAA surgeries in Taiwan now performed using this method.6

Despite the widespread adoption of EVAR and extensive research on the topic worldwide, the effect of these technological advancements on the population-based incidence of AAA following the introduction of EVAR remains underexplored.7 Large observational studies and meta-analyses have highlighted EVAR’s advantages in reducing perioperative morbidity and mortality.8,9 However, some randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing EVAR with OSR have revealed that EVAR’s initial survival benefits diminish over time, potentially because of the higher reintervention rates and complications associated with the procedure.10 The current study leveraged national data to investigate how AAA incidence and outcomes have evolved since the introduction of EVAR and to comprehensively compare EVAR with OSR in terms of surgical trends, morbidity, treatment strategies, and mortality outcomes for both nonruptured AAA (nr-AAA) and r-AAA. The ultimate goal of this study was to develop more effective treatment strategies for patients with AAA.

2. METHODS

2.1. Study design

This retrospective cohort study utilized data from 2007 to 2018 from the National Health Welfare Database, managed by the Center for Health Welfare Data Science under the Taiwanese Ministry of Health and Welfare. This database integrates the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD), the Cause of Death Database, the Cancer Registry, and the Notifiable Disease Dataset of Confirmed Cases. The NHIRD is one of the largest medical administrative databases in the world, covering 23 million individuals and containing data from 99.6% of Taiwanese residents and 93% of Taiwan’s medical facilities.

2.2. Study population

The study population included individuals aged 20 years and older given a diagnosis of AAA from 2007 to 2018, as recorded in the NHIRD. Patients with AAA were identified using ICD-9-CM codes from 2007 to 2015 and ICD-10-CM codes from 2016 to 2018. Diagnoses were categorized as nr-AAA or r-AAA. The specific diagnostic codes that were used were ICD-9-CM codes 441.3 to 441.4 and ICD-10-CM codes I71.3 to I71.4. Patients were excluded if they were transferred without undergoing surgery (OSR or EVAR). The flowchart presented in Fig. 1 details the study methodology and patient categorization.

2.3. Ethics statement

The Institutional Review Board of National Cheng Kung University Hospital approved the study (IRB No. B-ER-109.130). Because data from the national database are anonymized and deidentified, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

2.4. Definition of covariates

The demographic variables of this study included age and sex. Comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus (DM), coronary artery disease (CAD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, liver cirrhosis, dyslipidemia, cerebrovascular disease (CVD), peripheral vascular disease, chronic kidney disease (CKD), acute kidney injury (AKI), and heart failure (HF) were identified on the basis of at least three outpatient visits or one hospital admission before the index date by using relevant ICD-9-CM codes (Supplementary Table S1, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/A278). All-cause in-hospital mortality was defined as death during the index admission or within 72 hours after discharge.

2.5. Statistical analysis

We selected common comorbidities, age, sex, and AAA type (r-AAA or nr-AAA) as covariates. Continuous variables are expressed as means and SDs. We calculated the annual incidence rate per 100 000 individuals on the basis of the total adult population in Taiwan, using the 2000 world standard population for age-standardization:

Data analysis was performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Incidence trends and all-cause mortality were analyzed using binary logistic regression models, with survival outcomes evaluated using Kaplan–Meier plots and log-rank tests. Backward selection procedures were used to determine associations with covariates, with a significance level of α = 0.1 for model inclusion and significance reported at α = 0.05. All statistical tests were 2-sided, with p values of less than 0.05 considered significant.

3. RESULTS

3.1. Patient characteristics and general management

Between 2007 and 2018, 22 538 patients were given a diagnosis of abdominal aneurysm, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The annual age-standardized incidence of r-AAA decreased from 1.3 to 0.8 per 100 000 people, whereas that of nr-AAA decreased from 9.0 to 6.9 per 100 000 people, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The incidence of r-AAA was significantly lower than that of nr-AAA (approximately 1 vs 7 per 100 000). Among the patients, 15 392 had AAA, with 1988 (13%) having r-AAA and 13 404 (87%) having nr-AAA. Surgery was conducted on 60% of the patients with r-AAA and 28% of the patients with nr-AAA, and specific details regarding the surgeries were provided in the study database (EVAR: 892 and OSR: 302 for r-AAA; EVAR: 3205 and OSR: 486 for nr-AAA).

4. DISCUSSION

Our research demonstrates that AAA is predominantly observed in older adults and that the annual age-standardized incidence rate of AAA significantly decreased during the 2010s. Hypertension, diabetes, and CAD were the most frequently diagnosed comorbidities at the time of AAA diagnosis. Notably, younger patients with r-AAA exhibited a lower prevalence of conditions such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, CAD, and CVD than did older patients with r-AAA. Additionally, our findings indicate a shift in treatment preferences characterized by increased adoption of EVAR and reduced utilization of OSR.

Our analysis revealed distinct patterns in the in-hospital mortality risks associated with AAA. In the patients with nr-AAA, factors such as undergoing OSR and the presence of HF, cardiovascular disease, or CKD were associated with higher in-hospital mortality rates. For the patients with r-AAA, increased mortality rates were linked to advanced age, AKI, HF, and arrhythmia. Supplementary Fig. S1A, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/A279, presents the survival rates of the patients with r-AAA who underwent OSR compared with those of the patients who received EVAR. Contrary to common perceptions and some published data suggesting superior outcomes for EVAR, our findings indicate that OSR is associated with improved survival rates. However, no significant difference was observed in the long-term survival between the two treatment methods. This study excluded nonoperative treatment for r-AAA because of its association with a high mortality rate; the data for such treatment were too limited to allow for meaningful long-term survival analysis. Additionally, as depicted in Supplementary Fig. S1B, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/A279, patients with nr-AAA treated with either EVAR or OSR exhibited comparable survival rates, which were generally more favorable than those for patients without surgical intervention. This indicates that although OSR and EVAR improve early survival prospects for patients with AAA, neither treatment offers a significant long-term survival benefit.

Statistics from the United States indicate that aneurysmal disease, including dissection, is the 15th leading cause of death among adults aged 85 to 89 years11; aneurysmal disease accounts for 0.13% of deaths in men and 0.07% of deaths in women in the United States. In addition, the rate of aneurysm rupture per 100 000 people is declining.12 Globally, the prevalence and incidence of AAA have significantly decreased over time, a trend attributable to enhanced population screening and widespread adoption of EVAR. Consistent with global observations, our data from Taiwan from between 2007 and 2018 indicate a decline in AAA incidence, along with a notable increase in the proportion of patients treated with EVAR.

Numerous factors are known to contribute to the development of AAA. High blood pressure, advanced age, male sex, heavy alcohol consumption, other large vessel aneurysm, and atherosclerosis are considered high-risk factors for the development of AAA. The main processes involved in the development of AAA include inflammation, oxidative stress, proteolysis, and biomechanical wall stress.13,14 Our findings reveal that in Taiwan, AAA is more commonly observed in older individuals and those with cardiovascular conditions, aligning with the presence of comorbidities such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, CAD, and CVD. Although uncontrolled hypertension is typically considered a substantial risk factor for AAA, our study indicated that its role is less pronounced among younger individuals experiencing r-AAA. This insight challenges traditional approaches to AAA risk assessment, particularly for the younger population, hinting at an influence from potentially overlooked factors in the development and severity of r-AAA among these individuals. Several studies have delineated the risk factors associated with the development and progression of AAA. For example, Shuai et al15 identified hypertension, particularly uncontrolled hypertension, as a prominent risk factor for AAA, in addition to other high-risk factors, such as newly diagnosed DM. Notably, their research also suggests that younger individuals with r-AAA are less likely to present with traditional cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, and CVD.15

Our analysis led to a noteworthy observation: the prevalence of hypertension among the patients with r-AAA in this study was 31%, which is significantly lower than the 48% observed in the patients with nr-AAA. Notably, younger patients with r-AAA exhibited a lower prevalence of hypertension than did their nr-AAA counterparts, as indicated by an adjusted odds ratio value of 0.62. Further subgroup analysis of the patients with r-AAA and hypertension revealed that those undergoing EVAR or OSR had significantly lower in-hospital mortality rates than did those receiving conservative treatment. These findings suggest that although hypertension is a recognized risk factor for AAA, its role in the context of r-AAA, particularly among younger individuals, warrants further investigation. The findings underscore the importance of employing a holistic approach to managing AAA that includes consideration of a wide range of risk factors beyond hypertension, especially in the treatment and risk stratification of younger patients with r-AAA. Older patients routinely monitored for chronic conditions are often screened for other vascular comorbidities, such as dyslipidemia and CVD. Studies have indicated that individuals with well-managed hypertension experience lower rates of morbidity and mortality.13,16 Thus, early detection and management of hypertension remain critical in reducing the risk of AAA rupture.

According to the current study findings, compared with OSR, EVAR significantly reduced perioperative mortality and early postoperative morbidities. The new equipment and surgical techniques used in EVAR have led to decreased blood loss, eliminated the need for cross-clamping the aorta, and led to shorter recovery periods than those associated with traditional surgery. In 2010, EVAR surpassed OSR as the dominant surgical strategy for AAA disease.8 AAA treatment approaches require careful consideration of comorbidities and corresponding perioperative mortality and morbidity risks, as well as consideration of the natural history of untreated aneurysms. The lower perioperative mortality rate associated with EVAR has led to a change in the approach to treating nr-AAA and r-AAA, with EVAR serving as an alternative treatment option for older patients with AAA who were previously considered inoperable. Our study also indicates that the proportion of EVAR procedures exceeds that of OSR for all AAA surgical conditions in Taiwan and that EVAR has become the predominant surgical intervention. More than 90% of AAA procedures are performed using the EVAR approach (Fig. 3).

The benefits of endovascular repair of ruptured AAA remain unclear. The key problems that may occur after rupture include hemostasis and postoperative abdominal compartment syndrome management. Numerous reports have indicated that EVAR is associated with reduced mortality rates after r-AAA repair, is associated with fewer complications (eg, bleeding, renal, and respiratory complications), and requires shorter hospital stays, meaning that more patients are likely to be able to return home rather than remain in institutional care.11–13 Giles et al14 conducted a mortality analysis of REVAR cases and reported that EVAR significantly reduced the mortality rate of patients with r-AAA (31.85% vs 50.8%; p < 0.001). However, in the IMPROVE randomized trial, which included 613 patients, Powell et al reported a 30-day mortality rate of 32% after EVAR vs a rate of 35% in the OSR arm.15,16 Our study also indicated that EVAR had a lower mortality rate in the first few days after the initial diagnosis of r-AAA. However, the patients receiving OSR had higher long-term survival rates than did those who received EVAR. Research indicates that the early survival benefit of EVAR is lost over time; a growing number of early RCTs has reported higher rates of late reintervention and endograft-specific complications to be associated with EVAR. Common late complications of EVAR include endoleaks and endotension and those specific to the endograft.10 Antoniou et al17 perform a systematic review and reported that compared with OSR, EVAR resulted in better outcomes in the first 6 months but was associated with an increased risk of aneurysm-related mortality after 8 years. A propensity-matched analysis of Medicare beneficiaries who received EVAR and OSR that was conducted by Schermerhorn et al18 indicated that the trend toward higher survival outcomes among patients who receive early EVAR might persist for 3 years, after which the survival outcomes are equal between EVAR and OSR, and only a limited mean survival benefit was associated with EVAR at 7 years. The long-term results of EVAR outcome analyses indicate that younger patients with a longer life expectancy and lower perioperative risk may obtain a greater benefit from OSR.19 Our data reveal that the 1-year survival rates were significantly lower in the patients with r-AAA who underwent OSR than in those who underwent EVAR; however, although the patients who underwent EVAR had higher initial survival rates than those who underwent OSR did, the survival rate was significantly lower in these patients after 1 year. That is, the patients who received OSR had better long-term survival than did those who received EVAR. Schermerhorn et al7,9,19 reported that EVAR should not be overused in patients with inappropriate anatomical conditions. The main disadvantage of EVAR is its association with a high rate of postoperative complications that often require reintervention. Therefore, most authorities recommend lifelong monitoring through imaging after EVAR.20

In addition to preexisting comorbidities, AKI is a critical factor influencing interhospital mortality. AKI is a major complication after AAA surgery. It is common after acute type B aortic dissection (ABAD) and is associated with higher early mortality rates and hospital stay lengths and poorer long-term survival outcomes. The mechanisms contributing to AKI after ABAD include renal malperfusion or underlying renal dysfunction.21 Our data indicate that AKI is associated with interhospital mortality rates but does not significantly influence long-term mortality rates. Early identification of high-risk patients, early diagnosis of AKI, stabilization of hemodynamic parameters, avoidance of nephrotoxic drugs, and optimization of the use of contrast agents should be implemented as major strategies for reducing AKI in patients with AAA.22

Our study has several strengths. First, ours was a large-scale, population-based, long-term follow-up study. We conducted a well-designed cohort study by using national medical claims data to evaluate short- and long-term health consequences among patients with AAA. Because this study was conducted on a large-scale and was population-based, the potential effects of selection bias were minimized. In addition, the high coverage rate of the National Health Insurance program led to a minimized influence from patients being lost to follow-up. Second, the study period and follow-up time exceeded 10 years, enabling observation of nearly all short- and long-term consequences. Third, although most of the patients with AAA were older, we investigated potential risk factors for both older and younger patients and assessed the key factors contributing to short- and long-term consequences.

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the databases we used lacked information on potential confounders such as smoking status and lifestyle factors, which might be risk factors for AAA. Second, the incidence of AAA might have been underestimated because only patients who received imaging could be identified as having AAA. Some patients without a definite AAA diagnosis before death may not have been identified; however, the number of such cases is likely sufficiently limited that the potentially effects can be ignored. Third, the exact reasons for and duration of AKI occurrence could not be determined because the databases we used included claims data for insurance reimbursements rather than detailed medical records. Therefore, stratifying the patients by the type and duration of AKI for further analysis would have been difficult.

Furthermore, This study did not include notable genetic illnesses, such as heritable connective tissue disorders and congenital cardiac diseases, as comorbidities in Supplementary Table S1 and Table S2, http://links.lww.com/JCMA/A278. This exclusion does not imply that they were not considered crucial; rather, it reflects the limitations of our dataset and the specificity of the focus of our initial analysis. As we progress, these genetic risk factors should be incorporated into our research framework. Doing so will address the current gap in the literature and enhance the understanding of AAA’s complex etiology, particularly that with respect to establishing distinct risk profiles for younger vs older populations. Future studies should include these genetic factors to ensure comprehensive evaluation of all potential risk factors contributing to the incidence and progression of AAA.

Our study provides valuable insights obtainable because of its design and manner of execution. This study used a population-based approach with a substantial follow-up duration, leveraging national medical claims data to assess a wide spectrum of health outcomes in patients with AAA. The breadth of the data coverage helped to mitigate selection bias and reduce the influence of patients being lost to follow-up, which enhanced the reliability of our findings. Furthermore, the study period spans more than a decade, which allowed for a comprehensive investigation of both the short-term and the long-term consequences of AAA. Furthermore, by including a diverse patient demographic, our research shed light on risk factors and outcomes across different age groups, enriching the understanding of AAA’s effects.

In conclusion, AAA is more common in patients who are older and have hypertension, and the annual age-standardized incidence rate has decreased over the past 10 years. The proportion of patients treated with EVAR has been increased since its introduction, and the proportion of patients treated with OSR has decreased. In the current study, the mortality rates in the patients with AAA (both nr-AAA and r-AAA) who received EVAR and OSR were lower than those of patients treated only with medical measures; however, the patients with AAA who received OSR had better long-term survival outcomes. Further follow-up studies are warranted to determine whether endovascular stent-prosthetic materials offer any benefits related to improving survival outcomes.
