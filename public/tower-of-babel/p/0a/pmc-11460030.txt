The efficacy of ciprofol versus propofol on anesthesia in patients undergoing endoscopy: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Jikai Liu, Aonan Hong, Jinfang Zeng, Xiao Liang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCiprofol is a new intravenous anesthetic with a similar chemical structure to propofol. We aimed to compare the incidence of adverse actions like injection pain and time indexes of ciprofol versus propofol on anesthesia in patients undergoing endoscopy. We also compared anesthetists’ satisfaction during the procedure.MethodsTwo independent researchers (Liu and Zeng) searched the Cochrane Library, Embase databases, and PubMed for controlled clinical trials. This meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) was performed with the Review Manager, Stata and the Cochrane Risk-of-Bias 2 tool to evaluate methodological quality. Relative risks with 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated for outcomes.ResultsTen trials, including 1545 patients, were examined in the current meta-analysis. During anesthesia in patients undergoing endoscopy, the incidence of injection pain was significantly reduced in the research group. Compared with propofol, the pooled risk difference (RD) with the use of ciprofol for injection pain for all the procedures was − 0.34 (95% confidence interval [CI], -0.48 to 0.19), and RR for hypotension was 0.73(95% CI:0.58 to 0.92). GRADE showed this meta-analysis has moderate or low confidence. Trial sequential analysis for mortality indicated insufficient sample size for a definitive judgment for lower incidence of hypotension.ConclusionIn painless endoscopy, compared with propofol, ciprofol exhibited non-inferiority anesthesia/sedation in patients, and had a good safety profile with a lower incidence of pain on injection and may reduce the chance of hypotension. Trial sequential analysis suggested the need for more cases, and GRADE highlighted moderate certainty, emphasizing the necessity for further targeted RCTs.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO, CRD42023433627.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-024-02721-4.

Introduction

Endoscopy is the mainstay for diagnosis of early and advanced cancer, for the treatment of early lesions, and also for the alleviation of advanced cancer [1], it has gradually become an important diagnostic and therapeutic routine for the treatment of related diseases and was associated with a significant decrease in cancer incidence and mortality [2]. The introduction of sedative agents and the application of comfortable anesthesia during endoscopy help to relieve the anxiety and pain of patients. Then, painless endoscopy is popular with the administration of sedative agents [3]. Propofol, as the commonly used short-acting intravenous sedative, is also associated with high patient comfort and complete awakening [4]. However, the nature of injection pain reported incidence of propofol injection varied from 28 to 90% occurs during the administration of this drug has been ranked as one of the most obvious drawbacks in the practice of its clinical anesthesia [5, 6]. Therefore, identification of the ideal sedative drug in endoscopy remains crucial.

Based on the above-mentioned limitations of traditional sedatives, new sedative drugs that meet the requirements need to be found and applied. Ciprofol is a new intravenous anesthetic developed in recent years in China, which has a chemical structure similar to that of propofol and binds with the gamma-aminobutyric acid A (GABA-A) receptors. But it is especially worth noting that at similar concentrations, the recent studies implied that injection pain of ciprofol might be less than the pain on propofol injection (POPI) [7, 8], with the incidence of injection pain varying from 4.9 to 16.7% [9–18]. To the best of our knowledge, relative quantitative analysis has not been conducted yet, which should be carried out at once.

This current meta-analysis was designed to compare the incidence of injection pain associated with the use of ciprofol versus propofol for endoscopic procedures, and hopefully to provide a favorable reference for the clinical application of ciprofol.

Methods

We conducted a meta-analysis to assess the efficacy of ciprofol versus propofol on anesthesia in patients undergoing endoscopy.

Search approach and eligibility standards

Two authors (Liu and Zeng) independently conducted a systematic literature search by using Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Embase, Web of Science, and PubMed. The search strategy contains the following keywords: (ciprofol) or (HSK3486) or (Cyclopropofol) and (propofol). The literature search was updated on August 10, 2024, limited to English papers only. After screening titles and abstracts, Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were filtered out, and literature not RCTs or not related to endoscopy were excluded.

The search strategies are listed in the Supplementary file 1. Besides, we manually checked the reference list of related review articles and identified studies to identify any further RCTs. This systematic review and meta-analysis was performed in accordance with the Cochrane methods and Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) (Supplementary file 2) recommendation [19, 20] and the study protocol was registered in PROSPERO [CRD42023433627].

Qualitative assessment

The quality of the studies was evaluated, independently, according to the guidelines of Cochrane Collaboration, containing six categories (Randomization process, Deviations from intended interventions, Missing outcome data, Measurement of the outcome, Selection of the reported result, overall assessment). The categories above could be summarized into three levels, high risk, unclear risk(some concerns), and low risk. The risk of bias of each study was evaluated according to the levels of the three key domains: “High” (high risk of bias for one or more key domains), “Some concerns” (unclear risk of bias for one or more key domains), and “Low” (low risk of bias for all key domains).

Inclusion criteria

(1) RCTs; (2) Endoscopies; (3) Ciprofol versus propofol; (4) Provided sufficient data to calculate relative risk (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Exclusion criteria

(1) Not RCTs; (2) Abstract only or not full text; (3) Patients with severe cerebrovascular disease; (4) Contraindication for sedation or anesthesia or a history of sedation/ anesthesia adverse events (AEs); (5) The patient refuses to give informed consent; (6) Studies that could not provide actual frequencies of complications.

Data extraction

Relevant data were extracted from qualified studies including the first author’s name, publication year, type of operation, gender of patients, duration of induction, awaken time, duration of operation, discharge time, and satisfaction ratings. Adverse events include injection pain, hypotension, body movement, and bradycardia. All the data were counted on a uniform table. Divergence was settled promptly by discussion among group members.

Assessment of quality of evidence

Overall certainty of evidence was evaluated with the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) system using the Guideline Development Tool.3.4. Quality of evidence. The risks of systematic errors (bias) and random errors were assessed, and the overall certainty of evidence was evaluated using the GRADE guidelines [21]. We labeled studies as having very low, low, moderate, or high-quality evidence based on the presence of risk of bias, inconsistency of results, imprecision, publication bias, and magnitude of treatment effects.

Trial sequential analysis

Trial sequential analysis (TSA) was performed using Trial Sequential Analysis software ver. 0.9.5.10 beta [Computer program]. We assessed the adequacy of the available number of patients and performed TSA to establish the implication for further research. We calculated the required diversity-adjusted information size. Diversity, which indicates the percentage of variability between trials, is calculated as the sum of the between-trial variability and a sampling error estimate based on the required information size. We performed TSA to maintain an overall 5% risk of type I error, which is considered the standard for most meta-analyses and systematic reviews. The calculated information size, adjusted for required diversity, and targeting a 5% risk of type I error, 20% risk of type II error [22]. Firm evidence is likely established if the trial sequential monitoring boundary is crossed before reaching the required information size, but if this boundary is not crossed, it is highly likely that trials will need to be continued.

Outcome measures

The combined risk difference (RD) was calculated to evaluate the efficacy of injection pain, nausea and vomiting, bradycardia. The combined mean difference (MD) was used to evaluate awake time and discharge time, with a confidence interval (CI) of 95%. The combined relative risk (RR) was used to evaluate hypotension and body movement, success rate and anesthesiologists’ satisfaction. Z-test was used to demonstrate whether the overall effect was significant. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. And a random effects model was used. The robustness of the results is analyzed through sensitivity analysis by only reanalyzing data from low risk and unclear risk studies.

As is shown in Fig. 1, after searching four databases, 234 trials were found. After eliminating 96 duplicate trials, 52 of the remaining 86 trials were not RCT, four of the remaining 35 trials were not endoscopy, and the remaining nine trials were RCT that met the inclusion criteria. One of them had no data, so ten trials were finally included for statistical analysis.

Results of the meta-analysis

Among the 234 researches searched, ten original studies were qualified for this meta-analysis and included 1545 patients. Of these, 765 received ciprofol, and 780 received propofol. Ten trials investigated the efficacy of ciprofol compared with propofol in patients undergoing endoscopy.

Ciprofol versus propofol on injection pain: In ten trials [9–18], the incidence of injection pain (pooled RD -0.34, 95%CI -0.48 to -0.19 in the ciprofol group was significantly lower than that in the control group (Fig. 3). Begg’s and Egger’s tests suggested no significant publication bias existed in comparisons (Begger ’s Test p = 0.929, Egger’s Test p = 0.127) (Fig. 4). Factors that affected injection pain were evaluated through further subgroup analysis.

Trial sequential analysis

We performed TSA for injection pain and use information from all ten RCTs involving 1545 patients. The cumulative Z curve intersected both the futility boundaries and the monitoring boundary before reaching the information size, indicating firm evidence of the effect that ciprofol was significantly superior to propofol in reducing injection pain (Fig. 10A). Additionally, we performed TSA for the secondary outcome hypotension, utilizing data from eight of the RCTs involving 1374 patients. Low risk of bias trials investigating the expected effect, amounted to 2668 patients. The cumulative Z curve only intersected the futility boundaries but did not intersect the monitoring boundary, indicating an insufficient number of studies to show substantial evidence for a -30% RRR in reduced incidence of hypotension (Fig. 10B). We currently possess only 51.49% of the necessary information size required to make a definitive judgment.

Quality of evidence

According to the GRADE system grades, the quality of efficacy of ciprofol compared with propofol on injection pain was evaluated as moderate-certainty evidence, which suggested that the actual effect might differ from the estimated effect due to probable risk of bias and issues of imprecision. However, the efficacy of ciprofol on hypotension, movement, nausea and vomiting, bradycardia, awaken time, success rate, discharge time and anesthesiologists’ satisfaction had low or very low-certainty evidence level, the quality assessment is detailed in Supplementary Tables 1–9. No publication bias was found after inspection of the funnel plots of the primary outcomes.

Sensitivity analysis

Results of the sensitivity analysis of outcomes (lower incidence of injection pain and hypotension) using the low risk of bias studies, are presented in Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2. The results of the analyses were consistent with the main results.

Discussion

POPI is a long-standing problem [23], not a new concept in the procedure of traditional sedation anesthesia propofol, which causes patients to experience discomfort, increased tension, anxiety, and other uncomfortable experiences and may also induce the body movements and other symptoms that are not conducive to the completion of endoscopy [15]. Therefore, finding a probable alternative drug to prevent POPI is still an urgent problem to be solved.

This present meta-analysis was undertaken to evaluate the efficacy of ciprofol versus propofol on anesthesia in patients undergoing endoscopy. Our main findings are as follows: (1) The incidence of injection pain in the ciprofol groups was significantly reduced compared to control groups. (2) Additionally, the hemodynamics were more stable in ciprofol groups. (3) In the ciprofol group, the recovery time and discharge time were prolonged to some extent. (4) Ciprofol was non-inferior with respect to the success rate of endoscopy and satisfaction of anesthesiologists.

Propofol belongs to the group of phenols that can directly irritate the skin, mucous membrane, and venous intima and could immediately stimulate nociceptors and free nerve endings [24]. In addition, by activating the kallikrein-kinin system and releasing bradykinin by its indirect action on the endothelium, propofol thereby produced venous dilation and hyperpermeability, which increases the contact between the aqueous phase of propofol and free nerve endings, results in delayed pain [25]. Although the concentration of an irritating agent in the aqueous phase, because the outer aqueous phase comes into contact with the intima of the vein, leads to injection pain [26], ciprofol adds a cyclopropyl group to the side chain of the core structure, which reduces the lipophilicity of the parent structure by increasing the spatial effect [27]. These changes lead to a higher receptor affinity (about 4–5 times higher) for ciprofol than for propofol, which enables it to achieve the same sedative effects at a much lower dosage [28]. Therefore, the significantly reduced aqueous drug concentration might be the main factor responsible for less injection pain in the ciprofol group.

When anesthesiologists encounter POPI from patients, they are more intended to use additional opioids [29], which could cause harmful adverse effects [30] or postoperative nausea and vomiting [31]. Jing Zhong [10] declared that ciprofol has a tendency to reduce the induced dose of opioids, but that requires more large-scale studies to confirm. Furthermore, both the reduction and the incidence of hypotension during the administration of ciprofol were statistically significant, therefore patients could benefit from the dominance in hemodynamic stabilization of ciprofol [32] because the inhibitory effect on circulation increases the risk of anesthesia monitoring [33, 34].

Sudden unexpected movement can result in injury to undesired tissues or nerves due to rotating or sharp instruments, and it can be difficult to predict [35]. Our meta-analysis suggests that ciprofol may reduced the incidence of body movements during sedation, but that requies more randomized controlled trials to confirm. Additionally, it is worth noting that awake time and discharge time of ciprofol were longer comparing to propofol, which is possibly related to its slightly longer elimination half-life (1.58–2.47 h) [36], the clinical implication of the delay is of limited clinical significance [18]. Another reason is that when anesthesiologists apply the new drug ciprofol, they continue the previous injection habit, therefore better timing of injection and more reasonable dosage of this new drug will benefit patients in the future.

Limitation

There are several limitations in this meta-analysis. First, according to the GRADE system, the certainty of our findings ranked low and moderate across different outcomes, the main limiting factors that contribute to the low quality included serious risk of bias. Second, number of subgroups such as surgical types and drug concentrations is too small. Third, if injection pain and hypotension could be graded uniformly, it would make this meta-analysis more robust. There is still a need for more trials to further investigate the efficacy, safety, and applicability of ciprofol in various subgroups.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis including ten RCTs demonstrated an obvious reduction in the incidence of injection pain, and TSA indicates the need for a larger number of cases to confirm the advantage of ciprofol in terms of lower incidence of hypotension in the implementation of anesthesia. In conclusion, ciprofol is safe and effective in endoscopy sedation and has no obvious adverse reactions.
