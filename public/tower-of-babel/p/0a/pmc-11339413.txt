Rapid function analysis of OsiWAK1 using a Dual-Luciferase assay in rice
Authors: Wuzhong Yin, Hongxia Yang, Ping Feng, Pan Qi, Biluo Li, Yuanyuan Li, Qingxiong Huang, Youlin Peng, Nan Wang, Yungao Hu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the past decade, the exploration of genetic resources in rice has significantly enhanced the efficacy of rice breeding. However, the exploration of genetic resources is hindered by the identification of candidate genes. To expedite the identification of candidate genes, this study examined tapetum programmed cell death-related genes OsiWAK1, OsPDT1, EAT1, TDR, and TIP2 to assess the efficacy of the Dual-Luciferase (Dual-LUC) assay in rapidly determining gene relationships. The study found that, in the Dual-LUC assay, OsiWAK1 and its various recombinant proteins exhibit comparable activation abilities on the EAT1 promoter, potentially indicating a false positive. However, the Dual-LUC assay can reveal that OsiWAK1 impacts both the function of its upstream regulatory factor OsPDT1 and the TDR/TIP2 transcription complex. By rapidly studying the relationship between diverse candidate genes and regulatory genes in a well-known trait via the Dual-LUC assay, this study provides a novel approach to expedite the determination of candidate genes such as genome-wide association study.Keywords: Rice, Gene function analysis, Dual-Luciferase assay, Wall associated kinase, Candidate genes researchSubject terms: Plant cell biology, Plant development

Introduction

Rice is one of the most important food crops, and the breeding of rice is vital for global food security. Various significant genes that affect the growth and development of rice have been identified through functional genomics research, and have been employed in rice breeding over the past decade1,2. For instance, the research on the selfish gene locus qHMS7 in rice revealed that the linked genes ORF2 and ORF3 collaborate to control the pollen abortion in hybrid rice, shedding light on the underlying mechanism of hybrid sterility between wild and cultivated rice3. This finding has the potential to assist in the determination of appropriate combinations of hybrid rice. Moreover, with the progress of functional genomics research, an increasing number of elite genes have been identified, and genome-wide association study (GWAS) is an important method to explore them. Through GWAS, elite genes such as OsTCP19, OsNAC42, and OsNLP4 that influence rice's nitrogen absorption and utilization efficiency4–7, along with OsGF14h, OsUGT75A that affect rice germination under low-oxygen conditions8–10, and the GS3 that controls rice yield and resistance have been cloned11. These findings provide a wealth of genetic resources for rice breeding2.

In many GWAS, single nucleotide polymorphisms with high correlation may only be closely associated with candidate genes, and precise identification of candidate genes from the numerous genes within the GWAS linkage interval is a crucial factor that affects the efficiency of GWAS12,13. Currently, candidate genes can be identified through methods such as gene functional annotation and multi-omics joint analysis, which possess significant limitations1,14. Thus, rapidly identifying the function of genes within the target region of GWAS is crucial for uncovering elite genes in rice and optimizing rice breeding1. When utilizing GWAS and other methods to explore genetic resources, the trait of the candidate genes involved is known. Therefore, by identifying the association between the candidate genes and regulatory genes related to the trait, it enables a prompt identification of candidate genes.

The dual-luciferase (Dual-LUC) assay is a rapid and convenient detection system that can be used in the research of promoter function, transcription factor interaction, as well as RNA interaction13. For example, the study utilized the Dual-LUC assay to investigate the activation of the NPF6.1 promoter by various haplotypes of OsNAC424; moreover, bZIP73 and bZIP71 collaborate to regulate the expression of qLTG3-1 and OsINV415. Currently, Dual-LUC assays are primarily used for detecting direct gene effects. Nonetheless, the detection of indirect gene effects, which can provide valuable insight into the study of various gene functions, is rarely performed using the Dual-LUC assay.

Previous studies have shown that O. sativa PECTIN-DEFECTIVE TAPETUM1 (OsPDT1) encodes a Golgi-resident galacturonosyltransferase; furthermore, O. sativa indica WALL-ASSOCIATED KINASE1 (OsiWAK1) encodes a receptor-like kinase found in secretory vesicles and the cell wall, which is modulated by pectin in ospdt116. In the wild-type background, overexpression of OsiWAK1 does not affect ETERNAL TAPETUM1 (EAT1) expression during stages 7–9 of anther development, conversely, overexpressing OsiWAK1 in the ospdt1 background alters the expression pattern of the EAT1 gene compared to ospdt116. EAT1 encodes a basic helix-loop-helix transcription factor and is directly regulated by the transcription complex composed of TAPETUM DEGENERATION RETARDATION (TDR) and TDR INTERACTING PROTEIN2 (TIP2), which are nuclear-localized17–19. In addition, OsiWAK1, OsPDT1, TDR, TIP2, and EAT1 are all involved in the regulation of programmed cell death (PCD) in the tapetum16–19. Although OsiWAK1 plays a pivotal role in pectin-mediated signaling pathway and tapetal PCD, the downstream regulatory network of OsiWAK1 remains unclear.

Due to the disparate localization of proteins in protoplasts, the effectiveness of the Dual-LUC assay in investigating indirect effects on genes was assessed using OsiWAK1, OsPDT1, TDR, TIP2, and EAT1. Our research suggests that OsiWAK1 and its various functional recombinant proteins exhibit comparable activation abilities to the promoter of EAT1, potentially indicating a false positive. At the same time, the activation ability of OsiWAK1 towards EAT1pro is influenced by the TDR/TIP2 transcription complex and OsPDT1 function. In other words, the Dual-LUC assay may not accurately detect the indirect influence between OsiWAK1 and the promoter of EAT1. However, it can assess the indirect impact between OsiWAK1 and OsPDT1, as well as the indirect effect between OsiWAK1 and the TDR/TIP2.

Results

To evaluate the effectiveness of the Dual-LUC assay in detecting indirect gene effects, OsiWAK1 and EAT1p vectors were constructed as shown (Fig. 1A). After transiently expressing in rice protoplasts, it was found that EAT1p had a weak expression, similar to mini35Sp (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, OsiWAK1 vector had an activating effect on EAT1p (Fig. 1B). The EXD, DEL, and 2DEL vectors were generated by deleting the intracellular kinase domain, extracellular N-terminal domain, and extracellular domains of OsiWAK1, respectively, following the schematics (Fig. 1A). Studies showed no changes in the activation ability of EXD and DEL vectors on EAT1p, whereas 2DEL vector lost its ability to activate EAT1p (Fig. 1B). Due to the potential significance of the extracellular domain of OsiWAK1, we removed additional amino acids from EXD vector’s extracellular N-terminal, extracellular C-terminal, intracellular juxta-membrane region, and the amino acids linking the extracellular N- and C-terminal regions, respectively. As a result, we constructed 3DEL, 4DEL, 5DEL, and 6DEL vectors according to the schematics (Fig. 1A), and the Dual-LUC assay revealed that their activation ability on EAT1p did not change (Fig. 1B). The pectin-binding ability of wall-associated kinase (WAK) family proteins may benefit potentially from the hydrophobic basic amino acids in the extracellular domain, thus, the hydrophobic basic amino acids in OsiWAK1 were analyzed20. The analysis showed that OsiWAK1’s extracellular domain has six hydrophobic basic amino acids (Lys) (Fig. S1A–C), which were substituted with neutral amino acid (Gly) to create EXD(R70A), EXD(R109A), EXD(R128A), EXD(R145A), EXD(R223A), and EXD(R340A) vectors, respectively (Fig. S2A). However, no alterations in their activation ability were detected on EAT1p compared to the EXD vector (Fig. S2B). Additionally, both six hydrophobic basic amino acids were substituted with neutral amino acid (Gly) to create OsiWAK1(6spm) and EXD(6spm) vectors according to the schematics (Fig. 1A). Consist with above results, no changes were observed in their activation ability on EAT1p according to the Dual-LUC assay (Fig. 1B). The OsiWAK1/mini35Sp was utilized as a negative control (Fig. S2C). These results suggest that OsiWAK1's activation of EAT1p in the Dual-LUC assay is not affected by the deletion of the intracellular kinase domain, intracellular juxta-membrane domain, and modifications to the extracellular domain, except for 2DEL. In other words, the EAT1p reporter gene-based Dual-LUC assay is incapable of detecting alterations in the function of OsiWAK1 recombinant proteins, with the exception of 2DEL.

In previous research, it was demonstrated that the extracellular domain of OsiWAK1 controls its capacity for subcellular localization16. Consequently, the subcellular localization of the OsiWAK1 recombinant protein was examined to investigate the cause of the alteration in EAT1p activation ability induced by 2DEL vector (Fig. 2A, Fig. S3A). In accordance with previous research, the OsiWAK1-eYFP fluorescence signal was detected within spherical structures of secretory vesicles (Fig. 2B), whereas the DEL-eYFP fluorescence signal exhibited irregular structures (Fig. 2D)16. Additionally, the fluorescence signals of EXD-eYFP, 4DEL-eYFP, 6DEL-eYFP, OsiWAK1(6spm)-eYFP, EXD(6spm)-eYFP, EXD(R70A)-eYFP, EXD(R109A)-eYFP, EXD(R128A)-eYFP, EXD(R145A)-eYFP, EXD(R223A)-eYFP, and EXD(R340A)-eYFP were found to be consistent with that of OsiWAK1-eYFP, appearing in the spherical structures of the secretory vesicles (Fig. 2C,G,I–K, Fig. S3B–G); while the fluorescence signals of the 3DEL-eYFP and 5DEL-eYFP were in alignment with the DEL-eYFP, appearing in the irregular structures (Fig. 2F,H); furthermore, the subcellular localization fluorescence signal was lost in 2DEL-eYFP (Fig. 2E). Therefore, although the above mentioned Dual-LUC assay did not show any changes in the ability to activate EAT1p, the extracellular N-terminal domain and the intracellular juxta-membrane domain, do affect the subcellular localization of OsiWAK1.

Previous studies have revealed that the extracellular domain of OsiWAK1 is involved in pectin binding16, and the intracellular juxta-membrane domain may have a role in controlling receptor-like kinase dimers, which can influence kinase activity21. The EXD-Yn and EXD-Yc vectors were constructed according to the demonstrated procedure (Fig. S4A), and their fluorescence signals were evaluated in rice protoplasts through bimolecular fluorescence complementation assay (BiFC) experiments. Co-expression of EXD-Yn and EXD-Yc produced dot-like fluorescence signals in rice protoplasts (Fig. S4B), distinct from those of EXD-eYFP (Fig. 2C). Then we constructed the vectors 3DEL-Yn, 3DEL-Yc, 4DEL-Yn, 4DEL-Yc, 5DEL-Yn and 5DEL-Yc according to the schematics (Fig. S4A), and transient expression in rice protoplasts was carried out. It was discovered that co-expression of 3DEL-Yn/3DEL-Yc and 4DEL-Yn/4DEL-Yc both detected dot-like signals, respectively (Fig. S4C,D). However, under equivalent conditions, co-expression of 5DEL-Yn/5DEL-Yc, EXD-Yn/5DEL-Yc, and 5DEL-Yn/EXD-Yc in rice protoplasts did not produce any fluorescence signals, respectively, and they were also utilized as negative controls (Fig. S4E–G).

In a previous study, it was discovered that OsPDT1 is a protein homologous to Arabidopsis AtGAUT1 and participates in the regulation of pectin synthesis22, additionally, OsiWAK1 is influenced by the ospdt1 pectin pathway16. We employed the Dual-LUC assay to investigate the correlation between OsiWAK1 and OsPDT1. We generated AtGAUT1-GFP, OsPDT1-GFP, and GFP vectors, as illustrated in the figure (Fig. 3A). The study discovered that transient expressing in wild-type rice protoplasts, the AtGAUT1-GFP and OsPDT1-GFP vectors did not have any impact on the expression of EAT1p, however, the luminescence signals of AtGAUT1-GFP/OsiWAK1 and OsPDT1-GFP/OsiWAK1 were 0.69 and 0.48 times that of GFP/OsiWAK1, respectively (Fig. 3B). We further conducted transient expression experiments in ospdt1 mutant protoplasts. When expressing EAT1p transiently, the luminescence signal of ospdt1 mutant protoplasts was 2.67 times that of the wild-type (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, the luminescence signal of ospdt1 mutant protoplasts was 7.10 times that of the wild type when expressing OsiWAK1/EAT1p vectors (Fig. 3B). The OsPDT1-GFP/mini35Sp was utilized as a negative control (Fig. S2C). The findings suggest that enhanced expression of OsPDT1 and AtGAUT1 collaborates with OsiWAK1 to hinder the ability of EAT1p activation in rice protoplasts, conversely, the deficiency in function of OsPDT1 amplifies this ability.

The Dual-LUC assay not only detects the activation ability of transcription factors on promoters, but also their interactions with each other. For instance, the Dual-LUC assay utilizing the EAT1p reporter gene is capable of detecting the synergistic impact of TDR and TIP219. Additional tests were conducted using the Dual-LUC assay to investigate the correlation between OsiWAK1, TDR and TIP2. The TIP2, TDR, TDR_TIP2 vectors were constructed according to the schematics (Fig. 3A). TDR and TIP2 vectors exhibit a low activation capacity towards EAT1p, causing the luminescence signal to rise to 1.83 and 5.53 times that of EAT1p, respectively (Fig. 3C). However, the luminescence signal of EAT1p is significantly increased with the activation of TDR_TIP2 vector, reaching 17.88 times that of EAT1p (Fig. 3C). This finding is consistent with previous research indicating collaborative regulation of EAT1p expression by TDR and TIP219. Interestingly, co-expression of OsiWAK1 with TDR_TIP2 vectors in rice protoplasts yields a luminescence signal that is 33.40 times greater than EAT1p (Fig. 3C). When expressed separately, OsiWAK1, TDR/OsiWAK1, and TIP2/OsiWAK1 vectors produce luminescence signals that are 3.96, 3.72, and 7.11 times greater than EAT1p, respectively (Fig. 3C). The TDR/mini35Sp and TIP2/mini35Sp were utilized as negative controls (Fig. S2C). These findings propose that OsiWAK1 may cooperatively regulate the TDR/TIP2 transcription complex's activity in rice protoplasts to enhance its activation towards EAT1p.

To investigate the effectiveness of RNA interference (RNAi) in the Dual-LUC assay, we constructed OsiWAK1_GUSi, OsiWAK1_TDRi, OsiWAK1_TIP2i, OsiWAK1_PDT1i, GUSi, TDRi, TIP2i, PDT1i and OsiWAK1i vectors as depicted in the figure (Fig. 4A). After transient expression in rice protoplasts, the luminescence signals of OsiWAK1_TDRi/EAT1p, OsiWAK1_TIP2i/EAT1p, and OsiWAK1_PDT1i/EAT1p were 0.61, 0.68, and 1.81 times that of OsiWAK1_GUSi/EAT1p, respectively (Fig. 4B). Meanwhile, the luminescence signals of TDRi/EAT1p, TIP2i/EAT1p, and PDT1i/EAT1p were 0.66, 0.98, and 2.13 times that of GUSi/EAT1p, respectively (Fig. 4B). However, the activation of EAT1p by OsiWAK1i/TDR_TIP2 transiently expressed in rice protoplasts showed similarities with TDR_TIP2 or TDR_TIP2/GUSi (Fig. 4C), indicating that this Dual-LUC assay cannot establish a connection between TDR/TIP2 transcription complex and the RNAi of OsiWAK1. What is more noteworthy is that when OsiWAK1_PDT1i/TDR_TIP2 was transiently expressed in rice protoplasts, it exhibited reduced activation of EAT1p in comparison to OsiWAK1_GUSi/TDR_TIP2 and OsiWAK1/TDR_TIP2 (Fig. 4C). These findings demonstrate some limitations of the Dual-LUC assays.

Discussion

The WAK family proteins are important in the pectin signaling pathway23. In previous studies, it was found that OsiWAK1, a WAK family protein member, is impacted by pectin in ospdt1 mutations, and is crucial in the role of OsPDT1 function16. Furthermore, in the ospdt1 mutant, the highest level of EAT1 expression is observed at St8a–St8b but is absent at St8b–St9 in rice spikelets, however, in the ospdt1 background with overexpressing OsiWAK1, EAT1's expression peak appears at both St8a–St8b and St8b–St9 in spikelets, indicating that OsiWAK1 overexpression in the ospdt1 background modifies EAT1's expression pattern16. EAT1 is a transcription factor that regulates tapetal PCD, and the delay in tapetal PCD observed in eat1 mutants suggests a positive regulatory role for EAT1 in the PCD process of the tapetum17. Overexpression of OsiWAK1 leads to a delay in the tapetal PCD process, whereas RNAi lines display acceleration of PCD, suggesting a negative regulation of the tapetum PCD process by OsiWAK116. Thus, OsiWAK1 and EAT1 are all involved in the regulation of PCD in the tapetum, in addition, the mRNA in situ hybridization results revealed the expression of both OsiWAK1 and EAT1 in rice tapetum cells (Fig. S5A–C). These studies suggest a potential intricate regulatory relationship between OsiWAK1 and EAT1, both of which play a role in regulating the PCD process of the tapetum.

Because OsiWAK1 is a receptor kinase and not a transcription factor localized in the nucleus, its interaction with the EAT1 promoter expressing in the nucleus is indirect. Using OsiWAK1 and EAT1p as effector and reporter genes, respectively, in a Dual-LUC assay, we conducted a preliminary investigation into the detection effect of the indirect interaction between genes. The research indicates that OsiWAK1 is capable of triggering the expression of EAT1p in protoplasts of wild-type (Fig. 1B). Further investigation into the regions responsible for activating EAT1p expression in OsiWAK1 indicates that only the 2DEL, lacking the entire extracellular domain, cannot activate EAT1p (Fig. 1A,B). All other modifications made to the intracellular and extracellular domains did not impact OsiWAK1's capability to activate EAT1p (Fig. 1A,B). In previous studies, the involvement of the extracellular domain of OsiWAK1 in assembly with pectin impacts its subcellular localization16. The research discovered that removing the N-terminal extracellular domain (DEL-eYFP and 3DEL-eYFP) and the intracellular juxtamembrane region (5DEL-eYFP) has an impact on the subcellular distribution of their fluorescence signals (Fig. 2D,F,H). In addition, the fluorescence signal of 2DEL-eYFP, which lacks the extracellular domain entirely, vanishes completely (Fig. 2E). None of the other alterations to the domains had an impact on the distribution of fluorescence signals from the OsiWAK1-eYFP recombinant protein (Fig. 2B,C,G,I–K and Fig. S3B–G). Thus, it is our belief that the lack of activation ability of 2DEL to EAT1p results from its abnormal accumulation within protoplasts, rather than the extracellular domain of OsiWAK1 mediating EAT1p activation. In previous experiments, it was discovered that modifications in the extracellular domain's pectin-binding ability have an impact on OsiWAK1's subcellular localization in secretory vesicles16. This can be observed in the absence of fluorescence signal of DEL-eYFP in secretory vesicles (Fig. 2D). Interestingly, 5DEL-eYFP also displayed similar fluorescence signals to DEL-eYFP (Fig. 2H), which offers additional understanding of the involvement of the intracellular juxtamembrane region in OsiWAK1 protein's function. For receptor-like kinases, the intracellular juxtamembrane region plays a vital role in its function, encompassing dimer formation, ligand binding, and kinase activity21. This study did not observe any fluorescence signal in the BiFC experiment for 5DEL-Yn/5DEL-Yc (Fig. S4E), in contrast to the significant fluorescence signal detected in EXD-Yn/EXD-Yc (Fig. S4B). The results of these preliminary experiments indicate that the intracellular juxtamembrane region of OsiWAK1 not only affects its cellular localization, but also potentially modulates its function in BiFC assay. Thus, it is believed that the intracellular juxtamembrane region is important for the function of OsiWAK1, but more evidence is needed to confirm this conclusion. Consequently, the deletion of different regions in OsiWAK1 has no impact on the ability to activate EAT1p, which suggests that OsiWAK1's activation of EAT1p in the Dual-LUC assay could be a false positive.

Additional research was conducted to assess the detection capabilities of the Dual-LUC assay for indirect gene interactions. As the expression pattern of EAT1 is altered in ospdt1 spikelets16, the study aimed to determine whether the correlation between OsiWAK1 and its upstream regulator OsPDT1 could be detected using the reporter gene EAT1p. The study revealed that overexpression of OsPDT1 collaborates with OsiWAK1 to hinder the ability of EAT1p activation in wild-type rice protoplasts (Fig. 3B). The use of AtGAUT1, the homologous protein of OsPDT1 in Arabidopsis, yielded a similar result (Fig. 3B). Although OsiWAK1's ability to activate EAT1p may be a false positive, the Dual-LUC assay suggests potential interactions between OsiWAK1 and OsPDT1, as well as between OsiWAK1 and AtGAUT1. The OsPDT1, along with its homolog AtGAUT1, participates in pectin synthesis, therefore, it is possible that OsiWAK1 is linked to pectin pathway16,22. Interestingly, overexpressing OsiWAK1 in ospdt1 mutant protoplasts significantly enhanced EAT1p activation compared to both wild-type OsiWAK1/EAT1p combinations and ospdt1/EAT1p combinations (Fig. 3B), which further verifies the interaction between OsPDT1 and OsiWAK1.

The TDR/TIP2 transcription complex is thought to have a crucial function in EAT1 expression18,19. Consistent with this, the TDR_TIP2 can significantly enhance the expression of EAT1p, compared to TDR or TIP2 individually (Fig. 3C). OsiWAK1, TDR and TIP2 were used to assess the detection capabilities of the Dual-LUC assay for indirect gene interactions, and revealed that OsiWAK1 can significantly enhance the activation ability of the TDR/TIP2 transcription complex to EAT1p when compared to TDR or TIP2 alone (Fig. 3C). These experiments indicate that OsiWAK1 may have a connection to the function of TDR/TIP2 transcription complex in rice protoplasts, but the underlying mechanism is currently unknown.

It seems that the Dual-LUC assay could detect the relationship between OsiWAK1 and OsPDT1, as well as the relationship between OsiWAK1 and TDR/TIP2 transcription complex, in rice protoplasts. We performed BiFC assay to further verify that the interaction between OsiWAK1-cEYFP and OsPDT1-nEYFP, TDR-nEYFP, or TIP2-nEYFP is indirect (Fig. S6). RNAi was employed in a Dual-LUC assay, revealing the impact of OsPDT1, TDR, and TIP2 on OsiWAK1’s activation ability to EAT1p within protoplasts, respectively. Compared to complete gene cloning, RNA interference proves to be a simpler approach, making it crucial in studying gene relationships24. Compared with OsiWAK1/GUSi, the expression activity of EAT1p was significantly decreased by the combinations of OsiWAK1/TDRi and OsiWAK1/TIP2i, respectively, and was significantly increased by OsiWAK1/PDT1i (Fig. 4B). In the control group without OsiWAK1, only the OsPDT1 RNAi experiment significantly enhanced the expression activity of EAT1p (Fig. 4B). This indicates that the Dual-LUC assay is capable of detecting the indirect interactions among genes by employing RNAi, but the RNAi in the Dual-LUC assay also has limitations. For instance, RNAi of OsiWAK1 in protoplasts does not impact the activation ability of TDR/TIP2 transcription complex to EAT1p (Fig. 4B). Further analysis revealed that OsiWAK1 exhibits significantly lower expression levels in rice seedling leaves and sheaths as compared to roots (Fig. S5D). This may explain why RNAi of OsiWAK1 in rice seedling protoplasts was ineffective, but more evidence is needed to confirm this conclusion.

Previous research suggests that the regulatory mechanism involving OsiWAK1, OsPDT1, TDR, TIP2 and EAT1 is complex, and cannot be fully understood through the results of the Dual-LUC assay in protoplasts. For instance, in the tapetum layer of rice, OsPDT1 and OsiWAK1 function as negative regulators of the PCD process16, whereas TDR, TIP2, and EAT1 act as positive regulators17–19. However, the results from the Dual-LUC assay conducted on rice protoplasts indicate that OsiWAK1 enhances the activation ability of TDR/TIP2 transcription complex and ospdt1 on ETA1p (Fig. 3B,C), contradicting their relationship in the tapetal PCD regulatory network. In order to clarify this, we suggest that the alterations in the EAT1p expression among rice protoplasts under various conditions merely demonstrate the capability of OsiWAK1 to interact with either OsPDT1 or TDR/TIP2 network, rather than the exact regulatory relationship between them. Consist with this, when OsiWAK1_PDT1i/TDR_TIP2 combination was co-expressed in rice protoplasts, its ability to activate EAT1p decreased significantly compared to OsiWAK1/TDR_TIP2 or OsiWAK1_GUSi/TDR_TIP2 combinations (Fig. 4C), which is the opposite of the activation effect caused by PDT1i on EAT1p expression.

In order to explain above results, a model is proposed (Fig. 5): OsPDT1, a galacturonosyltransferase gene, that affects components of the pectin-mediated signaling pathway, such as OsiWAK1, thereby regulating tapetal PCD16. However, the mechanism by which OsiWAK1 participates in the regulation of PCD in the tapetum remains unclear. Our results indicate that OsiWAK1 exerts an indirect influence on the activity of TDR/TIP2. Given that OsiWAK1 negatively regulates the PCD process16, while TDR/TIP2 positively regulates it17–19, it can be postulated that OsiWAK1 can negatively regulate the activity of TDR/TIP2 in the rice tapetum. Previous studies showed that the TDR/TIP2 transcription complex directly controls the expression of EAT1, which could activate the expression of aspartic protease genes such as OsAP25 and OsAP37 to trigger tapetal PCD17. TDR/TIP2 also plays a significant role in the tapetal PCD involving gibberellin (GA) signals. For instance, GA signals can facilitate the degradation of SLENDER RICE1 (SLR1), thereby enhancing the activation ability of Undeveloped Tapetum1 (UDT1) and TDR in the tapetum, which positively regulate the tapetal PCD process25. Moreover, TDR can directly activate the expression of BAYMAX1 (BM1), and BM1 can work synergistically with SLR1 to enhance the expression of genes such as CYP703A3, OsPKS1, DPW, and ABCG15, which participate in the regulation of rice tapetum on the formation of pollen exine26–28. In addition, UDP-GLUCOSE 4-EPIMERASE1 (OsUGE1), a key factor involved in UDP-glucose signaling, is regulated downstream of TDR and TIP2, and can directly activate the expression of EAT129. Moreover, the absence of OsUGE1 can also affect the balance of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in anthers, thereby participating in the regulation of tapetal PCD30. These works facilitate a more profound comprehension of the regulatory networks of OsiWAK1 and TDR/TIP2.

A proposed model for the function of OsiWAK1 in tapetal PCD. OsPDT1 exerts its influence on the pectin signaling component, OsiWAK1, via the pectin synthesis pathway. OsiWAK1, in turn, can indirectly inhibit the activity of TDR/TIP2. GA gibberellin, ROS reactive oxygen species, UDP-Glc UDP-glucose.

In our study, using the Dual-LUC assay on rice protoplasts, we discovered that the EAT1p activation by OsiWAK1 was probably a false positive. Additionally, a relationship may potentially exist between OsiWAK1 and OsPDT1, as well as OsiWAK1 and TDR/TIP2 transcription complex. This study investigates the capability of the Dual-LUC assay to detect indirect gene interactions: it is possible that directly detecting A gene and promoter of B gene (Bpro) with the Dual-LUC assay may lead to inaccurate results. However, if A or (and) C gene could impact the expression activity of Bpro in Dual-LUC assay, it is feasible to reveal the presence of a relationship between A and C genes by comparing the Bpro expression activity in A/Bpro, C/Bpro, and A/C/Bpro. This study presents a potential approach to detecting genetic relationships and increasing the efficiency of gene interaction research. Additionally, it also offers a potential means of quantifying gene function, thereby enabling the assessment of functional differences between distinct haplotypes of genes, particularly those resulting from natural variation sites within the coding region. Thus, it may improve the effectiveness of methods for identifying elite genes in GWAS.

Methods

Plasmid construction

OsiWAK1-eYFP, DEL-eYFP and OsPDT1-GFP had been used in our previous study16. Other plasmids were constructed in accordance with schematics and the native vector is PAN580 or pGreenII0800-LUC. The different fragments of OsiWAK1 were amplified using PCR, subsequently, the fragments were fused using cohesive end ligation or homologous recombination to construct the OsiWAK1 recombinant vector. The hydrophobic basic amino acids in OsiWAK1 were analyzed according to previous study20. The primer sequences used and methods are listed in Supplemental Tables 1–3.

Protoplast isolation and transfection

Wild type rice (Xinong 1B) were cultured in nutrient soil at 28 ℃ under a 12 h/12 h (light/dark) photoperiod with illumination of 12,000 lx for 10 days, then washed with distilled water and soaked in 0.6 M mannitol for plasmolysis. After 30 min, the stemlike leaf and sheath tissues were cut into approximately 0.5 mm strips through sharp blade, followed by enzymatic digestion (0.6 M mannitol, 10 mM MES, 1.5% cellulase R-10, and 0.75% mecerozyme R-10, pH 5.7) in the dark with shaking at 40 rpm for 4 h. The protoplasts were filtered through 75 μm mesh, an equal volume of W5 buffer (154 mM NaCl, 125 mM CaCl2, 5 mM KCl, 2 mM MES, and 5 mM glucose, pH 5.7) was added. The solution was centrifuged at 200 × g for 2 min and washed with W5 buffer once. After standing for 30 min on ice, the pellet was resuspended with MMG buffer (0.6 M mannitol, 15 mM MgCl2, and 4 mM MES, pH 5.7). The final cell concentration was almost 5 × 106 cells/ml as measured using a hemocytometer.

The ospdt1 mutant is identified in our previous study16. For the isolation of homozygous ospdt1 mutant protoplasts, the seeds of ospdt1 heterozygous plants were cultured under the aforementioned conditions. Subsequently, 210 seedlings were sequenced, resulting in the acquisition of 45 homozygous ospdt1 seedlings and 53 wild-type seedlings. Protoplasts were isolated from ospdt1 mutants and wild-type seedlings using the aforementioned methods, respectively. The primer sequences used are listed in Supplemental Table 4.

For transfection, 10 μl plasmid, 100 μl protoplasts, and 110 μl PEG solution (0.2 M mannitol, 100 mM CaCl2, and 40% PEG 4000, pH 7.5) were added to centrifuge tubes, mixed immediately, then after standing for 20 min at room temperature in the dark, 440 μl W5 buffer was added and mixed to terminate transfection. Finally, the solution was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 3 min, and the pellet was resuspended and cultured in W5 buffer for 10 h.

Dual-LUC Assay

For the dual-luciferase reporter gene assay, equal amount of effector plasmids and reporter plasmids were transiently co-expressed in wild type or ospdt1 protoplasts. The FLUC/RLUC signals were detected using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega) and a Glomax® 20/20 luminometer (Promega) in accordance with the supplier’s instructions. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics veriosn 26.0 (IBM Inc., Armonk, USA) software (https://www.ibm.com/support/pages/downloading-ibm-spss-statistics-26).

Subcellular localization

For intracellular distribution of OsiWAK1 recombinant proteins. Each plasmid was transiently expressed in wild-type protoplasts, and the protoplasts were photographed using a Zeiss LSM800 confocal microscope.

BiFC Assay

For bimolecular fluorescence complementation assay, equal amount of plasmids were transiently co-expressed in wild-type, and the protoplasts were photographed using a Zeiss LSM800 confocal microscope. Images were obtained from one Z-stack photograph or the maximum intensity projection.

Expression analysis

Wild type rice seedings were cultured in nutrient soil at 28 ℃ under a 12 h/12 h (light/dark) photoperiod with illumination of 12,000 lx for 10 days. RNA was extracted from the roots, leaves, and sheaths to analyze the expression of OsiWAK1 via RT-qPCR. 313 bp and 356 bp OsiWAK1 cDNA, and 332 bp EAT1 cDNA were transcribed and labeled in vitro using the DIG RNA Labeling Kit (Roche), respectively. Pretreatment of sections, hybridization, and immunological detection were performed as described previously16. The primers used are listed in Supplemental Table 4.

Research involving plants

The wild type rice (Xinong 1B) and ospdt1 mutant used in this study is maintained in the Rice Research Institute, Southwest University, China. This study complied with institutional and national guidelines for experimental research involving plants.
