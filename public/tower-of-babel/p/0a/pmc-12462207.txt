An analytical pipeline for dose-response effect: laboratory tests assessment and early pregnancy preeclampsia risk
Authors: Yinyao Ma, Xiao Wang, Jinjiang Mao, Hanlin Lv, Yanhua Ma, Hua Wu, Chun Zhang, Lei Wang, Xuxia Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFew studies have explored the associations between early pregnancy laboratory tests and preeclampsia, leaving rich first-trimester data underutilized. This study introduces a pipeline, integrating Generalized Additive Models with dose-response analysis, to elucidate the complex associations.MethodsOur pipeline includes identifying turning points (pathway A) and risk/protective intervals (pathway B). We used Generalized Additive Models with cubic regression splines. For pathway A, turning points were identified at the second derivative extrema of nonlinear monotonic fitted probability curves. For pathway B, risk/protective intervals were determined where the Odds Ratios equaled 1. Propensity score matching and the risk ratios were used for evaluation. This pipeline was applied to a retrospective GZ cohort of 12,474 pregnancies and evaluated in an external GG cohort.ResultsThrough analyzing 99 unique laboratory tests within GZ cohort, our pipeline highlighted 16 exhibiting optimal turning points via pathway A and 4 showing risk/protective intervals through pathway B. The turning points from pathway A were comparable to those from traditional piecewise logistic regression. Evaluation within GG cohort confirmed the statistical robustness. Additionally, our experiments demonstrated that hyperparameter fine-tuning of Generalized Additive Models fitting had minimal effect, and pathway output metrics are sensitive to pregnancy stages, leading to considerable variability in conclusions.ConclusionsThe proposed pipeline was rigorously validated for its efficacy across two independent cohorts, achieving consistent outcomes. Furthermore, the laboratory tests identified mostly align with conclusions from prior studies. We believe it both advances our understanding of the mechanisms during early pregnancy disorder and offers vital insights for early preeclampsia detection and preventive interventions.Trail registrationNA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-07940-2.

Background

Predicting disease progression through significant biomarkers, particularly in conditions like preeclampsia (PE), remains a pivotal yet challenging focus in medical research [1, 2]. The rich data available from early pregnancy, particular during the first trimester, including routine laboratory tests covered by medical insurance, remain underutilized and offer significant potential for clinical and research application. For example, uric acid (UA) levels - with a recommended cutoff of 4 mg/dL [3, 4], have demonstrated a significant association between cutoff-based grouping and the probability of PE across diverse ethnic cohorts [5].

In epidemiology, dose-response assessment is key to understanding the relationship between exposure levels and disease risk. Significant shifts, termed change-points [6] or breakpoints [7], often occur at specific exposure levels. Analysis typically differentiates between two scenarios: (1) Threshold effects [8], where the relationship initiates only upon exceeding a certain level, and (2) Inflection points [9, 10], where significant changes occur in the rate of the response curve.

Various studies [11–16] have applied a broad range of evidence-based statistical and modeling techniques to elucidate the link between a singular clinical or environmental factor and observed health outcomes, including PE and other pregnancy-related outcomes. For instance, Ma et al. [17] analyzed 24-hour urinary excretion data to identify specific intervals associated with cardiovascular risk in a dose-response model. Guo et al. [18] explored the dose-response relationships of diabetic kidney disease with routine laboratory tests, while Du et al. [19] evaluated the impact of abnormal serum lipids on sleep duration, both indicating significant associations. Within these studies, leveraging Generalized Additive Models (GAM) to construct fitting models through the dose-response relationship is a widely accepted methodology. Although the effectiveness of GAM in mapping complex relationships and identifying nonlinear trends has been well demonstrated, two-piecewise logistic regression modeling is often used to estimate change-points [20, 21].

In this study, we proposed a primary pipeline for exploring turning points (refer to change-points) on the dose-response GAM fitting curve, and a derived pipeline, based on the Odds Ratios (ORs) to identify protective/risk intervals. Our exploration is founded on the hypothesis that the GAM and the segmented logistic regression are consistent in terms of data fitting when characterizing the change-points; thus, the turning points observed in the GAM fitting curve should correspond to the inflection points of maximizing model likelihood in the segmented logistic regression.

However, adopting GAM curve-based definitions for change-points requires addressing several issues: (1) The selection of dependent variable for modeling — the occurrence probability of the outcome or ORs: a gap exists in exploratory application research concerning such choice and its implications; (2) Statistical considerations: The variability among cohorts complicates the integration of statistical errors and population-specific nuances into analytical findings; (3) Principle generalizability: Universal principles, transcending specific laboratory tests or populations, require validation and applicability across diverse cohorts; (4) Model configuration selection: Previous study often showcases models employing a broad spectrum of parameter combinations without clear disclosure on the selection or tuning process, failing to establish standardized metrics for parameter set selection.

To address these issues, it is necessary to create a methodological pipeline adept at analyzing complex interactions between various laboratory tests and the risk of development of PE across large populations. This effort included integrating exploratory experimentations and correlational analyses to set a firm foundation for deeper exploration into disease mechanisms and the development of laboratory biomarker-driven screening models.

In our research, we introduced a novel approach that advances two distinct dependent variables in parallel, resulting in an integrated analytical pipeline. By employing a specifically designed workflow with the GAM fitting model, we facilitated automated analysis of extensive laboratory test data. This approach enabled the identification of critical points and intervals on the fitting curves, which would signify key intervals for PE risk stratification and demarcate variations in PE risk levels.

Methods

Data preparation

In this study, we retrospectively collected data from two pre-established cohorts: GZ, hailing from the People’s Hospital of the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, China (approval number: KT-KJT-2021-67), and GG, from the People’s Hospital of Guigang, China (approval number: GYLLPJ-20220424-42).

The GZ cohort encompassed 12,474 pregnancies receiving prenatal care before 14 weeks of gestation from April 2012 to September 2021, whereas the GG cohort included 1,991 pregnancies under identical inclusion and exclusion criteria from January 2020 to December 2021. Both cohorts included PE outcomes alongside comprehensive laboratory test data. PE occurrences were determined from inpatient diagnoses, which were classified as positive upon the onset of PE. The GZ cohort was utilized for pipeline construction and validation, while the GG cohort was leveraged for external evaluation.

To manage outliers, symmetric trimming was applied to the laboratory data distribution from both ends. Additionally, laboratory tests demonstrating insufficient variability were excluded. If a patient underwent laboratory tests at multiple time points before 14 weeks of gestation, the first one served as the exposure factor for this study.

Pipeline construction

We devised a pipeline to autonomously investigate and assess the correlations between the individual laboratory tests and corresponding PE outcomes, which encompassed two parallel pathways – turning point identification pathway and interval identification pathway (Fig. 1(A) and (B)). Pathway A: turning point identification

The turning point pathway utilized monotonic probability fitting curve to determine a threshold, which established the dose-response relationship between the laboratory tests and PE development with a possible change point where the risk of PE would start to change. It could be served as a warning point for close monitoring, or as an intervention threshold beyond which prompt and appropriate intervention would be recommended.

We applied GAM for smooth curve fitting, to examine the quantitative-effect relationship between laboratory tests and PE probability using cubic regression splines [22, 23]. The fitting curve covered the original range of independent variables entirely, providing a 95% confidence interval (CI). As we exclusively focused on the nonlinear and monotonic GAM fitting curves, we first applied a criterion of effective degrees of freedom (edf) ≥ 2 in the GAM fitting process. In the context of clinical modeling, edf quantify the flexibility of the smooth function, that relates a predictor to the outcome. An edf of 1 implies a linear association, values between 1 and 2 suggest a weakly nonlinear (near-linear) trend, while an edf ≥ 2 indicates increasing complexity in the predictor–outcome relationship, which may capture important clinical nonlinearity [19, 24, 25]. Additionally, we employed |τ| ≥ 0.6 and a significance level of p < 0.05 in the Mann-Kendall test. The Kendall Tau, or Kendall rank correlation coefficient, which detects monotonic trends of the fitted curve, varies between − 1 and 1; it is positive when the trend increases and negative when the trend decreases [26, 27]. Nonlinearity and monotonicity were considered valid if these criteria were both met. If nonlinearity and monotonicity were observed, the second order derivatives were estimated over sufficiently small intervals, adopting a step size of 10−5. If any of the criteria were not met, the laboratory test was excluded from further pipeline analysis. Finally, we investigated the turning points where the curve exhibited essential changes in its geometric properties and estimated its uncertainty interval (UI) [28], adopting the maximum range that could potentially satisfy the criteria of turning points. Pathway B: risk/protective interval identification

The interval pathway utilized dichotomization of laboratory tests to reflect the unit changes in the ORs and to identify risk and protective intervals in the univariate analysis. This approach delineates ranges associated with elevated or lowered risk of PE occurrence.

ORs for PE were estimated through a sliding threshold approach on continuous laboratory test data, employing iterative dichotomization. For each incremented cutoff across the data spectrum, ORs were computed by dividing the odds of outcomes above the threshold by those below, at a granularity of the smallest difference between consecutive laboratory test data. Following the calculation of ORs, we used GAM and cubic regression splines [22, 23] to explore the quantitative relationship between each laboratory threshold and ORs. Predictions of PE ORs were generated using the same laboratory threshold grid with CI on the fitted curve.

The initial criterion to identify risk/protective intervals involved assessing intersection points with the reference line OR = 1 on the curve. In our study, we restricted our analysis to laboratory tests that exhibited only a single intersection with the reference line. We determined the two points on the lower and upper CIs of the OR curve that were vertically closest to the reference line from above and below, respectively. The risk interval is the portion of the OR curve where the lower CI exceeded OR = 1, while the protective interval is the segment where the upper CI remained below OR = 1. Statistical analysis

The effectiveness of our pipeline was evaluated using Risk Ratios (RRs) for PE development. RRs were calculated by dividing the incidence rate of PE in the subgroup with higher laboratory test values by that with lower values. To assess the impact of turning points and risk/protective intervals, and reduce selection bias induced by baseline characteristics, we employed the propensity score matching (PSM) [29] to evaluate the statistical significance of outcome difference, incorporating confounders of age, parity, body mass index (BMI) before pregnancy and the gestational week when the laboratory test was conducted. The propensity score was estimated by a multivariable logistic regression model. The 1:1 nearest neighbor matching protocol without replacement was performed [30–32], with a caliper width at 0.2 times the standard deviation (SD) of the logit propensity score [33, 34].

To ensure turning points and risk/protective intervals hold statistical significance, we mandated that the minority subgroups, with fewer participants, satisfy a minimum sample size requirement. We employed a two-sided non-parametric test, setting the significance level at 0.05, with a power of 0.8. The majority subgroups were matched at a 1:1 ratio for comparison. The minimum minority sample size required would be 1,017 under a ratio of 60% increase margin, and 565 under a ratio of 60% decrease margin, respectively.

Software packages

The scripts used for pipeline construction and validation are available on GitHub [https://github.com/Lvhanlin-wh/DoseResponse-Preeclampsia-Pipeline]. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) version 4.3.2 with the packages mgcv, Kendall, MatchIt, segmented and rBayesianOptimization. The statistical differences were considered significant at p < 0.05 (two-sided).

External evaluation in GG cohort

To assess the robustness and efficacy of the turning points and risk/protective intervals derived from our pipeline for the GZ cohort, we directly applied them to another independent cohort, the GG cohort. This allowed us to evaluate and examine the generalizability of our pipeline.

Among the 16 selected laboratory tests in pathway A from the GZ cohort, 8 (50%) were identified in the GG cohort; while for the 4 selected laboratory tests from pathway B, 2 (50%) were found in the GG cohort. It is noteworthy that all reticulocytes-related laboratory tests, such as reticulocytes, reticulocytes (%), medium fluorescence reticulocytes (MFR), low fluorescence reticulocytes (LFR), and immature reticulocyte fraction (IRF), were absent from the GG cohort, due to variations in practice across different hospitals. The RRs of PE derived from both pathways, along with the corresponding 95% CIs in the GG cohort are illustrated in Fig. 2(C) and (D). In the analysis of the 10 overlapping laboratory tests, it was observed that 9 exhibited RRs greater than 1, while 1 showed RRs less than 1. However, we observed that the CI boundaries displayed a wider range, often encompassing 1 and occasionally extending to substantially distant values, thus Fig. 2(D) was presented in log scale. All the CIs of RR intersected with RR = 1.

Additional research

Previous studies have explored extending gestational age up to 20 weeks to investigate the association between laboratory tests and PE [35–38]. In line with this approach, we expanded the inclusion criteria for pregnancies from 8 to 20 weeks and focused on the first laboratory test conducted during this period. As a result, the sample size increased to 18,309 pregnancies in the GZ cohort, with a PE occurrence rate of 5.34%. Notably, the GZ cohort exhibited variations in outcomes across two pathways, identified 10 additional laboratory tests, such as gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT), free triiodothyronine (FT3) and activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT). Meanwhile, we observed that several common laboratory tests demonstrated closely aligned turning points, including reticulocytes (105.3 VS 106.6), reticulocytes (%) (2.28 VS 2.29), RBC (4.62 VS 4.01), platelet count (PLT) (303.41 VS 300.08), plateletcrit (PCT) (0.21 VS 0.34), monocytes (%) (5.93 VS 6.53) and apolipoprotein B (ApoB, 0.84 VS 0.82). Further details are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Meanwhile, few studies have explored GAM hyperparameter tuning, which piqued our interest, and we diligently explored hyperparameter tuning during the GAM fitting phases within the pipeline. The exploration encompassed a comprehensive array of hyperparameters, such as spline types [22, 23, 39–41] and different smoothing parameters in wide ranges. We employed several optimization strategies, including Bayesian optimization [42, 43] and minimized the Mean Squared Error (MSE) using five-fold cross-validation (CV). Despite the extensive hyperparameter tuning efforts, we observed minimal change in the outcomes of GAM fitting: turning point locations in pathway A shifted by 0.21–1.36% relative to the total range, and risk/protective interval boundaries in pathway B by 0.13–0.96%. More details on hyperparameter tuning and Bayesian optimization are provided in the csv file in the Supplementary Material.

Discussion

Principal findings

Previous studies highlight the necessity of pipeline research

In this study, our proposed pipeline revealed significant metrics derived from early pregnancy laboratory tests, including trends, turning points, and risk/protective intervals.

We attempted to validate the practical relevance of our study through a meta-analysis. Unfortunately, relevant studies are limited for two main reasons: (1) Most existing research is confirmatory analysis of biochemical differences post-diagnosis. Since early-onset PE diagnosis requires at least 20 weeks of gestation, and laboratory parameters vary significantly across different gestational periods, direct comparisons become problematic; (2) A substantial proportion of the laboratory tests we explored have rarely been studied as PE risk factors in prior studies, leading to a lack of comparable research. Our quantitative analyses, based on dose-response analysis from a single cohort, also suggest a scarcity of comparable literatures.

Nevertheless, we are encouraged that overall trends in selected laboratory tests generally align with previous studies, despite some discrepancies. For example, the pipeline identified a critical threshold for high-density lipid cholesterol (HDL-C) at 1.45 (UI: 1.43–1.47) mmol/L in pathway A, aligning with (Wang et al. 2018) findings from a 17,610-patient cohort in China, which recommended a 1.23 mmol/L cutoff for HDL-C in early pregnancy [36]. For total cholesterol (TC), we determined a risk interval of ≥ 3.91 mmol/L in pathway B, slightly differing from the ≥ 5.64 mmol/L threshold in the same study that analyzed HDL-C [36]. These discrepancies do not diminish the effectiveness of our pipeline, as it has been rigorously validated. These contradictions are likely attributed to differences in cohort characteristics, data processing methods and statistical approaches, underscoring the need for further investigation. It is noteworthy that certain well-studied laboratory tests in relevant research, such as UA and GGT, known as risk factors for PE [5, 63], were not selected in our pipeline. The exclusion stemmed from UA’s edf falling within the range of 1 and 2 (indicating a semi-linear association), and GGT’s |τ| falling below 0.6 (indicating a non-monotonic relationship). If we relaxed the linearity filter condition from edf ≥ 2 to edf ≥ 1.5, UA would remain in pathway A with a turning point close to 4 mg/dL as expected.

The application of pipeline might benefit clinical screening and prediction

In this study, our proposed pipeline not only offers valuable insights for the discovery of mechanisms driving PE but also facilitates its early screening. Our pipeline captures specific warning signs for clinicians to identify high-risk PE patients in early pregnancy, serving as a warning point for close monitoring or as an intervention threshold for appropriate clinical intervention. By incorporating these parameters into routine screening tests and clinical workflows, clinicians can enhance the early detection and management of PE risk, as depicted in Supplementary Fig. 4. For instance, patients with HDL-C levels below 1.45 mmol/L or MFR level above 3.91 mmol/L should be monitored more closely. If additional risk factors for PE are observed, interventions should be considered.

Drawing inspiration from the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) [64] (combining neutrophils, lymphocytes and platelet count into one single index to facilitate PE diagnosis [65]) and the Area Deprivation Index (ADI) [66] (integrating population-level socioeconomic indicators into one composite index to examine neighborhoods vulnerability and its impact on various health outcomes, including PE [67] and other pregnancy-related conditions [68, 69]), we conducted experiments to develop a preliminary predictive model to stratify high-risk PE patients.

This involved the integration of 15 categorized laboratory tests, leveraging the output from the GZ cohort via pathway A. We anticipate significant enhancements in predictive power through integrating our pipeline with more comprehensive data sets and sophisticated modeling techniques. Specifically, incorporating data beyond laboratory tests from early pregnancy—such as commonly used sociodemographic characteristics, health services utilization, obstetric history, medical comorbidities, mental and behavioral health comorbidities, pharmacy data, genetic variations and social determinant of health (SDOH) [70]—can provide a more holistic and accurate view for PE prediction. Limitations

Our study presents a novel analytical pipeline, yet certain limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, while our pipeline demonstrates robust associations between early pregnancy laboratory tests and PE risk, we acknowledge that the retrospective design does not allow for the establishment of causal relationships.

A significant methodological consideration lies in the employment of stringent filtering and constraints. Specifically, pathway A required nonlinearity and monotonicity, while pathway B was restricted to GAM fitting curves with one intersection point. Although these rigorous conditions ensured robust findings for the identified markers in our analysis, it presented a trade-off and led to exclusion of other potentially relevant markers. For example, some well-known PE risk factors, such as UA and GGT, were excluded from the pipeline. This exclusion not only impeded us from validating our pipeline against existing research for specific markers, but also potentially restricted the exploration of other less-studied laboratory tests. Future research could explore the impact of relaxing these strict criteria to identify a broader range of relevant biomarkers and broaden the applicability of the pipeline.

Another limitation of our analysis pertains to the PSM process, which incorporated a limited number of confounders. While the selected four confounders for PSM were chosen based on clinical relevance and data availability, we acknowledge that the constrained selection may have compromised the accuracy of matching procedure, potentially impacting the robustness and precision of the evaluation metric. Future studies incorporating a more comprehensive set of sociodemographic, health services utilization, and obstetric history data would enhance the accuracy of the matching procedure and provide a more robust evaluation.

Regarding the generalizability of our findings, while we sought to validate the statistical robustness of our initial cohort’s findings with an independent cohort, and comparisons with prior research revealed relatively consistent findings, the wide CIs observed in the validation cohort indicate a need for further large-scale independent cohort validation. Specifically, findings for TSH, HGB and GLOB appear more reliable, while RBC, Eosinophils (%), and PLT require additional investigation.

Finally, the limited existing literature for some of the identified laboratory tests underscores the need for further independent validation and mechanistic studies to fully elucidate their roles in PE pathogenesis.

Conclusions

In summary, our study successfully developed an automated pipeline that elucidates the relationship between early pregnancy laboratory tests and the occurrence of PE through dose-response analysis, identifying critical turning points and risk/protective intervals. The pipeline was evaluated in two distinct cohorts, confirming the consistency of our findings with both the data and existing literature. These insights offer promising clinical intervention points for further investigation. They pave the way for a cost-effective early screening protocol and provide opportunities for timely intervention and management strategies in pregnancies at risk of PE.
