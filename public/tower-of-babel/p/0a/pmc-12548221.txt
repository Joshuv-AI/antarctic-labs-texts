Exploring genotype-phenotype correlation of FSHR polymorphisms in polycystic ovary syndrome
Authors: Mandeep Kaur, Sukhjashanpreet Singh, Archana Beri, Anupam Kaur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeSingle nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in FSHR were reported to increase PCOS susceptibility. The present study was conducted to analyse the association of FSHR polymorphisms (rs1394205, rs11692782, and rs2349415) with PCOS in Punjab, India.MethodsA case-control study comprised of 823 women (443 PCOS cases and 380 healthy controls). Along with anthropometric measurements, lipid and hormonal profiles (LH, FSH, and testosterone levels) were also recorded. The genotyping of FSHR polymorphisms was performed with PCR-RFLP method. Continuous variables were compared using the student’s t-test while the genetic association analysis was performed utilizing chi-square, binary logistic regression, and odds ratio with a 95% confidence interval. All the statistical analyses were performed on SPSS v.21 and GraphPad 9.ResultsA significant association of rs2349415 polymorphism was observed with PCOS. The recessive model conferred higher PCOS risk (Adjusted OR-1.64, p = 0.012). The genetic association of rs1394205 and rs11692782 remained non-significant (p > 0.05). rs2349415 and rs1394205 were significantly related to dyslipidemia, while rs11692782 had shown a role in the modulation of gonadotropic hormones. Haploview analysis showed a modest linkage disequilibrium in the block of 133 kb, and no association of FSHR haplotypes was identified with PCOS.ConclusionThe present findings concluded that a polymorphism, rs2349415, has a significant role in PCOS in the Punjabi population. Also, variations in the FSHR significantly modulate lipid metabolism and hormonal levels.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12902-025-01979-0.Keywords: FSHR, Polymorphisms, PCOS, FSH, Linkage disequilibrium

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is an endocrine-metabolic disorder characterized by multiple hormonal imbalances dominated by hyperandrogenism, menstrual irregularities, and/or small cysts in one or both ovaries, which lead to short and long-term consequences on female health [1]. As one of the primary factors causing infertility in females, it has been reported that 6–26% of women of fertile age suffer from PCOS globally [2]. In India, the prevalence of PCOS is estimated to be 19.6% [3]. Earlier, PCOS has mostly been conceived of as a reproductive disorder; however, it is now well-acknowledged that women with PCOS have a higher chance of developing metabolic ailments, such as type 2 diabetes, impaired glucose tolerance [4], cardiovascular disorders [5], psychological morbidity [6] asthma [7] and metabolic syndrome [5]. The Rotterdam criteria, which calls for excluding other causes of hyperandrogenism such as adrenal or pituitary dysfunction, and the presence of two of the following three characteristics: oligo- or anovulation, clinical and/or biochemical signs of hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovaries on ultrasound, is the globally renowned standard for diagnosing PCOS [8]. Family studies have suggested that PCOS is inherited in a pseudo-autosomal inheritance pattern with varying penetrance, confirming the long-established fact that this trait has a family history [9]. However, it is now known that PCOS is a multifaceted disorder with a broad array of anomalies, such as hyperandrogenism, anovulation, and insulin resistance, that occur due to the interactions among various proteins and genes influenced by environmental factors [10].

Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), a glycoprotein hormone synthesized by a pituitary gland, is an essential component of the hypothalamus-pituitary-gonadal axis (HPG) [11]. In response to an increase in LH, FSH promotes the growth of granulosa cells, assists in the development and maturation of follicles, and prepares the dominant follicle to undergo ovulation [12]. FSH must bind with its receptor (FSHR) on granulosa cells to transmit its signal [11]. This receptor is encoded by FSHR mapped to chromosome 2p16.3 and contains 10 exons. There is evidence that polymorphisms in FSHR lower the sensitivity of the receptor, which could lead to the development of PCOS and other conditions that are often dependent on ovarian function, such as OHSS, infertility, and aberrant secondary sex traits [13]. Apart from the reproductive health concerns, FSHR polymorphisms also significantly affect alzheimer disease, obesity, malignancies, and osteoporosis [14].

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have been performed on European and Han-Chinese populations, investigating that chromosomal region 2p16.3 (which consists of LHCGR and FSHR) is associated with the development of PCOS [15–16]. The role of two non-synonymous polymorphisms, rs6165 and rs6166, was evaluated by several groups of different racial backgrounds [17–18]. There are very few investigations on the other FSHR polymorphisms i.e., rs1394205, rs11692782, and rs2349415. Polymorphism rs1394205 is located on 5’ UTR and the remaining two are intronic polymorphisms. A promoter of FSHR is similar to the cellular homolog of the viral E26 transformation-specific sequence (cETS-1). It was reported that the activity of the promoter was reduced to 56% if substitution was present during the in-vitro experiment in CHO cells [19]. In women undergoing IVF, rs1394205 was demonstrated to weaken promoter activity and thus respond poorly to exogenous FSH [20–21]. The polymorphism rs11692782 showed significant susceptibility towards PCOS, and its role was further highlighted through SNP–SNP interaction models [22–23]. The rs2349415 also showed increased susceptibility to PCOS in GWAS studies [16]. In another study, rs2349415 significantly modulated the serum SHBG level [24]. Further, a meta-analysis revealed that rs2349415 was significantly associated with PCOS risk and suggested FSHR as a common susceptibility gene for PCOS, irrespective of ethnicity [25]. Given that there is a lack of data on FSHR polymorphisms from our region, the present study evaluated the association of FSHR polymorphisms (rs1394205, rs11692782, and rs2349415) with PCOS in Punjab, India.

Material and methodology

Study participants

The present study recruited 823 subjects from Punjab, comprising 443 PCOS cases and 380 age-matched healthy controls. The PCOS cases were examined at Beri Hospital, Amritsar, while controls were taken from the general population. PCOS was defined according to the Rotterdam criteria. The presence of hyperandrogenism was determined by examining hirsutism (a clinical sign) and measuring testosterone levels. Females of reproductive age and having regular periods were recruited as controls and did not exhibit any symptoms of excess androgen or other reproductive abnormalities. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guru Nanak Dev University, Amritsar, India (following the Declaration of Helsinki). Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects before starting the investigation in the present study.

Blood sample collection, anthropometric and biochemical measurements

Predesigned proforma, which includes demographic and clinical variables, were recorded for all the subjects. The anthropometric measurements, including height, weight, and waist and hip circumferences, were taken from all the subjects to determine their body mass index BMI and WHR. Five millilitres (mL) of blood were collected out of which 2 mL was used for serum isolation. Lipid profile including cholesterol, triglycerides and HDL-C and hormonal profile consisting of LH, FSH, and testosterone levels were measured. The protocol utilized for biochemical analysis was earlier explained by Singh et al. [26].

DNA isolation

The remaining 3 mL of blood was added to the 0.5 M EDTA vacutainer, and DNA isolation was carried out following the Phenol-Chloroform method given by Adeli and Ogbonna [27]. Further, extracted DNA was quantified using a Thermo-Fisher scientific NanoDrop™ 2000/2000c spectrophotometer.

Genotyping of FSHR polymorphisms

FSHR regions of rs1394205 [Chr#2:49154446 (GRCh38)], rs11692782 [Chr#2:49064754 (GRCh38)] and rs2349415 [Chr#2:49020693 (GRCh38)] polymorphisms were genotyped using the polymerase chain reaction-restriction fragment length polymorphism (PCR-RFLP) method. PCR amplification was performed in 15µl reaction mixture containing 100ng of DNA, 10 pmol of each primer, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 0.2 mM dNTP, and 0.2 9U Taq DNA polymerase. For rs1394205, the sequences of the forward primer (FP) and reverse primer (RP) were taken from Kambalachenu et al. [28]. Primer 3 software was used to design the primers for rs11692782 (FP: 5’-CAGATGCTGGAGACTTGATTTTG-3’ and RP: 5’-AGATGCTGTCCCTCTGTCTAA-3’) and rs2349415. (FP: 5’-CTGTGGCTCACCTTGGAGAT-3’ and RP: 5’-TCTCATCTCTCAAAGCCAGATT-3’). For all polymorphisms, initial denaturation was 5 min at 95 °C, followed by denaturation at 95 °C for 45 s, extension at 72 °C for 50 s, and final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. The annealing temperatures for rs1394205, rs11692782, and rs2349415 were 60.3 °C, 60.1 °C, and 58.6 °C for 45 s, respectively. The amplified products of size 266 bp, 452 bp, and 405 bp were obtained for rs1394205, rs11692782 and rs2349415, respectively. To digest the 266 bp product, MboII restriction enzyme (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used. The undigested product of 266 bp signifies AA genotype, a product of 211 bp and 55 bp represents the mutant GG genotype and bands of 266 bp, 211 bp and 55 bp illustrate AG genotype. The amplified product of 452 bp was digested with restriction enzyme DraI (New England Biolabs) for 2 h at 37 °C and digested was analysed on 2.6% ethidium bromide-stained agarose which showed an undigested product of 452 bp representing homozygous wild type TT genotype, bands of 452 bp, 248 bp and 204 bp fragments representing TA heterozygous genotype and bands of 248 bp, 204 bp for homozygous mutant genotype (AA). For rs2349415 genotyping, 405 bp product was digested overnight at 37 °C with A1wI restriction enzyme (New England Biolabs) and the product of 405 bp signified CC genotype, a product of 405 bp, 318 bp and 87 bp signified heterozygous genotype and bands of 318 bp and 87 bp represented TT genotype (Supplementary 1). To verify the genotyping performed using a PCR-based assay, 10% of randomly chosen samples were sequenced, and the results were 100% consistent.

Statistical analysis

The sample size and power of the study were calculated using the CaTS power calculator (https://csg.sph.umich.edu/abecasis/CaTS/) [29], and it was found to be > 90% at CIs of 95%. A chi-square test was performed to analyse the association of FSHR polymorphisms between the PCOS cases and controls. Deviation from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) was calculated using a goodness-of-fit chi-square test. Different genetic models and their odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) and corresponding p-values were calculated with binary logistic regression, and the p-value was considered significant after applying the Bonferroni correction. Continuous variables were analysed by applying the student’s t-test, results were represented as mean and standard deviation, and p-value was considered significant after applying the Bonferroni correction. SPSS (version 21, IBM SPSS, NY, USA) software was used for the above-mentioned statistical analysis. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was carried out to compare the distribution of biochemical markers such as total testosterone levels, FSH, LH, HDL-C, LDL-C, and triglyceride levels concerning the genotypes of FSHR polymorphisms using GraphPad 9 software. A p-value less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant. Haploview 4.2 was used to create haplotype blocks, and all the haplotypes were further evaluated for associations with PCOS risk.

Results

Characteristic features of study participants

The demographic features of the study participants are listed in Table 1. The mean age of PCOS cases was 24.1 ± 4.8 and of controls was 24.03 ± 4.7 (p = 0.76). The difference in mean menarche age between cases and controls was significant (12.8 ± 1.31 and 13.02 ± 1.29, respectively; p = 0.01). Obesity-related parameters such as BMI and WHR are significantly higher in PCOS cases when compared with the control group (p < 0.0001). Lipid profile, including cholesterol (p < 0.0001), triglycerides (p < 0.0001), and VLDL-C levels (p < 0.0001), was significantly higher, while HDL-C levels (p < 0.0001) were significantly lower among cases. Women with PCOS exhibit statistically higher testosterone levels (p < 0.0001), whereas the difference between the levels of LH, FSH, and the ratio of LH to FSH remained non-significant among both groups.

Genotype analysis

The genotype frequencies of rs1394205, rs11692782, and rs2349415 in the control group followed Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (p = 0.30, 0.90, and 0.41, respectively). The genotypic and allelic frequencies of the aforementioned polymorphisms are represented in Table 2. The distribution of genotypes and alleles did not differ significantly for rs1394205 (p = 0.47; 0.29, respectively). No significant difference was observed in genotypic (p = 0.41) and allelic (p = 0.19) frequencies of rs11692782 between PCOS cases and controls. None of the genetic models (dominant, recessive, and heterozygote) of rs1394205 and rs11692782 were associated with PCOS risk. The genotype distribution differed significantly between PCOS cases and controls for rs2349415 (p = 0.003). The frequency of mutant genotype TT was 23.9% in PCOS women, while in controls was 15.5%. It was demonstrated that the TT genotype conferred ~1.5 times higher risk towards PCOS development (Crude OR-1.49, CI-1.01-2.22, p = 0.04; Adjusted OR-1.47, CI-0.96-2.26, p = 0.07). In genetic models, the recessive model was recorded as a best-fit model for rs2349415 and showed ~1.7-folds higher PCOS risk (Crude OR-1.73, CI-1.21-2.46, p = 0.002; adjusted OR- 1.64, CI-1.11-2.42, p = 0.02).

Haplotype analysis

The distribution of FSHR haplotypes was examined in PCOS cases and controls (Table 3). A block spanning 133 kb, comprised of rs2349415-rs11692782-rs1394205, was constructed (Fig. 1). These three SNPs were not found to be in linkage disequilibrium (D = 0.316, LOD = 9.3, r2 = 0.055). Haplotype TTG was found to be more frequent in both groups. However, none of the haplotypes showed any risk for PCOS occurrence.

Genotype-Phenotype correlation

PCOS cases were further divided based on genotypes to evaluate the impact of FSHR polymorphisms on lipid and hormonal profiles. For rs1394205, triglyceride and VLDL-C levels (p < 0.05) were significantly higher, whereas LDL-C levels were significantly lower with AA genotype (Fig. 2). Furthermore, endocrine biomarkers, including LH, FSH, and LH/FSH, have been significantly co-related with genotypes of rs11692782. PCOS women who carried TA genotype of rs11692782 had lower levels of testosterone and LH (p < 0.05), and those with the AA genotype had higher FSH levels (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3). In addition, the TT genotype was moderately associated with higher levels of LH/FSH. Cholesterol levels showed a significant association among the genotypes of rs2349415 in women with PCOS (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The current case-control study recruited 443 PCOS women diagnosed according to the Rotterdam criteria and age-matched 380 healthy women as the control group. We aimed to compare the characteristic, metabolic, and endocrine features and evaluate the frequency distribution of FSHR polymorphisms (rs1394205, rs11692782, and rs2349415) between cases and controls.

In the present study, women who had PCOS were more likely to be obese and suffer from dyslipidemia, their BMI and WHR, cholesterol, triglycerides, and VLDL-C were significantly higher than controls. In contrast, HDL-C levels were significantly lower in PCOS cases than in controls (Table 1). Total testosterone levels were significantly higher in PCOS cases, which is an indication of hyperandrogenism. However, the differences in the gonadotropic hormone levels such as LH, FSH, and the ratio of LH to FSH remained non-significant among both groups.

FSH binds to its receptor FSHR to facilitate various physiological processes, including follicular development, ovulation, and estradiol production. Ovulatory failure is one of the hallmarks of polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), which may be potentially attributed to inherited variations in FSHR [32]. The findings of GWAS I and GWAS II, conducted on Chinese women by Chen et al. [15] and Shi et al. [16], suggested a strong relationship between FSHR polymorphisms with PCOS. Hence, the significance of FSHR polymorphisms in the etiology of PCOS should not be disregarded. Our study demonstrated a statistically significant difference in the genotypic frequency of rs2349415 polymorphisms between PCOS cases and controls (p = 0.003) (Table 2). The recessive model of rs2349415 is associated with a greater risk of PCOS (AOR-1.64, p = 0.012). GWAS performed by Shi et al. [16] on Asian women also confirmed the susceptibility of rs2349415 polymorphism towards the development of PCOS. In contrast to our study, studies on the European population did not find any significant association of rs2349415 polymorphism with PCOS [30–32]. Another study conducted the association analysis of rs2349415 with PCOS among PCO and non-PCO Chinese women showed a non-significant association [24]. A familial study by Zhao et al. [33] recruited first-degree trios consisting of PCOS women and found that the transmission of rs2349415 is statistically significant. The cross-ethnic meta-analysis of Dutch, US, and Chinese data also showed an association of rs2349415 polymorphism with PCOS (OR-1.24, p-value = 3.6*10− 4) [25]. Other polymorphisms (rs1394205 and rs11692782) included in the present study did not demonstrate any significant association with PCOS occurrence. Similar to our results, studies on South Indian and Bahrain women did not find significance among rs1394205 and PCOS [22, 28]. SNP rs11692782 demonstrated a significant association with PCOS in Bahrain women [22], disagrees with our study.

The analysis of linkage disequilibrium of FSHR polymorphisms was conducted as it has been established that the interaction of SNPs within a haplotype is more informative than single polymorphisms in determining disease susceptibility. The findings of our study indicate a modest to low degree of linkage disequilibrium among the scrutinized FSHR SNPs (Fig. 1). None of the haplotypes were associated with increased susceptibility to PCOS. The complex etiology of PCOS suggests that genetic factors such as SNPs, copy number variants, altered upstream promoter methylation, regulatory elements in FSHR, and interactions with neighboring genes may collectively contribute to PCOS susceptibility [22].

Folliculogenesis is affected by excessive tiny follicular development and an ineffective selection process of a dominant follicle, which requires FSH for dominant selection. Polycystic ovaries develop when FSH function or secretion is inhibited [34]. PCOS women have FSH levels similar to normal women in the early follicular phase [35]. Thus, the coupling of FSH with FSHR may be defined as its most significant role in PCOS [24]. The current investigation examined the association between genotypes of FSHR polymorphisms and biochemical features (Figs. 2, 3 and 4). The results indicate that the mutant genotype of rs11692782 was associated with elevated FSH levels (p < 0.05). In contrast to our study, Almawi et al. [22] stated that FSHR rs11692782 was associated with a marginal reduction in FSH levels (p > 0.05). The heterozygous genotype of rs11692782 has been linked to significantly lower levels of testosterone and LH (p < 0.05). Several studies explained that polymorphisms in FSHR decrease FSH efficacy; therefore, to compensate, FSH secretion from the pituitary gland increases [36]. The impact of FSHR SNPs has often been studied in the context of reproductive issues, however, their function in extragonadal tissues cannot be overlooked. In our study, it was seen that FSHR polymorphisms had a significant impact on serum cholesterol, triglyceride, LDL-C, and VLDL-C levels. The polymorphic genotype (AA) of rs1394205 was associated with elevated triglycerides, VLDL-C levels, and lower LDL-C levels in PCOS cases. The cholesterol levels were elevated with the mutant genotype of rs2349415 (Fig. 4a). Du and coworkers demonstrated that SHBG levels were significantly influenced by rs2349415, supporting our findings [24]. The relationship between SHBG and lipid metabolism is well established. SHBG and cholesterol are both partially synthesized in the liver and are negatively correlated [37]. In the liver, FSH downregulates the expression of LDL-R, which plays a crucial role in the metabolizing of LDL-C. The reduction of LDL-R elevates LDL-C or bad cholesterol level in the circulation. FSH stimulates adipocytes to synthesize the FSHR receptors, which regulate the lipogenesis pathway. FSH also hinders the process of beiging of white adipocytes, leading to the accumulation of white adipose tissue in the body [38].

Strengths and limitations

The power of the current study is > 90%, and samples were collected exclusively from Punjab, India, which reduces the likelihood of a Type II error and strengthens our findings. The inclusion of only three FSHR polymorphisms is a limitation of the present study, and the association of additional FSHR polymorphisms with PCOS needs to be analyzed. We employed a conventional method, which may lack the sensitivity and specificity of contemporary genotypic approaches like MassARRAY or TaqMan assay, due to financial constraints.

Conclusion

The current study highlights the potential role of FSHR polymorphisms in the pathogenesis of PCOS among Punjabi women. Specifically, the rs2349415 is associated with increased susceptibility to PCOS, while the rs11692782 polymorphism appears to modulate levels of key gonadotropic hormones, including LH, FSH, and testosterone. Furthermore, the rs1394205 and rs2349415 variants influence lipid metabolism, suggesting a broader metabolic impact of FSHR polymorphisms in PCOS. These findings underscore the importance of genetic screening in understanding individual variations in PCOS presentation and support the need for larger, multi-ethnic studies to confirm these associations. Ultimately, integrating genetic markers into clinical practice could enhance early diagnosis, risk stratification, and the development of targeted therapeutic interventions for women affected by PCOS.
