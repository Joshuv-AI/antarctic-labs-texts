Health technology assessment in Jordan: current landscape, challenges, and future roadmap
Authors: Khawla Abu Hammour, Faris El-Dahiyat, Zaheer-Ud-Din Babar, Fahmi Y. Al-Ashwal, Rana K. Abu-Farha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHealth Technology Assessment (HTA) is increasingly recognised as an essential tool for directing decisions on health policy, particularly those concerning coverage, reimbursement, price and value, especially in nations aiming for Universal Health Coverage (UHC). Jordan is a middle-income country with limited resources for the health sector and rising needs; thus, despite its significant progress toward institutionalising HTA, there are still many gaps.ObjectivesTo assess the present situation of HTA in Jordan, pinpoint the main facilitators and obstacles, and suggest a plan for enhancing HTA at Jordan University Hospital so that it can more methodically influence Jordanian health policy.MethodsStakeholder studies, policy documents and published literature on HTA were reviewed, along with lessons learned from similar contexts.ResultsJordan's progress in HTA has been noteworthy. The Jordan Food and Drug Administration has specific requirements for pharmacoeconomic data in pricing and reimbursement, for instance, and university programs are providing capacity building. Other pioneers include the Ministry of Health, Royal Medical Services and the King Hussein Cancer Center's Centre for Drug Policy and Technology Assessment (KHCC CDPTA). However, funding for HTA is unstable, transparency and stakeholder engagement are uneven, institutional roles are fragmented, there is no national HTA methodological guideline or comprehensive, mandated legal framework, and data infrastructure (especially local cost, outcomes and real-world evidence) is limited.ConclusionsA strategic plan is required for all institutions to achieve the potential benefits of HTA in Jordan, including more equitable access, better prioritisation, more efficient spending and contributions to UHC. Formalising legal requirements, creating national methodological guidelines, enhancing institutional and human capacity, enhancing data systems, obtaining long-term funding, establishing precise decision criteria and thresholds and boosting transparency and stakeholder participation are important stages.

Background

Health Technology Assessment (HTA) assesses the medical, economical, social and the implication of ethics of health technologies, broadly defined to include not only the pharmaceuticals, but to include the medical diagnostic devices, and also healthcare interventions (Altawalbeh et al., 2024; O'Rourke et al., 2020). HTA can support evidence-informed policymaking through the assessment of the value of technologies mainly in the clinical effectiveness, safety and cost-effectiveness. For example, robust pharmacovigilance reporting systems can enhance the accuracy of HTA by providing the essential real-world safety data to evaluate the therapeutic value of medications. This, by other means, informs the analysis of the cost-effectiveness by incorporating the profile of adverse event and the outcomes into economic modelling (Khader et al., 2023; Rababa, 2023). Through managing the processes of the management of the formulary, substitution by generics, and optimisation of pharmacotherapy, pharmacists and healthcare providers significantly contribute to the reduction of pharmaceutical prices. They can aid greatly in reducing the wastes and encouraging the optimal utilisation of medicines, this will reduce the costs of the healthcare and improve the patient outcomes (Hammour et al., 2022; Salameh et al., 2019). In Jordan, HTA is increasingly becoming relevant as the healthcare system faces significant financial strains from ever-rising expenditures, not only due to the growing demand for availability of innovative and high-cost medications, such as oncology drugs, but also due to the pressures of a huge refugee population and constraints on the budget of the public health sector (Khader et al., 2023; Rababa, 2023). Moreover, several years ago, Jordan has committed to achieving Universal Health Coverage (UHC), and thus there is wide recognition of HTA as a critical tool for guiding appropriate decisions on benefit package design, better allocation of the resources, and price negotiations (Glassman et al., 2017).

Regionally, several lo- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are now investing in HTA institutions and capacity building as part of broader strengthening of the healthcare efforts (Almomani et al., 2022; Sharma et al., 2020). Jordan, as one of the LMICs, aligns with this trend. However, it remains in the initial stages of HTA institutionalisation. This paper aims to map the current HTA landscape in Jordan, assess system readiness and key barriers and propose a roadmap for future HTA development and institutionalisation.

Methods

A comprehensive literature review was conducted, covering both peer-reviewed academic publications and ‘grey’ literature (government reports and NGO/WHO documents). To gather the available data in Jordan on both the creation and application of HTA, this study didn't perform a systematic review; instead we used a structured narrative review method to present a contextual overview of the current available HTA ecosystem.

A thorough search of peer-reviewed and available grey literature was carried out throughout the last ten years. Initially, to find peer-reviewed articles, electronic databases such as PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus and Web of Science were searched. Then, to find the grey literature, focused searches of the websites of pertinent governmental entities, reports from HTA regional health organisations, as well as international organisations and health policy institutions were used.

Several keywords related to HTA and health policy, such as ‘HTA,’ ‘health policy’ and ‘decision-making,’ were used in the search. To find more literature, other reference lists of pertinent reports and papers were also assessed.

Sources to be taken into considerations are the studies that are relevant to Jordanian HTA policy, its governance, implementation or capacity building. Formal inclusion and exclusion criteria were not determined previously because the evaluation process was exploratory and narrative; nonetheless, the sources that did not relevant to the Jordanian context or did not directly address HTA were excluded. Secondly, a narrative process was used to identify the material after iterative evaluation. Finally, using the recurrent concepts, the organisation and discussion of the results were made.

Key studies included: Implementation Road Map of Health Technology Assessment in Middle-Income Countries: The Case of Jordan, which uses surveys and semi-structured interviews with policy experts to identify priorities for HTA development, including training, funding, institutional structures and increasing reliance on local evidence (Almomani et al., 2021). Capacity Building for Health Technology Assessment in Jordan: Institutionalisation and Its Use in Pricing and Reimbursement Decisions, which describes HTA capacity building, the evolution of institutionalisation in entities, such as King Hussein Cancer Center (KHCC) and Royal Medical Services (RMS), and challenges around methodological guidelines and transparency (Almomani et al., 2022). Understanding of the Readiness for HTA Implementation in Jordan as a Step Towards Universal Health Coverage, which employs a cross-sectional stakeholder survey (Ministry of Health (MOH), regulatory bodies, etc.) using an HTA implementation scorecard, to gauge readiness across legal, data-quality, human resources and financial dimensions (Alamer et al., 2023). In addition, several stakeholder surveys and interview studies have been used. For example, ‘Comparison of Current and Preferred Status of HTA Implementation in Jordan’ (Almomani et al., 2022) uses an international scorecard across eight domains (capacity building, legislation, decision criteria, etc.) to map what is currently done vs what stakeholders prefer (Almomani et al., 2019). There are also illustrative comparative examples from the region: HTA roadmaps and readiness assessments have been carried out in other Middle-Income Countries, including Egypt. These provide useful lessons for Jordan in structuring HTA institutionalisation, stakeholder engagement, evidence-use and capacity development (Fasseeh et al., 2022).

Results

Current landscape of HTA in Jordan

Major public and academic healthcare institutions are spearheading efforts to institutionalise HTA over time. The RMS and the MOH have become early public-sector leaders in incorporating HTA into decision-making. Both organisations have started using clinical and economic data when choosing, pricing and acquiring medications, especially for expensive technology. Although these procedures are still developing and not yet formally standardised, they show a rising understanding of the importance of HTA in promoting cost-effective and evidence-based health policy (Almomani et al., 2019, 2021; Sharma et al., 2020). While RMS has created internal processes to evaluate the technologies utilised in its hospital network, the MOH has expressed, additionally, interest in maximising public spending under budgetary restrictions.

The nation's most technologically sophisticated HTA actor is the KHCC, with an emphasis on oncology treatments. It’s Centre for Drug Policy and Technology Assessment (CDPTA) produces structured HTA studies that integrate clinical, economic, safety and resource use data. The Pharmacy and Therapeutics (P&T) committee at KHCC uses these evaluations to guide formulary choices and price discussions, which occasionally lead to manufacturer cost reductions. To create local competence, KHCC also spearheads capacity building through specialised training and an HTA fellowship (Almomani et al., 2019, 2021; Fasseeh et al., 2022; KHCC, 2021).

More recently, the P&T committee at Jordan University Hospital (JUH), a significant tertiary academic institution connected to the University of Jordan, has started implementing HTA concepts, particularly cost-effectiveness and clinical evidence evaluations. JUH's early HTA initiatives, which are backed by the pharmacy department and providing training in Pharmacoeconomics and HTA, demonstrate the growing academic engagement in evidence-based decision-making and establish the hospital as a possible model for expanding HTA in Jordan. According to the Jordanian Drug Law, the Jordan Food and Drug Administration (JFDA) mandates pharmacoeconomic consulting in price and reimbursement decisions. This need represents a first step toward incorporating HTA into national regulatory frameworks.

To promote the growth of HTA-literate professionals, a number of universities have also implemented HTA short courses and modules. Surveys show that a wide range of stakeholders in the public, commercial and academic sectors support HTA because they see its potential to increase openness, maximise spending and improve the quality of decisions (Alzyoud, 2025). A recent workshop on HTA took place in Amman in September 2025, bringing together important players from the public-sector, academic institutions, medical facilities and regional partners. An urgent need to formalise a legal and institutional mandate was emphasised by the workshop participants. Additionally, they stressed the need to create uniform, context-specific methodological principles that consider equity, economic evaluation and budget impact. Thus, to further support thorough assessments, it was pointed out that enhancing the local data infrastructure and growing capacity-building efforts through educational programs and f training were crucial. The workshop also included integrating HTA functions into regular health planning. Other important aspect was related to increasing the openness through report publication and decision-making processes that involve a variety of stakeholders, patients and healthcare providers. The findings of this paper draw on three distinct sources of insight: (i) first, in regard to empirical evidence and policy analyses identified through the narrative literature review, (ii) secondly, perspectives of stakeholders and other expert consensus articulated during the recent HTA workshop in Amman and (iii) lastly, the future role of HTA in Jordan.

Although the consensus statements that emerged from the workshop don’t support empirical evidence, they enhance the understanding of the normative suggestions and strategic priorities. The inclusion of them serves to highlight the areas of agreement and current stakeholder thinking throughout different institutional, academic and policy actors who are engaged in several HTA-related activities in Jordan.

The institutionalising HTA, as widely acknowledged by workshop participants, is necessary to enable evidence-informed decision-making; but, several of implementation factors should be taken into consideration: Limitation of the technical capability, disintegration of the data infrastructure and lacking of well-defined governance frameworks for HTA procedures are some of the main challenges to feasibility. Furthermore, to incorporate the findings of HTA into the choices of current pricing and reimbursement may necessitate modifications to decision-making processes in Jordan.

These obstacles and challenges imply that the recommendations of the workshop should be considered as aspirational objectives for additional empirical research and capacity-building initiatives. To go toward an evidence basis and beyond expert agreement that can guide policy improvement, future studies to evaluate the impact of application of HTA-informed decisions in Jordan would be beneficial.

To guarantee continuous system development, continuous monitoring and adaptive learning were emphasised. These consensus-driven ideas offer a workable road map that is in line with Jordan's health priorities in order to enhance UHC and maximise resource allocation (Alzyoud, 2025).

Major challenges and barriers to HTA implementation in Jordan

Several major challenges prevent the systematic and mature integration of HTA in Jordan into both: the health policy and decision-making, despite encouraging institutional advances. One of the main obstacles is the lacking of a clear legislative mandate, and currently, in Jordan, there is no law mandating HTA evidence for pricing, reimbursement or procurement choices, in spite of the fact that different regulatory bodies, such as the JFDA or the High Health Council, have shown interest in HTA (11, 14), there is currently no official national HTA centralised institution with clear decision-making authority (Alamer et al., 2023). The absence of Jordanian standardised methodological guidelines, such as agreement on cost-effectiveness measures, utility metrics, discount rates and willingness-to-pay thresholds, increases this institutional fragmentation, resulting in non-standardised HTA practices with little comparability across assessments (Almomani et al., 2019, 2021; Alzyoud, 2025; Fasseeh et al., 2022; KHCC, 2021).

Moreover, the lack of reliable local data, such as comprehensive information regarding the direct medical and non-medical costs, clinical outcomes, epidemiological profiles, indicators of quality of life, long-term follow-up and empirical evidence, is a major limiting factor. These data shortages are made worse by fragmentation of the health information systems, which compromise HTA analyses (MoH, 2025). Other important challenge is related to lacking of resources for capacity-building and, therefore, lacking of the skilled workers with advanced knowledge of decision modelling, biostatistics and health economics (Almomani et al., 2021). Financial constraints present yet another obstacle because HTA operations frequently include expenditures for gathering of data, analytical modelling and revision by expertise.

Regarding the payment for assessment, there is still uncertainty whether it should be paid by the medical facilities, the government or by the manufacturers (Almomani et al., 2019).

Furthermore, lacking of a cohesive national framework for HTA integration is another challenge, and inadequate coordination is resulted from institutional fragmentation, which includes overlapping roles among the MOH, JFDA, specialised hospitals such as the KHCC and academic institutions (JUH) (Almomani et al., 2022).

Participation of stakeholders and openness are further hindered by the irregular publication judgements of HTA reports. Key stakeholders, including patients, healthcare providers and the public, are frequently not involved in a sufficient manner in the HTA process, and the standards that inform better choices regarding the cost-effectiveness thresholds or prioritising guidelines are not always stated clearly (MoH, 2025). Lastly, the positive outcomes of HTA are impacted by practical limitations of the time; protracted procedures of evaluation can postpone accessibility to new technologies, pin particular in fields that are developing quickly, like oncology, where any delay could limit beneficial outcomes for the patient or make technology outdated. The implementation process may also be made difficult by manufacturers’ opposition to pricing negotiations that is based on HTA (Almomani et al., 2022). To enable the successful implementation of institutionalisation HTA in Jordan, these obstacles must be filled by a clear roadmap that includes the investment in data infrastructure, building up capacity, reforming legal standards and robusting multi-sectoral governance.

Future roadmap for strengthening HTA in Jordan

A strategy roadmap is put forth to potentiate and move Jordan’s HTA system from the initial stages to an influential framework, building on insights from stakeholders and current available literature. It is important to formalise initially the institutional and legal mandate, this entails passing laws requiring HTA to help in making the judgments on inclusion, pricing and reimbursement for expensive or novel medications and medical technologies. At the same time, the specialised national HTA unit currently available inside the MOH offered both: the responsibility and well-defined duties, which are essential for cogent governance (Alamer et al., 2023).

On the other hand, the creation of a national methodological framework is essential. Standardised standards that are outlining the economical evaluation techniques, utilsation and quality of life metrics and willingness-to-pay thresholds must be parts of this framework. For further clarification of trade-off management, decision criteria must specifically include all factors such as cost-effectiveness, equality, burden of the disease and budget impact (Almomani et al., 2019). To support strongly the analysis of HTA, strengthen of data infrastructure as well as investments in integrated health information systems, disease registries and electronic medical records is crucial. This calls to expand the availability of local data regarding hospital and outpatient cost data, epidemiologic statistics and outcomes related to patient outcomes, such as quality of life indices.

Promoting data sharing will enhance the transparency and at the same time maintaining strict privacy aspects (MoH, 2025). Thus, developing competent practitioners and decision-makers could be increased by expanding academic programs to build up capacity (Master's, PhD) in health economics, epidemiology and outcomes research. These programs should be supplemented through the implementation of short courses and international partnerships (KHCC, 2021). Obtaining sustainable long-term funding is important to support initiatives of HTA through foreign grants initially in the early stages, and thereafter through specific public budget lines. Clarification of financial obligations and connecting the results of HTA to reimbursement procedures could be obtained by investigating agreements of cost-sharing between the government and other sectors, such as hospitals and manufacturers (Alamer et al., 2023; Almomani et al., 2022). Disclosure of the underlying methods and data, publishing the findings and decisions of HTA, and involving patients, civil society and healthcare providers in the process of defining priorities and evaluating performance should be given top attention.

Credibility and confidence may be increased through putting in place procedures for reconsideration (MoH, 2025). Establishment of an unambiguous decision-making framework could be achieved by developing specific cost-effectiveness thresholds. Therefore, the scope of HTA should be gradually expanded to include not only the pharmaceuticals but also to include medical devices, as well as diagnostics and health programs (Almomani et al., 2019).

Discussion

Health systems worldwide are currently facing challenges due to increasing costs for therapy that are not on national lists of essential medications. This review offers a thorough analysis of the current situation and future directions of HTA in Jordan, emphasising both noteworthy successes and enduring difficulties. Through the innovative work of important organisations, including the MOH, RMS and KHCC, and, more recently, JUH, Jordan has advanced HTA significantly. These organisations have been important in performing assessments, developing capacity, incorporating HTA concepts into healthcare decision-making and participating in pharmacoeconomic consultations. Notwithstanding these developments, HTA implementation is still dispersed and constrained by several factors, such as lacking of thorough legislative requirements, ambiguous institutional roles, data restrictions and resource shortages, all of which work together in preventing the wider and more organised institutionalisation of HTA.

Although awareness and technical skills have increased as a result of capacity-building activities such as training programs and academic programs, there are still huge gaps in methodological standardisation, the availability of trustworthy local data and sustainable long-term funding. Consistent and comparable evaluations are hindered by the absence of well-defined, locally appropriate HTA methodological, which reduces their impact on choices about coverage, reimbursement and price at the country level. Coordination and integration of HTA into health policy are further hampered by institutional fragmentation among the MOH, hospitals and academic institutions. By producing consensus-driven suggestions that were in line with the body of existing knowledge, the September 2025 Amman HTA meeting marked a significant turning point in tackling these issues (Alzyoud, 2025). The session underlined how urgently formal institutional and legal mandates must be established in order to give a centralised HTA agency distinct authority and accountability. It also emphasised the creation of national methodological guidelines that are specific to the healthcare environment in Jordan, bolstering data infrastructure, and extending capacity-building programs to reach a critical mass of HTA specialists. Sustainable funding sources, openness in HTA procedures, significant stakeholder participation and a progressive extension of HTA’s purview beyond pharmaceuticals to encompass devices, diagnostics and health initiatives were all emphasised at the event.

In addition, the most recent HTAI Regional Meeting, which took place in Tunis (HAT, 2025), provided further insightful regional and international viewpoints relevant to Jordan’s HTA development. The meeting emphasised how crucial it is to promote global cooperation and knowledge sharing in order to hasten the development of HTA capabilities in LMICs. Enhancing digital infrastructure to facilitate data sharing and transparency, encouraging multi-stakeholder engagement to guarantee that diverse perspectives are incorporated into HTA processes, and implementing adaptive HTA frameworks that can keep up with the rapidly changing health technologies, particularly in precision medicine and oncology, were among the main recommendations. Crucially, the workshop made clear that in order to support equitable and inclusive access to care, equity issues must be specifically incorporated into HTA methodology. These suggestions highlight the importance of matching national policies with international best practices while tailoring them to local requirements, and they are highly compatible with the findings of the Amman workshop and Jordan’s larger HTA issues.

Jordan’s HTA strategy should be strengthened even more by incorporating these global ideas, which should improve system responsiveness, transparency and capacity building. Jordan’s experience is representative of a larger global trend in which a growing number of LMICs are institutionalising HTA in order to promote UHC goals and maximise the effectiveness of the health system while adhering to budgetary restrictions. Moreover, cooperation related to HTA and horizon scanning should continue and be extended (Almomani et al., 2019). In conclusion, Jordan is at a turning point in its HTA development. To create a strong and long-lasting HTA system, the actionable roadmap from the Amman workshop must be put into practice. It is based on law, methodological standardisation, data improvement, capacity training, sustainable financing, transparency and governance. Such a system, as mentioned in other studies, will assist the nation’s overall health policy objectives, including the attainment of UHC, and allow for more effective, equitable healthcare decisions (Angelis & Kanavos, 2015; Bastani et al., 2021; Brandt et al., 2018; Tarantola et al., 2023).

Conclusion

Jordan has achieved important strides in the advancement of HTA. Taking into consideration the rising of health technology expenditures and Jordanina’s goal to attain UHC, these measures highlight the growing awareness of HTA as an important tool for evidence-based healthcare decision-making. Nonetheless, there are still several challenges in the way of the widespread and methodical institutionalisation of HTA, such as the lacking of complete legal mandates, data restrictions and constraints of the budget. The 2025 Amman HTA workshop’s consensus recommendations provide a strategic roadmap, highlighting the necessity of methodological standardisation, improved data infrastructure, capacity building, sustainable financing, enhanced transparency and stakeholder engagement in addition to legislative reform. In order to create a strong and long-lasting HTA framework that can maximise resource allocation and assist Jordan in making fair health policy decisions, these steps must be adopted and put into practice.

Future research directions

Future research should focus on the methodical assessment of HTA adoption and its impact on Jordanian healthcare reimbursement policies and practices, decision-making procedures and outcomes. To guarantee applicability to Jordan’s unique epidemiological and socioeconomic context, researches related to the creation and validation of locally appropriate HTA methodological frameworks, such as cost-effectiveness thresholds, utility metrics and economic evaluation model, is important. Strengthening the evidence basis that support HTA could be obtained by research focused on improving the collection and usage of real-world data and linking disparate health information systems. Gaining a qualitative understanding of stakeholder views, obstacles and enablers can help guide engagement tactics and boost industry representatives, physicians, patients and legislators adoption of HTA. Best practices and cooperative opportunities to hasten the institutionalisation of HTA can be clarified by comparative studies with other middle-income nations in the region. Lastly, a thorough evaluation of the financial worth and return on investment of HTA processes and infrastructure would offer vital proof in favour of ongoing funding and governmental support.
