OLFM4 orchestrates the immunosuppressive microenvironment and drives gallbladder cancer progression
Authors: Wenxin Li, Kun Liu, Fan Yang, Huisi He, Wen Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeGallbladder cancer (GBC) is the most common biliary tract malignancy, characterized by complex microenvironment and poor prognosis. Olfactomedin-4 (OLFM4) has been identified as a key regulator of GBC progression. This study aims to comprehensively elucidate the molecular profiling, tumor microenvironment (TME) and underlying mechanisms through which OLFM4 drives GBC progression.MethodsSingle-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was performed on tumor tissues and adjacent non-tumor tissues (ANT) from clinical GBC samples. We analyzed scRNA-seq data to characterize the landscape of heterogeneous TME and intricate intercellular communication networks of the single cells within GBC. Findings were validated through in vitro experiments.ResultsCompared with ANT, GBC exhibited a distinct remodeling of the tumor microenvironment. Specifically, epithelial cells were markedly enriched in GBC (18.2% vs. 11.6% in ANT), accompanied by a pronounced reduction in endothelial cells (2.4% vs. 9.7%). In parallel, myeloid cells decreased from 16.8% in ANT to 11.5% in GBC, while fibroblasts remained comparably abundant in both conditions (21.1%). Within the OLFM4+epithelium, there was a significant enrichment of genes and pathways related to malignant progression, including signatures driving cell proliferation, stemness maintenance (WNT/β-catenin), and metastasis, as well as crucial molecules involved in TME remodeling (SPP1, TGF-β). Pseudotemporal trajectory analysis revealed an upregulation of OLFM4 expression correlating with disease progression, suggesting its close association with malignant transition. Cell-cell communication analysis identified OLFM4+epithelial cells as central signaling hubs, primarily communicating with stromal and immune cells via the SPP1-CD44 and SPP1-Integrin axes. Importantly, OLFM4 knockdown inhibited cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and gemcitabine chemoresistance in vitro, underscoring its central role in GBC progression.ConclusionThese findings offer a comprehensive insight into the atlas of molecular signatures and TME within GBC, identifying OLFM4 as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for GBC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00432-026-06466-0.Keywords: Gallbladder cancer, Olfactomedin-4, tumor microenvironment, single-cell RNA sequencing

Introduction

Gallbladder cancer (GBC) is a highly aggressive malignancy arising from the biliary epithelium. In 2020, an estimated 115,949 new cases of GBC occurred worldwide (ranking 25th out of 36 cancers in 185 countries) (Sung et al. 2021). GBC is often diagnosed at advanced stages owing to its atypical symptoms. Surgical resection is currently a recognized treatment for GBC, however, a large proportion of patients (> 70–90%) are not candidates for surgery, and instead receive non-surgical management, primarily in the form of comprehensive therapy (Song et al. 2020). Although various treatments including adjuvant therapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and neoadjuvant therapy are available, the efficacy for GBC remains limited (Roa et al. 2022). The 5-year survival rate of advanced GBC patients typically drops to less than 5% (Rodas et al. 2023). Therefore, improving the management of GBC necessitates addressing several critical challenges, including the identification of reliable biomarkers for screening, clinical management, and prognosis prediction.

Recent studies highlight that the recalcitrance of GBC to chemotherapy is largely driven by a subpopulation of cancer stem-like cells (CSCs) and their crosstalk with the tumor microenvironment (TME) (Shi et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2024). Therefore, identifying key regulators that maintain stemness and orchestrate TME remodeling is critical for overcoming therapeutic resistance. Olfactomedin-4 (OLFM4, also known as GW112 or hGC-1), an olfactomedin domain-containing glycoprotein, is a “robust marker” of Lgr5+stem cells and plays important roles in innate immunity against bacterial infection, gastrointestinal inflammation, and cancer (Grover et al. 2010; Liu and Rodgers 2016). Dysregulated expression of OLFM4 is closely associated with the progression of multiple cancers, including gastric cancer, colorectal cancer, pancreatic cancer, cervical cancer, and OLFM4 exerts tumor-suppressing or tumor-promoting effects depending on the tissue and cellular context (Zhao et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2018; Li et al. 2019; Liu and Rodgers 2025). Notably, upregulation of OLFM4 is considered closely related to the malignant progression of GBC, serving as a potential biomarker assessing tumor aggressiveness, chemoresistance, and patient prognosis (Lin et al. 2022; He et al. 2024). However, the exact role of OLFM4 in regulating GBC progression and its tumor-promoting mechanisms needs further investigation and validation.

In this study, we utilized single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data of clinical GBC samples and revealed the molecular signatures and heterogeneity of the TME. Also, the role of OLFM4 in regulating GBC progression was validated. This research aims to profile the molecular and microenvironmental landscape of GBC, identify crucial regulators of progression, and provide novel insights into potential therapeutic targets.

Materials and methods

scRNA-seq data processing and analysis

Quality-controlled scRNA-seq data of GBC was obtained from a previously published study by He et al. (He et al. 2024). Cells derived from GBC tissues and adjacent non-tumor tissues (ANT) were retrieved from a previously published dataset and included in all subsequent single cell analyses. In total, 31 clinical tissue samples were analyzed, comprising 15 GBC tumor tissues and 16 ANT samples. Among these, 15 patients provided paired tumor–ANT samples, while one patient contributed only an ANT sample.

All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.0). Data processing, cell subtype proportion analysis, differential gene expression analysis, marker gene identification, and visualization were performed using the Seurat package (version 5.3.0) (Stuart et al. 2019). Differentially expressed genes between groups were identified using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with genes considered significant at an adjusted P value < 0.05 and an absolute log fold change (logFC) > 0.25.

Single-cell pathway activity was evaluated using AUCell (version 1.28.0), which calculates the enrichment of predefined gene sets at the individual cell level (Aibar et al. 2017). Gene sets used for AUCell analysis were sourced from the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/) (Subramanian et al. 2005).

To investigate cellular differentiation states, CytoTRACE2 (version 1.0.0) was applied to epithelial cells to infer their relative differentiation potential based on transcriptional diversity (Gulati et al. 2020). Pseudotemporal trajectory analysis was further performed using Monocle (version 2.34.0) exclusively on epithelial cell populations to reconstruct dynamic state transitions and infer potential developmental trajectories during GBC progression (Qiu et al. 2017).

Functional enrichment analyses of differentially expressed genes were conducted using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.14.6), including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses (Yu et al. 2012). Cell-cell communication analysis was performed using CellChat (version 1.6.1) to infer and compare intercellular signaling networks between GBC and ANT samples (Jin et al. 2025).

Cell lines

The human GBC cell lines GBC-SD and NOZ cells were respectively obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) and Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources (JCRB, Japan). GBC-SD cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium, while NOZ cells were cultured in DMEM. The culture media was supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin.

Construction of stable cell lines

Lentiviral constructs for OLFM4 knockdown and overexpression were purchased from Obio Technology, Shanghai, China. Cells, reaching at a confluence level of 60–70%, were incubated in a growth medium containing appropriately diluted lentivirus along with polybrene. 48 h after transfection, the cells were subjected to selection with 5 µg/mL puromycin to isolate and establish stable cell lines.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

Cells (1–5 × 105) were resuspended in 200 µL serum-free medium and seeded in the 24-well transwell upper chambers (Corning, NY, USA). A total of 600 µL culture medium containing 10% FBS was added into the lower chambers. After culturing for 24–48 h, cells on the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min. Subsequently, the stained cells were counted and photographed under light microscope (Olympus). Invasion assays were performed following the same procedures with the diluted Matrigel (Corning) (RPMI 1640/DMEM: Matrigel = 8:1) spread on the inside bottom of the upper chambers.

Wound healing assay

The cells were resuspended and seeded in a six-well plate and cultured to 90% confluence. The wounds were scratched with a sterile 10 µL pipette tip on a cell monolayer. After cells cultured in a serum-free medium for 48 h, the images were acquired using light microscope (Olympus) at 0 h and 48 h.

Cell viability assay and half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) determination

Cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3,000 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. 10 µL of Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK8) solution was added to each well at 0 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h, followed by incubation for an additional two hours at 37 °C and 5% carbon dioxide. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The relative cell viability or proliferation rate was calculated by comparing the absorbance of treated wells to that of control wells.

Cells (3,000 cells/well) were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured overnight. The adherent cells were then treated with a gradient concentration of the gemcitabine (10− 2–103 µM). After incubation for 48 h, 10 µL of CCK8 solution was added to each well, and then cells were incubated for an additional two hours at 37 °C and 5% carbon dioxide. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Western blot

The Western blot analysis was carried out following established procedures. Cells were collected and then lysed using IP lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology in Shanghai, China), with the addition of 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), and kept on ice for 30 min. Protein concentrations were quantified using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit from ThermoFisher Scientific in MA, USA. Equal quantities of protein were loaded onto sodium dodecyl-sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto 0.22 μm nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore in Cork, Ireland). The membranes were blocked with 5% BSA for one hour at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with corresponding secondary antibody (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, USA) for one hour at room temperature. Following the removal of unbound antibodies through washing, the labeled bands were scanned using the Odyssey® CLx Infrared Imaging System from LI-COR Biosciences in MA, USA.

Tumor sphere formation assay

GBC cells were prepared and seeded into low attachment 6-well plates (Corning, USA) at a density of 1000 cells per well in serum-free DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with B27 (1:50), epidermal growth factor (EGF, 20 ng/mL), and basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF, 10 ng/mL). The cells were cultured for 10 days, and the medium was replenished every three days. The number of spheres with a diameter greater than 50 μm was counted under an inverted microscope, and images were captured for analysis.

Colony formation assay

Cells were trypsinized to generate a single-cell suspension and seeded into 6-well plates at 500, 1000, 1500 per well respectively in complete growth medium. The medium was refreshed every three days. Following a culture period of 14 days, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min. The plates were then gently rinsed with distilled water, air-dried, and photographed.

Statistical analysis

Graphpad Prism (v9.0) was used to perform the statistical analyses. All data were presented with mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). P value less than 0.05 was considered to be significant. All vitro functional experiments were performed in three biological replicates. The details of statistical tests were described in the respective figure legends.

Results

Landscape of TME in GBC by scRNA-seq

To explore the cellular diversity and microenvironment composition in GBC, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from 31 clinical tissue samples derived from 16 patients, including 15 GBC tumor samples and 16 ANT samples, as reported in the study by He et al. (He et al. 2024). According to canonical markers expression, cells were classified into eight major cell types, encompassing B/plasma (CD79A+), endothelium (PECAM1+), OLFM4+epithelium, OLFM4−epithelium, “fibroblasts (COL1A1+), mast cells (TPSAB1+), myeloid cells (LYZ+) (monocytes, macrophages and dendritic cell), and T/ natural killer (NK ) cells (CD3D+) (Fig. 1a and c), which were differently colored by sample origin: ANT and GBC tissues (Fig. 1b). Specifically, OLFM4+epithelial cells were almost exclusively enriched in GBC tissues compared to ANT. Quantitative cell composition analysis further revealed pronounced differences between GBC and ANT tissues (Fig. 1d). Epithelial cells were substantially enriched in GBC, accounting for 18.2% of cells compared with 11.6% in ANT, whereas endothelial cells were markedly reduced (2.4% vs. 9.7%). In contrast, myeloid cells decreased from 16.8% in ANT to 11.5% in GBC, while fibroblasts remained similarly represented in both groups (21.1% in ANT and GBC). These compositional changes suggest a profound remodeling of the tumor microenvironment in GBC, which is further characterized by altered intercellular communication networks described below.

.

Identification of epithelial cell populations and OLFM4 characteristics

We identified 13 epithelial clusters based on the expression profiles of canonical biomarkers, revealing heterogeneous expression patterns of OLFM4 across different tissue sources and various epithelial cell clusters (Fig. 2a and b). Consistent with the global analysis, GBC tissues exhibited significantly higher OLFM4 expression levels than ANT (Fig. 2c). Differential expression analysis identified distinct molecular signatures: compared to their OLFM4−counterparts, OLFM4+epithelial cells significantly upregulated genes such as LEFTY1, a key modulator of stemness and cell asymmetry, and CYP4B1, involved in xenobiotic metabolism (Fig. 2d). Pathway enrichment analysis via AUCell revealed that OLFM4+epithelial cells were markedly enriched for pathways associated with malignant progression, including cell proliferation and stemness (WNT/β-catenin, PI3K-AKT-mTOR), pro-metastatic programs (Metastasis-Up, Angiogenesis), and mechanisms involved in immunosuppression and TME remodeling (TGF-β signaling) (Fig. 2e).

Developmental trajectory and stem cell characteristics of the OLFM4+subsets

Pseudotemporal trajectory analysis delineated three distinct cell states, identifying State1 as the starting point of differentiation (Fig. 1a and b), and revealed a dynamic upregulation of OLFM4 expression over pseudotime, indicating its significant role in marking a malignant cellular transition within GBC (Fig. 1c). We classified the included cells into four clusters based on the changed gene sets along with the trajectory. Cluster 1 was predominantly associated with transcriptional regulation and RNA processing, including mRNA splicing, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER)-stress apoptosis. Cluster 2 exhibited strong enrichment for adaptive immune activation pathways, such as T-cell activation and nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) signaling. Cluster 3 highlighted proteasome-mediated degradation and innate immune responses, including interferon-β (IFN-β) signaling and antibacterial/antiviral defense mechanisms. Cluster 4 converged again on RNA splicing machinery—particularly U2 prespliceosome and small nuclear ribonucleoprotein particles (snRNP) assembly—coupled with redox homeostasis and innate immune signaling (Fig. 1d). OLFM4+cells, presenting at the terminal of the cellular differentiation trajectory, correlated with a high differentiation potential, which indicated a close relation to the malignant progression of GBC (Fig. 1e).

Cell-cell communication network dominated by OLFM4+cells via SPP1

OLFM4+epithelial cells are embedded in a multicellular signaling network, where they mainly exhibit active outgoing signaling toward endothelial, myeloid, and stromal cells, primarily through pathways including VEGF, growth differentiation factor (GDF), ANNEXIN, CXCL, SPP1, and macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF), suggesting a crucial role of OLFM4+epithelium in regulating angiogenesis, immune modulation, and matrix remodeling (Fig. 2a and b). Ligand-receptor analysis identified the important role of SPP1-CD44 and SPP1-integrin in mediating interactions between OLFM4+epithelium and other cell types, axes involved in the core processes of stem cell maintenance, invasion and metastasis, and microenvironment reprogramming (Fig. 2c). Given that CD44 is a canonical CSC marker and SPP1 is a potent immune modulator, this interaction suggests a positive feedback loop where OLFM4+cells actively maintain their own stem cell niche while suppressing anti-tumor immunity (Fig. 2d). These results suggest that OLFM4+epithelial cells may promote an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by regulating intercellular crosstalk.

OLFM4 promotes malignant phenotypes and chemoresistance in GBC

To validate the function of OLFM4, we established OLFM4-knockdown (NOZ, GBC-SD) and OLFM4-overexpressing (NOZ) cell lines. Western blot confirmed efficient knockdown and overexpression (Fig. 3a). The wound healing assay and transwell migration and invasion assays revealed that the downregulation of OLFM4 significantly inhibited the migration and invasion of GBC cells. Conversely, upregulation of OLFM4 revealed an opposing effect (Fig. 3b and c). Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is a crucial biological process, which is closely associated with increased metastatic potential and malignant progression in cancers (Dongre and Weinberg 2019). To better understand the effects of OLFM4 in GBC cells, we examined the expression of the EMT-related proteins. Western blot revealed that knockdown of OLFM4 in GBC cells downregulated the expression of N-cadherin, matrix metallopeptidase 9 (MMP9), matrix metallopeptidase 2 (MMP2) and vimentin, while upregulating E-cadherin. Conversely, overexpression of OLFM4 exerted opposite effects on these EMT markers (Fig. 3d). The raw data of Western blot was shown in supplementary Fig. 1. The detailed information for all primary and secondary antibodies used in western blot analysis was listed in Supplementary Table 1. Furthermore, OLFM4 knockdown in GBC cells displayed decreased proliferation rate (Fig. 3e), diminished colony forming abilities (Fig. 3f), and suppressed sphere formation capacity (Fig. 3g). Crucially, drug sensitivity assays revealed that OLFM4 downregulation sensitized GBC cells to gemcitabine (decreased IC50), while overexpression enhanced gemcitabine resistance (Fig. 3h). Taken together, OLFM4 plays a key role in driving the malignant progression and chemotherapy drug resistance of GBC, which is confirmed by in vitro studies.

Discussion

GBC is the most aggressive biliary tract cancer and faces formidable therapeutic challenges owing to the elusive driver which promotes the malignant progression of GBC. Advances in scRNA-seq have enabled the deciphering of heterogeneous TME and unique molecular signatures, leading to a more comprehensive understanding of GBC and the development of novel therapeutic strategies (Zhang et al. 2021a, b; Wang et al. 2022; Zhou et al. 2025). Similarly, our scRNA-seq analysis of GBC profiled a more comprehensive landscape of TME and identified OLFM4 as a pivotal molecular driver upregulated in malignant epithelial cells, which was strongly correlated with the maintenance of an aggressive phenotype of GBC.

Consistent with Zhang’s study (Zhang et al. 2021a, b), a more immunosuppressive TME in tumor tissues was identified through cellular composition analysis. A significant increase of OLFM4 was found in malignant GBC epithelium by scRNA-seq data and pseudotime analysis revealed a progressive upregulation of OLFM4+epithelium, indicating its potential association with disease progression. Besides, OLFM4+epithelium acted as central signal-sending hubs within the TME, orchestrating a pro-tumorigenic communication network through multiple ligand-receptor interactions, including SPP1-CD44, SPP1-integrin, MDK-SDC, and MIF-(CD74 + CXCR4). Therefore, we propose that a pro-tumorigenic signaling network centered on OLFM4+epithelium can actively regulate the malignant progression of GBC. Downregulation of OLFM4 potently suppressed the migratory, invasive, and proliferative capacities of GBC cells and attenuated tumor stemness in vitro, while overexpressing OLFM4 exhibited opposite effect.

Building on the single-cell–resolved view of GBC, our data support OLFM4 as a clinically actionable node linking malignant epithelial programs to an immunosuppressive, pro-tumorigenic microenvironment. OLFM4+ epithelial cells were progressively enriched along the malignant trajectory and emerged as dominant signal-sending hubs, suggesting that OLFM4 may serve both as a marker of aggressive disease and as a functional driver of stromal and immune crosstalk. The prominence of drug-accessible communication axes associated with the OLFM4 program, particularly SPP1–CD44/integrin signaling, indicates that targeting OLFM4 and/or its downstream effector pathways could represent tractable vulnerabilities in GBC. Although OLFM4 is an extracellular/secreted glycoprotein and is therefore amenable in principle to extracellular modalities such as neutralizing antibodies or immune-based strategies (Wen et al. 2024), no clinically approved agents that directly inhibit OLFM4 are currently available, and dedicated OLFM4-directed therapeutics would need to be developed. Nevertheless, preclinical studies in other disease contexts provide proof-of-concept that OLFM4 can be therapeutically targeted, including the use of an OLFM4-neutralizing antibody in a colitis-associated cancer model. Furthermore, aberrant OLFM4 expression correlates with chemoresistance in various cancers (Gao et al. 2019; He et al. 2025). Consistent with this, our drug-sensitivity assays demonstrated that OLFM4 depletion sensitized GBC cells to gemcitabine, whereas its overexpression conferred resistance. While prior studies have implicated elevated OLFM4 as a key regulator of GBC progression (Lin et al. 2022; He et al. 2024), our work extends these findings by defining the OLFM4+epithelium as a critical signaling orchestrator within the TME. Collectively, we demonstrate that OLFM4 drives GBC aggressiveness and chemoresistance by remodeling the TME via the SPP1 signaling axis, thereby highlighting it as a promising therapeutic target.

While our study delineates the tumor microenvironment landscape and implicates OLFM4 in gallbladder cancer pathogenesis, the limited sample size for certain subtypes may constrain generalizability, and future studies with larger, independent cohorts are warranted. It also remains to be determined whether the OLFM4-associated malignant programs and microenvironmental remodeling observed here are specific to gallbladder cancer or extend to other biliary tract cancers, which should be evaluated in additional biliary tract cancer datasets and clinical specimens. Moreover, the precise mechanisms by which OLFM4 regulates tumor progression and shapes the microenvironment require further elucidation using complementary in vivo models and mechanistic experiments in vitro. Finally, although our data support the translational potential of OLFM4 as a biomarker and therapeutic target, the feasibility, safety, and optimal therapeutic modality for OLFM4-directed intervention, as well as targeting actionable downstream pathways, will need to be systematically assessed.
