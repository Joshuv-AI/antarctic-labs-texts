Catching Up With Time: Endovascular Treatment Beyond 24 Hours
Authors: Manon Kappelhof, Johannes Kaesmacher
Published: 2023

Over the past decade, endovascular treatment (EVT) has revolutionized the management of acute ischemic stroke. 1 In 2018, the DAWN (Diffusion‐Weighted Imaging or CT Perfusion Assessment With Clinical Mismatch in the Triage of Wake‐Up and Late Presenting Strokes Undergoing Neurointervention With Trevo) and DEFUSE‐3 (Endovascular Therapy Following Imaging Evaluation for Ischemic Stroke) trials extended the original 0‐ to 6‐hour treatment window to ⩽24 hours from stroke onset or time of last seen well, if patients had favorable imaging characteristics on computed tomography (CT) perfusion or magnetic resonance imaging. 2 , 3 The number needed to treat to reduce disability by at least 1 level in the 6‐ to 24‐hour window was ≈3, compared with 2.6 in the 0‐ to 6‐hour time window. 1 , 4 Though these numbers remind us that EVT truly is one of the most effective treatments in modern medicine, their similarity in the early and late windows appears contradictory at first glance. Overselection is likely occurring in the late window with the relatively strict CT perfusion/magnetic resonance imaging–based selection criteria from the trials, leaving patients with potential benefit untreated. The recent MR CLEAN LATE (Endovascular Treatment of Acute Ischemic Stroke in the Netherlands for Late Arrivals) trial showed that patient selection in the 6‐ to 24‐hour window is also possible with single‐phase CT angiography and noncontrast CT. Among patients outside the DAWN and DEFUSE‐3 population, with at least some collaterals on CT angiography and demarcation of one‐third of the middle cerebral artery territory on noncontrast CT, EVT was effective compared with best medical care, with a number needed to treat for 1 additional functionally independent patient of ≈10. 5 The SELECT2 (A Randomized Controlled Trial to Optimize Patient's Selection for Endovascular Treatment in Acute Ischemic Stroke) and ANGEL‐ASPECT (Study of Endovascular Therapy in Acute Anterior Circulation Large Vessel Occlusive Patients With a Large Infarct Core) trials found that EVT is also effective in patients with larger cores in the 6‐ to 24‐hour as well as 0‐ to 6‐hour window (SELECT2: >50 cc core on CT perfusion or magnetic resonance imaging; ANGEL‐ASPECT: Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Scan 3–5 or 0–2/>5 and core volume 70–100 cc). 6 , 7 TESLA (Thrombectomy for Emergent Salvage of Large Anterior Circulation Ischemic Stroke; presented at ESOC 2023) did not reach statistical significance for EVT benefit in patients with Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Scan 3 to 5 but showed an effect estimate in the direction of benefit.

Overall, EVT selection criteria are widening within the entire 0‐ to 24‐hour treatment window. It seems challenging to find patient populations with large‐vessel occlusion within this window not showing benefit from EVT. The inevitable follow‐up question is: Why should we stop at 24 hours? The earth completing 1 rotation around its axis unlikely marks any biological limit of salvageable penumbra. Among the “slow progressors” benefiting from EVT in the 6‐ to 24‐hour window, there are likely “super slow progressors” sustaining their penumbras even longer.

In this issue of Stroke: Vascular and Interventional Neurology (SVIN), Dhillon et al evaluated the results of EVT beyond 24 hours from stroke onset or last seen well, in a single‐center, observational cohort study of 35 patients with ischemic stroke due to anterior circulation large‐vessel occlusion, with Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Scan ≥5. 8 During the study period (January 2018–May 2022), EVT was only available from 8 am to 6 pm, Monday through Friday. Patients presenting beyond 24 hours from stroke onset but within service hours, who were eligible for EVT, could be treated. This may have partially decreased selection bias among the non–EVT‐treated patients, since part of their treatment indication was based on their relatively random time of presentation. In total, 19 of 35 (54%) patients were treated with EVT plus best medical care and 16 of 35 (46%) with best medical care only.

Regarding selection bias, it should be noted that 4 patients (11%) who presented outside service hours were reassessed for EVT eligibility when service hours started and were then still treated with EVT. This could have introduced a small “super slow progressor” selection bias, possibly also reflected in baseline characteristic imbalances: EVT‐treated patients were younger and had slightly less severe strokes. Furthermore, selection bias is inherently present in the EVT‐treated patient group; details of EVT‐eligible patients not treated with EVT within service hours are not provided by the authors. Finally, only a subset of patients with stroke in the >24‐hour window will present at the comprehensive stroke center; many will stay at their primary center or maybe not even go to the hospital at all.

In addition, over two‐thirds of the included patients (24/35, 69%) had an unobserved symptom onset time, well balanced between the treatment groups. The mean time from last seen well to hospital admission was 35 hours (SD 11.5) in the EVT group, and 38 hours (SD 17) in the no‐EVT group. The actual stroke onset time of the unobserved patients may have been well within the 24‐hour window. Magnetic resonance imaging studies in patients with wake‐up stroke suggest that the time of stroke onset is likely in the early morning hours, indicating that patients with wake‐up stroke may in fact be early‐window patients. 9 Would it be closer to the truth to consider their wake‐up time as the time of stroke onset, rather than their time of last seen well? If so, studies may paint an unfairly optimistic picture of late‐window EVT benefit if the majority of their patients have wake‐up strokes. This may be true for all patients with unobserved‐onset stroke, as their duration of symptoms most likely is overestimated.

Further limitations include, as the authors state, a small sample size, limiting the interpretability of outcome differences between the treatment groups. Especially their multivariable analyses adjusting for 6 covariates should be interpreted with caution. Patients with stroke presenting outside service hours may differ from patients presenting during service hours; care quality may also differ. Finally, ethical issues exist in choosing which patients to treat when the service hour “window for EVT” opens—a situation not representative for many other health care settings offering EVT.

Nevertheless, taking these limitations into account, the setting of the study by Dhillon et al offers an interesting assessment of potential EVT benefit outside a randomized controlled trial. Perhaps unsurprisingly given the study's sample size, their outcomes showed no statistically significant differences between patients who were and were not treated with EVT. Numerically, however, the findings are interesting: the rate of 90‐day functional independence was 37% in the EVT group (7/19) compared with 19% in the no‐EVT group (3/16). Mortality rates were 26% (5/19) and 38% (6/16), respectively. Rates of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage were too low to derive meaningful estimates. 8

These findings are in line with the results of an increasing body of retrospective studies on EVT outcomes beyond 24 hours. 10 A recent study reported on 334 patients with large‐vessel occlusion stroke presenting >24 hours in 11 centers in the United States, of which 214 (64%) were treated with EVT, and 120 (36%) were not. 11 Baseline imaging consisted of noncontrast CT and CT angiography with or without CT perfusion. They found higher rates of 90‐day functional independence among EVT‐treated patients (40% versus 28%) and lower mortality rates (34% versus 63%). The difference was more pronounced among patients with more severe strokes (National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale ≥6). Unfortunately, the article does not mention why patients were treated beyond international guidelines, so selection bias is likely present. 11

A similar retrospective observational cohort study was done by Sarraj et al in 17 centers across multiple countries: the optimizing patients selection for endovascular treatment in acute ischemic stroke‐late (SELECT Late) study. 12 Among 301 patients presenting >24 hours from stroke onset or time of last seen well, 185 were treated with EVT, and 116 received medical management only. The decision to treat was made by the physicians on a case‐by‐case basis, again leaving room for selection bias. In a propensity score adjusted analysis, patients’ chances of 90‐day functional independence were significantly higher with EVT (38% versus 10%) at the cost of a higher risk of symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (10% versus 2%). 12

The group of Dhillon et al also recently compared EVT outcomes of 104 patients in the >24‐hour window (likely including the 19 patients from their current article) to 208 patients in the 6‐ to 24‐hour window, in a national stroke registry. 13 They found no differences in terms of functional outcome, reperfusion, death, or symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage. In the >24‐hour window, outcomes did not differ between patients selected with CT perfusion or noncontrast CT+CT angiography. A recent meta‐analysis summarized 7 observational studies on EVT >24 hours, reporting similar rates of 90‐day functional independence (32%; 95% CI, 25–40), successful reperfusion (90%; 95% CI, 79–85), and death (27%; 95% CI, 23–32). 10 The summarized symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage rate was 7% (95% CI, 4–11), slightly higher than in the 0‐ to 6‐hour and 6‐ to 24‐hour windows (≈4% and 5%, respectively). 1 , 4

Nearly all observational studies mention the option of a randomized controlled trial in this population. Is the time ripe to pull the randomized controlled trial trigger? If we are confronted with a previously healthy patient, last‐seen‐well 27 hours ago, with evidence of salvageable penumbra on baseline imaging, would we randomize rather than treat? On the other hand, will international guidelines change without a trial? As always, the answers may not be straightforward. Randomized evidence facilitates the most solid, most reliable way to introduce EVT >24 hours into clinical practice—and in the long run, globally, perhaps also the fastest. However, from a pathophysiological point of view, few arguments seem to exist to support a considerably smaller benefit of EVT in patients presenting beyond 1 rotation of the earth, if some basic assessment of salvageable tissue is maintained. Some of these arguments may be limited evidence of reperfusion benefit in noncollateralized tissue (eg, basal ganglia in M1 occlusion or occlusion of the terminal bifurcation of the internal carotid artery), and limited evidence on perfusion estimate reliability >24 hours. Perhaps, after 8 years of positive EVT trials, the available data can support evidence‐based EVT eligibility criteria for this particular group of patients.

Meanwhile, the first shot has already been fired: the LATE‐MT (Large Artery Occlusion Treated in Extended Time With Mechanical Thrombectomy; NCT05326932) trial is currently randomizing patients in China between EVT plus standard care and standard care only. Patients are eligible if they present within 24 to 72 hours of stroke onset or time of last seen well, have an anterior circulation large‐vessel stroke, National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale ≥6, and a CT of magnetic resonance perfusion assessed infarct core volume of <50 mL, a mismatch ratio of ≥1.8, and a mismatch volume of ≥15 mL. The future evidence of this trial could be supported by and substantiated with additional trials from other regions. However, in the design and execution of such trials, care should be taken to not end up in a basilar artery international cooperation study (BASICS)‐like scenario 14 : When there is insufficient clinical equipoise, patients will be treated outside of the trial, leaving only patients with questionable benefit to be randomized, in the end possibly slowing down scientific progress. One alternative could be to start treating patients with EVT beyond 24 hours in clinical practice, in centers with the capacity and conviction to do so. Efficacy and safety data could be compared with patients from other centers or historical cohorts. On a side note, in both randomized and observational data, a separate analysis of patients with observed and unobserved symptom onset would be highly advisable.

In the end, primum non nocere causes our difficulties: We should not harm the individual patient by offering them only a 50% chance of receiving a treatment that we know is effective, but we should also not harm all future patients by sustaining uncertainty in international guidelines on treatment efficacy, possibly causing damage on a much larger scale. The uncertainty around a possible benefit determines the approach by which the least harm is done, limited by the available knowledge. One of this editorial's authors would give EVT >24 hours a shot in clinical practice; the other one is trigger happy for randomization. As always, time will tell what the next decade brings.
