Empathy for pain in humans and animals differs based on species, psychosocial and cultural factors
Authors: Maria Suñol, Brock Bastian, Marina López-Solà
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Empathy for others’ pain guides moral concern and prosocial behaviour, yet how it extends across species remains to be elucidated. This study examined how people perceive and respond to pain in humans and animals, and how these responses relate to psychosocial traits. We developed and validated the Cross-Species Pain Empathy Task (C-SPET), the first tool to compare perceived pain and prosocial intentions toward humans, pets, and farmed animals under pain and no-pain conditions. 407 undergraduate participants completed the C-SPET and psychosocial questionnaires. Participants showed greater willingness to help animals than humans, possibly due to perceived vulnerability. Within animals, farmed animals were perceived as suffering more and received more support than pets when no pain was visible. This may reflect inferred suffering based on knowledge of farmed animals’ living conditions. When pain was explicit, pets were seen as suffering more and received more support. Pain cues may trigger closeness to pets or cognitive dissonance to farmed animal suffering. Canonical correlation analyses identified psychosocial profiles linked to C-SPET responses. A caring, pro-animal profile was associated with greater support for animals. A profile marked by emotional reactivity and cultural factors was linked to greater pain sensitivity. Empathic biases were shaped by distinct traits. Favouring animals over humans was associated with pro-animal attitudes and lower prejudice. Favouring pets over farmed animals was linked to ethnicity, higher meat consumption, conservatism, and social dominance, suggesting a tendency to maintain traditional roles around animal categories. The results highlight the psychosocial factors that shape pain empathy across species.

Introduction

Pain empathy, the capacity to recognize and respond to the pain of others, is a fundamental aspect of human social life, influencing caregiving, moral decision-making, and social cohesion1,2. While pain empathy is influenced by situational factors, such as the context in which pain occurs and the observer-target relationship3,4, evidence highlights the role of stable psychosocial traits in shaping individual differences in empathic responses. These traits include sociodemographic factors (e.g., gender, socioeconomic status)5,6, pain-related experiences and beliefs (e.g., chronic pain history, fear of pain)7–9, and social attitudes (e.g., dispositional empathy, prejudices)10,11. However, to date, no studies have systematically examined which psychosocial traits predict inferences about others’ pain, and whether those same traits also drive prosocial behaviour. Identifying these traits is key to understanding how pain empathy unfolds. While perceiving pain in others often elicits empathic concern and willingness to help12, not everyone who recognizes pain takes supportive action. Factors, such as dispositional personal distress (i.e., anxiety to others’ suffering), can modulate whether pain recognition translates into support13. Understanding how psychosocial traits shape pain inferences and the subsequent decision to help is key to identifying and overcoming barriers to compassionate action.

Research on pain empathy has largely focused on human-to-human interactions14–17, yet extending this work to non-human animals is essential to promote reduction of suffering and ethical treatment of animals. It is well established that many animals experience pain18–21. However, human perceptions of animal suffering vary depending on species category, cultural norms, and individual beliefs22–24. Sentience (i.e., the capacity for subjective experiences such as pain) is attributed differently across species depending on perceived intelligence, emotional expressiveness, familiarity, similarity to humans, and utility to humans25. These biases have real-world implications, influencing moral judgments, policies, and individual behaviour toward animals24,26–28. Despite a recent surge in human-animal relationship studies, there is a lack of validated tools for assessing empathic responses to animal pain, particularly in ways that allow for direct comparison with human pain. This methodological gap has limited research into how people perceive and respond to suffering across species. As a result, no studies have directly compared empathy for human vs. non-human animal pain and examined how responses differ between animal categories (e.g., pets vs. farmed animals). Moreover, little is known about the psychosocial traits that predict species-specific empathic biases.

This study addresses these gaps by developing and validating the first image-based task that enables comparable assessment of pain inferences and prosocial intentions toward humans, pets and farmed animals. We will examine differences across species categories (humans vs. animals, pets vs. farmed animals) and pain conditions (pain vs. no pain). Previous work suggests that empathy varies across species. People typically ascribe greater moral standing to humans and prioritise human needs over those of non-human animals when the two conflict26. Greater empathy for humans may also be reinforced by perceived similarity and biological proximity29, consistent with evidence that people empathize more with similar or in-group others11,30. Nevertheless, seeing animals as helpless or dependent could shift concern in the opposite direction31,32. Within animals, companion animals are typically afforded more concern and moral protection than farmed animals, whose suffering is minimised when they are framed as food or economic resources22,25,26,33,34. Based on this literature, we anticipated an empathic gradient favouring humans over animals, and, within animals, pets over farmed animals. Additionally, we will identify psychosocial traits linked to sensitivity to others’ pain (i.e., inferred pain levels) and prosocial intentions, revealing distinct psychosocial profiles linked to empathic responses. Prior research shows that other-oriented traits, such as empathic concern, perspective taking, broader moral concern, and inclusive social attitudes, are associated with greater recognition of others’ suffering and stronger willingness to help in both human- and animal-directed contexts23,35–38. In contrast, threat-related traits, including fear of pain, pain catastrophizing, and personal distress, are more reliably linked to heightened affective reactivity to others’ suffering, but may not necessarily motivate prosocial behaviour38–41. Demographic and cultural factors such as gender, socioeconomic background, and collectivistic cultural orientations have also been shown to modulate empathy and prosocial tendencies38,42–45. Based on this evidence, we hypothesised that other-oriented traits and female gender would predict greater perceived pain and prosocial intentions, whereas threat-related traits would be more strongly associated with heightened inferred pain than with prosocial behaviour. Last, we aim to identify psychosocial traits linked to species-specific empathic biases (i.e., differences in perceived pain and prosocial intentions across species categories). Caring and pro-animal traits, as well as female gender, have been linked to greater concern for non-human animals and to narrower gaps in moral standing across species23,24,26,34,46,47. In contrast, group-dominance and hierarchy-supporting attitudes, along with a strong commitment to eating meat, predict reduced concern for animals, particularly farmed animals, and stronger endorsement of human-animal and within-animal hierarchies24,48–51. We therefore expected caring and egalitarian orientations to be associated with smaller biases, and hierarchical and meat-committed orientations to predict larger differences favouring humans over animals and pets over farmed animals. This study aims to provide insights into the psychosocial mechanisms of pain empathy, which can inform interventions aimed at reducing suffering and promoting an ethical treatment of all sentient beings.

Methods

Participants

The main study sample included 407 first- and second-year psychology students from the University of Melbourne enrolled in the university’s Research Experience Program. Their participation was conducted remotely via REDCap running on University of Melbourne’s Sona Systems website. It involved completing both the newly developed Cross-Species Pain Empathy Task (C-SPET) and a comprehensive set of validated psychosocial questionnaires (detailed below). Demographic information is provided in Table 1. All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. The study protocol and consent procedure were approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the University of Melbourne (Project ID: 26718). All research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, the Australian Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research, and all relevant institutional guidelines and regulations.

Development and validation of the cross-species pain empathy task (C-SPET)

During the C-SPET, participants viewed 36 images depicting either wounded (pain condition) or unwounded (no-pain condition) humans, pets (cats and dogs), and farmed animals (cows and pigs) (Fig. 1). After observing each image, participants answered two questions using visual analogue scales (VAS): (1) pain inference: How much physical pain is the subject experiencing? No pain at all (0%) – The worst pain imaginable (100%) and (2) prosocial intentions with a personal cost of time: How much of your weekly free time would you personally dedicate to helping the subject recover from the physical pain they are experiencing? None of my weekly free time (0%) – All of my weekly free time (100%). To control for order effects, images were presented in two counterbalanced pseudo-randomized sequences, ensuring that no category (species or pain condition) appeared in a predictable pattern across participants. Regarding species, cats and dogs were chosen as the pet category because they are the most widespread and emotionally valued companion animals globally and consistently elicit strong empathic responses26,52. Cows and pigs were selected as farmed animals because (1) their mammalian status ensures biological comparability with pets, given that evolutionary proximity and morphological similarity are known to influence empathy and pain attribution29; (2) they are among the most commonly farmed mammal species and widely used in research on human–animal empathy and moral concern (e.g.50); and (3) they were the best represented farmed species in available image repositories, which was essential for constructing multiple stimuli per category.

The 36 final C-SPET images were selected from a pool of 52 piloted in two samples of 50 U.S. adults via Prolific (prolific.com). Both pain and no-pain human images were extracted from the DIRTI database53 and the EPPS-limb database54. Pain and no-pain animal images, including both pets and farmed animals, were extracted from WeAnimalsMedia (weanimalsmedia.org) and Adobe Stock Images (stock.adobe.com). Both human and animal images depicted wounded or unwounded body parts (e.g., arm, leg), excluding faces. Some animal images were cropped to avoid facial expressions. This approach was taken because humans are generally better at interpreting facial expressions in other humans than in animals. By using images that focused on body wounds rather than facial cues, we aimed to enhance comparability across species, ensuring that pain assessments were based on similar visual information rather than differential abilities to interpret facial expressions. Piloting ensured participants could differentiate between pain intensities (pain vs. no pain images) and confirmed the absence of ceiling and floor effects, and habituation and sensitization throughout the task. Images of different species categories did not significantly differ in arousal, disgust, or valence, except for no-pain pet images, which were perceived as slightly more positive than those of humans or farmed animals. Given that positive perceptions of pets are an inherent aspect of human-animal relationships, excluding these images would have reduced the ecological validity of the task. Additionally, since this effect was limited to the no-pain condition, it did not confound analyses focused on responses to explicit pain cues. Participants were involved in the study through their feedback on the clarity and usability of the C-SPET during piloting. Their input helped refine the instructions and improve the overall participant experience. To support transparency, replication, and broader scientific use, all C-SPET images and materials will be openly shared upon publication via Open Science Framework (OSF) (osf.io/hqkv5).

Psychosocial questionnaires

Participants from the main sample (N = 407) completed a series of validated self-reported questionnaires on pain beliefs and experiences. Current pain was assessed with questions 5 and 9 A from the Brief Pain Inventory-Short Form55. Participants rated (i) their average pain intensity over the past 24 h (item 5) and (ii) the extent to which pain interfered with their general activity over the same period (item 9 A), each on a 0–10 scale. We averaged these two items to create a single index of current pain, with higher scores indicating greater pain intensity and interference. We also asked them about chronic pain using a single yes/no question: “In the past 12 months, did you experience pain lasting more than 3 months or received treatment for chronic pain?”. Participants also completed the 9-item Fear of Pain questionnaire56, which measures pain-related fear, and the 13-item Pain Catastrophizing Scale57, which assesses catastrophic cognitions about pain-rumination, magnification, and helplessness. For these two questionnaires, we used the derived total scores. Last, we asked participants about the frequency of their exposure to others’ pain with the question “Throughout your life, how often have you personally observed someone else experiencing pain?” which they answered with a VAS ranging from 0 (never) to 100 (very often).

Participants also completed a set of validated self-reported scales on social attitudes, including dispositional empathy, morality, altruism, attitudes toward animals, and prejudices. Specifically, they completed the 28-item Interpersonal Reactivity Index58, which provides a multi-dimensional assessment of empathy through four subscales: Perspective Taking, measuring the tendency to adopt the psychological point of view of others; Fantasy, tapping into the tendency to transpose imaginatively into the feelings and actions of fictious characters; Empathic Care, assessing the other-oriented feelings of sympathy and concern for unfortunate others; and Personal Distress, measuring self-oriented feelings of anxiety and unease in tense interpersonal settings. We kept the four subscales in our analyses, as we wanted to examine the associations of different types of empathy in task responses. Participants also completed the 10-item Moral Expansiveness Short Form59, which assesses the extent to which a range of entities, both human and non-human, are deemed worthy of moral concern and treatment. We used the total score, which reflects the overall inclusivity of participants’ moral circles, with higher scores indicating greater moral expansiveness. Participants also completed the 9-item Self-Report Altruism scale60, assessing altruistic behaviour. To determine the solidarity and attitudes of participants toward non-human animals, they completed the 5-item Solidarity with Animals Scale34, assessing the connection and identification with other animals, and the 5-item Animal Attitude Scale61, measuring attitudes toward the use of other species by humans. To assess prejudices toward human groups, participants filled the 16-item Social Dominance Orientation Scale62, which measures the propensity for prejudice and preferences for group dominance over equality. Prejudices toward non-human animals were assessed with the 6-item Speciesism Scale23, which measures anthropocentric speciesist attitudes, defined as discrimination against non-human animals based on their species.

Finally, participants provided demographic information, including age (mean ± SD = 20.47 ± 3.97 years), gender (271 women, 76 men), ethnicity, yearly household income before taxes, education level, political orientation, religiosity, pet ownership, and diet (see Table 1 for full details).

Data analysis

All analysis code will be made openly available upon publication via OSF and GitHub (osf.io/hqkv5 ; github.com/neuroPENlab/publications) to facilitate reproducibility and reuse.

Differences in perceived pain in others and prosocial intentions based on species categorization and pain cues

We conducted linear mixed-effects models (LMEMs) with Jamovi running on R (jamovi.org) to examine differences in pain inferences and willingness to donate time based on the species depicted (humans vs. animals; pets vs. farmed animals), pain intensity shown in the images (no pain vs. pain), and their interaction. These factors were included as fixed effects. To account for individual variability in responses, we included random intercepts for participants.

Canonical correlation analysis

Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) was employed to investigate the multivariate associations between psychosocial predictors and task responses (i.e., pain inferences and willingness to donate time across species). Specifically, we ran two CCA models with Python 3 using pandas for data manipulation, numpy for numerical operations, statsmodels and scipy for statistical analyses, scikit-learn for CCA and data preprocessing, and matplotlib for data visualization.

In both CCA models, independent variables were the same and included pain-related variables (e.g., chronic pain diagnosis, pain catastrophizing) and social attitudes (e.g., perspective-taking, empathic care), assessed through self-reported questionnaires, and demographics factors (e.g., age, gender, political orientation). All predictor variables used in CCA models are shown in Table 1. The dependent variables in model 1 included perceived pain and willingness to donate time in response to images depicting explicit pain cues in humans, pets, and farmed animals. This approach allowed us to identify shared and species-specific patterns in responses to suffering. We focused solely on responses in the pain condition, as explicit pain cues act as strong social signals likely to elicit heightened emotional and cognitive responses2. Moreover, task responses in the no-pain condition were generally low and less variable, reducing their utility for detecting meaningful psychosocial relationships. Model 2 focused on differences in perceived pain and prosocial intentions between humans and animals (e.g., perceived pain in animals – perceived pain in humans), as well as between pets and farmed animals (e.g., perceived pain in pets – perceived pain in farmed animals), specifically within the pain condition. This provided insight into potential psychosocial factors influencing empathic biases between species categories.

In both CCA models, we standardized predictors and outcomes using z-scores to ensure comparability across measures with different scales. Participants with missing data were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 338. To identify robust and generalizable canonical dimensions, we applied CCA with 5-fold cross-validation (in each fold: training set size = 270/271; test set size = 68/67). Stratification was performed using participants’ gender to maintain balanced representation across folds. To further ensure fold comparability, we statistically assessed differences in all included variables across training and test sets for each fold, confirming no significant imbalances (p’s > 0.05). This allowed model performance to be assessed across multiple data splits, reducing the risk of overfitting. Within each fold, canonical correlations were computed between the predictor (psychosocial factors) and outcome (task responses) sets. To assess statistical significance, we performed permutation testing with 10,000 permutations. Specifically, we permuted the outcome matrix while keeping the predictors fixed. This procedure yielded null distributions for each canonical correlation, from which p-values were derived. The optimal number of components was determined based on a scree plot of cross-validated canonical correlations, retaining only the components whose canonical correlation was statistically significant (p’s < 0.05) based on permutation testing. To facilitate interpretation, canonical loadings were examined, highlighting variables with the strongest contributions to each canonical dimension. We focused on loadings above the 70th percentile, considering these as the most influential predictors of task responses63. Additionally, we calculated the proportion of variance in the data explained by each canonical component, expressed as the squared canonical correlation.

Post hoc mediation analysis

Evidence suggests that pain empathy or the capacity to recognise suffering may be a key precursor to prosocial behaviour64,65. Therefore, we performed follow-up mediation analyses to examine whether prosocial intentions were directly influenced by individual traits, or whether they emerged indirectly through heightened sensitivity to others’ suffering (i.e., higher levels of perceived pain). In each mediation model, the independent variable (X) was defined as participants’ scores on the canonical variates obtained from the predictor set (i.e., a weighted combination of psychosocial variables derived from each component of the canonical correlation analysis). In the mediation models following the first CCA, where the dependent variables were task responses, the mediator (M) was computed as the mean perceived pain rating across humans, pets, and farmed animals, and the outcome (Y) was the mean willingness to donate time across the same species. In contrast, the mediation models following the second CCA, which focused on species-specific biases, used difference scores. For Component 1, which had strong loadings reflecting a bias between humans and animals, the mediator (M) was computed as the difference in perceived pain between humans and animals, and the outcome (Y) as the corresponding difference in willingness to help. For Component 2, which captured differences between pets and farmed animals, the mediator (M) and outcome (Y) were calculated as the differences in pain perception and prosocial intentions between these two animal categories. This approach allowed us to test whether observed empathic biases were driven by differences in pain perception across species. All variables were z-standardized prior to analysis. Classic mediation models were computed on Python 3 using ordinary least squares regression to estimate path coefficients.

Perceived pain and prosocial intentions by species and pain conditions (N = 407).

We first examined differences in perceived pain and willingness to donate time to humans and animals across no-pain and pain scenarios (Fig. 2 and Table 3). For perceived pain, the model revealed significant main effects of species and pain intensity, along with a significant species-by-pain intensity interaction. Within-species comparisons indicated that both humans and animals were rated as experiencing significantly more pain in pain scenarios compared to no-pain scenarios. Between-species comparisons showed that participants perceived animals as experiencing more pain than humans in the absence of pain cues, though this difference was no longer significant when pain was present. Regarding willingness to donate time, the model revealed significant main effects of species and pain intensity, while the interaction term was not significant. Within-species comparisons confirmed that participants were significantly more willing to donate time in pain scenarios compared to no-pain scenarios for both humans and animals. However, participants consistently reported greater willingness to help animals compared to humans, regardless of pain cues.

To further investigate whether responses varied based on animal categorisation, we compared perceptions of pets and farmed animals to examine whether the type of relationship typically associated with each category influenced participants’ judgments (Fig. 2 and Table 3). For perceived pain, the model revealed significant main effects of pain intensity and a significant species-by-pain intensity interaction. Post-hoc comparisons indicated that participants perceived farmed animals as experiencing more pain than pets when no pain cues were present. However, this pattern reversed when explicit pain cues were present, with participants perceiving pets as experiencing more pain than farmed animals. A similar pattern emerged for willingness to donate time, with significant main effects of species, pain intensity, and their interaction. Post-hoc comparisons showed a preference for aiding farmed animals over pets in the absence of pain cues, which shifted toward prioritizing pets when pain was explicit.

Psychosocial factors associated with perceived pain in others and prosocial intentions

The first CCA model was conducted to examine the relationship between psychosocial factors (independent variables) and levels of perceived pain and prosocial intentions to pain in humans, pets, and farmed animals (dependent variables). The scree plot indicated that two canonical components significantly captured the relationship between predictors and outcomes (cross-validated canonical correlations of Component 1: r = .45, p < .001 and Component 2: r = .35, p = .006). These components explained 20.4% and 12.3% of the cross-validated variance in the data, respectively.

Component 1 captured a pro-animal and general caring psychosocial profile characterized by high solidarity and positive attitudes toward animals, empathic care (disposition to feel sympathy for others’ distress), previous exposure to others’ pain (frequency of witnessing others in pain throughout one’s lifetime), and low speciesism (prejudice against animals compared to humans). This caring/pro-animal profile was associated with greater prosocial intentions to animals and, less strongly, to humans, and greater sensitivity to animals’ pain (Fig. 3A). Post hoc mediation analysis revealed that the relationship between pro-animal and caring traits loading in this first component (X) and prosocial intentions (Y) was partially mediated by sensitivity to others’ pain (M) (Fig. 3A).

Psychosocial factors associated with species-based empathic biases

A second CCA was conducted to explore how psychosocial factors relate to species-specific biases in C-SPET responses, focusing on differences between humans and animals, as well as between pets and farmed animals. The scree plot indicated that two canonical components optimally captured these relationships, explaining 14.5% and 9.6% of the cross-validated variance in the data, respectively. Both components showed significant cross-validated canonical correlations (Component 1: r = .38, p = .002; Component 2: r = .31, p = .020).

Component 1 revealed that the bias favouring animals over humans was associated with pro-animal attitudes (i.e., high solidarity with animals, positive attitudes toward animals, and low speciesism) and lifestyle choices consistent with these pro-animal values (i.e., following a plant-forward diet and cohabiting with pets). This profile also included broader sociomoral traits, including high moral expansiveness and low social dominance orientation (prejudice and preferences for group dominance over equality) (Fig. 4A). Post hoc mediation analyses showed that greater sensitivity to animals’ pain (vs. humans) (M) partially mediated the relationship between the pro-animal and sociomoral traits loading in Component 1 (X) and stronger prosocial intentions toward animals compared to humans (Y) (Fig. 4A).

Discussion

This is the first study to examine how perceived pain in others and prosocial intentions vary based on species and are shaped by psychosocial traits. Overall, animals were perceived as more vulnerable and received more support than humans, with responses differing by animal category. Farmed animals were perceived as more vulnerable in no-pain scenarios, while pets were when pain was explicit. Multivariate analysis revealed that pain sensitivity, prosocial intentions, and species biases were linked to distinct psychosocial profiles, involving dispositional empathy, pro-animal attitudes, emotional reactivity, and sociocultural factors.

Participants perceived more pain and reported greater willingness to help others when pain was visible (vs. no-pain scenarios). Visual salience enhances empathic engagement by increasing affective arousal and attention to suffering2. Since C-SPET stimuli consisted of static images of isolated body parts, the task likely engaged cognitive and inferential aspects of empathy rather than affective resonance16,66–69. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted in the context of primarily cognitive judgments of pain and prosocial concern rather than automatic affective responses. We observed differences in responses to human vs. animal pain. Animals were perceived as more vulnerable than humans in no-pain scenarios. This contradicts our hypothesis, which was based on evidence that humans typically receive higher moral standing and elicit greater empathy due to in-group membership, perceived similarity, and biological proximity11,29,30. This animal-over-human pattern emerged only in no-pain scenarios and disappeared when pain was explicit, suggesting that, in the absence of pain cues, participants may rely on generalized beliefs about animals as more vulnerable and helpless31. Given the well-documented challenges in assessing animal pain e.g.,70, participants may compensate by erring on the side of caution and assuming suffering where it could not be confidently ruled out. Participants were consistently more willing to help animals than humans regardless of pain cues, which also stands in contrast to similarity-based accounts of empathy11,29,30. Perceived vulnerability and interpretive uncertainty may evoke higher prosocial concern. Additional factors like perceived innocence or moral obligation to protect those seen as dependent may also contribute to this pattern32, particularly given that many animals live in human-controlled contexts where they are viewed as especially reliant on human care24,27. The preference for animals may only emerge in low-cost helping contexts and may not extend to moral dilemmas or scarcity contexts where people often prioritize human over animal needs26.

Responses within the animal group were not homogenous. We observed an interaction between species categorization (pets vs. farmed animals) and pain salience (pain vs. no-pain). In no-pain scenarios, farmed animals were perceived as suffering more and received greater support than pets. This could reflect a lack of emotional conflict: participants may rely on general knowledge about the poor conditions of farmed animals71, inferring a higher baseline level of suffering in the absence of pain cues. When pain was explicit, participants attributed more pain to pets and showed greater willingness to help them, in line with our hypothesis. This shift may reflect the cognitive dissonance that arises when people confront the suffering of farmed animals, which conflicts with their dietary habits or cultural norms. Evidence consistently shows that, to resolve this moral conflict, people reduce empathic engagement by minimizing the animals’ mind and capacity to suffer22,33. Simultaneously, visible suffering in pets may trigger stronger empathy due to emotional closeness and familiarity25,26,34. These results suggest that differences in empathy toward pets and farmed animals’ pain are shaped by motivated reasoning driven by moral conflict and social norms. We observed this pattern across Australian and U.S. samples (main and C-SPET validation samples), suggesting some generalizability across westernized societies. Caution is warranted when extrapolating these findings globally, as perceptions of animal welfare, attitudes toward animals, meat consumption norms, and moral beliefs vary substantially across cultures72,73.

We identified two psychosocial profiles associated with inferred pain in others and prosocial intentions. These two constructs did not load onto separate components, suggesting that sensitivity to others’ pain is linked to the motivation to help. This supports the notion that recognising suffering may be a key precursor to compassionate action64,65. The first profile was characterized by high solidarity and positive attitudes toward animals, empathic care (disposition to feel sympathy for others’ distress), previous exposure to others’ pain (frequency of witnessing others in pain throughout one’s lifetime), and low speciesism (prejudice against animals compared to humans). This caring/pro-animal profile predicted greater prosocial intentions to animals and, less strongly, to humans, and greater sensitivity to animals’ pain. Sensitivity to pain in others (levels of inferred pain) partially mediated the link between caring/pro-animal traits and prosocial intentions. This suggests that individuals with caring/pro-animal traits tend to help because they are both sensitive to others’ suffering and intrinsically motivated to care. These findings align with our hypothesis and with evidence showing that empathic care and pro-animal attitudes support generalizable concern across species34,65. The role of previous exposure to others’ pain deserves further attention. Unlike dispositional empathy or pro-animal attitudes, which are relatively stable traits, exposure to others’ suffering may reflect a dynamic, experience-based pathway to compassion, potentially trainable through caregiving, volunteering, or education. While repeated exposure to others’ pain can lead to empathic fatigue, it can foster greater prosocial concern when paired with emotion regulation and a compassionate stance74,75. The second profile was characterized by identification with Asian ethnicity, religiosity, and heightened emotional reactivity to suffering (dispositional fear of pain and personal distress). This profile was linked to greater sensitivity to others’ pain (greater inferred pain across species) and prosocial intentions, particularly toward humans and pets. Sensitivity to others’ pain fully mediated the association between these emotional and cultural traits and prosocial intentions, suggesting a form of distress-based helping or a culturally shaped sense of responsibility triggered by visible suffering. In some Asian cultures, collectivist norms promote helping as a social obligation when a need is recognized76. Rather than stemming from individual empathic concern, this response may reflect culturally driven norms of care. Religiosity may reinforce it by emphasizing moral duties to help, though its link to actual prosocial behaviour is weaker than to self-reported intentions77. In line with our hypotheses, we found a dissociation between other-oriented and threat-related traits in predicting pain sensitivity and prosocial intentions. Empathic care (other-oriented) and personal distress (threat-related) loaded onto the first and second profiles, respectively. Empathic care was consistently linked to prosociality, while personal distress motivated helping only when suffering was explicit. This supports evidence that both can drive prosocial behaviour, but care is typically a more reliable motivator39,40.

We identified two psychosocial profiles linked to species-specific biases. Favouring animals over humans and pets over farmed animals loaded onto separate components, suggesting distinct psychosocial drivers behind these biases. The profile associated with a preference for animals over humans was characterized by pro-animal attitudes and lifestyle choices consistent with these values (following a plant-forward diet and cohabiting with pets). It also included greater moral expansiveness and lower social dominance orientation. Hence, favouring animals reflects not only pro-animal attitudes but also a broader egalitarian orientation. In this context, favouring animals may reflect a prioritization of groups seen as vulnerable or overlooked, but it also raises the possibility that a strong animal focus could, in some cases, diminish attention to human suffering. Our interpretation aligns with current theories of social identification with animals, in which feeling connected to other animals is associated with a greater desire to help them, even if this implies withdrawing privileges to humans34,78. Prior work linked greater empathy for animals over humans to female gender46 and cohabiting with pets47. In our study, gender did not emerge as a significant predictor of empathic biases. This result should be interpreted with caution given the marked gender imbalance in our sample (≈ 77% women), which likely reduced our ability to detect gender effects. At the same time, recent research suggests that gender differences tend to be more pronounced in affective forms of empathy than in cognitive or evaluative judgments5,79,80. The outcomes modelled in CCA (pain inferences and willingness to help) primarily reflect cognitive appraisal processes, which could partially explain why gender effects were not significant. Consistent with this interpretation, we observed no post-hoc gender differences in cognitive empathy (IRI perspective taking) and no reliable gender effects in species-specific biases (i.e., animals > humans, pets > farmed animals). Within this study, empathic biases were therefore more closely associated with pro-animal attitudes and moral values than with gender per se. The preference for pets over farmed animals was shaped by sociocultural and ideological factors, including identification with Asian and not European/American ethnicities, higher meat consumption, social dominance, and conservative political views. This pattern aligns with our hypothesis and with cross-cultural research showing that cultural and ideological contexts shape concern across species boundaries26,81. We caution against overinterpreting the role of ethnicity, given the heterogeneity of Asian backgrounds and the multicultural context of participants living in Australia. A plausible yet speculative explanation is that cultural frameworks that emphasize in-group loyalty and relational hierarchies81,82 may influence differential treatment of animals. Social dominance and conservative orientations have been consistently linked to stronger support for human-animal hierarchies, which extends to hierarchies among animals and to reduced concern for farmed animals48. Higher meat consumption has been associated with cognitive dissonance, where individuals downplay farmed animals’ suffering to ease the conflict between their dietary habits and moral values22,33. Overall, favouring pets over farmed animals seems rooted in ideological and cultural frameworks that shape a tendency to maintain societal norms around animal roles and justify differential moral treatment. Fear of pain and lower empathic care had weaker effects but reinforced the bias within this profile.

This study has important limitations. First, our Australian undergraduate sample limits generalizability to other contexts, particularly given cross-cultural variation in attitudes toward animals and pain26,81. Due to sample size constraints, we grouped together diverse ethnic backgrounds, which limits cultural interpretations. Additionally, although we collected information on animal-related occupations and rural upbringing or residence, the number of participants in these categories was very small (e.g., 1.9% working with animals, 3.7% living in rural areas, 9.3% raised in rural areas). This prevented meaningful analyses of how exposure to farm animals might influence responses. Future studies with more diverse samples are needed to address this question. However, in our study, post-hoc comparisons showed no reliable differences between rural- and urban-raised participants in any of the variables significantly loading into CCA components. Second, our sample was unbalanced in terms of gender (≈ 77% women), which limits interpretation of null gender effects in CCA. Although exploratory tests did not reveal gender differences in empathic biases, the unequal distribution prevented testing CCA models separately by gender. Future studies using more gender-balanced samples are needed to rigorously assess potential gender differences. Third, animal categories included only two species each (cats and dogs as pets, and cows and pigs as farmed animals). Cats and dogs were chosen because they are the most common and emotionally valued companion animals26,52, while cows and pigs were selected because they are mammals (ensuring biological comparability29), are widely studied as representative farmed species, and were the best represented in available image repositories. Future work should examine whether our findings generalize across a broader range of species. Fourth, using static images of body parts in the C-SPET likely reduced ecological validity, as static body part cues mainly engage cognitive and inferential aspects of empathy, capturing only part of the broader affective and experiential dimensions16,66–69. We chose this design to control perceptual features across species and to ensure suitability for future fMRI use, where simple and standardized stimuli help minimise confounds. We acknowledge that richer and dynamic stimuli (e.g., faces, full bodies, videos) would evoke more naturalistic empathic responses and should be incorporated in future work. Fifth, we assessed prosocial intentions rather than behaviour and relied on self-reported psychosocial traits, which may be subject to social desirability and not fully capture how individuals behave in real-world settings. Finally, while CCA reveals multivariate associations, it does not establish causality, underscoring the need for experimental and longitudinal studies.

In conclusion, inferences about others’ pain and prosocial intentions are shaped by species categorization and distinct psychosocial and cultural traits. Perceptions of who deserves help depend not only on visible suffering, but on who the suffering being is and the observer’s values, attitudes, and social context. By identifying key drivers of empathy for pain in others across species, this work offers a foundation for addressing barriers to extending compassion toward the suffering of all sentient beings.
