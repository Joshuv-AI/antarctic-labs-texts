Lactiplantibacillus plantarum Attenuates Diet‐Induced Obesity and Insulin Resistance Through Gut Microbiota‐Driven PPAR/PI3K‐Axis Modulation
Authors: Yecheng Gao, Anqi Zhu, Jingjin Li, Hualin Liu, Xiaomin Li, Heping Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLPPerfectus001 (LPP; GDMCC 62334) is a strain isolated from traditional pickled vegetables and identified as Lactiplantibacillus plantarum by 16s rRNA sequencing and biochemical analysis. Whole‐genome sequencing and functional prediction revealed that it is rich in carbohydrate metabolism and lipid metabolism genes, as well as short‐chain fatty acid synthesis genes. Preliminary experiments on high‐sugar and high‐fat zebrafish showed that LPP has the potential to improve glucose and lipid metabolism. Subsequently, in a high‐fat/high‐fructose obese rat experiment, a 7‐week intervention with LPP was conducted. Without altering energy intake, the high‐dose LPP (LPPH) significantly reduced body weight and fat mass (p < 0.05), improved insulin sensitivity (reduced HOMA‐IR and fasting blood glucose, p < 0.01) and normalised lipid profiles, thereby enhancing glucose homeostasis. This intervention reversed gut microbial dysbiosis by enriching beneficial bacteria (Akkermansia and Lactobacillus) and suppressing pathogens ( Klebsiella pneumoniae and Ruminococcus), while also increasing short‐chain fatty acids (butyrate/acetate) associated with improved metabolism. Transcriptome analysis revealed that LPPH remodelled adipose tissue through the PPAR signalling pathway (downregulating Pparg and upregulating Cpt1a) and activated the hepatic PI3K‐Akt signalling pathway to enhance insulin sensitivity. These results suggest that LPPH alleviates metabolic disorders by synergistically regulating the gut microbiota‐fat‐liver axis (independent of appetite suppression), making it a promising probiotic therapy for the treatment of obesity‐related metabolic disorders.

Introduction

Obesity has become a major global public health challenge, defined by the World Health Organization as abnormal or excessive fat accumulation (Dragano et al. 2020). Excessive intake of high saturated fat, refined sugar and processed foods in the modern diet is a major contributing factor. Obesity not only significantly increases the risk of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and various cancers, but also places a heavy burden on healthcare systems (Aziz et al. 2024). Current mainstream diet and exercise interventions are often limited in effectiveness due to lack of compliance, while existing drug therapies (such as phentermine, orlistat and liraglutide) face limitations such as unstable efficacy and adverse reactions such as gastrointestinal disturbances and cardiovascular risks (Afshin et al. 2017; Chooi et al. 2019). Therefore, the search for safe and effective alternative treatment options is urgent (Brown et al. 2023; Hu 2023; Jin et al. 2023). Although traditional therapies such as drug therapy and lifestyle interventions often have limited long‐term efficacy (Müller et al. 2022; Bulik and Hardaway 2023), emerging research indicates that gut microbiota regulation is a promising therapeutic target that may provide a safer and more sustainable new strategy for obesity management (Bae and Lee 2021; Wang et al. 2023).

Emerging evidence underscores the pivotal role of gut microbiota as a metabolic ‘organ’ influencing energy harvest, systematic inflammation, glucose and lipid metabolism and obesity (Geng et al. 2022). Comparative analyses between obese and lean individuals reveal distinct microbial compositional differences, including reduced bacterial diversity (Kasai et al. 2015; Liébana‐García et al. 2021), altered Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratios (Turnbaugh et al. 2006; Haro et al. 2016) and diminished microbial gene counts in obesity (Le Chatelier et al. 2013). A seminal faecal microbiota transplantation study demonstrates that transferring gut microbiota from obese donors to germ‐free mice recapitulates the obese phenotype even under isocaloric conditions, resulting in increased adiposity, insulin resistance and dyslipidaemia (Bäckhed et al. 2004). Similarly, this phenomenon has been replicated in twins exhibiting varying degrees of obesity and leanness (Turnbaugh et al. 2009; Xu et al. 2022).

Mechanistically, the gut microbiome orchestrates systemic metabolic homeostasis through the production of microbial‐derived metabolites such as short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs), lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and tryptophan derivatives (Zhang et al. 2017). These metabolites mediate bidirectional communication between the gut and peripheral tissues (Cani et al. 2007), modulating systemic inflammation, adipose tissue function and hepatic lipid metabolism by interacting with host receptors. For instance, LPS from Gram‐negative bacteria can exacerbate adipose tissue inflammation by activating Toll‐like receptor 4 (Tran et al. 2020). Conversely, SCFAs like acetate and butyrate help regulate energy balance through G protein‐coupled receptor signalling, enhancing insulin sensitivity and mitochondrial function (Torres‐Fuentes et al. 2019).

Dietary interventions, particularly those involving lactic acid bacteria, have shown promise in regulating immune function, restoring microbial balance and improving metabolic parameters in various in vitro and in vivo studies (Kim et al. 2022; Wang and Li 2022; Xie et al. 2024). For example, a study in mice demonstrated that administering Lacticaseibacillus paracasei can attenuate hepatic steatosis by enhancing peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor (PPAR) activity and suppressing inflammatory pathways (Ding et al. 2016). Additionally, Lactiplantibacillus (L.) plantarum, a well‐characterised probiotic species, has been shown to have multifaceted benefits, including immune regulation, enhancement of lipid metabolism and antioxidant activity (Crakes et al. 2019; Heeney et al. 2019; Salomé‐Desnoulez et al. 2021). It has been shown in mouse studies that one of its anti‐obesity mechanisms involves regulating leptin and adiponectin levels, which reduces fat accumulation and promotes lipid metabolism (Wang et al. 2020). In a high‐fat diet mouse model, administering L. plantarum enhances insulin sensitivity, downregulates hepatic genes Glut‐4 and G6pase, reduces low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) and restores intestinal microbiota diversity by decreasing the Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes ratio (Jacouton et al. 2023). Furthermore, a human intervention trial demonstrated that L. plantarum strains effectively but variably lower cholesterol levels, achieving a 13.6% reduction in total cholesterol (TC) among patients with high initial cholesterol levels, which highlights the strain‐specificity of probiotic effects of these bacteria (Fuentes et al. 2013). These findings collectively position the gut microbiome as a dynamic modulator of obesity progression and a promising therapeutic target.

In this study, a new strain, LPPerfectus001 (LPP; GDMCC 62334), was isolated from traditional pickled vegetables in Ganxi Town, Pujiang County, Sichuan Province. 16S rRNA gene sequence and biochemical characterisation analysis revealed that LPP is a novel strain of Lactiplantibacillus plantarum. Whole‐genome sequencing and KEGG annotation analysis revealed that LPP is enriched with genes involved in carbohydrate and lipid metabolism. Compared to previously reported anti‐obesity Lactobacillus strains, LPP not only possesses beneficial properties such as gastric acid and bile salt tolerance, but also has a unique SCFA biosynthesis gene profile, suggesting enhanced metabolic regulation potential. Initial zebrafish experiments confirmed LPP's potential effects on glucose and triglyceride metabolism. Subsequent studies in rat models further revealed its ability to alleviate HFFD‐induced obesity, insulin resistance and dyslipidaemia by modulating the gut microbiota, metabolites, hepatocytes and adipocyte gene transcription. These findings highlight the innovative nature of LPP compared to similar strains in terms of metabolite production (such as SCFAs) and host interaction mechanisms, while also elucidating the interplay between the gut microbiota, host metabolism and dietary intervention.

Materials and Methods

Identification of LPP Strain

LPP was activated on MRS agar plates inoculated with a single strain. The V3–V4 variable region of the 16S rDNA gene was amplified by the primers of F 5′‐ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG‐3′ and R 5′‐GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT‐3′. PCR products were purified and validated, and the libraries were used for sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China). The sequences were BLAST in NCBI. The activated LPP was diluted in physiological saline to McFarland scale of 2.7 to 3.3 and inoculated onto VITEK ANC test cards. The results were observed following 24 h of anaerobic cultivation (Caporaso et al. 2011). The strain identification was performed by the China Center of Industrial Culture Collection.

Whole‐Genome Identification and Functional Prediction

Genomic DNA was extracted using the TIANamp Bacterial DNA Kit (Tiangen, China). Libraries were constructed with the BGISEQ‐500 RS Nano DNA Kit (BGI‐Shenzhen) following protocols. Whole‐genome sequencing was performed using the DNBSEQ platform and Nanopore platform (BGI‐Shenzhen, China). Low‐quality reads (≤ 20, with 40% as the default threshold) and reads with a high proportion of Ns (10% as the default threshold) were filtered out using SOAPnuke 1.5.6 to obtain clean reads. The clean reads were then assembled into a whole genome using Canu (v1.0) and GATK (v1.6‐13). Gene prediction was carried out on the assembled genome using Glimmer (v3.02) (Delcher et al. 2007). The amino acid sequences of the predicted genes were annotated by comparing them against the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) database (Kanehisa et al. 2016). Whole‐genome sequencing was performed by BGI‐Shenzhen.

Preliminary Experiments on Glucose and Lipid Metabolism in the Zebrafish

One‐day‐old normally developed zebrafish were selected and randomly assigned to a 6‐well plate. Each well was supplemented with culture medium containing 2.5% glucose and the test substance. The zebrafish were incubated for 2 days in a biochemical incubator at 28.5°C. After homogenisation, the glucose was measured using a glucometer (Roche, USA). One‐day‐old normally developed zebrafish were selected and randomly assigned to a 6‐well plate. Each well was supplemented with culture medium containing 0.1% yolk powder, 1% glucose and the test substance. The zebrafish were incubated for 2 days in a biochemical incubator at 28.5°C. After homogenisation, the triglycerides were measured using an ELISA kit (MEIAOBIO, Shanghai, China).

Preparation of LPP for the Rat Experiment

The LPP formulation was prepared as a freeze‐dried powder (1 g/bag) with a viable bacterial count of 2.7 × 1011 CFU/g. The powder was stored at −20°C ± 2°C and provided by Perfect (Guangdong) Co. Ltd., China.

Rat Experimental Design

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval No.: SPF2022011) of the Guangdong Pharmaceutical University (Guangzhou, China). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines to ensure rigorous and transparent reporting of in vivo experiments (Kilkenny et al. 2010).

Male specific pathogen‐free Sprague–Dawley rats (5–6 weeks old) were procured from the Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center (Guangzhou, China) and housed under specific pathogen‐free conditions with a 12‐h light–dark cycle, temperature maintained at 22°C–24°C and humidity at 60%–65%. Following a 1‐week acclimatisation period on a standard diet, the rats were randomly divided into two groups: the control group (CONT, n = 10) was allowed free access to standard diet and water, the HFFD group (Li et al. 2022) was allowed free access to high‐fat diet and a high‐fructose corn syrup‐sweetened beverage. The standard diet contained 19.6% protein, 55.3% carbohydrate and 4.5% fat, providing 23% of calories from protein, 65% from carbohydrates and 12% from fat, with a digestible energy content of 3.40 kcal/g. The high‐fat diet contained 26.2% protein, 26.3% carbohydrate and 34.9% fat. Protein and carbohydrate each provide 20% energy, while fat provides 60%, with an energy density of 5.24 kcal per gram (D12492, Research Diets Inc., New Brunswick, NJ, USA). A high‐fructose corn syrup‐sweetened beverage (12.5% fructose solution, 3.54 kcal/mL) was supplied separately. Food and water were provided ad libitum to the mice. The energy intake and energy efficiency (body weight gain (g)/total energy intake (kcal)) of each group were calculated. After a 2‐week observation period, the top 20% of obesity‐resistant rats with the lowest weight gain were excluded. To assess dose‐dependent protective effects of LPP, the HFFD group was further subdivided into four subgroups in the third week (n = 10 per group): HFFD (saline), LPPL (low dose), LPPM (medium dose) and LPPH (high dose) (Figure 1A). For the next 8 weeks, LPP was administered orally to the LPPL, LPPM and LPPH groups at doses of 1.7 × 108 CFU/kg/d, 8.3 × 108 CFU/kg/d and 4.2 × 109 CFU/kg/d.

Body weight was recorded twice per week and the feed intake and water consumption were recorded every other day. At the end of the 10‐week study, OGTT and ITT tests were performed, body composition was analysed using a Minispec LF90II Body Composition Analyser (Bruker Corporation, Billerica, MA, USA), following a previously established protocol (Li et al. 2019). At the end of the trial, rats were euthanised via sodium pentobarbital injection, and blood was collected and centrifuged to isolate serum. The resulting serum samples and the faecal samples from the dissected colon were kept at −80°C until further analyses. Liver, inguinal white adipose tissue (iWAT), and epididymal white adipose tissue (eWAT) were harvested. Tissues were either fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological assessment or flash‐frozen in liquid nitrogen, stored at −80°C for RNA sequencing.

Histopathologic Evaluation

Hepatocytes and adipose tissues were rinsed with cold saline and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Fixed tissues were paraffin‐embedded, sectioned into 5‐μm slices and stained with haematoxylin and eosin as previously described (Zhang et al. 2021).

Serum Biochemical Parameter Analysis

After fasting for 12 h, fasting blood glucose levels were determined using a glucometer (Accu‐Chek Guide, Accu‐Chek, Mannheim, Germany). Serum triglycerides (TG), TC, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), and LDL‐C were quantified with a biochemical analyser (Urit Medical Electronics Co. Ltd., Guilin, China). Serum fasting insulin (FINS) was analysed using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR) index was calculated using the following formula: fasting blood glucose (mmol/L) × FINS (μU/mL)/22.5 (Burdge et al. 2000).

Oral Glucose Tolerance (OGTT) and Insulin Tolerance Test (ITT)

At week 11, an OGTT and an ITT were performed. For OGTT, rats were fasted overnight and administered a 50% glucose solution (2 g/kg body weight) via oral gavage. Blood glucose was measured via tail vein sampling at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min using a glucometer. The area under the curve (AUC) for glucose response was calculated. For ITT, fasted rats were injected intraperitoneally with recombinant human insulin (0.75 U/kg body weight; Lot F2214635, Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China). Blood glucose was monitored at 0, 30, 60, 90 and 120 min post‐injection.

Free Fatty Acid (FFA) Analysis

To 50 μL of serum sample, 350 μL of chloroform‐methanol solution (CHCl3‐MeOH; 2:1, v/v) was added. The mixture was vortexed for 30 s, followed by the addition of 300 μL of distilled water and a subsequent vortexing for 30 s. The solution was then incubated in an ice bath for 5 min. For adipose tissue samples, 700 μL of the CHCl3‐MeOH (2:1, v/v) was added to 25 μg of tissue and homogenised for 4 min. The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min, after which 450 μL of the upper organic layer was removed. The remaining mixture was centrifuged again at 14,000 rpm for 5 min at room temperature, yielding 160 μL of the lower organic layer of total lipid fraction.

Free fatty acids were fractionated using high‐performance thin‐layer chromatography silica gel plates as previously described (Li et al. 2018). Silica strips containing FFAs were identified, scraped and resuspended in 100 μL of CHCl3‐methanol (2:1, v/v). The suspension was vortexed for 30 s and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for two minutes at ambient temperature to isolate the supernatant. This supernatant was derivatised prior to gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS) analysis. Free fatty acid quantification was performed using a 7890B‐5977B GC–MS instrument (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with an Agilent DB‐23 capillary column (60 m × 0.25 mm × 0.15 μm) following established protocols (Chan et al. 2025).

Faecal SCFA Analysis

To process the faecal samples, they were carefully weighed with a precision of 0.001 g and placed into EP tubes. Each tube received a 1.0% v/v phosphoric acid solution (0.75 mL) along with a steel bead. After thorough vortexing, the mixture underwent ultrasonication on ice for 10 min. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and 4°C for 10 min. A 200 μL portion of the supernatant was transferred to a fresh EP tube, followed by the addition of 500 μL of methyl tert‐butyl ether. The mixture was vortexed and ultrasonicated on ice for an additional 10 min. A second centrifugation was conducted at 12,000 g and 4°C for 10 min, and 200 μL of the resulting supernatant was collected for further analysis.

Metagenomics Analysis

Microbial genomic DNA was extracted from faecal samples using the MagPure Stool DNA Kit (Magen Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Guangzhou, China). The purity and concentration of the extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000c spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Libraries were constructed with the TruePrep Flexible DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme International LLC, Nanjing, China), which included transposase‐mediated fragmentation, adapter ligation and magnetic bead‐based purification. Fragmented DNA was PCR‐amplified using the TruePrep Index Kit V2 for Illumina (Vazyme International LLC, Nanjing, China) to ligate N5 and N7 dual‐index adapters for multiplexed sequencing. The PCR programme consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 3 min, followed by five cycles of 98°C for 30 s, 60°C for 15 s and 72°C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72°C for 5 min and a hold at 4°C. Amplified products were purified twice with VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme International LLC, Nanjing, China) using 0.60× and 0.15× bead ratios. Library quality was verified using a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and quantified using the Qubit 1× dsDNA HS Assay Kit. Paired‐end sequencing was performed using the Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Raw sequencing data were processed using kneaddata v0.10.0 (https://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/kneaddata/) to remove low‐quality reads and host sequences. The resulting data were assembled into contigs using Megahit, and contigs larger than 2000 bp were filtered and binned using MetaBAT2 (Kang et al. 2019), VAMB (Nissen et al. 2021), SemiBin (Pan et al. 2022), MetaDecoder (Liu, Dong, et al. 2022; Liu, Liu, et al. 2022) and Das Tool (Sieber et al. 2018). To assess the quality of these MAGs, we used CheckM software (Parks et al. 2015) and categorised them into high, medium and partial quality based on completeness and contamination. High‐quality MAGs were further clustered to eliminate redundancy, and the dRep tool (parameters: ‐pa 0.95 and ‐sa 0.95) was used to identify the most representative genomes from each replicate. This process ultimately identified species‐level genome bins (SGBs). The relative abundance of each SGB was calculated using CoverM (https://github.com/wwood/CoverM) with the following parameters: –min‐read‐percent‐identity 0.95 –min‐covered‐fraction 0.4. Based on information from published literature and the MetaCyc metabolic database, relevant gut metabolic modules (GMMs) encoded by the SGBs were predicted. Open reading frames were aligned to the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) orthologous database to annotate key metabolic modules for each SGB. The distribution of modules involved in synthesis or degradation within SGB was identified using Omixer‐RPM (parameter: ‐c 0.66).

Transcriptomics Analysis

Transcriptomics analysis profiling was conducted in accordance with established methodologies. In essence, RNA was isolated from hepatic and adipose tissues utilising a Trizol reagent kit (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA), following the manufacturer's protocol. Complementary DNA libraries were synthesised and sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq6000 platform by Guangzhou Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co. Ltd., China. The RNA‐Seq data were filtered, and transcriptome analysis was performed using the R Bioconductor suite and DESeq2 package (Love et al. 2014). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on a significance threshold of p < 0.05 and fold change > 1.5. These DEGs advanced to subsequent investigative stages. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis was conducted using GSEA software (Broad Institute Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), with gene sets compiled from Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways. Enrichment significance was determined using a normalised enrichment score absolute value > 1, p < 0.05 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.25. Protein–protein network analysis using the STRING database (https://string‐db.org/) and data visualisation through the software Cytoscape_v3.9.1.

Statistical Analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean. Statistical significance was assessed using the one‐way ANOVA test for the physiological phenotypic indicators and the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test for the microbiological results, with a threshold of p < 0.05. Graphical representations were generated using R packages (ggplot2 and R heatmap) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA).

Results

Isolation, Identification and Functional Prediction of LPP Strain

The 16s rRNA sequence was determined and subjected to BLAST analysis, revealing a 100% homology with known Lactiplantibacillus plantarum ATCC 14917T (ACGZ01000098) species in the GenBank database. Physiological and biochemical characteristics using VITEK ANC revealed it to be Lactiplantibacillus plantarum, and it was named Lactiplantibacillus plantarum LPPerfectus001 (Table S1).

Whole‐genome sequencing and KEGG annotation of LPP previously conducted revealed that it possesses a rich set of genes involved in carbohydrate and lipid metabolism. Further analysis identified the gene encoding the core enzyme 3‐oxoacyl‐[acyl‐carrier protein] reductase in the SCFAs biosynthesis pathway (FAS II) (Figure S1); SCFAs are key metabolites that the gut microbiome uses to regulate host metabolism.

Therefore, we conducted a preliminary experiment on the effects of LPP on glucose and lipid metabolism in zebrafish embryos; we found that LPP can significantly reduce the glucose level (41%–48%, p < 0.01) and triglyceride level (21%–35%, p < 0.01) of zebrafish embryos under high‐sugar/high‐fat conditions, indicating that it has the potential to regulate glucose and lipid metabolism (Figure S2).

Administering LPP Alleviated HFF Diet‐Induced Obesity Without Affecting Energy Intake in Rat

At the end of the trial in rats, significant differences in body weight gain were observed among the groups. The HFFD group demonstrated a significant increase in cumulative weight gain relative to the CONT group (p < 0.001; Figure 1B–D; Table S2). However, rats administered medium‐ and high‐dose LPP (LPPM and LPPH) exhibited a markedly lower body weight gain compared to the HFFD group (p < 0.05), while the low‐dose group (LPPL) showed a nonsignificant downward trend. However, no significant differences were observed in the LPPL and HFFD groups (Figure 1D; Table S2), suggesting the probiotic effect was dose‐dependent. Notably, the energy intake of the CONT group was significantly lower than that of the HFFD group (p < 0.001). Meanwhile, energy intake remained consistent across all HFFD‐fed groups, indicating that LPP‐mediated weight modulation was independent of appetite suppression (Figure 1E; Table S2).

It is well established that a HFFD is linked to an increased tendency for weight gain and greater adiposity. Body composition analysis via nuclear magnetic resonance Minispec LF90II analyser revealed that HFFD‐fed rats experienced elevated fat mass and fluid retention (p < 0.001), with no significant changes in lean mass compared to the CONT group (Figure 1F–H; Table S2). However, LPPM and LPPH interventions attenuated these effects, significantly reducing fat accumulation and fluid retention (p < 0.01) without affecting the lean mass level (Figure 1F–H; Table S2).

Effects of LPP Intake on Glucolipid Metabolism in HFFD‐Induced Obesity Rats

Obesity induced by a HFFD is strongly associated with systemic insulin resistance and dyslipidaemia. To evaluate the regulatory effects of LPP on glucose metabolism, OGTT and ITT were conducted after Week 11. The OGTT results revealed that medium‐ and high‐dose LPP (LPPM and LPPH) significantly improved glucose tolerance compared to the HFFD group (p < 0.05, p < 0.01), reducing the AUC reductions of the blood glucose curves by 16% and 13%, respectively. No significant improvement was observed in the LPPL group (Figure 2A,B; Table S3). Moreover, fasting blood glucose levels in the HFFD group were higher than in the CONT group (p < 0.01; Figure 2C; Table S3), whereas all LPP‐treated groups normalised fasting blood glucose to near‐control levels (p < 0.01; Figure 2C; Table S3). The ITT results demonstrated that low‐dose LPP administration (LPPL group) reduced the AUC by 22% (p < 0.01), indicating enhanced insulin sensitivity, while LPPM and LPPH groups showed no significant differences compared to the HFFD group (Figure 2D,E; Table S3). There were no significant differences between LPPL, LPPM and LPPH in AUC of OGTT or AUC of ITT (Figure S3). Serum fasting insulin levels and the HOMA‐IR index were significantly elevated in the HFFD group compared to the CONT group (p < 0.01), suggesting an increased insulin resistance due to HFFD. Although high‐dose LPP administration did not alter FINS levels, it reduced HOMA‐IR significantly (p < 0.01), suggesting partial restoration of insulin signalling (Figure 2F,G; Table S3).

Serum lipid analysis revealed that HFFD significantly elevated TG compared to the CONT group (p < 0.001), but TC and LDL‐C showed nonsignificant increased trends (Figure 2H–J; Table S3). Supplementation of LPP effectively reversed these effects, although the LPPL group only showed a nonsignificant TG reduction trend (Figure 2I; Table S3). After LPP intervention, the serum LPS level in the LPPH group was significantly decreased (p < 0.05) compared with that in the HFFD group (Figure 2K; Table S3).

These results demonstrate that LPP ameliorates HFFD‐induced dyslipidaemia and hyperglycaemia in a dose‐dependent manner, with high‐dose LPP exhibiting the greatest therapeutic potential for metabolic disorders management, prompting us to conduct more studies on LPPH.

Administering LPP Reshaped Gut Microbiota Composition in HFFD‐Fed Rats

The gut microbiota was analysed using metagenome sequencing; five faecal samples from each group (CONT, HFFD and LPPH) were analysed. No significant differences in the Simpson's and Shannon diversity indices were observed between groups (Figure 3A,B; Table S4). However, principal coordinate analysis (Bray–Curtis distance) revealed distinct clustering of microbial communities between CONT versus HFFD and LPPH groups (Figure 3C; Table S4), representing diet‐induced variations in their gut microbiota.

At the level of phyla, Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes were the dominant phyla. The HFFD group showed a significant increase in Bacteroidetes and a decrease in Firmicutes compared to the CONT group, a trend that was reversed in the LPPH group (Figure 3D,E; Table S4). While HFFD reduced the faecal Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes ratio, a marker associated with obesity, this was reversed in the LPPH group, though the effect was not significant (Figure 3E; Table S4). Additionally, high‐dose LPP administration (LPPH) increased the abundance of Verrucomicrobia (Figure 3D; Table S4).

Further species‐level linear discriminant analysis effect size analysis revealed that the top 10 enriched species in the LPPH group were Bacteroides massiliensis , Akkermansia (A.) muciniphila, Eubacterium sp_3_1_31, Collinsella tanakaei , Clostridium symbiosum , Enterobacter cloacae , L. plantarum , Helicobacter hepaticus , Holdemania filiformis and Lachnospiraceaebacterium_6_1_63FAA. For the HFFD group, the top five enriched species were Parabacteroides goldsteinii , Ruminococcus torques , Klebsiella pneumoniae , Oligella ureolytica and Corynebacterium glutamicum . In the CONT group, the top five enriched species were Lactobacillus murinus , Lactobacillus johnsonii , Eubacterium plexicaudatum , Bacteroides dorei and Parabacteroides johnsonii respectively (Figure 3F; Table S4).

Of particular interest is the enrichment of the beneficial species, A. muciniphila , a mucin‐degrading bacterium associated with improved metabolic health. Conversely, opportunistic pathogens like Klebsiella pneumoniae and Enterobacter cloacae (Enterobacteriaceae), prevalent in the HFFD group, were suppressed in the LPPH group (Figure 3F,G; Table S4). Predictive functional analysis indicated that, in the LPPH group, genes associated with the pyruvate‐to‐butanoate fermentation pathway, reduced by the HFFD, were significantly restored (p < 0.05; Figure 3H; Table S4), a critical route for microbial butyrate synthesis. This finding aligns with faecal SCFA quantification, where administering high‐dose LPP (LPPH) increased acetate, butyrate and total SCFA levels compared to HFFD (p < 0.05; Figure 3I; Table S4), corroborating the role of butyrate in enhancing gut barrier integrity and insulin sensitivity.

In conclusion, high‐dose LPP intervention counteracted HFFD‐induced dysbiosis by enriching beneficial taxa (such as A. muciniphila ), suppressing opportunistic pathogens and revitalising SCFA biosynthesis.

Administering LPP Modulated Glucolipid Metabolism via Regulating Fatty Acids in iWAT

Lipid metabolism in adipose tissue was systematically evaluated to elucidate the anti‐obesity mechanisms of LPP. Both iWAT and eWAT are types of white adipose tissue, with eWAT being a visceral adipose and iWAT being a subcutaneous adipose. Research has indicated that iWAT has superior regulatory effects on glucose and lipid metabolism compared to eWAT (Foster et al. 2011). The iWAT from LPP‐treated rats exhibited a reduction in adipocyte size and a decrease in lipid accumulation compared to the HFFD group (Figure 4A; Figure S4; Table S5). Histological analysis of both iWAT and eWAT further confirmed that LPP intervention resulted in smaller adipocytes (Figure 4B; Table S5), consistent with enhanced lipolysis and suppressed adipogenesis. Moreover, GC–MS‐based lipidomics revealed that HFFD raised the levels of total FFAs, saturated fatty acids (SFAs), palmitic acid and stearic acid in both serum and adipose tissue both serum and iWAT compared to the CONT group, and such effects were reversed in the LPPH group (Figure 4C–J; Table S5).

Administering LPP Reduced Adipose Tissue Accumulation in HFFD Rats by Regulating PPAR Signalling

After establishing the protective role of LPP in mitigating HFFD‐induced glucolipid dysregulation by attenuating SFA accumulation and promoting adipocyte remodelling, we investigated the transcriptional regulation of lipogenic and lipolytic pathways. Transcriptional profiling of iWAT revealed that HFFD‐induced aberrant gene expression in these pathways (Figure 5A; Table S6). Administering LPP effectively countered the HFFD‐induced dysregulation of lipid metabolic pathways. The KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs identified fatty acid degradation, fatty acid elongation, and PPAR signalling as the top pathways modulated in the LPPH group (Figure 5B; Table S6). Network analysis of the top 100 DEGs using the STRING database highlighted PPARγ (Pparg) as a central hub gene, interacting with lipid oxidation regulators (e.g., Cpt1a, Cpt2) and lipogenic factors (e.g., Scd, Lpl) regulators (Figure 5C; Table S6). Further validation of gene expression confirmed significant decreases in Pparg, Scd and Lpl (p < 0.05), alongside increases in Ppard, Acly, Cpt1a, Eno1, Cpt2 and Acot7 (p < 0.05; Figure 5D; Table S6).

Administering LPP Attenuated HFFD‐Induced Hepatic Steatosis and Modulated Key Metabolic Pathways

Prolonged HFFD consumption induces adipose tissue hypertrophy, triggering ectopic lipid deposition in the liver through enhanced lipogenesis (Morigny et al. 2021). Our results showed that, compared to the CONT group, HFFD‐fed rats developed hepatic steatosis characterised by enlarged liver volume and abundant lipid vacuoles in hematoxylin and eosin‐stained tissue sections, which were attenuated by high‐dose LPP intervention (Figure 6A; Figure S5; Table S7). RNA sequencing demonstrated that LPPH reshaped hepatic transcriptomic profiles, with KEGG enrichment analysis identifying phosphoinositide 3‐kinase (PI3K)‐protein kinase B (Akt) signalling, PPAR signalling and Extracellular Matrix (ECM)‐receptor interaction as key modulated pathways (Figure 6B; Table S7). Gene set enrichment analysis further revealed significant downregulation of glucose metabolism pathways involving Forkhead box O (FoxO) proteins, AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) signalling and PI3K‐Akt signalling. Additionally, autophagy‐related pathways (lysosome, autophagy‐other and autophagy‐animal) were similarly downregulated. Notably, the PI3K‐Akt pathway exhibited a pronounced shift in the LPPH group compared to HFFD (NES = −1.5, p < 0.01), suggesting its central role in LPPH‐mediated improvement of hepatic insulin resistance (Figure 6C–E; Table S7). Moreover, cell activation pathways (Hedgehog signalling, ECM‐receptor interaction, focal adhesion and Wnt signalling) were suppressed in HFFD‐fed rats but restored by LPPH intervention (Figure 6C–E; Table S7). This regulatory reversal was further supported by corresponding expression changes in key metabolic genes (Irs2, Cyp4a2, Pck1; Figure 6F; Table S7).

Correlation Network Analysis Reveals LPP Modulated Gut Microbiota‐SCFA‐Metabolism Axis to Improve Metabolic Parameters

To investigate the mechanistic link between LPP‐induced gut microbiota changes and metabolic improvements, we performed comprehensive correlation analyses (Figure 7A; Table S8). Our results demonstrated significant negative associations between SCFAs (particularly acetic acid and butyric acid) and key metabolic disorder markers, including OGTT, Uric Acid (UA), LPS, fluid retention and fat mass. Notably, acetic acid was also significantly negatively correlated with HOMA‐IR (r < −0.5, p < 0.05). Further integration of lipid transcriptome and serum metabolite data revealed that circulating FFAs and SFAs were negatively correlated with the expression of fatty acid oxidation genes (Ppard, Eno1, Cpt2, Acot7; r < −0.5, p < 0.05), but positively correlated with adipogenic Pparg (r > 0.5, p < 0.05; Figure 7B; Table S8). Notably, specific beneficial bacteria showed distinct metabolic correlations: A. muciniphila exhibited a positive association with lean mass (r > 0.5, p < 0.05; Figure 7C; Table S8), while L. plantarum was inversely associated with LPS, TC and LDL‐C (Figure 7D; Table S8).

Discussion

Obesity is a complex disease influenced by genetic, environmental and behavioural factors, posing a global health challenge with limited therapeutic options (Ziętak et al. 2016; Hall and Guo 2017). Conventional interventions, such as pharmacotherapy or bariatric surgery, often entail adverse effects, highlighting the need for safer alternatives (Yanovski and Yanovski 2014; Bodeker and Kronenberg 2015). Probiotics, particularly L. plantarum strains, show promise in modulating host metabolism, though their effects are strain‐specific. In this study, the LPP strain was identified by 16S rRNA sequencing and VITEK ANC test, and its potential to regulate glucose and lipid metabolism was predicted by a zebrafish model. Then the study investigates the dose‐dependent protective effects of the novel probiotic strain LPP in HDDF‐induced obesity in rats. Our results indicate that LPP supplementation alleviates body weight gain, insulin resistance and dyslipidaemia without affecting energy intake, which has unique advantages compared with traditional weight loss therapies that rely on caloric restriction.

The conduct and reporting of this study adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Microbiome Therapeutics Across Medical Disciplines (PRIM) Guidelines: An International Delphi Consensus (J Gastroenterol Hepatol. 2025), including transparent reporting of key aspects such as experimental design, data analysis and clinical relevance. By adopting the risk prevention measures recommended by the PRIM guidelines, we strive to enhance the reproducibility of our study and provide a more reliable basis for the clinical translation of microbiome‐based therapeutics.

The gut microbiota is integral to intestinal homeostasis and systemic metabolic health (Shelton et al. 2023). Growing evidence underscores its role in the pathogenesis of obesity, type 2 diabetes, non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease and cardiovascular disease (Aron‐Wisnewsky et al. 2021; Takeuchi et al. 2023), with the Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes ratio emerging as a hallmark of dysbiosis linked to enhanced energy harvest and adiposity. In our study, high‐dose LPP effectively restored the HFFD‐disrupted Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes ratio, aligning with prior reports on probiotic interventions (Baek et al. 2023; He et al. 2023). Chronic HFFD consumption is known to compromise gut barrier integrity, elevating circulating LPS and triggering metabolic endotoxaemia, chronic inflammation and insulin resistance (Blasco et al. 2017). Our findings corroborate this paradigm, revealing HFFD‐induced enrichment of pro‐inflammatory taxa such as Ruminococcus torques (linked to inflammatory bowel disease, metabolic disorders and obesity (Vieira‐Silva et al. 2020, Cani et al. 2022, Ding et al. 2023)) and opportunistic pathogens ( Klebsiella pneumoniae and Oligella ureolytica (Depommier et al. 2019)) at the species level and Enterobacteriaceae at the family level. These microbial shifts likely contribute to the pro‐inflammatory milieu and metabolic disorders observed in obesity.

The enrichment of Ruminococcus torques , Klebsiella pneumoniae and Enterobacteriaceae in HFFD‐fed rats is of particular concern, as these taxa are established producers of pro‐inflammatory metabolites such as circulating LPS (Plovier et al. 2017; Farfour et al. 2024). Their association with intestinal inflammation and metabolic dysfunction (Jiang et al. 2022) underscores the significance of our finding that high‐dose LPP administration markedly suppressed these pathogenic bacteria, potentially contributing to its anti‐obesity and insulin‐sensitising effects. The core of these benefits lies in the significant enrichment of A. muciniphila in the LPPH group. This mucin‐degrading bacterium has emerged as a keystone species in metabolic health, with demonstrated roles in enhancing gut barrier function, mitigating inflammation and improving insulin sensitivity (Wong Fok Lung et al. 2022). Notably, its increased abundance coincided with a rise in Verrucomicrobia at the phylum level, reinforcing the gut‐adipose‐liver axis as a therapeutic target for obesity (Hersoug et al. 2018). In addition to enriching A. muciniphila , LPPH administration significantly increased the abundance of other metabolically beneficial taxa, including Bacteroides massiliensis , Lachnospiraceae bacterium_6_1_63FAA, Eubacterium_sp_3_1_31 and L. plantarum . Notably, Bacteroides massiliensis and Lachnospiraceae, both of which are typically depleted in obese individuals, were promoted (Ashrafian et al. 2021). The L. plantarum species also showed marked expansion, consistent with its established role in reducing LPS and TC in a hypercholesterolemia mouse model (de Vos et al. 2022). Furthermore, LPPH promoted populations of Eubacterium spp., a group renowned for their ability to produce SCFAs (Huang et al. 2013; Salazar‐Jaramillo et al. 2024), reinforcing the critical link between microbial composition and host metabolic regulation.

Functional metagenomic analysis revealed that the high‐dose LPP administration enhanced pyruvate‐to‐butanoate fermentation, a critical pathway for butyrate synthesis. This aligns with our observation of elevated faecal acetate, butyrate and total SCFAs in the LPPH group. Given that SCFAs improve glucose tolerance (as seen in our OGTT results), strengthen the gut barrier, attenuate systemic inflammation and alleviate type 2 diabetes (Deng et al. 2022), their increased production likely underlies the metabolic benefits of administering the high‐dose LPP. The significant inverse correlations observed between faecal SCFAs (particularly acetic acid and butyrate) and key metabolic markers, including fasting blood glucose, TC, HDL‐C and LDL‐C, provide compelling evidence for this mechanistic pathway. These findings align with previous clinical observations in type 2 diabetes patients (Ma et al. 2020) and are further corroborated by our current results in the HFFD rat model, strengthening the translational relevance of SCFA‐mediated metabolic regulation.

Our study demonstrates that LPP exerts multifaceted anti‐obesity effects through adipose tissue remodelling and metabolic pathway modulation. In the HFFD‐induced obesity model, LPP intervention effectively attenuated pathological adipose tissue expansion, reducing adipocyte hypertrophy and hepatic lipid accumulation while decreasing circulating FFAs, a major contributor to ectopic fat deposition, aligning with a previous study demonstrating promising probiotic effects in reducing the sizes of liver adipocytes and adipose tissue, as well as hepatic lipid droplet accumulation (Rekha et al. 2024). Moreover, FFAs from adipose tissue are strongly associated with enhanced lipolysis, particularly increased TG hydrolysis (Liu, Dong, et al. 2022; Liu, Liu, et al. 2022). In obese patients with non‐alcoholic fatty liver disease, there was greater lipolysis in adipose tissue, which may account for 60%–70% of the fat that accumulates in the liver (Kang et al. 2023). These morphological improvements were accompanied by significant transcriptional reprogramming in adipose tissue, reflecting a metabolic shift from lipid accumulation to oxidative utilisation. Specifically, key lipogenic regulators were downregulated, including Pparg (PPARγ), which is the master transcriptional regulator of adipocyte differentiation and lipid storage, Scd (stearoyl‐CoA desaturase), which catalyses monounsaturated fatty acid synthesis, and Ldl/Vldlr, which mediate lipoprotein uptake. Concurrently, genes driving fatty acid oxidation were upregulated, such as Ppard (PPARδ), a key activator of fatty acid catabolic pathways, Acly (ATP‐citrate lyase), which generates acetyl‐CoA for both lipogenesis and oxidative metabolism depending on the metabolic context, and the critical mitochondrial fatty acid transporters Cpt1a and Cpt2 (carnitine palmitoyltransferases), which control the rate‐limiting step of β‐oxidation (Lee et al. 2024). This coordinated gene expression profile strongly suggests that LPP promotes a fundamental metabolic reprogramming in adipose tissue.

The administration of LPP systemically improved glucose homeostasis through coordinated modulation of three key interconnected pathways governing insulin sensitivity and energy metabolism (Barbieri et al. 2023). First, the PI3K/Akt pathway, a central mediator of insulin signalling, was enhanced, promoting GLUT4 translocation to the plasma membrane in muscle and adipose tissues (Amorim et al. 2022), enhancing insulin sensitivity, thereby facilitating insulin‐stimulated glucose uptake (Schultze et al. 2012). Second, AMPK, a serine/threonine protein kinase, acts as a cellular energy sensor, complementing this effect by independently stimulating GLUT4 translocation (Ueda et al. 2013; Chao et al. 2016; Fan et al. 2023) while restoring metabolic balance through its conserved role in regulating both glucose and lipid metabolism (Li et al. 2014). Third, LPP intervention suppressed FoxO proteins, mirroring the physiological feeding response observed previously, where FoxO1 phosphorylation leads to nuclear exclusion and downregulation of genes encoding gluconeogenesis‐related enzymes, including glucose‐6‐phosphatase and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase, as well as the transcriptional coactivator PPARγ coactivator‐1α in C57BL/6 mice (Ueda‐Wakagi et al. 2018). This tripartite mechanism, simultaneously enhancing peripheral glucose utilisation via PI3K/Akt and AMPK while inhibiting hepatic glucose production through FoxO regulation, demonstrates the multifaceted action of LPP in restoring metabolic homeostasis. The convergence of these pathways on GLUT4 trafficking and hepatic gluconeogenesis provides a unified explanation for the observed improvements in glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity. Notably, our findings provide evidence of the dual action of LPP on both PPAR signalling and PI3K‐Akt pathways, offering novel mechanistic insights into its anti‐diabetic effects that parallel known pharmacological agents like metformin (Hughey et al. 2023). These results significantly advance our understanding of dietary interventions for metabolic disorders by elucidating specific microbiota‐adipose‐liver axis interactions that underlie the therapeutic potential of LPP against obesity and insulin resistance.

While our findings demonstrate the beneficial effects of LPP on obesity and metabolic disorders, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the differential effects observed between the OGTT and ITT for different doses of LPP may be attributed to distinct metabolic pathways in various tissues, such as the AMPK‐GLUT4 pathway in muscle and the PI3K‐Akt pathway in the liver; further research is needed to explore these mechanisms. Second, the unexpected dominance of lactic acid bacteria in the faecal microbiota of the CONT group, contrary to previous studies (Aoyama et al. 2006), may be related to the influence of genetic and breeding background variability on baseline microbiome composition. This finding underscores the need for future studies to account for such biological variability when designing probiotic interventions. Third, while our transcriptome analyses identified key pathways (PPAR signalling, PI3K‐Akt) potentially mediating the probiotic effects of LPP, the lack of protein‐level validation (such as confirmation by western blot) due to insufficient sample amounts represents an important technical limitation. The LDA analysis showed that LPP intervention enriched A. muciniphila in the gut microbiota. However, it remains unclear whether this enrichment was due to LPP metabolites or changes in the mucus layer. Future studies should include faecal microbiota transplantation experiments and in vitro co‐culture experiments with LPP and A. muciniphila to further investigate this. Finally, the sample size constraints (n = 5 per group for metagenome and transcriptome analyses) imposed by funding limitations reduced our statistical power to perform comprehensive correlation analyses across parameters. These limitations notwithstanding, our findings provide compelling preliminary evidence for the anti‐obesity effects of LPP while highlighting critical areas for future research, including: (1) mechanistic studies to resolve the OGTT/ITT discrepancy, (2) larger‐scale validation incorporating protein‐level analyses and (3) multi‐omics integration to fully elucidate the gut‐adipose‐liver axis underlying the therapeutic potential of LPP.

Conclusion

The LPP strain mitigates HFFD‐induced obesity and metabolic disorders by reshaping gut microbiota to enrich the mucin‐degrading taxa (e.g., A. muciniphila ), increasing microbial metabolites (e.g., butyrate), activating adipose Pparg/Cpt1a driven lipid oxidation and restoring hepatic PI3K‐AKT signalling to enhance insulin sensitivity. These findings underscore the capacity of LPP to rebalance energy partitioning and suppress adiposity without energy restriction and highlight probiotics as holistic modulators of the gut‐liver axis, offering novel strategies for combating metabolic disorders.
