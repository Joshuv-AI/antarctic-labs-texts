Exploration of implementation determinants and strategies for same-day oral PrEP in community-based organizations and federally qualified health centers
Authors: Samantha V. Hill, Portia Thomas, Mariel Parman, Jeannette Webb, LaRon Nelson, Michael Mugavero, Russell A. Brewer, Latesha Elopre, Larry Herald
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) remains underutilized in communities over-burdened by HIV. Same-day PrEP, prescribing and starting PrEP at the initial visit, may be an implementation strategy to address this gap. Federally qualified health centers (FQHC) and community-based organizations (CBO) provide healthcare to un- and under-insured populations and have the potential to increase PrEP services via same-day PrEP. This exploratory mixed methods study explored same-day PrEP program implementation strategies and determinants.MethodsKey stakeholders, recruited from FQHC and CBO in Georgia, Texas, and Illinois, participated in virtual interviews (qualitative strand) grounded in the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Science. Thematic analysis in NVivo identified implementation strategies. Purposively sampled FQHC and CBO stakeholder focus groups (FG) rank-ordered same-day PrEP implementation strategies (quantitative strand) based on perceived effectiveness and feasibility to create meta-inferences. N = 5 individuals participated in both interviews and FG. We then calculated the mean rank order score for each implementation strategy (range = 1–12), within each state and across all three states. We calculated these mean scores separately for both perceived strategy effectiveness/impact and perceived feasibility within their respective settings.ResultsTwenty-four stakeholders completed interviews. 46% (N = 11) were clinic directors/managers, 63% (N = 15) were affiliated with a CBO, 71% (N = 17) worked in settings where same-day oral PrEP was offered. Theme 1) Medicaid expansion is a useful resource for same-day PrEP implementation; however, same-day PrEP is feasible in non-Medicaid expansion states by leveraging additional financial resources. Theme 2) Leadership buy-in and PrEP champions spearhead programs. Theme 3) Intercommunity relationships and formal evaluation are needed. The three most highly ranked strategies in terms of perceived effectiveness were: 1. Leadership buy-in (mean ranking = 2.51); 2. PrEP champion (mean ranking = 3.62); and 3. PrEP navigators (mean ranking = 4.68). Leadership buy-in first (mean ranking = 2.91), followed by the use of a PrEP champion second (mean ranking = 3.91) and consumer outreach (mean ranking = 4.81) were ranked highest in terms of perceived feasibility.ConclusionsDiversification of funding, support from leaders, and customization of implementation strategies are consistent factors necessary for same-day PrEP programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s43058-025-00787-w.

Background

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s (CDC) 2021 updated HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) guidelines recommend discussion of PrEP with all sexually active adolescents and young adults [1]. PrEP modalities continue to expand with two pill and two injectable options currently available, with more options in the pipeline. Yet PrEP uptake remains suboptimal, with observed disparities mirroring disparities seen in HIV incidence [2]. Specifically, people of color, transgender and heterosexual women of color, emerging adults (14 to 24 years of age), and populations living within the United States (US) Deep South are disproportionately impacted by HIV and are least likely to use PrEP [2]. With the CDC’s goal to increase PrEP coverage to 50% among individuals with PrEP indications [3], more strategies are needed to ensure populations most in need of PrEP can access PrEP services.

Successful PrEP implementation is hindered by multi-level barriers. Organizational barriers include the absence of supportive leadership and funding sources for clinic visits, medications, labs, and transportation. Community barriers include the surrounding community climate including the need for welcoming services for sexual and gender diverse populations, people who inject drugs, cis and trans women, and youth, and community specific resources addressing negative social determinants of health [4]. Clinical barriers include inefficient clinic workflows and inappropriate numbers of, limited skill sets among, and diversity of staff including a lack of a) non-prescribing providers (e.g. patient navigators), b) staff with expertise in insurance reimbursement and navigating patient assistance programs, and c) multilingual staff representative of the communities served [5]. Intrapersonal and interpersonal barriers include HIV and PrEP stigma, homophobia, and low perceived HIV risk; all of which can occur among clients and providers alike [4]. Structural barriers are less studied but include access to health insurance and racism [4].

Same-day or rapid PrEP, the prescribing and initiation of PrEP during an initial PrEP visit (even while waiting for an HIV test result), has been hypothesized to be an effective strategy to address current health disparities. Same-day PrEP differs from traditional PrEP starts. The traditional method involves evaluating clients and having them return at a later date to start PrEP after the HIV test result is available [6]. Same-day PrEP prescriptions involve evaluating and prescribing PrEP to a client on the same day, but asking them to wait until their HIV test results to start PrEP.) [6]. Same-day PrEP starts (henceforth referred to as same-day PrEP) have the potential to reduce the attrition seen in usual care between initial evaluation and receipt of a PrEP prescription [7, 8]. Same-day PrEP is appropriate for most clients regardless of gender or sexual behaviors unless they have symptoms of acute HIV, cannot haven an HIV test collected, or are unable to provide contact information [1]. While there are no formal trials comparing same-day PrEP to standard prescribing practices of PrEP. The CDC provides guidance on how to safely initiate same-day PrEP [1]. And, it is well aligned with the HIV prevention pillar of the Ending the HIV Epidemic (EHE) [3]. Additionally, rapid antiretroviral initiation, a similar strategy, has been successful at decreasing time to viral suppression and increasing linkage to care for individuals with HIV [9].

While same-day PrEP is a promising implementation strategy for addressing PrEP disparities, it is not an option for everyone. One important group who may not have access to same-day PrEP are individuals who are un- or underinsured as explicitly stated in the CDC PrEP recommendations [1]. Access to insurance varies throughout the US. Private insurance and Medicaid provide coverage for inpatient and outpatient services including certain physician fees, facility fees, prescription medications, and lab tests [9]. Medicaid provides insurance coverage for low-income individuals. Medicaid, in alignment with PrEP’s Grade A recommendation by the United States Preventative Services Task Force, covers generic PrEP, the outpatient visit, labs, and provider fees with no cost-sharing in certain circumstances [10]. Additionally, Medicaid can provide transportation to medical visits [11]. In 2010, legislation that provided individual states with the authority to expand access to Medicaid to adults up to 138% of the federal poverty level was passed [12]. As of 2024, 10 states have not expanded Medicaid, with 70% percent of those states being in the Southern US, the region with the greatest PrEP unmet need [13–15]. Additionally, the Southern US has the greatest rates of uninsured individuals [16], with rates 20% higher compared to other regions in the US where Medicaid access was expanded [15].

Individuals without insurance often access care at community-based organizations (CBO) and federally qualified health centers (FQHC), organizations that through offering same-day PrEP could decrease health inequities. Even though CBOs sometimes offer health services, they more often perform community outreach activities, HIV and sexually transmitted infection (STI) testing, and refer clients to PrEP services [17]. FQHCs are community primary care clinics serving medically underserved communities regardless of the individual’s ability to pay and are more likely to prescribe PrEP to clients [18]. Both organizations are critical to reducing disparities seen in PrEP uptake and adherence, as they serve populations most impacted by the HIV epidemic.

Improving glaring health inequities with HIV prevention will require increasing access to PrEP and same-day PrEP services. CBOs and FQHCs are known providers of PrEP services, as an estimated 19% of FQHCs in 11 of the US largest metropolitan areas and 26% of CBOs offer PrEP services; available literature on same-day PrEP in these settings is lacking [19, 20]. Furthermore, much of the literature focuses on community pharmacies, telehealth, and emergency department same-day PrEP services [21]. Improved knowledge of how CBOs and FQHCs operationalize their services may allow for greater application of same-day PrEP. Furthermore, to our knowledge, there is no literature on how expansion of Medicaid impacts CBOs’ and FQHCs’ abilities to provide same-day PrEP services to the most vulnerable populations. This study explores key determinants of implementing same-day PrEP in CBOs and FQHCs in EHE prioritized jurisdictions with (Illinois (IL)) and without (Alabama (AL) and Texas (TX)) Medicaid expansion. It also identifies and prioritizes strategies to support same-day PrEP implementation.

Methods

This was a pre-implementation exploratory, QUALITATIVE (QUAL)-quantitative (quant) sequential mixed methods study grounded in the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR). CFIR provides a multi-level systematic assessment of contextual factors involved in the implementation of same-day PrEP that can be organized into the development of implementation strategies [21]. It focuses on five constructs- innovation (specific characteristics of the intervention), outer-setting (community, state, national setting in which the intervention is housed), inner-setting (specific setting where the intervention is implemented), individual setting (characteristics of the intervention recipients and deliverers), and process-level (activities used to implement the intervention)- to assess barriers and facilitators to implementing an intervention [22]. We used a two phased approach to identify and prioritize same-day PrEP implementation strategies within CBOs and FQHCs.

Phase 1: explore key determinants and identify potential implementation strategies

Using purposive sampling, we recruited participants from CBOs and FQHCs in Alabama, Texas, and Illinois. In Alabama, participants were recruited from seven CBOs and two FQHCs. In Texas, participants were recruited from two CBOs and one FQHC, while in Illinois participants were recruited from five CBOs and three FQHCs. Participants were recruited via e-mail solicitation from each site’s study champion. Site study champions were self-identified staff who were leading PrEP services and championing this study and included prescribing providers, administrative staff, and pharmacists.

Key informant interviews based on the following CFIR major domains- intervention characteristics, individual-setting, inner-setting, outer-setting, and process-level- were conducted to identify barriers and facilitators to same-day PrEP implementation [23]. Eligibility for key-informant interviews included: 1) providers, practice managers, medical assistants, nurses, and pharmacists, 2) work at CBOs and FQHCs, 3) work in either Alabama, Texas, or Illinois, 4) work in settings that offer HIV/STI testing, and 5) settings provide either PrEP referral or PrEP care. Interview guides consisted of study-team developed, open-ended questions that reflected each CFIR domain (Table 1) and were conducted by one member of the study team (MP). The interviewer was a research coordinator, with no clinical experience in the subject area, with an MPH, with no prior relationship with participants. She has training and significant prior experience conducting interviews. All consented individuals completed a 60-min interview via Zoom. Interviews were professionally transcribed by Landmark Transcription, deidentified, organized, analyzed in NVivo (QRS International, Melbourne, Australia), and conducted until thematic saturation [24] was achieved. Participants were compensated $50. Thematic analysis used deductive and inductive coding. Two coders (SVH, PT) coded a subset of transcripts, compared codes, and refined code definitions. Initial findings and preliminary themes were discussed every other week with the entire team during analysis meetings. After discrepancies were resolved, a final codebook was created. Additional File 1 illustrates the key informant interview codebook. Kappa was 97.3%. After major and minor themes were identified, the research team met (SH, PT, MP JW, LN RB, LE, LH) and reviewed study findings. Using thematic coding, we identified strategies used by CBOs and FQHCs and categorized them based on implementation strategies identified in the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) project [10]. Implementation strategies were then mapped back to CFIR. The most commonly mentioned CFIR domains and constructs and the domains and constructs generally agreed to be the most important for same-day PrEP were reported on in this study.

Phase 2: prioritize implementation strategies

The objective of Phase 2 was member checking and prioritizing the implementation strategies identified in Phase 1 based on stakeholder input provided via three focus groups (FG). Texas and Illinois participants were recruited via email; Alabama participants were stakeholders present for the HIV Prevention and Care Planning Group quarterly meeting. Purposive sampling was used to recruit all participants. Participant inclusion and exclusion criteria were the same as for interviews. Participants from Phase 1 were eligible for participation. (There was no participant overlap in the Texas FG, while there were 2 people from Alabama and 3 people from Illinois who participated in both phases).

We conducted three FGs during April–May 2024. (See Additional File 2 for FG guide.) The Alabama FG was conducted in-person. Texas and Illinois FGs were conducted virtually, via Zoom. FGs lasted approximately 60–90 min in duration and ranged from 6 to 24 participants. (For the FG with 24 participants, we had breakout groups with 4–5 people to facilitate more discussions.) Following an overview of the study purpose, participants were presented with findings from Phase 1, discussed Phase 1 findings, were shown the top 12 implementation strategies identified from Phase 1 (see Results below), asked to add, eliminate, or combine strategies, and then surveyed during the FG to rank order the perceived effectiveness and feasibility of these strategies (Fig. 1). Each participant provided a ranking for each implementation strategy regarding its potential impact on the adoption of same-day PrEP (1 = most effective, 12 = least effective) and their organization’s ability to deploy the implementation strategy (1 = most feasible, 12 = least feasible). We then calculated the mean rank order score for each implementation strategy (range = 1–12), within each state and across all three states. We calculated these mean scores separately for both perceived strategy effectiveness/impact and perceived feasibility of implementation within their respective settings.Fig. 1Questions to key stakeholders to prioritize implementation strategies

Mixed methods analysis

As a QUAL-quant sequential mixed methods study, the results from Phase 1 were used as inputs for Phase 2. Specifically, the implementation strategies identified from semi-structured interviews in Phase 1 were further refined and then rank-ordered via surveying FG in Phase 2. We integrated the results of both Phase 1 (QUAL) and Phase 2 (quant) in the form of a list of implementation strategies that could be distinguished in terms of their perceived effectiveness at promoting same-day PrEP and perceived feasibility of being deployed by CBO and FQHC. We also combined Phase 1 and Phase 2 results to identify complementary implementation strategies.

Results

Phase 1 results

Twenty-four stakeholders completed interviews. Forty-six percent (N = 11) identified as clinic directors/managers, 67% (N = 16) were in non-Medicaid expansion states, 63% (N = 15) were affiliated with a CBO (Table 2). Seventy-one percent (N = 17) worked in settings where same-day oral PrEP was offered, including 46% (N = 11) in non-Medicaid expansion states. Major CFIR aligned themes included: 1) Medicaid expansion is a useful resource for same-day PrEP implementation; however, same-day PrEP is feasible in non-Medicaid expansion states by leveraging additional financial resources (Inner Setting: Readiness for Implementation), 2) Leadership buy-in and PrEP champions help spearhead the programs (Inner Setting: Implementation Climate), 3) Optimization and adaptation of clinic workflows facilitate same-day PrEP (Inner Setting: Readiness for Implementation), and 4) additional factors impacting successful implementation of same-day PrEP included the importance of intercommunity relationships and culture (Outer Setting: Cosmopolitanism and External Policies) and how formal evaluation of same-day PrEP programs is lacking (Process: Reflecting and Evaluation). Medicaid expansion is a useful resource for same-day PrEP implementation; however, same-day PrEP is feasible in non-Medicaid expansion states by leveraging additional financial resources (Inner Setting: Readiness for Implementation).Participants from Medicaid expansion states reported that Medicaid expansion made a great difference in their ability to provide same-day PrEP to clients by removing financial barriers that often made PrEP navigation challenging and by increasing opportunities to care for under resourced clients. Affordable medications and transportation to visits were two of the most common benefits to Medicaid expansion. [Medicaid expansion] absolutely been a facilitator for my patients receiving PrEP services. I would say a majority of my patients are Medicaid recipients or enrollees, so it's vital to have Medicaid be able to cover this for my patients. If Medicaid did not, then I would think I'd run into a lot more issues with getting coverage, and I think it would be a lot more challenging for the PrEP navigators to navigate PrEP access for them. (FQHC, IL, Medicaid expansion state) When we expanded Medicaid, like anybody can get on Medicaid if they make under a certain amount, and it’s just been so helpful. I’ve worked in states where they haven’t, and it’s been such a barrier. (CBO, IL, Medicaid expansion state) Participants from non-Medicaid expansion states also felt Medicaid expansion would greatly facilitate the ease of getting individuals on PrEP as articulated by the following participant: I think that would be a large boon to it [same-day PrEP prescriptions]. At that point, a lot of our patients who are currently uninsured would qualify for Medicaid expansion, and I think we'd see a lot more people coming in, because well, I've been uninsured, and poor at the same time. (CBO, AL, non-Medicaid expansion state, offers same-day PrEP) However, these same participants felt diversification of funding sources was more important for the provision of same-day PrEP. Both expansion and non-Medicaid expansion states identified other financial resources that are crucial to same-day PrEP provision, including federal grants (e.g. Ending the HIV Epidemic grants and Ryan White (RW) funded clinics), discounted drug pricing programs, and capitalizing on free medication samples. Two participants from non-Medicaid expansion states emphasized discounted drug pricing programs that facilitate free or discounted prices for PrEP, as an important resource. These drug pricing programs create revenue organizations can use to cover costs of labs, salaries, or even individuals contracted to serve as patient navigators. Our LabCorp bill, 100 percent comes out of [a discounted drug program] revenue right now, and if suddenly we didn't have that tomorrow, we would probably have to cease a lot of our services for a little while as we figure out another way to pay for them. (CBO, AL, non-Medicaid expansion state) I think that being able to have the ability to purchase the medications and have medications available through our [discounted drug pricing program], I think that has simplified things so much. If they had to go to a pharmacy, if we had to do a patient-assistance program for every single patient that doesn't have insurance, if we had to do PAs [prior authorizations] and overrides for every single patient, all that would be, I think, so obtrusive as to possibly keep many people from taking the medication. (CBO, AL, non-Medicaid expansion state) Pharmaceutical companies were also a reported asset, as they offered a limited number of starter packets at no-cost for clients which greatly facilitated clients starting same-day PrEP. We've got a really good relationship with our [TAF-FTC pharmaceutical] rep, and she keeps us stocked with those packs, … I wish they'd bring back sample packs of [generic PrEP], because that will help us cover everybody else. (CBO, AL, non-Medicaid expansion state) State and organizational grant funding were also mentioned as key funding sources for same-day PrEP programs. Right now we do receive an Ending the Epidemic CDC grant, and that is actually what we primarily use to fund our clinic. That grant covers the salaries for our coordinator, our prevention specialist, and our nurse practitioner, who all work in the clinic providing PrEP services. Right now that funding is one of the primary ways that allows us to provide our services at no cost. (CBO, AL, non-Medicaid expansion state) Additionally, clinic staff engaged with drug companies’ representatives to understand and advocate for the companies’ co-pay assistance programs, allowing patients to access the medication at little or no cost for those who financially qualified. Organizations coupled these programs with patient navigators and other staff to facilitate clients receiving same-day PrEP.Leadership buy-in and PrEP champions help spearhead the programs (Inner Setting: Implementation Climate).Additional key factors for implementing same-day PrEP pertained to the structure and culture of the organization’s inner setting, beginning with the implementation climate. Participants emphasized the importance of support from the organization’s leadership. The outward support from leadership was essential in the success of same-day PrEP programs, as it allowed the program to be developed intentionally and intuitively with buy-in from key personnel. I think you definitely have to have supportive leadership. … You have to have these gatekeepers, these champions, to be able to implement these programs. (FQHC, IL, Medicaid expansion state) Institutions with strong leadership championing their programs were able to strategically utilize and adapt their current infrastructures and processes to integrate same-day PrEP seamlessly. We built this, as I mentioned, it was built with the idea that we were always gonna do same-day PrEP, and so that idea came from leadership down, and we recruited people as we were building the clinic, then we're on board with that idea. (CBO, AL, non-Medicaid expansion state) Optimization and adaptation of clinic workflows facilitates same-day PrEP (Inner Setting: Readiness for Implementation).Another necessary component of same-day PrEP readiness pertained to how these facilities adapted pre-existing organizational infrastructure like personnel, departments, resources, and processes to develop same-day PrEP workflows. Workflows from HIV clinics, STI clinics, and onsite pharmacies were adapted to provide same-day PrEP services. What we have here is we do a free STI testing, pregnancy testing, morning after pill, so people know us as a place they can come and get tested for free and get treated for free for their STIs. What we did was we took this concept and then came up with the idea that we were gonna offer every single person who comes in for PrEP, and we were gonna offer them same-day PrEP with the thought that if they think they're at risk to get HIV, then obviously, they need PrEP. …so they actually have to opt out of same-day PrEP. (CBO, AL, Non-Medicaid expansion state) On-site pharmacies had experience and existing protocols in place to process insurance claims and complete follow-up calls which removed barriers clients would otherwise face when filling same-day prescriptions. You know what clinics are near you, and so but then we had an inhouse pharmacy since about 2019 or 2020, so they're in the downstairs of our clinic. Our patients can either go straight downstairs or if our patients, I feel, need the additional benefit or help, we can often have a navigator come bring up the medication to the clinic. Sometimes I just bring the medication up to the clinic to just again, to make it as easy as possible for the patients to get the medications they need. (FQHC, IL, Medicaid expansion state) Once they started having the in-house pharmacy, it made it easy and seamless. (CBO, TX, non- Medicaid expansion state)Additionally, both non-Medicaid and Medicaid expansion states shared how personnel working in STI and HIV clinics were cross trained to work with PrEP protocols as well. Participants reported integration of current STI and HIV clinic protocols facilitated follow-up with same-day PrEP clients’ HIV results as these types of follow-up calls were already standard procedure. Furthermore, some organizations used prior COVID-19 telehealth protocols to facilitate same-day PrEP services, especially for clients in rural areas or lacking adequate and consistent transportation options. Participants specifically emphasized the importance of using case managers and PrEP navigators who have prior experiences in other clinical settings. These staff members provided knowledge with navigating the healthcare setting and insurance approval for same-day PrEP prescriptions. I feel that insurance is a primary barrier to people receiving PrEP services. The way, at my agency, that we get around that is we have a really robust ancillary support staff, PrEP navigators, that can help navigate the insurance system for these patients, which is extremely complicated and complex. Without those support staff or without the pharmacy staff that I work with who are familiar with PrEP coverage, I do not think I would be able to initiate same-day PrEP on a lot of folks. (FQHC, TX non-Medicaid expansion state) Then we have specific case managers that are dedicated to getting the [patient assistance program application] PAP done for those who are uninsured at that time. Phase 2 results

Aggregated implementation strategy rankings

On average across the three sites, the three most highly ranked strategies in terms of perceived effectiveness were: 1. Leadership buy-in (mean ranking = 2.51), 2. PrEP champion (mean ranking = 3.62), and 3. PrEP navigators (mean ranking = 4.68; Table 3). Participants also ranked leadership buy-in first (mean ranking = 2.91) and the use of a PrEP champion second (mean ranking = 3.91) in terms of their perceived feasibility of being applied in their organization, followed by consumer outreach (mean ranking = 4.81). The least effective same-day PrEP implementation strategies were: 1. Co-locating clinics within RW clinics to share personnel, protocols, and equipment (mean ranking = 8.38), 2. Telehealth (mean ranking = 8.35), and 3. Mobile units (mean ranking = 7.97). Likewise, participants ranked telehealth as least feasible as a same-day PrEP implementation strategy (mean ranking = 8.66), followed by mobile units (mean ranking = 7.88). Participants ranked the use of starter packs as the third least feasible same-day PrEP strategy. Variability between sites

Our analysis also revealed variability between sites with respect to their same-day PrEP implementation strategy rankings. In terms of perceived effectiveness, the three sites varied most with respect to: 1. mobile units (minimum ranking = 5.00, maximum ranking = 9.13, range = 4.13), 2. telehealth (minimum ranking = 5.50, maximum ranking = 9.29, range = 3.79), and 3. use of discounted drug pricing program (e.g. 340B) resources to pay for medicines (minimum ranking = 4.14, maximum ranking = 7.83, range = 3.69). For perceived feasibility, the three sites varied most with respect to: 1. telehealth (minimum ranking = 5.43, maximum ranking = 9.67, range = 4.24), 2. medication co-pay programs (minimum ranking = 2.71, maximum ranking = 7.95, range = 5.24), and 3. PrEP navigators (minimum ranking = 4.00, maximum ranking = 7.48, range = 3.48). The three sites were in closest agreement regarding perceived effectiveness for: 1. co-locating clinics within federally funded (e.g. RW) clinics to share personnel, protocols, and equipment (minimum ranking = 7.86, maximum ranking = 9.50, range = 1.64), 2. consumer outreach (minimum ranking = 4.83, maximum ranking = 6.86, range = 2.03), and 3. PrEP navigators (minimum ranking = 3.67, maximum ranking = 5.71, range = 2.04).

Metainferences

Phase 1 and Phase 2 data revealed a variety of implementation strategies that may be effective and feasible to apply by FQHCs and CBOs, and thus may be considered as strong candidate strategies for additional testing. In particular, participants ranked both leadership buy-in and the use of PrEP champions as being highly effective and feasible (Fig. 2). Figure 3 reveals Phase 1 and Phase 2 complement each other in that both data sets identify leadership buy-in and PrEP Champions as key implementation strategies. Consumer outreach, while less highly ranked than leadership buy-in and PrEP champions, was also in the high perceived effectiveness-high perceived feasibility quadrant, and may warrant further consideration. In contrast, the use of telehealth and mobile units were ranked lowest in terms of their combined perceived effectiveness and feasibility. Discussion

Same-day PrEP is one underutilized strategy with the potential to increase PrEP uptake. With US discussions for implementing a national PrEP program whose goals would be to expand PrEP, access to Medicaid among uninsured populations, and scale up generic PrEP [25, 26], we sought to understand how same-day PrEP can be implemented on a larger scale. With key populations continuing to be disproportionately burdened and with that burden being amplified in regions like the Southern US which also comprises a large percentage of the population that is un- and underinsured [14, 15], exploring how Medicaid access impacts same-day PrEP prescriptions is key. Fortunately, participants did not believe that Medicaid-expansion was necessary for successful same-day PrEP implementation. Operationalizing a successful oral same-day PrEP program requires key elements including diversification of funding sources, buy-in from key stakeholders, cosmopolitanism as well as organization-specific customizations. The strategies FQHCs and CBOs use to increase same-day PrEP access to lower resourced populations though varied, can be categorized into similar categories. Nevertheless, these strategies may not be transferrable or scalable in other clinical settings as participants consistently emphasized the importance of tailoring these approaches to their specific settings.

Medicaid expansion did not emerge as an absolute necessity for same-day PrEP programs within FQHCs and CBOs. Even with Medicaid providing affordable coverage for generic formulations of PrEP and associated labs, estimates are generic PrEP only had a 25% penetrance in 2019 when it first came on the market, with rates of its use still being low as of 2021 [27]. Ballreich et al. speculates that there may be two factors impacting PrEP use among Medicaid populations [27]. First, they suspect PrEP users may be accessing PrEP from organizations that do not require insurance [27]. Second, they suspect additional support is needed to complete provider visits and lab tests [27]. Additionally, uninsured patients who are often residing in non-Medicaid expansion states experience fragmented care because even though there may be pharmaceutical, public health, or national programs that can provide PrEP medications, coverage for labs and access to pharmacies able to dispense the medication may be limited [26]. Furthermore, discounted drug-pricing programs provide access to PrEP medications for un- and underinsured individuals who access care at large safety net facilities like FQHCs [27]. Thus, it is not surprising that most participants emphasized diversity in funding sources as a necessary factor in same-day PrEP programs.

While discounted drug-pricing programs were mentioned as adjunctive strategies facilitating same-day PrEP programs, participants held varying opinions on what implementation and adjunctive strategies worked best within their organizations. What participants were able to agree upon was that effective and feasible same-day PrEP strategies were those optimized to the organization and population’s needs. This is also suggested by the literature. For example, one STI clinic created a same-day PrEP program by optimizing their STI clinic workflow and utilizing starter packs and patient navigators [28]. Studies focused on optimizing PrEP workflows by integrating them into existing workflows and expanding access using different care models (e.g. telehealth, mobile units) reveal mixed evidence on if these strategies work [29]. PrEP navigation is one of the few adjunctive interventions with consistent supportive evidence, but even this needs to be tailored to the individual organization and varies based on how the PrEP navigator or coordinator is trained and funded [29]. Furthermore, more analyses evaluating the use of these strategies in same-day PrEP programs are needed and have been highlighted by study participants.

Most importantly, the lack of differences between Medicaid expanded and non-Medicaid expanded states points to the generalizability of the barriers to same-day PrEP within FQHCs and CBOs. While some customization is needed within each organization, the implementation strategies that can be applied to overcome these barriers have potential generalizability. This is perhaps best reflected in the most common implementation strategies revolving around funding, adaption of existing protocols, leadership, and integration and reputation within the community. Thus, while this study focused on same-day PrEP specifically, the use of a stakeholder-driven, integrated (perceived effectiveness-feasibility) approach may be a valuable approach for identifying best considerations for implementing evidence-based interventions.

Although this study broadly explored same-day PrEP strategies among diverse healthcare professionals from numerous organizations within three states, there were some limitations. A non-probability sample was used, which may have increased self-selection biases. Use of a convenience sample and recruitment based on state limited the ability of this study’s results to be stratified by both Medicaid expansion status and organization status. Additionally, study instruments were not designed to assess the sequence or timing of the factors necessary for starting same-day programs. Because diverse healthcare professionals were recruited, there may have been knowledge gaps about all the intricacies within their organization. For Phase 2, smaller numbers of participants in two of the focus groups may have resulted in less stable rankings. Likewise, given some of the observed variability between sites in their respective rankings, additional focus groups among other sites may help establish more definitive implementation strategy rankings and prioritizations.

Conclusion

While a variety of implementation strategies were found to be integral to same-day PrEP implementation, workflow, funding, and organizational customization are the essential components to success. These findings make it challenging to scale successful same-day PrEP strategies and continue to place an emphasis on how the complex and fragmented US healthcare system contributes to structural challenges in ending the HIV epidemic. Furthermore, these findings also suggest that a national PrEP program will need to have enough flexibility to address the unique needs of the diverse communities throughout the US.
