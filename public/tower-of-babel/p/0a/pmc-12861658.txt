Acute Left Ventricular Dysfunction After Mitral Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair
Authors: Luca Raone, Marco Ferlini, Francesco Maria Sparasci, Costantino Pelosi, Daniele Cucchi, Silvia Miette Pontremoli, Giuseppe Lanzillo, Giulia Magrini, Rita Camporotondo, Leonardo De Luca
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMitral transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER) is an established treatment for severe mitral regurgitation. History of Presentation

An 81-year-old man with symptomatic severe mitral regurgitation (MR) was referred for elective transcatheter mitral edge-to-edge repair (M-TEER). He was previously hospitalized for acute decompensated heart failure, during which the severe MR was diagnosed. Despite optimized medical therapy, the patient remained assessed as NYHA functional class III, with exertional dyspnea and reduced exercise tolerance.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of permanent atrial fibrillation on oral anticoagulation, arterial hypertension, dyslipidemia, and peripheral artery disease previously treated with percutaneous revascularization procedures.

During the hospitalization, electrocardiogram (ECG) on admission revealed an atrial fibrillation rhythm, with an average ventricular rate of 73 beats/min and no ST-T abnormalities (Figure 1). Echocardiography at baseline showed no regional wall motion abnormalities, with preserved global and segmental contractility and severe primary MR due to rupture of a posterior leaflet chord, resulting in flail motion and an eccentric jet toward the interatrial septum (Figure 2). Additional findings included marked biatrial enlargement, with an estimated systolic pulmonary artery pressure of 45 mm Hg. After discussion by the heart team, he was indicated for M-TEER owing to advanced age and patient preference.

The patient subsequently underwent M-TEER under general anesthesia with transesophageal echocardiographic and fluoroscopic guidance. During the procedure, mild hypotension was managed with low-dose norepinephrine, and M-TEER was performed via femoral venous access using 2 MitraClip G4 XTW devices (Abbott Vascular). The postprocedural mean transmitral gradient was 3, and there was a marked reduction of MR from severe to mild (Figure 2, Video 1).

However, immediately after clip release, intraprocedural transesophageal echocardiography revealed the abrupt onset of left ventricular (LV) dysfunction, with a striking global reduction in LV performance evident as soon as the devices were deployed (Video 1).

Investigations

After the procedure, the patient was transferred to the intensive care unit. Shortly after arrival, he reported persistent nausea and mild epigastric discomfort, without typical chest pain.

Repeat echocardiography demonstrated an extensive akinetic region involving the apical and mid-distal segments, accompanied by compensatory basal hypercontractility, consistent with apical ballooning in a noncoronary distribution pattern, with a markedly reduced LV ejection fraction (LVEF) of 28%. The right ventricle remained normal in size and function, without evidence of systolic impairment (Figure 3, Video 2).

ECG on postprocedure day 1 revealed new, broad-based T-wave inversions across leads II, III, aVF, and precordial leads V4 to V6, with a prolonged corrected QT interval (QTc: 470 ms) (Figure 4). In parallel, laboratory testing disclosed a rise in high-sensitivity troponin I, peaking at 2,505 ng/L (normal: <47 ng/L).

Differential Diagnosis

The abrupt fall in LV systolic function immediately after clip deployment initially raised concern for acute afterload mismatch (AM). However, as the clinical course evolved, additional findings emerged that broadened the diagnostic spectrum. Within the first 24 hours, echocardiography revealed a typical apical ballooning pattern with basal hypercontractility, accompanied by new T-wave inversions, QTc prolongation, and a significant rise in cardiac biomarkers. These features prompted consideration of Takotsubo syndrome (TTS) as an alternative explanation. Biomarker elevation made it necessary to rule out an acute ischemic event, considering the patient's multiple cardiovascular risk factors, and also to support the possible diagnosis of TTS. For this reason, coronary angiography was subsequently performed. TTS was eventually established as a diagnosis of exclusion.

Management

The patient remained under close monitoring in the intensive care unit. In the immediate aftermath of the intervention, given the suspicion of acute AM, intravenous sodium nitroprusside was initiated to reduce afterload and support LV performance. However, as the clinical picture evolved, the diagnosis of TTS was considered more likely, and accordingly, nitroprusside was discontinued, and medical therapy was adjusted with the initiation of oral beta-blocker and angiotensin receptor blocker. No inotropic support was required at any stage.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient remained hemodynamically stable throughout the entire hospitalization. Serial measurements showed a progressive decline of cardiac biomarkers, with complete normalization over the subsequent days. On postprocedure day 7, echocardiography documented full recovery of LV systolic function, with restoration of a normal LVEF and absence of regional wall motion abnormalities (Figure 6). Concomitantly, ECG abnormalities regressed. The patient was discharged on postprocedure day 9 in stable clinical condition, assessed as NYHA functional class I.

Discussion

M-TEER is an established treatment for severe MR in candidates at high or prohibitive surgical risk, with proven long-term benefits in select populations.1 Despite its safety profile, acute reduction in LV systolic function after M-TEER may occur, representing a clinically relevant phenomenon reported in up to one-fifth of patients.2 This event may have prognostic implications on both short- and long-term outcomes and may arise from different underlying causes.

Traditionally, acute LV dysfunction after M-TEER has been attributed to AM, a phenomenon in which abrupt closure of the regurgitant pathway into the left atrium increases the effective afterload faced by the ventricle, thereby unmasking latent contractile dysfunction and causing a sudden decline in systolic performance. This mechanism has been classically described in secondary MR, in patients with baseline LV systolic impairment, increased LV dimensions, and elevated systolic pulmonary artery pressure (the latter possibly reflecting concomitant diastolic dysfunction superimposed on severe MR).3,4 However, a recent large-scale analysis by Perl et al2—the largest study to date assessing changes in LVEF after M-TEER—yielded unexpected findings: The investigators observed that acute LVEF reduction was more frequent in primary MR than in secondary MR, and that in primary MR, interestingly, a higher baseline LVEF was an independent predictor of postprocedure dysfunction. These results put into question the traditional paradigm of AM as the sole explanation, suggesting that different or additional mechanisms may be involved.

In recent years, emerging evidence has highlighted TTS as another potential mechanism for acute LV dysfunction. This transient stress-induced cardiomyopathy, typically presenting with apical ballooning and basal hypercontractility, has been described in sporadic reports after M-TEER.5,6 In this context, periprocedural factors such as surgical stress, anesthetic induction, and the use of catecholamines for hemodynamic support may act as triggers, precipitating an exaggerated adrenergic response with transient myocardial impairment.5, 6, 7 Our case exemplifies for the first time the diagnostic dilemma that may arise in this setting and, by confirming TTS as a plausible etiological mechanism, provides novel evidence that this stress-induced cardiomyopathy should be regarded as an additional and innovative explanation for acute LV dysfunction after M-TEER. The abrupt onset of LV dysfunction immediately after clip deployment was initially consistent with AM, and nitroprusside was administered accordingly. However, the subsequent clinical evolution—characterized by the appearance of apical ballooning with basal hyperkinesia, troponin elevation, new ECG abnormalities, and the exclusion of coronary artery disease on coronary angiography—was more typical of TTS. Notably, this patient presented with primary MR, preserved LVEF, and a nondilated LV, a substrate less compatible with classic AM. Furthermore, the complete recovery of systolic function within 1 week strongly supported the diagnosis of TTS. Unfortunately, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was not performed owing to intolerance of the examination related to the patient's known claustrophobia, and invasive hemodynamic data were not collected.

Prompt recognition of the underlying mechanism is essential to guide appropriate management and anticipate the patient's clinical evolution. Although pathophysiologically distinct, AM and TTS may sometimes present with overlapping features, making early differentiation challenging. AM typically occurs intraprocedurally8 and manifests as a diffuse reduction in contractility with global hypokinesia, usually proportional to pre-existing LV reserve. Conversely, TTS generally emerges within the first 24 hours, with the echocardiographic and electrocardiographic features already described, together with a mild to moderate troponin elevation. From a prognostic standpoint, this distinction is crucial: Although recovery is possible from both conditions, AM—particularly when accompanied by acute LV remodeling—has been associated with worse outcomes, including higher mortality and heart failure hospitalization rates.4,8 In contrast, TTS is usually reversible, with complete recovery of systolic function.5, 6, 7 Therapeutic strategies also diverge: In AM, where ventriculoarterial uncoupling represents the defining pathophysiological condition, treatment by default includes intravenous nitroprusside to promptly reduce afterload. This is not necessarily the case in TTS, where vasodilator therapy is not routinely required and management more often relies on cardioselective beta-blockers in stable patients, together with conventional heart failure therapy as appropriate.

Finally, a third, albeit less common, mechanism of acute LV dysfunction after M-TEER may be represented by ischemic injury. Periprocedural myocardial infarction is rare, typically related to underlying atherosclerotic disease,9 though isolated cases of coronary air embolism have been reported, occasionally facilitated by pulmonary vein injury and positive-pressure ventilation.10 Although infrequent, such events underscore the importance of vigilant monitoring, as their presentation may overlap with both AM and TTS, further complicating the diagnostic assessment.

Conclusions

Acute LV dysfunction after M-TEER remains a clinically relevant complication, with mechanisms that extend beyond the traditional paradigm of AM. Our report illustrates a rare case of postprocedural TTS, emphasizing the diagnostic ambiguity that may arise when overlapping features of AM and TTS coexist in the same patient. Awareness of this pathophysiological spectrum is crucial, as it directly affects therapy and prognosis.
