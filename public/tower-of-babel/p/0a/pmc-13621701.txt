Effect of GLP-1RA on blood eosinophil levels in adults: a real-world study
Authors: Yiting Wu, Yi Lin, Jifei Wu, Lei Zhao, Yanmei Lin, Wenwen Chen, Yufan Zhu, Yufan Wang, Min Zhang, Wuping Bao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) are widely used for glycemic control and weight management, especially semaglutide. Potential benefits of GLP-1RAs in asthma, especially in eosinophilic phenotypes, prompt growing scientific interest.ObjectiveTo investigate the impact of semaglutide on eosinophilic inflammation and to identify clinical and metabolic determinants of eosinophil reduction after treatment.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, single-center study in Shanghai, China. Eligible subjects were those first prescribed semaglutide in our outpatient clinic. Demographic characteristics, complete blood counts, and lipid profiles before and after semaglutide treatment were collected from electronic medical records. Subgroup analyses were stratified using a blood eosinophil count (BEC) cut-off of 150/µL and a BMI threshold of 28 kg/m². Multiple linear regression analysis was employed to identify factors influencing the reduction in eosinophil counts.ResultsAmong 371 participants included in the final analysis, BEC decreased significantly from 160(150)/µL at baseline to 110(100)/µL after semaglutide treatment (P < 0.001), and eosinophil percentage declined from 2.20(1.80)% to 1.60(1.40)% (P < 0.001). The proportion of individuals with eosinophil counts < 150/µL increased from 44.20% to 65.79%. Eosinophil reduction occurred in both baseline eosinophil < 150/µL and eosinophil ≥ 150/µL groups. The percentage reduction in eosinophil count was greater in the BMI-low group than in the BMI-high group (− 31.58% vs. −21.04%, P = 0.037). Multiple linear regression identified baseline eosinophil count as the only independent predictor of eosinophil reduction after treatment.ConclusionsGLP-1RA, semaglutide treatment significantly reduced BECs regardless of baseline levels, with a more pronounced effect observed in non-obese individuals. Higher baseline eosinophil levels independently predicted greater treatment-induced reductions after adjustment for BMI and lipid parameters.Trial registrationChiCTR2500110394, www.chictr.org.cn. Introduction

Asthma is a common chronic inflammatory respiratory disease, with a substantial disease burden, affecting 260.48 million individuals globally [1]. Eosinophils play a pivotal role in asthma pathogenesis [2], particularly in severe asthma, where eosinophil-driven airway inflammation can disrupt the epithelial barrier, enhance airway hyperresponsiveness (AHR), and promote airway remodeling, thereby contributing to poorer disease control and a greater disease burden [3, 4]. An elevated blood eosinophil count (BEC) is independently associated with an increased risk of future moderate-to-severe exacerbations in asthma patients [5–7]. The International Severe Asthma Registry (ISAR) [8] reported that 70.4% of patients with severe asthma were overweight or obese, frequently accompanied by metabolic dysregulation, including insulin resistance, hyperglycemia, and even type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). These obesity-related metabolic issues, in turn, act as comorbidities that exacerbate asthma by increasing its prevalence and mortality risk [9].

Semaglutide, a long-acting glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist (GLP-1RA), is increasingly prescribed for patients with T2DM, particularly those who are overweight [10]. Beyond glycemic control, accumulating evidence suggests that GLP-1RAs may also confer benefits in asthma management [11–13]. Lee et al. [14] reported that adding a GLP-1RA to metformin was associated with a 40% further reduction in asthma exacerbation risk. These findings raise a compelling question: could the benefits of GLP-1RAs in asthma extend beyond simple weight loss?

The mechanism underlying this potential benefit remains unclear. A key link may lie in the interaction between GLP-1 signaling and airway inflammation. Previous studies found that GLP-1R was widely expressed in pulmonary tissues, including airway smooth muscle, type II pneumocytes, epithelial cells, eosinophils, neutrophils, and platelets [15–17]. Intriguingly, GLP-1R expression appears substantially higher in the lung than in other organs [18], suggesting a specific biological role. Preclinical studies support this notion, demonstrating that GLP-1RAs can suppress eosinophilic airway inflammation, AHR, and mucus production in allergic obese animal models, with reduced Interleukin (IL)-4, IL-5, IL-33 expression in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and MUC5AC in mucus [19, 20]. In humans, Mitchell PD et al. [16] found significantly higher GLP-1R expression in eosinophils of healthy controls compared to allergic asthmatics. Additionally, GLP-1 analog has been shown to modulate eosinophil activation and cytokine production. However, no study has directly confirmed whether GLP-1RAs reduce BECs in humans.

Of note, although the protective effect of GLP-1RAs on asthma and eosinophilic airway inflammation has been validated in preclinical studies, prospective clinical cohort studies targeting semaglutide intervention in asthmatic patients are still in progress, and definitive clinical data verifying its regulatory effect on asthma-related inflammatory biomarkers in pure asthmatic populations are still lacking. Restricted by the current clinical research progress and drug approved indications, it is difficult to carry out targeted observational studies in specialized asthma cohorts at this stage. Therefore, we conducted a real-world study focusing on patients who received semaglutide for T2DM and metabolic regulation indications, to explore the association between semaglutide use and changes in BECs, the core pathogenic factor of asthma. We further analyzed the correlation of BEC changes with weight loss and lipid metabolism, aiming to fill the gap in human clinical evidence regarding the effect of semaglutide on eosinophil regulation, and provide preliminary supportive data for the subsequent clinical application of GLP-1RAs in asthma intervention.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective, single-center study of 400 patients who initiated semaglutide therapy at the outpatient department of Shanghai General Hospital between January 2022 and December 2024, using the hospital’s medical record system (Fig. 1). Eligible patients were ≥ 18 years old, prescribed semaglutide for the first time, and attended regular follow-up appointments for at least three months. Only individuals with at least one complete blood count (CBC) recorded prior to treatment and at least three months post-treatment, in addition to lipid profile data and demographic information, were included in the analysis. Comorbidities documented in the medical records were also recorded.

Exclusion criteria included: any respiratory or systemic infection occurring within 4 weeks prior to enrollment or at any time during the 3-month follow-up period; use of medications impacting eosinophil levels (e.g., systemic or inhaled glucocorticoids, leukotriene receptor antagonists or biologics that may affect eosinophil levels); participation in other interventional trials affecting eosinophil counts; and pregnancy or breastfeeding.

Demographic data, including gender, age, body mass index (BMI), indications for semaglutide therapy, and pre- and post-treatment laboratory results were collected. Laboratory data included eosinophil (EOS) count and percentage, white blood cell (leukocytes), neutrophil and lymphocyte counts, total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), apolipoprotein A1 (ApoA1), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), apolipoprotein E (ApoE), and lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)].

The study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai General Hospital with a waiver of informed consent as it is a retrospective study (approval number: NO.2025[604]).

Study endpoints and subgroup classification

The primary outcome of this study was to assess changes in blood eosinophils after semaglutide treatment. Secondary outcomes included changes in lipid parameters before and after treatment, as well as an investigation of factors influencing the reduction in blood eosinophils following semaglutide therapy.

Subgroup analysis was performed, categorizing subjects into the EOS-high and EOS-low groups, based on a BEC cut-off of ≥ 150/µL. Additionally, subjects were classified into the BMI-high and BMI-low groups based on a BMI threshold of ≥ 28 kg/m2.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 27) and R (version 4.4.1), with a two-tailed p-value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed variables and as median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed variables.

Group comparisons were made using the Mann-Whitney U test. Within-group comparisons before and after semaglutide treatment were conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Spearman’s rank test was used to assess correlations.

Multiple linear regression analysis (enter) was performed to identify factors influencing the reduction in eosinophil count, and two models were conducted. Variables were included in the model when p < 0.250 in univariate regression; age, sex and comorbidities with elevated BECs were forced into the model. The first model included the following variables: gender, age, pre-treatment BMI, pre-treatment eosinophil count, change in HDL (∆HDL), change in Lp(a) [∆Lp(a)], and the use of lipid-lowering medications. The second model incorporated an additional variable: change in BMI (∆BMI).

The statistical analysis plan was developed prior to data collection, and the study was conducted in accordance with the pre-specified protocol.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

Of the 400 participants enrolled, 29 were excluded after eligibility assessment, including 19 lacked CBCs, 9 had follow-up of < 3 months, and 1 had a respiratory infection within 4 weeks prior to enrollment. Finally, 371 participants were included in the analysis, with 364 (93.26%) prescribed semaglutide for T2DM (some patients had a normal BMI) and 25 (6.74%) for obesity. The median age was 58 [23] years, and 52.83% of participants were male. The median body mass index (BMI) was 27.60 (5.53) kg/m². The median BEC was 160 (150) /µL and median eosinophil percentage was 2.20 (1.80)%.

Primary analysis: whole-cohort changes after semaglutide treatment

Following treatment, both the BEC and eosinophil percentage decreased significantly. The eosinophil count declined from 160 (150)/µL at baseline to 110 (100)/µL after treatment (P < 0.001), while the eosinophil percentage decreased from 2.20 (1.80)% to 1.60 (1.40)% (P < 0.001). Correspondingly, the proportion of participants with eosinophil counts < 150/µL increased after treatment compared with baseline (65.79% vs. 44.20%). Most lipid parameters, namely TC, TG, LDL, ApoB and ApoE, also decreased significantly after treatment (all P < 0.05), suggesting semaglutide’s potential favorable effect on lipid metabolism. In contrast, Lp(a) levels remained unchanged, and neither did HDL (1.21[0.35] vs. 1.19[0.36] mmol/L, P = 0.600) or its major protein component, ApoA1 (1.31[0.33] vs. 1.30[0.34] g/L, P = 0.269). In a subgroup of participants with paired BMI measurements available before and after treatment (n = 150), BMI decreased significantly from 27.86 (4.50) kg/m2 at baseline to 26.13 (3.80) kg/m2 after treatment (P < 0.001), further confirming the weight-reducing effect of semaglutide (Fig. 2, eTable 1).

Subgroup analysis based on eosinophil count

Using a cutoff of 150/µL, participants were stratified into an EOS-low group (n = 164; baseline eosinophils 100 [50]/µL) and an EOS-high group (n = 207; 240[150]/µL) (P < 0.001 for intergroup difference, Table 1). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of age, BMI, TG, TC, LDL, ApoA1, ApoB, ApoE or Lp(a). However, the EOS-high group had a higher proportion of males and a greater prevalence of coronary heart disease as a comorbidity. Additionally, this group exhibited higher white blood cell counts (7.58 [1.67)] vs. 6.69 [1.56)] ×109/L, P < 0.001) and lower HDL levels (1.23 [0.37] vs. 1.16 [0.35] mmol/L, P = 0.034).

Subgroup analysis based on BMI (cut-off value 28 kg/m2)

Among the 371 patients, the largest subgroup was the obese group (n = 164, 44.20%), followed by the overweight (n = 127, 35.77%) and normal-weight groups (n = 64, 18.03%). Baseline BECs were comparable across the three BMI subgroups, with median values of 160(90), 150(140), 170(160)/µL (P = 0.509), respectively (eTable 3). While in the obese group, a significantly higher proportion of patients had an eosinophil count ≥ 150/µL (92 patients, 56.10%) compared to those with an eosinophil < 150/µL (72 patients, 43.90%) (Table 1), suggesting a potential association between obesity and higher eosinophil levels. In contrast, the distribution between EOS-high and EOS-low patients showed no significant difference in the normal-weight or overweight subgroups (P > 0.05 for both).

Subsequently, participants were stratified using a BMI cutoff of 28 kg/m2 to investigate whether obesity modified the eosinophil-lowering effect of semaglutide. At baseline, no significant difference in eosinophils counts was observed between the BMI-low and BMI-high groups. After semaglutide treatment, eosinophil counts decreased significantly within both subgroups (P < 0.001). In contrast, a significant increase in HDL was seen only in the BMI-low group, rising from 1.20(0.32) mmol/L at baseline to 1.23 (0.35) mmol/L after treatment (P = 0.049), whereas no significant change in HDL was detected in the BMI-high group (P = 0.166). When comparing the magnitude of treatment-induced changes between groups, the BMI-low group demonstrated a significantly greater percentage reduction in eosinophil count than the BMI-high group (-31.58 [45.00]% vs. -21.04 [38.04]%, P = 0.037). Similarly, the percentage increase in HDL was significantly greater in the BMI-low group compared with the BMI-high group (2.75 [24.47]% vs. -2.44 [21.92]%, P = 0.021) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this real-world study, we observed that semaglutide, a widely used agent for glycemic control and weight management among patients with T2DM and obesity, can reverse the mild subclinical BEC elevation present in obese/T2DM Chinese patients, providing possible reasons to explain the reduced asthma exacerbation risk associated with GLP-1RA use reported in prior clinical studies [11, 12]. Notably, this reduction appeared more pronounced in non-obese individuals and was independent of changes in body weight and lipid metabolism after multivariable adjustment.

Normal BEC ranges differ across ethnic populations [21], indicating substantial heterogeneity in baseline eosinophil levels. A recent large-scale cross-sectional study of over 680,000 Chinese participants reported a right-skewed BEC distribution in the general population, with a median of 100/µL (IQR 60–170/µL) [22]. Our cohort had a higher baseline median BEC of 160/µL, likely related to its comorbid profiles: 5.1% had chronic airway diseases, 12.4% had allergic rhinitis or dermatitis, and 46.2% had obesity of our participants (BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2). Consistent with prior evidence, elevated BMI and respiratory conditions are associated with higher BECs [22].

BECs are also susceptible to acute infection [23, 24]. To ensure stable baseline measurements, we only included samples with normal leukocyte and neutrophil counts. Semaglutide treatment significantly reduced BECs, and higher baseline BECs independently predicted a greater reduction after adjustment for demographics, lipid parameters, and comorbidities. Since eosinophils serve as key effector cells of type 2 inflammation in asthma, and BECs are generally higher in asthmatic patients, we hypothesize that semaglutide may exert a more prominent eosinophil-lowering effect in asthma population. However, this hypothesis remains speculative and requires further prospective verification.

To explore the relevance of our findings to type 2 inflammatory airway diseases, we used the GINA 2025 threshold of 150/µL to stratify subclinical type 2 inflammation [25]. This threshold targets subtle airway inflammatory risk rather than overt hematological eosinophilic disease. BEC reductions were observed in both high-eosinophil and low-eosinophil subgroups.

The EOS-high group had a higher prevalence of obesity, and baseline BMI correlated positively with BECs in participants with eosinophils ≥ 150/µL but not below this threshold. The Nagahama study in Japan reported a non-linear positive association between BMI and BEC. However, this association became negative when eosinophils ≥ 200/µL [26]. Another study also identified a non-linear, inverted U-shaped relationship between BMI and eosinophils [27]. The discrepancies between these findings and ours may be attributable to differences in study populations, as approximately half of our participants had obesity, whereas most participants in the two previous cohorts had a normal BMI. Nevertheless, these observations collectively suggest that eosinophil levels should be dynamically monitored in relation to changes in body weight. To account for potential confounding by obesity, we adjusted for BMI in regression models, confirming that the eosinophil-lowering effect of semaglutide was independent of BMI changes.

Patients in the EOS-high group had significantly lower HDL levels. HDL, a major circulating lipoprotein, mediates reverse cholesterol transport by transferring cholesterol from peripheral tissues and vascular walls to the liver for metabolism or excretion, thereby exerting cardioprotective effects. In line with previous reports [28–30], elevated BECs were inversely associated with HDL, supporting a potential link between eosinophilic inflammation and cardiometabolic risk. HDL may modulate eosinophil migration and infiltration. Rani A et al. [31] found that synthetic high-density lipoprotein (sHDL) nanoparticles markedly suppressed eotaxin-induced eosinophil migration in vitro, an effect reversed by blocking HDL receptors SR-BI and ABCA1. Moreover, sHDL significantly reduced eosinophil infiltration in bronchoalveolar lavage in an IL-5 transgenic mouse model. We adjusted for changes in HDL in multivariable analyses, further supporting the robustness of semaglutide’s effect on BECs.

We further performed subgroup analysis using the Chinese obesity cutoff of 28 kg/m2, recommended by the Chinese adult weight criteria (WS/T428-2013). The BEC-lowering and HDL-elevating effects of semaglutide were more pronounced in non-obese patients. This may reflect confounding metabolic disturbances in obesity, such as insulin resistance and adipose dysfunction, which may attenuate anti-inflammatory responses. Because most participants were overweight or obese, the generalizability of these findings to individuals with normal BMI remains unclear and requires further study.

The observed eosinophil-lowering effect of semaglutide may have implications for biomarker interpretation in clinical practice, especially in patients with asthma where BECs guide phenotyping and biologic treatment decisions. Although preclinical studies suggest that GLP-1RAs may inhibit eosinophil survival and activation via the PKA/NF-κB signaling pathway [32–34], the precise mechanisms in humans remain incompletely understood. These findings support a potential biological link between GLP-1 signaling and eosinophilic inflammation but do not confirm therapeutic efficacy in asthma.

This study has several important limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective real-world study, the design limits causal inference and generalizability, and reliance on electronic medical records may have introduced selection bias, information bias, and residual confounding. Second, the cohort comprised primarily patients with T2DM and/or obesity receiving semaglutide for approved metabolic indications, with only a small proportion having chronic airway diseases. Although we adjusted for available eosinophil-associated comorbidities including asthma, allergic rhinitis, and atopic dermatitis in multivariable analyses, some conditions such as chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, eosinophilic esophagitis, parasitic infections, and smoking status were not systematically documented and could not be fully adjusted for. Third, respiratory outcomes including asthma control, exacerbation frequency, lung function, and FeNO were not systematically evaluated, so the clinical relevance of reduced BECs to asthma-related benefits remains unclear and cannot be confirmed. In addition, detailed data on semaglutide dose, treatment duration, and cumulative exposure were incomplete due to clinical dose titration in routine practice. Finally, while our findings suggest that semaglutide may lower BECs, these results reflect only biomarker-level associations and should not be extrapolated to therapeutic efficacy in asthma. Definitive conclusions require further validation in well-designed prospective controlled trials.

Interpretation

Our study reveals that, among patients with obesity, a higher proportion had BECs ≥ 150/µL than < 150/µL, and eosinophil counts were positively associated with BMI in the EOS-high subgroup. Semaglutide reduced BECs regardless of whether baseline eosinophil levels exceeded 150/µL, with a more pronounced effect observed in non-obese individuals (BMI < 28 kg/m2). After adjustments for BMI and lipid metabolism parameters, higher baseline eosinophil counts were significantly associated with greater post-treatment reductions. Additionally, HDL levels increased significantly after treatment in non-obese patients. These findings suggest that semaglutide’s anti-inflammatory effects extend beyond glycemic control and weight reduction, potentially offering additional therapeutic benefits for patients with eosinophilic inflammation. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the mechanistic links among GLP-1 receptor signaling, HDL, and eosinophil regulation.
