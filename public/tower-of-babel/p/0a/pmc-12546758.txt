Toward an ion-based large-scale integrated circuit: design, simulation, and integration
Authors: Noa Edri Fraiman, Barak Sabbagh, Gilad Yossifon, Alexander Fish
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Iontronics combines ions as information carriers with electronic-like operations, enabling the creation of ion-based integrated circuits that offer unique signal processing, chemical regulation, and enhanced bio-integrability. Existing simulation tools encounter difficulties in effectively modeling integrated iontronic components, highlighting the need for specialized design and simulation methodologies. Here we present a design approach toward ion-based large-scale integrated circuits, inspired by electronic integrated circuit abstraction levels. We develop a compact model for the iontronic bipolar diode, with a conceptual framework applicable to other iontronic components. The model is implemented using standard Electronic Design Automation tools, allowing simulation of static and dynamic properties of iontronic circuits. Simulated results match measurements from fabricated small-scale iontronic circuits. The proposed simulation approach employs Monte Carlo methodology and enables exploration of how component non-uniformity influences circuit behavior. We demonstrate the model’s utility by simulating ion-based integrated circuits, including an iontronic decoder and diode bridge. Expanding traditional circuit design tools to support iontronics could advance the development of hybrid systems that leverage both electronic and ionic functionalities.

Introduction

The ability to integrate a large number of electronic components onto a single small piece of semiconductor, and the invention of Integrated Circuit (IC) have changed the world beyond imagination. The lengthy trajectory from the invention of the bi-polar transistor to today’s integration capabilities has prompted the development of new fields specializing in new devices1–3, advanced fabrication processes for integration4, simulation tools and Electronic Design Automation (EDA) to allow for reliable chip design5,6, and ongoing miniaturization to advance integrated circuit capabilities7–9.

In recent years, emerging computational domains have been proposed and developed, with the potential to expand solid-state electronics10–12. Iontronics explores the ability to perform computations using ions that serve as information carriers, and is based on the elegant functionalities found in biological systems13,14. This approach leverages the unique ability of nanoscale fluidic structures such as nanochannels and ion exchange membranes to selectively transport ions based on their charge. In fluidic nanostructures, the overlapping electric double layers (EDLs) cause co-ions (related to the nanostructure’s surface charge) to be excluded from the inner volume, while counter-ions can transport freely. As a result, modulating the surface charge or functionalized molecular probes enables control over the flux of specific ions and molecules when an external electric field is applied15,16. Combining various micro- and nanofluidic structures that exhibit different ion perm-selective behaviors, ranging from low to high perm-selectivity to either anions or cations, enables the creation of ion-based computing elements such as iontronic resistors, diodes17, and transistors18 that create new possibilities for information processing.

The interest in exploring the integration capability of iontronic components on a single chip stem from the possibilities it provides to perform more complex computations and develop alternative processing capabilities. Greater computational complexity could augment the capacities of numerous applications. These include smart drug19 and ion20 delivery and complex sensors arrays that require sensing and parallel processing in real time without necessarily needing to convert the information to the electronic domain17.

A key challenge in iontronic circuit design is the intrinsically slow mobility of ions. Ionic mobility in aqueous environments ( ~ 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻⁹ m²/V·s) is 6–7 orders of magnitude lower than electron mobility in semiconductors ( ~ 10⁻² to 10⁻¹ m²/V·s), fundamentally limiting circuit speed. To address this, strategies based on device scaling and enriched signal encoding may offer solutions. Reducing the characteristic dimensions of iontronic components — from ~300 µm to below 10 µm — using more precise fabrication techniques such as photolithography or femtosecond laser machining, could substantially shorten ionic diffusion times. The response time (τ) scales with the square of the characteristic length (L) and inversely with the diffusion coefficient (D), as τ ∝ L2/D. This reduction could improve response times from ~10 seconds to ~10 milliseconds, making them compatible with a broad range of biological timescales, including millisecond-scale action potentials, slower synaptic integration, and long-term potentiation. In parallel, iontronic systems inherently support rich signal encoding capabilities. By using multiple ion species, signals can be distinguished by ion type21,22— enabling ‘multi-color’ processing13,23. This can support multi-bit parallelism and increase information throughput, helping to mitigate the limitations of slow ionic transport. The aqueous nature of iontronic systems, using ions and molecules-based signals, enables biocompatible and biodegradable sensing and actuation, including potential applications in neuro/brain interfaces14, and offers a key advantage over standard complementary metal–oxide–semiconductor (CMOS) materials, which often require encapsulation for biological interfacing and are using electron-based signals. In contrast, iontronic components can be fabricated using inherently biocompatible materials such as hydrogels14 and polyelectrolytes24.

In addition to these advantages, iontronics has a natural interface to electronics. Integrating iontronics and electronics provides opportunities for hybrid systems that can leverage both electrical signals based on electrons and ion-based signals transmitted through fluidic elements25. This synergy creates the potential for innovative applications that combine the strengths of both technologies26.

Recent studies have demonstrated that iontronic diodes can perform Boolean computations by implementing basic logic gates such as OR and AND17,27–29. Despite these advances, studies have remained limited to the integration of only a few iontronic logic gates. Our recent study30, which demonstrates a circuit integrating four such gates, illustrates both the functionality and key design challenges at the circuit level. Using terminology from the microelectronics field, we analyzed the basic performance of the gates and identified relevant limitations. The main hurdles include the design and fabrication of suitable devices with small parameter variations and high reliability, the integration and fluidic connectivity of these devices onto a single microfluidic chip, and no less importantly, a set of tools to allow efficient design and simulation of integrated computational systems to enable prediction and optimization of the complex iontronics systems already at the design level. These capabilities have existed in integrated electronic circuits since the mid-1960s31, when scientists and engineers managed to connect dozens or hundreds of electronic devices in an integrated circuit, and reached a peak in 2024 when one of the world’s most complex electronic chips comprising trillions of transistors was developed32.

In electronic integrated circuits, the hurdle of developing complex systems can be solved by a design approach based on the creation of abstraction levels, as depicted in simplified form in Fig. 1a. Each level of abstraction has a design methodology that corresponds to that level and design, and simulation tools that support this methodology. The transition between levels of abstraction is supported by dedicated models that only describe the phenomena and characteristics that are important to the targeted level of abstraction. Technology CAD (TCAD) tools use Poisson, transport, and continuity equations to model electron and hole concentrations and current flow in semiconductor devices under specific bias conditions. However, TCAD is too complex for circuit simulation, since solving these equations for each component is highly time-consuming. For circuit-level designs, a compact model is utilized to characterize the current and charge behavior of a semiconductor device as a function of voltage, process parameters, temperature, and geometry. This model, which represents the device relative to its terminals, is incorporated into circuit simulation tools to enable both accurate and efficient simulations31,33. The transition from the circuit level to the functional and logic design adheres to a similar approach. It involves shifting the focus from detailed components and interconnects to abstract logical functions and their interactions within the system. The logic design is implemented with tools developed to handle logic gates and utilizes a gate model that only describes the significant properties of the gate such as speed, power consumption, area, etc. Although gate characteristics depend heavily on the physical properties of the device, gate models do not include these properties at all. This enables an efficient, fast, yet reliable simulation of a large number of integrated gates. In iontronics, employing standard device simulation approaches is impractical when attempting to evaluate iontronic circuits. For example, a physically- based simulator such as COMSOL Multiphysics ® can evaluate the influence of ionic transport processes in the bipolar nanostructure that forms an iontronic diode but would face an uphill battle when trying to simulate two integrated diodes (especially if dynamic characteristics are needed). This emphasizes the need for a simulation tool that can simulate iontronics-based circuits and predict their response.

Inspired by the electronic integrated circuit abstraction levels and design approach, we propose a compact model for nanofluidic diodes to initiate iontronic circuit design capabilities and support the development of circuit design methodologies for iontronic integrated circuits. The model is based on the physical characteristics of the device and its experimental behavior. It is implemented using a Verilog-A and Spectre simulator, thus enabling seamless integration and straightforward use within commercial Very Large-Scale Integration (VLSI) EDA environments. It not only predicts the behavior of iontronic circuits but can also serve as a tool for further exploration and improvement of nanofluidic devices toward larger scale integration. The development of iontronic circuit-level simulation capabilities can also facilitate the future development of iontronic gate-level models, by enabling higher abstraction tools for efficient design of large-scale iontronic integrated circuits. This model also supports the simulation and integration of hybrid electronics and iontronics systems. As shown in Fig. 1(a).

As depicted in ref. 35 compact models of metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor (MOSFET) transistors (the basic components of all electronic IC) start with a core model, which only includes the basic effects. However, to produce an accurate compact model, numerous add-on real-device models must be added. Advances in transistor fabrication technology made real-device effects dominant, making these models crucial for accurate circuit simulation33. As depicted in Fig. 1b, we propose a similar approach for developing the iontronic diode compact model. The basic structure of the iontronic diode is shown in Fig. 1c.

While we focus here on demonstrating our approach to iontronic circuit design using iontronic bipolar diodes, the method is generic and can be extended to other combinations of iontronic components, such as transistors36, memristors37, resistors and capacitors38. In this work, we first present an iontronic diode core model that accounts for the basic effects. The model has an I-V static feature, which is crucial to calculating the steady state response of the iontronic logic gate, and a C-V feature, which describes the relationship between the voltage and the large signal capacitance to predict the switching time of the diode. Next, a very preliminary statistical model is presented as an add-on to account for random variations that take place during circuit fabrication, which impact device functionality. Clearly, as can be seen in Fig. 1b, more advanced add-ons must be included in future models, to deal with the influence of current saturation, aging, temperature, etc. In addition, iontronics has its own unique set of effects compared to electronics, such as fluid convection, ion concentration gradients, water splitting and multi-ions currents, which would be valuable in future models to enhance accuracy and explore innovative iontronic circuits applications.

Results

The development of a compact model for the iontronic diode was carried out in two phases.

Phase I

Iontronic and PN (P-type and N-type) semiconductor diodes have several similarities in structure and in the underlying mechanisms that induce asymmetric transport of oppositely charged mobile charge carriers. In both iontronic and electronic diodes, the main transport mechanisms are drift (electro-migration) and diffusion39. We thus used the SPICE semiconductor diode model40, which is widely implemented in electronic integrated circuit simulators, as an inspiration for the iontronic diode compact model. This relationship can also be described by a voltage-dependent resistor. In Fig. 2c, R0, R1 and Y01, α01 are associated with the microchannel and electrode-solution interfaces (both anode and cathode), while R2 and Y02, α02 are associated with the polyelectrolyte junction.

Figure 2f illustrates how the components of the simplified equivalent circuit shown in Fig. 2c values vary with applied DC voltage. For example, the resistance associated with the diode’s junction (R2) increased by two orders of magnitude when switching from forward to reverse bias, unlike R0 and R1, which remained relatively constant as the solution’s ohmic resistance in the microchannel and redox processes at the electrode-solution interfaces remined unchanged. Similarly, Y02 changed by two orders of magnitude from forward to reverse bias, with its dispersion coefficient (α02) varying with the bias voltage. By contrast, Y01 and its α01 remained unchanged, with values around a few pF and 1, respectively. The impact of C1 was primarily observed at high frequencies, suggesting that for lower frequencies (frequencies well below 800 kHz), a simpler equivalent circuit could describe the system (Fig. S1b). This simpler circuit, consisting of a parallel RC circuit with a voltage-dependent resistor and a voltage-dependent CPE capacitance in series with a constant resistance (R0 + R1), closely resembles the proposed iontronic compact model in Fig. 2b. In this model, R0 + R1 corresponds to Re, and R2 corresponds to Rp, with the key difference being the use of a fractional capacitor (Y02 and α02) and not a voltage dependent regular capacitor (Cp). While some CAD tools support the simulation of fractional capacitors using Laplace-domain representations with fractional powers42, this approach presents challenges for transient circuit analysis and is not supported in all CAD environments. Moreover, as for now, modeling a voltage-dependent fractional-order capacitor in modern CAD tools remains a challenge and is still an active area of research. Therefore, our current model uses an integer-order, voltage-dependent capacitor.

After calibrating the parameters, we implemented the model in MATLAB to examine its suitability to assess the full dynamic electric behavior of the iontronic diode in various step voltage scenarios (Fig. 3a). As expected, the model predicted all scenarios well, except for a single case involving the transition from forward to reverse bias. This transition exhibited more complex transient behavior that could not be fully captured by the simplified model. We believe that this may be related to a memory effect that can be associated with the extensive accumulation of mobile ions at the junction under forward bias bias14,43. Although the applied bias can be switched instantaneously, the response time required to deplete all the ions from the junction following their accumulation largely depends on the diode’s length37 and the ion diffusion coefficients. This relation to a memory effect is supported by the observation of fractional capacitance in the EIS measurements and the scan-rate-dependent hysteresis observed in previously published studies on polyelectrolyte diodes30,37,39.

As one step towards the simulation of iontronic circuits containing several iontronic diodes, we successfully simulated the model in Cadence Spectre, a very common tool for simulating electronic circuits (Fig. 3a), achieving results closely matching those obtained in MATLAB. For this purpose, the suggested voltage-dependent resistor and capacitor models were written in VerilogA (Supplementary Notes 1) the de-facto language for defining and distributing compact models for electronic components44.

Model of process variation effects as the first add-on to the core model

Similar to electronic devices, iontronic diodes exhibit random variations in their physical characteristics related to the fabrication process. For instance, Fig. 3b illustrates the variation in junction length (i.e., the polyelectrolytes constructing the iontronic bipolar diode) in 9 different diodes on the same chip. To accurately simulate an iontronic circuit, the diode model must account for these variations, given their potential to substantially impact the electrical behavior of each individual diode and, consequently, the overall circuit performance.

In this work, we propose using a Monte Carlo (MC) simulation technique45 to evaluate the functionality of integrated iontronic circuits, and specifically as regards intra-chip variations. Inter-chip variations which involve global factors that impact all devices identically on a chip (such as the solution, polymer, or material properties), are beyond the scope of this study, since assessing them would require fabricating and measuring multiple chips. By conducting numerous random simulations, the Monte Carlo technique estimates circuit performance and yields in a range of possible conditions, thus allowing designers to make informed decisions that lead to more reliable and manufacturable designs.

Major variations were observed in the I-V curves (Fig. 3c), particularly in the distribution of quasi steady-state currents under reverse bias (Ioff at −1V) and forward bias (Ion at 1 V) for each diode. The total steady-state resistance (RTSS) of the diode under forward and reverse bias was extracted from current measurements, and a log-normal distribution was fitted to the results (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). Statistical validation of the fit was performed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov (K–S) test. The log-normal fit suggests a non-linear relationship between the source of variation and the resulting electrical characteristics. This interpretation is supported by findings in refs. 46,47, which report a non-linear dependence of the rectification factor on the geometric dimensions (length, height, and width) of nanofluidic diodes.

We attribute these variations primarily to inconsistencies in the diode junction dimensions, in particular related to Rp. Given that the solution, electrodes, and microchannel geometry can be controlled with precision, we assumed a constant Re. Instead of viewing components as isolated, they are evaluated based on the ways they affect the overall circuit. As circuits get more complex, predicting these interactions and their impact on performance becomes more difficult. The design process centers on achieving the desired circuit output by managing these interactions.

Implementing a fully iontronic circuit that functions independently of traditional electronic components would likely be a groundbreaking step forward17. This approach opens up new pathways for energy-efficient, biocompatible, and potentially self-sustaining systems that leverage ion-based principles without the constraints of conventional electronics. One theoretical solution is an iontronic circuit designed for harvesting radio frequency (RF) energy. The key feature of this system is a diode bridge (rectifier bridge), which ensures that the current flows in only one direction (Fig. 5d), and allows both halves of the AC waveform to be used, thus producing a pulsating DC output that can be then converted to a direct current using a capacitor. In ref. 49 A possible implementation of such ionic rectifier based on nanochannels was presented by J. Li et al.44.

Currently, the response times of the existing iontronic diodes are excessively long, and do not meet typical RF frequency requirements. However, the developed simulations enabled us to explore the maximum operating frequency and assess how changes in the diode’s resistance or capacitance can impact its performance.

In the current diode characterization, the maximum operating frequency is 0.1mHz. Therefore, as shown in Fig. 5e, the output signal (with the Cp multiplication equal to 1) fails to follow the input signal (with an input signal frequency of 0.1 Hz). However, reducing the diode’s capacitance (Cp) by a factor of 1000 can increase the maximum operating frequency to 0.1 Hz and enhance the circuit’s functionality.

Conclusions

This paper presents a design approach for iontronic integrated circuits, inspired by electronic design methodologies. As a foundational step, we introduced a compact model for an iontronic diode. Although demonstrated here with iontronic bipolar diodes, this method can be extend to other iontronic components. The model, based on the diode’s physical properties and observed behavior, was implemented using Verilog-A within the Spectre circuit simulator, allowing integration with standard VLSI tools.

The simulation results provide insights into the behavior of diode-based iontronic circuits, and emphasize how the parameters of individual components affect circuit interactions and overall performance. Incorporating a Monte Carlo technique from electronic circuit design allows the model to account for variability within iontronic circuits.

Expanding circuit simulation tools to include iontronic components supports the design and analysis of iontronic circuits and allows evaluation of their potential use in applications such as energy harvesting and logic circuits. By improving component uniformity, optimizing diode parameters and better understanding diode characteristics, we can achieve more complex and efficient iontronic systems, thus moving closer to practical and groundbreaking applications in the field.

Validating complex circuits using experimental data from fabricated implementations is an important step in developing accurate models. In this work, we evaluated our diode model and circuit simulation flow using a fabricated circuit consisting of five cascading gates (15 diodes). The validated model was then used to simulate circuits of greater complexity, such as a dual-rail 3-to-8 decoder comprising 24 gates—an architecture that remains challenging to implement experimentally. Implementing circuits with more than a few diodes and cascaded gates would require improvements in diode-to-diode variability (standard deviation) and rectification performance, as indicated by the simulation results in Fig. 4d. These findings highlight the utility of a reliable simulation tool for identifying design constraints and supporting further circuit development. In future work, we aim to explore the implementation and experimental evaluation of the simulated architectures, such as decoders and diode bridges, and to extend the simulation framework to larger-scale systems comprising potentially hundreds of components.

The developed model, which includes an integer-order, voltage-dependent capacitor, reproduces key features of the transient response of a single diode and captures the switching time scale of the logic gate. At the same time, a deviation is observed between the model and measurements during the transition from forward to reverse bias. This may be related to memory effects, and reducing the diffusion length of ions from the junction to the interconnected microchannel could help mitigate these effects and reduce discrepancies between the model and experimental data, thereby improving overall accuracy. In a complementary approach, precise modeling of the full dynamic behavior of the diode—including its memory characteristics—would enhance the simulation tool’s capabilities. This, in turn, could open the door to a range of advanced circuit designs. For example, it could enable the development of iontronic logic gates with built-in memory—circuits that exhibit functionalities not found in their electronic counterparts.

The development of a predictive, physics-based compact model that incorporates parameters such as device geometry, membrane permselectivity, and solution ion concentrations offers advantages in terms of reliability and scalability. In this context, the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and current–voltage (I–V) measurements conducted in this work provide important insights into the underlying processes of the diode. Notably, the results indicate that a fractional-order element should be incorporated in future models to more accurately describe the diode’s behavior, particularly in accounting for memory effects and parasitic resistances in the microfluidic interconnects. While various approaches exist to model such fractional elements in circuit simulators, further work is needed to determine the best implementation — especially for voltage-dependent fractional capacitors. In addition, to enhance the model’s accuracy and applicability, further extensions can be developed, for example, adapting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${R}_{p}$$\end{document}Rp to include temperature-dependent factors in order to model thermal effects.

Another critical area for future work involves developing simulation tools that address the dynamics of the current caused by the movement of multiple ion types in iontronic components. Since different ions can simultaneously contribute to the electric current—each potentially moving at its own velocity—new, complex behaviors may emerge in iontronic circuits that are not yet fully explored in current simulations or applications. Overcoming the challenges of designing circuits that leverage this complexity will be essential for advancing iontronic technology.
