TIMELESS deficiency sensitizes thyroid cancer to cisplatin treatment via the DNA damage repair pathway
Authors: Chaoyu Wang, Ping Yuan, Dai Yang, Boke Liu, Juyong Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlight•DNA damage repair (DDR) pathway was activated in thyroid cancer under cisplatin treatment.•TIMELESS, a circadian rhythm gene, was correlated with the cisplatin sensitivity in thyroid cancer.•TIMELESS knockdown could suppress the activation of DDR pathway.•4.TIMELESS deficiency significantly enhanced the efficacy of cisplatin treatment in vitro and in vivo.

Introduction

Malignant tumor progression often involves DNA damage accumulation. DNA damage repair (DDR) pathways are responsible for repairing these damages, thus maintaining genomic stability and cell survival. Aberrations in DDR pathways are associated with tumor progression and sensitivity to cisplatin [1]. Cisplatin is widely utilized clinically and is often part of comprehensive treatment regimens for various types of cancer. However, thyroid cancer [2], with rapid incidence [3,4] and significant medical burden [5], is clinically resistant to cisplatin treatment [[6], [7], [8]]. Understanding the resistance mechanism and developing potential sensitizing strategies is critical for improving thyroid cancer patient prognosis [9].

Circadian rhythm refers to the physiological and behavioral variations over a 24-h cycle, which help adapt to daily environmental changes [10,11]. This complicated fluctuation can impact various physiological processes such as sleep, eating patterns, and body temperature regulation [[12], [13], [14]]. Previous studies have showed that circadian rhythm can significantly influence the malignant progression in various cancers [15,16].

Considering the key role of endocrine and metabolic function of the thyroid gland, dysregulation of circadian rhythm genes may significantly affect thyroid cancer, compared to other non-endocrine malignancies [17,18]. Previous clinical studies have indicated that circadian disruption correlates with the malignant transformation of thyroid nodules [19] and revealed the potential role of circadian genes in thyroid cancer prognosis and therapeutic sensitivity [20]. Meanwhile, those with sleep disturbance have a significantly increased risk of thyroid cancer [21,22]. However, there is a lack of research regarding the correlation between circadian rhythms and cisplatin resistance in thyroid cancer. The proposed study aims to investigate the association between circadian rhythms and the sensitivity of thyroid cancer to cisplatin therapy, and try to improve the efficacy of cisplatin treatment by intervening circadian rhythms.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cell culture and reagents

Human thyroid epithelium cell line (Nthy-ori 3-1) was purchased from China Center for Type Culture Collection (Wuhan, China). Thyroid cancer cell lines BCPAP, KTC-1, IHH-4, and TPC-1 were conserved by the Institute of Clinical Medicine, Zhejiang Provincial People's Hospital. All cell lines were authenticated by STR/DNA profiling by BIOWING BIOTECHNOLOGY (Shanghai, China). All cell lines were cultured in RPMI-1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, KEL Biotech, China). Antibiotics were added to media formulations. Cells were cultured at 37°C in 5% CO2. All cells were preserved at -80°C using CELLSAVING (New Cell & Molecular Biotech, China). Cisplatin was purchased from Selleck (USA), and dissolved in DMF and diluted with medium to the desired concentration.

Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

CCK-8 assay (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) was utilized to confirm the cytotoxicity of cisplatin on cancer cells. Cells were plated at 5,000 cells and were treated with cisplatin at different concentration for 24 h. At the test point, 100 μl CCK-8 was added and then the viability values were detected by spectrometer (BIO TEK, China).

Colony formation assay

Cancer cells (1000 per well) were seeded in six-well plates and incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 for 24 h. The 6-well plates were washed 3 times with sterile PBS to remove the exfoliated cells. Then, cisplatin was added into the treatment groups. One week later, 500ul paraformaldehyde was added to each well for 30min fixation, then the fixative was discarded and replaced by 500μl of crystalline violet dye per well. After 30 min staining, clone number could be calculated.

Transwell assay

Migration and invasion assay were performed using Transwell Permeable Plate (LABSELECT, China, 6.5mm). Serum-free medium (200μl) containing 5 × 104 cells were added to the upper chamber, and 700 µl serum-containing medium with cisplatin were added to the lower chamber. After incubation of 24 or 48h, cells were stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min. The number of migrating cells was normalized to the number of total cells and was calculated per microscopic field. The mean number was estimated by counting average cells in five visual fields of three independent experiments.

Western blot (WB) analysis

WB was performed as previous described [23]. Briefly, proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Tanon, China). After the block with 5% bovine serum albumin for 2 h, membranes were incubated at 4°C overnight with primary antibodies. Blots were probed with: anti-phospho ATR (Ser428) (2583, 1:500), anti-ATR (13934, 1:1000), anti- phosphor ATM (Ser1981) (5883, 1:500), anti-ATM (2873, 1:1000), anti-phospho Chk1 (Ser345) (2348, 1:500), anti-Chk1 (2360, 1:1000), anti-phospho Chk2 (Thr68) (2197, 1:500), anti-Chk2 (6334, 1:1000) (Cell Signalling Technology, USA), anti-TIMELESS (ab109512, 1:500), and GAPDH (ab8245, 1:10000). Goat anti-rabbit or anti-mouse horseradishperoxidase-conjugated IgG was used as secondary antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology). Membranes were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence detection system (Bio-Rad). Band density was quantified using NIH Image J. Three independent experiments were replicated.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

PCR was performed as previous described [24]. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol® Reagent (Ambion). The purity and concentration of RNA was quantified with a UV-Vis spectrophotometer (NanoDrop 1000, USA). cDNA was synthesized with a reverse transcription kit (Toyobo Life Science). Quantitative amplification was achieved using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Toyobo Life Science). Relative RNA expression was calculated normalized to GAPDH.

Generation of gene overexpression and knockdown cells

Gene knockdown of TIMELESS was achieved by siRNA. The stable TIMELESS-knockdown cells with monoclonal (shRNA lentivirus; selection with puromycin for 2 weeks) were used in mouse experiments. Transfection was performed as described before [25]. After cells were seeded into 6-well plates at 80% concentration, plasmids were transfected using Lipofectamine™ 3000 Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen, L3000001) for 48 h according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Apoptosis signaling antibody array

Human Apoptosis Signaling Array C1 (RayBio® C-Series) was employed to analyze the expression of target molecules. BCPAP with TIMELESS knockdown or control was treated with cisplatin or control for 24 h. Cell lysates were harvested and then measured as manufacturer's instructions. Raw images were visualized by chemiluminescence detection kit (Millipore, Billerica, MA) and analyzed by Image Lab Software (Bio-Rad). Two replicates were included.

In vivo Xenograft tumor model

Thyroid cancer xenograft models in nude mice were established [26]. Three-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were purchased from Shanghai slac laboratory animal co. ltd. All experiments were performed following the official recommendations of the Chinese Society of Zoology, and animals received humane care according to the standards listed in the Ethical Review Form for Laboratory Animal Welfare. Suspensions containing the stable TIMELESS-knockdown cells (BCPAP/shControl and BCPAP/shTIMELESS cells) were subcutaneously injected into the right flank of the nude mice. Mice were divided into six groups (group 1: BCPAP/shControl treated with control medium; group 2: BCPAP/shTIMELESS treated with control medium; group 3: BCPAP/shControl treated with cisplatin 2 mg/kg weekly; group 4: BCPAP/shControl treated with cisplatin 4 mg/kg weekly; group 5: BCPAP/shTIMELESS treated with cisplatin 2 mg/kg weekly; group 6: BCPAP/shTIMELESS treated with cisplatin 4 mg/kg weekly). Tumor size was measured using vernier calipers and the volume was calculated using formula: V=W2*L/0.5. Mice were euthanized by cervical decapitation after five weeks, and tumors were removed, weighed and fixed in formalin.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were processed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. One-way ANOVA and Student’s t test were chosen for comparison among groups. Categorical data were evaluated with the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Pearson correlation coefficient was used to analyze the correlations. p < 0.05 were considered significant.

RESULTS

As shown in Fig. 1D, the minimal increase in ROS level was observed in BCPAP and KTC-1 under cisplatin treatment (1 μg/mL). Considering that the aberrations in DDR pathways are closely associated with the sensitivity to cisplatin, we further evaluated the expression of DDR markers under cisplatin treatment. Higher levels of p-ATR, p-ATM, p-CHK1 and p-CHK2 were detected in thyroid cancer cells that are not sensitive to cisplatin, such as BCPAP (Fig. 1E) and KTC-1 (Fig. 1F), than in sensitive cell lines (TPC-1/IHH-4, supplementary Fig. 1). This phenomenon indicated that DDR pathway activation might compromise the sensitivity of cisplatin to thyroid cancer cell lines.

TIMELESS level was correlated with the sensitivity to cisplatin

The most important target that affects cisplatin efficacy was screened among circadian rhythm genes using the Biomarker Exploration of Solid Tumors (BEST) database [27]. Interestingly, only TIMELESS was correlated with the sensitivity to cisplatin in thyroid cancer (Fig. 2A). This relevance was not observed in other circadian rhythm genes, such as ARNTL, CLOCK, PER2, PER3, RORA and TIPIN (supplementary Fig. 2). Higher TIMELESS level indicated a higher IC50 value in different six datasets (GSE5364, GSE33630, GSE58545, GSE60542, GSE82208, TCGA-THCA, Fig. 2B). To verify the potential correlation between TIMELESS and cisplatin sensitivity, the basal production of TIMELESS in thyroid cancer cells was assayed by WB and PCR. Compared to other cell lines, BCPAP and KTC-1 presented higher TIMELESS levels in both protein and RNA samples (Fig. 2C-D). In addition, the TIMELESS level was positively correlated with IC50 and AUC levels (p = 0.0218 and 0.0240, respectively, Fig. 2E-F). We further found that TIMELESS expression significantly increased in BCPAP and KTC-1 cell lines upon cisplatin treatment, suggesting stress-induced upregulation (supplementary Fig. 3B). Moreover, we conducted TIMELESS expression assay every 4h over 24h in BCPAP and KTC-1 cell lines, and the oscillating phenomena were not detected (supplementary Fig. 3C).

TIMELESS was clinically correlated with thyroid malignancy

The clinical relevance between TIMELESS and malignant signatures of thyroid cancer was further explored using the BEST database. TIMELESS level was significantly higher in tumor tissue than in the corresponding peritumor normal tissue in GSE5364, GSE33630, GSE58545, GSE60542, GSE82208 and TCGA-THCA datasets (Fig. 3A-B). Higher expression of TIMELESS was observed in more poorly differentiated type, the lymphocyte reaction group, and the BRAF WT group (Fig. 3C-D, supplementary Fig. 3E). TIMELESS was also positively correlated with thyroid cancer staging, including T stage (Fig. 3E-F, supplementary Fig. 3D). Moreover, other malignant signatures, such as capsular effraction, extrathyroidal extension, tumor number, vascular invasion and patient age were also detected (supplementary Fig. 3F-I). TIMELESS knockdown enhanced the efficacy of cisplatin treatment in vitro

To explore the function of TIMELESS in thyroid cancer, we initially knocked down the expression of TIMELESS in BCPAP and KTC-1 (Fig. 4A-D). Then, BCPAP/siTIMELSSS and KTC-1/siTIMELESS cells and their corresponding control cells were treated with cisplatin in a series of concentrations. The CCK-8 results indicated that TIMELESS knockdown could increase the cytotoxicity of cisplatin (Fig. 4E). The IC50 and AUC values of cisplatin in BCPAP/siTIMELESS and KTC-1/siTIMELESS cells were lower than those in their control cells (Fig. 4F-G). Furthermore, ROS level could be significantly increased upon TIMELESS knockdown under cisplatin treatment (Fig. 4H). Additionally, we overexpressed TIMELESS in TPC-1 cells, which is relatively more cisplatin-sensitive than BCPAP and KTC-1. The CCK-8 results indicated that TIMELESS overexpression could significantly increase IC50 to cisplatin in TPC-1 cells (supplementary Fig. 4A-B). We further determined whether TIMELESS could affect the cellular phenotypes under cisplatin treatment. Notably, cisplatin alone could hardly inhibit the colony formation, migration and invasion ability of BCPAP and KTC-1 cells. However, TIMELESS knockdown could significantly enhance the efficacy of cisplatin (Fig. 4I-K). In addition, in TIMELESS-knockdown cells, the sensitivity of paclitaxel was not changed (supplementary Fig. 4C-D), supporting the specific role of TIMELESS in stabilizing replication fork of DNA. Overall, TIMELESS deficiency could sensitize BCPAP and KTC-1 cells to cisplatin in vitro.

TIMELESS knockdown could impair the DDR pathway activation mediated by cisplatin

To determine the mechanism pattern of TIMELESS on thyroid cancer, GSEA hallmark analysis was performed using the BEST database. We found that pathways concerning DNA repair and replication were significantly enriched (Fig. 5A-B, supplementary Fig. 4E-L). Then, the expression levels of the DDR pathway components were assayed after TIMELESS knockdown in BCPAP and KTC-1 cells. The levels of p-ATR, p-ATM, p-CHK1 and p-CHK2 were decreased in BCPAP/siTIMELESS and KTC-1/siTIMELESS cells, compared with their corresponding control groups (Fig. 5C-D). TIMELESS is known to impact the DDR pathway through the maintenance of replication fork stability via physically interacting with DDR proteins, such as CHK1 and ATR complex [[28], [29], [30]]. TIMELESS deficiency sensitizes thyroid cancer to cisplatin treatment in vivo

Considering the in vitro findings, a xenograft model was used to validate the role of TIMELESS in cisplatin-treated thyroid cancer (Fig. 7A-B). Comparison with the control group indicated that treatment with cisplatin (2 mg/kg or 4 mg/kg) alone decreased tumor weight and volume. Although TIMELESS knockdown could hardly inhibit tumor growth, it could significantly increase the therapeutic effect of cisplatin at both 2 mg/kg and 4 mg/kg concentrations (Fig. 7C-E). DISCUSSION

Circadian rhythm is considered important for tumor progression [31,32]. We first confirm that TIMELESS, a circadian rhythm gene, can closely impact cisplatin sensitivity in thyroid cancer via the DDR pathway (Fig. 7F), which is different from previous interpretations [33].

The alternation of light and dark cycles and temperature changes in the environment is the theoretical basis for the maintenance of the circadian cycle [10]. Some reports suggest an interaction mechanism between circadian rhythm and tumor microenvironment; and circadian dysrhythmia can induce the accumulation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and dysfunctional CD8+ T cells in the lungs, which accelerates the metastasis of colorectal cancer to the lungs [15]. Moreover, fatty acid oxidation can act as a circadian rhythm sensor, which can accelerate tumor growth when it senses circadian disorders [32]. Reportedly, TIMELESS depletion can inhibit cell proliferation and increase cisplatin sensitivity in cervical cancer [34], and can impact the cell cycle in colon cancer [35]. These findings indicate that TIMELESS may play a critical role in tumorigenesis [36]. In this study, we systematically screened the circadian rhythm genes, and found that TIMELESS was closely related to the sensitivity of cisplatin treatment in thyroid cancer. TIMELESS knockdown significantly enhanced the efficacy of cisplatin treatment in vitro and in vivo. Thus, TIMELESS may be a newly identified target for thyroid cancer treatment.

Interestingly, other circadian rhythm genes, such as ARNTL, CLOCK, PER2, PER3, RORA and TIPIN, were not associated with cisplatin sensitivity. What makes TIMELESS so special? The non-oscillating nature of TIMELESS may represent the underlying distinction that enables it to confer cisplatin resistance, whereas other circadian genes with oscillating properties cannot. Our study found that TIMELESS could regulate the DDR pathway, which was the molecular basis of synthetic lethal therapeutics [37,38]. Recent studies identified that TIMELESS was involved in the PARP inhibitor-mediated synthetic lethal process [29]. In homologous recombination (HR)-deficient cancer cells, the PARP inhibitors do not depend on their ability to retain PARP proteins on DNA, but on their ability to inhibit the catalytic activity of PARP. Specifically, PARP inhibitors inhibit the catalytic ability of PARP, resulting in its inability to transmit transcription-replication conflicts to TIMELESS and TIPIN, resulting in DNA damage, which is synthetically lethal in HR-deficient cancer cells [29]. Some targets, such as NRDE2, could promote the homologous recombination repair pathway, and its defect has been found to form synthetic lethal effects with PARP inhibitors, such as Olaparib, in hepatic cancer [39]. Considering that DDR markers were activated under cisplatin treatment, similar to NRDE2, we found that TIMELESS deficiency could improve cisplatin efficacy through the DDR pathway in thyroid cancer.

CONCLUSION

TIMELESS, a circadian rhythm gene, might be a newly identified synthetic lethal target, and may help develop new therapeutic strategies to sensitize thyroid cancer to cisplatin. Our preclinical findings can be used as a proof to demonstrate the importance of circadian rhythm in thyroid cancer treatment.
