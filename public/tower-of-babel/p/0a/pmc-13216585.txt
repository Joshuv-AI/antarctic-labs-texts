Genome-wide identification and expression analysis of HMAs gene family in tobacco under abiotic stress
Authors: Baorui Huang, Fugui Wang, Gang Luo, Qiuliang Yuan, Jin Huang, Zhenhua Wang, Xinfa Wang, Shiying Nong, Yachun Lu, Junlin Li, Fenglin Huang, Qi Zhao, Chunlai Wu, Huanhuan Wang, Zongcan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe heavy metal ATPase (HMA) family, classified as P1B-ATPases, plays a pivotal role in maintaining plant metal homeostasis by coupling ATP hydrolysis to the active transport of metal ions across biological membranes. However, comprehensive characterization of the HMA family in tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum) remains insufficient. In this study, we conducted a systematic genome-wide bioinformatic analysis and identified 27 HMA-encoding genes in tobacco, designated NtHMA1 through NtHMA27. Phylogenetic and structural analyses revealed that these genes fall into two major clades, further resolved into six evolutionarily conserved subfamilies. Chromosomal localization showed that NtHMA genes are predominantly distributed on chromosomes 1, 17, and 25, and all retain hallmark domains characteristic of the HMA family. Comparative genomic analysis identified ten segmentally duplicated NtHMA gene pairs, all exhibiting signatures of purifying selection (Ka/Ks < 1), suggesting functional constraint and evolutionary conservation. Cis-regulatory element analysis uncovered 14 types of stress-responsive cis-acting elements associated with responses to biotic and abiotic stresses. Integrated transcriptomic profiling across multiple abiotic stress conditions and heavy metal treatments indicated that NtHMA6, NtHMA14, and NtHMA23 are preferentially upregulated under heavy metal exposure, implicating their roles in metal transport and detoxification, while NtHMA2, NtHMA7, NtHMA10, NtHMA11, NtHMA13, NtHMA18, NtHMA22, and NtHMA25 show significant transcriptional responsiveness to abiotic stresses or hormone stimuli, suggesting involvement in stress adaptation. In summary, this study provides the first comprehensive genomic, structural, evolutionary, and expression-based characterization of the tobacco NtHMA family, establishing a foundation resource for functional genomics studies and offering valuable insights to guide molecular breeding strategies aimed at enhancing tobacco resilience and metal tolerance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45494-1.Keywords: NtHMAs, Heavy metal ATPase, Abiotic stress, Gene expression, Synteny and evolutionSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Genetics, Molecular biology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum), an important allopolyploid economic crop and model plant in the Solanaceae family with a chromosome constitution of 2n = 4x = 48, originated from interspecific hybridization between Nicotiana sylvestris (2n = 2x = 24) and Nicotiana tomentosiformis (2n = 2x = 24) followed by chromosome doubling events1–3. Currently, tobacco is widely cultivated in more than 120 countries and regions around the world, making significant contributions to global economic development. However, tobacco is highly sensitive to environmental stresses. During its growth cycle, it frequently encounters abiotic stresses such as heavy metal pollution, extreme temperatures, and drought, as well as biotic stresses like pathogen infections4–6. These stresses can lead to inhibited growth or even death of tobacco, resulting in severe production losses. In particular, against the backdrop of intensified global climate change and frequent extreme weather events, the stress-induced damage faced by tobacco has further escalated, leading to increasingly severe economic losses. Therefore, in-depth research on the stress resistance mechanisms of tobacco holds significant theoretical and practical significance for enhancing its environmental adaptability and promoting the breeding of elite tobacco varieties.

Membrane transporters are proteins localized on membranes and are involved in the transport of different types of molecules7. Transporters usually exhibit specificity for specific ligands; thus, specific transporters transport specific molecules or a group of structurally related molecules8. In plants, heavy metal transporters include heavy metal ATPases (HMA)9, ATP-binding cassette proteins (ABC)10, copper transporters (COPT)11, natural resistance-associated macrophage proteins (NRAMP)12, metal tolerance proteins (MTP)13, zinc-iron transporters (ZIP)14, and yellow stripe-like proteins (YSL)15.

HMA (heavy metal ATPase) is a member of the P1B-ATPase family and plays a key role in the transport and detoxification of heavy metals in plants16. It can selectively absorb and transport essential metal ions (e.g., Cu2⁺, Zn2⁺) required for plant growth and development, while also participating in the translocation of non-essential heavy metal ions such as Cd2⁺ and Pb2⁺17. According to the substrate specificity of the transported metal ions, P1B-ATPases are classified into two main groups: Zn/Co/Cd/Pb-ATPases (zinc clade) and Cu/Ag-ATPases (copper clade), both of which contain the E1-E2_ATPase and Hydrolase domains18,19. A more comprehensive phylogenetic analysis, based on similarity measures of amino acid sequences, has further divided the HMA proteins of Arabidopsis and rice into six distinct subclasses (1–6)20,21. In Arabidopsis, Subclass 1 (AtHMA1) is localized in chloroplasts and plays an important role in the transport of various heavy metals, such as zinc, copper, cobalt, and calcium, and is also involved in Cd2⁺ detoxification22; Subclass 2 (AtHMA2-4) is crucial for the transport of toxic heavy metals like cadmium and lead23; Subclass 3 (AtHMA5) is mainly expressed in roots and alleviates copper toxicity by exporting Cu ions from the cytoplasm24,25; Subclass 4 (AtHMA7) is a copper-induced gene that plays a vital role in maintaining copper homeostasis26; both Subclass 5 (AtHMA6) and Subclass 6 (AtHMA8) are localized in chloroplasts, possess high-affinity copper transport capacity, and control copper transport in chloroplasts as well as deliver copper to the chloroplast stroma and thylakoids, thereby maintaining photosynthetic and antioxidant functions19,27,28.

Currently, HMA genes have been identified in various plant species, such as 8 in Arabidopsis thaliana21, 9 in Oryza sativa29, 17 in Populus trichocarpa17, 11 in Sorghum bicolor30, 20 in Glycine max31, 23 in Brassica napus32, 48 in Solanum lycopersicum33, 14 in Raphanus sativus34, 11 in Camellia sinensis cv. Fenghuang Dancong35, 37 in Phytolacca americana36 and 28 in Triticum aestivum37. Although previous studies have reported on the functions of HMA genes in tobacco–such as NtHMAa/b and HMA4.1/2, which influence zinc and cadmium transport in tobacco38,39—a systematic analysis of the HMA gene family in tobacco has not yet been conducted. In this study, using bioinformatics and omics strategies, we systematically identified the NtHMA family genes in cigar tobacco, analyzed their phylogenetic relationships, gene structures, conserved domains, chromosomal distributions, intra- and interspecies collinearity, and cis-acting elements, and focused on investigating the expression characteristics of these genes in different tissues and under various stress conditions. This study provides a theoretical basis for subsequent analysis of HMA genes in tobacco and offers candidate genes for further functional verification or utilization.

Results

Genome-wide identification of HMAs in tobacco

To identify members of the NtHMA family in tobacco, this study used 8 AtHMA protein sequences from Arabidopsis thaliana and 9 OsHMA protein sequences from Oryza sativa as bait sequences to screen for HMA sequences in the tobacco genome (Supplementary Table S1)23,25. Through screening with multiple databases including Pfam, CDD, SMART, and NCBI BLAST, a total of 27 NtHMAs were finally identified and named NtHMA1-27 (Supplementary Table S2). Analysis of the physicochemical properties of NtHMA proteins showed that their amino acid counts ranged from 242 (NtHMA25) to 266 (NtHMA12), with molecular weights varying from 26.73 kDa (NtHMA25) to 286.91 kDa (NtHMA12), and isoelectric points spanning 4.99 (NtHMA8) to 9.21(NtHMA17). Subcellular localization prediction using the WoLF PSORT database indicated that all 27 NtHMAs were localized to the cell membrane, suggesting that NtHMAs may be involved in the transport of substances or molecules in tobacco.

Phylogenetic and comparative analyses of NtHMAs

To explore the phylogenetic relationships of HMAs, this study performed evolutionary analysis using 44 HMA protein sequences from Oryza sativa, Arabidopsis thaliana, and tobacco. Based on the classification and naming conventions of HMA genes in Arabidopsis thaliana23, the NtHMAs in tobacco were classified into two major clades: Cu/Ag and Zn/Cd/Pb/Co. Furthermore, they could be subdivided into six subgroups, designated as Subgroup 1 to Subgroup 6 (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table S3). Among these, the Cu/Ag clade comprises Subfamilies 1 and 2, containing a total of 5 genes; the Zn/Cd/Pb/Co clade includes Subfamilies 3, 4, 5, and 6, encompassing a total of 22 genes. Subfamily 3 contained the highest number of NtHMA members (12), whereas Subfamilies 2, 5, and 6 each contained the fewest (only 2 genes each). Evolutionary analysis inferred that HMA members within the same subfamily across Oryza sativa, Arabidopsis thaliana, and tobacco likely share similar biological functions.

Phylogenetic analysis of HMAs from tobacco, Arabidopsis and rice. Using MEGA software, a Neighbor-Joining (NJ) phylogenetic tree was constructed with 27 NtHMA, 8 AtHMA, and 9 OsHMA sequences, and 1000 bootstrap replicates were performed. The NtHMAs were classified according to the classification method of AtHMAs in Arabidopsis thaliana.

Conserved motifs analysis of the NtHMAs

To investigate the conserved motifs of NtHMAs, this study analyzed all NtHMA protein sequences using the MEME database and functionally annotated these conserved motifs via the InterPro database. As shown in Fig. 2, Motifs 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 8 were all identified as possessing characteristics of the P-type cation-transporting ATPase family (P-TYPE CATION-TRANSPORTING ATPASE); Motif 7 was found to exhibit features of the P-type cation-transporting ATPase superfamily (ATP7, ISOFORM B); while no family-specific characteristics were detected for Motifs 9 and 10. Analysis of conserved motifs among HMAs from tobacco, Arabidopsis thaliana, and rice shows that each of the 27 NtHMA proteins contained at least one canonical motif characteristic of the P-type cation-transporting ATPase family (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Conserved motif analysis of 27 NtHMAs in tobacco. Conserved motifs of NtHMAs were identified using the MEME database and functionally annotated through the InterPro database. Gray lines represent non-conserved regions, and the 10 differently colored boxes below indicate different conserved motifs.

Further analysis revealed that most members of the NtHMA Subfamily 3 (except NtHMA3 and NtHMA27) contain 10 motifs, and all 12 members of this subfamily share a conserved motif arrangement order: Motif 3-2-5-10-1-9-8. Members of Subfamily 4 contain 3 to 10 motifs, with a core arrangement of Motif 6-3-2. Members of Subfamily 1 (except NtHMA17) harbor 4 core motifs arranged in the order of Motif 2-5-9-7; Subfamily 2 members contain 6 motifs arranged as Motif 2-5-1-9-8-7. Subfamily 6 contains 5 to 8 motifs with a conserved sequence of Motif 3-2-5-1-9. Among the 27 NtHMAs, each sequence contains at least one characteristic motif of P-type cation-transporting ATPases, indicating the correctness of the identified tobacco HMAs.

These results demonstrate that members within the same subfamily exhibit similar conserved motif arrangements, further validating the reliability of the classification.

Gene structure analysis of the HMAs in tobacco

To explore the gene structure of tobacco NtHMAs, we retrieved NtHMA-related information from the tobacco genome annotation file and visualized the data using the GSDS database. As shown in Fig. 3, the number of exons in Subfamily 3 ranges from 5 (NtHMA-5, -24) to 10 (NtHMA27), in Subfamily 4 from 5 (NtHMA14) to 15 (NtHMA18), in Subfamily 1 from 14 (NtHMA9) to 24 (NtHMA16), in Subfamily 2 from 11 (NtHMA19) to 26 (NtHMA12), in Subfamily 5 from 21 (NtHMA17) to 22 (NtHMA7), and in Subfamily 6 from 14 (NtHMA11) to 18 (NtHMA21). Among them, NtHMA24 in Subfamily 3 has the fewest exons (5), while NtHMA12 in Subfamily 2 has the most exons (26). In summary, these results indicate that genes within the same subfamily exhibit similar exon numbers.

Chromosome localization, duplication, and collinearity analysis of the NtHMAs

To investigate the chromosomal distribution of the tobacco NtHMAs, we used TBtools to perform a visual analysis of the positions of tobacco NtHMA family members. The results showed that the 27 members of the tobacco NtHMA family are scattered across 9 chromosomes and the Un00390 chromosome fragment (Fig. 4). Among them, chromosome 1 contains the largest number of genes, with 7 NtHMA members (NtHMA1-NtHMA7); chromosomes 15 and 19 contain the fewest genes, with each chromosome harboring only one tobacco NtHMA gene, specifically NtHMA11 and NtHMA21, respectively. In addition, the distribution positions of NtHMA genes on different chromosomes exhibit relative conservation. For example, genes on chromosome 1 are mostly concentrated in the upper region, while genes on chromosomes 19 and 23 are primarily located in the lower region.

To study the duplication relationships among members of the NtHMAs, we used TBtools to analyze intra-specific collinearity in tobacco. As indicated by the red lines in Figs. 5 and 10 segmental duplication gene pairs were identified among the 27 NtHMA family members: NtHMA7/NtHMA22, NtHMA3/NtHMA23, NtHMA8/NtHMA10, NtHMA9/NtHMA16, NtHMA8/NtHMA15, NtHMA10/NtHMA18, NtHMA10/NtHMA15, NtHMA11/NtHMA21, NtHMA12/NtHMA19, and NtHMA15/NtHMA18. The Ka/Ks calculations revealed that the Ka/Ks ratios of all 10 duplicated gene pairs are less than 1, indicating they are under purifying selection pressure (Supplementary Table S4).

Analysis of cis-acting elements in promoters of the NtHMAs

To investigate the cis-acting elements in the upstream regions of tobacco NtHMA genes, this study utilized the PlantCARE database to predict the functions of cis-acting elements within the 2000 bp nucleotide sequences upstream of NtHMA genes, followed by visualization using TBtools software. We retrieved the promoter sequences of all 27 NtHMA genes and identified 248 cis-acting elements associated with hormones, transcription factors, and abiotic stresses. As shown in Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table S6, the hormone-responsive elements in the promoters primarily include: abscisic acid (ABA)-responsive elements (61, 25%), methyl jasmonate (MeJA)-responsive elements (48, 19%), gibberellin (GA)-responsive elements (30, 12%), auxin-responsive elements (15, 6%), and salicylic acid (SA)-responsive elements (8, 3%). Additionally, the promoters contain: MYB binding sites (including drought-induced MYB binding sites, light-responsive MYB binding sites, MYBHv1 binding sites, and MYB binding sites regulating flavonoid biosynthesis; total 56, 23%), defense and stress-responsive elements (15, 6%), low-temperature responsive elements (12, 5%), and wound-responsive elements (3, 1%). These results suggest that the tobacco NtHMA families may respond to induction by multiple hormones and play a significant role in both plant growth and development, as well as abiotic stress resistance.

Expression profiles of NtHMA genes in different tissues of cigar tobacco

To investigate the expression patterns of NtHMA genes in cigar tobacco tissues, we performed transcriptome sequencing analysis on eight different tissues (root, flower, upper stem, middle stem, lower stem, upper leaf, middle leaf, and lower leaf) of cigar tobacco using the RNA-Seq method, and obtained tissue-specific expression data for all 27 NtHMA genes. As shown in Fig. 8, among the eight tissues, the number of NtHMA genes with high expression levels (FPKM ≥ 10) was as follows: 1 in the root (NtHMA10), 4 in the flower (NtHMA9, NtHMA10, NtHMA12, and NtHMA22), 1 in the upper stem (NtHMA13), 1 in the middle stem (NtHMA22), 0 in the lower stem, 3 in the upper leaf (NtHMA9, NtHMA16, and NtHMA22), 3 in the middle leaf (NtHMA9, NtHMA10, and NtHMA15), and 2 in the lower leaf (NtHMA9 and NtHMA10). Further analysis revealed that during the flowering stage of cigar tobacco, the number of highly expressed NtHMAs in flowers and leaves was significantly higher than that in stems and roots. Meanwhile, three tobacco NtHMAs (NtHMA9, NtHMA10, and NtHMA22) exhibited high expression in at least three tobacco tissues. These results suggest that multiple NtHMA genes play important roles in the flowers, leaves, stems, and roots of cigar tobacco during the flowering stage.

Expression profiles of NtHMA genes under various abiotic treatments

To study the expression patterns of NtHMAs in cigar tobacco under various treatments (including hormone treatments (MeJA, MT, BR, SA, ABA, and IAA), environmental stresses (H₂O₂, NaCl, PEG, Cold, and Heat), heavy metals treatments (Cd, Cu, Cr, and Pb)), we performed transcriptome sequencing analysis on cigar tobacco leaves after 15 different treatments using the RNA-Seq method, and obtained expression data for all 27 NtHMAs. Under any stress treatment, the expression level of NtHMAs is considered induced if it is greater than or equal to 2 (relative to the control, CK) at any time point, and conversely, it is considered suppressed if it is less than or equal to 0.5 (relative to the control, CK) at any time point.

In cigar tobacco, under different hormone treatments, NtHMA13 responded to all six types of hormone stress at least at one time point (Fig. 9). NtHMA7, NtHMA11, and NtHMA22 responded to MeJA treatment (Fig. 9A), while NtHMA18 responded to ABA induction, showing high expression 6 h post-treatment (Fig. 9E).

It was found that under H₂O₂ induction, NtHMA2, NtHMA6, NtHMA10, NtHMA18, and NtHMA25 were induced to be up-regulated at least at one treatment time point; under high temperature induction (Fig. 10A); under heat stress, NtHMA14 and NtHMA25 were induced to be up-regulated at least at one treatment time point (Fig. 10B); under high salt induction, NtHMA13, NtHMA14, and NtHMA25 were induced to be up-regulated at a certain time point (Fig. 10C); under drought induction, NtHMA13 was induced to be up-regulated at 12 h (Fig. 10D); under low temperature induction, NtHMA6 was induced to be up-regulated at 24 h (Fig. 10E).

In summary, NtHMA6, NtHMA13, NtHMA14, NtHMA18, and NtHMA25 all respond to at least two types of stress treatments. Notably, the NtHMA13 gene is induced under eight different stress treatments, and these candidate genes can serve as potential targets for tobacco genetic breeding. Meanwhile, we observed that NtHMA7, NtHMA11, and NtHMA22 are highly expressed only under hormone (MeJA) induction; NtHMA23 is highly expressed only under heavy metal (Pb) stress; and NtHMA2 and NtHMA10 are highly expressed only under environmental stress induced by hydrogen peroxide. These genes, which respond exclusively to specific treatments, provide a new perspective for better understanding the HMAs in tobacco.

Discussion

As important transporter proteins in plants, HMA proteins play significant roles, necessitating a systematic investigation into the characteristics of this family in tobacco8,20,21. In this study, we successfully identified 27 NtHMA family genes from the tobacco genome using bioinformatics approaches (Supplementary Table S2). The number of HMA genes in tobacco is markedly higher than that in P.trichocarpa (17)17, A.thaliana (8)21, O.sativa (9)29, S.bicolor (11)30, G.max (20)31, B.napus (23)32, R.sativus (14)34, and C.sinensis cv. Fenghuang Dancong (11)35; however, it is lower than that in S.lycopersicum (48)33, P.americana (37)36, and T.aestivum (28)37. These findings indicate that, compared with P.trichocarpa, A.thaliana, O.sativa, S.bicolor, G.max, B.napus, R.sativus, and C.sinensis cv. Fenghuang Dancong, the HMA gene family in tobacco exhibits an expansion trend; whereas, relative to S.lycopersicum, P.americana, and T.aestivum, it demonstrates a contraction trend.

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that the 27 NtHMA genes could be divided into 2 clades, which were further classified into 6 subfamilies (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S3). This classification is consistent with the previously reported grouping of HMA genes in A.thaliana21, and the clade-level classification aligns with that of P.trichocarpa17, S.bicolor30, and S.lycopersicum48, while the subfamily-level classification matches that of G.max31 and B.napus32. All 27 NtHMA proteins contain at least one typical HMA protein motif, and each subfamily exhibits a corresponding conserved motif arrangement order, further validating the reliability of the identification and classification methods (Fig. 2). The number of exons in the 27 NtHMAs ranges from 5 to 26 (Fig. 3), and members within the same subgroup show relatively similar exon counts, which is consistent with previous findings on HMA genes in P.trichocarpa and S.bicolor17,30. In summary, genes within the same subfamily exhibit similar motif positions and gene structures.

The expansion of plant gene families primarily occurs through three modes: tandem duplication of individual genes, segmental duplication of multiple genes, and whole-genome duplication40,41. Studying these modes helps to gain a deeper understanding of their evolutionary relationships. First, chromosome localization analysis of the 27 tobacco NtHMAgenes revealed that they are not uniformly distributed across all chromosomes; instead, they are exclusively located on 9 chromosomes and 1 chromosomal fragment (Un00390) (Fig. 4). This distribution pattern differs significantly from the more even distribution of the HMA gene family across all chromosomes observed in S.lycopersicum33 and Tartary buckwheat42. Subsequently, collinearity analysis software was used to perform a whole-genome collinearity analysis of genes within tobacco, with red lines specifically highlighting collinear gene pairs of NtHMAs (Fig. 5). A total of 10 segmental duplication gene pairs were identified (Supplementary Table S4). Here, Ka and Ks refer to nonsynonymous and synonymous mutation rates, respectively. Ka/Ks ratios less than, greater than, or equal to 1 correspond to purifying, positive, or neutral selection pressures, respectively43. Calculation of Ka and Ks for these 10 NtHMA gene pairs revealed that all this had Ka/Ks values < 1, indicating that these gene pairs are subject to purifying selection pressure (Supplementary Table S4). A total of 9 segmental duplication pairs were identified in S.lycopersicum, all of which had Ka/Ks values < 133. In T.aestivum, 35 duplication pairs were identified, and all of their Ka/Ks values were also < 137. Our results are largely consistent with the findings on HMA genes in tomato and wheat, indicating that the expansion of the HMA family primarily occurs through segmental duplication, and these duplicated genes have experienced purifying selection pressure. Additionally, it is particularly interesting to note that among these paralogous pairs, NtHMA9/NtHMA16 and NtHMA10/NtHMA15 both exhibit high expression in at least one common tissue (Fig. 8); NtHMA10/NtHMA 18 are induced under hydrogen peroxide stress (Fig. 9); and NtHMA7/NtHMA22 are induced by methyl jasmonate (Fig. 9). These results suggest that paralogous gene pairs may share certain similarities in gene expression under specific conditions.

Previous studies have found that HMA genes, as important transporter proteins, can transport the essential trace elements copper (Cu) and zinc (Zn) required by plants44,45. Through the expression analysis of NtHMAs in different tissues during the flowering stage of cigar tobacco, it was found that the number of NtHMA genes with high expression (FPKM ≥ 10) in flowers was higher than that in leaves, stems, and roots (Fig. 8). Additionally, the highly expressed genes in flowers, NtHMA9/12 belonging to the Cu/Ag clade and NtHMA10/22 to the Zn/Cd/Pb/Co clade (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S3), correspond to copper and zinc, both essential trace elements for plant flowering46,47. During the flowering stage of cigar tobacco, the highly expressed NtHMA13/22 in stems belong to the Zn/Cd/Pb/Co clade, while the highly expressed NtHMA9/16 in leaves are part of the Cu/Ag clade (Fig. 1, Supplementary Table S3), and the trace element copper is known to directly affect plant leaf growth and development48. Furthermore, we observed that 6 genes (NtHMA-9, -10, -13, -15, -16, -22), each highly expressed in at least one tissue (Fig. 8), have at least one auxin- or gibberellin-responsive cis-acting elements in their promoter regions (Fig. 7, Supplementary Table S6).

Previous studies have confirmed that HMA genes play a crucial role in the homeostatic regulation of plants in response to heavy metal stress26,34,36,38,39,49. In tobacco, existing research has shown that HMA family genes can regulate cadmium transport38,39, and the relevant genes in previous tobacco studies show high homology with NtHMA12/19 in this study; we further found that these two genes begin to be slightly induced at 24 h (Fig. 11). Meanwhile, we observed that NtHMA6/14 are induced under copper stress, among which NtHMA14 also exhibits significant upregulation under cadmium stress (Fig. 11). Additionally, NtHMA23 is induced under lead stress (Fig. 11), providing a new perspective for elucidating the functions of the HMA gene family. However, under chromium stress, no HMA genes were observed to be induced (Fig. 11), which may be related to chromium being transported via its own specific pathways50,51. These results indicate that tobacco NtHMA genes play an important role in the transport of various heavy metals.

It is also noteworthy that in this study, we found that NtHMA genes in cigar tobacco during the seedling stage are induced by various hormones and environmental stresses (Figs. 9 and 10). Furthermore, we observed that under H₂O₂ stress treatment, five NtHMA genes were induced to express, representing the highest number of induced genes across all stress conditions tested (Figs. 9 and 10). We speculate that as heavy metal transporters, HMAs may not directly respond to these hormones or various non-heavy metal environmental stresses; however, when stress induces excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS), HMAs might be indirectly involved.

Similarly, we found that the NtHMA genes induced by hormones and environmental stresses—specifically NtHMA2, NtHMA6, NtHMA7, NtHMA10, NtHMA11, NtHMA13, NtHMA14, NtHMA18, NtHMA23, and NtHMA25—all possess hormone-responsive and abiotic stress-responsive cis-acting elements in their promoter regions (Fig. 7, Supplementary Table S6). These genes will serve as potential candidate genes for further in-depth investigation of the functions of tobacco HMA genes.

Materials and methods

Materials and treatments

In this experiment, Yunxue No. 1 cigar tobacco was used, sourced from the Baise Branch of the Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region Tobacco Company, and the seedlings were raised using the plug-tray seedling method52. After successful plug-tray seedling cultivation, the seedlings were transplanted into the field in Baise City, Guangxi. After 160 days of growth, samples of roots, flowers, upper stems, middle stems, lower stems, upper leaves, middle leaves, and lower leaves were collected and sent to Beijing Biomarker Technologies Corporation for sequencing analysis. After raising the tobacco seedlings using the plug-tray seedling method, they were transplanted into 8 × 8 cm pots filled with soil and vermiculite at a ratio of 3:1, and placed in a greenhouse at Henan Agricultural University. When the plants reached 60 days of growth, the tobacco seedlings were separately treated with different hormones [100 μmol methyl jasmonate (MeJA), 100 μmol melatonin (MT), 100 μmol brassinosteroid (BR), 100 μmol salicylic acid (SA), 100 μmol abscisic acid (ABA), 100 μmol auxin (IAA)] by irrigation and foliar spraying, as well as abiotic stress treatments: irrigation with 300 mmol NaCl, 10% H₂O₂, 20% PEG, and temperature stress treatments at 4 °C (cold) and 45 °C (heat). At 0 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h after treatment, tissue samples of the first fully expanded leaf were collected. In addition, the seedlings were irrigated with various heavy metals (200 μmol PbCl₂, 200 μmol CdCl₂, 200 μmol K₂Cr₂O₇, 200 mmol CuSO₄·5H₂O), and root tissue samples were collected at the same time points (0 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h). All samples had three biological replicates per time point and were sent to Beijing Biomarker Technologies Corporation for sequencing analysis.

Identification and analysis of physicochemical properties of the NtHMAs

The genome and genome annotation information of tobacco (K326) were downloaded from the Zenodo database, and a local tobacco protein database was constructed using TBtools (TBtools-II 2.441, https://github.com/CJ-Chen/TBtools-II)53. Additionally, 8 AtHMA protein sequences from Arabidopsis thaliana21and 9 OsHMA protein sequences from Oryza sativa29 were downloaded from the Arabidopsis database (TAIR) and the rice database (RGAP), respectively54,55. These protein sequences were used as bait to perform a similarity search against tobacco NtHMA family proteins via the BLASTP in TBtools, yielding candidate NtHMA protein sequences53. Subsequent screening was conducted using the Pfam, CCD, and SMART databases, with the final NtHMA family members in the tobacco genome confirmed via NCBI BLAST database56,57. The relevant physicochemical properties were analyzed using the ExPASy online tool, including amino acid count, molecular weight, and isoelectric point58.

Phylogenetic and comparative analyses of NtHMAs

First, the multiple sequence alignment module in MEGA7 software (MEGA7.0, https://www.megasoftware.net/) was used for alignment analysis, followed by construction of the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) phylogenetic tree for the HMA gene families of tobacco, Arabidopsis thaliana, and Oryza sativa using the tree-building module in MEGA7, with the bootstrap parameter set to 1000 replicates59. The phylogenetic tree was then optimized using the online tool iTOL and further refined with graphic software such as Adobe Illustrator (Adobe Illustrator 26.5, https://www.adobe.com/)60.

Analysis of conserved motifs and gene structures HMAs in cigar tobacco

The identified NtHMA protein sequences were submitted to the online MEME tool for analysis, with the maximum number of motifs to be discovered set to 1061. Subsequently, the obtained conserved motif sequences were input into the InterPro database for functional annotation62,63.

The coding sequence (CDS) and full-length gene sequences of NtHMAs were input into the GSDS database for gene structure analysis, and structural schematic diagrams were generated64. These diagrams were further optimized using graphic processing software (Adobe Illustrator).

Chromosomal localization and Synteny analysis of NtHMAs

The positional information of all tobacco NtHMA genes was input into TBtools software to generate chromosome distribution maps of the genes, with gene density on each chromosome also visualized53,65. In addition, TBtools was also used for duplication analysis of tobacco NtHMA genes and collinearity analysis between tobacco, Arabidopsis thaliana, and Oryza sativa, and was further applied to calculate the synonymous (Ks) and non-synonymous (Ka) substitution rates of gene pairs with duplication relationships in tobacco53,66,67.

Analysis of cis-acting elements of the NtHMAs

Using TBtools software, the 2000 bp nucleotide sequences upstream of the start codon (ATG) of NtHMA genes were extracted from the tobacco genome as candidate promoter region fragments, and cis-acting elements within these sequences were predicted using the PlantCARE database68. After screening cis-acting elements related to plant hormones, defense, and stress responses, visualization diagrams were generated using TBtools53.

Analysis of tissue expression patterns of the NtHMAs

Total RNA was extracted from samples of different tissues (root, upper stem, middle stem, lower stem, upper leaf, middle leaf, lower leaf, and flower) collected at 160 days from the field in Baise City. The cDNA libraries were constructed and sequenced using an Illumina sequencer at Biomarker Technologies Corporation. After sequencing, raw reads were processed into clean reads, which were then aligned to the K326 reference genome using HISAT2 software (HISAT 2.2.1, https://daehwankimlab.github.io/hisat2/)69. Gene expression levels were quantified using the FPKM (Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads) method70. Statistical testing was performed using edgeR to screen for significantly differentially expressed genes (FC ≥ 2 and P ≤ 0.05)71,72. Finally, expression data of all cigar tobacco NtHMA genes across the 8 tissues were filtered from the processed expression data files, heatmaps were generated using TBtools, and further refined with graphic software such as Adobe Illustrator.

Analysis of expression patterns of the NtHMAs under various stress treatments

Leaf samples from the first fully expanded leaf of tobacco seedlings treated with 15 different conditions (methyl jasmonate (MeJA), melatonin (MT), brassinolide (BR), salicylic acid (SA), abscisic acid (ABA), indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), lead chloride (PbCl2), cadmium chloride (CdCl2), potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7), copper sulfate pentahydrate (CuSO4·5H2O), sodium chloride (NaCl), hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), polyethylene glycol (PEG), 4 °C cold, and 45 °C heat) were collected from the laboratory of Henan Agricultural University and sent to Beijing Novogene Co., Ltd. (Biomarker Technologies Corporation). Following the transcriptome data processing method described above, the expression data of NtHMA genes under the 15 different treatments were obtained, and heatmaps were generated using TBtools.
