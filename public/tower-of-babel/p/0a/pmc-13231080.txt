FusionTarget: Computational framework for drug repurposing against modeled fusion protein structures from genomic breakpoints
Authors: Himansu Kumar, Susan V Tsang, Mi-Sook Lee, Yung-Hsing Huang, Juyoung Choi, Shalaka R Lotlikar, Ji-Young Song, Andrea Toh, Yoon-La Choi, Kristopher W Brannan, Pora Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMany fusion genes have been recognized as biomarkers and therapeutic targets. However, the lack of knowledge on protein structures and targeting approaches made it challenging to develop effective targeting therapeutics. To fill this, we developed a computational pipeline, FusionTarget, which annotates the genomic DNA breakage to RNA and protein sequences, predicts the 3D structures of fusion proteins, and performs comparative virtual screening, comparative molecular dynamics simulation, and quantitative analyses to identify the fusion protein-selective small molecules by selecting drugs with consistent high-fold binding affinity between fusion and wild-type proteins in multiple isoforms. We applied our pipeline to EWSR1::FLI1 in Ewing sarcoma and KMT2A::AFF1 in infant acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Further cell assay experiments confirmed that cells expressing individual fusion genes were more sensitive to the suggested drugs, and the key downstream genes were affected by our drugs. FusionTarget provides a unique foundation for developing therapeutics targeting fusion proteins.Subject areas: biological sciences, biochemistry, biochemistry applications, biomolecules, nucleic acids, protein, biochemistry methods, properties of biomolecules, applied computing in medical science

Introduction

In specific tumorigenic contexts or under the influence of carcinogens, if DNA breakage occurs in multiple genes simultaneously and the DNA keeps the failed DNA damage repair products in the form of fused genes, we refer to them as fusion genes. Fusion genes provide valuable insights into the aberrant functional mechanisms of disease genesis, and numerous fusion genes have been identified as biomarkers and therapeutic targets in human diseases. Out of ∼120,000 known human fusion genes, we found that only 1% of them have research records.1 Many fusion genes are awaiting study for their functional roles and targeting in human diseases. However, the lack of information and knowledge about fusion proteins, as well as fusion protein-targeting tools/pipelines, made it challenging to develop effective fusion protein-targeted therapeutics. Even though multiple studies have utilized fusion protein sequences to predict 3D structures and identify structural features and targets,2 there has been no specific pipeline aiming to find potential drug repurposing of fusion oncoproteins to date.

In this study, we introduce a novel fusion protein-targeting bioinformatics pipeline named FusionTarget (Figure 1). Our study assumes that fusion protein context-based 3D structures can provide more useful information than the wild-type protein context-based 3D structures to target the fusion proteins. To do this, we first generated fusion protein sequences from the full-length RNA sequences of individual fusion exons and their junction DNA breakpoints in the in-frame fusion genes, then predicted their 3D structures, and selected the reliable ones. Next, we performed ABCD virtual screenings between four relevant proteins in fusion protein targeting against FDA-approved drugs, identifying fusion protein-specific interacting small molecules based on a consistently high-fold change in the binding affinity between fusion and wild-type proteins across multiple fusion iso-proteins. For the selected potential drug repurpose candidates, we searched the literature for cancer relevance and performed molecular dynamics simulation (MDS). To ensure the fusion protein-specific binding, we also analyzed the MDS between the wild-type protein and the drug. We assessed the significance of the difference in normalized drug-binding affinities between the fusion proteins and the wild-type protein. Finally, we compared the drug efficacy of our suggested drug, a known small molecule, and a negative drug, using cell assays in cancer cells expressing our targeting fusion genes and in control cells lacking the fusion genes. Compared with the typical high-throughput screening approach, FusionTarget’s in silico approach is time and cost effective. Most of all, FusionTarget can consider all known fusion gene breakpoints and the isoforms of each targeted fusion protein to identify potential drug repurposing candidates. FusionTarget provides rigorous filtering to identify fusion protein-specific drug repurposing.

In silico approach (FusionTarget) versus classical high-throughput drug screening approach to identify drug repurposing candidates targeting fusion oncoproteins

To validate the impact of FusionTarget, we applied it to two driver fusion oncoproteins. EWSR1 fusion genes were identified in 100% of children with Ewing sarcoma. ∼85% of Ewing sarcoma patients expressed the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion gene.3 Most therapies for pediatric sarcomas rely on the heavy use of surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation therapy for local control. More diverse treatments have been tried. However, it is difficult to establish effective therapeutics for children’s sarcoma because one-third of pediatric sarcomas present with metastasis at baseline, and another one-third, recurring after initial curative treatment, show a very low survival rate.4 Furthermore, the current structure-based drug design approach involves virtual screening against target proteins solely on the basis of limited information, relying on the known partial domain structures. This led to the identification of molecules with low binding affinity in the fusion protein-positive patients’ clinical trials due to a lack of knowledge about the fusion protein structures. For example, TK-216 was reported and welcomed as a promising inhibitor of EWSR1::FLI1; however, its performance in clinical trials was not successful due to its low binding affinity to the fusion proteins.5

KMT2A fusion proteins are the major drivers in acute lymphocytic leukemia (ALL) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML). They are present in more than 70% of infant patients.6,7,8 AFF1 is the most common fusion gene partner of KMT2A in AML and ALL, accounting for ∼35% of all KMT2A-rearranged leukemia.9 The KMT2A::AFF1 fusion gene is most commonly found in ALL but can also occur in AML and lymphoma.10 Compared with other ALL patients, KMT2A::AFF1-positive patients showed a significantly reduced overall survival.11 From the fact that the menin-binding motif is conserved across all KMT2A fusion proteins, a first-in-human phase 1 clinical trial was conducted investigating revumenib (SNDX-5613), a potent and selective oral inhibitor of the menin-KMT2A interaction, in patients with relapsed or refractory acute leukemia.12 Given the predominance and poor prognosis of KMT2A::AFF1, targeting this fusion as a therapeutic approach appears a legitimate strategy to improve the clinical outcome of pediatric acute leukemia.

For these two driver fusion oncoproteins, we applied our FusionTarget pipeline, validated the efficacy of the selected drug candidates using drug-sensitivity and cell-viability assays, and identified two drug repurposing candidates. Our study demonstrates that FusionTarget can serve as a platform for identifying potential drug repurposing opportunities for fusion proteins. Specifically, FusionTarget can target even those fusion proteins whose 3D structure is not known, which is common for most fusion proteins; develop drugs targeting all known breakpoints (BPs) of the individual fusion genes, considering isoforms; and avoid off-targeting to the wild-type protein. Further validation of the direct interaction between fusion proteins and drugs is needed. FusionTarget will provide new insights into cancers, drugs, biology, and clinical studies, as well as its unique annotation and analytical pipeline methods, serving as the foundation for the development of therapeutics targeting fusion proteins.

While AlphaFold2-based modeling of fusion proteins faces inherent challenges, such as the lack of co-evolutionary multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) for genomic breakpoints and the structural uncertainty of intrinsically disordered regions (IDRs), our strategy focuses on the well-folded functional domains within these oncoproteins. Transcription factor fusions like EWSR1::FLI1 and KMT2A::AFF1 are characterized by large, dynamic protein-DNA and protein-protein interfaces often involving IDRs. However, their tumorigenic activity is fundamentally dependent on structured “anchor” domains—the DNA-binding domain (DBD) of FLI1 and the C-terminal homology domain (CHD) of AFF1.

We hypothesized that modeling these specific domains within their fusion protein context provides a better template for virtual screening than relying solely on wild-type structures, even though the conformational change is not large around the same domain. Despite the absence of a complete 3D structure for the entire fusion protein, targeting these “druggable” and well-folded pockets allows for the identification of small molecules that can functionally disrupt the oncogenic transcriptional machinery. This “domain-centric” approach was further validated by our downstream analysis, which demonstrated that compounds selected via this pipeline can effectively modulate key target genes, confirming that full structural resolution of disordered regions is not a prerequisite for successful bioinformatic-led drug discovery. A comprehensive address is provided in “discussion” and limitations of the study.”

Results

Overview of FusionTarget with the application to EWSR1::FLI1 in Ewing sarcoma

Formation of the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion proteins in cancer cells is illustrated in Figure 2A. In tumorigenic cells, DNA double-strand breakages occur simultaneously in multiple genes, including EWSR1 and FLI1, separately. The DNA damage repair system attempted to repair those breaks, but it focused on the breakpoints between other genes (EWSR1 and FLI1) rather than the breakpoints within the single broken gene. As a result, this chromosomal rearrangement event formed a fusion gene (EWSR1::FLI1). Through the central dogma of biology, this fusion gene was transcribed into the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion transcript, and this fusion mRNA was translated into the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion amino acid sequence. This translated fusion peptide folded into a 3D structure, and the resulting fusion protein then performed its tumorigenic functions, leading to the development of Ewing sarcoma (Figure 2A). EWSR1::FLI1 can activate a large set of target genes by functioning as a pioneer factor at GGAA microsatellite repeats and inducing active enhancers de novo, thereby initiating a transition from a closed to an open chromatin conformation.13 Therefore, targeting the DBD of EWSR1::FLI1 is a promising strategy to inhibit this fusion protein’s tumorigenic mechanism.

The starting part of the FusionTarget pipeline mimics the cellular mechanisms of the fusion gene, from the DNA breakpoint to the formation of the fusion protein with a 3D structure. Next, based on the modeled 3D structures, FusionTarget identifies the potential drug repurposing candidates (Figure 2B). First, we integrated all known EWSR1::FLI1 fusion gene breakpoints from public resources, performed open reading frame (ORF) analysis, generated full-length fusion mRNA and fusion protein sequences, predicted 3D structures of these fusion proteins, and selected reliable 3D structures. Next, we performed multiple virtual screenings (ABCD screening) to determine the binding affinities of inhibitory small molecules and assessed the MDS of the selected small molecules. More details of individual steps are explained in the following sub-sections.

From the most comprehensive fusion gene resource, FusionGDB2,14 and additional manually curated fusion gene resources (COSMIC15 and Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia [CCLE]16), we integrated known fusion breakpoint information for the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion gene across 866 cells and patient samples (information of all samples is provided in Table S1). After checking the ORFs by using our previously reported approaches,14,17 we identified 74 in-frame EWSR1::FLI1 fusion breakpoints. Among these, we selected the top six most frequent breakpoints, each of which was observed in more than 10 fusion gene samples. Based on these six breakpoints and considering 35 gene isoform combinations, we made 114 unique potential EWSR1::FLI1 fusion full-length mRNA sequences. After putting it into the ORFfinder, we finally made 22 unique EWSR1::FLI1 fusion protein sequences for six fusion breakpoints (Table S2). Before performing further protein structural analyses, all 22 predicted EWSR1::FLI1 fusion protein 3D structures were preprocessed using the protein preparation module of Schrödinger (Release 2022-2, LLC, New York, NY, 2021).

For 22 individual fusion protein sequences from six fusion gene breakpoints, we predicted the 3D structures by using AlphaFold2.18,19 For each protein sequence input, AlphaFold2 generates multiple structural predictions. These predictions are ranked by the confidence score, which reflects the model’s certainty for each predicted structure. We selected the 3D structure with the highest confidence score. Residues with high predicted local distance difference test scores (pLDDT > 70) are generally considered well-modeled residues. Figure S1 shows the distribution of the pLDDT scores across the fusion protein sequence lengths of 22 EWSR1::FLI1 fusion isoform proteins.

Next, the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion protein structure, characterized by a notably high pLDDT score distribution around the binding sites, was selected. Recognizing the importance of the binding site in a target protein for drug design, we selected binding site regions with elevated pLDDT scores. Schrödinger’s SiteMap tool20 (Schrödinger Release 2024-3, Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2024) was used to identify these binding sites. SiteMap provides rankings for multiple potential ligand-binding sites, considering parameters such as druggability score, site score, number of site points, and site volume. For all 22 predicted 3D structures, the 1st-ranked binding site was selected. Then, the significance of the difference in the pLDDT scores between the binding and non-binding sites was assessed. For each of the six fusion gene breakpoints, we selected the best fusion protein 3D structure with the optimal pLDDT distribution for the binding sites and a higher minimum pLDDT score for the virtual screening process (Figure S1). Information on fusion gene breakpoints and sequence lengths of these six chosen ones out of 22 is available in Table 1.

To check the reliability of our predicted structures, we first compared the predicted longest isoform of EWSR1::FLI1 with its closest known PDB structure (PDB: 5E8G) by superimposing their conserved regions (Figure S2A). The superimposition revealed a significant structural resemblance between the predicted model and the experimentally determined structure, with minor deviations noted in loop regions. This suggests that our predicted structures maintain the overall structural integrity comparable to that of known homologs. As shown in Figure S2B, we compared the protein lengths to evaluate differences in sequence length between the predicted fusion protein structures and known experimental structures. We created a violin plot of the sequence lengths. The distribution shows that EWSR1::FLI1 fusion proteins are significantly longer than the known PDB structures. The violin plot in Figure S2C compares pLDDT scores between the predicted EWSR1-FLI1 structures and the known PDB structures, revealing that our predicted models exhibit confidence regions similar to those of known PDB structures. Through the MSA of conserved regions in Figure S2D, we further investigated the structural conservation between predicted fusion protein isoforms and their closest known PDB structures. Figure S2D shows that the conserved structured regions are well aligned across different isoforms, reinforcing that key functional domains remain intact in fusion proteins. We analyzed pLDDT score distributions across all residues of the predicted and known structures. As shown in Figure S2E, the core structural regions exhibited high pLDDT scores, whereas terminal regions demonstrated greater variability. This indicates that the functionally important regions of EWSR1::FLI1 are well folded.

We tried to understand how the structures differ between wild-type and fusion proteins. Figures S2F and S2G show the pLDDT and Z score distributions across the fusion protein length of the wild-type and fusion proteins before and after the breakpoint. To evaluate the stereochemical quality and conformational feasibility of the predicted protein structures, we utilized the MolProbity tool,21 which assesses global and local structural quality metrics of protein and nucleic acid structures. One of its primary metrics is the Ramachandran plot Z score. Z score provides a statistical measure of the quality of phi (ϕ) and psi (ψ) torsion angles in a protein structure. A Z score close to zero suggests that the protein’s backbone conformations are typical, while a high positive or negative value might indicate potentially unstable configurations. Overall, the DBD retained in the FLI1 part of the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion protein had high pLDDT scores.

Figure S3 shows an example of the predicted fusion protein EWSR1::FLI1 structure and related information, with Figures S3A and S3B showing the fusion protein sequence and 3D structure of the fusion protein with a well-defined architecture with distinct functional domains, respectively. The predicted aligned error (PAE) plot in (C) indicates high confidence in the core structural regions. In contrast, pLDDT scores in (D) highlight well-folded domains with a subset of disordered regions, particularly in the activation domain. Notably, the binding site residues exhibited significantly higher pLDDT scores than non-active regions (p = 2.5E−06), suggesting greater structural stability in the targetable functional regions (Figure S3E). The Ramachandran plot further confirmed the quality of the predicted structure, with most residues occupying energetically favorable conformations (Figure S3F). Domain analysis mapped key functional regions, including the EWS activation domain (EAD), Prion-like domain (PrLD), and ETS DBD, reinforcing its role as a transcriptional regulator in oncogenesis (Figure S3G).

Identifying EWSR1::FLI1 fusion protein-specific cancer drugs through the FusionTarget pipeline

Finding small molecules with high binding affinity for the fusion proteins but little or no binding affinity for the wild-type proteins is key to avoiding off-target effects when targeting fusion oncoproteins. To highlight the interaction between our predicted fusion protein 3D structures and small molecules, we require informative control protein structures, including the wild-type protein and known PDB structures that retain the functional domain of the fusion protein. In this context, we utilized the differences in structure, binding sites, and binding affinities to identify small molecules that target fusion proteins specifically. Here, we introduce four types of virtual screening in FusionTarget—ABCD virtual screening. Here, ABCD virtual screening was performed under the assumption that each of the six EWSR1::FLI1 fusion protein isoforms is treated as an independent conformational ensemble for docking simulations. In identifying a higher fold change between A and D, using only the normalized drug binding scores in A and D creates a biased or less reliable reference distribution for a chance of ignoring information from B and C. By using the normalized drug binding scores in the context of A, B, C, and D, we can establish a more stable, representative baseline that better accounts for the technical variability across all four binding score contexts. Virtual screening A used the AlphaFold2-predicted 3D structure of the fusion protein to screen a library of FDA-approved drugs. To define the search space for Glide docking in this condition (Schrödinger Release 2024-3, LLC, New York, NY, 2024), the scoring grid was generated based on the top-ranked binding sites within well-folded regions flanking the DBD of the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion protein, specifically excluding the IDRs. Virtual screening B served as an experimental baseline, utilizing the experimentally determined PDB structure of the FLI1 DBD and its reported binding sites as defined in the literature. Virtual screening C employed a hybrid approach, using the AlphaFold2-predicted 3D structure of the fusion protein while mapping the docking grid to the same experimental binding sites used in condition B. Finally, virtual screening D utilized the predicted 3D structure of the wild-type FLI1 protein and its corresponding top-ranked binding sites to establish a baseline for non-fusion binding affinity. This four-way comparative analysis enabled systematic normalization and identification of compounds with high specificity for the fusion protein-specific conformational environment.

To perform the ABCD virtual screening, first, we curated a ligand library of 1,600 FDA-approved drugs from DrugBank.22 Then, we performed virtual screening between six individual fusion proteins and the FDA-approved drug library, using Glide. All six EWSR1::FLI1 fusion proteins were found to retain the DBD of the FLI1 part. Selecting the consistently interacting drugs in these six DBD-retained fusion proteins can yield more diverse conformations than using multiple conformations within a single fusion protein, and it is a more stringent criterion. Then, following the filtering outlined in Figure 4B, we selected the potential drug repurposing candidates that selectively target EWSR1::FLI1. First, we set the binding affinity score to zero for positive values. Then, we quantile-normalized the binding scores across four screening sets (A, B, C, and D) for each isoform based on the assumption that each of the six EWSR1::FLI1 fusion protein isoforms is treated as an independent conformational ensemble for docking simulations. After sorting based on the fold change of fusion protein-based screening (norm.A) and wild-type FLI1-based screening (norm.D), we chose cases with the normalized fold change larger than 1.0 (norm.A/norm.D > 1.0). We filtered out drugs with weaker binding than that of TK-216, the most-studied molecule so far. We repeated all these steps for the six EWSR1::FLI1 fusion proteins. Next, we selected the drugs listed in the normalized screening profile of at least two EWSR1::FLI1 fusion proteins. Then, there were 200 drugs. Out of these, 21 have been reported in cancer studies. Among these 21 drugs, 7 consistently showed strong binding in all six EWSR1::FLI1 fusion proteins (Tables S3 and S4).

Figure 5A shows the normalized binding scores of the final seven drugs. Here, we present three EWSR1::FLI1 isoform proteins, which are predicted to be present in cancer cell lines (Figure 5B). We performed MDS analyses for the 7 drugs to compare molecular stabilities across three fusion protein structures (520 aa, 598 aa, and 609 aa) (Figure 5C). For this, we used Desmond, Schrödinger Release 2021-4. The simulation was executed over 50 ns, capturing trajectory frames every 50.0 ps and recording energy values at 1.2 ps intervals, resulting in approximately 1,000 frames for a detailed analysis. After conducting the MDS, we assessed the stability and consistency of the protein-drug complex by using RMSDs in the atomic positions over the simulation time frame. The RMSD plateaued from 40 ns onward during the interaction between the fused EWSR1::FLI1 protein and the molecules. By this analysis, we found that valrubicin, lenvatinib, trifluridine, and thalidomide showed stable molecular dynamics after initial fluctuations in all three different lengths of fusion proteins. Figure 5D shows the docking poses of the chosen molecules and our predicted fusion proteins.

Ewing sarcoma sensitivity to predicted EWSR1::FLI1 inhibitors

The robust predictive accuracy of the FusionTarget pipeline is underscored by the fact that several identified candidates, such as trifluridine, lenvatinib, thalidomide, and mitomycin, have already been documented in the Ewing sarcoma literature or are currently in clinical trials. These external clinical and experimental benchmarks independently validate our structure-based screening approach, confirming its ability to prioritize compounds with established therapeutic potential against fusion oncoproteins. Further investigation of these four drugs revealed no records for valrubicin or trifluridine clinical trials (Figure 6A). We evaluated the sensitivity of Ewing Sarcoma cells to the drugs valrubicin, trifluridine, TK-216, and gilteritinib. Gliteritinib is a general inhibitor, and not specific to the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion, according to our ABCD virtual screening results.

We tested drug sensitivity in the Ewing sarcoma cell lines RD-ES-1 and SK-ES-1. These specific lines were selected because they harbor EWSR1::FLI1 fusion genes with the exact breakpoints utilized in our AlphaFold2-based 3D protein modeling. This alignment ensures that the experimental cellular context directly reflects the structural environment of the fusion protein used for our virtual screening and binding site analysis. The rhabdomyosarcoma cell line RH30 was used as the negative control, as it is also a soft-tissue cancer cell but lacks the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion. A cell viability assay (CCK-8) was performed to assess the sensitivity of Ewing sarcoma and rhabdomyosarcoma cells to valrubicin, trifluridine, TK-216, and gilteritinib. The median IC50 for trifluridine was 10–70× lower in cell lines expressing EWSR1::FLI1 than in the RH30 cell line, which lacks the EWSR1::FLI1 region. The controls showed the expected results, as TK-216 demonstrated enhanced sensitivity in Ewing sarcoma models compared with rhabdomyosarcoma, and both cancer models exhibited similar sensitivity to gilteritinib (Figures 6B and 6C). On the basis of cell viability findings (IC50 values), we performed a proliferation assay for each drug type to determine whether cell sensitivity affects cancer cell proliferation. We treated the Ewing sarcoma and rhabdomyosarcoma cells with the highest Ewing sarcoma IC50 value to determine if both cancer models proliferate at an Ewing sarcoma-specific drug concentration. Ewing sarcoma cells treated with drug concentrations at or below their respective IC50 values showed suppressed growth. In contrast, models with higher IC50 values showed continuous cell growth throughout the proliferation study, as observed in the cells treated with trifluridine. The SK-ES-1, RD-ES-1, and RH30 cells were treated with the IC50 < 0.253 μM; the treatment with trifluridine in the SK-ES-1 and RD-ES-1 cells showed a reduction in the proliferation rate, while RH30 cells had continual growth (Figure 6D). Our studies indicate that cells expressing the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion were consistently more sensitive to trifluridine. The increased sensitivity of Ewing sarcoma cells led to the suppression of cancer cell growth, which was not observed in cancer cells lacking the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion.

As a final confirmatory step to ensure that its predicted affinity for the fusion protein was not due to computational noise, we conducted a statistical significance test (Figure 6E). The mean docking score of trifluridine for the fusion protein was −3.547 ± 0.49 kcal/mol, while that for the wild type was −1.745 ± 0.09 kcal/mol. The difference between the two groups was highly significant (p = 0.00022), with a mean difference (−1.801 kcal/mol) substantially exceeding the inherent noise level of the scoring protocol. Trifluridine and valrubicin showed a significantly stronger binding (lower binding score) to the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion protein than the wild-type FLI1 protein.

For further validation of the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion protein-specific binding compared with the wild-type FLI1 protein, we compared the molecular dynamics across 200 ns between EWSR1::FLI1 (587 aa) and trifluridine, as well as between wild-type FLI1 and trifluridine (Figure 6F). It showed a high degree of fluctuation between the wild-type FLI1 and trifluridine, which suggests that our chosen drug may interact in a fusion protein-specific manner. However, valrubicin showed a more stable binding to the wild-type FLI1 than the fusion protein. Therefore, we cannot say that valrubicin is not a fusion protein-specific small molecule. TK-216 showed a stable status of interaction with the fusion protein, but the fluctuation range of fusion protein and wild-type FLI1 in the MDS was similar (38–45 Å), whereas trifluridine’s fluctuation range of fusion protein was much less than that of wild-type FLI1 (7.2 Å < 120 Å). Therefore, we suggest trifluridine as a potential EWSR1::FLI1-selective inhibitor.

Structural basis for fusion-specific recognition of the FLI1 DBD by trifluridine

To investigate the structural basis for the fusion-specific activity of our lead compound, trifluridine, we performed a comparative structural analysis between the well-folded DBD region of the predicted EWSR1::FLI1 (587 AA) model and the experimental X-ray crystal structure of the human FLI1 DBD (PDB: 5E8G) (Figure 7). While the core ETS domain remained largely conserved in the superimposition (Figure 7A), we identified a critical structural divergence in the 408–410 residue region (LEU 408, ALA 409, and ASN 410). This new structured region was identified in all six EWSR1::FLI1 fusion protein isoforms, as shown in Figure 3. Notably, this specific segment is entirely unresolved in the wild-type crystal structure, as indicated by the high structural disorder and lack of stable conformation in the native, non-fusion protein (Figure 7F, pLDDT plot). In stark contrast, our fusion model revealed that this previously disordered region undergoes fusion-induced stabilization, folding into a discrete, prominent α helix (Figure 7B, red circle). This “induced helix” creates a well-defined, actionable deep pocket specific to the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion environment, contrasting sharply with the shallow surface of the known PDB structure (Figure 7C). MDS demonstrated that trifluridine is intricately anchored within this fusion-stabilized pocket. Specifically, Figure 7D shows that trifluridine forms essential hydrogen-bond networks (indicated by blue dashed lines) with side chains of the residues such as GLN 415 and GLN 417, which are stabilized by the adjacent C-terminal structure.

Furthermore, the newly positioned LEU 408 and ALA 409 on the fusion-induced helix (shown in the lower right of Figure 7D) provide a tailored hydrophobic environment that closely packs against the trifluoromethyl group of trifluridine, ensuring a tight geometric fit within the pocket. The high confidence of this binding architecture is further supported by the PAE map (Figure 7E), which confirms the rigid orientation of these key residues. Because the residues required for these interactions are structurally undefined or highly entropic in the wild-type PDB structure, trifluridine cannot achieve a stable “anchor” in the native FLI1 protein. This “conformation-specific” recognition provides a robust structural explanation for the fusion-specific selectivity of trifluridine, as supported by our experimental data showing the modulation of key downstream effector genes.

Our structural modeling of the interaction between trifluridine and the FLI1 DBD suggests that trifluridine engagement may physically or conformationally interfere with the protein’s capacity to associate with its cognate DNA (Figures 7G and 7H). While the native fusion protein stably anchors to GGAA microsatellite repeats (modeled as a [GGAA]4 sequence via AlphaFold323) to establish de novo enhancers (Figure 7G), the trifluridine-binding pocket is situated in immediate proximity to the DNA-binding interface. The occupancy of this fusion-specific cryptic pocket by trifluridine is predicted to induce steric hindrance or alter the local geometry of the DBD, thereby obstructing the stable recruitment of the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion protein to these critical genomic loci (Figure 7H). Although further biochemical validation is required to definitively characterize the displacement kinetics, our current computational and functional data provide a plausible rationale that trifluridine disrupts aberrant transcriptional assembly by impairing the initial anchoring of the fusion protein to its target DNA. This proposed blockade likely leads to the subsequent repression of downstream oncogene expression observed in our Ewing sarcoma cell models.

Trifluridine suppresses the EWSR1::FLI1-driven oncogenic transcriptional program

To understand the specificity of trifluridine in modulating EWSR1::FLI1 downstream targets, we assessed the transcriptionally activated target NROB1 and the repressed target TGFBR2 (1,3). As TK-216 is a known modulator of EWSR1::FLI1, we treated EWSR1::FLI1 fusion-positive and -negative cells with TK-216 as the positive control inhibitor (2). We found that trifluridine and TK-216 treatment of EWSR1::FLI1-positive cells (RD-ES-1) decreased NROB1 expression and increased TGFBR2 expression, as expected. However, treatment of the EWSR1::FLI1-negative cell line RH30 with trifluridine and TK-216 did not modulate NROB1 and TGFBR2 expression levels compared with untreated RH30 cells (Figure 6G). On the basis of our mechanism studies, we conclude that trifluridine is specific to EWSR1::FLI1-positive models, as our EWSR1::FLI1-negative model showed no modulation of EWSR1::FLI1-specific targets.

The rationale for targeting the DBD of EWSR1::FLI1 is rooted in the fundamental molecular mechanism of Ewing sarcoma. As established by Boulay et al. (2017), the stable anchoring of the FLI1 DBD to GGAA microsatellite repeats is a prerequisite for the subsequent recruitment of the BAF (BRG1/BRM-associated factor) complex by the EWSR1 PrLD.

This initial DNA-binding event triggers pathological phase transitions and chromatin remodeling. Therefore, we focused on identifying “actionable” structured pockets within the FLI1 DBD across six fusion-specific conformations. This “anchor-targeting” strategy allowed the identification of potent inhibitors without modeling the full, highly dynamic IDRs. Our experimental data support this approach; reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR) confirmed the modulation of key effector genes following drug treatment, suggesting a successful blockade of the fusion protein’s recruitment to enhancer regions.

Overview of FusionTarget with the application to KMT2A::AFF1 in AML/ALL

The oncogenic mechanism of KMT2A::AFF1 in AML is its ability to recruit the super elongation complex (SEC), thereby altering the epigenetic landscape and inducing aberrant oncogene expression. Figure 8 provides an overview of the application of FusionTarget to identify potential drug repurposing candidates targeting the KMT2A::AFF1 fusion protein in AML. First, we extracted the breakpoint information from various cell line and primary cell sequencing databases, including FusionGDB 2.0, COSMIC, and CCLE. There were 152 samples expressing the KMT2A::AFF1 fusion gene, with 140 unique exon-junction fusion gene breakpoints identified by examining the exon junction loci of individual breakpoints. We investigated the ORFs of individual fusion gene breakage, considering multiple gene isoform structures.14 There were 22 breakpoints annotated to create the in-frame fusion genes (Table S5). For these in-frame fusions, we generated full-length mRNA sequences for the fusion genes. Then, we ran ORFfinder to predict in-frame transcripts that encode fusion protein sequences. Table S6 includes these sequences. On the basis of 22 in-frame fusion gene breakpoints, three KMT2A gene isoforms, and AFF1 gene isoforms, we could make 58 unique in-frame full-length transcript sequences and 26 unique fusion protein sequences (Table 2). Then, we predicted the 3D structure of these KMT2A1::AFF1 fusion protein isoforms, using AlphaFold2. Because our goal was to identify small molecules with high binding affinity to fusion proteins but no binding affinity to the wild-type protein, we also predicted the 3D structure of the wild-type protein of the fusion partner, which contains the targeting domain, and downloaded the known 3D structure from the PDB (PDB: 6R80). Out of 26 KMT2A::AFF1 fusion proteins, we chose three reliable 3D structures characterized by a notably high pLDDT score distribution around the binding sites (Figure S4). These three chosen proteins were made from the top three most frequent breakpoints. For each isoform, we performed ABCD screening against the FDA-approved drug library. To select for a high fold change in binding affinity between the fusion protein and the wild-type protein, we performed quantile normalization and calculated the fold change. We followed the same procedure for individual isoforms and selected the consistently high fold change in binding affinity to the fusion proteins but observed no binding to the wild-type protein in any of the three fusion protein isoforms. This process identified 75 drugs. After conducting a literature search of relevant cancer research reports for each identified drug, we identified 13 drugs. Among these, only one drug, brigatinib, showed no binding to the wild-type AFF1 protein. For brigatinib, we further explored the downstream effects on cancer cells.

ALL sensitivity to the predicted KMT2A::AFF1 inhibitor

To validate the applicability of our in silico findings in clinical settings, we tested whether brigatinib effectively and specifically eliminates KMT2A::AFF1-positive cells. We searched for the cell lines harboring the KMT2A::AFF1 fusion protein, based on fusion information from the CCLE. Of the ALL/AML cell lines, Sanger sequencing results confirmed that MV4-11 and RS4;11 cells possess the KMT2A::AFF1 fusion gene, which are equivalent to sequences #7 (exon #8 of KMT2A fused with exon #5 of AFF1) and #16 (exon #9 of KMT2A fused with exon #4 of AFF1), as shown in Figures 11C–11E. The expression of KMT2A::AFF1 mRNA transcript in MV4-11 and RS4;11 cells was confirmed by quantitative PCR compared with a KMT2A::AFF1-negative AML cell line, K562. Figure S7 shows information on the primer sequences used for the validation of fusion genes in the cancer cell lines. Figure 11F shows the 2D structure of two drugs. Cytotoxicity assays demonstrated that brigatinib is cytotoxic in MV4-11 and RS4;11 cells, with IC50 values of 77.39 and 138.1 nM, respectively. On the other hand, pinometostat, an FDA-approved compound used to treat AML patients with KMT2A rearrangements, was found to be cytotoxic only to MV4-11 cells, not to RS4-11 cells (Figure 11G). As a negative control for comparison, cell cytotoxicity was assessed using a discontinued anti-cancer drug, everolimus, which is an mTOR inhibitor.34 Both MV4-11 and RS4-11 cells exhibited IC50 values over 10 times higher than those treated with brigatinib. Treatment with brigatinib of the MV4-11 and RD-RS4;11 cells showed a reduction in the proliferation rate, while the K562 had continual cell growth (Figure 11H). In summary, these results demonstrated that brigatinib can potentially be repurposed as a KMT2A::AFF1-specific therapy for AML.

Brigatinib suppresses the KMT2A::AFF1-driven oncogenic transcriptional program

To investigate whether brigatinib treatment leads to loss of fusion protein function, we performed RNA-seq on MV4-11 cells. GSEA (gene set enrichment analysis) revealed a robust suppression of several hallmark oncogenic pathways. Specifically, significant negative enrichment was observed in the pathways critical for cell proliferation, including E2F targets, MYC targets (V1 and V2), and the G2/M checkpoint (Figure 12A). These results indicated that brigatinib effectively attenuates the proliferative transcriptional state driven by the KMT2A::AFF1 fusion. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering further highlighted a marked downregulation of the KMT2A::AFF1-associated transcriptional program (Figure 12B). Brigatinib treatment led to a dose-dependent reduction in the expression of key HOXA cluster genes (HOXA9, HOXA10, and HOXA11) and critical transcriptional regulators, including MYC, MYB, and MEN1. Interestingly, while some factors, such as PBX family members and RUNX1, clustered with increased relative expression in this heatmap, the primary oncogenic drivers, most notably the HOXA-MEIS axis, showed a profound and consistent decrease.

To validate these transcriptomic findings, we performed quantitative RT-PCR (RT-qPCR) for the canonical downstream targets HOXA9 and MEIS1 (primer information is provided in Figure S8). As a positive control, treatment with the DOT1L inhibitor pinometostat resulted in clear dose- and time-dependent reductions in HOXA9 and MEIS1 mRNA expressions in the MV4-11 cells, confirming the sensitivity of our assay system (Figure 12C). Next, we assessed the effects of brigatinib across different leukemia cell lines. Brigatinib treatment induced a significant, dose-dependent suppression of both HOXA9 and MEIS1 in fusion-positive MV4-11 and RS4;11 cells (Figure 12D). In K562 cells, which lack the KMT2A rearrangement, HOXA9 transcripts were undetectable, and MEIS1 levels remained largely unaffected across all concentrations, demonstrating that these transcriptional effects are specific to the KMT2A-rearranged context. Time-course experiments confirmed a progressive reduction of HOXA9 and MEIS1 transcripts in the MV4-11 and RS4;11 cells within 24–72 h of brigatinib exposure (500 nM) (Figure 12E). No such temporal reduction was observed in the K562 cells, further supporting the specificity of brigatinib’s action. Together, these data provide strong evidence that brigatinib functionally disrupts the KMT2A::AFF1-dependent oncogenic network, specifically by suppressing the HOXA-MEIS transcriptional program.

We assessed whether brigatinib treatment alters the abundance of the fusion protein itself in KMT2A::AFF1-positive cells. To this end, we performed western blot analysis using an anti-KMT2A (MLL) N-terminal antibody, which reliably detects the KMT2A::AFF1 fusion protein, in parallel with RT-qPCR analysis of the KMT2A::AFF1 transcripts (Figure S9). Brigatinib treatment did not alter KMT2A::AFF1 mRNA expression levels across the indicated doses. In contrast, although the expression of downstream signaling molecules was reduced following brigatinib treatment, the levels of the KMT2A::AFF1 fusion protein remained unchanged, as assessed by immunoblotting. These findings indicated that brigatinib does not suppress fusion protein expression but instead primarily affects the downstream signaling pathway. Together, these data demonstrated that the observed suppression of the HOXA9-MEIS1 transcriptional program occurred without alteration of the expression or stability of the KMT2A::AFF1 fusion protein itself. This supports the conclusion that brigatinib acts by inhibiting the fusion-driven transcriptional network rather than by transcriptional or post-translational downregulation of the fusion protein.

Our identified compound, brigatinib, specifically targets the AFF1 CHD, thereby functioning as a protein-protein interaction (PPI) inhibitor. By sterically blocking the CHD interface, brigatinib prevents the recruitment of the SEC to the fusion protein. This effectively arrests the transcriptional elongation machinery and silences the oncogenic transcriptional program without necessitating the degradation of the fusion protein itself. Our experimental data, which demonstrated a profound suppression of key downstream effectors (HOXA9/MEIS1) despite stable fusion mRNA and protein abundance, are fully consistent with this mode of action. These results corroborate that brigatinib acts as a functional inhibitor of the transcriptional machinery, rather than a proteolytic degrader. Unlike proteolysis targeting chimeras (PROTACs) or molecular glues, which induce ubiquitination and proteasomal clearance, our identified small-molecule inhibitor functions by sterically occluding specific functional domains (e.g., DBDs or PPI interfaces) essential to the fusion protein’s oncogenic activity. By physically blocking the recruitment of essential co-factors (such as the SEC) or preventing chromatin engagement, the drug effectively “silences” the fusion protein’s ability to drive downstream gene expression. Consequently, the fusion protein remains detectable at stable levels (unchanged abundance) because it is not marked for degradation, yet it is rendered functionally inert, leading to the observed profound suppression of tumorigenic downstream targets.

Discussion

Several strategies have been developed to target fusion proteins; each is associated with distinct clinical hurdles. PROTACs utilize the ubiquitin-proteasome system to degrade target proteins35; however, their large molecular size often results in poor cellular permeability and challenges in vivo delivery.36 While peptide inhibitors offer an “occupancy-driven” alternative,37 they frequently suffer from metabolic instability and rapid clearance, necessitating high-dose or frequent administration.38 CRISPR-based approaches offer a potentially permanent solution by inactivating fusion genes at the DNA level39; however, they remain in early stages due to significant safety concerns regarding off-target editing and delivery barriers.40

Furthermore, targeting the large and dynamic protein-DNA or PPIs of transcription factor fusions remain a major challenge. While interface disruption with large molecules or peptides can be effective, these “undruggable” surfaces often lack the deep, well-defined pockets required for high-affinity small-molecule binding. Moreover, though protein degradation strategies like PROTACs do not strictly require an actionable functional site, they still necessitate a reliable “bindable” pocket to serve as an anchor for the E3 ligase recruitment.

In contrast, our FusionTarget pipeline identifies orally bioavailable small molecules that achieve functional inhibition of the fusion-driven oncogenic network without necessitating protein degradation. By focusing on structured, “gatekeeper” domains such as the FLI1 DBD, we identified actionable pockets that, once occupied, can disrupt the initial anchoring required for the assembly of the entire oncogenic transcriptional complex. This approach leverages accurate structural modeling of the fusion interface to identify compounds with high specificity and favorable pharmacokinetic profiles. Furthermore, the pipeline facilitates drug repurposing from the existing FDA-approved library, offering a significantly accelerated translational pathway toward clinical application compared to traditional drug discovery, de novo interface disrupters, or gene therapy modalities.

We acknowledge that the full 3D structure of these fusion proteins remains elusive due to the prevalence of IDRs. However, our study demonstrates that targeting the structured DBD in a fusion context-reflected manner is a potential pathway for drug discovery. By inhibiting the initial “anchoring” event, we can effectively suppress the entire oncogenic transcriptional assembly. In the future, if we can develop a complete structural model that includes the disordered region, we may be able to identify optimal drugs targeting the fusion proteins.

In this study, we evaluated six distinct EWSR1::FLI1 fusion isoforms, each characterized by unique genomic breakpoints and junctional sequences. While these isoforms are derived from the same parental genes, the resulting structural variations at the fusion interface render each docking simulation a physiochemically independent event. We treated these as an independent conformational cohort to assess the broad-spectrum selectivity of our lead candidates. However, we acknowledge that a sample size of n = 6 may limit the overall statistical power for generalizability. Future work incorporating a larger diversity of rare fusion breakpoints will be instrumental in further validating the robustness of these selective interactions.

To provide experimental support for our in silico predictions, we conducted a preliminary cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) on the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion protein. While we observed a noticeable thermal shift in the presence of trifluridine within the 55°C–65°C window, the results exhibited variability at higher temperatures (≥70°C) (Figure S10). This inconsistency is likely attributable to the protein’s N-terminal IDR and PrLD, which are known to drive rapid, irreversible aggregation under extreme heat stress, potentially confounding sigmoidal melting profile analysis. Despite these technical challenges inherent to the fusion protein’s biophysical properties, the observed stabilization at critical melting temperatures serves as a promising initial indicator of direct target engagement, warranting further high-resolution structural validation.

Despite the compelling evidence provided by our structural modeling and cellular assays, we acknowledge the absence of direct in vitro biophysical binding data as a limitation of this study. High-sensitivity assays such as isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) or surface plasmon resonance (SPR) typically require highly purified recombinant proteins in large quantities. However, full-length fusion oncoproteins like EWSR1::FLI1 are characterized by notorious structural instability and a high propensity for non-specific aggregation, presenting substantial technical challenges for stable protein purification. To address this, we prioritized the CETSA using whole-cell lysates. This in situ system preserves the native protein conformation and associated cellular co-factors within a complex proteome, providing a more biologically relevant context than purified fragments that may lose their fusion-specific “induced-fit” conformations during artificial purification. Performing CETSA in a lysate-based system—where metabolic signaling and downstream transcriptional cascades are largely inactive—minimizes the likelihood of indirect stabilization mediated by active cellular responses. The observed thermal stabilization of EWSR1::FLI1 within the 55°C–65°C range, combined with our structural discovery of a previously “hidden” pocket at residues 408–410 and a dense network of hydrogen bonds with GLN415 and GLN417, provides a robust physical rationale for direct engagement. The predictive reliability of this mechanism is further supported by our pipeline’s ability to accurately prioritize compounds like lenvatinib and thalidomide, which are already among clinical benchmarks for Ewing sarcoma. While the convergence of structural, cellular, and transcriptomic data strongly indicates a direct targeting mechanism, we recognize that future studies utilizing optimized protein stabilization systems will be instrumental for detailed thermodynamic and kinetic characterization of these complex fusion targets.

To determine if brigatinib binds to the KMT2A::AFF1 fusion protein within living cells, we performed the CETSA, which measures changes in the protein’s thermal stability (melting point) under the compound’s presence versus absence. To ensure that our CETSA workflow was functioning properly,1,2 we performed the assay using brigatinib (250 nM), a compound known to bind EML4::ALK in ALK fusion-expressing cell lines, and it was successful (Figure S11). This validation step was performed to confirm that our CETSA protocol reliably detects drug-induced thermal stabilization of a known fusion oncogene under the experimental conditions used in this study. However, applying the CETSA experiment to KMT2A::AFF1 was not technically feasible in our hands due to constraints on target detectability. KMT2A::AFF1 is an exceptionally large (∼250 kDa) fusion protein that is predominantly localized to the nucleus and tightly associated with the chromatin. As a result, CETSA was technically not feasible for this target in our experimental system. Under CETSA-compatible solubilization conditions, the KMT2A::AFF1 fusion protein could not be reliably extracted or quantitatively detected. We attempted detection using both an MLL (KMT2A) N-terminal antibody and an AFF1 C-terminal antibody; however, neither approach yielded a reproducible, quantifiable signal suitable for CETSA. The mild lysis buffers required to preserve CETSA sensitivity failed to solubilize sufficient amounts of the nuclear fusion protein, whereas the stronger detergents necessary for protein recovery markedly compromised signal quality and reproducibility. Consequently, the absence of a stable, quantifiable target band constitutes a fundamental technical limitation, rendering CETSA results uninterpretable. Repeating the assay in additional isothermal conditions or in alternative cellular backgrounds would not help overcome this intrinsic limitation in target detectability.

Clinically, it is evident that KMT2A::AFF1 contributes significantly to the poor prognosis in patients with AML and ALL. However, there were no effective drugs to target this fusion protein specifically. This is the first study of in silico drug-docking simulations using the central dogma, beginning with the DNA breakpoint and proceeding through the fusion transcript and protein sequences. The 3D structures of the fusion protein and virtual screening were employed to identify potential drugs that bind to the AFF1 CHD of KMT2A::AFF1, which is essential for the recruitment of SEC and histone methyltransferases that upregulate leukemogenic genes, such as HOXA9, MEIS1, and MYC. In this study, we generated 26 possible KMT2A::AFF1 sequences and their corresponding 3D protein structures. The binding site determination using SiteMap showed that ligand binding occurred within the correct region and was thermodynamically favorable. The list of tested drugs with FDA approval limits the study’s scope to those of high clinical relevance. The design of the ABCD virtual screening and filtering schemes provided insight into the drug-binding specificity of KMT2A::AFF1, but not for wild-type AFF1. From this study, we concluded that six compounds—avobenzone, sulisobenzone, nilutamide, samarium (153Sm) lexidronam, and valrubicin—possess high binding affinity to the CHD in KMT2A::AFF1. However, due to the large size of KMT2A::AFF1, universally low pLDDT and Verify3D scores were observed in the larger regions in all predicted KMT2A::AFF1 structures. Moreover, these regions are presented as random coils in the 3D presentation. This was due to a lack of predicted structures in the database, suggesting that the AlphaFold2 model still relies on the accessibility of training data. To successfully conduct a similar drug docking simulation study on the inner motifs of a large protein, a more potent learning model that requires less training data is needed. We pivoted our structures of interest to #7, #10, and #16 due to the lack of established leukemia cell lines that express the breakpoints of the best ones (i.e., #4 and #22). To circumvent this issue, a leukemia cell line with desired KMT2A::AFF1 breakpoints or protein can be established by CRISPR gene editing or gene transduction into human leukocyte progenitor cells to induce in vitro leukemogenesis. Indeed, a study published in 2019 used CRISPR-Cas9 to induce t(11;19) translocation and generate the KMT2A-ENL fusion protein in human hematopoietic cells, which successfully transformed into leukemia cells in vitro.41 This approach would allow validation of in silico findings in the fusion protein, even if it is not present in any established human cell line. The future direction of this study would involve: (1) determining the worthiness of our clinical pursuit of the six drug candidates, and (2) in vitro validation of our findings using leukemic cell lines. For validation, drug candidates with good docking scores will be tested in AML/ALL cell lines (MV4;11, RS4;11, NALM-19, SEM, and SEMK2) to evaluate cytotoxicity, KMT2A::AFF1 binding kinetics, and downstream gene expression, which are known to be specifically modulated by KMT2A::AFF1. Drugs with a lower docking score and/or AML/ALL cell lines devoid of the KMT2A::AFF1 fusion protein can be included as a negative control to confirm the drug potency against KMT2A::AFF1. While the FusionTarget pipeline is designed to be broadly applicable to various human fusion proteins, its practical utility in different disease contexts remains to be further validated through direct experimental evidence of drug-protein interactions.

Limitations of the study

Our fusion protein modeling is based on AlphaFold2. The AlphaFold2 sequence database for MSAs does not include fusion protein sequences, and the known templates are usually limited to the functional domains of wild-type proteins, meaning it was trained on wild-type protein information. Most of all, the key to the AlphaFold2algorithm lies in co-evolutionary information across species. Therefore, fusion proteins, typically made by the structural variants of the individual genome, are expected not to have co-evolutionary single-nucleotide variants in general. As a matter of fact, the fusion protein is a monomer; however, because of the special nature of the two proteins joined, the MSA and template searching steps are similar to those for a multimer prediction problem. So, for the MSA part, we could use AlphaFold-multimer42 or DMfold.43 But, we still lack the known templates.

One can discern the lack of learned features in the disordered, low-complexity regions, which is related to phase separation shown in multiple transcription factor fusion proteins. In this study, we did not intend to predict the full 3D structure, including disordered and low-complexity regions of our fusion proteins. Rather, we hypothesized that a little longer, fusion-context-based modeling of the targeting domain area structure will provide better knowledge of our targeting domain area than one relying on the wild-type protein’s targeting domain area. With current technology, targeting the disordered, low-complexity regions of proteins based on their structures is very difficult.

Despite these limitations, one rationale for us moving forward is that we have a clear target domain in our targeting fusion oncoproteins. For our fusion proteins, we have a clear rationale for targeting their functional domains with proper folding. We wanted to block the binding of the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion protein to DNA and inhibit its tumorigenic effects on downstream oncogenes. So, our target domain in EWSR1::FLI1 corresponded to the DBD of FLI1. This domain has a well-folded structure. A slightly extended and modified conformation of this domain, based on the fusion protein context, will provide better guidance for identifying its small molecule binding partners. Our filtering based on virtual screening of six DBD-retained EWSR1::FLI1 fusion proteins provides a robust criterion for identifying consistent, potentially inhibitory small molecules. For KMT2A::AFF1, unlike the largely disordered regions of the fusion protein, the CHD is a well-folded, globular domain that provides a “druggable” pocket for small-molecule binding. As a scaffold for the SEC, the CHD is essential for recruiting P-TEFb to drive oncogenic transcription. Disruption of this specific domain offers a precise therapeutic window to collapse the aberrant transcriptional machinery of the fusion oncoprotein while minimizing off-target effects. Sequence wise, the CHD is largely conserved across all discovered KMT2A::AFF1 forms, providing a stable motif for therapeutic targeting. Currently, the 3D structures of the targeting domains in breakpoint-specific fusion proteins are unavailable. AlphaFold2 is the best option for us among other tools. We do not 100% trust AlphaFold2. However, we can consider that the AlphaFold2-predicted functional domain area of our target protein is reliable (all RMSDs were very similar to those of the known wild-type-based functional domain structure PDB, as less than 2 Å), but we assume that there exists fusion protein context-contributed conformational change of the targeting functional domain structure in our predicted fusion protein structure. From our downstream analysis, we see that treating our chosen drugs on the AlphaFold2-predicted structure of the well-folded area with our approach affected the key downstream genes as expected.

A recent study provided a potential ligand to the input protein sequence (DrugCLIP44). However, basically, it requires a 3D structure. If the user provides the target protein sequence, it runs the default AlphaFold2background and uses the AlphaFold2-predicted 3D structure. One generative AI approach45 suggests novel peptides that balance the target and off-target proteins based on the user-input fusion protein sequence, with a rationale like ours—targeting a fusion protein specifically while avoiding off-targets. However, it predicts the peptides, not the small molecules.

From the superimposition of six individual fusion protein structures of EWSR1::FLI1 and known PDB structures, we identified a putative fusion-specific pocket in all fusion proteins by using the modeling framework. However, these features are model-derived hypotheses that require future high-resolution experimental validation (e.g., via X-ray crystallography or cryo-electron microscopy [cryo-EM]).

Our molecular docking and MDSs were initiated from a single representative conformation for each isoform, typically the top-ranked AlphaFold2 model. While this approach allows for a consistent comparative analysis across the pipeline, it does not fully capture the stochastic nature of protein-ligand interactions. Future research incorporating multiple starting seeds, ensemble-based docking across diverse conformational states, and enhanced sampling molecular dynamics simulation (MDS) methods would further validate the dynamic robustness and reproducibility of the reported binding modes.

While our data demonstrated the functional impact of brigatinib on the KMT2A::AFF1 signaling axis, the absence of direct biochemical evidence for target engagement remains a limitation of this study. KMT2A fusion proteins are exceptionally large and structurally complex, forming dynamic interaction networks and transcriptionally active condensates that are technically challenging to study using conventional biophysical or antibody-based approaches. For instance, compounds that modulate transcriptional coupling may do so without disrupting the overall condensate architecture, making it difficult to detect binding through routine immunofluorescence or CETSA. Therefore, we cannot exclude the possibility that the observed effects are mediated by indirect mechanisms or by the modulation of downstream signaling.

To address this, future studies should employ high-sensitivity in vitro biophysical assays, such as SPR, biolayer interferometry (BLI), or ITC, using purified recombinant AFF1-CHDs or ectopically expressed fusion constructs. While such simplified systems may not fully recapitulate the native 3D structural context or the in vivo conformational accessibility of the full-length protein, they would provide essential quantitative data on the binding affinity and kinetics of the brigatinib-AFF1 interaction.

Our current repurposing screen was restricted to a library of FDA-approved drugs sourced from DrugBank. To expand the chemical space of the FusionTarget pipeline, larger and more diverse libraries should be integrated. Future iterations could incorporate the Broad Institute’s Drug Repurposing Hub, which includes clinical-stage and pre-clinical compounds or the National Cancer Institute (NCI) database of clinical trial-associated molecules. By applying a rigorous filtration process—encompassing drug-likeness, Lipinski’s Rule of Five, and molecular weight constraints—the pipeline could be scaled to evaluate broader collections, such as 6,125 compounds from the Drug Repurposing Hub or over 10,000 molecules from the NCI database.

Ultimately, while further validation of lead compounds using diverse experimental models is necessary, this study provides proof-of-concept for a bioinformatics-driven discovery framework. Our findings demonstrate that the FusionTarget pipeline can successfully nominate repurposable drugs or identify novel small molecules specific to fusion oncoproteins. This approach serves as a systematic cornerstone for the bioinformatic-led development of targeted therapies against fusion-driven malignancies.

Method details

Fusion breakpoint information

From the most comprehensive fusion gene resource, FusionGDB2, and manually curated fusion gene resources (COSMIC fusion and CCLE fusion), we integrated the known fusion breakpoint information for the EWSR1::FLI1 and KMT2A::AFF1 fusion genes.

Open reading frame (ORF) annotations

Between the 5’-partner gene and the 3’-partner gene, we checked the open reading frame of the full-length fusion transcript sequence. When both breakpoints of 5’- and 3’-genes are located inside the coding region (CDS), the number of fusion transcript sequences from the transcription start site of the 5 ’- gene to the transcription end site of the 3 ’- gene is a multiple of three. We reported this fusion gene as ‘in-frame.’ If there are one or two nucleotide insertions, we report it as a ‘frame-shift.’ For the in-frame fusion genes, we created the full-length fusion transcript sequences, and we ran the open reading frame finder (ORFfinder), which mainly selects the longest ORFs from the six-frame-based translation.55

3D structure prediction of fusion proteins

We predicted the 3D structures of these 22 protein sequences, generated by multiple fusion gene breakpoints and multiple gene isoforms of EWSR1::FLI1, using AlphaFold2. For the prediction of KMT2A::AFF1 fusion protein 3D structures, we input 26 KMT2A::AFF1 fusion protein sequences. For each protein sequence input, AlphaFold2 generates multiple structural predictions. We selected the PDB structure with the highest confidence score. The best output structure, ranked_0.pdb, was used for downstream analyses and binding-site prediction.

Small molecules library

We curated a ligand library consisting of 1,600 FDA-approved drugs sourced from DrugBank. This collection was further refined using the ZINC database, ensuring a tailored set of compounds. All ligands were preprocessed and optimized using the LigPrep module from the Schrödinger suite. LigPrep is a pivotal tool in drug discovery, ensuring consistency and optimization of input ligands.

Preparation of fusion protein structure and binding site identification

We preprocess the receptor protein before performing virtual screening to achieve optimal accuracy. We used the Protein Preparation Wizard from Schrödinger (Schrödinger, LLC, New York, NY, 2024). This tool was designed to handle metal ionization states effectively and bond-order enumerations for HET groups, and it also provides the option to exclude co-crystallized water molecules. Crucial quality checks were implemented to highlight residues with missing atoms or multiple occupancies. Additionally, this tool enabled the accurate determination of ligand protonation states and the optimal protonation states of histidine residues and corrected any misplaced heavy atoms in specific amino acid side chains, such as arginine, glutamine, and histidine. Ultimately, the hydrogen bond network within the protein was systematically optimized, and a restrained minimization was executed to resolve any strained molecular geometries and clashes. Once we obtained the processed, energy-minimized stable protein structure, we performed a binding site identification search using Schrödinger’s SiteMap tool. SiteMap provided a site score for the predicted binding site based on multiple factors, including site size, solvent exposure, hydrophobicity, hydrophilicity, and the presence of hydrogen bond donors and acceptors.

Selection of the fusion protein structures

Among all predicted fusion protein structures, we selected the structures with reliable evidence. Among all predicted fusion protein structures, we selected those with high pLDDT scores (>70) in binding sites within the targeting domain for the next step. We predicted the binding sites of individual fusion proteins using Maestro’s SiteMap (Schrodinger Inc.). The SiteMap provides rankings for multiple potential ligand-binding sites, taking into account parameters such as druggability score, site score, number of site points, and site volume. For all predicted fusion protein 3D structures, the 1st-ranked binding sites were chosen. Residual pLDDT scores were calculated using AlphaPickle42 with the plot size of 12 inches and an increment of 50 residues. After preprocessing of predicted protein structure .pdb files, including H-hydrogen optimization, water molecule removal, and restrained minimization. The parameter for binding sites was set to require at least 15 residues and to use the most restrictive definition of hydrophobicity.

Receptor grid generation and virtual screening

We generated receptor grids that encapsulated the volume of the receptor’s binding sites, where ligands could potentially bind during docking. This process was executed using the GLIDE module in the Schrödinger software suite, specifically focusing on the binding-site regions identified by predictive tools such as SiteMap. The grid dimensions were tailored to accurately represent the spatial constraints and characteristics of these binding sites, ensuring an optimal environment for ligand docking. Following grid generation, we conducted a virtual screening process to identify small molecules with a high probability of binding effectively to the target fusion proteins associated with pediatric sarcomas. We screened a comprehensive library of small molecules against the receptor grids using the GLIDE module. The screening process employed standard precision (SP) and extra precision (XP) docking modes to enhance the reliability and accuracy of the results. The virtual screening yielded scores for each molecule, including docking scores, Glide scores, hydrogen bonding interactions, and estimates of free energy binding. These scores provided a comprehensive view of the binding potential of each molecule. We selected the top candidates based on their lowest docking and Glide scores, which indicate the strongest and most energetically favorable interactions with the receptor. The identified compounds represent promising leads for further investigation in targeting pediatric sarcoma fusion proteins.

Hierarchical lead drug identification

To identify small molecules with high binding selectivity for the fusion protein over the wild-type (WT) counterpart, we employed a multi-stage hierarchical filtering strategy. Each of the six EWSR1::FLI1 fusion protein isoforms was treated as an independent conformational ensemble for the docking simulations. Binding affinity scores were obtained through ABCD condition screening for each isoform, and to ensure comparability across different structural models, we performed quantile normalization for each fusion protein isoform across the four screening conditions (A, B, C, and D). Initial drug candidates were selected if the normalized binding score for condition A (fusion protein) was higher than that for condition D (wild-type) and if the compound was consistently identified across multiple fusion protein isoforms. These candidates were further prioritized based on their biological relevance, specifically focusing on compounds with previously reported anti-cancer activity in literature or clinical databases. To evaluate the structural stability of the predicted binding poses, we performed individual molecular dynamics simulations (MDS) for each fusion protein isoform in complex with the selected drugs. Subsequently, the prioritized candidates underwent in vitro cell viability assays to confirm their functional impact on Ewing sarcoma cell models. The Welch’s two-sample t-test was utilized as a final confirmatory step for the most promising lead, Trifluridine, to statistically validate its binding selectivity. We compared the normalized binding affinities of the six fusion protein isoforms (n=6) against the normalized wild-type scores (n=6) derived from the corresponding independent normalization matrices. The Welch’s t-test was selected as the appropriate model to account for potential differences in variance between the fusion and wild-type groups. Since the t-test was used to confirm a single pre-validated lead candidate rather than to explore whole-library screening, no multiple-testing correction was applied at this stage.

Molecular dynamics simulation (MDS)

After identifying the highest-affinity drug molecules from our virtual screening, we searched the cancer drug literature to validate our findings and chose candidates for subsequent Molecular Dynamics Simulations (MDS). To assess the stability of the complex, MDS was performed for all top drug molecules and all fusion protein isoforms to analyze atomic and molecular motion during drug-target interactions in the system using Desmond. The MD simulation coordinates were obtained from the receptor-ligand complex. Simple Point Charge (SPC) water molecules were added with each water box measuring orthorhombic dimensions of 9 Å × 9 Å × 9 Å to ensure that the entirety of the complex was covered in the solvent. The system was neutralized by balancing the net charge of the system by adding Cl- counter ions. The system was minimized for 2000 iterations of a hybrid of the steepest descent and the limited memory Broyden – Fletcher – Gold farb-Shanno LBFGS algorithms, with a convergence threshold of 1.0 kcal/mol/Å. The whole system was subjected to 300 K and 1.0325 bar pressure for 50 ns at NPT ensemble of MD simulation with a recording interval of 50 ps for total energy (kcal/mol). The structural modifications and real-time behavior of the complex were computed using root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) and potential energy of the receptor-ligand complex over 50 ns of simulation. For further validation of the EWSR1::FLI1 fusion protein-specific binding compared to the wild-type FLI1 protein, we compared the MDS across 200 nsec between EWSR1::FLI1 (587AA) and trifluridine, as well as the MDS between FLI1 wild-type and trifluridine.

Drug sensitivity assay for EWSR1::FLI1

IC50 was determined for each cell line/drug combination. The drug concentration range was based on previous literature for each drug. Cells were plated at 1000-2000 cells/well into a 96-well dish. The cells were treated with a drug dilution series for 72 hours. After 72 hours, cell survival was determined using a colorimetric reagent, CCK-8 (Dojindo: Rockville, MD). 10 uL of CCK-8 was added to each well, and wells were incubated for 2 hours. 450 nm absorbance was read 2 hours after the addition of the reagent using the vice Plate Reader, and it was performed in replicates of four. IC50 was calculated using Graphpad 9.5.1. Data was normalized based on the drug solvent. The non-linear fit algorithm calculated the IC50 for each cell/drug combination.

Cell viability assay for EWSR1::FLI1

Cell proliferation was assessed by seeding 1000-5000 cells/well into a 96-well dish. Cell viability was monitored over a 72-144-hour time frame. Before each time point, colorimetric reagent CCK-8 was added to the corresponding wells (Dojindo). Absorbance was read 3 hours after the addition of the reagent using the Molecular Devices Plate Reader.

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR (qPCR) for EWSR1::FLI1

'RNA was extracted using the Direct-zol RNA Kits (R2051, Zymo) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (Bio-Rad, Cat# 170-8840, Bio-Rad). qPCR was performed using SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Sypermix (Cat# 1725270, Bio-Rad) using the Bio-Rad Opus 384 Real-Time PR System. Gene expression levels were normalized to Beta-Actin.

Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA) for EWSR1::FLI1

Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA) was performed as previously described (1,2) to evaluate drug–protein interactions in RD-ES1 cells. Cells were cultured in 100 × 20 mm tissue culture dishes to approximately 90% confluence, detached with trypsin, collected by centrifugation, and resuspended in PBS. Cell lysis was achieved by liquid nitrogen treatment followed by three freeze–thaw cycles. Whole-cell lysates were adjusted to a final concentration of 1.5 μg/μL and divided into two treatment groups: trifluridine (Tri; 2× IC50, 1 μM) or DMSO control. Lysates were incubated for 30 min at room temperature. Thermal denaturation was carried out using a Bio-Rad T100 thermal cycler for 3 min at temperatures ranging from 55°C to 85°C. Following heating, samples were centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 30 min at 4°C to separate precipitated proteins from soluble fractions. Supernatants were collected and stored at −80°C until further analysis. For Western blotting, 5 μg of protein per sample was loaded onto SDS–PAGE gels. Membranes were probed with anti-FLI1 primary antibody (Abcam, EPR4646) followed by HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, #7074). Chemiluminescent signals were detected using a Bio-Rad ChemiDoc imaging system. Band intensities were quantified by densitometric analysis using ImageJ (v1.48).

PCR and sanger sequencing for KMT2A::AFF1

Genomic DNA was extracted using the DNA Mini Kit (51306, Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA concentration and purity were measured using a Nanodrop® (NanoDrop Technologies Inc.). PCR amplification was performed using the AccuPower HotStart PCR Premix (K5051-1, Bioneer) with primers specific to the target sequences. PCR products were purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (28106, Qiagen). Purified amplicons were subjected to Sanger sequencing using the same primers employed for PCR amplification. Sequencing reactions were performed by Macrogen (Seoul, South Korea).

Drug sensitivity assay for KMT2A::AFF1

Drug sensitivity was assessed by determining the IC50 values for each cell line and drug combination. The concentration range for each drug was selected based on previously published literature. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 10,000-20,000 cells per well in 100 μL of culture medium, depending on the cell line. Cells were treated with serial dilutions of each drug by adding an additional 100 μL of drug-containing medium and incubated for 120 hours.

Following incubation, cell viability was assessed using the Ez-Cytox reagent (EZ-3000, DoGenBio). 20 μL of Ez-Cytox reagent was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for an additional 2–3 hours at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a SpectraMax® 96-well plate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA). All experiments were performed in triplicate. Absorbance values were normalized to the corresponding solvent controls, and IC50 values were calculated using a non-linear regression model in GraphPad Prism 10.

RNA sequencing for KMT2A::AFF1

RNA sequencing was performed by Macrogen following the manufacturer's protocols and reagents. Sample libraries were prepared independently using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, Inc.). The indexed libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, Inc.) by Macrogen using paired-end (2 × 101 bp) sequencing. The quality of the sequencing data was assessed using FastQC (v0.11.7, RRID:SCR_014583), and trimming was performed using Trimmomatic (v0.38, RRID:SCR_011848). High-throughput sequencing data were mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.1.0, RRID:SCR_015530) and Bowtie2 (v2.3.4.1, RRID:SCR_016368). Following alignment, StringTie (v2.1.3b, RRID:SCR_016323) was used to assemble the aligned reads into transcripts and estimate their abundance.

Transcriptome data analysis for KMT2A::AFF1

Given the exploratory nature of the experiment, transcriptome analyses were designed to focus on dose-dependent transcriptional trends and pathway-level patterns rather than statistical inference at the level of individual genes. Gene-level expression analyses were performed using DESeq2 (v1.50.2, RRID:SCR_015687), with raw read counts used as input for normalization and downstream analyses. To evaluate global transcriptional programs modulated by Brigatinib treatment, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted using Hallmark gene sets from the MSigDB(v25.1.1, RRID:SCR_016863). Genes were ranked according to dose-associated expression changes, and enrichment analysis was performed using a preranked GSEA framework. Normalized enrichment scores (NES) and adjusted p-values were calculated for each pathway, and significantly enriched pathways (adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05) were visualized based on their NES values. To further characterize transcriptional programs associated with KMT2A::AFF1-driven leukemia, a selected gene set comprising KMT2A::AFF1-associated transcriptional regulators and downstream target genes was assembled based on prior literature.56 This gene set included core components of the KMT2A transcriptional complex, HOXA cluster genes, and key downstream effectors implicated in leukemogenic transcriptional regulation. Normalized expression values for the selected genes were scaled per gene using a z-score transformation and visualized using unsupervised hierarchical clustering. Heatmaps were generated to illustrate relative expression patterns across control, Brigatinib 100 nM, and Brigatinib 500 nM treatment conditions, enabling visualization of dose-dependent transcriptional reprogramming.

qRT-PCR for KMT2A::AFF1

MV4-11 and RS4;11 cells were seeded at 5 × 105 cells per well in 12-well plates and treated with 500nM of Brigatinib for 24, 48, or 72 hours. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 125 × g for 5 min, and total RNA was extracted from the resulting pellets using the RNeasy Mini Kit (74004, Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop® spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies Inc.).

cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using SuperScript™ IV Reverse Transcriptase (18090200, Thermo Fisher Scientific). qRT-PCR was performed on QuantStudio™ 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, RRID:SCR_013188) using 2× SYBR Green Master Mix (4368702, Applied Biosystems) in 384-well plates. Gene expression was normalized to that of the housekeeping gene UBQLN.

CETSA workflow validation using an ALK fusion–positive model

To validate the CETSA workflow, an independent CETSA experiment was conducted using the ALK fusion–positive lung cancer cell line H2228. H2228 cells (1 × 107 cells per condition) were treated with either DMSO or Brigatinib (250 nM) for 6 hours under standard culture conditions. Following treatment, cells were harvested and resuspended in 800 μL of ice-cold PBS supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail. Cell suspensions were aliquoted into PCR tubes and divided into seven temperature conditions ranging from 46 °C to 58 °C at 2 °C intervals using a thermal cycler. Samples were heated for 3 minutes at each temperature point, followed by equilibration at room temperature for 3 minutes. Cells were subsequently lysed by three cycles of freeze–thawing using liquid nitrogen, followed by incubation at 37 °C in a thermal cycler. Lysates were centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 20 minutes at 4 °C to remove insoluble and aggregated proteins. The resulting soluble fractions were collected and subjected to immunoblotting analysis as described above.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) for KMT2A::AFF1

Cellular thermal shift assays were performed to evaluate target engagement of Brigatinib in intact cells. K-562, MV-4-11, and RS4;11 cells were treated with vehicle or Brigatinib at the indicated concentrations for 1–3 hours under standard culture conditions. Following treatment, cells were harvested by centrifugation, washed once with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in PBS supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (P3100-001, GenDEPOT). Cell suspensions were aliquoted into PCR tubes and subjected to a temperature gradient ranging from 40°C to 68°C using a thermal cycler. Samples were heated for 3 minutes at each temperature point, followed by equilibration at room temperature for 3 minutes. Cells were subsequently lysed by three cycles of freeze–thawing using liquid nitrogen, followed by incubation at 37 °C in a thermal cycler. Lysates were centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 20 minutes at 4 °C to remove insoluble and aggregated proteins. The resulting soluble fractions were collected and subjected to immunoblotting analysis as described below.

Immunoblotting for KMT2A::AFF1

Cells treated with Brigatinib (10, 100, or 1000 nM) were lysed in 1× RIPA buffer (9806S, Cell Signaling Technology) supplemented with protease inhibitors, incubated on ice for 15 minutes with intermittent vortexing, and centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 20 minutes at 4 °C. Protein concentrations were quantified using the Bradford assay, and equal amounts of lysate were resolved by SDS-PAGE followed by transfer onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010). Membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk for 30 minutes and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies directed against MEIS1 (Abcam, ab19867), HOXA9 (Proteintech, 18501-1-AP), and HSP90 (Cell Signaling Technology, 4877), each used at a 1:1000 dilution. After washing in TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. Signals were developed using an ECL substrate (GE Healthcare, 12316992) and visualized on film (AGFA, CP-BU).

Quantification and statistical analysis

We clarify that the Welch’s t-test was used as a final confirmatory step for our top-ranked lead, Trifluridine, which had already passed multiple stringent filtering stages, to ensure the significance of the difference in the binding affinities between A and D (n=6, p<0.05). Because this was a hypothesis-driven verification of a single pre-validated candidate rather than an exploratory inference across the entire chemical library, multiple testing correction was not applied. This prioritized sensitivity within our discovery-oriented framework, ensuring that potentially effective therapeutic candidates were not overlooked due to overly conservative statistical adjustments.
