Exploring the optimal treatment modality for non-small cell lung cancer after stage N2 surgery based on the SEER database and constructing a predictive model for the beneficiary population
Authors: Lei Liu, Liangxin Sun, Leilei Guo, Yilong Mao, Chencong Li, Yiqian Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•For surgically operable N2 stage NSCLC patients, adjuvant radiotherapy post-surgery is not recommended.•Use the "best candidate model" to select reasonable factors.•Treat a benefit probability greater than 0.62 as an exploratory conclusion. If the patient's benefit probability is greater than 0.62, consideration can be given to performing only adjuvant chemotherapy post-surgery.

Background

Currently, NSCLC (Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer) patients account for approximately 85 % of the lung cancer patient population [1], and as extremely heterogeneous patients with III-N2 staged NSCLC, their survival variations are extremely obvious, and even after systematic surgical treatment, their postoperative 5-year survival rate have substantial fluctuations. Considering the possibility of local recurrence and distant metastasis after surgery, it is particularly important to provide necessary adjuvant therapy after surgery. However, even after complete resection and subsequent adjuvant chemotherapy, treatment failure occurs in 35 %−55 % of patients [2]. In order to control local recurrence as much as possible, some studies have pointed out the need for postoperative local radiation therapy to the lesion stump and involved lymph nodes in these patients to further improve disease-free survival [3].

After decades of development, although PORT (postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy) has been widely used in patients with pN2 stage NSCLC, there has been an ongoing debate in the medical community as to whether or not PORT should be used as a standard treatment modality for patients with completely resected pN2 stage NSCLC [4]. Both most of the retrospective studies over the past decades and two large prospective clinical trials, Lung ART [5] and PORT-C [6], which have recently concluded that PORT has no statistically significant benefit on the OS(overall survival) of patients with stage N2 NSCLC. Among them, in the PORT-C study, patients who did not undergo PORT had a longer median survival time than those who underwent PORT, although there was no statistically significant difference between the two. Subsequent research over the past five years has likewise failed to yield a consensus. For instance, a retrospective study similarly concluded that postoperative radiotherapy (PORT) did not confer an or patients with stage IIIA-N2 non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) who underwent surgical resection [7]. A comprehensive survey of radiation oncology experts indicated that PORT is recommended based on risk factors such as the status of lymph node metastasis stations [8]. A 2023 retrospective study utilizing the Chinese SEER database found that N2 NSCLC patients who did not receive PORT exhibited superior OS compared to those who did, with the difference achieving statistical significance; this finding was supported by external validation [9]. A prospective study published in 2025, involving 132 patients, demonstrated that PORT increased the 3-year OS rate in patients with completely resected stage IIIA(N2) NSCLC who were at high risk for locoregional recurrence [10]. A review published in 2023 integrating 11 past trials and 2343 subjects showed that PORT was detrimental to those patients with R0 resected NSCLC and that there was an 18 % relative increase in the risk of death associated with PORT, which corresponded to a decrease in five-year OS from 58 % to 53 % [11] and that, based on the current national and international guidelines, the There is no uniformity as to whether PORT should be performed for NSCLC under stage N2. The 2024 ASCO [12] (American Society of Clinical Oncology) guidelines suggest a weak recommendation for routine continuation of PORT for pN2 stage patients who have received platinum-based chemotherapy and have no evidence of extracapsular invasion of lymph nodes. The 2024 NCCN [13] (National Comprehensive Cancer Network) guidelines recommend considering radiotherapy for pN2 stage patients, with a recommendation grade of 2A. The 2024 CSCO [14] (Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology) updated guidelines classify PORT for N2 stage NSCLC as level 2B evidence, and there is still controversy over whether PORT should be performed for postoperative patients with postoperative pathological stage N2 NSCLC. Therefore, combining the current national and international guideline consensus, the recommended level of PORT is not high for patients with stage N2 NSCLC. Selective application is recommended.

Most of the current research directions for PORT for patients with stage N2 NSCLC focus on screening for populations that can benefit from it, for example, higher positive lymph node statistic values [15],males [16]or the adoption of sequential radiotherapy and chemotherapy, newer radiotherapy techniques, etc. [17]. However, the vast majority of the studies were retrospective, which may cause selection bias, and the cardiopulmonary toxicity of radiotherapy is an unavoidable and important issue related to PORT. In 2007, by retrospectively analyzing whether the cardiac mortality rate of patients with NSCLC in the SEER database had been reduced over time, and by setting the independent variable to the use or non-use of PORT, the final conclusions were drawn : The risk of cardiac death associated with PORT declined between 1983 and 1988. The researchers suggested that this may be related to advances in radiotherapy equipment and radiotherapy delivery protocols [18]. In the transition from conventional 2D radiotherapy to 3D-CRT (Three-Dimensional Conformal Radiation Therapy), several studies have demonstrated that new radiotherapy techniques can significantly reduce radiation toxicity [19]. Moreover, IMRT (Intensity-modulated radiation therapy) and VMAT (Volumetric Modulated Arc Therapy), which have been vigorously developed in recent years, increase the radiation dose to the target area while more strictly controlling the radiation dose to the surrounding normal tissues. Technically, this may be able to further minimize the side effects of radiotherapy and ultimately achieve an OS benefit. However, large prospective studies in recent years have not supported this conjecture; in the Lung ART study, the radiotherapy technique used for most of the patients was 3D-CRT, and the final results proved that the incidence of grade 3–4 cardiopulmonary toxicity in the PORT group was much higher than that in the group that did not undergo PORT (11 % vs. 5 %) [5]. In the follow-up PORT-C study, most patients took IMRT, and although only one patient developed grade 3 radiation pneumonitis and no patient developed radiotherapy-related grade 4–5 adverse reactions, there was still no significant difference in OS between the two groups [6]. Thus, on the one hand, the benefits of PORT do not seem to offset the toxicity disadvantages based on current technology, and even cardiopulmonary toxicity may lead to a further shortening of patient survival. On the other hand, most studies have suggested that PORT can improve local control rates, but it has not been able to translate DFS (disease-free survival) benefits into OS benefits in terms of final outcomes. With the existence of therapeutic controversies, it is specifically a matter of choice of treatment regimen for the individual physician and patient.

Previous discussions of PORT in the seventh edition staging of lung cancer have focused on the T1–3N2M0 staging. In two large prospective studies, the analysis of IIIA-N2 staging remained in the seventh edition of the AJCC (American Joint Committee on Cancer), whereas according to the eighth edition of T staging, a portion of patients with T3 needed to be classified as T4. Most of these patients were classified as T4 because of postoperative pathology suggesting tumor size and are classified as T4, but are not associated with invasion of adjacent tissues such as the esophagus, heart, and great vessels, and surgical radical resection can be attempted as well [20]. Some studies have pointed out that under the eighth edition of staging, patients with T4N2M0 also have the value of benefiting from surgical treatment first, and the possibility of PORT can be discussed [21,22]. Moreover, in recent years, only the benefit of radiotherapy in IIIA-N2 staging (T1–2N2M0) has been discussed in retrospective studies of operable N2 NSCLC in the eighth edition of the AJCC staging guidelines [23,24], which has led to the possibility that some patients with N2 staging who remain surgically indicated during the change in staging from the seventh to the eighth edition may have been overlooked. 2024 CSCO [14] Guidelines, the controversy over adjuvant radiotherapy for patients with postoperative pathology suggestive of stage N2 NSCLC is also not limited to fixed T staging. Survival information on these patients, who appear to have lost their surgical indication in the new staging system, is also of value for retrospective analysis.

Although the Lung ART and PORT-C trials preliminarily established that PORT is not recommended for patients with operable stage III-N2 NSCLC, they did not resolve the critical need for selecting superior treatment modalities for these patients. Furthermore, contemporary retrospective clinical studies rarely provide clear stratification strategies to guide the choice of optimal treatment approaches. Consequently, the majority of patients within this stage lack detailed evidence-based guidance for selecting postoperative adjuvant therapy modalities. This study will utilize real-world databases to analyze patients with treatment-eligible stage III-N2 NSCLC, comparing survival outcomes between POCRT (postoperative adjuvant chemoradiotherapy) and POCT (postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy) cohorts. Survival differentials will identify prognostically favorable subgroups, enabling the development of a predictive model. Currently, validated models specifically designed to estimate the likelihood of benefit from specific postoperative regimens in this patient population remain underdeveloped. Our research aims to address this critical gap by establishing a multidimensional predictive framework, thereby providing an objective, evidence-based foundation for therapeutic decision-making. Moreover, the model will deliver clinician-specific recommendations to facilitate comprehensive patient selection for optimized treatment strategies. This approach will enable the formulation of precision-tailored therapeutic regimens while concurrently mitigating treatment-related financial burdens for select patients, ultimately enhancing treatment adherence and patient satisfaction.

Materials and methods

Study data sources

The SEER database is an important resource hosted by the National Cancer Institute. Since 1973, the database has included oncologic disease data of oncology patients in many states of the U.S., covering nearly one-third of the population of the entire U.S. region, including the patient's own information (age, gender, ethnicity, place of residence, etc.), pathological characteristics (type of pathology, pathological grading, degree of diffusion, etc.), therapeutic Most of the data in the SEER database is open and free to researchers worldwide, and anyone can apply for access to specific datasets for legitimate academic research. In this study, after downloading version 8.4.3 of the SEER database, we extracted from it patients with operable N2 staged NSCLC diagnosed with a primary single tumor type from 2010–2015. All patients in this study were restaged based on AJCC 8th edition TNM staging. The patient screening process is detailed in Fig. 1. External validation data were obtained from 36 patients with stage N2 NSCLC that were evaluated for feasible surgical treatment in the Departments of Thoracic Surgery, Oncology, and Radiotherapy at the First Hospital of Dalian Medical University during 2010–2020. All of these patients underwent radical surgery, were definitively pN2 after surgery,and were restaged according to the eighth edition of the AJCC staging. Based on the longest survival time of patients extracted from the SEER database in this study, the follow-up time was 143 months. This research has been approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University. All experiments were conducted in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, and informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians. Data inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

(1) NSCLC patients diagnosed with stage T1–3N2M0 according to AJCC 7th edition staging (2010–2015,T1-T3, N2 and N2a/b, M0 under 7th edition staging were selected from SEER database). These patients did not have invasion of the mediastinum, heart, great vessels, ronchi, laryngeal reentrant nerve, bronchus, esophagus and other surrounding adjacent large organs. and restaged according to the eighth edition of the AJCC; (2) age greater than or equal to 18 years; (3) only one type of tumor and a primary tumor in the lung; (4) pathological type of NSCLC (shown in the SEER database as ① squamous carcinoma (ICD-O-3 codes: 8052/3, 8070/3, 8071/3, 8072/3, 8074/3, 8083 /3); ② adenocarcinoma (ICD-O-3 codes: 8140/3, 8250/3, 8251/3, 8252/3, 8253/3, 8254/3, 8255/3, 8260/3, 8263/3, 8290/3, 8310/3, 8323/3, 8333/3, 8470/3, 8471/3, 8480/3, 8481/3, 8490/3, 8550/3, 8551/3, 8574/3); ③ Other (ICD-O-3 codes: 8012/3, 8013/3, 8022/3, 8033/3, 8050/3, 8082/3, 8230/3, 8246/3, 8249/3, 8560/3)). (5) Patients initially underwent surgical treatment, receiving sublobar, lobar, or total lung surgery; (6) underwent conventional postoperative chemotherapy; (7) received or did not receive postoperative radiotherapy; (8) the number of lymph nodes examined and the number of positives were ≥1; and (9) all were pathologically confirmed.

Exclusion criteria

(1) The scope of lymph node removal and the number of removed lymph nodes are not clear;(2) Tumor size is unclear;(3) Follow-up information is missing;(4) Missing clinical or demographic information.

Patient clinical and pathologic information

The clinical and pathological information characteristics of the patients included in this study included the following: Age, Sex, Marital status, Primary tumor site, Specific orientation of the tumor, Grade, Tumor pathology type, T stage, Type of surgical treatment, Postoperative adjuvant treatment modality, LODDS (log odds of positive lymph nodes), Status of pleural invasion, Maximum tumor diameter(mm), and Lymph node clearance range. Detailed categorization was also performed according to the data situation provided by the SEER database. In this study, no missing values occurred in any variables across both the SEER database-derived dataset and the external clinical validation dataset. Among them, Age and Maximum tumor diameter were used as continuous type variables, which were divided into three categorical variables by X-tile software. Age was classified as ≤60, 61–74, and >74; Maximum tumor diameter was classified as ≤38 mm, 39 mm-62 mm, and >62 mm; Sex was classified as female and male; Marital status was classified as Married, Unmarried, and Other; Primary tumor site was classified as Upper lobe, Middle lobe, Lower lobe, and Other; Specific orientation of the tumor was classified as Left and Right; and Grade was classified as I-II, III-IV and Unknown; Tumor pathology type is divided into Squamous carcinoma, Adenocarcinoma, Other; T stage is divided into T1, T2, T3, T4 according to the 8th edition; Surgical treatment type is divided into Sublobular surgery, Lobar surgery, Whole lung surgery; Postoperative adjuvant treatment mode is divided into POCRT, POCT; Status of pleural invasion is divided into No pleural invasion, Pleural invasion; Lymph node clearance range is divided into 1–3, ≥4.

LNR (Lymph node ratio) is often used as an important predictor in studies related to PORT in stage N2.LNR reflects the extent of lymph node dissection. Its main weakness is the inability to further stratify cases with LNR values of 0 or 1, which had been questioned and criticized [25]. And most of the literature is not clear or even conflicting about the extent of LNR classification, in order to overcome this problem as much as possible, LODDS was introduced as a new classification, which is defined as the logarithm of the ratio of the probability of being positive to its probability of being negative when 1 lymph node is examined, which takes into account the degree of surgical radicality of the lymph node dissection, and for the cases where there are no infiltrated lymph nodes or where all the harvested Cases in which all lymph nodes were infiltrated were categorized. In this study, the LODDS was calculated using the formula: loge [(number of positive lymph nodes + 0.5)/(number of negative lymph nodes + 0.5)]. In the formula, a value of 0.5 was added to both the numerator and denominator to avoid singularity. The LODDS was finally categorized into two groups based on the median: ≤ −0.82, > −0.82.

Study groups

A total of 951 patients with operable N2-staged NSCLC were included in this study from the SEER database, and were divided into the “POCRT group” and “POCT group” according to the “Postoperative adjuvant treatment modes”. After the initial classification, PSM (propensity score matching) was performed to reduce the selection bias. After PSM, the group with better prognosis will be selected according to the Kaplan-Meier curve, and divided into the “training set” and “internal validation set”, statistically analyzed and constructed as a prediction model. 36 pN2-staged (AJCC 8th edition staging) NSCLC patients from the First Hospital of Dalian Medical University were considered as an external validation set. Finally, according to the scores of the Nomogram prediction model, the high, and low benefit groups were screened out using R software.

Study endpoints

The primary endpoint of this study is the OS of the study population, which refers to the time from the initiation of treatment (surgical treatment) for patients with NSCLC to the time of death from any cause in the study population. OS is an important indicator for survival analysis in medical statistics. It is used as one of the primary endpoint indicators that can help assess the effectiveness of certain treatments or certain drugs in prolonging patient survival.

Statistical methods

SPSS (version: 27.0) R software (version: 4.4.1) was used as the main statistical research software in this study.

Firstly, the subtype variables will be validated by taking chi-square test. As a non-parametric test, chi-square test is applied to test whether there is correlation or difference between categorical variables.

Subsequently, to minimize selection bias and confounding factors in patient data, PSM and IPTW (Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting) were performed on the preliminarily screened SEER database data using the MatchIt, cobalt, and survey packages in R software. The nearest neighbor matching method was employed to assign the “POCRT group” and “POCT group” at a 1:1 ratio. The caliper value was set to 0.1 on the logit scale, generating SMD (standardized mean differences) and Love plots. Survival outcomes were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves to compare differences in patient information before and after PSM. Survival curves were plotted for both groups before and after PSM. Additionally, to assess whether postoperative adjuvant treatment patterns significantly impacted OS, the Log-rank test was employed to compare differences in survival curves between the two groups before and after PSM.

Finally, using data from patients selected for PSM, robust Cox regression analysis was performed again on the aforementioned 14 clinical and pathological characteristics to identify independent prognostic factors. Both global and covariate-specific Grambsch-Therneau tests were recorded, with the time origin set at the start of surgical treatment. Variables with a two-sided P-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Predictive modeling to identify the beneficiary population

Based on the Kaplan-Meier curve, the group with longer mOS(median overall survival) was considered as the treatment modality favorable group, the group with shorter mOS was considered as the treatment modality unfavorable group, and the mOS of the treatment modality unfavorable group was used as the cutoff; for patients in the treatment modality favorable group who survived longer than the mOS of the treatment modality unfavorable group, they were defined as having a survival benefit while the rest of the patients were defined as having no benefit. This approach, which is derived from several previous retrospective studies, allows for the selection of an advantageous side of the two treatment modalities, the analysis of the data to derive statistically significant influencing factors, and ultimately the selection of the “best candidate” for this advantageous treatment modality [[26], [27], [28]], which can then be further subdivided into the subsequent modalities of the patient's treatment.

After that, the patients in the treatment modality favorable group were randomly divided into the training set and the internal validation set with a ratio of 7:3, and the statistical differences between the training set and the internal validation set were analyzed. The variables with independent prognostic factors were analyzed by one-way and multi-factor logistic regression, and the Nomogram prediction model for the treatment modality favorable group was constructed based on the above variables. The Nomogram prediction model was constructed based on the “rms” package and the “regplot” package in R software.

Validation method

After the Nomogram prediction model is constructed, in order to further evaluate the prediction model, this study will use the AUC value and the calibration curve to evaluate the discriminative ability and calibration ability of the prediction model, and at the same time, adopt the Bootstrap method to draw the calibration curve by 1000 times resampling, in order to validate the consistency of predicted values and actual values. Conduct clinical decision analysis on data from the training set, internal validation set, and external validation set to evaluate the predictive model's practical value in clinical decision-making.

Finally, to further validate the clinical value of the predictive model, this study will redefine the “high-benefit group” and “low-benefit group” based on the predicted probabilities from the Nomogram model using R software. Subsequently, Kaplan-Meier curves will be employed to compare OS between the two groups. Additionally, Kaplan-Meier curves will be used to compare both groups with the non-benefit group in terms of treatment patterns, thereby verifying the predictive capability of the model.

Results

Basic clinical and pathological conditions of screened patients

In this study, NSCLC patients with T1–3N2M0 according to the seventh edition of the staging from 2010–2015 were included in the SEER database, and 951 patients were finally enrolled according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, among which a total of 493 patients were enrolled in the POCRT group, and a total of 458 patients were enrolled in the POCT group. Before PSM, the SMD for Age, Type of surgical treatment, and LODDS in the study population were ≥0.1. Specifically, most patients in the POCRT group were aged 74 years or younger, while the majority of patients in the POCT group were older, and elderly patients were less inclined to undergo PORT. Regarding Type of surgical treatment, patients in the POCRT group were more likely to have Lobar surgery and Sublobar surgery, whereas patients who underwent Whole lung surgery were more inclined to receive POCT. In terms of LODDS, patients with higher LODDS were more likely to choose POCRT treatment, and current treatment guidelines also recommend PORT for patients with a high rate of lymph node metastasis. After PSM, 400 pairs of patients were successfully matched without performing common support trimming. Ultimately, 93 patients were removed from the POCRT group, and 58 patients were removed from the POCT group. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of both groups before and after matching. Before PSM, significant differences were observed between the two groups in certain clinical and pathological features. After PSM, the baseline characteristics of the two groups were balanced, reducing the impact of confounding factors. Supplementary Fig. 1 visually demonstrates the improvement in balance achieved through PSM. Survival analysis results

Data from the two groups of patients before and after PSM were analyzed for survival and compared by Kaplan-Meier survival curves and Log-rank tests, so as to discuss the survival differences of postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy for operable N2-staged NSCLC. In this study, regardless of whether PSM was performed or not, the median survival time in the POCT group showed a prolonged form compared to the POCRT group (Before PSM, 49 months vs. 40 months, HR: 0.82, 95 % CI: 0.71–0.96, P = 0.011; After PSM,48 months vs. 39 months, HR: 0.84, 95 % CI: 0.71–0.99, P = 0.031) (see Fig. 2 for details), therefore, based on this study, it can be preliminarily concluded that for patients with operable N2-staged NSCLC, a better survival advantage can be achieved by POCT alone. Following PSM, E-values were calculated to quantify the robustness of the observed associations. For postoperative adjuvant treatment modality, the E-value was 1.67 (95 % CI: 1.11–2.17). This indicates that unmeasured confounders would need to exhibit association strengths of at least 1.67-fold with both treatment allocation and survival outcomes to fully explain away the protective effect observed for POCT (HR=0.84). For the lymph node ratio index (LODDS > −0.82 vs. ≤ −0.82), the E-value reached 2.19 (95 % CI: 1.67–2.77). This suggests that substantially stronger unmeasured confounding—requiring minimum association strengths of 2.19-fold with both LODDS classification and survival—would be necessary to nullify the observed increased risk associated with higher LODDS values (HR=1.42).Fig. 2Kaplan-Meier curves before and after PSM in patients with operable N2 stage NSCLC.Fig 2

Robust Cox regression analysis results

After PSM, a robust Cox regression analysis was performed on the patient data based on 14 clinicopathological characteristic variables, including Age, Sex, Marital status, Primary tumor site, Specific orientation of the tumor, Grade, Tumor pathology type, T stage, Type of surgical treatment, Postoperative adjuvant treatment modality, LODDS, Status of pleural invasion, Maximum tumor diameter, and the Extent of lymph node clearance, in order to identify appropriate independent prognostic factors. Univariate COX regression analysis showed that advanced Age (61–74: HR:1.48, 95 % CI: 1.22–1.80, P < 0.001; >74: HR: 2.25, 95 % CI: 1.77–2.86, P < 0.001), Male (HR: 1.41, 95 % CI: 1.20–1.65, P < 0.001), higher T stage (T3: HR: 1.68, 95 % CI: 1.34–2.12, P < 0.001; T4: HR: 2.06, 95 % CI: 1.47–2.90, P < 0.001), and the larger the Maximum diameter of the tumor (39mm-62mm:HR: 1.31, 95 % CI: 1.07–1.61, P = 0.008; >62: HR: 1.99, 95 % CI: 1.54–2.56, P < 0.001), and higher LODDS (HR: 1.42, 95 % CI: 1.19–1.69, P < 0.001) were correlates of poorer prognosis for patients, whereas POCT alone (HR: 0.84, 95 % CI: 0.71–0.98, P = 0.026) was suggestive of a better prognosis. Age, Maximum tumor diameter, Sex, Status of pleural invasion, T-stage, LODDS and Postoperative adjuvant treatment modality were again included in the Multifactorial COX regression analysis, which ultimately confirmed that POCT alone was an independent protective factor for patients under the N2 staging (HR: 0.84, 95 % CI: 0.71–0.99, P = 0.037), whereas for the advanced Age (61–74: HR:1.47, 95 % CI: 1.21–1.79, P < 0.001; >74: HR: 2.03, 95 % CI: 1.59–2.61, P < 0.001), Male (HR: 1.41, 95 % CI: 1.20–1.67, P < 0.001), and T3 staging (HR: 1.41, 95 % CI : 1.07–1.88, P = 0.016), and high LODDS (HR: 1.42, 95 % CI: 1.19–1.69, P = 0.037), the survival prognosis of this population was worse. To validate robustness, both univariate and multivariate weighted Cox regression analyses were conducted following IPTW. The results from both approaches consistently showed that POCT significantly reduced the risk of mortality (after PSM-HR = 0.84, 95 % CI: 0.71–0.99, P = 0.037; IPTW-HR = 0.83, 95 % CI: 0.71–0.97, P = 0.02), indicating that the conclusions were not influenced by the method choice. The final analysis in this study utilized the post-PSM data for subsequent analysis for the following reasons: the matched sample achieved balance of covariates between groups, more closely approximating the design of a RCT (Randomized Controlled Trial), thus allowing for a direct comparison of between-group outcome differences. Additionally, the "pseudo-population" generated by IPTW may include fractional weights, which could reduce the interpretability of the results in descriptive statistics.

Based on the survival curves and COX regression analysis, this study now tentatively concludes the following: among the operable N2 stage NSCLC patients collected in the SEER database, the survival of patients who underwent POCT alone was significantly better than that of those who underwent POCRT, and that after surgical treatment and POCT, the need for routine PORT can be considered unnecessary, and POCT alone is a statistically significant protective factor. Based on the above data and conclusions, a nomogram prediction model will be constructed to screen out N2-staged NSCLC patients who are more suitable for POCT.

Based on the post- PSM data analysis in this study, censoring rates for both cohorts remained below 10 % within the 39-month follow-up period, with censoring events predominantly occurring beyond 60 months. Validation of the nomogram prediction model

The final Nomogram prediction model generated in this study will be used for model validation and effect assessment using ROC, AUC values, and calibration curves. In this study, the AUC values for the training set (AUC=0.723, 95 % CI: 0.664–0.782), internal validation set (AUC=0.710, 95 % CI: 0.617–0.803), and external validation set (AUC=0.729, 95 % CI: 0.549–0.910) were all in the range of 0.7–0.9. The calibration curve generated through 1000 bootstrap internal validations closely aligned with the diagonal line. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test yielded non-significant P-values (all >0.05) for each dataset. Specifically, the Brier scores for the training set, internal validation set, and external validation set were all below 0.26, with calibration intercepts approximating 0 and calibration slopes approaching 1, demonstrating strong robustness of the prediction model. These results indicate that the nomogram prediction model possesses excellent predictive accuracy. The ROC, AUC, and calibration curves are presented in Fig. 4. To further distinguish between high and low benefit probabilities and stratify benefits, this study used R software to select the optimal cut-off value based on the benefit probability and stratify patients accordingly. Ultimately, ≤0.62 was defined as the “POCT low-benefit group,” and >0.62 was defined as the “POCT high-benefit group.” Kaplan-Meier survival curves were plotted for the two groups, and Log-rank tests were performed. It should be emphasized that the cut-off value of 0.62 in this study should be regarded as an exploratory result. The final curves showed a statistically significant difference between the two groups (HR: 0.48, 95 % CI: 0.35–0.66, P < 0.001). Subsequently, patients in the POCRT group were compared with these two groups again, and the results are shown in Fig. 6. This indicates that if a patient’s probability of benefit is greater than 0.62, POCT alone can be considered (high-benefit group vs. POCRT group: HR: 0.46, 95 % CI: 0.36–0.58, P < 0.001); if the probability of benefit is ≤0.62, further analysis based on the patient’s specific situation is required (low-benefit group vs. POCRT group: HR: 0.97, 95 % CI: 0.77–1.22, P = 0.80). To prevent potential overfitting of the optimal cut-off value, this study performed internal optimism correction (bootstrap) through 1000 repeated internal validation procedures. The results demonstrated that using R software to select 0.62 as the optimal cut-off value yielded an original C-index (Concordance index) of 0.616 (95 % CI: 0.584–0.649), with an optimism of approximately 0.012. Discussion

Although some of the literature in the past indicated that N2 stage NSCLC patients with a higher percentage of positive lymph nodes have longer survival with PORT [29,30], and that PORT as a local treatment modality can destroy residual tumors and local subclinical foci, which theoretically reduces recurrence and improves treatment outcomes. However, cardiac involvement and lung tissue involvement cannot be avoided during radiotherapy planning designation in the lungs, and therefore radiotherapists try to strike a balance between the target and off-target areas as much as possible to minimize the effects of radiation on the heart. The use of advanced radiation modalities and the introduction of cardiac radiation dose limits in an increasing number of radiotherapy centers nowadays can reduce deaths due to cardiac events [18]. Moreover, dose limitation to the lungs, as the largest organ in the thoracic cavity, needs to be further strictly limited. Therefore, in this study, patients between 2010–2015 were selected to validate and analyze the efficacy of PORT and to construct a prediction model based on the group with a better survival, in order to select an appropriate treatment population.

The conclusion of the present study stated that longer survival was achieved without PORT in patients with operable N2 stage NSCLC, which is contrary to the conclusion of some retrospective studies, but whether or not to perform PORT in patients under this stage is itself controversial, and the two current prospective studies pointed out that there was no statistically significant difference between the two treatment regimens [5,6]. Meanwhile, Yu et al. concluded in a SEER-based retrospective analysis in 2023 that PORT was significantly associated with worsened OS, which supports the findings of the present study [9].

Prognosis and radiotherapy options for patients with N2 staging

Higher T-stages imply larger tumor size and potentially more severe invasion of surrounding adjacent tissues. A 2017 study by Mao et al. based on the seventh edition of staging noted that tumor size was a variable associated with postoperative survival in patients with NSCLC under IIIA-N2 staging [31], and a subsequent study by Chen et al. based on the eighth edition of staging similarly noted that a tumor size of <3 cm was a significant predictor of improved OS [32]. This suggests that smaller tumor loads may correspond to longer survival, thus avoiding, as much as possible, subsequent controversial treatment regimens that increase therapeutic toxicity. In this study, according to the conclusions and the 8th edition staging, if a patient is classified as T4 stage in terms of tumor size after surgery, this means that he or she will receive a lower score in the Nomogram and thus have a lower probability of benefiting from POCT, and similarly a lower T stage (e.g., T1-T2) will have a greater probability of benefiting from POCT. In fact, as mentioned earlier, there is an overlap between the T3 staging of the seventh edition and the T4 staging of the eighth edition in the delineation of tumor size, so in this study, the T4 staging in the delineation of the patient information in the SEER database only took into account the size of the tumor size, which may not be suitable as a reference for some patients with T4N2M0 who have already invaded the surrounding large organs in the clinical application. Although some retrospective studies have now concluded that some patients with T4 staging that includes invasion of large peripheral organs can still benefit from surgical survival under the eighth edition of staging, the surgical requirements for surgeons are higher and the R0 resection rate is lower. At the same time, the final COX regression and logistic regression models in this study did not in fact show statistically significant differences in benefit for patients with T4 staging, and, when combined with the Nomogram prediction model, the T4 staging scores were extremely low. Therefore, based on this study and the actual situation, further discussion is needed in the future for patients with T4 staging.

Elderly patients may themselves carry more underlying diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, diabetes mellitus, and diseases of the cardiovascular system. A META analysis pointed out [33] that for older patients, either radiotherapy or chemotherapy is a risk factor for ultimate survival.2023 A study pointed out that age is an independent prognostic factor regardless of whether PORT is performed or not, and the higher the age of the patient, the worse the subsequent survival [34], which may be related to the state of the patient itself, so from the perspective of age Therefore, at present, whether to supplement radiotherapy for operable N2 stage NSCLC still needs to be further explored.

Gender may also have a partial influence on the choice of radiotherapy, and some studies have also pointed out that biological Sex is an independent factor in the choice of radiotherapy [35,36]. We have not been able to find a detailed discussion of this issue in the literature, which may be related to gender-specific lifestyle habits and social division of labor. In the male population, smoking and working in heavy-duty, high-risk cancer environments account for a higher proportion of men with a higher likelihood of developing lung cancer, and may have poorer lung function status, requiring more treatment modalities to improve survival. Men are also physically larger than women, and a larger area of lung tissue may be able to tolerate more radiation damage from radiation therapy.

The number of positive lymph nodes and its index reflects the lymph node invasion, and most of the current studies point out that PORT is recommended for patients with positive mediastinal lymph nodes at multiple sites and insufficient number of lymph nodes cleared. However, at present, there is less literature for LODDS analysis of N2 stage NSCLC patients for PORT. Lower LODDS means lower probability of positivity of examined lymph nodes, which ultimately reflects lower number of positive lymph node invasions, and a retrospective study in 2022 pointed out [15] that a patient population with a LODDS>0.052, who underwent PORT, had a better tumor-specific survival, while this study noted that the LODDS classification had the best discriminatory ability and prognostic accuracy for patients with stage IIIA-N2 compared to other lymph node indicators. The analysis of this study confirms that patients may have a longer OS without PORT when the LODDS is ≤ −0.82. According to this study, this is the first study to determine whether or not to perform PORT based on the LODDS index using OS as the study endpoint.

In addition, there are a number of issues that should be explored in this study. First, all patients did not have information about subsequent targeted and immunotherapy treatments, and these subsequent treatment modalities will largely affect patient survival, which may lead to a longer survival time for those who did not undergo radiotherapy when survival analyses are performed because they may have supplemented with other treatments after chemotherapy. Secondly, this study relied only on clinical data to establish a predictive model, and failed to understand information about the molecular characteristics of the tumor and gene expression, etc. In a study by Yang et al. in 2021 [37], 34 patients with IIIA-N2 were collected, and the conclusions showed that EGFR (Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor) positive patients were poor predictors of progression-free survival. The team concluded that the postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy regimen was not suitable for this group of patients and that a more potent adjuvant therapy needed to be found for them. In another study by this team, it was similarly noted [38] that EGFR mutation-negative patients who underwent PORT had better DFS. Thus for NSCLC patients, EGFR positivity may influence the choice of PORT. Future studies need to collect further information on patients' targets to improve the prediction accuracy.

Impact of different adjuvant radiotherapy techniques

The conclusion of this study states that for patients with operable N2 stage NSCLC, postoperative OS is better without PORT. The most important radiotherapy factor affecting postoperative mortality is cardiopulmonary toxicity. The Lung ART study showed that patients who underwent PORT had a reduction in mediastinal recurrence rate to 25 %, but at the same time increased the mortality rate from 5.3 % to 14.6 % [5].Corso et al. found that if the adjuvant radiotherapy dose was greater than 54 Gy, the original survival benefit disappeared [39]. Karakoyun-Celik et al. concluded that doses greater than 54 Gy may exacerbate cardiotoxicity and thus be associated with higher mortality in patients with Left-sided tumors [40]. Therefore a more advanced IMRT paradigm was adopted in the later PORT-C study with strict radiotherapy dose limits for organs at risk. For example, lung V20 was limited to <20 % with a mean lung dose of <12 Gy, cardiac V30 to <40 %, and cardiac V40 to <30 %. In addition, CTV did not include the contralateral mediastinum or supraclavicular region. Although there was no OS benefit, adverse events were significantly reduced compared to the Lung ART study.

In this study, the need for further adjuvant radiotherapy for patients with a probability of benefit below 0.62 is indeed debatable, and the balance between the efficacy and toxicity of radiotherapy must be strictly controlled; however, the survival quality of NSCLC patients treated with IMPT (Intensity-Modulated Proton Therapy) was also Assurance. Several studies have shown that IMPT significantly reduces grade 3 and higher radiation pneumonitis events, as well as grade 3 and higher cardiac events, compared with IMRT [41]. IMPT is not limited to cardiopulmonary protection, but a multicenter prospective study has also demonstrated that IMPT achieves protection of the hematopoietic system as well as the immune system. On the one hand, greater safety ensures that patients are less likely to die from non-tumor-specific causes, and on the other hand, more advanced radiotherapy techniques mitigate treatment-induced medical damage so that patients have the opportunity to receive further treatment. Currently IMRT is the most widely used technique in PORT for thoracic tumors. The era of the patients in this study is also located in the transition period from 3D-CRT to IMRT, therefore, in the future when IMPT is applied on a large scale, new clinical evidence may be able to be obtained on whether or not to perform postoperative adjuvant radiotherapy in patients with stage N2 NSCLC.

Limitations of the study

This study has the following limitations:

The training set and internal validation set of this study were obtained from the SEER database, and due to the limitations of the database account and the limitations of the database itself, some important biological and clinical information could not be obtained, such as the family history of the patient's tumor, past medical history, specific radiotherapy regimen, gene mutation status, whether or not the patient was subsequently treated with immunotherapy or targeted therapy, and whether or not the patient suffered from a local or distant recurrence after treatment, as well as the treatment measures for such a situation. This information also affects the prognosis of patients, which makes it impossible to investigate the applicability and safety of PORT for patients with operable N2 NSCLC in a more detailed and comprehensive manner. Despite rigorous efforts to adjust for known confounding factors within this study, inherent limitations of the SEER database preclude the inclusion of several clinically significant unmeasured confounders. These include patient performance status, surgical margin status, and details regarding radiotherapy dose and technique.Performance status is a well-established, strong negative predictor of survival. Positive surgical margins are indicative of a higher risk of recurrence and poorer survival outcomes; consequently, patients with positive margins are more likely to receive additional interventions such as radiotherapy to mitigate recurrence risk [42,43]. Failure to account for margin status could therefore lead to an underestimation of the potential benefit associated with PORT. Furthermore, suboptimal radiotherapy dosing or the use of outdated techniques may be associated with inferior local control and survival. Patient subgroups with limited access to advanced care, potentially due to socioeconomic factors or temporal disparities in technology availability, might receive treatment with older techniques and equipment. This can impede the translation of radiotherapy's theoretical advantages into clinical benefit and may introduce confounding effects, as these subgroups might also differ in other prognostic characteristics. Additionally, variations in radiotherapy dose and technique per se can directly impact survival outcomes. These unmeasured confounders likely influence treatment selection patterns. Future research endeavors will prioritize the inclusion of these critical variables to enhance the robustness of the analysis.

In addition, the small number of patients in the external validation set in this study may have resulted in a poorer model fit. First, this may be related to the small number of eligible cases due to the late update of the treatment guidelines for stage N2 NSCLC. The Lung ART and PORT-C pilot study concluded around 2021, before which the majority of patients with operable stage N2 NSCLC undergoing conventional POCT will undergo conventional PORT. Moreover, the external validation data for this study came only from a single center, which made it extraordinarily difficult to screen such patients during the period 2010–2020; second, a significant proportion of the patients in the external validation set were older at the time of the initial diagnosis, were diagnosed earlier, and, even after systemic treatment, experienced unavoidable loss of visits at later stages and could not be confirmed to have a specific survival status, which further reduced the final inclusion of the Number. Therefore, in summary, this study requires further validation in a larger external independent validation set.

Conclusion

This observational analysis based on the SEER database shows that, regardless of whether PSM is performed, POCT performs better than POCRT in OS. Current randomized trials have not yet confirmed the benefit of PORT on OS. After PSM, the patient data in this study were used to successfully identify clinicopathologic characteristic variables as independent factors. Based on these factors, a Nomogram prediction model was constructed. Using the Nomogram, the final score was obtained, and the corresponding benefit probability value was explored. Using the probability of benefit, it is possible to more accurately screen patients who undergo POCT alone and achieve a survival benefit. The Nomogram prediction model has good predictive and discriminatory abilities.
