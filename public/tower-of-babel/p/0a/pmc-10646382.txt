Improved intra‐atrial conduction delay after successful ablation for atrial fibrillation by scar homogenization in right atrium
Authors: Jeongduk Seo, Yae Min Park, Yong Hoon Shin, Albert Youngwoo Jang, Woong Chol Kang, Wook‐Jin Chung, Young‐Hoon Kim, In Suck Choi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtrial fibrosis in the right atrium (RA) presenting as a low‐voltage zone might be the mechanism of atrial fibrillation (AF) and intra‐atrial conduction delay. The impact of scar homogenization in RA on intra‐atrial conduction delay is unknown. We describe a patient with paroxysmal AF and significant intra‐atrial conduction delay with repetitive atrial flutter, triggered from the lateral free wall in the RA between the significant low‐voltage zone and slow conduction area after pulmonary vein isolation. Linear ablation along the trabeculated lateral free wall in the RA to homogenize the scar was successfully performed, and the intra‐atrial conduction delay improved ultimately.

INTRODUCTION

The right atrium (RA) has been demonstrated as a substrate for triggering and perpetuating atrial fibrillation (AF) in prior studies (Nattel & Harada, 2014; Wieczorek & Hoeltgen, 2013). Intra‐atrial conduction delay is considered as an indicator of atrial electrical remodeling (Akyürek et al., 2001). We report a patient whose intra‐atrial conduction delay improved after successful scar homogenization in the RA during catheter ablation for AF.

CASE REPORT

A 68‐year‐old male was referred for radiofrequency catheter ablation of drug‐refractory paroxysmal AF. He denied any history of cardiac surgery or congenital or structural heart disease. He underwent implantation of a dual‐chamber pacemaker (PM) due to sick sinus syndrome 7 years earlier and had been taking antiarrhythmic drugs for AF since then. Initial echocardiography revealed a moderate degree of tricuspid regurgitation (TR) with RA enlargement and a left ventricular systolic ejection fraction of 50%. The pacemaker was programmed in the DDDR mode at a low rate of 50 bpm and an algorithm to minimize ventricular pacing. The atrial stimulus‐to‐P interval was measured at 80 ms, and the atrial stimulus‐to‐QRS interval was measured at 184 ms during pacing at 64 bpm immediately after PM implantation. The atrial stimulus‐to‐P interval gradually increased up to 200 ms, and stimulus‐to‐QRS interval up to 382 ms during pacing at 74 bpm over the previous 7 years, suggesting a significant intra‐atrial conduction delay (Figure 1a). Follow‐up echocardiography before catheter ablation demonstrated aggravation of TR to severe degree and aggravation of RA enlargement from 25 to 33 cm2. During the electrophysiological study and three‐dimensional electroanatomical mapping with a high‐density mapping system using a Grid catheter (EnSite Velocity, Abott), repetitive atrial flutter was triggered from the lateral free wall in the RA in between the significant low‐voltage zone (defined as an amplitude <0.5 mV) and slow conduction area after pulmonary vein (PV) isolation (Figure 1b,c). Linear ablation (FlexAbility, Abott, power of 30 W using an irrigated‐tip catheter, 13 mL/min) along the trabeculated lateral free wall in the RA to homogenize the scar was performed (Figure 2a), and tachycardia terminated during ablation at the cavotricuspid isthmus area. Subsequent voltage mapping of the lateral free wall in the RA during sinus rhythm showed a completely electrically silent area (Figure 2b). Interestingly, follow‐up electrocardiography (ECG) demonstrated that the atrial stimulus‐to‐P and atrial stimulus‐to‐QRS intervals were significantly shortened to 102 and 202 ms after catheter ablation (Figure 2c). He had been in sinus rhythm without episodes of atrial arrhythmia during the 24 months of the follow‐up after catheter ablation.

DISCUSSION

Although PVs are the main focus of triggering for AF and thus the cornerstone of ablation procedures for paroxysmal AF, it is clear that PV triggers are not the only mechanism responsible for AF development. There is growing evidence that atrial fibrosis plays a key role in the development and maintenance of AF (Andrade et al., 2014). These fibrotic areas can be translated into signal abnormalities such as low‐voltage amplitude zone, complex electrograms, or slow conduction area by electroanatomic high‐density map. Ablating those areas either in left atrium or RA after the circumferential PV isolation as an additional substrate modification strategy showed improved clinical outcomes in previous studies (Chen et al., 2013; Park et al., 2012). However, the efficacy of complete scar homogenization in RA, as an additional ablation strategy to further improve the overall clinical success rate, is unclear.

Data on targeting substrates in RA are sparse, and only a small series have been previously reported regarding this issue. Scar homogenization was performed in the extensive RA‐free wall low‐voltage zone in five patients (Al‐Kaisey et al., 2021). This study demonstrated an extensive low‐voltage zone in the RA‐free wall as an unusual substrate for AF and a triggering site for unstable atrial tachycardias, and targeting these sites as an effective ablation strategy. Two of these patients underwent successful redo ablation at residual low‐voltage zone channels in RA, which shows evidence of multiple channels through this area in between dense scars and rationalizes the homogenization of these substrates by catheter ablation for elimination of AF. However, none of the study patients showed electrical conduction delays, such as prolongation of the PR or HV intervals or sinus node dysfunction. Our patient already had sinus node dysfunction, necessitating permanent PM implantation. He showed significant progressive electrical remodeling and conduction delay in the ECG, with the long atrial stimulus‐to‐P and atrial stimulus‐to‐QRS intervals. Atrial conduction delay has been associated with atrial flutter and a marker of atrial electrophysiologic substrate and remodeling (Redfearn et al., 2006). Recent study demonstrated that patients with atrial flutter had significantly longer RA conduction time measured by right atrial collision time (RACT) during pacing from the coronary sinus than controls and patients who developed clinical AF later showed significantly longer RACT than those who did not among atrial flutter group (Ryckman et al., 2023).

Progressive extensive atriomyopathy might have resulted in severe intra‐atrial conduction delay, which was reverse remodeled after successful ablation in our patient. Scar homogenization completely converts low‐voltage regions into electrically silent regions, which may result in the elimination of zones of non‐uniform anisotropic conduction (Saini et al., 2017) and ultimately improve the intra‐atrial conduction delay in our patient (Figure 3).

CONCLUSION

Atrial fibrosis in the RA presenting as a low‐voltage zone might be the mechanism of AF development, and complete scar homogenization in these areas is an effective strategy to improve the outcome. Intra‐atrial conduction delay can be improved after successful substrate modification and scar homogenization in RA by catheter ablation.
