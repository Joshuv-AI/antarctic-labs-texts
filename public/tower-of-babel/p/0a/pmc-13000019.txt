Integrated sustainable valorization of seaweed (Ulva ohnoi) biomass into food-grade ingredients with year-round cultivated feedstock
Authors: In-Yung Sunwoo, Taeho Kim, Yong-Kyun Ryu, Won-Kyu Lee, Junseong Kim, Eun-Jeong Koh, Yeon-Ji Lee, Woon-Yong Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVia efficient, scalable, and climate resilient processes, seaweed biorefineries can advance cleaner production, delivering nutrient-rich ingredients with relatively lower land and freshwater requirements. Here, a land-based Ulva ohnoi platform that integrates optimized outdoor tank cultivation, pH-shift protein extraction with dual-product valorization, scale-aware techno-economic analysis (TEA), and a climate-scenario emulator was developed and evaluated. Year-round cultivation yielded a pooled feedstock with carbohydrate and protein contents at 46.6% and 26.8%, respectively, and under optimal conditions, protein yield was 10.30% DW. Further, the protein content of cultivated Ulva protein (CUP) was 47.64%, with bound amino acids showing predominance (96%), implying food-grade utility. pH-shift extraction also improved in-vitro pepsin digestibility to 68.08%. The total dietary fiber content of CUP residue (CUPR) was 40.59%, with insoluble dietary fiber (31.59%) showing predominance, supporting the applicability of CUPR in improving gastrointestinal tract function. TEA revealed strong economies of scale for the platform. The 10,000-kg production model outperformed the 100- and 1,000-kg production models, with gross margin, Return On Investment (ROI), and Internal Rate of Return (IRR) at 78.56%, 110%, and 66.02%, respectively, and a payback time of only 0.91 years. Based on site-specific regression analysis (R2 = 0.884), protein yield increased with temperature but decreased with rainfall, and further warming increased mean protein yield, while higher-emission pathways introduced rainfall-driven volatility, necessitating strategies for sustaining performance under climate variability. Overall, the use of the Ulva platform showed a broad growth temperature window as well as rapid acclimation for U. ohnoi, implying resilience even under global warming conditions and increasing weather variability.Graphical abstract Keywords: Ulva ohnoi, Land-based seaweed cultivation, pH-shift protein extraction, Dual-product valorization, Techno-economic analysis, Climate resilience

Introduction

Transitioning food systems toward resource-efficient and low-impact production is central to achieving cleaner production and establishing a circular bioeconomy. Blue foods are increasingly being recognized as efficient levers for reducing the environmental footprint of nutritious diets and alleviating terrestrial agriculture-related pressure on land, freshwater, and fertilizers use (Tigchelaar et al. 2022). Further, multiple studies have shown that blue foods, including seaweeds, can provide essential nutrients with a lower average footprint than most terrestrial animal-source foods. Thus, they represent promising inputs for sustainable diets and bio-based industries (Pandey 2025).

Among seaweeds, green macroalgae are unique owing to their rapid growth, high carbohydrate and protein contents, and suitability for biorefinery concepts aimed at valorizing multiple fractions and minimizing waste (Mendoza et al. 2018; Waqas et al. 2024). Compared with land-crop cultivation, seaweed farming can save substantial amounts of freshwater as it does not require arable land. Regardless, its environmental performance is driven by the production platform used and integrated processes (Khan et al. 2024). Syntheses of recent life-cycle assessment (LCA) and techno-economic analysis (TEA) studies have shown that although offshore and nearshore farms avoid costs related to aeration and pumping, they are associated with vessel-, gear-, and weather-exposure-related costs. Conversely, land-based tanks are advantageous in that they allow centralized operation and tight utility control. Regardless, their economic and environmental outcomes largely depend on energy sources, aeration set-points, and nutrient management.

Among macroalgae, Ulva species are suitable for biorefinary concepts owing to their eurythermal tolerance and rapid acclimation, which confer competitive robustness under warming conditions and weather variability. Additionally, across taxa and strains, Ulva species sustain high growth and photosynthetic rates over a wide range of temperatures, with multiple studies reporting stable or elevated growth at warm regimes. Specifically, U. ohnoi shows optimal growth at 20–25 °C and U. lactuca shows thermal tolerance at 26–28 °C (Veenhof et al. 2024). Ulva species also show tolerance to short-term salinity and light perturbation; thus, they strategically present as a robust feedstock for cleaner production biorefineries in a warming and variable climate (Simon et al. 2022).

pH-shift processing (alkaline solubilization followed by acidic precipitation) has emerged as a practical strategy for extracting and concentrating Ulva proteins, with a previous study reporting efficient solubilization and precipitation at pH 12 and 2, respectively (Juul et al. 2021). However, yields remain sensitive to operating parameters (e.g., solid–liquid ratio, extraction time, and pH window) and upstream biomass quality, implying the need for end-to-end optimization and scale-aware assessment.

Climate change, which alters temperature regimes and induces precipitation extremes, influences coastal cultivation via salinity dilution, turbidity variation, light limitation, and operational risks (Veenhof et al. 2024). According to the sixth assessment (AR6) framework of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), warming as well as increases in the frequency and intensity of heavy precipitation events are expected, particularly under higher-emission pathways. Therefore, it is necessary to translate these climate impacts into site-specific yield risks and assess the feasibility of year-round production (Maulu et al. 2021).

In this study, we developed and evaluated a land-based Ulva cultivation-conversion platform that aligns with cleaner production principles. Specifically, we optimized inoculum density and aeration requirements for outdoor tanks and conducted year-round cultivation to characterize seasonal Ulva biomass in terms of composition. We also employed pH-shift protein extraction to obtain cultivated Ulva protein (CUP) concentrate and valorized residual solids (CUPR) as a dietary fiber ingredient. Product quality indicators (e.g., amino acid profile, digestibility, and fiber fractions), which are of relevance to food-grade streams, were also quantified. Additionally, TEA was performed across scales to identify cost and resource-use drivers, and an empirical climate-sensitivity model with shared socioeconomic pathway (SSP) scenarios was integrated to assess sustainability and resilience under future temperature-precipitation trajectories. Therefore, by coupling multi-product valorization with scale-aware TEA and climate scenario risk, the findings of this study may provide actionable evidence for scalable and low-waste seaweed bioprocessing, advancing the establishment of a circular blue food-based bioeconomy.

Materials and methods

Cultivation optimization

Species acquisition, site description, and taxonomic verification

In January 2024, U. ohnoi thalli were collected from Shinyang Beach (Jeju, Republic of Korea; 33° 25′ 52″ N, 126° 55′ 31″ E) under low-tide conditions and gently rinsed onsite with ambient seawater to remove sediments and epiphytes. Next, they were placed in sterile polyethylene bags, and within 2 h, transported in insulated containers to our research facility for taxonomic identification following previously reported criteria and workflow (Sunwoo et al. 2025) and subsequent culturing.

Cultivation optimization

Given that it is challenging to control external factors, such as irradiance and ambient temperature for outdoor systems, our optimization efforts focused on controllable operational variables. First, we evaluated the effects of inoculum density (1.5–6.5 g L−1, fresh weight) and (ii) aeration on the growth of Ulva biomass, with aeration at 100.0 L min−1 provided via perforated lines using a blower (H&H Tech, Gimhae, Korea). The “aeration off” system, which served as the control received no forced aeration. Each condition was run for 30 days in a 100-L tank under flow-through operation at 20 °C, and unless otherwise specified, the treatments were performed in triplicates (n = 3). Further, the treatments were randomly assigned to the tanks to minimize position-related bias. The primary response was specific growth rate (SGR, % day−1), computed based on fresh weight at inoculation (WI) and fresh weight at harvest (WF) over duration, T (days) according to Eq. (1).

Year-round production

The outdoor Ulva biomass cultivation system

U. ohnoi was cultivated in semi-cylindrical acrylic tanks (working volume, 100 L) installed outdoors at the Jeju facility. The tanks were operated in the flow-through mode and were continuously supplied Jeju lava seawater to maintain steady-state conditions. Further, exchange/turnover settings were held constant across the different treatments, and unless specified otherwise, the treatments were performed under natural daylight and ambient photoperiod conditions.

Year-round cultivation schedule and operations

Using the optimal settings obtained as described in section “Cultivation optimization”, year-round production from January to December 2024 was assessed. The tanks were inoculated on the first day of each month, while harvesting was performed on the last day of each month, and throughout the experimental period, the system remained outdoors in the flow-through mode. The temperature and salinity of the system were measured in-situ at least weekly using a handheld multiparameter meter (YSI 556 MPS; Yellow Springs Instruments, Yellow Springs, OH, USA). Concurrent ambient air temperature data for the nearest shoreline station were obtained from the Korea Meteorological Administration (KMA) and used for comparison.

Harvest, post-harvest handling, and sample preparation

At the end of each month, Ulva biomass was harvested using a circular fine-mesh net (approximately 60 cm in diameter; mesh size 0.5–1 cm), collected from the culture system, drained, gently rinsed with distilled water, and blotted to remove surface moisture before weighing. For compositional analysis, the biomass was dried in a far-infrared dryer (Garea, Siheung, Korea) at 50 °C for 72 h, thereafter milled using a domestic blender (Shinil, Cheonan, Korea), and stored in airtight containers at room temperature (20 °C) until analysis to determine crude protein, lipid, ash, dietary fiber, mineral, and heavy metal contents following official AOAC methods (AOAC 1970). These analyses were performed by Chungnam National University.

Protein and dietary-fiber extraction

Preparation of Ulva biomass samples

U. ohnoi biomass samples harvested over a period of 12 months were pooled on an equal-mass basis to prepare representative composite cultured U. ohnoi (CU) samples. Seasonal composites were also prepared to assess analytical reproducibility (n = 3 per season), and unless specified otherwise, the CU samples were prepared and extracted in independent triplicates (n = 3). The protein extracts obtained from the CU samples under optimized conditions were denoted CUP (cultured Ulva protein), while the residual solid from the protein extraction process was denoted CUPR (cultured Ulva protein residue).

Reagents and instrumentation

Analytical-grade HCl and NaOH, sodium phosphate buffer, and a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Seoul, Republic of Korea) were used for extraction. pH was monitored using a calibrated two-point bench pH meter (pH 4.00/7.00/10.00 buffers verified). Centrifugation was performed at 8000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C followed by lyophilization at − 50 °C using a condenser. The resulting specimen was frozen and stored at − 20 °C until subsequent analysis.

Optimization of pH-shift solubilization

To identify the optimal pH for protein solubilization, 10-g CU samples was suspended in 100 mL water (1:10 w/v) and the pH was adjusted to target values spanning pH 2–12 at 0.5–1.0 pH-unit steps. Next, the prepared suspensions were stirred for 90 min at room temperature, and the resulting supernatant samples were recovered via centrifugation (8,000 × g, 10 min), immediately neutralized to pH 7.0, and assayed for soluble protein content using the BCA Protein Assay Kit. The pH with the highest soluble protein yield was then employed in subsequent process optimization. Finally, the soluble protein yield of the processed U. ohnoi biomass was calculated according to Eq. (2).

Preparation of CUPR

After protein solubilization and phase separation, the solid residue was immediately neutralized to pH 7.0 and rinsed three times with deionized water (10 volumes for each round of rinsing) to remove soluble salts and carryover proteins. Thereafter, the residue was gently pressed, frozen at − 20 °C, and lyophilized. Dried CUPR was then milled (≤ 500 µm) and stored in airtight containers at room temperature until subsequent analysis.

Composition profiling

Crude protein, total dietary fiber (TDF), and soluble as well as insoluble dietary fiber fractions (SDF and IDF, respectively) were quantified using the enzymatic gravimetric method (AOAC 1970). Further, total and free amino acids were quantified as previously reported (Sunwoo et al. 2025). Specifically, total amino acid (AA) was quantified via 6 N HCl hydrolysis (130 °C, 24 h) and HPLC using the Agilent 1200 system (C18, OPA/FMOC detection, UV 338 nm; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), while free AA were quantified via perchloric/metaphosphoric acid extraction followed by analysis using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 system (Dionex, Sunnyvale, CA, USA) under chromatographic conditions as described in previous studies (Marasinghe et al. 2024). Techno-Economic Analysis (TEA)

Process modeling and TEA were conducted using SuperPro Designer v10.0 (Intelligen, Inc., Scotch Plains, NJ, USA). All related calculations were performed in the batch mode, with discrete production campaigns scheduled over a 1-year operation period. Cost data for seaweed tank infrastructure were obtained from published reports and supplier quotations, and regional utility tariffs were applied to estimate water-related operating costs. Where industrial-scale data were estimated using SuperPro Designer, which employs built-in cost models derived from standard chemical engineering economic correlations, vendor data, and industry-standard references. These costs were automatically scaled to the specified equipment sizes and operating conditions using established cost-scaling relationships. Further, unless otherwise stated, financial data were expressed in USD. The modeled system encompassed land-based U. ohnoi cultivation in outdoor tanks, harvesting and dewatering, pH-shift protein extraction (alkaline solubilization to acidic precipitation) to yield CUP, recovery and the conditioning of residual solids (CUPR) as a dietary fiber ingredient, and on-site utilities. The functional unit for TEA was 1 year of plant operation at specific amount of biomass production (100-, or 1,000-, and 10,000-kg), normalized per kg of dry biomass and per kg of saleable product (CUP or CUPR) for comparison.

Scenario-based sustainability assessment

Monthly cultivation data were compiled for Gujwa-eup (Jeju Island, Republic of Korea) from March 2023 to December 2024 (n = 22). The response variable was monthly U. ohnoi biomass yield (g L−1) and the predictors were monthly mean air temperature (°C) and monthly total precipitation (mm) obtained from the Gujwa-eup meteorological station of the KMA. Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis were performed using SPSS software v23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Significant differences between groups were determined via one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s multiple range test. All the experiments were performed in triplicates and data were presented as the mean ± SD of three independent tests, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Optimal cultivation settings

To stabilize productivity considering the variability of the outdoor environment, we focused on optimizing controllable operational parameters, namely, inoculum density, which determines early growth kinetics and self-shading, and aeration, a first-order driver of gas exchange and mixing (Fig. 1).

Final biomass concentration (g L−1) and specific growth rate (g L−1) of A function of inoculum density (1–5 g L−1) and B aeration (on/off) at 30 d using 4.5 g L−inoculum at 20 °C. Bars show mean ± SD (n = 3)

Across the tested inoculum densities (1–5 g L−1, 20 °C, 30 days), higher values generally resulted in higher final biomass concentrations (Fig. 1A). Specifically, we increased total biomass loading mechanically and compared SGR values (% d−1) to identify a biologically and operationally efficient set-point. Thus, an inoculum density of 4.5 g L−1 was identified as optimal, with the SGR peaking at 8.60% day−1, implying faster compounding growth relative to lower or higher inoculum densities. Densities below this optimal value resulted in slower exponential growth and lower end-of-run biomass, while those approaching the upper bound exhibited self-shading and potential nutrient and light limitations as expected. In subsequent run, this optimal inoculum density was employed to ensure a balance between productivity and controllability.

Further, as shown in Fig. 1B, quantifying the effect of aeration at the optimal inoculum density (4.5 g L−1) on 30-day performance showed that aeration increased final biomass concentration by 67% and SGR by 25% (from 35.54 to 59.44 g L−1 and 6.89 to 8.60% day−1, respectively). These gains are consistent with the improved gas exchange, better boundary-layer mass transfer, and more uniform light exposure associated with mild mixing (Revilla-Lovano et al. 2021). From an operational standpoint, these significant improvements suggested that aeration is a first-order lever for controlling throughput in outdoor flow-through tanks, for which actively control irradiance and ambient temperature is challenging.

Furthermore, within the applied operational window, inoculum density was primarily responsible for the observed initial growth kinetics, whereas aeration served as a first-order lever for mass transfer and gas exchange, and together, these two factors determined end-of-run productivity. These findings are consistent with previous reports that aeration enhances CO₂ delivery, O₂ stripping, and boundary-layer mixing in Ulva tanks, increasing biomass yield and nitrogen uptake, while excessive stocking enhances self-shading and resource limitation, lowering SGR (Msuya and Neori 2008). These mechanisms have also been observed across Ulva species and scales, from 100-L tanks to pilot land-based ponds (Revilla-Lovano et al. 2021), and align with CFD/experimental evidence that aeration improves frond motion and light–dark cycling (Filip et al. 2024).

Year-round fed-batch cultivation in outdoor tank

The inoculum-aeration set-point established based on the100-L trials (4.5 g L−1 inoculum density with aeration at 100.0 L min−1) was scaled to 1.5-t outdoor tanks operated based on a monthly fed-batch schedule, with harvesting at the end of each month immediately followed by re-inoculation to 4.5 g L−1 (Fig. 2). For this larger system, the 30-day mean biomass concentration and SGR were 39.08 g L−1 and 5.87% day−1, respectively, lower than the 59.44 g L−1 and 8.6% day−1, respectively, obtained for the 100-L tank. This decline in performance may be attributed to the effects of non-biological scale-up in outdoor settings, such as diminished volumetric light availability, lower specific gas input, and rainfall-induced turbidity (Mabeau and Fleurence 1993). Notably, a mean SGR of approximately 4.00% day−1 was reported in a previous study using tanks of a similar scale (Gadberry et al. 2018). Thus, an SGR of 5.87% day−1 for the large-scale system represents a cultivation performance consistent with favorable TEA. Biomass production was also influenced by seasonality, with the highest monthly yield observed in August–September owing to high temperatures. However, in June and July, rainfall events hindered growth owing to light and nutrient limitations and salinity-related perturbations resulting from runoff, despite the consistently high air and water temperatures characteristic of these months. These findings align with previous reports that Ulva growth and biochemical traits vary seasonally and are strongly influenced by temperature, light availability, and salinity, and that declines in salinity and turbidity pulses can limit biomass growth (Jansen et al. 2022).

Monthly environment and biomass: air temperature, water temperature, rainfall, biomass concentration and SGR

Pearson correlation analysis (Fig. 3) showed a very strong positive correlation between seawater temperature and biomass concentration, whereas rainfall showed a negative correlation with biomass concentration in several warm months (e.g., March and July). Mechanistically, rainfall lowered system resistance via freshening and increased turbidity, limiting underwater irradiance and resulting in immediate yield loss. Although Ulva species are highly resilient and can rapidly recover and competitively take over, rainfall remained detrimental to their biomass output. These dynamics are consistent with reported mechanisms for constraining coastal primary producers, including opportunistic Ulva species (Bews et al. 2021).

Heatmap of Ulva biomass composition under changing environmental conditions (T.; Temperature, W.T.: Water temperature). The heatmap displays values ranging from − 1 to 1, with red representing positive values close to 1 and blue representing negative values close to − 1

Monthly data (n = 12) for the harvested biomass showed mean protein and dietary fiber contents of 26.72 ± 4.45% DW (CV 0.17) and 35.66 ± 4.73% DW (CV 0.13), respectively, indicating suitable product-quality stability for food-grade stream applications. Specifically, protein content exhibited consistent negative correlations with air temperature (r = − 0.21), seawater temperature (r = − 0.21), and precipitation (r = − 0.10), indicating a tendency toward lower protein levels under warmer and wetter conditions, whereas salinity showed a positive correlation (r = + 0.25), suggesting relatively higher protein contents under more saline conditions. In addition, the correlation heatmap revealed a moderate negative association between crude protein and dietary fiber contents (r = − 0.31), pointing to a compositional trade-off within the biomass. In warmer months, relatively higher carbohydrate and dietary fiber contents and lower protein levels were observed, consistent with seasonal metabolic shifts in macroalgal biomass composition, including Ulva species, which are known to exhibit enhanced photosynthate accumulation under higher temperatures and variable nitrogen availability (Jansen et al. 2022; Kamal et al. 2023). Conversely, biomass collected during cooler months showed higher protein and lipid fractions, supporting the feasibility of targeted harvesting when specific ingredient profiles are desired. Although these relationships do not imply intentional control, they indicate that Ulva biomass produced under varying outdoor environmental conditions may naturally favor either protein- or fiber-enriched compositions.

The mineral (Na, K, Mg, Ca, Fe, Cu, Mn, Zn, and Co) contents of the biomass samples were within the ranges reported for Ulva species (Rupérez 2002; Circuncisão et al. 2018). As is typical for Ulva species, the collected biomass samples showed higher Na than K contents, with their Na/K ratios > 2. Public health guidelines emphasize lowering dietary Na while increasing K such that the Na/K ratio is approximately 1:1 (Guideline 2012). In this regard, it has been demonstrated that freshwater washing can lower the Na/K ratio to 1.2–1.7 (Queirós et al. 2021). Thus, for certain food applications, it is necessary to employ washing and soaking steps to reduce Na content.

The contents of Pb, As, Hg, and Cd contents of the collected Ulva biomass samples were all below international thresholds, with Cd below the detection limit. Additionally, although harmonized maximum levels (MLs) for seaweed foods are still being developed, the MLs obtained in this study met or were all below current EU-set MLs for seaweed-based food supplements.

Seasonal outdoor cultivation was therefore treated as a driver of complementary biomass composition rather than a variable for single-component optimization. Accordingly, biomass harvested over 12 months was pooled to generate a representative composite feedstock, which was subsequently used to evaluate the integrated valorization of protein via alkali solubilization–acid precipitation and dietary fiber recovery from the residual fraction. This integrated perspective reinforces the potential for complementary feedstock utilization, in which protein-rich fractions are prioritized for extraction while fiber-enriched residues are valorized as co-products rather than being regarded as process losses.

Protein recovery and valorization of residual fiber in CUPR

Protein recovery

The carbohydrate, moisture, ash, dietary fiber, and protein contents of the pooled year-round U. ohnoi biomass (CU) were 46.6%, 7.4%, 18.9%, 33.1%, and 26.8%, respectively. Screening at pH 6–12 identified alkaline conditions (pH 12) as optimal for solubilization, with the dry matter yield at this pH reaching 30.80% (Fig. 4A). This observation can be attributed to protein unfolding and charge effects, which enhance solubility and mass transfer under alkaline conditions (Harnedy and FitzGerald 2013; Vilg and Undeland 2017).

Notably, the 3D response surface identified S/L = 1:29.08 (w/v) and 92 min as optimal conditions (Fig. 4B) for obtaining the maximum soluble protein concentration (2.94 mg/mL) and maximum biomass solubility (31.96%). From Fig. 4C, it is evident that contours showed a stronger response to S/L than to time, implying that protein extraction was more sensitive to S/L than to the duration of extraction and explaining the predominance of mass-transfer constraints. Therefore, in soluble protein extraction, it is necessary to prioritize solvent volume, mixing, and solid handling over extraction time. This conclusion aligns with seaweed extraction practices, during which S/L ratios of 1:10–1:30 (w/v) are frequently employed. Further, larger amounts of solvents generally improved protein yield, but increase beyond a certain threshold resulted in yield declines. Similar yield ceilings and gains, valuable for quantifying energy yield trade-offs in scale-up, have been reported in a previous study employing homogenization or double-screw pressing (Juul et al. 2021).

Following solubilization at pH 12, protein precipitation from the CU extract showed clear pH dependence, being favorable under acidic pH conditions (Table 1). Specifically, the highest protein content and dry-matter yield of the precipitate (47.64% and 21.63%, respectively) were observed at pH 2 (overall protein yield = 10.30%). Precipitate protein content and dry-matter yield were lower at pH 4 (39.01% and 18.43%, respectively) than at pH 2. Similarly, the overall protein yield at pH 4 (7.19%) was 30% lower than that at pH 2, and protein precipitation was poor at pH 6 and negligible at pH 8. This trend indicated that the precipitation region, close to which protein solubility is minimized, lies at ~ pH 2. Thus, aggregation and settling were maximized, whereas increasing the pH toward neutral or alkaline conditions increased solubility and suppressed precipitation. Specifically, final CUPR protein contents at pH 2, 4, 6, and 12 were 11.84%, 8.26%, 1.21%, and 0.08% of the initial amount, respectively, confirming that pH 2 was optimal for protein precipitation. Consistent with this observation, several studies have also reported acidic pH ranges as optimal for precipitation, with typical maximum attainable protein yields of 5–9% obtained for pH-shift extraction-only processes (Juul et al. 2021; Vega-Gómez et al. 2024; Harrysson et al. 2019). However, with additional unit operations, such as ultrasound and pulse electric field pretreatment as well as optimized washing, yields of up to 20–30% can be attained.

Additionally, the protein content of CUP under optimal conditions was 47.64% DW (476.4 g kg−1), with constituent (bound) amino acids (443.02 g kg−1) showing predominance at 96%. This observation suggested a low non-protein nitrogen content. Free amino acids only accounted for 2.74% (13.03 g kg−1) of the total CUP protein content, suggesting minimal hydrolytic damage owing to pH-shift extraction. The obtained amino acid profile also showed predominance for acidic amino acids (Glu 66,349; Asp 54,052 mg kg−1) and branched-chain amino acids (Val 28,003; Leu 31,443; Ile 19,607 mg kg−1), implying nutritional functionality. In-vitro pepsin digestibility (0.2%) also improved from 49.45% (CU) to 68.08% (CUP), comparable to the 60–75% range frequently reported for algal proteins and above typical cereal baselines (~ 70%). This observation supports the possibility of valorizing Ulva biomass as food and feed via blue food pathways.

Feasibility assessment of CUPR as dietary fiber

The feasibility of valorizing CUPR as food-grade dietary fiber was assessed. Reportedly, Ulva proteins are intracellular and can be solubilized via chemical treatment, while their cell walls contain water-soluble ulvan, insoluble cellulose, and other glucans (Kidgell et al. 2019). Accordingly, after protein extraction, insoluble fibers generally concentrate as solids (CUPR), whereas a portion of ulvan may partition into the aqueous phase unless retained via ionic crosslinking or subsequently precipitation.

The TDF content of CUPR was 40.59 ± 0.87% DW, with the proportions of IDF and SDF at 31.59% and 7.52%, respectively. The near-closure of IDF + SDF ≈ TDF suggested good analytical mass balance under the AOAC enzymatic gravimetric framework for fiber, which allows the quantification of TDF and its soluble and insoluble fractions after sequential enzymatic digestion and precipitation (McCleary and McLoughlin 2023).

The high proportion of IDF is beneficial for typical physical gastrointestinal functions, such as stool bulking, shortened transit, and laxation via water binding and the mechanical stimulation of the mucosa, effects that are well documented in the literature and form the basis of regularity claims for fiber-enriched foods (El-Salhy et al. 2017). In contrast, SDF contributed to viscosity and fermentability, which both moderate postprandial glycemia and lipidemia. Meta-analyses and randomized controlled trials have consistently indicated that SDF lowers glycemic response and improves selected cardiometabolic markers (Müller et al. 2018). Therefore, the CUPR profile, when formulated to deliver an adequate SDF dose, supports bowel function, while providing ancillary metabolic benefits.

In addition to its generic fiber effects, ulvan, the hallmark sulfated polysaccharide of Ulva species, has been implicated in microbiota modulation, short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) generation, and anti-inflammatory signaling in preclinical models (Flórez-Fernández et al. 2023). A recent study involving Ulva australis (formerly Ulva pertusa) provided evidence in this regard, associating ulvan intake with improved bowel movement and motility, plausibly via SCFAs, which downregulate NF-κB and IL-1β (Koh et al. 2024). Although human confirmatory trials are still needed, these mechanisms position CUPR as a functional fiber ingredient.

The in-vitro pepsin digestibility of CU (49.45%) was higher than that of CUPR (38.34%). The most common and accepted definition of dietary fiber is any plant edible part or a similar carbohydrate that is resistant to digestion and absorption in the small intestine. Systematic reviews of several trials have indicated that fiber supplementation can lower ad libitum energy intake, with the effect size influenced by the fiber type, dose, and food matrix (Lattimer and Haub 2010). In practice, predominantly insoluble matrices, such as CUPR, contribute to volume and oral effort. Thus, combining them with modest amounts of viscous SDFs enhances fullness and post-meal control in diet-style products.

Based on the compositional characteristics of CUPR obtained after protein recovery, it presents advantages as a dietary fiber ingredient in applications emphasizing laxation, regularity, and stool bulking, while allowing flexibility for SDF-targeted formulation strategies when glycemic endpoints are of interest. Therefore, from a cleaner production perspective, valorizing CUPR alongside the protein stream supports dual-product generation, thereby improving raw material efficiency and reducing process-derived waste.

TEA outcomes and cost drivers

A simplified flowchart of the land-based Ulva biorefinery used for TEA in this study is shown in Fig. 5. The harvested seaweed was routed through a washing step (P-1) before loading into the outdoor cultivation tank (P-5), where inoculum density and low-pressure aeration were controlled to ensure monthly Ulva harvest. After harvesting, the biomass was subjected to a second washing step (P-3). Thereafter, the effluent is discarded, while the washed biomass (CU) was dried (P-4) and milled (P-2) to obtain CU powder. Then, for protein extraction and recovery, the CU powder was dispersed in an agitated reactor (P-6), with the pH adjusted to 12 using NaOH to solubilize proteins. Subsequently, slurry was removed using a decanter/centrifuge (P-7), while the solubilized protein phase (supernatant) was transferred to an acid precipitation reactor (P-8) to induce protein precipitation. Next, centrifugation (P-9) was performed to recover the protein curd, which was then dried (P-10) to yield food-grade proteins. The insoluble solids retained at P-7 (CUPR) were further dried (P-12) and used as dietary fiber. Batch scheduling was optimized to avoid equipment idle time and maintain a monthly fed-batch cadence. Operating costs were dominated by downstream unit operations rather than by minor utilities. Further, with scheduling optimization and recovery loops sized according to the design throughput, the baseline operating expenditure (OPEX) profile was technically and commercially plausible and showed consistency with an intensification-driven land-based tank configuration.

Overall, the economic performance of the platform improved with scale, consistent with established economies of scale in seaweed aquaculture and macroalgae biorefineries and comparative TEA/LCA literature on scale sensitivity and cost floor shaping feasibility (Coleman et al. 2022; Mendoza et al. 2018). These results collectively demonstrate the quantitative potential for achieving a commercial threshold through economies of scale. All assumptions were grounded in literature data, supplier quotations, and established engineering scaling laws. While the absolute economic indicators may vary under different market or cost scenarios, the profitability transition trend observed across the 100 → 1000 → 10,000 kg scales remained robust. This pattern is consistent with scale-sensitivity analyses reported in macroalgae biorefinery and aquaculture techno-economic literature, supporting the overall feasibility of cost reduction through process intensification and capacity expansion.

Economic assessment across biomass production scales

A direct techno-economic comparison with open-water cultivation was not performed, as land-based and offshore systems differ fundamentally in scale, risk profile, product specification, and system boundaries. Reported production costs for offshore seaweed cultivation vary widely across regions, with literature values ranging approximately from 190 to 2790 USD per dry ton, depending on capital intensity, line and seeding costs, labor inputs, and yield assumptions (Kite-Powell et al. 2022). However, offshore systems are inherently exposed to environmental variability, including storms, biofouling, grazing pressure, and fluctuating nutrient availability, which can lead to variable yields and inconsistent harvest quality, thereby increasing the effective cost per usable ton. stability issues, structural failure, storm losses, and standby vessel costs (Lian et al. 2024). These factors can dominate OPEX and capital expenditure (CAPEX) and introduce cash-flow volatility for sea-based production. Therefore, in the literature, the application of robust mooring and a line design and taking into account the financial risk associated with weather downtime have been emphasized. In terms of energy and climate, sea-based farms often score lower than land-based farms, which however, can perform competitively when grids are low-carbon and tanks are integrated with waste-nutrient streams (Eikenbusch 2024), underscoring the importance of employing context-specific designs to ensure cleaner production.

In contrast, land-based cultivation enables tight control over growth conditions, reduced contamination risk, and stable product specifications. These attributes are particularly advantageous for ingredient-oriented applications, where consistency and quality can support higher selling prices per ton, even if production costs are higher on a mass basis. Additionally, land-based production eliminated vessel use and marine infrastructure, centralized operation, and enabled tight control over process conditions. These features provided a free-cost headroom for multi-product valorization (e.g., protein and fiber) and helped stabilize product quality and yield. Regardless, tradeoffs included higher on-site electricity/thermal loads, siting and permitting requirements, and biosecurity/effluent management. Therefore, under favorable conditions, land-based tanks can approach or outperform sea-based footprints. However, under unfavorable conditions, environmental impacts can escalate, necessitating renewable energy integration, heat and solvent recovery, and the adoption of a climate-aware design.

Climate-scenario impacts

Based on scaled-up TEA results, we examined how projected climate variability might alter biomass yield as well as process economics by quantifying the sensitivity of Ulva production to temperature and rainfall. Via multiple linear regression, air temperature and rainfall were identified as key predictors of monthly Ulva biomass yield (g L−1). The fitted model based on Eq. (4) was as follows: Ulva = 55.94 + 1.44 × (Temp, °C)—0.0372 × (Rain, mm), with R2 = 0.884 and the overall F-test P < 0.001, indicating a strong explanatory power and supporting the statistical validity and predictive use of the model in subsequent scenario analyses.

At the observed mean conditions (temperature, 18.53 °C; precipitation, 199 mm), the model predicted a biomass yield of 75.3 g L−1. Further, maintaining precipitation at 199 mm and increasing temperature to 28.9 °C increased the predicted biomass yield to 90.3 g L−1. Conversely, maintaining temperature at 18.53 °C and increasing precipitation to 403 mm resulted in a decline in the predicted biomass yield to 67.7 g L−1. Under high-temperature and precipitation conditions (28.9 °C and 403 mm, respectively), the predicted biomass yield was 82.6 g L−1, implying that warming and heavier rainfall positively and negatively affected biomass yield, respectively. This conclusion is consistent with rainfall-driven salinity dilution, increases in turbidity, and decreased light availability as previously documented for coastal macroalgae, including Ulva species (Martins et al. 2001).

To assess the sustainability of Ulva biomass production under a changing climate, we coupled site-specific regression with temperature and precipitation trajectories representative of Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) SSPs, namely, SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5, as summarized in the IPCC AR6 framework, with the projected climate-driven impacts on biomass production illustrated in Fig. 6. Notably, increases in heavy precipitation intensity and frequency are expected over most land areas under higher emission scenarios, implying greater rainfall variability relative to future means.

In SSP1-2.6 (low forcing), modest warming resulted in a gradual increase in Ulva biomass yield, while precipitation variability resulted in comparatively moderate effects, suggesting stable and scalable cultivation under this low-carbon emission pathway. In SSP2-4.5 (intermediate), warming resulted in incremental yield gains; however, mid-century rainfall variability introduced intervals of muted growth. Further, in SSP3-7.0 (high), stronger warming expanded the positive contribution of temperature on biomass yield, widening the modeled yield envelope, while concurrent increases in precipitation extremes increased the frequency of rain-limited months. Furthermore, in SSP5-8.5 (very high), the strongest warming resulted in the largest mean increase in predicted biomass yield as well as the greatest volatility owing to heavier and more frequent precipitation events, consistent with the IPCC AR6 framework. Operationally, this very high temperature increased stability risk even as mean biomass yield increased.

Overall, our model indicated that warming increased Ulva biomass yield, whereas rainfall, acting as a countervailing stressor via light and salinity mechanisms, characteristic of coastal systems, limited growth. In low-emission pathways (SSP1-2.6), reliable cultivation appeared attainable under current practices. However, in high-emission pathways (SSP5-8.5), although higher mean yields were possible, greater rainfall variability and extremes necessitated adaptive control measures (e.g., salinity buffering, turbidity management, and the establishment flexible harvest windows) to maintain year-round productivity. These results collectively outline the plausible trajectories of Ulva productivity and the principal climatic risk factors under future scenarios. While the findings are model-based and should be interpreted considering the simplified representation of environmental drivers and the lack of field validation, the qualitative implications remain robust, highlighting the importance of adaptive design and operational resilience.

Extending this perspective, the regression-based coupling with CMIP6 climate scenarios developed in this study provides a simplified yet mechanistically interpretable framework for assessing the climate sensitivity of Ulva cultivation systems. The model revealed a consistent climatic response structure—characterized by a positive temperature–productivity relationship and a negative rainfall–productivity relationship—offering a foundational dataset for the development of climate-resilient cultivation and control strategies. Although absolute quantitative outcomes may vary across regions and temporal scales, the overall pattern (temperature-positive, rainfall-negative) aligns with numerous prior observations in macroalgal ecophysiology. Accordingly, this study establishes a meaningful hypothesis-driven framework for future experimental validation and adaptive system design under variable climate conditions.

Conclusion

In this study we developed and evaluated a land-based Ulva biomass production platform in terms parameter optimization, scale-aware TEA, and climate-scenario risk assessment, focusing on the composition of protein yields. Our findings highlighted this platform as an integrated and cleaner production pathway for land-based Ulva biomass production with high-yield pH-shift protein recovery and residual fiber utilization. Specifically, under the optimized conditions (alkaline solubilization at pH 12 followed by acidic precipitation at pH 2, with S/L = 1:29 for 92 min), protein extraction yield reached 10.30%, while protein integrity was largely preserved (bound amino acids = 96%) and in-vitro digestibility increased to 68.08%. Further, based on dry-weight measurements, the TDF content of residual solid after solubilization (CUPR) was 41%, with the IDF fraction showing predominance. These findings supporting the use of CUPR as dietary fiber as well as its regularity applications, and highlight an avenue for expanding revenue via dual product valorization. TEA showed clear economies of scale. Notably, production at 10,000 kg·month−1 showed strong economic viability, with gross margin, ROI, NPV, IRR, and payback at 78.6%, 110%, 274 million USD, 66.02%, and 0.91 year, respectively. Importantly, utilities constituted a minor share of the operating cost, establishing a headroom for quality assurance and fiber valorization. Further, temperature positively affected Ulva biomass yield, which was however inhibited by rainfall, and climate scenario analysis identified SSP1-2.6, a low emission pathway, as suitable for stable, scalable Ulva biomass production, whereas SSP5-8.5, a very high emission scenario, although offering a higher mean yield, resulted in greater volatility, necessitating adaptive measures, such as renewable power, salinity buffering, turbidity management, and flexible harvesting windows, to maintain annual throughput. Overall, by minimizing resource intensity via strict water and aeration control, valorizing co-products to reduce waste, and proactively planning for climate variability, our proposed platform advances circular bioeconomy objectives and delivers a higher output per unit resource input with improved profitability at scale.
