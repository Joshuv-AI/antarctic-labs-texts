Translational prioritization of genetically supported candidate targets and pharmacological annotations for chronic lung diseases: a single-cell eQTL-guided multi-cohort study
Authors: Ren Li, Jiaji Cheng, Yaru Liu, Xu Zhang, Zhihong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic lung diseases impose a massive global burden, yet translating genetic findings into biologically interpretable target hypotheses remains challenging. We aimed to prioritize genetically supported candidate gene-cell type-disease associations and characterize pharmacological annotations for asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), and bronchiectasis (BE).MethodsWe integrated immune-cell-specific single-cell cis-eQTL data (14 immune cell subsets) with two-sample cis-Mendelian randomization and Bayesian colocalization. Using FinnGen R12 and UK Biobank as independent outcome cohorts, we developed a cross-cohort tiered framework to rank candidate gene-cell type-disease associations based on MR evidence, colocalization support, and cross-cohort consistency.ResultsAsthma yielded the most robust signals, highlighting 6 “Tier 1” candidates (e.g., CD247, FADS1) with replicated colocalization support across both cohorts. We mapped 17 prioritized druggable genes to existing drug-, compound-, or metabolite-related annotations via DrugBank, DGIdb, and HMDB. These annotations nominate CD247, FADS1, and other loci for mechanistic and pharmacological follow-up, but they should not be interpreted as direct evidence of clinical repurposing readiness. The limited peripheral immune signals observed in IPF and BE suggest that local tissue niches, together with limited power and phenotype heterogeneity, may influence signal detection.ConclusionsBy bridging single-cell genomics with pharmacological databases, our tiered prioritization framework provides a statistically grounded map of genetically supported candidate genes for chronic respiratory diseases. The results refine broad genetic loci into cell-contextualized candidate genes and pharmacological annotations that require independent, functional, and pharmacological validation before therapeutic inference. These findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating prioritization evidence rather than proof of therapeutic efficacy, clinical utility, or drug-repurposing readiness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08625-w.

Introduction

Chronic lung diseases impose a substantial and persistent burden on both clinical care and public health. In this study, we focused on four clinically important and high-burden chronic respiratory diseases: asthma [1], chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [2], idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) [3] and bronchiectasis (BE) [4]. Although these conditions differ in their pathological mechanisms and clinical manifestations, they are all characterized by chronic symptoms, functional limitation, recurrent exacerbations, and sustained healthcare needs, collectively resulting in considerable loss of quality of life and substantial healthcare utilization. Current therapeutic strategies across these diseases are largely centered on symptom control, exacerbation reduction, and slowing disease progression, whereas interventions capable of durably altering the natural history of disease remain limited. In asthma, a subset of patients continues to experience suboptimal treatment response and recurrent disease activity [5, 6]. In COPD, effective interventions that reverse or halt progressive structural lung damage remain lacking [7]. IPF is a progressive fibrosing lung disease with poor prognosis, and currently available antifibrotic therapies slow, but do not halt, disease progression [8, 9]. Long-term management of bronchiectasis still relies predominantly on infection control and supportive care, with relatively few disease-modifying options targeting key pathogenic processes [10, 11]. These unmet needs underscore the importance of identifying biologically plausible and causally informed pathways that may support disease stratification and future target discovery. However, despite these pressing clinical needs, the rate of successful drug development for chronic respiratory diseases remains low, largely due to the translational gap between discovering genetic risk loci and prioritizing biologically interpretable candidate targets.

At the genetic level, large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified susceptibility loci for asthma, COPD, and IPF [12–15], whereas systematic GWAS evidence for BE remains comparatively limited. However, GWAS primarily localize disease-associated genomic regions, and because most signals reside in noncoding regions and are influenced by linkage disequilibrium, individual loci often cannot directly resolve the relevant effector genes, the direction of regulation, or the specific cellular context in which these effects operate [16, 17]. To bridge genetic variation with gene function, transcriptome-integrative approaches based on expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL), such as transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS), have been widely applied. Nevertheless, conventional bulk eQTL/TWAS frameworks are typically based on mixed-cell expression profiles derived from tissues or peripheral blood, limiting their ability to distinguish immune-cell-subset-specific and state-specific regulatory effects [18]. In the context of chronic lung diseases, where immune involvement and cellular composition are highly dynamic, genetically regulated effects concentrated in specific immune cell subsets may be attenuated, obscured, or misattributed when evaluated only at the bulk level.

To improve causal inference at immune-cell resolution, we applied an integrative framework combining single-cell cis-expression quantitative trait loci (cis-eQTLs), two-sample cis-Mendelian randomization (cis-MR), and Bayesian colocalization. Specifically, we used immune-cell-specific single-cell cis-eQTL data from the OneK1K resource as genetic instruments [18] and evaluated the effects of genetically predicted gene expression across 14 immune cell subsets on the risk of four chronic lung diseases using GWAS summary statistics from FinnGen and the UK Biobank. To account for population-specific genetic architectures and the inherent heterogeneity of complex respiratory traits, we employed a stringent parallel evaluation framework. Rather than relying on a conventional discovery-validation design—which may inadvertently discard biologically meaningful population-specific signals—we independently assessed FinnGen and UK Biobank, establishing a tiered evidence gradient to capture both universally shared and cohort-specific genetic effects. Cis-MR analyses were conducted separately in each cohort, and cross-cohort reproducibility was incorporated into a tiered prioritization framework so that associations with stronger and more consistent support could be assigned higher priority. Bayesian colocalization was further performed within each cohort to assess whether gene-expression and disease-association signals were likely to share the same causal variant, thereby reducing the risk of spurious attribution due to linkage disequilibrium. Using this unified evidence chain, our aim was not to establish definitive therapeutic targets within a single study, but to prioritize immune-cell-specific candidate gene-cell type-disease associations across asthma, COPD, IPF, and BE for downstream functional validation, target ranking, and translational follow-up.

Methods

Study design and analytical overview

This study used a two-sample cis-Mendelian randomization (cis-MR) framework based on single-cell cis-expression quantitative trait loci (cis-eQTLs) to systematically evaluate the potential causal effects of genetically predicted gene expression on the risk of four chronic lung diseases, namely asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), and bronchiectasis (BE), at the resolution of immune cell subsets. This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE-MR guidelines [19]. FinnGen R12 and the UK Biobank (UKB) were treated as independent outcome data sources, and all MR and colocalization analyses were conducted separately within each cohort without statistical meta-analysis of effect estimates. Because all analyses were based on publicly available GWAS summary statistics, no additional ethical approval was required. The overall analytical workflow included the construction of genetic instruments from immune-cell-specific single-cell cis-eQTLs, the implementation of cis-MR analyses in each outcome cohort, multiple-testing correction to identify statistically significant associations, Bayesian colocalization analysis to assess whether gene-expression and disease-association signals were likely to share the same causal variant, and cross-cohort evidence stratification to rank prioritized candidates. Only associations supported by both statistical significance and colocalization evidence were retained for final prioritization. The overall study design is shown in Fig. 1.

Data sources

Exposure and outcome data

Exposure data were obtained from immune-cell-specific single-cell cis-eQTL summary statistics generated from the OneK1K cohort, which included peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from 982 healthy individuals of Nordic ancestry and a total of 1,267,758 cells. Cell-type annotation was available for 14 immune cell subsets, including Plasma, B IN, B MEM, CD4 NC, CD4 ET, CD4 SOX4, CD8 NC, CD8 ET, CD8 S100B, NK, NK R, Mono C, Mono NC, and DC [18]. Outcome data were obtained from GWAS summary statistics from FinnGen R12 and the UK Biobank (Lee Lab). The outcomes of interest included asthma, COPD, IPF, and BE. In FinnGen R12, the case/control sample sizes were 59,100/259,839 for asthma, 24,138/409,070 for COPD, 2,700/494,583 for IPF, and 2,932/409,070 for BE. In the UKB dataset, the corresponding case/control sample sizes were 26,167/373,887 for asthma (phenotype code: 495), 10,516/373,881 for COPD (phenotype code: 496), 917/395,582 for IPF (phenotype code: 502), and 1,883/373,884 for BE (phenotype code: 496.3). MR and colocalization analyses were performed independently within each cohort, and no combined effect-size analysis was conducted.

Phenotype definitions were not fully identical between FinnGen and UKB. FinnGen outcomes were based primarily on registry-derived clinical diagnosis codes, whereas the UKB outcomes from the Lee Lab resource were based on PheCode-derived binary phenotypes analyzed using SAIGE. Because only summary-level GWAS data were available, individual-level clinical features such as smoking exposure, spirometry, imaging confirmation, disease severity, and diagnostic adjudication could not be harmonized across cohorts.

Because the OneK1K resource was derived from PBMCs collected from healthy individuals, the exposure data primarily represented genetically regulated expression programs in circulating immune cells rather than disease-state regulatory programs in lung tissue, airway epithelium, bronchoalveolar immune cells, fibroblasts, or lesion microenvironments. Accordingly, our framework was intended to prioritize peripheral immune-cell-related genetic signals and was not designed to comprehensively capture all tissue-resident mechanisms in chronic lung diseases.

Instrument selection

For each gene-cell type pair, candidate instrumental single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were selected from the cis-region spanning ± 1,000 kb around the transcription start site of the target gene at a significance threshold of P < 5 × 10− 8. Candidate SNPs then underwent linkage disequilibrium (LD) clumping using a threshold of r² < 0.001 and a window size of 10,000 kb to obtain a set of approximately independent genetic instruments. Gene-cell type pairs were excluded from downstream analyses if no valid instruments were available, if the instrument set became empty after clumping, or if the selected variants could not be reliably matched to the outcome GWAS datasets. The retained instrumental SNPs were harmonized with the FinnGen and UKB outcome data, and variants that could not be reliably aligned, such as ambiguous palindromic SNPs with indeterminate strand orientation, were removed.

Two-sample cis-MR analyses

Two-sample cis-MR analyses were conducted independently in FinnGen and UKB using the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.6.6). When only one independent instrumental SNP was available for a given gene-cell type pair, the Wald ratio method was used to estimate the causal effect. When two or more independent instrumental SNPs were available, the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method was used as the primary analysis [20]. Instrument strength, directionality, and robustness were further assessed using F statistics, Steiger directionality tests, and heterogeneity/pleiotropy diagnostics where applicable. All MR analyses were performed separately within each cohort. Within each outcome dataset, Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied to all MR tests to account for multiple comparisons. Associations with a false discovery rate PFDR < 0.05 were considered statistically significant within the corresponding cohort and were advanced to the subsequent colocalization analysis. For single-SNP Wald ratio estimates, multi-instrument sensitivity analyses such as MR-Egger, weighted median, and heterogeneity tests were not applicable; these associations were therefore interpreted together with instrument strength, Steiger directionality, and colocalization evidence. Benjamini–Hochberg correction was applied separately within each disease–cohort outcome dataset across the gene–cell type pairs entering the corresponding analytical stage, rather than across all diseases, cohorts, and downstream annotation analyses as a single global correction universe.

Bayesian colocalization analysis

To further determine whether the observed MR associations were driven by a shared causal variant between the exposure and outcome, rather than by LD-induced correlation alone, Bayesian colocalization analysis was performed for associations that passed the cis-MR screening step [21, 22]. Colocalization analysis was conducted within the cis-region of each target gene, defined as ± 1,000 kb around the transcription start site. The posterior probability of hypothesis 4 (PPH4) was used as the primary metric for evaluating whether the gene-expression signal and the disease-association signal were likely to share the same causal variant. Colocalization analyses were performed using the coloc framework under default prior settings, and PPH4 was used as the primary indicator of a shared causal variant. A threshold of PPH4 ≥ 0.8 was considered to indicate strong colocalization evidence [23, 24]. Associations that did not meet this threshold were considered to lack sufficient causal support and were not included in downstream analyses. Because cis-eQTL instruments may tag regulatory variants that influence multiple nearby genes or regulatory elements, colocalized gene-expression and disease signals were interpreted as evidence supporting a candidate gene–locus relationship rather than definitive proof that the named gene was the sole causal effector gene. The default prior setting and PPH4 threshold were therefore used as uniform operational screening criteria across all tests, rather than as evidence that a locus contained only one causal variant or that the named transcript was the sole effector gene.

Cross-cohort replication and prioritization

Effect estimates from FinnGen and UKB were not statistically combined. Instead, the two cohorts were treated as independent sources of genetic evidence to evaluate the robustness and reproducibility of each association. All reported gene-cell type-disease associations were required to satisfy both of the following criteria: first, they had to reach MR statistical significance (PFDR < 0.05) in at least one cohort; second, they had to show strong colocalization support (PPH4 ≥ 0.8) in the corresponding cohort. Associations that achieved MR significance and strong colocalization support in both cohorts were classified as Tier 1 and were considered to represent the highest-confidence genetic evidence. Associations that achieved MR significance in both cohorts but showed strong colocalization in only one cohort were classified as Tier 2 A and were considered genetically supported but potentially cohort-specific candidate signals. Associations that achieved both MR significance and strong colocalization support in only one cohort, with no inclusion from the other cohort, were classified as Tier 2B. Given the potential differences in phenotype construction, diagnostic coding, sample composition, and clinical ascertainment between FinnGen and UKB, we used cross-cohort consistency as an evidence-ranking criterion rather than assuming that the two cohorts represented exchangeable estimates of the same phenotype. This tier system was intended to rank statistical prioritization evidence rather than clinical readiness; therefore, Tier 2 A candidates were considered weaker than Tier 1 candidates because colocalization support was not reproduced across both outcome resources despite cross-cohort MR support. We did not perform a formal cross-cohort meta-analysis because differences in phenotype construction, ascertainment, and sample composition could make pooled effect estimates difficult to interpret; consequently, the two outcome resources were used for concordance-based prioritization rather than for pooled causal estimation.

Translational annotation and PheWAS analysis

Drug-gene and metabolite-gene relationships were queried using DrugBank [25], DGIdb [26], and HMDB [27] to identify drug-, compound-, or metabolite-related annotations for prioritized druggable genes and to support downstream hypothesis generation for translational prioritization in chronic lung diseases. To characterize broader phenotypic context rather than establish drug safety or clinical efficacy, phenome-wide association analysis was conducted using the online PheWAS portal. These database-derived annotations were not treated as evidence of respiratory drug efficacy or repurposing readiness. No formal direction-of-effect matching, target-mechanism adjudication, indication matching, safety-profile evaluation, or airway-delivery feasibility assessment was performed within this study.

As an exploratory pathway-level annotation, GO, KEGG, and Reactome functional analyses were further compiled for the prioritized protein-coding candidate genes. GO terms were summarized across biological process, cellular component, and molecular function categories, whereas KEGG and Reactome pathways were summarized separately. Because the candidate set was small and non-coding transcripts were not consistently mapped by pathway databases, these analyses were interpreted as descriptive functional annotation rather than confirmatory enrichment evidence.

Results

Selection of immune cell-specific eGenes

Across the 14 immune cell subsets, we initially identified 26,597 eGenes from the cis-eQTL data. After LD clumping (r² < 0.001), 8,733 approximately independent eGenes remained for downstream MR analyses. The overall distribution of eGenes across immune cell types remained largely stable before and after clumping, with CD4 NC and NK cells accounting for the largest proportions, suggesting that these two cell subsets harbored relatively abundant immune genetic regulatory signals for subsequent causal inference analyses (Fig. 2A). The sample sizes of the immune-cell-specific eQTL datasets ranged from 643 to 982 individuals (Fig. 2B). The largest datasets (n = 982) were observed for CD4 NC, CD8 NC, CD4 ET, CD8 ET, B IN, and B Mem. Other cell types with relatively large sample sizes included Mono C (n = 969), DC (n = 968), NK R (n = 967), CD8 S100B (n = 981), Mono NC (n = 932), and NK (n = 928). In contrast, CD4 SOX4 and Plasma showed comparatively smaller sample sizes of 857 and 643, respectively. Stratification by the number of independent SNPs showed that most eGenes were instrumented by a single independent SNP, whereas only a minority were supported by multiple independent SNPs. These findings indicated that the selected genetic instruments were predominantly independent and were suitable for subsequent cis-MR analyses.

To make the instrument structure of the retained signals more transparent, we further summarized the number of independent cis-eQTL instruments among the 154 cohort-level prioritized records retained after MR and colocalization filtering (Supplementary Table 1). Overall, 150 of 154 records (97.4%) were based on a single independent cis-eQTL instrument and were therefore estimated using the Wald ratio method, whereas 4 records (2.6%) were supported by two or more instruments and analyzed using IVW. All Tier 1 cohort-level records were single-SNP estimates (36/36); the corresponding proportions were 23/24 for Tier 2 A and 91/94 for Tier 2B. The single BE record and all IPF Tier 2B cohort-level records were single-SNP estimates.

Comprehensive analysis framework and results overview for four chronic lung diseases

In a two-sample cis-MR analysis based on 8,733 independent eGenes, the four chronic lung diseases showed clear differences in the number and strength of prioritized immune-cell-specific candidate signals. Following a predefined screening strategy, all reported gene–immune cell type–disease associations were required to simultaneously satisfy two criteria: MR statistical significance (PFDR < 0.05) in at least one outcome cohort and strong colocalization evidence (PPH4 ≥ 0.8) in the corresponding cohort. Results were further stratified into Tier 1, Tier 2 A, and Tier 2B based on cross-cohort consistency, thereby reflecting the relative strength of statistical evidence for target prioritization. Overall, asthma yielded the richest and most reproducible set of prioritized signals, whereas COPD provided a more limited but still informative set of leads. Candidate associations for IPF and BE were comparatively sparse and were primarily confined to single cohorts, suggesting that these signals should be interpreted mainly as exploratory leads under the current framework. These findings indicate that the quantity and confidence level of prioritized candidates vary substantially across chronic lung diseases and provide an evidence gradient for downstream target ranking, hypothesis generation, and follow-up validation. A consolidated summary of the prioritized targets and their cross-cohort evidence levels is provided in Table 1. Accordingly, the tier labels should be read as relative statistical evidence classes rather than therapeutic priority levels or validated clinical-actionability categories.

Immune cell-specific genetic associations with asthma

Using FinnGen asthma GWAS data as the outcome, we first performed systematic two-sample cis-MR analyses across all eligible immune-cell-specific eGenes (Supplementary Table 2). After heterogeneity and pleiotropy assessments (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4), we identified 762 MR associations that remained statistically significant after multiple-testing correction (PFDR < 0.05) (Fig. 3A). In addition, all instrumental variables passed the directionality test and weak-instrument evaluation, as indicated by acceptable Steiger test results and F statistics (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). These findings suggested that the asthma-related MR results in FinnGen were generally robust in terms of causal direction and instrument strength.

After integrating the MR and colocalization results from the two cohorts, we ultimately prioritized 50 immune-cell-specific candidate associations for asthma (Figures S1-2). All of these candidate associations met the criterion for strong colocalization with asthma-risk signals (PPH4 ≥ 0.8). These results suggested that the retained associations were unlikely to reflect statistical correlation driven solely by linkage disequilibrium and were more likely to represent candidate causal associations supported by shared genetic architecture. Compared with the other diseases examined, asthma contributed not only the largest number of significant MR associations but also the greatest number of retained prioritized candidates for downstream ranking.

We further stratified the asthma-related candidate associations according to cross-cohort consistency in MR significance and colocalization support. Six druggable genes were classified as Tier 1 because they reached MR significance in both FinnGen and UK Biobank and showed strong colocalization in both cohorts. These genes were CD247, FADS1, LINC00299, RP11-104L21.3, RPS26, and TMEM258 (Figure S1). This group represented the most robust and reproducible candidate signals associated with asthma risk in our study.

In addition, 14 druggable genes were classified as Tier 2 A, indicating that they achieved MR significance in both independent cohorts but showed strong colocalization support in only one cohort (Figure S1). These associations therefore had clear genetic support, although their fine-mapping evidence was not fully consistent across cohorts. The remaining candidate associations were classified as Tier 2B, including 19 genes supported in FinnGen and 11 genes supported in UK Biobank (Figure S2). Although these associations satisfied both MR significance and strong colocalization criteria in only a single cohort, they still represented an important set of candidates for further investigation. Overall, asthma contributed the largest and strongest set of prioritized immune-cell-specific candidates in this study and therefore provided the most informative signals for downstream target ranking.

To provide pathway-level context for the prioritized candidates, we summarized GO, KEGG, and Reactome functional annotation results (Supplementary Table 13). The provided outputs mapped four protein-coding Tier 1 candidates (CD247, FADS1, RPS26, and TMEM258) to GO terms, and three candidates (CD247, FADS1, and RPS26) to KEGG and Reactome pathways. GO analysis yielded 66 retained terms, including 16 terms with adjusted P < 0.05, whereas KEGG yielded 12 exploratory terms with adjusted P < 0.10 and Reactome yielded 62 retained pathways, including 48 with adjusted P < 0.05. These annotations provided descriptive support for immune-regulatory, lipid-metabolic, and translation-related biological coherence, but they were not interpreted as independent pathway-level validation because of the small input gene set.

Distinct genetic features of COPD, IPF, and BE

For COPD, we first performed MR analyses in the FinnGen cohort (Supplementary Table 14), followed by sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Tables 15 and 16) and instrument directionality and weak-instrument evaluations (Supplementary Tables 6 and 17). These analyses identified 163 significant MR associations (Fig. 3B). We then repeated the same procedure in the UK Biobank cohort and identified 12 significant MR associations (Supplementary Tables 6 and 18–31; Fig. 4B). After performing colocalization analyses separately in the two cohorts (Supplementary Tables 22 and 23), we ultimately retained 17 COPD-related candidate associations.

In terms of evidence stratification, 3 COPD-related druggable genes were classified as Tier 2 A, whereas the remaining 14 were classified as Tier 2B (Figure S3). These findings indicate that COPD still contains immune-cell-specific candidate signals, although the number of reproducible high-confidence candidates is substantially smaller than that observed for asthma. Overall, the COPD-associated signals provide a more limited but still potentially informative set of leads for downstream prioritization and follow-up validation.

For IPF, 50 significant and eligible MR associations were identified in FinnGen (Supplementary Tables 6 and 24–27; Fig. 3C), whereas no significant MR associations meeting the predefined criteria were detected when UK Biobank served as the outcome dataset (Supplementary Tables 6 and 28–31). Accordingly, colocalization analysis was performed only in the eligible FinnGen cohort (Supplementary Table 32). Ultimately, 4 IPF-related druggable genes met the final screening criteria, and all were classified as Tier 2B (Figure S3). These results suggested that, under the current analytical framework and available data conditions, immune-cell-specific candidate associations for IPF were relatively limited and were primarily supported by a single cohort.

Similarly, for BE, no valid MR associations were identified when FinnGen was used as the outcome dataset (Supplementary Tables 6 and 33–36), whereas 14 significant MR associations were identified when UK Biobank served as the outcome dataset (Supplementary Tables 6 and 37–40; Fig. 4C). On this basis, we performed additional colocalization analysis only in the UK Biobank cohort (Supplementary Table 41). Ultimately, only 1 candidate gene satisfied the final screening criteria and was classified as Tier 2B (Figure S3). Compared with asthma and COPD, BE showed much sparser candidate associations, suggesting that robust immune genetic regulatory signals were more limited under the current sample size, phenotype definition, and immune-cell eQTL resolution. Given that this BE candidate was supported only in UKB and had no corresponding FinnGen-supported signal, it should be interpreted strictly as an exploratory lead rather than as a robust BE-specific prioritized target.

Taken together, the four chronic lung diseases differed substantially in the number and confidence level of prioritized immune-cell-specific candidate associations. Asthma yielded the largest set of prioritized candidates and the strongest cross-cohort support, whereas COPD retained a more moderate but still informative set of leads. By contrast, candidate associations for IPF and BE were more restricted and were largely supported by a single cohort. These findings indicate that the evidence available for candidate gene prioritization is unevenly distributed across chronic lung diseases under the present analytical framework. These cross-disease differences should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating patterns rather than definitive evidence of disease-specific tissue localization. The BE result was the most limited among the four diseases and was retained only as a proof-of-principle signal for future validation.

Translational annotation and PheWAS analyses

After identifying druggable genes with strong colocalization support, we systematically queried DrugBank, DGIdb, and HMDB to evaluate their drug-related annotations. In total, 17 prioritized druggable genes showed drug-, compound-, or metabolite-related annotations across DrugBank, DGIdb, and HMDB (Table 2). These findings suggested that a subset of the prioritized candidates already had tractable connections to existing pharmacological resources and may therefore serve as hypothesis-generating leads for downstream translational prioritization and future mechanistic or pharmacological studies. The presence of a drug–gene or compound–gene entry was therefore interpreted only as a pharmacological annotation signal and not as evidence that modulating the target would benefit asthma, COPD, IPF, or BE.

For druggable genes that were linked to approved drugs, we further conducted PheWAS analyses to evaluate their potential phenotypic associations beyond chronic lung diseases and to provide a phenotypic annotation context (Supplementary Table 42). This additional annotation layer helps distinguish druggable genes with more focused phenotypic profiles from those displaying broader pleiotropic signals. Thus, the PheWAS results should be read as phenotypic annotation and pleiotropy screening rather than as a formal drug-safety, therapeutic-window, or clinical-feasibility assessment.

Discussion

This study systematically prioritized genetically supported candidate genes with stronger prioritization evidence across asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), and bronchiectasis (BE) by integrating single-cell cis-eQTL data from 14 immune cell types with two-sample cis-MR and Bayesian colocalization analyses [18]. To explicitly account for population-specific genetic architectures and the inherent heterogeneity of complex respiratory traits, we employed a stringent parallel evaluation framework. Rather than relying on a conventional discovery-validation design—which may inadvertently discard biologically meaningful but cohort-specific signals—we considered FinnGen and the UK Biobank as two independent yet complementary sources of genetic evidence to establish a tiered prioritization framework based on MR significance, colocalization strength, and cross-cohort consistency [28, 29]. The central aim of this framework was not to claim definitive target validation within a single study, but to identify druggable candidate genes with greater robustness, reproducibility, and translational plausibility for downstream functional validation, target ranking, and follow-up translational evaluation. Under this framework, prioritized candidates with different levels of evidence were identified across all four chronic lung diseases, with asthma yielding the richest and strongest cross-cohort signals, COPD providing a more moderate but still informative set of leads, and IPF and BE contributing predominantly exploratory candidates. Collectively, these findings support the use of single-cell eQTL-guided cis-MR and colocalization as a practical framework for candidate target prioritization in chronic lung diseases [23, 24].

Among the four diseases, asthma contributed the largest and most reproducible set of prioritized candidates and therefore represented the most informative component of the present target-prioritization results. This combination of signal abundance and cross-cohort reproducibility suggests that the current PBMC-derived immune-cell framework is particularly effective in disease contexts with strong immune involvement [30]. By contrast, COPD retained a smaller but still biologically informative set of leads, whereas IPF and BE showed substantially fewer prioritized candidates and were largely supported by single-cohort evidence. These differences are likely to reflect both disease biology and study power. Asthma is strongly shaped by immune-mediated mechanisms and is therefore more likely to display stable and detectable regulatory signals within peripheral immune cells, whereas the pathogenic processes of IPF and BE may depend more heavily on local lung tissue niches that are less well captured by PBMC-derived single-cell eQTL data. Accordingly, the present results should not be interpreted as indicating that IPF and BE lack relevant target biology, but rather that the current framework is better suited to identifying genetically supported candidate targets in diseases with stronger peripheral immune genetic architecture. Importantly, this interpretation remains inferential because the present design cannot distinguish true tissue specificity from differences in case number, phenotype heterogeneity, statistical power, or eQTL instrument performance.

This exposure context is particularly important for interpreting cross-disease differences. Peripheral blood transcriptomic clustering studies support the relevance of circulating immune-cell programs in asthma, particularly in severe or immune-active phenotypes [31]. In COPD, peripheral immune regulation may also capture part of the systemic inflammatory component, but the disease is additionally shaped by smoking-related oxidative stress, airway epithelial injury, lung structural remodeling, and local immune-cell infiltration [32]. Therefore, PBMC-derived signals should be viewed as biologically informative but incomplete representations of chronic lung disease biology.

Within the asthma results, the prioritized candidates converge most clearly on adaptive immune regulation [30, 33], lipid metabolism [34, 35], and intracellular homeostasis [36, 37], suggesting that the present framework captures multiple but biologically coherent axes of immune-mediated disease risk [38, 39]. Among these candidates, CD247 stands out as a plausible lead because it is closely related to T-cell receptor signaling and adaptive immune activation, indicating that genetically regulated variation in T-cell-mediated inflammatory amplification may represent one of the central pathogenic axes captured in this study [33, 40]. Although direct evidence linking CD247 specifically to asthma remains limited, its immune-regulatory role and its cross-cohort Tier 1 support make it a reasonable priority candidate for downstream validation. The DrugBank/DGIdb links involving CD247, including oncology-oriented monoclonal antibodies such as elranatamab and glofitamab, should therefore be interpreted as database-derived pharmacological annotations rather than practical asthma repurposing candidates.

FADS1 is another particularly informative lead because it not only maps to lipid metabolism and inflammatory mediator generation, but also illustrates one of the main advantages of single-cell target prioritization [34, 35]. In the present analysis, higher genetically predicted FADS1 expression was associated with increased asthma risk in B Mem and CD4 ET cells, but showed a protective direction in CD8 ET and NK cells. This pattern suggests that the direction of effect of the same candidate gene may differ across immune cell types and therefore may be obscured or averaged out in bulk-expression frameworks. In this sense, FADS1 is valuable not only as a prioritized asthma lead, but also as an example of why immune-cell-resolved analysis can improve mechanistic interpretation and downstream target ranking in complex diseases. This interpretation should also consider the broader FADS locus context, because regulatory variants in this region may influence multiple nearby transcripts or correlated lipid-metabolic pathways; therefore, FADS1 should be regarded as the prioritized candidate gene within a shared regulatory locus rather than as a definitively isolated causal gene. This cell-type-dependent directionality also limits direct therapeutic interpretation: a systemic intervention such as icosapent would not necessarily reproduce a beneficial immune-cell-specific regulatory effect and could theoretically influence both risk-increasing and protective pathways.

TMEM258 and RPS26 further suggest that the genetically supported architecture of asthma is not limited to canonical inflammatory signaling alone, but may also involve more fundamental processes related to endoplasmic reticulum homeostasis, protein translation, and intracellular stability [36, 37]. These candidates currently have less disease-specific support than CD247 or FADS1 and should therefore be interpreted more cautiously. Nevertheless, their repeated prioritization indicates that non-classical cellular stress and homeostatic programs may represent additional dimensions of asthma biology that merit further investigation. Likewise, the recurrence of non-coding candidates such as LINC00299 and RP11-104L21.3 suggests that regulatory variation beyond protein-coding genes may also contribute to the immune genetic architecture of asthma, although these signals should presently be regarded as hypothesis-generating leads rather than near-term translational targets.

Compared with asthma, COPD yielded a smaller and less reproducible set of prioritized candidates, indicating that immune-cell-specific regulatory signals are present but are more dispersed and less consistently captured across cohorts. This pattern is biologically plausible. Unlike asthma, COPD arises from the interaction of smoking exposure, oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, protease–antiprotease imbalance, tissue destruction, and abnormal repair, and its genetic architecture therefore may not converge as strongly on peripheral immune-cell expression regulation alone [41, 42]. Accordingly, the more limited COPD signal should not be interpreted as absence of genetic relevance, but rather as evidence that the immune component of COPD is broader, less concentrated, and potentially more context-dependent. This interpretation is also compatible with redox-regulatory biology, because NADPH oxidase/NOX2-related reactive oxygen species production and Nrf2-mediated antioxidant responses represent important oxidative-stress pathways that can interact with inflammatory injury, although these pathways were not directly prioritized as causal targets by the present analysis [43–45].

Among the prioritized COPD candidates, HLA-DQA1, HLA-DQA2, and ERAP2 point most directly to antigen presentation and immune recognition, suggesting that peripheral immune regulation still contributes meaningfully to COPD susceptibility. These genes may represent the most biologically interpretable COPD leads under the current framework because they map onto established immune pathways rather than isolated or weakly contextualized signals. By contrast, candidates such as DRAM2 and RHBDF1 may reflect broader processes related to cellular stress, tissue homeostasis, and injury–repair responses [46–48]. Taken together, the present findings suggest that COPD should be viewed as a disease with a genuine but more diffuse immune-genetic component, and that its prioritized candidates are best interpreted as moderate-confidence leads for downstream validation rather than as near-definitive targets. The HLA region is especially complex because dense LD and extensive immune regulatory architecture can make it difficult to assign a shared genetic signal to a single named gene; therefore, HLA-DQA1 and HLA-DQA2 should be interpreted as candidate genes within a broader antigen-presentation locus.

In contrast to asthma and COPD, IPF and BE contributed substantially fewer prioritized candidates under the present framework. This relative paucity is likely to reflect both disease biology and study power. The core pathogenic processes of IPF are closely related to alveolar epithelial injury, aberrant repair, fibroblast activation, extracellular matrix remodeling, and the fibrotic microenvironment, whereas BE is characterized more prominently by chronic infection, impaired mucus clearance, airway structural damage, and a self-reinforcing cycle of local inflammation and infection [3, 49–51]. These features support the possibility that important disease-driving regulatory mechanisms in IPF and BE may be more strongly rooted in local lung tissue niches than in peripheral immune-cell expression profiles derived from PBMCs, although this interpretation cannot be separated from power, phenotype, and instrument-related factors in the current study.

Conversely, the limited number of prioritized candidates in IPF and BE is biologically plausible under a PBMC-based framework but should not be overinterpreted as evidence of weak or absent target biology. IPF involves spatially organized fibrotic niches, epithelial injury, macrophage-fibroblast interactions, and extracellular-matrix remodeling [52]. BE is dominated by airway infection, impaired mucociliary clearance, chronic neutrophilic inflammation, and progressive airway damage [53]. These mechanisms are likely to be better represented by lung tissue, airway, bronchoalveolar lavage, macrophage/fibroblast-focused, or disease-state single-cell and spatial datasets than by healthy PBMC eQTLs alone.

Notably, while our PBMC-derived single-cell eQTL framework successfully captured systemic immune signals in asthma, it yielded fewer prioritized candidates for IPF (4 targets) and BE (1 target). From a translational perspective, this divergence is informative. It provides genetic support for the distinct pathobiological features among chronic lung diseases. Asthma appears to be heavily influenced by systemic adaptive and innate immune networks, making peripheral immune candidates particularly informative for target prioritization. In contrast, the core pathogenic processes of IPF and BE—such as aberrant alveolar repair, fibrotic extracellular matrix remodeling, and impaired local mucus clearance—may be more closely tied to local lung tissue microenvironments. This suggests that future target-discovery efforts for IPF and BE might benefit from prioritizing airway- or lesion-derived local multi-omics data alongside peripheral blood markers, offering a helpful methodological consideration for future research designs. The single retained BE association should therefore not be interpreted as a stable disease-specific target, but as a preliminary signal that requires replication in larger bronchiectasis GWAS datasets with more refined radiological and clinical definitions.

Beyond disease-level differences, an important contribution of this study is the establishment of a cross-cohort evidence-based prioritization framework. In this framework, Tier 1 candidates represent the strongest level of support because they satisfy both MR significance and strong colocalization in two independent cohorts; Tier 2 A candidates retain cross-cohort MR support but have incomplete fine-mapping concordance; and Tier 2B candidates represent exploratory signals supported within a single cohort. The value of this structure lies not merely in grouping results, but in translating heterogeneous statistical findings into an operational evidence gradient that can guide downstream decision-making. These tiers should be interpreted as statistical prioritization classes rather than as direct measures of clinical readiness or validated therapeutic importance [28, 29]. This evidence gradient is especially important in a study spanning multiple diseases, multiple immune cell types, and two independent outcome resources. Without such stratification, nominally significant associations may be interpreted too uniformly, even when their degrees of causal support and reproducibility differ substantially. By contrast, the present framework more clearly distinguishes which candidates should be prioritized for functional validation, which should be replicated in additional datasets, and which should remain as exploratory leads for future follow-up. In this sense, the framework itself is part of the main contribution of the study, because it converts broad single-cell genetic screening into a more operational target-ranking strategy. Specifically, Tier 2 A candidates should be interpreted as lower-confidence than Tier 1 candidates because they lack complete cross-cohort colocalization concordance, even when MR associations are observed in both cohorts.

At the translational level, the present study extends beyond candidate-gene identification by linking strongly colocalized candidates to DrugBank, DGIdb, and HMDB and by using PheWAS to provide broader phenotypic context. These additional analyses do not establish therapeutic efficacy, but they do help distinguish candidates that may offer more tractable entry points for downstream follow-up from those that are currently more useful as pathway-level leads [54, 55]. In other words, the translational value of the present framework lies less in claiming immediate clinical applicability and more in improving the prioritization of candidates for the next stage of validation. This distinction is important. A gene–drug connection does not by itself indicate that the corresponding intervention is appropriate for chronic lung disease, because true translational relevance still depends on concordance of effect direction, immune-cell specificity, therapeutic window, disease context, and the possibility of systemic adverse effects. Accordingly, the drug-database and PheWAS results presented here are best interpreted as tools for translational contextualization and target ranking. They help define which genetically supported candidates may be most practical to investigate next, but they should not be overinterpreted as direct evidence of clinical utility. The PheWAS results further support this interpretive framework. In the present study, PheWAS was not intended to validate respiratory disease causality, but to provide phenotypic context for target ranking and translational caution. When a prioritized gene shows associations with multiple non-respiratory trait domains, this may reflect shared biology, pleiotropic regulation, or potential on-target liabilities that should be considered during downstream development. Conversely, candidates with more limited or biologically coherent phenotypic extensions may represent more focused leads for follow-up investigation. Thus, in this study, PheWAS should be understood primarily as a phenotypic annotation layer that helps refine prioritization rather than as an independent source of target validation. In particular, candidates should not be advanced as repurposing opportunities without matching the genetically inferred direction of effect to the expected pharmacological modulation, confirming the relevant target mechanism, considering the approved clinical indication and safety profile, and evaluating whether systemic or airway-directed delivery is feasible.

Importantly, our study extends beyond traditional risk-gene identification by providing a framework for pharmacological contextualization rather than direct drug repurposing. By matching prioritized, colocalization-supported genes with DrugBank and DGIdb, we identified 17 candidates with database-derived drug or compound annotations. For example, CD247 was linked to monoclonal antibodies such as elranatamab and glofitamab, and FADS1 was linked to small molecules such as icosapent. Because these annotations differ substantially in indication, mechanism, delivery route, and disease relevance, we now present them as hypothesis-generating links that require direction-of-effect matching, cell-type-specific validation, pharmacological feasibility assessment, and respiratory disease-specific testing before any repurposing inference.

From a broader study-design perspective, the present work also highlights the distinctive value of combining single-cell eQTLs with MR and colocalization for target discovery in complex diseases. Compared with bulk-expression frameworks, a single-cell design is better able to resolve cell-type-specific regulatory effects that may otherwise be diluted or misattributed in mixed-tissue contexts [56]. The present findings support this point in a disease-comparative setting: not only do prioritized candidates differ across diseases, but even the strength and direction of evidence vary across immune cellular contexts. This observation suggests that single-cell genetic integration is particularly informative when disease biology is strongly shaped by immune heterogeneity and changing cell composition. At the same time, these results do not imply that single-cell approaches replace bulk frameworks in all settings. Rather, the two are likely to be complementary. For diseases such as asthma, in which peripheral immune driving forces appear stronger, the advantages of an immune single-cell eQTL framework are especially evident. For diseases such as IPF and BE, which may depend more heavily on the local tissue microenvironment, future progress will likely require integration with lung tissue, airway, or lesion-derived multi-omics data. Thus, the main methodological message of this study is not that one framework is universally superior, but that cell-resolved causal prioritization can substantially improve target ranking when aligned with the biology of the disease under study.

Several limitations warrant cautious consideration. First, the exposure data were derived from PBMC-based single-cell cis-eQTLs in healthy individuals rather than from disease-state lung tissue, bronchial samples, or lesion-microenvironment cells. As a result, the present findings are more reflective of peripheral immune genetic regulation and may not fully capture the dominant pathogenic programs of chronic lung disease, especially in IPF and BE, where local tissue niches are likely to play a larger role. Second, although FinnGen and the UK Biobank were treated as independent evidence sources, differences in phenotype definition, case composition, sample size, and population structure may influence judgments of cross-cohort consistency. Third, while the combination of cis-MR and colocalization improves causal prioritization, genetic evidence cannot replace functional experimentation, particularly when clarifying disease-stage specificity, cell-state dependence, temporal effects, and interactions between immune cells and local lung-resident cells. Phenotype comparability between FinnGen and UKB should also be considered when interpreting cross-cohort consistency. COPD, IPF, and BE are particularly vulnerable to differences in diagnostic coding, smoking-related ascertainment, spirometric or imaging confirmation, disease severity, and misclassification. Therefore, incomplete replication across FinnGen and UKB may reflect phenotype heterogeneity or ascertainment differences in addition to biological or population-specific genetic effects. Generalizability is also limited because OneK1K was derived from individuals of Nordic ancestry and both outcome resources were largely European-ancestry datasets; validation in non-European populations and in settings with different environmental exposures is therefore required. Because the two outcome resources were not meta-analyzed, non-replication should not be interpreted as definitive cohort specificity, and may reflect limited power, phenotype heterogeneity, or ancestry- and ascertainment-related differences.

This tissue and disease-state mismatch may have led to preferential detection of systemic immune regulatory signals and under-detection of lung-resident, epithelial, fibroblast, macrophage, infection-related, or fibrosis-related mechanisms, particularly for IPF and BE. Thus, the IPF and BE findings are best regarded as exploratory patterns requiring validation in larger and more disease-relevant datasets rather than as conclusive evidence of tissue-specific pathobiology.

Additional interpretive limitations should also be acknowledged. Genetically predicted transcript abundance does not necessarily translate directly into functional protein activity or therapeutic modulation, and some genes may be upregulated as compensatory responses rather than as primary drivers of disease [55, 57]. Moreover, summary-level MR assumes an approximately linear relationship between exposure and outcome and may not fully capture threshold effects or context-dependent biology. For these reasons, even the highest-priority candidates identified here should still be regarded as genetically supported leads rather than definitive therapeutic targets [28, 58]. In addition, because most prioritized cohort-level records relied on single cis-eQTL instruments, standard multi-instrument sensitivity analyses could not be applied to most retained associations. The corresponding Wald ratio estimates should therefore be interpreted as prioritization signals rather than fully sensitivity-tested causal effects. Another locus-level limitation is that cis-MR and colocalization do not completely resolve effector-gene ambiguity when regulatory variants affect multiple neighboring genes. This is particularly relevant for complex LD regions such as HLA and FADS in the present study and for related respiratory susceptibility regions such as ORMDL3. These signals should therefore be interpreted as candidate gene–locus associations requiring future fine-mapping and functional validation rather than definitive single-gene causal assignments. Because formal alternative-prior and multi-signal colocalization sensitivity analyses were not performed, coloc-supported associations in complex LD regions should be interpreted under the default coloc model rather than as locus-resolved causal assignments. Likewise, druggability and PheWAS annotations do not define therapeutic efficacy, safety, or clinical utility; they should be used only to prioritize candidates for subsequent biological, pharmacological, and disease-specific validation.

Future work can proceed in three directions. First, disease-state single-cell eQTLs and lung- or airway-derived local multi-omics data should be incorporated to test whether the prioritized candidates retain concordant regulatory effects under true disease conditions. Second, the present findings should be re-evaluated in larger independent cohorts with more refined phenotype definitions, particularly for the weaker or single-cohort-supported candidates identified in COPD, IPF, and BE. Third, the most strongly prioritized candidates should be followed up using targeted perturbation studies in disease-relevant models, such as primary human airway epithelial cells or lung organoids. Future functional validation should specifically investigate the mechanistic crosstalk between these genetically supported targets and local tissue remodeling or inflammatory amplification pathways. Such targeted approaches will bridge the critical gap between computational prediction and clinical translational feasibility. Future studies should further evaluate these loci using alternative prior settings, fine-mapping, and multi-signal colocalization approaches when dense locus-level summary statistics are available. For asthma-prioritized candidates such as CD247, FADS1, RPS26, and TMEM258, appropriate follow-up could include perturbation experiments in relevant immune cell subsets, cytokine-response assays, allergen-stimulation models, and co-culture systems with airway epithelial cells. For IPF and BE candidates, validation should instead prioritize lung tissue, fibroblast, macrophage, airway epithelial, bronchoalveolar lavage, or infection/remodeling models that better match the dominant disease microenvironment.

In addition to larger independent GWAS cohorts, future validation should integrate lung tissue eQTLs, airway epithelial datasets, bronchoalveolar lavage immune-cell profiles, fibroblast-focused datasets, and disease-state single-cell or spatial multi-omics resources, with priority given to IPF and BE candidates that were weakly supported or supported by a single cohort in the present study [52].

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides a single-cell eQTL-guided statistical prioritization framework for chronic lung disease candidate genes, with the strongest and most reproducible evidence observed in asthma and more exploratory evidence in COPD, IPF, and BE. The findings should be viewed as genetically supported hypotheses for target ranking and experimental follow-up rather than as proof of therapeutic efficacy, clinical utility, or drug-repurposing readiness. Independent cohort replication, disease-relevant multi-omics validation, and functional perturbation experiments are required before any prioritized gene can be considered a validated therapeutic target.
