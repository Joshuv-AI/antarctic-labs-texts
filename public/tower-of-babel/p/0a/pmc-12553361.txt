Toward a payment model for augmenting Adult Day Services (ADS) with caregiver support: costs, willingness, and ability to pay for the ADS Plus program
Authors: Laura T Pizzi, Katherine M Prioli, Alexa Molinari, Eric Jutkowitz, Katherine A Marx, David L Roth, Joseph E Gaugler, Laura N Gitlin, Amanda N Leggett, PhD, FGSA
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesThe evidence-based Adult Day Services (ADS) Plus program, delivered by ADS staff to caregivers, offers dementia education, support, and strategies to manage care challenges and has been shown in randomized trials to decrease caregiver depressive symptoms and increase ADS utilization. This study examines costs, cost savings, and caregiver willingness to pay (WTP) and ability to pay (ATP) for this evidence-based program.Research Design and MethodsIn a 34-site cluster-randomized trial (16 ADS Plus sites; 18 ADS-only usual care sites) involving 203 caregivers, we surveyed program delivery costs, payer-perspective costs (healthcare utilization, formal care/social services utilization), and societal-perspective costs (caregiver time + payer costs) at baseline and 12-months. Enrollees were caregivers who reported on their outcomes and those of the person living with dementia. Costs were calculated using unit costs or appropriate wage rates. We assessed mean per-dyad costs and the between-group difference in mean change in payer and societal costs from baseline to 12-months. Multilevel mixed models considered clustering, and caregivers’ WTP and ATP were evaluated.ResultsADS Plus delivery costs were $433/dyad over 12-months. From the payer perspective, ADS Plus resulted in nonstatistically significant but policy-significant cost savings of $1,501 (95% CI: −$2,771 to $4,804). From a societal perspective, ADS Plus led to cost savings of $1,185 (95% CI: −$11,187 to $7,036). ADS Plus caregivers’ median per-session WTP was $90 at baseline and $100 at 12-months, compared to $50 and $100 for usual care. ADS Plus caregivers’ median ATP was $31.50 at baseline and $50 at 12-months, versus $25 at both timepoints for usual care.Discussion and ImplicationsAdult Day Services Plus yielded meaningful cost savings, though statistical significance was not achieved. Caregivers were WTP and ATP toward ADS Plus sessions, which could offset program costs. This study elucidates the financial implications of incorporating caregiver support into community-based dementia care programs.Keywords: Dementia, Family caregiving, Caregiver burden, Home- and community-based services, Cost savings

Augmenting ADS with systematic caregiver support delivered by staff (ADS Plus) cost $433 per caregiver over 12 months. Results of this analysis suggest potential payer and societal savings at 12 months. Findings on caregiver ATP for ADS Plus inform their ATP for a dementia support program embedded in ADS and the design of a payment model involving caregiver copayments with subsidies provided for low-income individuals. Overall, ADS Plus offers a low-cost dementia support solution for family caregivers and potential cost savings compared to standard ADS.

Background and objectives

Over 7 million people are living with dementia in the United States, with a projected 13.8 million expected to have a diagnosis by 2060 (Alzheimer’s Association, 2025; GBD 2019 Dementia Forecasting Collaborators, 2022). Over the course of the disease, people with dementia require long-term care services and supports. Most people with dementia want to receive these services in a community-like environment rather than a nursing home (Binette & Farago, 2021; Kane & Kane, 2001). However, helping people with dementia safely age in the community also requires supporting their family caregivers. Family caregivers provide the majority of long-term care for older adults, and dementia care is often intensive and may require constant supervision (Schulz & Eden, 2016, p. 67). An estimated 11.9 million caregivers provided 19.2 billion hours of unpaid care (Alzheimer’s Association, 2025), and caregivers are responsible for paying over 70% of the total lifetime costs of dementia care (Jutkowitz et al., 2021) estimated at an economic value of care provided by unpaid caregivers of $413.5 billion in 2024 (Alzheimer’s Association, 2025).

Home- and community-based services (HCBS) can help people with dementia age in the community. HCBS includes several services (e.g., nonmedical transportation and home care) to address health needs and support daily activities. One important HCBS for people with dementia and their caregivers is adult day services (ADS), which are nonresidential day programs for individuals with daily care needs ranging from health-related to social. Although the number of ADS in the United States is unclear and may range from 5,000 to 7,500, this is a growing community-based service (https://www.nadsa.org/). Importantly, ADS involve highly diverse communities, with Hispanic and Asian participation growing the fastest. ADS located in urban areas tend to be more racially and ethnically diverse than those located in rural areas. Also, there are culturally-specific centers tailored to various racial and ethnic groups, including Korean, Chinese, African American, and Vietnamese (Gitlin et al., 2024).

ADS have been shown in multiple studies to benefit both clients and their caregivers (Dabelko & Zimmerman, 2008; Fields et al., 2014; Gaugler & Zarit, 2001; Parker & Gitlin, 2021; Tretteteig et al., 2016; Zarit et al., 2014). Although ADS afford caregivers opportunities for respite, with some providing workshops or brief education sessions, caregivers typically have many unmet needs that remain unaddressed. One strategy to address caregiver unmet needs is to augment ADS with systematic caregiver support programs. A recent meta-analysis showed that augmenting ADS with other supportive programs can benefit both the person with dementia and caregivers (Walter & Pinquart, 2020).

In response, we developed the Adult Day Services Plus (ADS Plus) program, which augments ADS with systematic caregiver support provided by ADS staff over 12 months. A previous randomized trial of ADS Plus found improvements in caregiver well-being, caregiver’s ability to manage daily challenges and caregiver depressive symptoms, as well as increased use of ADS and reduced nursing home admissions compared to usual ADS (Gitlin et al., 2006). A more recent replication trial involving 34 sites throughout the United States (Gitlin et al., 2019), similarly found that caregivers receiving ADS Plus had reduced depressive symptoms and used adult day services more (>40 additional days) compared to usual ADS (Gitlin et al., 2024). We also found that caregivers who received ADS Plus in sites with moderate-to-high fidelity or treatment adherence benefited more with regard to depressive symptoms and distress than caregivers in sites implementing ADS Plus with low fidelity (Gitlin et al., 2025).

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the costs of ADS Plus compared to usual ADS from a payer perspective (i.e., Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services [CMS]) and societal perspective, which considers payer-perspective costs plus costs related to caregiving. A secondary aim was to assess caregivers’ WTP and ATP for ADS Plus sessions. This cost study was conducted in parallel with the multisite cluster randomized trial with 34 sites.

Research design and methods

ADS Plus program

ADS Plus has been described previously (Gitlin et al., 2006; 2019). Briefly, it is a 12-month program for family caregivers of clients in adult day sites, which is delivered by staff trained in the program. Caregivers work with staff to identify their three most distressing or difficult care challenges, such as helping the person bathe, coordinating care, or taking care of themselves or others (Gitlin et al., 2025). Staff then provide five treatment components: (1) dementia education, (2) strategies to address caregiver-identified care challenges, (3) strategies for taking care of themselves, including stress-reduction techniques, (4) referral and linkages, and (5) validation and support. Strategies are tailored to the needs and cultural values and preferences of caregivers. Sessions could be delivered in Spanish if staff and caregivers preferred.

Delivered either in-person onsite or via telephone to family members of ADS clients, the first 3 months involved up to eight sessions that were followed by periodic check-in calls or in-­person sessions every month for up to 12 months to support strategy use, problem-solving of newly emerging care challenges, and to validate and support caregivers.

The ADS Plus program was tested using a site-level cluster randomized trial in which 16 of the 34 participating sites received ADS Plus, and 18 received usual care (ADS only) (Gaugler et al., 2024; Roth et al., 2020). Site inclusion criteria and randomization procedures have been reported in detail elsewhere (Gitlin et al., 2019; Roth et al., 2020). At participating sites, caregivers meeting the following inclusion criteria were invited to participate in the 12-month study: being a caregiver of a relative with dementia enrolled in the participating ADS, planning to stay in the area over 6 months with at least one planned ADS visit per week, and were willing to complete three telephone interviews. Families were excluded if either the caregiver or person with dementia had more than three hospitalizations in the past year, if either were receiving terminal care (Gitlin et al., 2019), or if the person with dementia scored below 3.6 on the Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE) cognitive screening (Ding et al., 2018). Caregivers in sites randomized to ADS Plus received the program from on-site staff trained in its protocols, and caregivers in sites randomized to the usual care group only received usual ADS. Caregivers reported on their outcomes and the outcomes of the person living with dementia.

Program delivery cost, healthcare utilization, formal care utilization, and caregiving time

We valued all costs in $US 2021 and performed analyses from both the payer and societal perspectives, as recommended by the US Panel for cost effectiveness in health and medicine (Neumann et al., 2016). The payer’s perspective included only direct costs, whereas the societal perspective included both direct and indirect costs.

Program delivery costs comprised the time (and value) spent screening caregivers for program eligibility (both ADS Plus and usual care), training adult day care center staff (ADS Plus only), preparing and delivering the intervention (ADS Plus only), and materials and supplies (both ADS Plus and usual care). All personnel time was costed using an appropriate national mean wage rate plus fringe benefits (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2022a, 2022b). Eligibility screening (5 minutes per dyad) was costed based on a community health worker wage rate. Training was delivered via Zoom; the trainees’ time was costed as a community service specialist, with the trainer’s time costed at 20% above the trainees’ wage rate. ADS Plus staff interventionist time preparing for and delivering the intervention was also costed as a community service specialist. Materials and supplies over the course of the ADS program included $149.90 in Zoom costs (ADS Plus only) plus $20.72 per dyad for a book of strategies for dementia caregiving given to each caregiver (both ADS Plus and usual care).

Healthcare utilization, use of formal care (e.g., meals on wheels and social work visits), social services, and caregiver time providing care were collected via caregiver survey at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months, with a lookback period of 6 months for the assessments. At each survey, caregivers also reported the number, reason, and, when appropriate, intensity (e.g., hours of home health) of each encounter by service type.

Healthcare utilization includes hospitalizations (captured for both the person with dementia and caregiver), emergency department (ED) visits (both person with dementia and caregiver), nursing home stays (person with dementia only, inclusive of long-term and postacute stays), overnight respite care stays (person with dementia only), outpatient visits (both person with dementia and caregiver), home health aide visits (person with dementia only), and visiting nurse utilization (person with dementia only).

Each hospitalization event was mapped to an appropriate Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG) code and costed via the published national average payment rate (Krawzik & Schmidt, 2019). Similarly, reasons for ED visits were mapped to a Clinical Classification Software Refined (CSSR) code and costed via the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project lookup tool in $US 2018 (Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality, 2022). These ED costs were then inflated to $US 2021 using medical inflation rates using an online calculator tool (Halfhill, 2022).

Nursing home stays were captured as the number of days the person with dementia was admitted to a nursing home ($216.79/day; AMA-ASSN, 2022). Overnight respite-care stays were costed as equivalent to 8 hours at a home health aide (HHA) wage rate (assuming that the value of a HHA’s time would be a reasonable proxy for the value of respite care, regardless of whether a HHA in particular provides it), i.e., $14.07/hour plus fringe benefits (U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2022a; 2022b) using an approach taken in previous studies (Jutkowitz et al., 2010). Outpatient visit costs were estimated using specialist-appropriate Current Procedural Terminology codes (American Medical Association, 2022; Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, 2022a). To avoid overestimation, we applied the lowest level of reimbursement for outpatient visits (e.g., the lowest intensity of service code). HHAs were costed using the HHA wage rate plus fringe benefits. Similarly, visiting nurse utilization was costed using the national mean wage rate for a registered nurse in 2021 ($39.78/hour) plus fringe benefits.

Formal care and social services comprise days in an assisted living facility (person with dementia only), social worker visits (both person with dementia and caregiver), delivered meals (person with dementia only), and transportation to medical care (person with dementia only). Assisted living stays were costed using a daily rate of $148.39 (National Adult Day Services Association, 2010). Social worker visits were costed via wage rate ($25.94/hour) plus fringe benefits and assumed to last 1 hour each. Home-delivered meals were assumed to cost $12.63 each (Ziegler, 2015). Transportation to medical care was costed at $3.36 per round trip for both public transit and family transit, the latter assuming that if a family member were not available to drive the person with dementia, public transit would be used instead (U.S. Bureau of Transportation Statistics, 2022; National Aging and Disability Transportation Center, 2018). Ambulance transport was costed at $234.44/trip (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, 2022b).

Caregivers reported the time they spent actively assisting the person with dementia (average hours per day, capped at a maximum of 16 hours per day), time spent meeting with staff interventionists (ADS Plus only), time and mileage for round-trip travel to adult day care centers, time the caregiver was paid for caregiving, and (for caregivers who were working), the number of full (i.e., 8-hour) and half (4-hour) days of work missed due to caregiving responsibilities. All caregiver time was costed by assigning an appropriate wage rate (plus fringe benefits) to the caregiver’s stated occupation; if the caregiver was not employed, a home health aide wage rate was used instead. Mileage for caregiver travel to adult day care centers was costed at the standard federal mileage rate of $0.56/mile (Internal Revenue Service, 2021).

WTP and ATP analyses

Caregivers’ WTP for ADS Plus was evaluated at baseline, 6, and 12 months using a contingent valuation method, in which caregivers in both usual care and ADS Plus sites were asked to indicate the amount they would be willing to pay per session; response options ranged from $0 to $200 in increments of $25. Though only intervention group participants received ADS Plus, usual care group participants were given a brief explanation of the program and its benefits and asked the amount (in dollars) they would pay to participate in the program. ATP was also evaluated using the same approach with the exception that “willing-to-pay” was replaced with “could pay.” Supplementary Material Section A reports the WTP and ATP questions administered to caregivers at each timepoint.

Analysis

We calculated the mean per-dyad (caregiver + person living with dementia) cost for each individual cost item (e.g., hospitalizations), each cost category (e.g., healthcare utilization), and total costs by perspective. Next, we calculated within- and between-group differences in mean costs captured at baseline versus those at 12 months (i.e., the difference in mean change [DMC]). Each between-group DMC was tested via two-sided t-tests. Primary outcomes were 12-month DMC between groups for each cost category (i.e., costs of healthcare utilization, formal care and social services, and caregiver time), and total costs (i.e., the sum of all costs other than the program itself) by perspective. Secondary outcomes included 12-month DMC for each cost type (e.g., inpatient hospitalization, assisted living). To adjust for Type I error inflation due to multiple familywise tests, the Bonferroni correction was used for the primary outcomes, with an adjusted significance threshold of αadj = .05/5 = .01. Costs were assessed from the payer and societal perspectives. Because the parent trial was cluster-­randomized at the site level, we performed sensitivity analyses by using mixed effect models to analyze the same data (Supplementary Material Section B). These models account for nesting of the dyads within sites with group assignment and including site- and dyad-level covariates. WTP and ATP were analyzed using descriptive statistics (median and interquartile range), and within-group change over time was assessed as the median of differences for baseline versus 6 months and baseline versus 12 months. All analyses were performed in R 4.1.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 203 caregivers were enrolled, with 102 in sites randomized to ADS Plus and 101 in usual care sites. Baseline demographics for the entire sample and by treatment group are shown in Table 1. As previously reported (Gitlin et al., 2024), participants with dementia, on average (standard deviation), were 80.3 (9.44) years of age, had an IQCODE cognitive impairment score of 4.78 (0.29), and most were White (75.9%) and female (51.2%). Caregivers similarly were mostly White (76.4%), female (80.3%), 65.7 (13.46) years of age, and either a spouse (50.2%) or adult child (41.4%). Most caregivers lived with the person with dementia (94.1%).

Healthcare utilization costs

Supplementary Material Section C reports service utilization, and Table 3 reports the individual- and category-level costs. From baseline to 12 months, there was a large but nonsignificant reduction in healthcare costs for people in the ADS Plus group compared to usual care (savings of $1,521, 95% CI: −$2, 435 to $4, 772). The lower health care costs were driven by hospitalization costs for ADS Plus dyads compared to usual care (DMC at the 12-month timepoint indicated savings of $1,393, 95% CI: −$693 to $3,106). There were also large but nonsignificant reductions in home health aide and outpatient visits for people in ADS Plus compared to usual care (HHA 12-month savings $973, 95% CI: −$602 to $2,319; outpatient 12-month savings $506, 95% CI: −$97.90 to $1,123). Formal care and social services costs

From baseline to 12 months, there was a small but nonsignificant increase in formal care and social service costs for people randomized to ADS Plus compared to usual care ($19.99, 95% CI: −$910 to $1,164; Table 3). The small cost increase was due to a nonsignificant increase in delivered meals for people randomized to ADS Plus compared to usual care ($276, 95% CI: −$36.20 to $752). At 12 months, there was a nonsignificant savings in assisted living costs for those randomized to ADS Plus compared to usual care ($217, 95% CI: −$740 to $1,108). There was no significant difference in mean costs among the ADS Plus group versus usual care for social worker use (small savings of $14.03, 95% CI: −$8.62 to $52.21) or transportation (savings of $25.22, 95% CI: −$42.05 to $92.27).

Caregiver time costs

From baseline to 12 months, there was a nonsignificant increase in caregiver time (and costs) for those randomized to ADS Plus compared to caregivers in usual care ($316, 95% CI: −$14,990 to $1,341; Table 3). The increase in caregiving time was attributed to time spent participating in the intervention, time spent providing active assistance, and productivity losses.

Total cost savings of ADS plus

At 12 months, there were large but nonsignificant cost savings from both the payer and societal perspectives for ADS Plus compared to usual care (payer perspective savings: $1,501, 95% CI: −$2,771 to $4,804; societal perspective savings: $1,185, 95% CI: −$11,187 to $7,036; Table 3). Sensitivity analysis results using mixed effects models are provided in Supplementary Material Section B and are consistent with those reported above.

WTP and ATP

Median WTP at baseline was $90 (first quartile [Q1] $50, third quartile [Q3] $150) for ADS Plus and $50 (Q1 $25, Q3 $100) for usual care. At 12 months, caregivers in both groups were WTP $100 (median for both groups Q1 $50, Q3 $150; Table 4). Median ATP for ADS Plus and usual care at baseline were $31.50 (Q1 $0, Q3 $75) and $25.00 (Q1 $0, Q3 $50), respectively. At 12 months, ATP increased to $50 (Q1 $20, Q3 $100) in the ADS Plus group and remained unchanged at $25 (Q1 $20, Q3 $50) for caregivers in control group sites.

Discussion and implications

The ADS Plus program positively impacts the well-being of caregivers and increases time spent in ADS for people with dementia (Gitlin et al., 2024; Gitlin et al., 2006). In this analysis, we found the mean per-caregiver cost of delivering ADS Plus was an average of $433 over 12 months. We also found that ADS Plus resulted in large but not statistically significant cost savings from the payer and societal perspectives. It is important to highlight that ADS Plus led to increased costs compared to usual care for nursing home stays, respite care, visiting nurse care, and meal expenses. This was a positive and expected outcome of ADS Plus given that one of the five core treatment components was providing referrals and linkages to local resources, and signifies increased utilization of essential services that can support the ability of the family caregiver to keep the person with dementia at home.

Our findings are important in the context of the recently announced CMS Guiding an Improved Dementia Experience (GUIDE) Model (Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, 2024). This Model will provide dementia beneficiaries with $2500 per year as a respite benefit. It will be important for health systems participating in the GUIDE Model to partner with ADS sites that are skilled in supporting caregivers, as is offered by ADS Plus, since that is an essential part of the GUIDE Model. Our findings demonstrate the cost savings from ADS Plus would benefit a health system operating in a capitated GUIDE Model. Not only does ADS Plus benefit service users, but it can result in cost savings for health systems.

The cost of delivering ADS Plus is less than the cost of other nonpharmacologic dementia support programs such as COPE (Pizzi et al., 2021) at $2,157, TAP (Jutkowitz et al., 2019) at $1,273, the Star-C program at $914 (Bouranis et al., 2017), and the FamTechCare intervention at $629 (Shaw et al., 2021). However, these programs are implemented in different settings, which limits comparison—but it stands to reason that ADS Plus would be low cost because it is embedded within existing ADS services. Prior investigations of nonpharmacologic interventions have identified potential avenues for reducing program delivery costs (Pizzi et al., 2021; Lindauer et al., 2018). For ADS Plus, program delivery costs could potentially be reduced by using asynchronous pre-recorded training for interventionists and making all program materials available in electronic documents (versus mailing printed binders). Additionally, there is a learning curve for newly trained staff during which their time spent preparing for intervention sessions is longer than it would be for those who have more experience with its delivery. Therefore, delivering this program over time and/or via experienced interventionists should yield lower program costs. Moreover, as ADS Plus is delivered by on-site staff, costs are associated with their time in training versus the need for sites to hire new or additional staff. Additionally, in delivering ADS Plus, staff are using their time differently versus adding on additional hours of work.

Our analysis was designed to capture the relevant costs from the payer and societal perspectives. However, recent efforts by the Innovation and Value Initiative and Academy Health have underscored the importance of the patient’s perspective when measuring costs (Malik et al., 2023). This work emphasizes that the impacts of disease and treatment on the individual with dementia and their families should be identified and measured in economic analyses. Though many of the costs that we measured also apply to the patient perspective, future work assessing dementia programs should involve individual and/or caregiver representatives in the design to ensure that the research includes costs that matter to them or are relevant to their lived experiences.

Caregiver-reported WTP and ATP for ADS Plus, as compared to usual care, provide insight into the monetary value that caregivers place on the program. However, as the baseline WTP for the ADS Plus group was so much greater than for usual care, we suspect the ADS Plus caregivers might have overvalued the program due to anticipatory excitement about the additional benefits they expected to receive from participation in the program. An alternative interpretation is that caregivers in the usual care group can value the program given a hypothetical compared to people who received it.

An important question regarding cost analysis in general, particularly related to dementia, is whether and how much families should contribute to receiving HCBS. Considering the 6-month ATP value to be most relevant since this measure was captured immediately after the ADS Plus program ended, caregivers were willing to pay $100 per session but were able to pay $30 per session. An ATP value of $30 per session suggests that despite the financial constraints posed by dementia, families value the ADS Plus program and would help pay for it. Logically, ATP is more relevant to cost-sharing considerations than WTP since financial constraints are likely to be factored into one’s ability, whereas willingness is typically captured using scenarios that assume unlimited financial resources. Given that payment models in the United States are commonly structured to have some cost sharing borne by the patient, our findings suggest that ADS Plus may be amenable to a benefit design involving copayments. Considering that ADS Plus costs $55.49 per session and the ATP was $30 per session, caregivers could potentially cover half of the program’s costs through copayments. However, this calculation does not account for the provision of subsidies to those who are low-income.

Limitations

Cost analyses alongside clinical trials are often challenged by insufficient statistical power, so it is not surprising that most of the cost differences we observed were nonsignificant. Since it would be impractical and cost prohibitive to conduct a clinical trial for ADS Plus that is powered on the cost measures, additional data from large-sample real-world evidence studies would help to inform the program’s value. Additional limitations include using self-reported utilization that we then mapped to reimbursements and applying national average wage rates, which do not account for regional variation. Finally, although ADS throughout the United States serve racially and ethnically diverse communities, the sample for this study was primarily White. This is due in large part to the differential study dropout rate of Latino/Hispanic caregivers compared to others and that sites with more White caregivers and clients with dementia compared to other sites were able to implement the intervention with higher fidelity.

In conclusion, ADS Plus results in meaningful benefits to families and may result in meaningful cost savings for payers and society. Future research needs to include a more heterogeneous sample and adapt the intervention to enable participation by Latino/Hispanic sites.
