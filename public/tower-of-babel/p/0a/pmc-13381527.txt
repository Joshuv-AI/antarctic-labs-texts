Crosstalk between inflammation and autophagy in CeD organoids
Authors: Claudia Bellomo, Francesca Furone, Renata Auricchio, Genoveffa D’Aniello, Raffaella Pennacchio, Roberta Rotondo, Domenico Palladino, Anna Marabotti, Vittoria D’Esposito, Pietro Formisano, Diego L. Medina, Antonietta Tarallo, Carla Damiano, Salvatore Auricchio, Maria Vittoria Barone, Merlin Nanayakkara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Celiac disease (CeD), a small bowel disorder characterized by mucosal inflammation, villous atrophy and crypt hyperplasia, is induced by gliadin peptides in genetically predisposed individuals. Alterations in inflammation, intracellular vesicular trafficking, mTOR signalling and autophagy have been reported in the intestinal epithelium of CeD patients. The aim of this manuscript was to investigate the interplay between autophagy and inflammation in intestinal mucosa of CeD patients. Increased levels of markers related to impaired autophagic flux, mTOR pathway activation and inflammatory signalling were observed in CeD biopsies and organoids from gluten-containing-diet-CeD (GCD-CeD) and gluten-free-diet-CeD (GFD-CeD) compared with controls (CTRs). Immunofluorescence staining confirmed an increase in p62 and miR-30a was decreased in CeD patients. Bafilomycin and rapamycin were used to respectively block and induce autophagic flux in intestinal organoids. Bafilomycin increased p62, pE4-BP1 and pNF-κB levels in CTRs, whereas rapamycin reduced p62, pE4-BP1 and pNF-κB levels in CeD. Bio-Plex analysis of CMs (culture medium) from CeD organoids revealed increased levels of 17/27 in CMs from GCD-CeD and of 5/27 in CMs of GFD-CeD. Control organoids treated with CMs from GCD-CeD showed a reduction in autophagic flux and an increase of inflammation, compared with the untreated CTRs. Overall, our findings support the presence of a crosstalk between autophagy and inflammation at the epithelial level in both intestinal biopsies and organoids from CeD patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42642-5.

Introduction

Celiac disease (CeD) is a multifactorial chronic disease that is induced by wheat proteins and related prolamins from cereals that can induce an immune-mediated enteropathy in genetically susceptible individuals. CeD is also characterized by a variety of gluten-induced symptoms and the generation of CeD-associated auto-antibodies1. The HLA (Human Leukocyte Antigen)-restricted gliadin-specific intestinal T-cell response plays a central role in the pathogenesis of CeD2. Therapy for this disease consists of a lifelong gluten-free diet (GFD).

Data from the literature indicate that intracellular vesicular trafficking is altered in CeD cells. In particular, a delay in endocytic trafficking from early to late vesicles also occurs at the level of intestinal epithelial cells3. Vesicular trafficking is a type of crossroad for cell signalling and can regulate several different pathways, including proliferation and inflammation. Both pathways are altered in CeD intestinal biopsies and at the level of the intestinal epithelium1.

Recent literature describes several different factors in CeD, such as cellular vulnerability, the proinflammatory effects of gluten and other wheat proteins, the Western diet, and environmental triggers, such as viruses, that prepare and/or amplify the T-cell-mediated response to gluten4. Multiple factors, both endogenous and exogenous, can create a proinflammatory environment in the CeD intestine, which leads to the expansion of gliadin-specific T cells in genetically susceptible individuals and further shifts them towards a proinflammatory phenotype4. The natural history of CeD, studied prospectively in subjects at risk, reveals an early inflammatory phase, even pre-gluten, which precedes the autoimmune response to gluten mediated by T cells5. A CeD cellular phenotype has also been identified, most likely genetically/epigenetically determined, which is characterized by constitutive cellular alterations, including inflammation, that could increase the sensitivity of celiac cells to some proinflammatory environmental factors (such as foods and gluten itself, infections, etc.)6,7. Most alterations found in the inflammatory pathways have been described at the level of the intestinal epithelium, both in biopsies and in intestinal organoids8. Interestingly, inflammatory markers, such as pNF-κB (phosphorylation of nuclear-factor-kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells), IL-6 (interleukin-6) and IL-1β, are altered in the intestinal biopsies of so-called potential patients, which present predisposing HLA and tTG autoantibodies (anti-tTG -tissue transglutaminase) but intact intestinal mucosa even before the onset of the intestinal lesion8.

The mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) pathway is activated in intestinal biopsies from CeD patients9. In particular, activation of the mTORC1 pathway has been observed together with an increase of mTOR at the level of the intestinal epithelium in CeD patients9. The mTORC1 pathway is linked to the activation of cell growth and proliferation in the presence of nutrients, growth factors, energy and stress10. Moreover, gliadin peptides can induce mTOR phosphorylation9,11,12. Autophagy has been found altered in genetic13 and expression studies in blood, in intestinal biopsies14 and skin fibroblasts from CeD patients15. Moreover, miR-30a, a known negative regulator of autophagy, has been reported to be altered in celiac disease (CeD), although direct CeD-specific studies addressing its role in autophagy remain limited16. Interestingly, gliadin peptides have been shown to reduce autophagic flux in Caco-2 cells11,12.

This study investigated autophagic flux and its link to inflammation in the intestinal biopsies and organoids from CeD patients both in the acute phase of disease at gluten-containing diet (GCD) and in the remission phase of the disease at GFD.

Materials and methods

Biopsies

Duodenal biopsies were obtained from patients with GCD-CeD that presented with villous atrophy (5 patients, mean age of 10 years), patients with GFD-CeD (4 subjects, mean age of 11 years) and controls affected by gastroesophageal reflux (5 subjects, mean age of 13 years). The age range for all patients was 2–16 years. Patients with villous atrophy (Marsh T3c) had positive serology (anti-tTG antibodies > 50 U/ml and endomysial antibodies positive). Anti-tTG antibody titres were measured via an EU-tTG Eurospital Kit (Table 1). Specimens were harvested, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound and stored at − 80 °C until further analyses were performed (Table 1). The study was conducted according to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Naples Federico II (Protocol number: 115/09/ESPROT). Informed consent from a parent and/or legal guardian was obtained for study participation.

Intestinal organoids

One duodenal biopsy from each individual from the CeD patients and controls was taken with standard endoscopic EGDS during routine gastroduodenoscopy (Table 2) and placed in 10 mL of cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) PBS supplemented with 2 mM EDTA (cat. 15575020, Thermo Fisher, Milan, Italy) and 0.5 mM DTT (D0632, Sigma‒Aldrich, Milan, Italy). After 50 min of agitation at 4 °C, biopsy samples were washed with PBS, filtered through a 70 μm strainer (Falcon, NY, USA) and centrifuged at 2800 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. Then, crypts were resuspended in 40 µL of ice-cold Matrigel matrix (cat.35623, Corning, Milan, Italy) to permit three-dimensional growth in 48-well plates. Afterwards, 300 µL of culture media enriched with supplements (CM-S) was added to each well. The intestinal organoids were grown for 4 weeks from the time of crypt isolation. These can be frozen between the second and fourth passage. The thawed intestinal organoids were grown for 2 weeks before being opened in 2D for experiments. To pass from a 3D to a 2D structure, organoids were seeded on 35 mm multiwell plates pretreated with Matrigel (1:40 in PBS). We generally processed the 2D organoids within 24–72 h after seeding. The human spheroids of GCD-CeD, GFD-CeD and CTRs (controls) were seeded in six multiwell plates (Corning, Milan, Italy) coated with Matrigel diluted at 1:40 in PBS for 3 days. They were then stimulated with bafilomycin (SML1661, Sigma‒Aldrich, Milan, Italy) at concentrations of 0.5 µM and 1 µM, and rapamycin (cat. PHZ1235, Life Technologies, Milan, Italy), at concentrations of 25 nM, 50 nM, and 100 nM, for 3 h (Caslo, Kongens Lyngby, Denmark), at 37 °C (Table 2)8,17. Western blotting

Intestinal organoids were cultivated for 4 weeks and opened in 2D from the duodenum of CeD patients, and CTRs were processed. After removing the Matrigel with Cell Recovery Solution (cat. 354270; Corning Milan Italy), organoids were homogenized in 40–50 µL of tissue homogenization buffer. Cell lysates (10 µg/mL) were analysed by SDS‒PAGE and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes by Trans Blot Turbo (cat. 1704158, Bio-Rad, Milan, Italy). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk and probed with anti-pNF-κB (cat. ab222249, Abcam, Microtech, Naples, Italy, E-AB-68093-200 Elabscience, Microtech, Naples, Italy, cat. FNab10840, Fine Test, Milan, Italy), anti-p62 (Sequestosome-1)/SQSTM1 (cat. P0067, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA), anti-pE4-BP1(eIF4E-binding-protein/phosphorylated) (cat. #9459 and cat#2855, Cell Signalling, Euroclone, Milan, Italy), anti-LC3B (Microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3) (cat. 2775 antibodies, Cell Signalling, Euroclone, Milan, Italy), and anti-NBR1 (Neighbor of BRCA1 gene 1) (cat. 9891, Cell Signalling, Euroclone, Milan, Italy), all antibodies were used 1:1000. Stain-free gel was used as loading controls (10% cat. #4568033 Bio-Rad, Milan, Italy) (4–15% cat. #4568086 Bio-Rad, Milan, Italy). Two 2–5-minute exposures using ECL-plus (Cat. 1705062 Bio-Rad, USA) were subsequently used to visualize the bands of interest. The band intensity was evaluated by integrating all pixels of the band after subtraction of the background to calculate the average of pixels surrounding the band8,17. 4E-BP1 is phosphorylated at multiple sites in vivo, generating distinct phospho-isoforms detectable as multiple bands by Western blotting18..

Immunofluorescence staining of biopsies

We used immunofluorescence staining to evaluate p62 levels. The 5-µm cryostat sections from biopsies were air dried, fixed with acetone (10 min), and stained with rabbit anti-p62 (1:100,cat. P0067, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, Missouri, USA) antibody for 1 h at room temperature after blocking with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 15 min at room temperature (RT). Then, sections were washed (3 times, 5 min each) with PBS at RT, and Alexa 488-conjugated anti-rabbit (1:50; cat.11001 Milan, Italy) antibody was applied for 45 min at room temperature in a dark box to avoid bleaching. After being washed with PBS, sections were counterstained with Hoechst (Sigma‒Aldrich, Milan, Italy), mounted with Mowiol (Sigma‒Aldrich, Milan, Italy) and observed under Zeiss LSM510 or Zeiss LSM700 confocal microscopes (laser scanning microscope) (Germany). Images were obtained with a 63× objective unless otherwise stated.

miR-30a-5p expression

Total RNA, including small RNAs, was extracted from intestinal biopsies and patient-derived intestinal organoids using the miRNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cDNA synthesis was performed using the TaqMan MicroRNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA), starting from 10ng of total RNA. Expression of microRNAs was analyzed using TaqMan MicroRNA Assays (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Specifically, hsa-miR-30a-5p (ID 000417_mir) was quantified and normalized to RNU6B (U6) (ID 001093_mir) as endogenous control. qRT-PCR was performed using the TaqMan Universal PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) on a Roche LightCycler 480 System. Each reaction was carried out in technical duplicate. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method. All data are representative of at least three independent biological experiments.

Immunofluorescence staining of organoids

Human spheroids were seeded in 15 multiwells (IBIDI) coated with Matrigel diluted at 1:40 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 2 days. Organoids in 2D culture were subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min, after which cells were washed 3 times with 1X PBS for 10 min each time. Afterwards, they were permeated with 0.2% Triton/1X PBS for 10 min at room temperature. Cells were washed 3 times with 1X PBS for 10 min each time. Slides were blocked with 5% BSA/1X PBS (blocking solution) or 10% FBS/1X PBS for 1 h at room temperature. The primary anti-rabbit p62 antibodies were diluted in blocking solution overnight at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. The cells were subsequently washed 3 times with 1X PBS for 10 min each time. Alexa Fluor 546 donkey anti-rabbit secondary antibody (A10040 Invitrogen, Milan, Italy) was added to organoids. The cells were then washed 3 times with 1X PBS for 10 min each time, mounted with Mowiol (cat. 475904-M Sigma‒Aldrich, Milan, Italy) and observed under a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM 510, Jena, Germany).

Cytokine analysis

Intestinal organoids were grown in 2D to investigate levels of cytokines, chemokines and growth factors in the CMs (culture media). The CMs of the 2D organoids were collected in 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes after 3 h of the experiment and stored at −80 °C. CMs were screened for concentrations of interleukin (IL)-1ra, IL-1b, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-7, IL-8, IL-9, IL-10, IL-12 (p70), IL-13, IL-15, IL-17 A, basic fibroblast growth factor (FGF), eotaxin, granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (G-CSF), granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), interferon-γ inducible protein 10 (IP-10), monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), macrophage inflammatory protein-1 (MIP-1) α, MIP-1β, C-C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5)/RANTES, TNF-α, platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF-BB), and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) using the Bio-Plex Multiplex Human Cytokine, Chemokine, and Growth Factor Kit (cat. n. M500KCAF0Y, Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol19. The magnetic bead-based assay was performed on a Bio-Plex 200 System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). All values obtained were within detection limits indicated by the manufacturer (Bio-Rad.com/Bio-Plex/Analyte Guide)20.

ELISA

The levels of IL-1β in the cell culture supernatants were measured using commercial test kits (IL-1β cat. 850.006.192, IL-6 cat. 950.030.192, Diaclone, Besancon Cedex, France).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and graphical representations were performed using GraphPad Prism 6 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Differences between groups were analysed using Student’s t tests with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. All analyses were two-tailed. Sample sizes were determined on the basis of control sample variance with an estimated need for three to five samples to assess the expected effect. Consequently, the sample size was typically three, four, or five, which was the most common sample size.

Bioinformatics analysis on cytokine expression

To ensure data accuracy, preprocessing was performed using an in-house developed Python script. ND (Not Detectable) values were replaced with the first quartile (Q1) of the corresponding variable’s distribution within each experimental group. This strategy was adopted to minimize data loss and reduce bias while preserving the overall distribution of the variables.

Due to the limited number of samples, differences in cytokine expression among groups were assessed using three statistical tests. Student’s t-test, Welch’s t-test, a variation of Student’s t-test that accounts for unequal variances between groups21, and the Mann-Whitney U test, a non-parametric test that compares two groups without assuming normality22. Only those cytokines with a significant (p ≤ 0.05) difference in expression in all three statistical tests were considered for further analysis.

The interactions of differentially expressed cytokines were analysed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org)23, which enables the construction of protein-protein interaction networks and the functional enrichment analysis of biological pathways. In STRING-based protein–protein interaction analyses, network nodes correspond to proteins and edges correspond to interactions. Protein–protein interactions describe functional relationships and do not necessarily imply direct physical binding. These associations are inferred by integrating multiple sources of evidence, including experimentally validated interactions, curated databases, and predicted interactions based on genomic context, such as gene neighborhood, gene fusion, and gene co-occurrence. Additional evidence is derived from text mining, gene co-expression, and protein sequence similarity. When available, information on protein 3D structure is included through annotation of proteins with known or predicted structures.

According to the STRING database, confidence scores are assigned to protein–protein associations to estimate the probability that an interaction is supported by available evidence. These scores describe the reliability of the predicted associations rather than their strength or biological specificity. The combined confidence score ranges from 0 to 1, with lower values indicating a higher probability of false positive predictions. Interaction networks were obtained as graphical outputs, while interaction tables were derived from STRING-generated .tsv files and processed in Excel. The functional enrichment analysis was conducted by applying default filtering parameters22,23. To ensure informative enrichment analysis results, enriched terms are ranked using the “signal” filter. This metric is calculated as a weighted harmonic mean of two components: the observed-to-expected gene occurrence ratio within each enriched term and the corresponding − log (FDR). By integrating these factors, the filter balances their relative contributions and yields a more interpretable prioritization of enriched terms.

Results

In intestinal biopsies from CeD patients, autophagic flux was reduced and inflammation was present

In intestinal biopsies from CeD patients in the acute phase of the disease (GCD-CeD) and control patients (CTRs), we analysed p62 protein levels by Western blotting (WB) to study the autophagic flux to lysosomes. Overall, the protein level of p62 was significantly increased (Fig. 1A and B; p < 0.05) in GCD-CeD respect to CTRs.

Immunofluorescence staining of biopsies from patients with GCD-CeD and CTRs confirmed the results obtained by WB analysis of p62 protein levels (Fig. 1C and D; p < 0.001). These data indicate that autophagic flux is impaired in CeD intestinal biopsies from CeD patients. Moreover, the protein level of p4E-BP1, a downstream effector of the mTOR pathway, was significantly greater in the GCD-CeD biopsies compared to control biopsies (Fig. 1A and E; p < 0.01). This confirms the observation already reported in the literature9.

Next, we analysed levels of pNF-κB, which is a marker of inflammation, in the same biopsies from patients with GCD-CeD and CTRs. The levels of pNF-κB were increased in GCD-CeD biopsies compared with those in CTRs as analysed by WB (Fig. 1A and F; p < 0.05). These findings indicate that defects in autophagic flux, mTOR pathway activation and inflammation were present in GCD-CeD biopsies.

Moreover, micro-RNA, miR-30a, involved in intestinal epithelial homeostasis and autophagy was decreased in the GCD-CeD biopsies respect to CTRs (Fig. 1G; p < 0.01), as already observed in the literature14.

In intestinal organoids from GCD- and GFD-CeD patients, autophagic flux was reduced, the mTOR pathway was activated, and inflammation was present

Intestinal organoids were derived from intestinal biopsies from GCD-CeD, GFD-CeD and CTRs. They represent mainly the epithelial compartment of intestinal biopsies from which they are derived8.

In the GCD- and GFD-CeD intestinal organoids LC3-II/LC3-I ratio and p62 protein levels were analysed by WB to study autophagy flux (Fig. 2). The LC3-II/LC3-I ratio was significantly decreased in GCD- and GFD-CeD organoids respect to CTRs (Fig. 2A-D; p < 0,01). The levels of p62 were significantly increased in the GCD-CeD and GFD-CeD groups as analysed by WB (Fig. 2A and E; p < 0.05).

Immunofluorescence staining of organoids from the CTRs, GCD-CeD, and GFD-CeD confirmed results obtained by WB on the p62 protein levels (Fig. 2F and G; respectively p < 0.01 and p < 0.05).

Moreover, in GCD-CeD organoids the levels of NBR1, a protein involved in autophagy, and the level of miR-30a were, respectively, increased (Fig. 2H and I; p < 0,05) and decreased (Fig. 2L, p = 0,053)16respect to CTRs.

mTOR pathway and inflammation were investigated by analysing the protein levels of pE4B-P1 and pNF-κB in intestinal organoids from CeD patients respect to CTRs used by WB analysis (Fig. 2). Phospho-E4-BP1 protein levels were significantly greater in both GCD-CeD and GFD-CeD patient’s organoids than in the CTRs (Fig. 2M and N; p < 0.01 for both GFD-CeD and GCD-CeD patients). The levels of pNF-κB were significantly greater in GCD- and GFD-CeD organoids (Fig. 2M and O; p < 0.05 for both) compared to CTRs.

These findings indicate that the defects in autophagic flux, the activation of the mTOR pathway and inflammation, previously described in CeD biopsies, were also present in CeD intestinal organoids.

Bafilomycin and rapamycin treatment in CTRs organoids

Bafilomycin works by disrupting the acidification of lysosomes, which is necessary for the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes, the final step of autophagy. This blockage results in the accumulation of autophagosomes, indicating that autophagic flux is inhibited24. The opposite effect is elicited by rapamycin, a drug that can induce autophagy by mTOR dephosphorylation. To understand the relationship between the delay of autophagic flux and inflammation described in CeD organoids, we induce the block of autophagy in CTR organoids by treating them with bafilomycin and induce the autophagy flux in CeD by rapamycin treatment.

Treatment with bafilomycin (0.5 µM and 1 µM) induced a statistically significant increase of p62 levels in CTR organoids (Fig. 3A and B; p < 0.01 and p < 0.05). This observation was confirmed by immunofluorescence (Fig. 3C and D; p < 0.01). Bafilomycin treatment also induced in CTR organoids a decrease of LC3-II/LC3-I ratio (Supplementary Fig. 1A and B). The blockade of autophagy induced by bafilomycin treatment also increased pE4-BP1 levels (Fig. 3A and E; p < 0.05 for both concentrations of bafilomycin). Under these same conditions, levels of pNF-κB also significantly increased as shown in Fig. 3 (F and G; p < 0.05 at both concentrations). These findings indicate that, in CTR organoids the mTOR pathway and inflammation were altered due to the autophagy block.

Rapamycin treatment resulted in a modest and variable modulation of p62, pE4-BP1 and pNF-κB levels in control organoids, without reaching statistical significance (Fig. 3).

Rapamycin and bafilomycin treatment in GCD- and GFD-CeD intestinal organoids

Organoids from GCD-CeD and from GFD-CeD were treated with different concentrations of bafilomycin (0.5 µM and 1 µM) and rapamycin (25 nM, 50 nM, or 100 nM) (Figs. 4 and 5, respectively). Rapamycin significantly reduced p62 levels in both GCD (Fig. 4A and B) and GFD-CeD (Fig. 5A and B) organoids (p < 0.05 at all concentrations used in GCD and GFD patients’ organoids) according to WB analysis. This increase was confirmed by immunofluorescence analysis of p62 staining of GCD- (Fig. 4C and D; p < 0.01) and GFD-CeD (Fig. 5C and D; p < 0.01) treated with 25 nM of rapamycin. Also the LC3-II/LC3-I ratio was increased after rapamycin treatment in both GCD- and GFD-CeD organoids respect to CTRs (Supplementary Fig. 1C–F).

After treatment with rapamycin, a statistically significant reduction in pE4-BP1 was detected in organoids from both the GCD- (Fig. 4A and E; p < 0.05 at all concentrations of rapamycin used) and GFD-CeD (Fig. 5A and E; p < 0.05 for 50 nM of rapamycin).

Moreover, rapamycin significantly reduced pNF-κB levels both in GCD- (Fig. 4F and G; p < 0.05 for 25 nM and p < 0.01 for 100 nM rapamycin) and GFD-CeD (Fig. 5F and G; p < 0.05 for all concentrations) intestinal organoids, respect to CTRs.

Bafilomycin was able to increase p62 in a statistically significant way in GCD-CeD organoids (Fig. 4A and B; p < 0.01 for both concentrations) but had no effect on LC3-II/LC3-I ratio, pE4-BP1 or pNF-κB levels under our experimental conditions (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Fig. 4). Similarly, bafilomycin increased LC3-II/LC3-I ratio (Supplementary Fig. 1E and F; p < 0.05), p62 (Fig. 5A and B), pE4-BP1 (Fig. 5A and E) and pNF-κB (Fig. 5F and G) levels in GFD-CeD organoids.

These data indicate that rapamycin inducing the autophagic flux, blocked the mTOR pathway and reduced inflammation in GCD- and GFD-CeD organoids.

Both GCD- and GFD-CeD-treated intestinal organoids produced inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors

Bio-Plex analysis of 27 proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors was performed on the culture medium (CMs) from CeD organoids derived from patients with GCD- and GFD-CeD and from CTRs (Fig. 6).

This analysis revealed that with respect to CMs from CTR organoids MCP-1 and VEGF were increased in a statistically significant way (p < 0.05) according to all the three statistical tests performed (Mann-Withney U test, T-Student, Welch) (Fig. 6A, p < 0.05).

Moreover, in the CMs from GCD-CeD respect to CTR organoids, 15 proinflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors out of 27 analysed were increased in a statistically significant way (p < 0.05) according to the three statistical tests performed (Fig. 6B). Namely the cytokines: IL-1β, IL-4, IL-7, IL-8, IL-9, IL12(p70), IL-13, IL-15, IFN-γ the chemokines: MIP-1β, IP-10, RANTES; the growth factors: PDGF-BB, GM-CSF, and G-CSF.

Tumor Necrosis Factor alpha, IL-2 and IL-5 were increased in a statistically significant way (p < 0.05) according to all the three statistical tests performed in CMs from GFD-CeD organoids, but not in GCD-CeD.

Interleukin-1Ra, IL-6, IL-10, IL-17, Eotaxin, MIP-1α and FGF- basic did not show any differences in the CMs from CeD organoids respect to the CMs from CTR organoids (Supplementary Fig. 2).

All cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors that were altered in the CMs from CeD organoids have been shown to play a role at the level of the intestinal epithelium, in the autophagy and inflammation pathways (Table 3).

Protein -protein interaction network of cytokines, chemokines and growth factors

We performed a protein-protein interaction network analysis of cytokines with altered expression in CMs from GCD-CeD with respect to CTRs (Fig. 7A). All cytokines but IL-12, PDGF-BB and VEGF appear to be strongly (combined score > 0.7) associated with each other (Fig. 7Ai), with different kinds of evidence. The functional enrichment analysis showed the positive regulation of biological processes involving tyrosine phosphorylation, including JAK/STAT pathway (Fig. 7Aii), and the tissue expression enrichment analysis showed a clear enrichment in inflammatory cells, supporting the presence of inflammation (Fig. 7Aiii).

An analogous analysis performed on the cytokines with altered expression in CMs from GFD-CeD with respect to CTRs still showed a network in which VEGF is not strongly associated with the other cytokines (Fig. 7Bi). The functional enrichment analysis showed also in this case a positive regulation of peptidyl-Tyr phosphorylation as biological process enrichment (Fig. 7Bii), of course with a lower signal. Moreover, the biological process enrichment lacks completely elements associated with leukocyte chemotaxis, which were present (despite a very low FDR and signal) in the GCD-CeD network. Finally, the tissue enrichment analysis still showed the presence of inflammatory cells among other non-specific tissues (Fig. 7Biii).

The same analysis performed on the cytokines with statistically significant increased expression in CMs from GCD-CeD with respect to GFD-CeD (Fig. 7C) showed that, among the cytokines in this network, PDGF-BB and IL-12 present a lower association (combined score < 0.7) than the other cytokines (Fig. 7Ci). The functional enrichment analysis showed essentially chemotaxis as biological process (Fig. 7Cii), and inflammatory cells as tissue enrichment analysis (Fig. 7Ciii).

CM from GCD-CeD organoids was able to reduce autophagic flux, activate the mTOR pathway and induce inflammation in CTR organoids

CMs conditioned by GCD-CeD organoids and previously analysed by the Bio-Plex assay were used to treat CTR organoids to test their ability to block autophagic flux, activate the mTOR pathway and induce inflammation in CTRs (Fig. 8). The CMs from 4 GCD-CeD organoids were used to treat organoids from 3 CTRs and then analysed by WB to test the levels of p62 (Fig. 8A-D), pE4-BP1 (Fig. 8A–C and E) and pNF-κB (Fig. 8A-C and F). Figure 7 shows in CTR organoids after treatment with CMs from GCD-CeD a statistically significant increase in protein levels of p62 (Fig. 8D; p < 0.01), pE4-BP1 (Fig. 8E; p < 0.05) or pNF-κB (Fig. 8F; p < 0.05). One of the controls (CTR 3) was treated with CMs from two different GCD-CeD organoids, and both CMs (from organoids of CM 3 and 4) were able to increase the expression of all markers tested (Fig. 8C-F). The LC3-II/LC3-I ratio was decreased in a CTR organoid treated with CMs from two different patients (pz-CM3 and pz-CM4) (Supplementary Fig. 1G and H). Interestingly, CTR organoids treated with CMs produced by different GCD-CeD organoids increased in a statistically significant way IL-1β in their CMs, tested by ELISA assay after overnight treatment (Fig. 8G, p < 0.05). These findings confirm the inflammatory state of the CTR organoids after treatment with CM from the GCD-CeD organoids.

Discussion

In this study, we showed that in both intestinal biopsies and organoids from patients with GCD-CeD, there was a delay in the autophagic flux, an activation of the mTOR pathway and an increase in inflammatory markers. Interestingly, such alterations were also present in the GFD-CeD organoids. To understand the relationship between the delay of autophagic flux and the activation of the mTOR pathway with inflammation, we induced in CTR organoids a delay in autophagic flux by bafilomycin treatment and observed increases in both mTOR pathway activation and inflammation. The opposite result was obtained when we induced autophagic flux by rapamycin treatment in both the GCD- and GFD-CeD organoids. The intestinal organoids from CeD patients were not only inflamed but also produced inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors, all of which were related to inflammation and autophagy (Table 3). Moreover, the CMs conditioned by the growth of GCD-CeD intestinal organoids were able to induce inflammation together with a delay in autophagic flux and activation of the mTOR pathway in CTR organoids. These findings indicate that in CeD organoids, there is crosstalk between autophagy/mTOR pathway activation and inflammation.

The mTOR pathway has already been shown to be altered in intestinal biopsies from GCD-CeD patients9. Sedda et al. demonstrated the activation of the mTOR pathway in intestinal biopsies from patients with GCD-CeD using pE4-BP1 as a marker of mTOR9 activation. Autophagy has also been described in CeD fibroblasts, where it has been shown that in skin fibroblasts from GFD-CeD patients, low levels of Ca2 + were present in the cytosol and in the ER lumen. Consequently, TG2 was less activated and there was defective autophagy15. Moreover, gliadin peptides can induce activation of the mTOR pathway in GFD-CeD intestinal biopsies in culture9 and can also activate the mTOR pathway and reduce the autophagic flux in intestinal cells in culture11,12.

The mTOR pathway is activated in the presence of nutrients, growth factors and stress91,92. In the absence of nutrients and/or in the presence of rapamycin, the mTOR pathway is switched off and autophagy is activated; the two biological processes are essentially alternatives93. We confirmed this observation in intestinal organoids from CeD patients, in fact when autophagy was delayed the mTOR pathway was activated. The causes of both the activation of the mTOR pathway and autophagy in CeD biopsies and fibroblasts are not clear. Genetic and expression studies have shown a reduction in Unc-51-like autophagy-activating kinase 3 (ULK3) in CeD intestinal biopsies13,94,95. ULK3 is an important protein in the regulation of autophagy in mammalian cells; homologous to ATG1 in yeast it is needed in the early steps of autophagosome biogenesis96. This protein is also involved in the pathogenesis of Crohn’s disease96,97. Moreover, recent studies highlight the role of autophagy dysregulation in celiac disease (CeD), also involving miR-30a, a negative regulator of Beclin-1–mediated autophagy, which is differentially expressed in both blood and intestinal epithelium of paediatric CeD patients14,16. Consistently, miR-30a-3p, a member of the miR-30 family, has been reported to be downregulated in duodenal biopsies from CeD patients and to be associated with immune and metabolic pathways, including targets such as STAT1 and TRAFD1 within CeD genetic risk loci14,16. MicroR-30a expression levels in CTR, GCD-CeD and GFD-CeD organoids were significantly reduced in GCD-CeD and showed a decreasing trend in GFD-CeD compared with controls.

Collectively, these observations support the hypothesis that the reduction in the expression of autophagy-regulating genes, such as ULK3, in the CeD intestine could lead to reduced autophagic flux and consequently to the activation of the mTOR pathway and inflammation. However, our experiments conducted on CTR organoids revealed that inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors produced by GCD-CeD could delay autophagic flux and induce activation of the mTOR pathway in CTR organoids. This indicating that inflammation can play a role in these processes. Interestingly, blood cytokine balance has been found altered in GCD-CeD98. Moreover inflammatory proteins can be present in the serum of CeD patients before the onset of the disease and even before the introduction of gluten in infants99. Inflammation is also present in intestinal biopsies from CeD patients not only at GCD but also at GFD with normal intestinal mucosa and without autoantibodies in the serum8. This can also occur in potential CeD patients with GCD and with predisposing genetics and autoantibodies in the serum but with normal intestinal mucosa8. These findings indicate that inflammation can be present very early in the natural history of this disease.

A reduction in autophagic flux and activation of the mTOR pathway have also been linked to inflammation in other systems100. In fact, autophagy plays critical roles in inflammation and sustains development, homeostasis, and the survival of inflammatory cells with the consequent production of several cytokines that can modulate autophagy flux100. Dysregulated autophagy may contribute to enhanced inflammatory signalling in celiac disease through increased IL-1β production101.

In this study, we showed that crosstalk between autophagy/activation of the mTOR pathway and inflammation was present in the intestinal epithelium of CeD patients not only in the acute phase of the disease but also in patients at GFD, with a normal mucosa. In fact, they were not only inflamed but also produced inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors, which are related to inflammation and the activation of NF-κB (Table 3). Interestingly, these cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors play a role in the regulation of autophagy (Table 3) and confirm that these two phenomena are closely related. MCP-1 and VEGF, the 2 cytokines and growth factors that were increased in both GCD- and GFD-CeD, as well as the other 3 cytokines (IL-2, IL-5 and TNF-α) increased in GFD-CeD but not in GCD-CeD, are all involved in the regulation of the autophagy and are related to the NF-κB pathway. Most of the 17 chemokines and growth factors that were increased in CMs from GCD-CeD, are in their turn related with NF-κB pathway and several of them with autophagy (Table 3). Looking at their protein-protein interaction network, it is evident that they are strictly interconnected, with an emerging role of this network in the JAK/STAT signalling pathway. The dysregulation of JAK/STAT pathway is associated with several malignancies, from hematologic malignancies to hepatocellular carcinoma, and several studies have shown that altered STAT3 expression is accompanied by abnormal autophagy activity25. Furthermore the JAK/STAT pathway plays a role also in inflammatory and immune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis and atopic dermatitis102. Among the enriched biological processes it is possible to identify also signals associated with leukocyte chemotaxis, which in its turn is often associated to the presence of inflammation. The presence of inflammation is also evident by looking the tissue expression enrichment panel, where inflammatory cells are predominantly associated with these chemokines. Overall, this analysis fits well into a picture in which inflammation predominates. It is interesting to note that both the specific JAK/STAT pathway and the group of chemotaxis-related biological processes are no longer enriched in the network of cytokines associated with GFD-CeD, confirming that in patients on a gluten-free diet the inflammatory picture is less pronounced. On the other side, 5 cytokines were still increased in GFD-CeD CM organoids, strongly suggesting that residual inflammation is present even in absence of gluten from the diet.

In conclusion, autophagy flux delay, activation of the mTOR pathway and inflammation are strictly connected in an interplay that ultimately may render the epithelial cells of CeD patients more prone to proinflammatory stimuli from the environment, including gliadin peptides, which can insist on the same pathways already altered in the intestinal epithelium of CeD patients. These findings, ones confirmed in a greater cohort of intestinal organoids and/or biopsies from CeD patients at different stage of the disease, could lead to new hypotheses for interventions in CeD prevention, by modulating, for example, the autophagy flux in these patients before the onset of the intestinal lesion. This approach would be very interesting in Potential CeD patients, a special population, HLA DQ2/8 positive, in which the autoantibodies are present but intestinal lesion is absent. One of the limitations of this study was the number of organoids used, but considering that they come from children, it is not very easy to obtain a sufficient number of biopsy samples to perform the culture. Other limitation of this study is the lack of HLA genotyping data for the analysed samples, which prevented us from assessing the potential influence of patient-specific genetic background on the observed epithelial responses103. The possible contamination in patients with GFD could represent a limit in the selection of this category of patients. However, our cytokine profiling revealed that a subset of inflammatory mediators was specifically altered in GFD-CeD organoids, showing a pattern that differed from that observed in patients on a gluten-containing diet. This finding suggests that, even in the absence of overt gluten exposure, GFD-CeD epithelium may retain a distinct inflammatory signature, potentially reflecting long-lasting epithelial reprogramming rather than acute gluten-driven inflammation.
