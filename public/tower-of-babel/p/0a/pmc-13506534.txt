Development and preliminary validation of the Exercise Fit Tool for adolescents undergoing cancer treatment in Hong Kong
Authors: Katherine Ka Wai Lam, Stanley Wu, Qi Liu, Ting Mao, Jingda Pan, Lulu Jiang, Yanjia Li, Yuwen Gao, Frankie Wai Tsoi Cheng, Godfrey Chi Fung Chan, Ka Yan Ho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to develop and preliminarily validate the Exercise Fit Tool (EFT), a patient-reported self-assessment tool for adolescents undergoing cancer treatment in Hong Kong.MethodsA convenience sample of 158 adolescents aged 13–18 years who were undergoing cancer treatment and could communicate in Cantonese and read Chinese was recruited in Hong Kong. Instrument development followed the procedures recommended in AMEE Guide No. 87. Candidate items were generated from a literature review and focus groups with adolescents and healthcare professionals, refined by an expert panel, assessed for content validity, and revised through cognitive interviews. Pilot testing was conducted in 158 adolescents. Convergent validity was examined using pain scores and forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1, % predicted), and criterion-related validity was evaluated by comparing EFT scores with physiotherapists’ ratings. Test–retest reliability was assessed in 50 participants, and principal component analysis with Varimax rotation was performed.ResultsThe final 10-item EFT demonstrated satisfactory content validity, with I-CVIs ranging from 0.86 to 1.00, and good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.92). The 2-week test–retest ICC was 0.82 (P < 0.001). EFT scores were negatively correlated with pain scores and positively correlated with FEV1 (% predicted), supporting convergent validity. The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin measure was 0.87, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (P < 0.001). Principal component analysis identified a two-component solution: cancer- and treatment-related influences on physical activity (7 items; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.96; 55.7% of the rotated variance) and perceived physical activity exertion (3 items; Cronbach’s alpha = 0.74; 20.6% of the rotated variance). Primary component loadings ranged from 0.74 to 0.93, and communalities ranged from 0.55 to 0.91. The two components explained 76.3% of the total variance.ConclusionsThe EFT demonstrated preliminary evidence of validity and reliability for adolescents undergoing cancer treatment in Hong Kong. Further evaluation of its latent structure and implementation in independent samples is required before wider clinical use.

Introduction

Despite a dramatic decrease in cancer mortality because of recent medical advancements, the impact of cancer and its treatment on the well-being of adolescents with the disease remains a significant issue.1, 2, 3 In particular, the accumulated adverse effects of cancer and its treatment substantially impair adolescents’ quality of life.1,2,4 Importantly, these effects may last for months or even years after the completion of therapy courses.1,4,5

It is well documented that physical activity (PA) ameliorates the adverse effects of cancer and its treatment.2,6, 7, 8 Specifically, increasing scientific evidence shows that engaging in regular PA reduces some of these adverse effects, consequently improving their quality of life.9,10 Nevertheless, there is growing concern about declining levels of PA in cancer patients and survivors.1,2,6,11,12 Evidence suggests that early PA interventions are beneficial to avoid inactivity complications by not bringing the risky behaviour, i.e. physical inactivity, to the adulthood.8,13 Early PA intervention is thereby warranted in the cancer population. In the early life stage, children and adolescents require different PA interventions since children are at a young stage in which they heavily rely on parents for decision and care. Some PA interventions involving parents may largely benefit children in PA adoption. Whereas, adolescents are establishing independence from parents and starting to make decisions by themselves which are regarded as the age-specific developmental tasks in adolescence.14 Age-specific PA intervention for adolescents with cancer cannot be overlooked.

Similar to other ages, the PA levels in adolescents with cancer were low when compared to their healthy counterparts.1,2,15 A cross-sectional study examining the impacts of cancer and its treatment on PA levels in 76 Hong Kong Chinese adolescents undergoing cancer treatment revealed that their PA levels were significantly reduced when compared with their pre-cancer situation.2 Their PA levels, self-efficacy, and quality of life were also much lower than those of their healthy counterparts. Apart from those who are undergoing cancer treatment, the PA levels of cancer survivors are also dramatically reduced. Another cross-sectional study investigating the PA levels and factors affecting regular PA among 128 young Hong Kong Chinese cancer survivors showed that their PA levels were significantly decreased when compared with their pre-cancer situation.1

A review of the literature reveals increasing evidence of interventions that are effective in promoting PA among adolescents with cancer.6,13 Nevertheless, there is a common challenge that the effect of these interventions in promoting PA cannot be maintained. Studies have consistently found that adolescents with cancer become physically inactive after the end of interventions when the funding is exhausted.16,17 The unsustainability of the intervention effects is explained by the difficulty in assessing adolescents' physical fitness in the busy clinical setting. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention,18 physical fitness refers to an integrated measure of most body functions (such as skeletomuscular, cardiorespiratory, hematocirculatory, psychoneurological and endocrine–metabolic) involved when performing PA, reflecting the ability of a person to perform PA without undue fatigue and with ample energy.19,20 Physical fitness can be measured by various methods depending on the interests and needs of the individuals but is required to be performed by physicians or physiotherapists with specific devices. Examples of methods are the maximum oxygen uptake on treadmill or ergometer, handgrip dynamometer, and sit-and-reach test, with formula calculations and predicted values according to individuals' data such as age and gender.21 Individuals’ physical fitness varies from low to high, indicating that individuals are fit to perform PA from lower to higher intensity respectively.21 For adolescents with cancer particularly those undergoing cancer treatment, even though they were motivated during the interventional programs, they were unable to continue the prescribed exercise after the programs due to their varying medical conditions during and after cancer and between each follow-up, leading to changes in physical fitness that were suitable for different intensities of PA over time. Even though they could consult the health care professionals between each follow-up, the busy clinical settings meant that the health care professionals could only focus on managing cancer and its treatment-related symptoms and not the problems of physical inactivity. The PA prescriptions were usually brief and without concrete information on the appropriate intensity, types, and levels of activities that the adolescents needed. This results in low adoption of the interventions in both clinical and community settings. Therefore, to promote PA sustainably in adolescents undergoing cancer treatment who are motivated to PA but experience changing levels of physical fitness over time, other strategies to facilitate the assessment of physical fitness are required.

Apart from the tests that required specific therapists and devices, evidence shows that several self-reported tools for assessing physical fitness in adolescents are available. A literature review found 6 related tools: the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q),22 the International Fitness Scale (IFIS),23 the Self-Perception Profile for Children (SPPC) - Athletic Competence,24 Children's Self-Perceptions of Adequacy in and Predilection for Physical Activity scale (CSAPPA),25 Physical Self-Description Questionnaire (PSDQ),26 and Children and Youth PSPP (CY-PSPP).27 Nevertheless, all were not specifically designed for adolescents with cancer, but for the general population. Adolescents undergoing cancer treatment presented lower physical fitness when compared to the general population due to various complications from cancer and treatment which could last over decades.1,4,5 Importantly, after assessing physical fitness, these tools did not provide actual guidance and suggestions on the appropriate intensity, types, and levels of PA that the adolescents needed to participate. Therefore, to facilitate the assessment of physical fitness and adoption of PA among adolescents undergoing cancer treatment in the busy clinical setting, it is vital to develop a patient-based, self-assessment tool which focuses on assessing the physical fitness to enable adolescents with cancer to self-assess the appropriate levels of PA that they can perform. However, a review of the literature indicates a lack of such a tool for adolescents who were undergoing cancer treatment .

Methods

Aim

This study aimed to develop and validate a patient-based, self-assessment tool (Exercise Fit Tool, EFT) for adolescents undergoing cancer treatment in Hong Kong to self-assess their own appropriate intensity and levels of PA.

Study design

This study was a methodological research study conducted using Artino's seven-step approach. This approach was recommended by the Association for Medical Education in Europe's (AMEE) Guide No. 87 which provided a systematic process for designing instruments with high quality.28 The seven steps include: 1) literature review, 2) focus groups, 3) synthesis of the literature review and focus groups, 4) developing items, 5) expert validation, 6) cognitive interviews, and 7) pilot testing.

Setting and study population

Participants were recruited from the Hong Kong Children's Hospital, which is the largest hospital in Hong Kong for admitting adolescents undergoing cancer treatment for hospitalization and follow-ups. The recruitment period was from September 2023 to August 2025.

A total of 158 adolescents were included in the final analysis. Adolescents were recruited by convenience sampling if they (1) were aged 13–18, (2) were diagnosed with cancer and undergoing treatment, and (3) were able to communicate in Cantonese and read Chinese. Those with physical or cognitive impairments and learning problems, as identified from the medical records and verifications with related staff by the research assistants, were excluded. Physical or cognitive impairments and learning problems in this study were defined as problems affecting participants’ physical mobility, thinking, or learning, which were severe enough to make participants unable to participate in the study or lead participants to encounter additional barriers in PA. Examples of the problems were amputations, Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder and Autism Spectrum Disorder. Also, those recommended or with detailed exercise prescriptions from physicians or physiotherapists during recruitment were excluded.

To estimate the sample size required, based on the recommendation from Hutcheson and Sofroniou,29 a minimum sample size of 150 was recommended. Also, the sample size of 150 fit into the other recommendation of 5–20 participants per item.30 Finally, 158 participants were recruited, resulting in the participant-to-item ratio of 15.8:1.

Tool development

Step 1. Literature review

To begin with, summarizing current evidence to understand the available tools and important criteria required to assess the physical fitness of adolescents with cancer was of paramount importance. Hence, a narrative review was conducted from March to June 2022 to search on 7 databases, including PubMed, EMBASE, CINAHL, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, Scopus, and PsycINFO. In the searching, the following keywords were used alone or in combinations: “exercise∗”, “physical activit∗”, “cancer∗”, “oncolog∗”, “neoplasm∗”, “surviv∗”, “fitness”, “level∗”, “criteri∗”, measurement”, “instrument”, “tool”, “questionnaire”, “survey”, “adolescent∗”, “child∗”, “young∗”, and “teen∗”. The searching period was from inception to 28 February 2022. The search strategy using one database as an example is shown in Table 1. Studies were included in the literature review if they: 1) involved patients aged under 18, diagnosed with any type of cancer, at any stage of the cancer survivorship (e.g. during or completed cancer treatment), 2) included analysis on studying factors that affected the physical fitness for PA, and 3) were in English or Chinese. Search Strategy (Taking PubMed as an example).

After searching, a list of potentially relevant articles was found. The first researcher (KKWL) adopted content analysis by first reading the full text to obtain its general sense and to become familiar with the articles included in the review. From the included articles, KKWL then identified the meaning units relevant to our research aim (a list of physical factors or criteria which were mentioned in the included studies as having associations with physical fitness or PA levels.) and labeled them with relevant codes. Psychological factors were excluded since they were more related to PA motivation which was not the focus of this study.31 After obtaining the list of factors or criteria (extracted codes), the second researcher (KYH) then checked the extracted codes. For any disagreement or ambiguity, discussions between KKWL and KYH were conducted and clarified any issues until agreement was reached via consensus.

Step 2. Focus groups

Apart from the important criteria identified in step 1, this step conducted focus group sessions to further identify any other key items to be included in EFT for assessing fitness for PA until data saturation. In this step, we conducted group interviews with health care professionals and adolescents with cancer. All informants were recruited through convenience sampling. For adolescents with cancer, the inclusion and exclusion criteria under Section 2.3 were applied. For health care professionals, the inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) experts with more than two years of experience working with adolescents undergoing cancer treatment and 2) experts who were able to communicate in Cantonese and read Chinese. The focus group session followed AMEE guidelines.32

At the start of the session, the moderator introduced herself and reassured the informants to freely express their viewpoints, feelings, and beliefs about physical fitness and PA levels in adolescents with cancer. Then the moderator started to ask directive and open-ended questions such as “What are the factors affecting the physical fitness/PA level in adolescents with cancer?” “How do you determine the physical fitness in adolescents with cancer?’” “How do you feel during cancer?” “How do you feel if you are asked to be physically active during cancer?” Probing was also used to ask for explanations and elicit more comprehensive information from the session. Examples of probing questions included “Why do you think in that way?” and “Can you provide an example?” The sessions were all audio-recorded and then transcribed verbatim. Two researchers (KKWL and KYH) analyzed the data using content analysis. Any disagreement or ambiguity was resolved by discussions between KKWL and KYH until agreement was reached via consensus.

Step 3. Synthesis of the literature review and focus groups

In step 3, the codes in step 1 and 2 were integrated. In other words, the codes extracted from the literature review were integrated with the codes in the focus groups. During the integration, KKWL and KYH repeatedly compared the combined codes with the initial codes, duplicate codes were merged, while relevant overlapping and unique codes from the literature review and focus groups were retained and a list of important codes was generated for the next step. Any disagreement or ambiguity was discussed between KKWL and KYH until reaching consensus.

Step 4: Developing items

This step involved 6 experts who were experienced in the field of pediatric oncology (including 1 associate professor, 1 assistant professor, 2 registered nurses, 1 oncologist, and 1 physiotherapist with at least 2 years of experience working in pediatric oncology). They formed the expert panel with an important mission i.e. to guide the development of the items in the EFT. The list of important codes in step 3 was presented to the expert panel. The panel then provided experts opinions according to their relevance and importance in assessing physical fitness in adolescents with cancer, as well as the appropriate types and intensities of PA for adolescents with cancer. According to the comments from the panel, the most appropriate statements for the EFT items were developed. Any disagreement or ambiguity was discussed in the panel for consensus.

Step 5. Expert validation

In this step, we invited 7 experts in the field of pediatric oncology to provide comments regarding the content validity. Experts were first explained in emails about the aims of the study and the procedure for gathering opinions on the content validity assessment process. The validity assessment form was also sent to experts in emails to guide them to review and evaluate the drafted items for the EFT and provide comments on content validity accordingly. After obtaining the assessment forms and comments from the experts, the content validity index (CVI) and content validity ratio (CVR) were calculated. For the CVI, experts rated the relevance and clarity of each item on a four-point Likert scale with 1 point representing not relevant and 4 indicating very relevant. To calculate the CVI index, the formula developed by Waltz and Bausell was used.33 If the value was above 0.83,34,35 the CVI index was confirmed. The Lawshe formula was used for the CVR index calculation. On a three-point Likert scale (necessary, useful but not essential, not necessary),37 experts rated each item of the EFT accordingly. A CVR value of over 0.75 indicated that the item could be retained.37

Step 6. Cognitive interviews

For step 6, four adolescents undergoing cancer treatment participated in the cognitive interviews. These interviews helped researchers to explore face validity of the drafted items in EFT. During the interviews, adolescents were instructed to read each item in the EFT aloud and asked to describe and explain each item's meaning in their language. This could verify the adolescents' understanding and ensure adolescents having sufficient understanding about the items. For those items identified by adolescents as similar to other domains were eliminated. Furthermore, adolescents helped to inform our team any confusing and inappropriate wordings that they found. These wordings were revised accordingly to enhance the face validity of EFT.

Step 7. Pilot testing

Pilot testing helped to explore the convergent and criterion-related validity and reliability of EFT. For the convergent validity, 158 participants (adolescents undergoing cancer treatment) were invited to complete the EFT and a numerical rating scale (NRS) for pain scores, in which NRS is an 11-point scale ranging from 0 to 10 where the end points indicate extremes of no pain (0) or worst pain (10). Participants marked their current pain level on the NRS according to their experience. It is simple, valid, reliable and appropriate for use in clinical practice with good sensitivity.38

Also, participants' measurements of forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) were recorded using a spirometer for the convergent validity. FEV1 was assessed using a portable spirometer (Spirobank II with Bluetooth Smart®, MIR, Rome, Italy) according to American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society guidelines for lung function testing and reporting.39 It is an FDA-approved and a widely used instrument for lung function testing. Its performance in screening patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease was satisfactory.40 Before the assessment, a 3-L syringe was used to verify spirometry volume accuracy (± 3%) and flow rates.41 Research assistants who were well-trained by our team with standardized spirometry procedures conducted all tests. Participants were tested in the sitting position and were given standardized verbal instructions and encouragement during each maneuver by our trained research assistant, which included the following steps based on the MIR Medical Research MIR059 Spirobank with Bluetooth SMART User Manual: (1) Place the mouth firmly around the mouthpiece; (2) Press the “FVC” button; (3) Inhale deeply to full lung capacity; (4) Exhale with maximum speed and force; when the first “beep” sound occurs, continue blowing; (5) When the double “beep–beep” sound occurs, keep exhaling until the long “--” tone appears; (6) Keep the mouth on the mouthpiece and inhale deeply again; (7) When the success symbol appears on the device screen, remove lips from the mouthpiece to complete the procedure.42 The spirometer then generated the FEV1 readings which were expressed as a percent of the predicted value. The predicted value was automatically produced by the spirometer according to the participants’ age, gender, height, and race.

For the criterion-related validity, the physiotherapist, who was blinded to each participant's measurements, interviewed the participant as usual and rated the participant with the most appropriate level of PA on a 5-point scale ranging from “1: not fit for PA,” “2: fit for stretching exercises (less than 3 METs, stretching on bed for at least 25 minutes daily with safety rails or with another person to monitor safety),” “3: fit for light-intensity physical activity (other stretching and low-intensity PA not confined to bed, with 3 METs for at least 25 minutes daily),” “4: fit for moderate-intensity PA (5 METs for at least 25 minutes daily)” to “5: fit for vigorous-intensity PA (6 METs or above for at least 25 minutes daily).” Different from a score of 2, a score of 1 “not fit for PA” indicates that participants should not conduct any PA temporarily, particularly those with advice from physicians or physiotherapists. Examples could be bed rest were recommended by physicians after surgeries. This 5-point scoring system was the same as the EFT. Accuracy was defined as the exact agreement between the EFT score and the physiotherapist-assigned score, and underestimation was defined as an EFT score lower than the physiotherapist-assigned score.

The internal consistency of EFT was calculated by Cronbach's alpha. A Cronbach's alpha of more than 0.7 was considered acceptable.43 For test– retest reliability, 50 participants were invited to respond to the EFT 2 weeks later. The intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated and a value of ≥ 0.75 represented an appropriate instrument for research.44

Convergent validity was assessed via investigating the Spearman's correlation coefficients between EFT scores and pain scores, and between EFT scores and FEV1, on the data from 158 participants. Literature review revealed a negative correlation between PA fitness and pain,45 whereas a positive correlation between PA fitness and FEV1.46

Principal component analysis (PCA) with Varimax rotation was performed on the data from 158 participants using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30.47 This initial stage of validation was intended to explore the dimensional structure of the newly developed EFT, which included examining empirical item clustering, reducing the item pool, and establishing a preliminary, interpretable component structure. Therefore, PCA was considered appropriate for this purpose because it captures the total variance of the observed items and facilitates the identification of coherent item groupings during early instrument development.48 The KMO index was calculated. A KMO index > 0.7 indicated the sample adequacy for factor analysis. Besides, Bartlett's test was conducted and a significant result (P < 0.05) indicates the suitability of the factor analysis. According to Kaiser's criterion, eigenvalues ≥ 1.0 were retained.49 Items with a factor loading of more than 0.40 were considered suitable. Scree plot was presented and retained factors that were above the elbow or the break in the plot, as they accounted for a larger portion of the total variance.50

Results

Step 1. Literature review

A total of 5057 records were retrieved from 7 databases, of which 753 studies were included in the narrative review. 3775 duplicated records were removed. 1282 records were screened by titles or abstracts. 398 records were excluded after screening titles or abstracts. An additional of 23 records were excluded as we were unable to retrieve full texts, remaining 861 records for full-text screening. A further 108 records were excluded since we were unable to extract data relating to physical fitness. Finally, 753 studies were included. Adolescents added that fatigue (AR = 80%) and fever (AR = 80%) could be their concern in conducting PA. Health care professionals further emphasized that the rate of perceived exertion (RPE) during PA (AR = 100%) was also a common indication of increasing or decreasing the PA level. Health care professionals added that PA levels in the last 7 days (AR = 83.3%) and for 25 minutes or more (AR = 66.7%) usually represented the current PA level, but adolescents with recent seizures (AR = 100%), fever (AR = 66.7%), and invasive brain surgeries such as craniotomy or craniectomy or burr holes should avoid conducting PA higher than 3 metabolic equivalents (METs). Health care professionals also mentioned 2 common tools for assessing physical fitness in general adolescents although they were not specifically developed for adolescents with cancer: PAR-Q and the Physical Activity Readiness Medical Evaluation (PARmed-X).64,65

Step 3. Synthesis of the literature review and focus groups

In Step 3, the codes from steps 1 and 2 were integrated, and the results are shown in Table 3. Ten criteria were consistently found in steps 1 and 2, including, CF, MF, pain, S/G, shortness of breath, use of medical devices, invasive brain surgery, drowsiness/ low level of consciousness, range of motion, and fatigue. Five criteria were added from the focus groups with health care professionals, including RPE, PA levels in the last 7 days, PA levels for 25 minutes or more, recent seizures and fever. As a result, these 15 identified criteria were used to develop items in step 4.

Step 4: Developing items

The 6 experts discussed in meetings and developed 15 items based on the 15 criteria identified in step 3, including CF, MF, pain, S/G, shortness of breath, use of medical devices, invasive brain surgery, drowsiness/low level of consciousness, range of motion, fatigue, RPE, PA levels in the last 7 days, PA levels for 25 minutes or more, recent seizures and fever, resulting in a 15-item EFT draft. All experts agreed the 15 items with 100% AR.

It is noted that some adolescent-specific items such as autonomy in PA, peer or parental influence, motivation, self-image, and treatment-related limitations unique to adolescents, were found not likely to affect the physical fitness but more related to the motivation to conduct PA,66,67 which is not the focus of this study (i.e. to develop a tool for assessing physical fitness in motivated adolescents who are undergoing cancer treatment in Hong Kong). Hence these items were not included in EFT. Besides, evidence shows that most complications of cancer and its treatment affecting the physical fitness are similar to that in adults, with complications such as seizures,36,68 fever,69,70 and pain71,72 that affect their physical fitness. For adolescent-specific items, our expert panel has particularly developed tool questions 9 and 10 which are focused on asking participants’ highest level of PA in the past 7 days and their RPE. According to World Health Organization (WHO), adolescents should do at least an average of 60 minutes per day of moderate to vigorous-intensity, mostly aerobic, PA, across the week.73 Whereas, the recommended PA duration for adolescents is higher than that for adults who require at least 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic PA throughout the week, for substantial health benefits.73 Given the above WHO guidelines, our expert panel has set questions 9 and 10 with the minimum PA duration to “at least 25 minutes in a day” which was about half the required of that from the WHO recommendations as a pre-requisite for participants to be fit for higher levels of PA.73 This minimum duration was set by the discussion of our expert panel including pediatric oncologists and physiotherapist.

Step 6. Cognitive interviews

The cognitive interviews reported that 3 items were considered difficult to be understood by adolescents who were undergoing cancer treatment and hence were revised. First, the item relating to use of medical devices were revised from “Are you having any medical devices affecting your mobility” to “Do you have any medical devices that affect your mobility, such as urinary catheters, drains, or medication infusion pumps?” to add examples of medical devices in the item since adolescents reflected that they did not understand the phrase of “medical devices affecting your mobility”. Second, another item relating to invasive brain surgeries were revised from “Have you undergone invasive brain surgeries?” to “Has a portion of your skull (bone flap) been removed and not yet replaced?” to clarify the definition of invasive brain surgeries because adolescents mentioned that it was difficult to define invasive brain surgeries. Third, the final item being amended was the item relating to drowsiness/ low level of consciousness. We revised the item from “Are you feeling drowsy or with a low level of consciousness?” to “Do you feel faint, dizzy, or drowsy?” to clarify the condition of drowsiness/ low level of consciousness as adolescents requested for more explanations.

Discussion

Main findings

This is the first study to address the literature gap by developing and validating a new patient-based, self-assessment tool, EFT, for adolescents undergoing cancer treatment in Hong Kong to self-assess their own appropriate intensity and levels of PA that they can perform. This study provided preliminary evidence of EFT's psychometric properties with adequate internal reliability, and satisfactory factor loading to support its validity. All retained items met the prespecified I-CVI and CVR criteria after expert review and item revision. It also demonstrated acceptable test-retest reliability, appropriate convergent validity with pain scores and FEV1, and criterion-related validity with physiotherapists' scores.

The study developed the EFT as a new and validated tool for adolescents undergoing cancer treatment to self-assess their own physical fitness and appropriate levels of PA to be conducted. This new tool is different from other commonly used exercise assessment tools. The literature review and expert panel revealed that PAR-Q is the most commonly used and internationally known assessment tool for assessing physical fitness across a wide age span of individuals before exercising.65 PAR-Q was developed on the basis of expert opinion, and it has been documented that it offers a brief and safe preliminary screening of individuals for exercise testing and prescription.75 Nevertheless, PAR-Q has a tendency to screen out what seems to be an excessive proportion of apparently healthy individuals,75 underestimating the proportion of individuals who are able to exercise, particularly those exercising at low-intensity levels. This may result in hesitation on the part of these individuals to exercise. In addition, all the self-reported tools available for assessing physical fitness in adolescents (i.e. PAR-Q, the IFIS, SPPC, CSAPPA, PSDQ, and CY-PSPP) were not specifically designed for adolescents undergoing cancer treatment. Adolescents undergoing cancer treatment are a vulnerable population who might experience various side effects with long-term cancer and therapy-related complications, affecting their ability to engage in moderate- and vigorous-intensity PA.

However, research indicates that PA is beneficial to this population by ameliorating their short- and long-term complications from cancer and its treatment.2,13 It also indicates that PA, at least at low-intensity levels, can be conducted and that intervention should be made for this population as early as cancer diagnosis to enable them to benefit from PA.2,13 Yet, adolescents undergoing cancer treatment were easily screened out by PAR-Q due to their complications from cancer and its treatment. Most importantly, as PAR-Q aims to assess an individual's readiness for PA and provide preliminary screening of individuals for exercise testing and prescription,75 it should be used in conjunction with appropriate clinical guidelines for guiding individuals toward graduated PA.65 It lacked information on exercise prescriptions or details of the exercise guidelines, which was consistently found absent in the other self-reported tools available for assessing physical fitness. Therefore, the EFT, which is being developed and validated in this study, offers a simple and valuable self-assessment tool for adolescents undergoing cancer treatment to self-assess their own physical fitness and appropriate levels of PA.

The EFT identified 10 items and 2 factors. Comparing with other self-reported tools available for assessing physical fitness in adolescents (i.e. PAR-Q, CSAPPA, PSDQ, and CY-PSPP) which included 3 to 9 factors, EFT only identified 2 factors. This could be attributed to the reason that EFT focused on the physical functioning of the respondents, but these other tools emphasized both the physical functioning and PA motivation. The factors which are more related to PA motivation include the factor “vitality” in PAR-Q assessing respondents' feelings of being energetic and revived,22 the factor “enjoyment of physical education class” in CSAPPA measuring the enjoyment of PA,25 the factor “appearance” in PSDQ26 and the factor “body attractiveness” in CY-PSPP27 reporting respondents’ perception of their body appearance/attractiveness for PA. All these factors are associated with PA motivations,76 however respondents are still fit for PA regardless of the responses in the above factors. Therefore, EFT did not include these factors into the scale and resulting in a smaller number of extracted factors.

When comparing the factors in EFT to SPPC and IFIS, SPPC contains five factors but only one factor, “athletic competence”,24 is relevant to physical fitness and IFIS includes five self-reported fitness domains: overall physical fitness, cardiorespiratory fitness, muscle strength, speed/agility and flexibility to assess overall physical fitness.23 SPPC – athletic competence and IFIS are similar to EFT in a way that they all focused on the respondents' physical functioning. Nevertheless, the factors in SPPC and IFIS do not consider cancer-specific situations. Different from all the self-reported tools for physical fitness, the first factor in EFT, “Cancer and treatment influence on PA,” focused on any cancer- and treatment-related procedures and complications that were important and could significantly hinder the physical ability of adolescents who were undergoing cancer treatment. Under this factor, craniectomies, seizures in the past 24 hours, medical devices related to cancer treatment, fever, dizziness, pain, and shortness of breath were included for low-intensity PA. Among them, all except craniectomies and medical devices were not found in other self-reported tools for assessing physical fitness including PAR-Q, IFIS, SPPC - athletic competence, CSAPPA, PSDQ, and CY-PSPP. EFT added craniectomies (bone flap removals) for low-intensity PA. Craniectomies are surgical procedures that might be performed on cancer patients, particularly those with brain tumors, that could be dangerous and severely affect patients’ physical abilities.77 Evidence shows that patients who have received craniectomies should avoid strenuous activities, but light stretching PA is beneficial.77 Strenuous activities or higher–intensity activities might result in a high risk of increasing intracranial pressure, brain swelling, or hemorrhage.77

Furthermore, EFT also incorporated medical devices related to cancer treatment since the presence of medical devices such as urinary catheters and current connections with pumping machines for medications and therapies severely affected the S/G of adolescents with cancer, which is also an important criterion for assessing physical fitness. Medical devices might hinder adolescents from performing high-intensity PA. Adolescents with these medical devices were also required to exercise with caution to avoid displacement of devices. In particular, seizures were included in PAR-Q to screen out younger populations for PA but requiring further medical advice before exercising.65 Nevertheless, seizures in the past 24 hours were included in the EFT for low-intensity PA. Studies document that PA is beneficial for individuals with seizures and that low-intensity PA are regarded as “sports with no significant additional risk”.78,79 Although PARmed-X may allow respondents to state freely any other items including cancer-specific ones under “other reasons”, the PARmed-X is a PA-specific checklist to be used by a physician with patients who have had positive responses to the PAR-Q.65 It is not intended to be a self-reported tool used by patients themselves.

Other items were commonly found in other tools. Fever is widely known to be a reliable indicator of acute infections or chronic disease,80 which requires immediate attention. Besides, dizziness and pain might significantly affect balance and coordination. In particular, pain might indicate muscle damage or injuries, and forcefully performing high-intensity PA regardless of pain might lead to impairment of body joints and tendons.53 Hence, EFT reminds adolescents undergoing cancer treatment that no PA should be conducted in painful areas. Apart from that, adolescents with fever or dizziness were also able to conduct PA under EFT score 2, which included merely stretching exercises on bed with safety rails or with another person to monitor safety. For shortness of breath, it may result in the inability to get air to breathe regularly;81 therefore, EFT prescribes only stretching exercises on bed with more safety precautions for adolescents with shortness of breath (EFT score 2).

The second factor in EFT was “Perceived PA exertion.” This factor was new and was supported by various studies but not stated explicitly in other self-reported tools for physical fitness. In this factor, the EFT focused on patient-reported feelings about PA. An increasing number of studies have demonstrated the importance of patient-reported outcomes and measures in precisely reflecting patients' condition,82 as they gathered patients' health-related information directly from patients, not merely focusing on interpretation from the clinical staff.83 In addition, this factor involved patients assessing their PA exertion according to the RPE. Importantly, RPE was necessary to predict exercise capacity and assess changes in exercising status, which suit the needs of adolescents undergoing cancer treatment who have fluctuating physical conditions and fitness due to cancer and its treatment. RPE in EFT was determined using the Borg CR10 scale, a CategoryRatio (CR) scale from 0 to 10 and a common tool for reflecting an individual's effort and exertion, breathlessness, and fatigue during PA.84 RPE is also linearly related to heart rate, which is an important indicator that should be monitored during exercise.85

Despite that EFT is firstly developed as a self-assessment tool to enable adolescents undergoing cancer treatment to self-assess the appropriate levels of PA that they can perform, it is anticipated that caregivers may use EFT with the adolescents or a caregiver-version may be developed for the future applications to assist the assessment of adolescents in the busy clinical setting. Particularly, the Chinese emphasized family cohesion that any decision-making including PA is preferred to be made with the family members.86 Therefore, different cultures may affect the adoption and further development of EFT. Also, for future applications, EFT may be clinically integrated into the physician visits, nursing protocols, and mobile applications, to facilitate the assessment of physical fitness, rather than merely depending on the tests that require specific therapists and devices. The integration may further enhance patient-reported outcomes and assessment, truly reflecting the feeling from patients for treatment and exercise prescription.87 However, it is aware that EFT's caregiver-assisted use, clinician-integrated use, mobile application integration, or use in nursing protocols required separate validation in the future.

Notwithstanding the above anticipations, EFT is developed solely based on the Hong Kong population. It is aware that cultural, linguistic, and health care system differences may limit generalizability of EFT. Especially, some cultures may present gender differences in PA adoption which limit female participation in PA or outdoor PA, viewing it as “unfeminine”.88 Other cultures may view a large body size as prosperity and thus avoid PA.89 Apart from cultures, language affects the understandability and different wordings may be required for different countries. Also, the health care system may vary among countries. Particularly, in areas that exercise may be strictly prescribed or monitored by specialists, EFT may need to be adapted appropriately to fit into the different culture, linguistic, and health care system.

Implications for nursing practice and research

PA ameliorates the adverse effects from cancer and its treatment, consequently improving quality of life. Nevertheless, the PA levels of adolescents who were undergoing cancer treatment were found significantly lower when compared with their pre-cancer situation and their healthy counterparts. In the busy clinical setting, the development and validation of EFT, a patient-based and self-assessment tool to enable adolescents undergoing cancer treatment to self-assess the appropriate levels of PA that they can perform, is of paramount importance. Future studies with EFA and CFA on EFT are recommended. It is expected that after further validation, health care professionals, especially nurses who frequently interact with patients, can introduce well-validated EFT to adolescents undergoing cancer treatment and their families as a self-assessment tool to self-assess the levels of PA that they can safely conduct even during cancer treatment, especially at times when they are unable to consult physicians or physiotherapists since health care professionals have limited time for assessing adolescents’ physical fitness for PA in their busy clinical setting. Introducing EFT to adolescents undergoing cancer treatment and their families can also enhance their awareness of importance of PA during cancer treatment period and survivorship, consequently promoting the adoption of PA. Family members and health care workers particularly physicians, nurses, and physiotherapists, may use EFT as a self-assessment tool in monitoring the physical fitness of adolescents undergoing cancer treatment. Family members and health care workers should increase their alertness for any decline in the physical fitness as assessed by EFT and be cautious for exploring the reasons behind. EFT may also be incorporated into different PA interventions such as the mobile applications to enhance the accessibility, and current nursing protocols for adolescents undergoing cancer treatment to facilitate the assessment of appropriate levels of PA. However, as EFT is a self-assessment tool, its family-assisted use, clinician-integrated use, mobile app integration, or use in nursing protocols will be the future applications that require separate validation. In addition, this study can also provide fundamental evidence to scholars to develop a similar assessment tool for other populations in need and countries for cross-cultural adaptation and validation.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this study lies in its originality, as it is the first to develop and validate a new patient-based, self-assessment tool, EFT, for adolescents undergoing cancer treatment in Hong Kong to self-assess their own appropriate intensity and levels of PA. In addition, the study adopted a systematic approach of Artino's seven steps, with the aim of combining the quantitative and qualitative data obtained in the process of developing and validating the EFT. Another strength is that the EFT items were developed based on information from oncology experts and representatives of adolescents undergoing cancer treatment.

This study has several limitations that may guide future research directions. First, it had a small sample size with only adolescents receiving treatment and was conducted in one hospital although the majority of adolescents undergoing cancer treatment are admitted to this hospital in Hong Kong. Information from adolescents in survivorship, multiple centres and other countries with different cultures was not collected. These factors limit the generalizability of using EFT. Hence, larger multicenter studies with adolescents in survivorship to provide evidence of the EFT's validity and reliability are warranted. Second, exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) were not performed. Considering that the EFT was a new tool, the study used PCA for exploratory dimensional reduction.48 Future studies with EFA and CFA will benefit from verifying the EFT's validity. Third, this study focused only on the physical aspect of fitness without other related aspects such as autonomy in PA, peer or parental influence, and self-image, which might affect the motivation for PA and were adolescent-specific items. This is because this study was based on previous evidence showing that the existing interventions were already able to significantly motivate and enhance the PA levels for a short period of time but the effects could not be maintained due to adolescents' changing physical fitness. With the development and further validation of this tool, it is expected to be used alone to assess whether motivated adolescents undergoing cancer treatment are physically able to conduct PA and which PA that they can perform. For those who were not motivated, this tool may be considered to be added into other motivation-enhancing interventions to sustainably promote the PA level of adolescents with cancer after validation. Fourth, only 4 adolescents were involved in the cognitive interviews. Fifth, cultural, linguistic, and health care system differences may limit EFT's generalizability as only Hong Kong patients were recruited in this study. Finally, although the EFT produced over 90% accuracy, all inaccurate scores were underestimations which may reflect conservative bias.

Conclusions

This study provided evidence of the preliminary validity and reliability of a new patient-based, self-assessment tool, the EFT, for adolescents undergoing cancer treatment in Hong Kong to self-assess their own appropriate intensity and levels of PA. The EFT has a two-component solution: cancer and its treatment's influence on PA and perceived physical exertion. This study offers important evidence for future studies to further examine the component structure and broader implementation of the EFT in independent samples, including caregiver-assisted, clinician-integrated, mobile health, and nursing protocol contexts, before wider use.
