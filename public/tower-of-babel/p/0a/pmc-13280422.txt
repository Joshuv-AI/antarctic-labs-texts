Comparing Two Distraction Techniques with Illumination Caps for Assessing Dental Anxiety in Pediatric Patients during Single-visit Pulpectomies Based on the Perfusion Index Parameter
Authors: Resham Payak, Anshul Gangwar, Chanchal Gangwar, Surbhi Saxena, Shilpa Kumari, Meghna Bhargava
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims and backgroundDifferent distraction techniques have been used in dentistry and have shown great results in managing anxious pediatric patients, especially during local anesthesia (LA) administration. Various physiological parameters are used to measure anxiety, among which peripheral perfusion index (PI) provides information about vascular tone and fluid status by interpreting the data obtained from pulse oximetry. In the present study, we aimed to evaluate the relationship between anxiety and PI values and also compare different distraction techniques in single-visit pulpectomy.Materials and methodsHealthy, cooperative 5–7 years old participants were selected for single-visit pulpectomy. Four groups were formed by simple random sampling. Group I (control), group II (illumination cap), group III (laser light), and group IV [audiovisual (AV)]. Two physiological parameters are recorded [pulse rate (PR), PI] at three intervals: (1) baseline—when the patient sat on the dental chair, (2) second reading—5 minutes after introduction of the distraction technique during LA administration, and (3) third reading—25 minutes after the second reading.ResultsIntergroup comparison and post hoc analysis are done for all the groups with two key parameters. Introduction

Pain and anxiety management during local anesthesia (LA) administration remains a significant challenge in clinical practice, particularly among pediatric and anxious patients. The anticipation of pain often exacerbates physiological responses such as elevated heart rate and blood pressure, which can negatively impact the overall treatment experience and outcomes. In recent years, the integration of nonpharmacological distraction techniques has gained considerable attention for their potential to mitigate these responses.

Although modern dentistry has developed rapidly, dental anxiety (DA) and pain caused by dental treatment still commonly affect the patient's experience of scoring the pain. Anxiety is related to the mental and emotional state characterized by various emotional, perceptual, and behavioral changes.1

It is an important matter in the dental care of adults, children, and adolescents. DA affects 10–20% of adults and 43% of children and adolescents.2 One in every six children reporting to the dentist has anxiety issues, and management is an intricate balancing act involving the triad of child, parent/caregiver, and the dentist.1

Various sensory-based modalities, including visual and auditory stimuli, have shown promise in reducing patient discomfort during invasive procedures. These methods function by diverting the patient's attention away from the nociceptive stimulus, thereby modulating the perception of pain and altering associated physiological parameters. Among these, the use of illumination-based tools such as specialized caps and laser light, as well as audiovisual distraction (AVD) techniques, has been explored for their calming effects on patients during dental and medical interventions.3

Peripheral perfusion index (PI) is a noninvasive measurement with the ratio of pulsatile and nonpulsatile photoplethysmography signals obtained from pulse oximetry. This noninvasive and easily applicable technique can be used before or after anesthesia to obtain information about the fluid status and vascular tone of the patients.4

This study aims to conduct a comparative evaluation of three such distraction techniques—illumination cap, laser light exposure, and AVD—during the administration of LA at different time intervals. By monitoring key physiological indicators such as pulse rate (PR) and PI, the study seeks to identify the most effective method for minimizing stress-related responses during LA delivery. The findings are expected to contribute to evidence-based enhancements in patient comfort and anxiety management protocols in clinical settings.

Materials and Methods

The study is a randomized, controlled trial for which ethical clearance was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the institution before the study (IEC/290/2024/04) (Flowchart 1 and Figs 1 to 3).

The study participants were recruited from those reporting to the outpatient department of the institutional department of pediatric and preventive dentistry of a reputed dental college and hospital in Bareilly city, India. Prior to participating in the study, the parent/guardian provided informed written consent.

Sample Size Calculation

A power analysis was estimated using G*Power, version 3.0.1, and the calculated sample size was 76, rounded off to 80, with effect size f = 0.22 and power = 0.99.

Study Design

A total of 80 participants were selected, aged between 5 and 7 years, and were assorted randomly into four groups of n = 20 each by simple random sampling using the lottery method. The present study was conducted over 6 months.

Data Collection

Physiological parameters (PR, PI) were recorded at three respective time intervals. Readings were taken of patients requiring single-visit pulpectomy of primary teeth following the standard protocol, whether maxillary or mandibular first or second molars, irrespective of quadrant.

The first (1st) baseline reading was taken when the patient sat on the dental chair. The second (2nd) reading was taken 5 minutes after the distraction technique was introduced during the administration of LA. The third (3rd) reading was taken 25 minutes after the second reading during the procedure.

All the procedures were done by a single operator, while the records were taken by another postgraduate (PG) student (observer) and supervised by a faculty member. The procedure was performed, and all the recordings were kept blind from the operator.

Clinical Evaluation

The child's PI and PR were objectively measured using a pulse oximeter. Children eligible for the study were those attending their first dental visit and meeting the inclusion criteria. Single-visit pulpectomy was performed with the administration of a local anesthetic nerve block (2% lignocaine with 1:80,000). The procedure was limited to a maximum of 40 minutes, from LA administration to obturation of the canal with Metapex and access cavity filled with IRM.

The tell-show-do technique was used with the patient before starting the dental procedure.

Group I: (Control group) Served as the control group, with no distraction technique used.

Group II: (Illumination cap) The PG (observer) used an illumination cap with dancing ears and a light to passively distract the patient.

Group III: (Laser light) A laser light pen was provided to the patient, allowing them to project it onto the walls. This served as a distraction technique by actively engaging them in the activity.

Group IV: (Audiovisual) In this group, the patient was given a mobile device and was offered the option to watch their favorite cartoon on YouTube, which was downloaded on the parents' mobile. Earphones or headphones were not used while the procedure was carried out.

All the participants were recalled for postoperative evaluation, followed by a stainless steel crown.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 21.0 software (SPSS statistical software 23.0 version). The descriptive statistics included mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage. The level of significance for the present study was fixed at 5%. The intergroup comparison was done using the one-way ANOVA test, followed by post hoc analysis depending upon the normality of the data. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to investigate the distribution of the data, and Levene's test was used to explore the homogeneity of the variables.

Results

The statistical comparison across the four groups was conducted using two key parameters—PR and PI. The analysis revealed that both the PR and PI exhibited statistically significant differences among the groups, which was recorded at three time intervals. This suggested that variations in these two parameters were notably different when compared within the groups, highlighting their importance in the study.

The results displayed in Table 1 showed that the differences in PR of participants during the use of different interventions, and when compared with the control group, were found to be statistically significant (pretreatment p-value = 1.021 to during-treatment p-value = 0.015). During LA administration, all groups showed increased values, but it was less in group II, which indicated that this group was effective in reducing anxiety. Group II (illumination cap) had the lowest mean PR, followed by group III (laser light), then group IV (AV distraction), and lastly, the control group.

When the comparisons were carried out for PR in before-posttreatment, the comparisons of group II, group III, and group IV were found to be statistically significant. While comparing multiple groups (before and posttreatment) in terms of PI, the comparison between group I (control) and group II (illumination cap) was found to be statistically significant.

Discussion

The present study was undertaken with the goal of identifying a potential remedy for the discomfort and anxiety among children that come along with administering LA. The age-group of the patients selected in the present study was 5–7 years.

Use of local anesthetic (LA) is a crucial component of pain management strategies in pediatric dentistry. Even with the ongoing advancement of many cutting-edge dental injection methods, many children still experience pain and suffering from LA injections, which have been identified as a major cause of DA.5 In order to encourage children to comply with dental clinical visits, a series of behavior guidance techniques to improve children's behavior has been suggested (AAPD, 2020).6

Among the noninvasive distractive behavior guidance, AVD is being utilized for children who watch and listen to movies during a stressful procedure.7

A substantial body of research has explored the relationship between anxiety and physiological parameters such as heart rate and PR, highlighting how anxiety can significantly influence cardiovascular responses. These studies7–10 have consistently demonstrated that heightened anxiety is often associated with increased heart rate or altered pulse patterns. However, despite this well-established connection, there appears to be a notable gap in the literature concerning the PI, a noninvasive measure of peripheral blood flow.

Numerous strategies for behavioral management have been suggested in order to assist children in conquering their dental phobias and anxieties, as well as to enhance their conduct throughout dental procedures.

Dental anxiety has been the primary reason for not seeking dental care. Avoidance of dental procedures in children ranges between 5.7 and 19.5% and is due to anxiety. Hence, to manage the anxiety and fear, proper assessment of DA followed by the requirement of treatment in a pleasant setting is essential.8

Jones et al. (2009)9 and Mallorqui-Bagué et al. (2016)10 proposed that when individuals interpret a situation as stressful or threatening, accompanied by physiological arousal such as an elevated heart or pulse rate, their anxiety levels may subsequently intensify. Furthermore, Mallorqui-Bagué et al. (2016) noted that anxiety symptoms can also escalate as a result of the individual's own perception of these bodily changes, particularly fluctuations in cardiovascular activity.10

The peripheral PI is derived from pulse oximetry and is defined as the ratio of the pulse wave of the pulsatile portion (arteries) to the nonpulsatile portion (venous and other tissues). PI reflects the change in blood volume with each heartbeat in the fingers. A recent review in 2024 found that PI can provide useful information for pain assessment, accuracy of saturation of peripheral oxygen (SpO2) and glucose measurement, vasopressor therapy, outcome prediction, fluid measurement, risk stratification, and shock resuscitation.11

Vasoactive drugs, anesthesia, and pain cause changes in vascular tone. In general, PI is negatively correlated with vascular tone.4,11

Yüksek and Yardimci did a study to evaluate the inverse relationship between anxiety and PI values. PI decreases at high anxiety levels in adult patients during elective surgery in a medical setup. It is in accordance with our study. During pretreatment, PI values were lower and gradually increased after the introduction of respective distraction techniques. Groups II and III showed the highest PI values, indicating that anxiety was lower in those participants.4

Pulse rate was another parameter assessed in our study. Several authors have reported a decrease in PR following the use of distraction techniques. Aditya et al. observed a significant reduction in PR when comparing three distraction techniques during IANB.7 Similarly, Khandelwal et al. found a notable decrease in PR after using four different distraction techniques during extraction and pulp therapy procedures.1 Findings from our study are consistent with these articles.

Previous research5,12–14 has highlighted the potential of AV methods in managing anxiety. Felemban et al. evaluated the effect of virtual reality distraction on anxiety and pain during buccal infiltration anesthesia in pediatric patients.5 Panda evaluated the effect of VR on pain perception during INB in pulp therapy.12 Fakhruddin et al. studied the effectiveness of behavioral modification techniques with VR using intrasulcular LA in hearing-disabled patients.13 Alsibai et al. evaluated the effectiveness of two distraction techniques (AV/video game) during primary mandibular pulpotomy procedures.14 They found that during the administration of LA, a peak in anxiety was observed in their study, and after the introduction of distraction techniques, a reduction in anxiety was noted.

Similar findings were also observed in our study, as AV was effective in reducing anxiety. Although AV has shown their potential in reducing anxiety, this passive technique does have certain disadvantages, such as increased screen time, missing out on verbal instructions, and short-term effect.

So, we decided to explore alternative distraction methods that are more cost-effective and can be utilized both actively and passively. To this end, we used two distinct methods—an illumination cap, which offers a soothing light experience, and a laser light, often used for its captivating visual effects.

Through careful observation and measurement of anxiety levels before and after the interventions, our results indicated that the illumination cap had the greatest impact in reducing anxiety.

This suggests that tactile and visual engagement, as provided by the moving toy cap, may provide a more effective form of distraction for certain children. Moreover, the novelty and physical interaction with the toy cap may create an additional sense of control and comfort.

To the best of my knowledge, there is no existing research in dentistry that has recorded the PI in pediatric patients, nor the use of an illumination cap and laser light pen as distraction techniques for anxiety control in single-visit pulpectomy.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, it can be concluded that the PI may serve as a supportive indicator, alongside other physiological parameters, in assessing anxiety. Additionally, the use of an illumination cap and laser light pen as distraction tools presents an effective approach for reducing anxiety in pediatric dental settings.
