Trends and Outcomes in Outpatient Revision Total Hip Arthroplasty: A Retrospective Matched Cohort Study
Authors: Claire Lin, Manjot Singh, Joseph E. Nassar, Jonathan Liu, Alan H. Daniels, Eric M. Cohen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeDue to recent shifts in healthcare reimbursement models, more primary total hip arthroplasties (THAs) are now being performed in the outpatient setting. However, there is a lack of knowledge around the comparative outcomes of revision outpatient THA.Materials and MethodsAdults undergoing revision THA between 2010 and 2020 with 2-year follow-up data were identified on a large insurance claims database. Following stratification by inpatient versus outpatient revision THA, patients from both groups were matched 1:1 by demographics and revision characteristics. Ninety-day postoperative medical and 1- and 2-year postoperative surgical complications after matching were compared.ResultsAmong 67,570 patients, mean age was 65.3 years, 57.0% were female, and mean Charlson comorbidity index score was 1.72. Subsequent to 1:1 matching, 1,555 inpatient and outpatient revision THA patients were identified. Outpatient THA patients had significantly lower rates of blood transfusions (3.6% vs 5.1%, P=0.049), broken prosthesis (1 year: 0.1% vs. 0.6%, P=0.027; 2 years: 0.2% vs. 0.9%, P=0.015), and other postoperative mechanical complications (1 year: 4.9% vs. 7.1%, P=0.010; 2 years: 6.2% vs. 8.4%, P=0.023). Otherwise, the data for outpatient THA patients was not statistically different from inpatient THA patients across all studied complications.ConclusionOutpatient revision THA may be a safe alternative to inpatient revision THA for carefully selected patients. Establishing patient- and case-specific parameters to optimize outcomes, satisfaction, safety, and value is critical.

INTRODUCTION

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is considered to be one of the most successful and cost-effective surgeries available in modern medicine1). Proven to be extremely effective, primary THA improves pain and function in patients with hip osteoarthritis2). However, with increased life expectancy and a rise in number of primary THA procedures performed on younger and more active patients, the demand for revision THA will continue to grow in the coming decades1,3). Concordantly, an exponential rise has been observed in the rate of outpatient primary THA with excellent outcomes and safety. However, as the emergence of revision THA performed in the outpatient setting is less common, outcomes are not well known at this time.

Healthcare reimbursement models have also undergone drastic changes in the past few years, with Medicare transitioning from fee-for-service to alternative payment structures4-6). A primary target for reducing costs, total joint arthroplasty (TJA) is the largest Diagnosis-Related Group expense under the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS)4-6). Compared to primary THA, revision THA is a more complex procedure requiring more resources, longer surgery times and higher costs7-13). As such, concerns have been raised regarding the financial impact of revision procedures especially since revision THA was removed from the CMS Inpatient Only (IPO) list in 202114). This change in policy has led to ongoing discussions about the performance of revision THA in an outpatient setting, thus prompting the need for further investigation into its safety and efficacy5).

While primary THA has been increasingly adopted in the outpatient setting since its removal from the IPO list in 2020, there is still limited data on whether the higher complexity and complication risks associated with revision THA could be accommodated for in an outpatient setting15). Previous studies on outpatient primary THA have shown comparable safety and efficacy to their inpatient counterparts with lower adverse events and similar readmission rates and revision rates16-18). However, it is still unclear whether revision THA would yield similar outcomes. Thus, the aims of this study are to assess the current trends in the utilization of revision THA and to help guide clinical decision-making and optimize patient selection through the evaluation of safety in revision THA performed in inpatient and outpatient settings.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study Design

In this retrospective cohort study, the PearlDiver (PearlDiver Technologies) Mariner 170 dataset was queried to identify all patients who underwent revision THA between January 1, 2010 and December 31, 2020. The Mariner 170 dataset contains over 170 million patients from all U.S. states and territories covered under commercial insurance, Medicare, Medicaid, government insurance, and self-pay. Patient records were retrieved using International Classifications of Diseases, ninth (ICD-9) and tenth revision (ICD-10), and Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) billing codes. This study used a publicly available de-identified database and did not require institutional review board approval.

2. Study Population

Adult patients who underwent revision THA between January 1, 2010 and December 31, 2020 were identified using ICD/CPT code algorithms defined beforehand (see Supplementary Table 1 for full code lists). Exclusion criteria included patients under 18 years of age as well as those with incomplete data. Furthermore, patients were excluded from this study if they had a history of tumor, trauma, infection, comorbid autoimmune conditions, or traumatic fracture within seven days after surgery. Patients were also excluded if they did not have two years of follow-up data after their THA.

3. Cohort Generation

Eligible patients were stratified by inpatient or outpatient revision THA based on the presence or absence of an inpatient stay associated with their surgery. Cohorts were subsequently matched 1:1 by age, sex, Charlson comorbidity index (CCI), diabetes, obesity, tobacco use, mechanical revision etiology (i.e., aseptic loosening, broken prosthesis, periprosthetic fracture, periprosthetic osteolysis, prosthetic joint infection, instability, bearing surface wear, mechanical or other complications), and revision procedure (i.e., total, acetabular component, femoral component).

4. Patient Characteristics

Among the 67,570 included patients, 65,249 underwent inpatient revision THA and 2,321 underwent outpatient revision THA. The mean age was 65.3 years, 57.0% were female, and mean CCI was 1.72. Higher mean age and higher rates of tobacco use (P<0.05) were found in inpatient THA patients. Frequently, revision etiology was aseptic loosening (14.7%), periprosthetic fracture (10.4%), or instability (9.0%). All mechanical reasons for revision were more common in the inpatient group (all P<0.001). Revision procedure was often full revision (59.3%) (Table 1). Using 1:1 matching, post-match balance resulted in no statistical differences in the following variables: age, sex, CCI, diabetes, obesity, tobacco use, mechanical revision etiology, and revision procedure. Of the matched revision THA patients identified, 1,555 were inpatient and 1,555 were outpatient. Mean age was 64.6 years, 57.0% were female, and mean CCI was 1.30.

5. Statistical Analyses

Initially, patient demographics, revision etiology, and revision procedure were compared using Student’s t-test for continuous variables and chi-square analyses for categorical variables. Subsequently, inpatient and outpatient cohorts were case-control matched by confounding variables as described above using a 1:1 case-control matching algorithm. Afterwards, medical complications occurring within 90 days (i.e., acute kidney injury, deep venous thromboembolism, pulmonary embolism, hematoma, transfusion, urinary tract infection, wound complications, site complications, foot drop, and readmissions) and surgical complications occurring between one and two years postoperatively (i.e., aseptic loosening, broken prosthesis, periprosthetic fracture, periprosthetic osteolysis, prosthetic joint infection, instability, bearing wear, other mechanical or non-mechanical complications, and revision) were identified using ICD-9, ICD-10, and CPT billing codes. These complications were similarly compared to controls using chi-square analyses. All statistical analyses were performed using the R program (ver. 4.2.1; R Core Team 2022) embedded within PearlDiver, with a P-value of <0.05 indicating statistical significance.

RESULTS

1. Trends in Inpatient and Outpatient Revision THA

From 2010 to 2020, there was a 33.8% decrease in the number of inpatient revision THA and a 380.2% increase in the number of outpatient revision THA (Fig. 1). The highest inpatient revision THA rates were seen in the 70-74 age group and the highest outpatient revision THA rates were seen in the 60-64 age group (Fig. 2).

2. Medical and Surgical Complications

Following 1:1 case-control matching, outpatient THA patients had significantly lower rates of blood transfusion (outpatient=3.6% vs. inpatient=5.1%, P=0.049), but were otherwise not statistically different from inpatient THA patients across 90-day medical complications. Moreover, outpatients had significantly lower rates of broken prosthesis (1 year: 0.1% vs. 0.6%, P=0.027; 2 years: 0.2% vs. 0.9%, P=0.015) and other mechanical complications (1 year: 4.9% vs. 7.1%, P=0.010; 2 years: 6.2% vs. 8.4%, P=0.023) (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

With an increasing number of patients and surgeons opting for outpatient revision THA, evaluating the comparative safety and efficacy of outpatient vs inpatient procedures is warranted. In this large retrospective matched-cohort study, there were no clinically significant differences in the incidence of the most investigated postoperative complications between inpatient and outpatient revision THA at 90 day, 1 year, and 2 years, postoperatively, with the exception of lower rates of blood transfusion and certain mechanical complications. These findings suggest that, in appropriately indicated patients, outpatient revision THA can be a safe alternative to the traditional inpatient setting.

In terms of cost-saving benefits, efficiency, and patient and surgeon satisfaction, outpatient procedures have become increasingly common in orthopedic surgery. Notably, with the removal of primary THA from the Medicare IPO list in 2020 and revision THA in 2021, there has been an increase in patients with higher CCI and Hierarchical Condition Category scores electing for outpatient THA19). Complications, readmissions, and re-operations remain relatively low for outpatient THA, despite the expanded eligibility, and are comparable to inpatient THA, suggesting that outpatient hip procedures may be safe and effective with appropriate patient selection and optimization3,20-24). Similarly, our study found increasing rates of outpatient revision THA and comparable outcomes across inpatient and outpatient THA patients. This may reflect improvements in patient education and surgical, anesthetic, and rehabilitation techniques that allow for safe, same calendar-day discharge (SCDD)25). Discharge failures, when they do occur, are often due to pain, hypotension, and nausea. Addressing these complications through comprehensive patient education and standardized management protocols has been shown to facilitate early and safe SCDD25-30). For instance, increased utilization of neuraxial anesthesia in outpatient joint surgery has been associated with lower rates of superficial infection, transfusion, and thromboembolic and cardiac events, as well as decreased length of hospital stay31-33). Following outpatient procedures, improvements in intraoperative blood conservation, wound management, emphasis on early mobilization, and venous thromboembolism prophylaxis may also contribute to the improved outcomes25).

Similar to primary THA, patient selection is critical. Not every patient may be a suitable candidate for outpatient revision THA. Revision arthroplasties can be challenging procedures, which, compared to primaries, may require significant resources and operative time with potentially higher complication rates and suboptimal outcomes11,12). Notably, multiple studies on revision THA patients have reported higher rates of complications, mortality, and rehabilitation facility discharge11,34). Thus, careful selection of patients for outpatient revision is warranted despite the growing popularity of outpatient procedures29,35). Factors associated with major complications following revision THA include age over 75 years, hypertension, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, general anesthesia, and preoperative anemia36). Prolonged hospital stays have been observed for patients with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) scores above 2, operative time over 180 minutes, and full revisions36). Finally, smoking, bleeding diatheses, congestive heart failure, coronary artery disease, and cirrhosis have also been associated with adverse outcomes37,38). Overall, it appears that the ideal candidate for outpatient revision is relatively young (age <75 years) and healthy (e.g., without significant cardiac or pulmonary comorbidities), with ASA scores <2, operative times <180 minutes, and with a solid support system at home to provide appropriate postoperative care26). As these risk factors have been identified in the inpatient revision setting, ensuring safety in the outpatient setting requires further investigation and more stringent or modified considerations39). As many institutions have well-defined medical optimization and screening protocols for primary THA, it is likely that outpatient revision THA will require updated and unique criteria that is supported by evidence40).

Moreover, revision THA is not a uniform surgery. It has a broad spectrum of complexity which has important implications when considering the outpatient setting. The inpatient group had marginally higher rates of both component revision, suggesting that complex procedures with prolonged surgical time, extensive implant needs, and higher expected blood loss may be preferentially performed in the inpatient setting29). Specifically, and in line with previous findings, etiologies like periprosthetic fracture and prosthetic joint infection were more common in inpatient revision THA patients5). Outpatient revision THA may be more suitable for lower surgical risk patients requiring simpler revision procedures, such as single component revisions or isolated polyethylene liner exchange5,29). Although shorter operative time is an independent predictor of early discharge regardless of number of components replaced, outpatient revision, if done efficiently in the correct patient, may be appropriate for full revisions39).

Interestingly, patients undergoing outpatient revision THA required significantly fewer blood transfusions. Otherwise, these outpatients had equivocal rates of short-term complications compared to their inpatient revision THA counterparts. This finding aligns with previous studies demonstrating lower rates of transfusions in outpatient procedures41). In the outpatient setting, this may reflect a more prudent use of antifibrinolytics, better fluid management, less blood loss during surgery, shorter operative time, or lower incidence of severe complications that require transfusion41). It is also important to consider that lower rate of transfusion is likely biased by careful patient and case selection, as well as discharge without postoperative labs. Furthermore, outpatient revision THA patients had lower rates of broken prostheses and other mechanical complications, which may similarly be driven by selection of patients with greater mobility for the outpatient setting. The higher rates of broken prostheses and other mechanical complications in the inpatient group may also likely reflect residual selection bias despite matching. Complications such as these are often associated with more complex revision procedures, higher surgical time, and increased implant requirements—all factors that tend to prompt inpatient management. However, ultimately, the absence of clinically-significant differences in complications between the two groups in the short- and long-term suggests that, when appropriately selected, patients undergoing outpatient revisions can achieve comparable outcomes to those undergoing inpatient procedures, reinforcing the safety and efficacy of the outpatient revision THA model39).

The results of this study demonstrate, from a patient and healthcare perspective, a substantial advantage for outpatient revision THA. This is most likely due to reduced need for postoperative interventions and less than 24 hour hospital stays42). Significant cost savings in the outpatient setting have been demonstrated by studies comparing costs between inpatient and outpatient THA. This consequence may improve access to services for patients and efficiency at healthcare centers43). For example, a study from Ontario, Canada, found that outpatient hip and knee arthroplasty, compared to inpatient care, resulted in average perioperative cost savings of approximately $3,800-$4,000 per case, with a cumulative 3-year savings exceeding $7,000 per patient. These savings were distributed between hospitalization, ambulatory care, physician billing, home care, and medication costs, with an estimated potential health system savings of up to $98 million annually if outpatient uptake increased further44). In the United States, Medicare data has shown a rapid shift of TJA volume to ambulatory surgical centers (ASCs). As ASC procedures cost significantly less than hospital-based inpatient arthroplasty, this has resulted in annual savings of $235 million45,46). Reporting an average cost reduction of nearly $6,800 for outpatient joint arthroplasty, systematic review and meta-analysis further confirm these findings, with no significant differences in complications or readmissions in selected populations47). Similarly, for the right candidates, outpatient revision THA may represent a safe and cost-effective alternative to inpatient revision THA.

With studies in the primary THA population demonstrating subjective improvements in medication explanation, staff assistance with pain management, delivery of discharge information, and staff bedside manner and rapport, outpatient revision THA may also lead to greater patient satisfaction compared to patients receiving inpatient THA48). In an ambulatory surgery center, this may be due to highly efficient and standardized protocols involving staff training, perioperative workflow, and discharge pathways29). Although earlier discharge can lead to the comfort of home recovery and a faster return to normalcy, adequate post-discharge monitoring and home healthcare support remains essential for successful same-day discharge in revision THA patients.

This study has several potential limitations. The sample size of patients who underwent outpatient revision THA is small compared to the entire study population. As the number of revision THA increases, additional research will be required. Moreover, this study is retrospective and uses an insurance claims database, so the reliability of the results is dependent on accurate coding. It was challenging to account for all possible confounding variables as we can also only consider and stratify patients based on information already recorded in patient records. Although this limitation may raise the possibility of residual selection bias, several factors mitigate its impact on our findings. First, a large, national dataset encompassing diverse practice settings and patient populations was used, reducing the influence of single-center or surgeon-specific selection patterns. Secondly, using 1:1 matching helped control for key demographic and comorbidity variables known to influence postoperative outcomes, minimizing the likelihood that the observed results were driven by differences in the health status of patients in the inpatient versus outpatient groups. Finally, the study outcome focuses on objective, clinically meaningful endpoints (e.g., transfusion rates, mechanical complications) with clear coding definitions which are less susceptible to subjective interpretation. While unmeasured procedural details such as operative time and implant complexity cannot be fully accounted for in claims data, the validity of our conclusions is supported by the consistency of our findings with prior literature on outpatient arthroplasty safety3,20-24).

CONCLUSION

This study supports the growing body of evidence that suggests that, for select patients, outpatient revision THA may be a safe alternative to inpatient revision THA. However, as more complex cases requiring extensive revisions or higher perioperative risk may still be better suited to inpatient care, careful patient selection remains critical. As an increasing trend in outpatient revision THA is noted, it is vital to further identify patient and case specific factors that may optimize outcomes, satisfaction, safety, and value.
