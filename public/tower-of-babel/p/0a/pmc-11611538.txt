How work engagement and workaholism relate to individuals’ and their intimate partners’ mental well-being: a test of the spillover-crossover model among Indonesian dual-earner couples
Authors: Fuad HAMSYAH, Akihito SHIMAZU, Jari J HAKANEN
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the relationship between work engagement, workaholism, and mental well-being of individuals and their intimate partners. This association was explored in the context of Indonesian dual-earner couples, using the Spillover-Crossover Model (SCM). The study examined how work-to-family spillover (i.e. work-to-family conflict and facilitation) and recovery experiences (i.e. psychological detachment, relaxation, mastery, and control) mediate these relationships. A cross-sectional survey was conducted among 186 Indonesian dual-earner couples with preschool children. Structural equation modeling was used to test the hypothesized model and bootstrap method was conducted to evaluate the indirect relationships. As hypothesized, among male and female workers, work engagement was positively related to individual’s mental well-being through work-to-family facilitation and recovery experiences. In contrast, workaholism was negatively related to individual’s mental well-being through work-to-family conflict and recovery experiences excluding psychological detachment. Individual’s mental well-being, in turn, was positively related to intimate partner’s mental well-being. These findings suggested that work engagement and workaholism were related to intimate partner’s mental well-being differently. These results further supported the SCM, suggesting that higher work engagement could increase workers’ and their intimate partners’ mental well-being through work-to-family facilitation and their recovery experiences, while workaholism acts oppositely.Keywords: Mental well-being, Spillover-crossover model, Workaholism, Work engagement, Work-family balance

Introduction

Indonesia is experiencing economic growth which led to the country earning the status of an upper-middle-income state in July 20231). The working population is experiencing the evident consequences of this growth, which include a significant productive age group, rapid economic expansion, and heightened job competition. Data suggests a gradual increase in the average number of working hours per week among Indonesian workers, reaching 42 h in February 20232). This number surpasses the government-regulated maximum of 40 h per week stipulated in the Indonesian Job Creation Law No. 11 of 2020. Consequently, a rising trend of high effort in work-related state is observed and characterized by increased dedication, time commitment, as well as energy allocation to work3, 4).

Furthermore, there has been a noteworthy shift in the dynamics of Indonesian families in recent years. While past decades saw many women primarily as housewives, there is a current surge in the number of wives engaging in full-time employment. Data reveals a gradual decline in the number of housewives over time, coinciding with a significant increase in the percentage of wives employed in professional roles, reaching 50% in 2021—the highest in the last decade indicating a growing prevalence of dual-earner couples5). Unlike traditional male-breadwinner couples where only the husband works, dual-earner couples have both partners engaged in employment6). For dual-earner couples with young children, the responsibility of childcare poses a particular challenge given their busy schedules. Mothers generally dedicate more time to taking care of their children despite the increasing childcare involvement of fathers in recent years7). Moreover, the recovery process from stress experienced during the working time of both partners may be affected by childcare responsibilities, as the time available for relaxation and pursuing hobbies is consumed by attending to their children. A supportive environment from both spouses will facilitate this recovery process, while a non-supportive environment will result in a different outcome.

This present study examined how work engagement and workaholism are associated with intimate partner’s mental well-being differently among Indonesian dual-earner couples with preschool children, based on the Spillover-Crossover Model (SCM)8, 9). Starting with each work engagement and workaholism, this study examines two types of associations with intimate partner’s mental well-being, mediated by work-to-family spillover, recovery experiences, and individuals’ mental well-being. Firstly, this study contributes to the research on SCM, work engagement, workaholism, and recovery experiences. Secondly, while previous research has studied the direct relationship between work engagement, workaholism, and mental well-being10, 11), this study focuses on the mechanisms of how employee well-being may relate to general mental well-being. Thirdly, this study contributes to the gender perspective on the mechanism of the spillover-crossover processes.

The spillover-crossover model

SCM represented a theoretical framework that showed the transfer of experiences between different life domains, utilizing two distinct mechanisms: spillover and crossover. Spillover involves the transfer of demands and experiences from one domain of an individual’s life to another. In this study, the two specific domains are the work and the family domains, specifically referred to as work-to-family spillover. Work-to-family spillover manifests in two distinct forms: positive and negative12,13,14). The positive manifestation was termed Work-to-Family Facilitation (WFF), where a person’s work experiences contributed to or eased the family experiences to be more enjoyable. Conversely, the adverse manifestation of this spillover was recognized as Work-to-Family Conflict (WFC), where experiences in the work domain disrupted or created discomfort in the family domain. Crossover on the other hand referred to the transmission of experiences from an individual to another, particularly those who were closely connected9, 15). The SCM was further effectively integrated both spillover and crossover approaches within its conceptual framework.

Work engagement vs. workaholism

In a work-related state, work attitudes characterized by high dedication and strong focus from an employee which typically contributed positively to work performance were known as high effort in a work-related state3, 16). There were two main types of high effort in a work-related state namely work engagement and workaholism13, 14, 17).

These two forms of high effort in a work-related state showed contrasting characteristics. Work engagement was more voluntary, originating from an individual’s intrinsic motivation and having a positive impact. Defined as “a positive, fulfilling, work-related mental state marked by vigor, dedication, and absorption”18), work engagement comprised a positive and fulfilling approach to work. Conversely, workaholism represented a more negatively nuanced form of intensive work investment driven by compulsion. Described as “a tendency to work excessively hard and be obsessed with the job as well as manifested in compulsive working”19), workaholism reflected a less healthy and more obsessive approach to work.

Recovery experiences

Recovery experiences were not simply the activity for workers to recover from work but were psychological experiences that underlie the activity20). It proposed four key aspects associated with the recovery process comprising psychological detachment, relaxation, mastery, and control20). Psychological detachment included a subjective experience characterized by a purposeful disengagement from work-related concerns, allowing individuals to redirect their attention both physically and mentally toward non-work activities. Relaxation was linked to low-effort or effort-free activities, creating a low activation state and generating pleasant feelings associated with the pursuits21). In contrast, mastery included engaging in leisure activities beyond work hours that demanded more effort, offering opportunities to learn from challenges and experience a sense of accomplishment22). The final dimension, control, pertained to the ability to exercise influence over one’s leisure time, including possessing the expertise to determine the optimal utilization of free time and effectively implementing the selected activities23).

The hypothesized models would be tested in the study.

Based on the previous research we expect that work engagement contributed positively to work-to-family positive spillover, often referred to as WFF24). As high levels of work engagement were associated with a sense of accomplishment and reduced work-related stress, it is likely to facilitate a more positive transition to family roles. While numerous research have investigated the positive effects of the recovery experience, there is a notable gap in understanding its antecedents25, 26). Based on the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory27) and the Work-Home Resources (W-HR) model28), it was posited that WFF supported the daily recovery process for individuals28, 29). WFF further provided opportunities for unwinding and reducing inter-role conflict within a supportive family environment. This support enabled individuals to mentally disconnect from work, take control of their leisure time, and engage in family activities or hobbies. Moreover, participating in recovery activities, such as disconnecting from work, unwinding, and pursuing enjoyable non-work activities could alleviate the adverse effects of work conditions and enhance the mental well-being of employees20, 30, 31). Therefore, we predict:

Hypothesis 1 (H1). Work engagement is positively related to individual’s mental well-being through WFF and recovery experiences.

On the contrary, we expect that workaholism led to negative repercussions on work-to-family spillover, termed as WFC9). The excessive and compulsive participation in work could lead to an imbalance between the responsibilities of work and family, arising from the difficulties associated with detaching from work-related thoughts and pressures9). Furthermore, following Hobfoll’s COR perspective27), WFC could hinder the daily recovery process by disrupting the balance between work and family responsibilities. This interference hampered the effective recuperation process by restricting the availability of time, energy, and opportunities for essential recovery mechanisms such as relaxation and revitalization. As recovery experiences were intricately connected to mental well-being, the limited occurrence of recovery experiences tended to lead to a diminished level of associated mental well-being. Therefore, we predict:

Hypothesis 2 (H2). Workaholism is negatively related to individual’s mental well-being through WFC and recovery experiences.

Following the theorized spillover process, a crossover process could occur from individuals to intimate partners as observed in this study. This crossover process could be explained by the emotion contagion mechanism that generally occurred in intimate social interactions such as married couples. Emotion contagion included a complex interplay of cognitive, psychological, physiological, behavioral, and social factors between closely related individuals32). While numerous research has primarily explored the negative crossover, there was also potential for a direct or indirect positive crossover that influenced the well-being of the partner33).

This study further contributed to the existing literature on the SCM by examining both positive and negative spillover processes originating from work engagement and workaholism as various forms of high effort in a work-related state13, 14), which subsequently acting as antecedents to recovery experiences. The investigation then extended to assess the impact of recovery experiences on mental well-being, eventually influencing intimate partner’s mental well-being.

Therefore, we predict:

Hypothesis 3 (H3). Work engagement is positively related to intimate partner’s mental well-being through WFF, recovery experiences, and individual’s mental well-being.

Hypothesis 4 (H4). Workaholism is negatively related to intimate partner’s mental well-being through WFC, recovery experiences, and individual’s mental well-being.

Method

Procedure and participants

This study was conducted in January 2022 in Indonesia with the data obtained online through the internet. This study used a non-random sampling method, specifically a snowball sampling technique. An announcement including Survey Monkey was made and spread through emails and social media platforms such as Instagram, WhatsApp, Twitter, and Facebook.

The participants in this study were Indonesian full-time married employees also known as dual-earner couples, with preschool children and a minimum of 40 h per week of working hours. Participants were asked to fill out the questionnaire individually, independently, and voluntarily. Furthermore, there was no penalty for not completing the survey and participants had the right to refuse to answer any question in the survey. Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee at Keio University, as they approved the procedures before starting the study (No. 389). The objective of this study was provided at the initial stage of the survey as part of the information for participants. The consent of participants was also verified through a review of their agreements and those who did not provide consent would not have access to the survey.

Responses from 1,027 participants were obtained and 740 completed the survey leading to a response rate of 72.3%. To enable matching between husband-and-wife data, participants were asked to fill in the same initials or pseudonyms as spouses and to provide the date of their marriage. From the survey, 186 matched couples (372 participants), were identified and used in the final analysis. Further participant details of the study were provided in Table 1.

Measures

The measures included work engagement, workaholism, work-to-family facilitation, work-to-family conflict, recovery experiences, and mental well-being.

Work Engagement was assessed using the short form of the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES)17), which was validated in the Indonesian version34). The UWES consisted of three subscales, namely vigor, dedication, and absorption. The subscales had three items each, namely “At my work, I feel bursting with energy” (vigor), “I am proud of my work” (dedication), and “I get carried away when I am working” (absorption). Every item was scored with a Likert scale of seven points from 0 (never) to 6 (always). The score was the total of all 9-item responses from three subscales to form an overall index of work engagement.

Workaholism was assessed using the Indonesian short version of the Dutch Work Addiction Scale (DUWAS)35). The DUWAS consisted of ten items divided into two subscales Working Excessively (WE) and Working Compulsively (WC). WE had five items (e.g., I seem to be in a hurry and racing against the clock), and WC possessed five items (e.g., I feel guilty when I take time off work). Every item on this scale was scored with a Likert scale of four points from 1 (never) to 4 (always). The score was the total figure of all ten item responses from two subscales to form an overall index of workaholism.

Work-to-family conflict was assessed using the work-to-family conflict sub-scale from the translated Survey Work-home Interaction-NijmeGen (SWING)36). The scale comprised eight items (e.g., You are irritable at home because your work is demanding). Every item on this scale was scored with a Likert scale of four points from 0 (never) to 3 (always). The score used in this scale was the total figure of eight items.

Work-to-family facilitation was also assessed using the work-to-family facilitation sub-scale from the translated SWING12). The scale comprised five items (e.g., After a pleasant working day/week, you feel more in the mood to engage in activities with your spouse/family/friends). Every item on this scale was scored with a Likert scale of four points from 0 (never) to 3 (always). The figure used in this scale was the total score of five items.

Recovery experiences were measured using the Recovery Experiences Questionnaire20), which was validated in the Indonesian version31). There were sixteen questionnaire items divided into four subscales, namely psychological detachment (e.g., I don’t think about work at all), relaxation (e.g., I kick back and relax), mastery (e.g., I learn new things), and control (e.g., I decide my own schedule). Each of these subscales consisted of four items and was scored with a Likert scale of five points from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The score used in the scale was a total subscale figure of all four items.

Mental well-being was assessed using the Indonesian version of the WHO-5 questionnaire36, 37) with five items asking respondents to rate their general psychological/mental well-being within the past two weeks (e.g. I have felt calm and relaxed). The items were scored on a six-point Likert scale from 0 (at no time) to 5 (all the time), and the figure used was the total score of all five item responses from the scale.

Demographic characteristics included age, gender, working sector, education, number of preschool children, and having assistance in childcare. During the survey’s opening, all participants were requested to provide certain information which was included as variables in the analyses.

Analyses

The matched couples data of this study were analyzed with Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) techniques, using the maximum likelihood method of estimation for the covariance matrix analysis. To avoid an overly complex model due to various items, all model variables were included as manifest, i.e., there is only a single indicator for a particular construct representing the mean of each variable38).

The chi-squared (χ2) goodness-of-fit statistic, the Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI), the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), the Incremental Fit Index (IFI), and the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) were examined to assess the model fit of the data. For each of these statistics, values of 0.90 were acceptable and 0.95 or higher were also indicative of a good fit. Furthermore, RMSEA values of <0.08 indicated a close fit between the hypothesized model and data39, 40). To test the hypotheses, individual paths were observed to be significant, as well as indirect relationships. The guidelines of Augunis et al.41) were followed for assessing mediation effects, specifically the mediation test without requiring that the relationship between the predictor and the outcome should be significant. Additionally, the study used the bias-corrected bootstrap approach to assess the statistical significance of the indirect effect42). For descriptive statistics and SEM analysis, IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 and Amos 24 for Windows were adopted respectively.

In terms of the number of children and having assistance in childcare, most couples had a single child (70.4%) and had assistance in childcare (79.0%). Regarding working hours, all participants were full-time workers with a minimum of 40 h of working time per week.

This was consistent with H3 for both husbands-to-wives and wives-to-husbands models.

For workaholism and partner’s mental well-being, the study tested the indirect relationship through WFC, recovery experiences, and individual’s mental well-being (H4). The bootstrap method showed that the indirect relationships between the two variables were significant in every recovery experience sub-scale except for psychological detachment for both husbands-to-wives and wives-to-husbands as observed in Table 4. This was consistent with H4 for both husbands to wives and wives to husbands models, except for psychological detachment as a mediator.

In the next step, additional analyses to control for educational level and availability of childcare assistance as potential confounder were conducted one by one. Specifically, each control variable was included in the models as a manifest variable and allowed to correlate with all model variables. After controlling for educational level and availability of childcare assistance one by one, the path coefficients were virtually the same as those of the proposed models, while the model fit decreased in every models. These results suggest that the control variables did not have an impact on the model variables. Only educational level as a control variable affected the recovery experiences variables in five of a total sixteen models. It affected work engagement husbands-to-wives models (β=−0.18, p<0.05 for psychological detachment; β=−0.18, p<0.05 for mastery) and workaholism husbands-to-wives models (β=−0.18, p<0.05 for psychological detachment; β=−0.15, p<0.05 for relaxation; β=−0.22, p<0.01 for mastery). Thus, the control variables were excluded from the final model in Fig. 2. The relevant findings are available upon request from the authors.

To test the gender differences in the strength of the relationships in the models, constraint multiple group analyses were conducted. Results showed that there were significant differences between husbands and wives in the models, specifically in the relationship of work engagement to individual’s mental well-being (Δx2 (1)=7.41, p<0.01 for psychological detachment; Δx2 (1)=6.47, p<0.05 for relaxation; Δx2 (1)=4.42, p<0.05 for mastery; and Δx2 (1)=4.68, p<0.05 for control). Furthermore, in the relationship of workaholism to WFC (Δx2 (1)=8.71, p<0.01 for psychological detachment; Δx2 (1)=6.56, p<0.05 for relaxation; Δx2 (1)=6.00, p<0.05 for mastery; and Δx2 (1)=7.04, p<0.01 for control).

Discussion

The study investigates the dynamic spillover-crossover relationships between Indonesian workers’ work engagement and workaholism, and the mental well-being of their own and their intimate partners. Notably, there is a limited body of spillover and crossover research specifically in the Indonesian context, and this study marks the first exploration among dual-earner couples in the country to assess the spillover and crossover effects of work engagement and workaholism on the mental well-being of intimate partners. The hypothesis posits that work engagement yields positive outcomes, while workaholism has adverse effects on both the workers’ and their intimate partners’ mental well-being.

This study contributes to the research on work engagement, workaholism, work-to-family spiillover, recovery experiences, and mental well-being in several ways. First, it incorporates work engagement and workaholism, as the starting point of the model. Second, the study comprises both positive and negative spillovers (WFF and WFC) along with various recovery experiences (i.e., psychological detachment, relaxation, mastery, and control) as mediator variables. Third, the study explores the association between work engagement and workaholism, and individual’s mental well-being through spillover (WFF and WFC) as well as recovery experiences. Lastly, the study investigates the relationship between work engagement and workaholism, and the mental well-being of intimate partners through spillover (WFF and WFC), recovery experiences, and the individual’s mental well-being. Additionally, demographic variables were examined and gender differences in the hypothesized relationship were explored.

Contributions

The results show a positive association between work engagement and WFF for both genders. Workers who exhibit elevated levels of energy, enthusiasm, and absorption experience higher levels of positive mood18), potentially translating the positive energies and drive from the workplace to their homes. This phenomenon contributes to fostering a more motivating and pleasant family atmosphere13, 14). WFF serves as a positive interconnection between the two domains increasing the probability of individual’s recovery. This finding represents a noteworthy theoretical contribution to existing research. Grounded in the Conservation of Resources (COR) theory27) and the Work-Home Resources (W-HR) model28), WFF supports workers’ daily recovery processes28, 29). Furthermore, engaging in recovery experiences mitigates the negative impact of working conditions and enhances workers’ mental well-being20, 30, 31). Eventually, the crossover process of mental well-being from individuals to their intimate partners within a marital or romantic relationship is influenced. In the domain of intimate social connections, affect contagion or emotion contagion is considered a mechanism that explains this crossover32, 44).

On the other hand, workaholism demonstrated a positive association with WFC in both genders. Individuals with workaholic tendencies often possess an intrinsic compulsion to be excessively and compulsively involved in their work45). This intense involvement can lead to an imbalance between work and family roles, originating from the challenges of disengaging from work-related thoughts and pressures9). According to COR theory by Hobfoll27), the negative interference between work and family roles impedes effective recovery by limiting the time, energy, and opportunities available for relaxation and rejuvenation as forms of recovery. As discussed earlier, recovery experiences exhibit a positive correlation with mental well-being. However, due to the low level of recovery experiences in this workaholism model, the associated mental well-being tends to be diminished. This reduced level of mental well-being influences the intimate partners’ mental well-being through a crossover process, employing the emotion contagion mechanism.

Similar to previous research13, 14), the study shows distinctive spillover-crossover pathways for work engagement and workaholism. This discovery aligns with the Passion Dualistic Model, the concept of harmonious vs obsessive passion proposed by Vallerand46). Individuals with harmonious passion (i.e. work engaged workers) integrate their passion into their lives and enhance their overall well-being, while individuals with obsessive passion (i.e. workaholics) experience internal pressure to engage in their activity leading to negative outcomes. Individuals exhibiting harmonious passion were engaged in meaningful and enjoyable work aligned with their values, leading to positive outcomes in work-life balance, recovery experiences, and overall positive affect. Conversely, those with obsessive passion may encounter internal conflict and feel controlled by their work activities, resulting in negative outcomes such as work-life imbalance, poor recovery, and diminished overall well-being.

Although the results generally align with our hypothesis, there is an unexpected discovery regarding psychological detachment as a recovery experience which is contingent on the initiation point of the model. In the model of work engagement as a starter, psychological detachment was positively related to individual’s mental well-being and acted as a mediator in the model. In contrast, psychological detachment was not related to individual’s mental well-being and did not function as a mediator in the model initiated with workaholism. Jimenez et al.47) assert that psychological detachment represents a pure form of off-job work-related thought, devoid of specified valence, unlike positive work-related thoughts (PWRTs) and negative work-related thoughts (NRWTs) which carry valence. Jimenez et al.47) also found that psychological detachment negatively correlates with NWRTs but is unrelated to PWRTs. These two types of off-job work-related thoughts, PWRTs and NWRTs, exhibit no correlation. This suggested that psychological detachment may pertain more to disengaging from negative work experiences. Meanwhile, individuals classified as workaholics exhibit an intense obsession with their jobs often engaging in excessive and compulsive work habits. Consequently, they struggle to disengage from work-related thoughts, even when those thoughts involve negative experiences. Further research are required to provide a clearer understanding of the relationship between workaholism and psychological detachment within dual-earner couples. The study further explores a model excluding the direct path from workaholism to mental well-being, finding that WFC and psychological detachment could act as mediators. However, the configuration was not adopted due to a significant decline in model fit. Future research should delve into psychological detachment, NWRTs, and PWRTs in the context of workaholism within dual-earner couples.

In addition, this study yielded a notable finding regarding gender differences. Husbands had stronger relationships between workaholism and WFC, as well as between work engagement and individual’s mental well-being compared to wives. Firstly, results showed a strong path coefficient from workaholism to WFC among husbands (β>0.8, p<0.001). Further analyses, CFA (confirmatory factor analysis) and EFA (exploratory factor analysis) confirmed that while there was some overlap between certain items of workaholism (e.g., I feel guilty when I take time off work) and WFC (e.g., You are irritable at home because your work is demanding), they did not fully collapse into a single factor. The study also found a strong correlation between working excessively (e.g., I find myself continuing to work after my co-workers have called it quits) as one aspect of workaholism with WFC (r=0.76). Secondly, husbands also exhibited stronger effects between work engagement and individual’s mental well-being. These strong associations between work engagement and individual’s mental well-being, as well as between workaholism and WFC among husbands can be influenced by Indonesia’s patriarchal culture that has developed over a long period of time48). This culture is where husbands as breadwinners, are expected to work diligently sometimes at the expense of family life. This patrilineal kinship system imposes social expectations on women, emphasizing a greater focus on their family roles than on their professional responsibilities49), particularly for those with preschool-aged children. The demands of both work and family can lead to conflicts between these two domains50), particularly among women who concurrently juggle the roles of workers, wives, and mothers. Additionally, patriarchal culture creates disparities in access to advanced career opportunities for male and female workers, leading to income differences51, 52). Consequently, it is possible that among working mothers in Indonesia, the spillover from work to family may not be as strong as it is for their working husbands. Further research is recommended to explore this speculation on the impact of patriarchal culture in dual-earner couples in terms of gender roles, career gap, and income differences between male and female workers.

Regarding childcare assistance, Indonesian couples depend on close relatives or daycare centers for childcare53). Many research show the demand for external help, like childcare services, has risen as these families find it challenging to manage childcare responsibilities alone and home-based childcare services have been recognized as a protective factor against stress for dual-earner couples54). For dual earner couples, having assistance in childcare can make a big difference. They can successfully manage their work and parenting responsibilities, when childcare services are available55). Childcare assistance can help dual-earner couples better manage their work and family responsibilities. Although dual-earner couples aim for a more balanced division of labor, gender disparities in household tasks persist. Implementing policies and interventions to support dual-earner families in balancing work and family life is crucial for the well-being of both partners and their children.

Strengths and limitations

This study offers several strengths, including a study design that investigates and matches data from dual-earner couples. Furthermore, the study contributes to the advancement of theories related to work engagement, workaholism, the balance between work and family life, recovery experiences, mental well-being, and the Indonesian cultural context on these topics.

Several limitations required to be discussed are found in this study. First, the participant pool is relatively small, which is a common constraint in crossover research given the busy schedules of dual-earner couples. Future research is encouraged to enhance validity by recruiting a larger participant sample. Second, this is a self-reported questionnaire survey, making the study susceptible to common method bias. Consequently, the actual correlation between the variables may be less robust than indicated by the reported results. Third, the study was conducted within the unique cultural and societal context of dual-earner couples in Indonesia, necessitating caution in generalizing the results. Similar research in diverse cultural settings is recommended to broaden the understanding of these dynamics. Fourth, the study employs a non-random snowball sampling method, resulting in a participant pool that is less diverse and more inclined toward individuals with higher education levels. Consequently, the results may be biased and less generalizable. To address this limitation, future research should consider using a random sampling method or aim for a more balanced demographic representation. Fifth, the cross-sectional nature of the study limits the ability to establish causal relationships between variables. It remains unclear whether work engagement and workaholism are outcomes or the cause of recovery experiences and mental well-being. While the study’s hypotheses were supported, the spillover and crossover processes require further exploration and description.

Conclusion

By testing a highly complex spillover-crossover model we could indicate how employee work engagement and workaholism, demonstrated contrasting relationships with individual’s mental well-being and intimate partner’s mental well-being. This was observed through the mediating factors of work-to-family facilitation, work-to-family conflict, and recovery experiences. These results align with the spillover-crossover model, indicating that reducing workaholism and enhancing work engagement can have a dual impact. Not only did the study contribute to increased personal well-being for employees, but it may also improve the mental well-being of their intimate partners.
