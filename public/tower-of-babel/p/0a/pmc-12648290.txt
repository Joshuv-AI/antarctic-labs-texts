Optimizing Electronic Topology of Curvature-Induced Cu–N2 Asymmetric Structure for Maximized Cu Utilization in CH3SH Photo-oxidation
Authors: Tao Zhong, Zhangnan Yao, Ting Li, Xianhu Long, Huinan Zhao, Shuanghong Tian, Dong Shu, Chun He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The highly symmetrical M-N4 structure with dispersed atoms exhibits a symmetric charge distribution, which leads to strong binding with adsorbed molecules but reduces active site accessibility and limits electron utilization. Herein, the curvature effect of carbon nitride nanotubes (CNNTs) was employed to modulate the geometric and electronic topologies of curved Cu–N2 asymmetric motifs. Surface stress induced the formation of unique “Cu protuberance” on CNNT, resulting in a maximum Cu atom dispersion of 5.66%, 13.8 times higher than that of the planar Cu-melon structure (0.41%). Additionally, the Cu–N2 site acts as a reservoir for electrons, enhancing the accessibility of Cu sites. This improved electronic interaction with CH3SH decreased electron occupancy in the Cu 3d antibonding orbital and induced unconventional d-p hybridization between Cu and S atoms, thereby improving CH3SH photo-oxidation efficiency. Consequently, Cu-CNNT-400 with the optimal curvature radius exhibited the highest CH3SH removal efficiency (90.4%), demonstrating a volcano-like relationship between catalytic activity and nanotube diameter. Importantly, maximizing Cu atom utilization can effectively reduce the required amount of Cu precursors and lower catalyst costs. This study provides valuable insights into precise catalyst design for maximizing metal atom utilization in gaseous contaminant removal.

Introduction

Methyl mercaptan (CH3SH) is a typical sulfur-containing volatile organic compounds (S-VOCs) that poses a serious threat to human health and the ecological environment. , Heterogeneous photocatalytic oxidation (HPO) has been considered as a feasible and environmentally friendly technology for S-VOCs elimination due to its straightforward operation, sustainability, controllable reaction conditions, and lack of secondary pollution. , However, the slow kinetic process of photogenerated electron migration as well as the nonessential thermodynamic process energy loss hinder the further practical application of HPO technology. ,

Recently, graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4) based photocatalysts have made great progress. , Metal nanoparticles (NPs) as cocatalysts can effectively improve the charge separation efficiency and catalytic activity. However, metal NPs with larger sized not only diminish the atomic utilization efficiency of the metal, but also exhibit suboptimal catalytic intrinsic activity. Due to the emphasis on atom economy, anchoring atomically dispersed metals (ADM) on g-C3N4 substrate can increase the number of active sites. , Previous studies have shown that ADM loaded on g-C3N4 substrate can construct a highly symmetrical M-C/N structure, achieving effective utilization of electrons and significantly improving photocatalytic activity. , However, it is noteworthy that most g-C3N4-based atomically dispersed catalysts exhibit a planar porphyrin-like structure (M-N4) with highly symmetrical metal centers and uniform electron distribution, which is not conducive to breaking the linear scaling relationship between the adsorption and desorption of complex multi intermediates. How to rationally design the fine structure of catalysts to achieve rapid electron transfer and maximize utilization of metal atoms are key challenges.

On the other hand, the adsorption and activation of reactants are directly related to the electronic state or atomic configuration of the active center in heterogeneous catalysis. Therefore, a feasible approach is to further develop geomorphic engineering of g-C3N4 to optimize the electronic topology structure, precisely control surface polarization and coordination environments to facilitate electron transfer via the “symmetry breaking” strategy. − Unlike traditional planar structure, highly anisotropic low-dimensional carbon nitride nanotubes (CNNTs) exhibit fascinating characteristics and offer multiple advantages as follows: (1) The “curvature effect” of CNNTs can easily induce the formation of asymmetric curvature M-N coordination motifs, which is more conducive to modulating the electronic configuration of active sites; (2) The lone electron pairs of strongly electronegative N atoms in CNNTs can accommodate metal atoms and form an appropriate electronic metal–support interaction (EMSI) effect. And the formation of asymmetric protuberance on the curved surfaces is more likely to converge photogenerated charges, further enhancing the electron interactions between adsorbed molecules; (3) The anisotropic resistance of CNNTs hinders charge movement in the radial direction, while the asymmetric of M-N motifs enhance surface polarization, which significantly enhanced the molecular mass transfer ability and enhanced the accessibility of metal site. This potentially optimizes the catalytic reaction not only in terms of electron transfer pathway but also by achieving the maximum ultilization of metal atoms. To our knowledge, the electronic topological and geometric characteristics of metal sites and the critical role in photocatalytic reactions are still not fully understood.

Herein, the unique architecture of Cu-CNNT-X catalysts containing atomically dispersed Cu sites were synthesized through a simple two-step pyrolysis and solvothermal method based on the formation and post coordination process of CNNTs, and further applied for photocatalytic CH3SH oxidation. KPFM tests and theoretical calculations were used to assess the facilitation of charge transfer by an enhanced BIEF, as well as comprehensively understand the structure–property relationship of distorted Cu center in Cu-CNNT-400. Furthermore, special surface area tests and FEM simulations were employed to reveal CH3SH mass transfer and O2 diffusion behaviors. CO pulse chemisorption was carried out to determine the dispersion of Cu atoms and molecular accessibility. This study pioneers a strategy to modulate the electronic topology of active sites with maximized metal atom utilization and opens new avenues for S-VOC disposal.

Experimental Section

Preparation of Cu-CNNT-X Photocatalyst

A 100.0 mg portion of CNNT-X (where X represents the outer diameter of the CNNTs) was dissolved in 16.0 mL of EG solution and sonicated for 0.5 h. Then, 5.0 mL of CuCl2/EG solution (2.0 mg L–1) was added to the mixture to form a suspension and further stirred for 12 h. Subsequently, the solution was transferred to a 100 mL Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 130 °C for 3 h. After they were cooled to room temperature, the resulting light-yellow samples were washed with DI and ethanol and then dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 12 h. The final products were collected and labeled as Cu-CNNT-X (X = 100, 400, and 1000 nm). The yields of Cu-CNNT-100, Cu-CNNT-400 and Cu-CNNT-1000 were 48.0%, 50.0%, and 46.6%.

Photocatalytic Activity Analysis

Photocatalytic CH3SH oxidation was performed in a fixed-bed quartz photoreactor under atmospheric pressure (Scheme S1). A 50.0 mg amount of catalyst was fixed between quartz wool plugs inside the photoreactor. The reactant gas mixture (40.0 ppm of CH3SH in air, total flow rate = 100 mL min–1) was fed to the reactor and the passed through the catalyst for 30 min to reach the adsorption equilibrium. And a 300 W xenon lamp (PLS-SXE 300/300 UV, Perfect Light, China) with full-wavelength emission was used to simulate AM 1.5G illumination (100 mW cm–2), directed from the top of the quartz reactor, 12 cm from the reactor window. The gas hourly space velocity (GHSV) equals 120 000 mL h–1 g–1. The CH3SH conversion (η), Cu utilization efficiency (μ) and mineralization rate (τ) were calculated by the following equation: 1 η(%)=(1−CtC0)×100% 2 μ(%)=ηCucontent×Cudispersion# 3 τ(%)=CCO2,outletCCH3SH,inlet×100% Where C t, C 0 represent the concentration of CH3SH at the outlet and the initial concentration, respectively. The catalyst after the reaction was recovered and washed with ethanol and DI water, dried, and subjected to cyclic experiments.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Structure Analysis of the Catalysts

The Cu-CNNT-X catalysts were synthesized via a solvothermal method using CNNT and Cu precursors. The periodic pyridinic nitrogen atoms within the CNNT framework provide lone electron pairs that serve as coordination sites, enabling the formation of Cu–N2 coordination structures (Scheme S2). Notably, the diameter of the CNNT is inversely correlated to curvature (Scheme S3), resulting in different degrees of surface tension experienced by the anchored Cu atoms. This variation allows for the modulation of the coordination environment around the Cu centers. The diameter of Cu-CNNT-X samples was approximately 100 nm (Figure S1a, S2a), 400 nm (Figure S1b, S2b) and 1000 nm (Figure S1c, S2c). All nanotubes exhibited a uniform size and smooth, crack-free surfaces, suggesting that CNNTs can anchor Cu atoms via the curvature effect, with no additional curvature features exceeding the tube diameter evident in the structures. Furthermore, no metal nanoparticles were observed on Cu-CNNT-400 (Figure a and Figure S3). High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) was conducted to discern atomically dispersed Cu species (Figure b). The small bright dots circled in red refer to Cu single atom species in photocatalyst Cu-CNNT-400. Meanwhile, energy dispersion X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) mappings (Figure c) signified the homogeneous distribution of C, N, Cu atoms in Cu-CNNT-400. The atomic dispersion of Cu atoms is beneficial for enhancing the EMSI effect and increasing the utilization efficiency of Cu atoms. The volumes of melon, Cu-CNNT-100, Cu-CNNT-400, and Cu-CNNT-1000 were approximately 0.1, 0.6, 0.7, and 0.8 cm3 per 50.0 mg, respectively (Figure d). The Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area and pore volume of Cu-CNNT-400 were measured to be 107.93 m2 g–1 and 0.0079 cm3 g–1, which are markedly higher than those of Cu-CNNT-100 (51.36 m2 g–1, 0.0062 cm3 g–1) and Cu-CNNT-1000 (40.79 m2 g–1, 0.0038 cm3 g–1) (Figure S4a, b). The higher specific surface area and pore volume of Cu-CNNT-400 are expected to significantly enhance its catalytic activity for two reasons: (1) during thermal polymerization, CNNT-400 develops an increased density of surface defects that facilitate anchoring of metal atoms and thereby raise the number of catalytically active sites; and (2) the larger pore volume concentrates reactant molecules within the pore network, producing steeper local concentration gradients that enhance mass transport to active sites and accelerate reaction kinetics.

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) of CNNT-X exhibited characteristic peaks at 13.2° and 27.5° corresponding to the (100) and (002) facets of g-C3N4 (Figure S5a). Furthermore, no diffraction peaks corresponding to Cu species were observed in Cu-CNNT-X samples (Figure e), indicating that the Cu atoms exhibit atomic-level dispersion. As shown in Figure f, the stretching vibration peak of Cu-CNNT-X closely matched those of CNNT-X (Figure S5b), indicating the structural stability of the CN matrix. Interestingly, the stretching vibrations of the C–N and CN heterocyclic rings of Cu-CNNT-400 were higher than those of Cu-CNNT-100 and Cu-CNNT-1000, indicating a greater proportion of the Cu–N2 asymmetric structure in Cu-CNNT-400. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was performed to characterized the defects of catalysts. All catalysts exhibited a Lorentzian signal centered at g = 2.002 (Figure g), attributed to the unpaired electrons within the delocalized π-conjugated structure. The higher peak intensity observed for Cu-CNNT-400 indicated a greater number of carbon defects, which serve as additional unpaired electron anchors for Cu atoms and effectively modulate the electronic environment of the Cu sites.

The elemental composition and chemical states of related catalysts were investigated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). And the survey spectra contained only C, N, and O elements. However, due to the low Cu content in prepared catalysts, no distinct Cu 2p peaks were observed in the XPS spectra (Figure S6a, b). Hence, the Cu content was quantitatively analyzed by ICP-MS, and the results showed the similar mass ratio of Cu in Cu-CNNT-100 (0.10 wt %), Cu-CNNT-400 (0.12 wt %), Cu-CNNT-1000 (0.09 wt %), and Cu-melon (0.08 wt %) (Table S1), indicating an equivalent number of active sites across various catalyst. The C 1s XPS spectra could be divided into four peaks, representing sp2 hybridized C–C bonds (284.8 eV), C-NH x (285.5 eV), N–CN (288.2 eV) and C–O bonds (289.3 eV) (Figure h). And the peaks of N 1s spectrum were deconvoluted into four peaks representing sp2 hybridized nitrogen in C–NC (398.8 eV), bridged nitrogen in N-(C)3 (400.3 eV), C-NH x groups (401.5 eV) and π-π excitation (404.5 eV) (Figure i). The area ratio of the C–O peak to the N–CN peak in Cu-CNNT-400 (0.102) was lower than those in Cu-CNNT-100 (0.139) and Cu-CNNT-1000 (0.156). In contrast, the area ratio of the C–NC peak to that of the nitrogen-containing species remained almost unchanged. These results revealed the formation of carbon-deficient atomic sites that primarily locate at N–CN (Figure S7). In the O 1s XPS spectra, two characteristic peaks at 532.3 and 533.6 eV correspond to surface O–H and C–O bonds (Figure j), respectively.

Atomic Structure Analysis

Furthermore, X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) was performed to further study the local coordination environment and the electronic structure of Cu. The Cu K-edge X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) disclosed that the Cu-CNNT-400 was located between Cu2O and CuO (Figure a), demonstrating that Cu species are maintained in mixed valence state between Cu1+ and Cu2+ (Figure S8a). A quantitative analysis of the XANES spectra was conducted using linear combination fitting (LCF) methodology to determine the proportion of different Cu species. The results indicated that Cu atoms in Cu-CNNT-400 were present as Cu1+ (0.581 ± 0.033) and Cu2+ (0.414 ± 0.027). Furthermore, there was almost no metallic Cu0 (0.005 ± 0.016), confirming that Cu0 was not present in Cu-CNNT-400 (Figure S8b). The extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectrum showed that Cu-CNNT-400 hold a dominant Cu–N coordination (1.49 Å) (Figure b) and no Cu–Cu coordination (2.15 Å), suggesting that Cu atoms are coordinated with unsaturated N sites and have an atomically dispersed structure (Figure c,d). , Wavelet transformation (WT) revealed that Cu-CNNT-400 had quite different positions of the maximum intensity of Cu foil, CuO, and Cu2O (Figure e-h), also indicating the presence of Cu–N coordination in Cu-CNNT-400. The EXAFS fitting curve revealed that the distinct peak at 1.88 ± 0.10 Å was assigned to Cu–N coordination. Notably, the coordination number (CN) of Cu–N was 1.72 ± 0.18, providing the evidence for the formation of N–Cu–N asymmetric motifs (Figure i and Table S2).

CO pulse adsorption experiments were conducted to evaluate the dispersion of the Cu atoms. As shown in Figure j,k, both Cu-melon and Cu-CNNT-400 reached saturation of Cu sites after a single CO pulse, attributed to the low Cu loading. However, the pulse intensity of Cu-CNNT-400 was higher than that of Cu-melon, reflecting stronger CO adsorption due to the higher dispersion of Cu sites in CNNT-400. Previous studies have established that the amount of chemisorbed CO correlates directly with the dispersion of active sites. Precise calculations revealed that the Cu atom dispersion in Cu-CNNT-400 was 13.8 times greater than that in Cu-melon (Figure l), indicating that more Cu atoms are exposed on the surface of CNNT-400. Additionally, the Cu surface area in Cu-CNNT-400 was 4.95 times larger than that in Cu-melon (Figure m), which optimized the structure of the Cu active sites and enhanced their interaction with the substrate.

Photocatalytic CH3SH Removal Performance

The mixture gas was circulated for 30 min to establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium before the photocatalytic reaction (Figure a). The catalytic performance of pure melon was only 18.3%, while the catalytic performance of Cu-melon was still unsatisfactory at 43.6% (Figure S9). On the contrary, CNNT-400 demonstrated superior CH3SH removal efficiency (73.2%) compared to CNNT-100 (50.8%), CNNT-1000 (72.3%) and melon (18.3%), indicating that the optimized CNNT tubular structure enhances the physical diffusion of CH3SH (Figure b,c). Upon anchoring Cu, the removal efficiency of Cu-CNNT-400 increased significantly to 90.4%, outperforming Cu-CNNT-100 (78.1%), Cu-CNNT-1000 (87.4%), and Cu-melon (43.6%), as well as exceedingly most previously reported photocatalysts in similar applications (Table S3). Furthermore, Cu-CNNT-400 exhibits the highest TOF among the catalysts listed in Table S4, demonstrating that tuning the CNNT radius of curvature can significantly enhance the intrinsic reactivity of Cu-CNNT catalysts. The effect of active sites on the photocatalytic activity was evaluated by varying the photocatalyst dosage. A linear correlation was observed between adsorption capacity, catalytic performance, and catalyst dosage, indicating that increasing the catalyst amount significantly raises the number of active sites, thereby enhancing the catalytic performance (Figure d and Figure S10a). The competition absorption between H2O and CH3SH was investigated under different relative humidity (RH) conditions. Cu-CNNT-400 showed only a minimal decrease in removal efficiency as RH increased from 0% to 15% (Figure e), indicating limited competition for adsorption sites between H2O and CH3SH. However, the removal efficiency gradually declined as RH rose further to 40% and 65%, dropping from 88.4% to 68.8%, accompanied by a significant decrease in adsorption capacity (Figure S10b). These results suggested that H2O molecules occupy the active sites at higher humidity levels, thereby inhibiting the photocatalytic activity. Meanwhile, the Cu-CNNT-400 catalyst demonstrated excellent cyclic stability (Figure f): after eight consecutive reaction-regeneration cycles, its catalytic performance remained essentially unchanged. TEM, XRD and FTIR analyses (Figure S11a-c) showed no significant structural changes after reaction. Notably, EXAFS analysis (Figure S12 and Figure S13) indicated a slight increase in the oxidation state and coordination number of Cu atoms after reaction, which may be attributed to adsorption of trace intermediates at the Cu sites. Nevertheless, the Cu–N bond lengths (1.88 ± 0.10 Å) remained essentially unchanged before and after the reaction. This consistency reflected that the atomic dispersion and local coordination environment of the Cu sites are maintained throughout the reaction, confirming that the Cu-CNNT-400 catalyst possesses excellent structural stability.

The cleanliness of the exhaust gas and the synthesis cost are key indicators for evaluating the environmental and economic benefits. The highest CO2 yield observed with Cu-CNNT-400 indicated a greater degree of CH3SH mineralization (Figure g), effectively preventing carbon accumulation on the catalyst surface and maintaining high catalytic activity and stability. The Cu utilization efficiency in Cu-CNNT-400 was 18.88 times higher than that in Cu-melon (Figure h), attributable to the high dispersion and improved accessibility of Cu atoms. Moreover, Cu-CNNT-400 was 50% less expensive than Cu-melon for removing the same amount of CH3SH (Figure i), demonstrating its cost-effectiveness.

Photogenerated Charge Separation and Transfer Analysis

Optical and electrochemical characterization were performed to elucidate the origin of the superior photocatalytic activities of prepared catalysts. Figure a and Figure S14 showed the UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectra (DRS) of all the catalysts. A slight red shift of the absorption edge and a significant change in the absorption tail were observed after Cu anchoring. These changes can be attributed to two main factors: (1) intrinsic absorption induced by the π → π* electronic transition of C defects as well as the n → π* transition of lone pair electrons at defect sites, which effectively regulate the light absorption capacity; (2) enhanced photon energy utilization due to refraction and reflection of incident light within the tube. According to the Tauc plot, the bandgaps of the catalysts were calculated as 2.72, 2.76, 2.76, 2.73, 2.75, and 2.75 eV (Figure b and Figure S15a). And the flat-band potential (E fb) was determined to be −0.53, −0.37, −0.41, −0.50, −0.50 and −0.60 eV (Vs. Ag/AgCl) through Mott–Schottky plots (Figure c and Figure S15c). The positive slopes of Mott–Schottky curves indicated typical n-type semiconductor properties, with the conduction band (CB) potentially being more than 0.2 eV negative compared to the flat band potential. Therefore, the CB was calculated to be −0.53, −0.37, −0.41, −0.50, −0.50 and −0.60 eV (vs NHE), respectively.

Open circuit voltage (OCV) measurements were used to evaluate the built-in electric field (BIEF) state of the catalysts. In Figure d, the OCV values of Cu-CNNT-X significantly exceeded those of CNNT-X, indicating that the incorporation of Cu atoms induces strong dipolar polarization, which enhances the BIEF intensity and promotes carrier migration. In Figure e and Figure S15b, the photocurrent intensity of CNNT-X exhibited minimal variation, whereas Cu-CNNT-400 showed the strongest photocurrent response. Quantitative analysis of surface charge aggregation states and BIEF strength was performed to elucidate the intrinsic mechanism behind efficient photogenerated charge transfer. The surface charge densities of Cu-CNNT-100, Cu-CNNT-400, and Cu-CNNT-1000 were 0.634 μc cm–2, 3.706 μc cm–2 and 0.702 μc cm–2, respectively (Figure f). And the BIEF intensities of Cu-CNNT-400 and Cu-CNNT-1000 were 2.53 and 1.07 times higher than that of Cu-CNNT-100 (Figure S16). Therefore, the catalysts rank in charge-transfer capability as follows: Cu-CNNT-400 > Cu-CNNT-1000 > Cu-CNNT-100. The enhanced BIEF supplies a stronger driving force for charge transfer, promoting the formation of electron-rich regions at curved Cu sites and thereby optimizing the local charge distribution. As depicted in Figure g and Figure S17, Cu-CNNT-400 exhibited the smallest arc radius, indicating superior charge separation and transport efficiency. This trend aligns with the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves (Figure h), the largest background current area of Cu-CNNT-400 indicating the highest charge storage capacity. Furthermore, Cu-CNNT-400 showed a reduced steady-state photoluminescence (PL) emission intensity (Figure S18a) and a longer average PL lifetime of carriers (Figure S18b and Table S5), indicating a greater number of photogenerated carriers available for photo-oxidation before recombination. Quantitative analysis of charge transfer efficiency (Table S6) revealed that the electron transfer efficiency of Cu-CNNT-400 (80.9%) was considerably higher than those of Cu-CNNT-1000 (75.5%) and Cu-CNNT-1000 (79.8%), suggesting that asymmetric electron localization at the Cu–N2 sites significantly enhanced the charge transfer rate. The energy-band structures of the prepared photocatalysts were depicted in Figure i and Figure S15d. And Cu-CNNT-400 has the most negative CB, suggesting the higher affinity for O2, which was more conducive to adsorption and activation (O2/•O2 –: −0.33 eV vs NHE). However, the relatively negative VB potential of all photocatalysts may hinder the direct oxidation of H2O to •OH by holes due to the highly oxidation potential (•OH/H2O: 2.40 eV vs NHE).

The surface potential difference was measured by using Kelvin probe force microscopy (KPFM). Under light illumination, the surface potentials of Cu-CNNT-100 (Figure S19a-c), Cu-CNNT-400 (Figure j–l) and Cu-CNNT-1000 (Figure S19d-f) all shifted toward more negative values. This shift results from the migration of photogenerated charges to opposite sites on the catalysts, causing a decrease in surface potential due to the charge screening effect. Notably, the contact potential difference (ΔCPD) for Cu-CNNT-400 (−67 mV) was significantly greater than those of Cu-CNNT-100 (−5 mV) and Cu-CNNT-1000 (−30 mV), indicating a higher accumulation of photogenerated electrons on the surface of Cu-CNNT-400. In addition, the mass transfer diffusion behavior of reactant molecules within CNNTs of different tube diameters also plays a crucial role in influencing catalytic activity. To further elucidate the mass transfer behavior during the catalytic process, finite element method (FEM) simulation was conducted. The flux distribution and diffusion efficiency of CH3SH in Cu-CNNT-1000 (Figure S20c,f) were higher than those in Cu-CNNT-400 (Figure S20b,e) and Cu-CNNT-100 (Figure S20a,d).

In the CNNT-1000 model, the diffusion flux of CH3SH molecules had already reached saturation by 50 μs. When the simulation time was extended to 0.5 ms, the diffusion flux at the 2000 nm position reaches saturation for all models (Figure S21), indicating that tube diameter effectively modulates CH3SH mass-transfer behavior. This effect is attributed to radial confinement and pore-size-dependent molecular overflow arising from differences in the curvature radius. Thus, Cu-CNNT-1000 exhibited similar performance with Cu-CNNT-400 in the photocatalytic removal of CH3SH, with only a 3.0% difference in catalytic activity (Figure c).

ROSs Identification and CH3SH Oxidation Pathway

Reactive oxygen species (ROSs) generated during the photocatalytic process were identified by using electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR). In Figure a, Cu-CNNT-100 and Cu-CNNT-1000 exhibited no DMPO-•OH signals under both dark and light conditions, indicating no •OH generation in these systems. In contrast, Cu-CNNT-400 showed the strongest characteristic signal of the four main DMPO-•OH peaks that appeared under visible light irradiation. This demonstrated that •OH is the primary active species in Cu-CNNT-400. In Figure b, signals of DMPO-•O2 – were clearly detected, with the peak intensity gradually increasing in both Cu-CNNT-400 and Cu-CNNT-1000. This increase is attributed to a higher amount of photogenerated electrons reducing O2. Furthermore, under light irradiation, the DMPO-•O2 – peak intensity in Cu-CNNT-400 was significantly higher than that in Cu-CNNT-1000, indicating that Cu-CNNT-400 generated more •O2 –. The TEMP–1O2 signals in Cu-CNNT-100 showed no significant change between dark and light conditions (Figure c), suggesting the absence of 1O2 generation. In contrast, the 1O2 signal remarkably increased in both Cu-CNNT-400 and Cu-CNNT-1000 systems. Moreover, the 1O2 signal intensity in Cu-CNNT-400 was higher than that in Cu-CNNT-1000, indicating that Cu-CNNT-400 generated more 1O2. In conclusion, all nanotube systems produced ROSs yields higher than those of Cu-melon, confirming the unique advantage of curvature in activating small molecules. To further investigate the effect of ROSs produced by Cu-CNNT-400 on photocatalytic process, we conducted trapping experiments using p-benzoquinone (BQ), histidine and isopropanol (IPA) as trapping reagents. , The results demonstrated a significant decrease in the photocatalytic efficiency of Cu-CNNT-400 after the addition of PBQ, histidine and IPA, leading to reductions of 83.62%, 40.8%, and 14.2% (Figure d). In Figure e, the observed CH3SH removal efficiency constants of Cu-CNNT-400, after the addition of IPA, histidine and PBQ were calculated to be 0.11, 0.0679, 0.0372, and 0.0084 min–1, respectively. Furthermore, the contributions of different ROSs were calculated to be in the order of •O2 – (92.4%) > 1O2 (66.2%) > •OH (38.3%) (Figure f). In conclusion, these results suggested that the main ROSs in Cu-CNNT-100 and Cu-melon was •O2 – and 1O2, both 1O2 and •O2 – dominate in the Cu-CNNT-1000 system, while in the Cu-CNNT-400 system, 1O2, •O2 – and •OH all played an important role in the photocatalytic reaction process (Table S7), indicating that the Cu-CNNT-400 system promoted the generation of ROSs.

The surface-adsorbed species and intermediate products formed during CH3SH oxidation were analyzed by using in situ diffuse reflectance infrared spectroscopy (in situ DRIFTS). As shown in Figure S22a-c, characteristic peaks corresponding to S–O bonds (∼860 cm–1), CH3SO3H (∼1210 cm–1), −CH3S– (∼1238 cm–1), adsorbed CH3SH (∼1451 cm–1), and COO– (∼1630 cm–1) were detected on Cu-CNNT-100, Cu-CNNT-400, and Cu-CNNT-1000 catalysts during the adsorption process. , Notably, the infrared peak intensities were the weakest for Cu-CNNT-100, indicating its lower CH3SH adsorption capacity. Furthermore, the presence of both S–O and COO– species suggested partial cleavage of the C–S bond in CH3SH, leading to the formation of sulfur-containing and carbon-containing intermediates (Table S8). Under light irradiation (Figure g–i), the adsorption peaks associated with CH3SH gradually weakened and eventually disappeared on Cu-CNNT-100, Cu-CNNT-1000 and Cu-CNNT-400 catalysts. Meanwhile, new infrared peaks emerged over time, indicating the progressive consumption of CH3SH adsorbed on the catalyst surface. Specifically, C–H bonds (∼760 cm–1) appeared and increasingly intensified on Cu-CNNT-100 and Cu-CNNT-1000, suggesting incomplete oxidation of CH3SH. In contrast, no C–H bonds were detected on Cu-CNNT-400, indicating a deeper and more thorough oxidation of CH3SH. Quantitative analysis of the main intermediate species during the reaction (Figure j–l) revealed that CH3SH is initially deprotonated on the catalyst surface to form CH3S– (∼1236 cm–1). Subsequently, the S atom in CH3S– was gradually oxidized to CH3SO3 – (∼1207 cm–1), while the C carbon was further oxidized to HCOOH (∼1630 cm–1) and CO3 2– (∼1287 cm–1). , Finally, these intermediates are fully oxidized to SO4 2– (∼1050 cm–1) and CO2 (∼2335 cm–1) (Figure S23a-c). The gradual accumulation of intermediate products on the surface of Cu-CNNT-100, particularly carbonates, indicates poorer catalytic activity due to incomplete oxidation. Similarly, for Cu-CNNT-1000, intermediate species initially increased and then decreased; however, the amounts of final oxidation products, SO4 2– and CO2, remained low, suggesting limited CH3SH oxidation. In contrast, Cu-CNNT-400 showed a greater tendency to produce sulfate, carbonate species, and CO2, alongside a lower formation of formate species, demonstrating superior mineralization capability during the photocatalytic oxidation of CH3SH. Specifically, the reaction path of CH3SH oxidation on Cu-CNNT-400 proceeded via the following steps: CH3SH → CH3SCH3/CH3SSCH3→ CH3SO3H → SO3 –/HCOO–/CO3 2–→ SO4 2–, CO2 and H2O.

Theoretical Calculation of Structure–Property Relationship

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to further clarify the structure–property relationship during the photocatalytic reaction process. First, the configurations of four different models were compared to identify the most stable structure. The formation energy (E form.) of the N1–Cu–N2 structure in Figure S24d (E form. = −3.55 eV) was more negative than those in Figure S24b (E form. = −2.16 eV), Figure S24c (E form. = −2.15 eV) and Figure S24e (E form. = −2.14 eV), indicating that the former structure was most stable. Additionally, the adsorption energy (E ads.) of O2, H2O, and CH3SH on melon were calculated as −0.07 eV, −0.10 eV and −0.20 eV, respectively (Figure S25a-c). These values are higher (weaker adsorption) compared to those on CNNT, which were −0.42 eV, −1.66 eV and −0.28 eV (Figure S26a-c), revealing that the pure melon plane is less favorable for molecular adsorption. Furthermore, the E ads. hierarchy suggested that CH3SH preferentially adsorbed on melon and CNNT, which may competitively hinder the adsorption of O2 and H2O, thereby impeding ROS generation. In contrast, for the Cu-CNNT system, the E ads. follow the order: E ads. (O2, −1.41 eV) > E ads. (H2O, −0.80 eV) > E ads. (CH3SH, −0.64 eV) (Figure a-c). In addition, the adsorption capacity of Cu-melon for O2 and CH3SH was significantly lower than that of Cu-CNNT (Figure S27a, b), suggesting that Cu-melon has a limited ability to activate small molecules. The stronger O2 adsorption of Cu-CNNT favors its activation to form 1O2 and •O2 –, while the weaker adsorption of H2O prevents competitive adsorption. Compared to the CH3SH+CNNT (Figure d) and CH3SH+Cu-melon (Figure S28b), the charge distribution at the interface in the CH3SH+Cu-CNNT (Figure e) was more delocalized, and the C–S bond in CH3SH was more polarized. Notably, the Bader charge transfer from the CH3SH molecule to CNNT and Cu-melon was 0.03e, whereas it significantly increases to 0.78e when transferred to the Cu sites in the Cu-CNNT catalyst. The elevated electron transfer rate in the Cu-CNNT catalyst arises from an enhanced BIEF. Consequently, the CH3SH molecule loses more electrons in the Cu-CNNT system, facilitating the weakening of intramolecular bonds. And the C–S bond length in the CH3SH+Cu-CNNT system (1.893 Å) was more stretched than that in the CH3SH+CNNT system (1.831 Å) and CH3SH+Cu-melon (1.829 Å) (Table S9), indicating bond weakening. The twisted conformation of Cu–N2 disrupted symmetric electron distribution in the localized microenvironment and enhances electron exchange between the S 2p orbitals and Cu 3d orbitals, making the C–S bond more susceptible to cleavage. Moreover, O2 exhibited a stronger electron affinity on the Cu-CNNT surface with a Bader charge transfer of +0.93e, significantly higher than that on the CNNT surface (+0.46e) (Figure S29a, b) and Cu-melon surface (+0.19e) (Figure S28a). Additionally, the O2 activation energy barrier on Cu-CNNT (ΔG = 1.46 eV) was much lower than that on CNNT (ΔG = 2.54 eV) (Figure f), indicating that increased electron transfer facilitates easier O2 activation.

The electronic interaction between CH3SH and Cu-CNNT was investigated using the projected density of states (PDOS) analysis. The PDOS values of CH3SH+CNNT (Figure g) and CH3SH+Cu-melon (Figure S30a) exhibited high spin symmetry, indicating weak electronic interaction between CH3SH and CNNT. In contrast, the orbital overlap between S 2p and Cu 3d orbitals near the E f in the CH3SH+Cu-CNNT system revealed enhanced electronic interaction (Figure h). Notably, the asymmetry in the Cu 3d DOS spin states suggested the presence of unpaired electrons in the d-orbitals, which can induce charge localization and promote occupancy of antibonding orbitals, thereby modulating the adsorption strength of CH3SH. The adsorption process always tends to satisfy Sabatier’s principle, and either too strong or too weak adsorption is detrimental to the catalytic reaction. Upon CH3SH adsorption, the εd of the Cu 3d orbital slightly downward from −1.536 eV in pure Cu-CNNT to −1.703 eV in the CH3SH+Cu-CNNT system (Figure i). The εd of the CH3SH+Cu-melon system is as low as −1.937 eV (Figure S30b), indicating that CH3SH binding on Cu-melon is too weak to enable activation for redox reactions. The above results indicated that suitable occupancy of the antibonding states facilitates electron redistribution and weakens the bonding strength of S atom at Cu sites (Figure j), thereby enhancing CH3SH removal efficiency and promoting desorption of sulfur-containing intermediates.

Based on the above analysis, a plausible reaction mechanism for CH3SH photo-oxidation over Cu-CNNT was proposed. Upon light irradiation, photoinduced electrons are excited from the VB to the CB and migrate axially through the asymmetric Cu–N2 coordination structure toward surface Cu sites, driven by an enhanced BIEF. These surface-enriched electrons form an “electron sponge”, continuously activating adsorbed O2 to generate abundant •OH, •O2 –, and 1O2 species. Subsequently, these ROS oxidize the adsorbed CH3SH and sulfur-containing intermediates (CH3SCH3, CH3SSCH3) into CO2, SO4 2– and H2O. Finally, the optimized specific surface area and pore structure of CNNT promoted efficient mass diffusion, enhancing catalytic reactivity, facilitating rapid desorption of degradation products, and enabling dynamic regeneration of active sites.

Conclusions

In summary, a unique Cu-CNNT catalyst featuring an asymmetric curved Cu–N2 protuberance structure was developed by leveraging the “curvature effect” of CNNT, and applied for photocatalytic CH3SH oxidation. The Cu-CNNT-400 with optimal curvature radius and large pore volume exhibited superior molecular diffusion properties. Additionally, Cu-CNNT-400 demonstrated a higher Cu atom dispersion (5.66%) and a Cu utilization efficiency was 18.88 times greater than that of planar Cu-melon, providing more effective reaction sites and an electron-rich environment that facilitates reactant adsorption and enhances ROS generation. The geometric distortion in the curved Cu–N2 configuration reduced electron occupancy in Cu 3d antibonding orbitals, induced unconventional d-p hybridization and weakened the C–S bond in CH3SH, promoting deep oxidation into CO2, H2O, and SO4 2–. Consequently, Cu-CNNT-400 with moderate radius of curvature achieved the highest CH3SH removal efficiency (90.4%), outperforming Cu-CNNT-100 (78.1%), Cu-CNNT-1000 (87.4%), and Cu-melon (43.6%). This work presents a general strategy for constructing asymmetric microstructures by precisely tuning the coordination environment via the “curvature effect”, thereby maximizing metal atom utilization for efficient S-VOC disposal.
