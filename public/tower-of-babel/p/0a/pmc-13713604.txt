Redlining and Prevalent Obesity, Hypertension, and Diabetes in GuLF Study Participants
Authors: Marina R. Sweeney, Kaitlyn G. Lawrence, Jennifer M. P. Woo, Kate E. Christenbury, W. Braxton Jackson, Emily J. Werder, Lawrence S. Engel, Dale P. Sandler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) created color‐coded maps of more than 200 U.S. cities in the 1930s to facilitate mortgage lending, with redlined neighborhoods marked in red and assigned the “worst” grade (D). Historic redlining has persistent present‐day consequences, although few studies have explored redlining in relation to cardiovascular disease risk factors. Cross‐sectional associations between living in an area of historic redlining and prevalent obesity, hypertension, and diabetes were evaluated.MethodsAt enrollment, participants reported physician‐diagnosed diabetes and hypertension, height, weight, and residential address. Redlining was based on available HOLC neighborhood grades (A/B/C/D) linked to participant addresses (N = 1653) at the census‐tract level. Modified Poisson regression was used to determine prevalence ratios (PRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for HOLC grade and obesity, hypertension, and diabetes. Effect measure modification was evaluated by race (Black/White) and oil spill cleanup worker status (yes/no).ResultsHistoric redlining (D vs. A/B) was associated with a higher prevalence of obesity (PR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.09–1.87), hypertension (PR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.09–1.87), and diabetes (PR = 1.58, 95% CI: 0.71–3.48). In stratified analyses, associations of redlining (D vs. A/B/C) with hypertension (PR = 1.19, 95% CI: 0.91–1.55) and diabetes (PR = 1.47, 95% CI: 0.66–3.66) were suggested only among Black participants. Redlining was associated with obesity among workers (PR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.23–1.82) but not among non‐workers.ConclusionLegacy consequences of historic redlining may negatively impact the cardiovascular health of present‐day residents.

Introduction

Redlining refers to the subjective practice by the Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) in the 1930s of creating residential maps to define mortgage lending potential following the Great Depression [1]. Neighborhoods in over 200 U.S. cities, including in the Deep South, received one of four grades and were marked on maps with an accompanying label and color code: the “best” neighborhoods were graded as A (green); “still desirable” as B (blue); “definitely declining” as C (yellow); and “hazardous” as D (red, hence the term “redlining”). These grades were largely based on the proportion of Black, immigrant, and other non‐White residents living in the neighborhood [2].

Redlining practices led to the emergence of entire neighborhoods where residents were systemically denied mortgages, resulting in concentrated areas of restricted socioeconomic resources and decreased racial and ethnic diversity. Despite the end of overt redlining practices in 1968 with the passage of the Fair Housing Act, differences in economic and neighborhood composition persist in many historically redlined neighborhoods [3, 4, 5, 6]. For example, redlined neighborhoods have higher rates of eviction filings and food insecurity; [3] lower proportion of homeowners, individuals with at least a high school education, and children living above the poverty line; and lower median household income [5]. Historically redlined neighborhoods are still considered disadvantaged when evaluating present‐day neighborhood deprivation, as measured by the Area Deprivation Index, and are further characterized by higher poverty levels and larger populations of non‐White racial groups [4]. As a result of many of these factors, previously‐designated grade D neighborhoods are also more likely to have higher rates of violent crime than grade A neighborhoods [6].

Evidence suggests that associations between living in a historically redlined neighborhood and contemporary poor health outcomes [2, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11]. Many of these studies have been ecological [2, 7] likely due to the small number of neighborhoods with HOLC grades. Individual‐level studies on the health impacts of living in HOLC neighborhoods are limited, and only a few have evaluated the association between redlining and risk factors for cardiovascular disease (CVD) [8, 10]. Social determinants of health are proposed to play a significant role in cardiovascular health differences [12], and redlining may contribute through psychological stress [13] and increased exposure to environmental hazards [2].

Many historically redlined areas face additional contemporary environmental exposures that may contribute to CVD‐related health risks. Contemporary neighborhood social vulnerability, for example, is higher in redlined than in non‐redlined neighborhoods [14]. Similarly, areas that received worse HOLC grades have also been shown to have higher environmental contaminants [15] and reduced access to resources, including healthcare [16].

The Gulf Long‐Term Follow‐Up (GuLF) Study is a large longitudinal cohort study initiated after the Deepwater Horizon (DWH) oil spill disaster in 2010 that occurred off the U.S. Gulf Coast. Participants were socioeconomically and medically disadvantaged [17, 18], and oil spill cleanup workers were additionally exposed to spill‐ and cleanup‐associated chemicals [19, 20]. While the study participants included individuals from throughout the U.S., most resided in a Gulf Coast state. Using data from GuLF Study participants living in neighborhoods with redlining data, this analysis evaluated the impact of legacy redlining in a population living primarily in the U.S. Gulf Coast with high contemporary environmental exposures. Specifically, associations were evaluated between HOLC neighborhood grades and CVD risk factors, overall and stratified by self‐identified race and participation in cleanup work following a major oil spill disaster affecting the region.

Methods

Study Population

The GuLF Study is a prospective cohort that enrolled 32,608 participants following the Deepwater Horizon oil spill that occurred off the U.S. Gulf Coast in 2010. Participants were recruited between March 2011 and March 2013 and were either cleanup workers or received worker safety training but did not participate in response efforts (non‐workers). All participants completed telephone interviews at enrollment, which collected information on demographic characteristics, spill‐related activities, medical history, and lifestyle. The study population is described in more detail elsewhere [18]. All participants provided informed consent, and this research was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Institutes of Health.

Public Involvement

The GuLF Study was overseen by a Community Advisory Board (CAB). The CAB was involved in developing community support for the study, as well as appropriate communications and study materials.

Exposure Assessment

Data on HOLC grades have been mapped to 2010 Census tracts and are publicly available through the Inter‐university Consortium for Political and Social Research [21]. Because of incomplete overlap between 1930s neighborhoods and present‐day census tracts, tract grades were assigned based on a weighted proportion of each grade within that tract, with numbers 1–4 representing grades A–D, respectively. The cut‐points presented in Meier and Mitchell (2022) were used: A = 1.00–1.749, B = 1.75–2.49, C = 2.50–3.249, and D = 3.25–4.00, which the authors state best aligned the census tracts to the redlining maps [22]. For example, a tract with 40% of its area classified as grade B and 60% of its area classified as grade C would have the following overall grade calculation: (0.4∗2)+(0.6∗3)=2.6, which would be classified as grade C. Historic HOLC grades were linked to the census tract that corresponds to participants' geocoded enrollment residence.

Outcome Assessment

Outcome data were ascertained from the enrollment interviews. Obesity was defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30.0 kg/m2 based on self‐reported height and weight. To assess hypertension and diabetes, participants were asked whether a doctor had ever told them that they had “high blood pressure” or “diabetes or sugar diabetes.” Participants who reported an age at diabetes diagnosis < 20 years were presumed to have type 1 diabetes and were excluded from the analysis [23, 24].

Covariate Assessment

Covariates were ascertained at the enrollment interview and included age (continuous), sex (male, female), highest educational attainment (high school diploma/equivalent or less, at least some college education), marital status (never married, married/living as married, widowed/divorced/separated), and oil spill cleanup worker status (yes, no).

Analytic Sample

After excluding participants not living in areas with HOLC grades (n = 30,904), participants were sequentially excluded if they were missing data on obesity, hypertension, or diabetes (n = 41), were assumed to have type 1 diabetes (n = 3), or who were missing model covariates (n = 7). The final analytic sample consisted of 1653 participants (Supporting Information S1: Figure 1).

Data Analysis

Descriptive Analysis

Participant characteristics were described using means and standard deviations for continuous variables and frequencies and proportions for categorical variables. Results for the analytic sample are presented overall and by HOLC grade (A/B, C, D). To compare the participants living in an HOLC‐graded neighborhood with those who did not, results are presented for the entire cohort sample (N = 32,608) for any HOLC grade, specific HOLC grade (A, B, C, D), and no HOLC grade.

Primary Analysis

Multivariable modified Poisson regression [25] was used to calculate adjusted prevalence ratios (PRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association of HOLC neighborhood grade (referent: grades A and B combined due to small numbers of cases in grade A neighborhoods) with obesity, hypertension, and diabetes. Two adjustment sets were considered: model 1 adjusted for age and sex, and model 2 additionally adjusted for educational attainment, marital status, and oil spill worker status. Educational attainment (as a proxy for socioeconomic position) and marital status can impact where an individual can afford to live, as well as the choice of neighborhood (e.g., individuals with young children might be drawn to neighborhoods with “good” schools if that is within their financial means). Oil spill cleanup worker status was included in the model to account for exposure to oil spill chemicals, which have been previously shown to be associated with CVD risk factors [18, 26].

Stratified Analyses

Effect measure modification was evaluated by self‐identified race (Black, White) and by oil spill cleanup worker status (yes, no) using the model 2 adjustment set. These potential modifiers were selected due to evidence suggesting racial heterogeneity of associations between redlining and contemporary CVD health [10] and evidence showing associations between oil spill exposures and CVD risk factors [26, 27]. The race‐stratified analysis was limited to Black and White participants (irrespective of Hispanic ethnicity) due to small numbers of participants who self‐identified as other races. A reference group of grades A, B, and C combined was used for the stratified analyses due to small numbers of diabetes cases in A‐ and B‐graded neighborhoods for several of the strata. Comparing redlined (i.e., grade D neighborhoods) to non‐redlined neighborhoods (i.e., grade A‐C neighborhoods) is an approach that has been used elsewhere [28]. The interaction p‐value was determined using the Wald test for the HOLC grade‐modifier (race or worker status) cross‐product term.

Sensitivity Analysis

As a sensitivity analysis, annual household income (≤ $20,000, > $20,000), with imputation for missing values, was added to the model 2 adjustment set. Household income was not included in the main adjustment set because of the level of missingness for the self‐reported variable and because educational attainment is more commonly used as a proxy for socioeconomic position [29]. Five repetitions of multiple imputation by chained equations with age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, employment status, and educational attainment as predictors were used to impute missing values for annual household income (n = 70). Imputation was conducted using the mice package in R (version 4.4.0).

Results

Descriptive Statistics

The 1653 participants in the sample lived in 866 Census tracts across 36 states (Figure 1). Few participants lived in an A‐ or B‐graded neighborhood, defined as “best” and “still desirable” neighborhoods (6% and 11%, respectively). Overall, participants were mostly male (78%) and oil spill cleanup workers (81%) and had received at least some post‐high school education (61%) (Table 1). The mean age was 40 years. Compared to those living in grade A or B neighborhoods, participants in grade D neighborhoods were more likely to be Black (58% vs. 13%), have lower income (≤ $20,000 per year: 46% vs. 15%), and are less formally educated (high school/equivalent or less: 52% vs. 16%). Participants in grade A or B neighborhoods were more likely to be married or living as married than those in grade D neighborhoods (53% vs. 36%).

Main Analysis Results

Overall, about one quarter of participants were categorized as obese based on BMI calculation (25%) or reported a diagnosis of hypertension (23%), while few (4%) reported a diagnosis of diabetes (Table 2). Living in a D‐graded neighborhood was associated with a higher prevalence of obesity (PRmodel1 = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.30–2.19), hypertension (PRmodel1 = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.11–1.83), and diabetes (PRmodel1 = 1.75, 95% CI: 0.84–3.67) when compared to living in an A‐ or B‐graded neighborhood when adjusting for age and sex (Table 2). Associations for C‐graded neighborhoods (compared to A‐ and B‐graded neighborhoods) were only suggestively elevated for diabetes (PRmodel1 = 1.58, 95% CI: 0.71–3.51). In the model with additional covariates, estimates remained elevated but were slightly attenuated (obesity: PRmodel2 = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.09–1.87; hypertension: PRmodel2 = 1.19, 95% CI: 0.92–1.55; diabetes: PRmodel2 = 1.58, 95% CI: 0.71–3.48). Results were similar when annual household income was added to the adjustment set (Supporting Information S1: table 2).

Stratified Analysis Results

In stratified analyses, living in a grade D neighborhood was suggestively associated with a higher prevalence of hypertension for Black participants (PRmodel2 = 1.19, 95% CI: 0.91–1.55), but not for White participants (PRmodel2 = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.58–1.15; p‐interaction = 0.09; Table 3). There were few cases of diabetes among Black participants living in grades A, B, or C neighborhoods (n = 7) or among White participants living in grade D neighborhoods (n = 6).

Discussion

In this analysis of GuLF Study participants, living in a historically redlined census tract was associated with higher prevalence of obesity, hypertension, and diabetes. In stratified analyses, redlining was suggestively associated with hypertension and diabetes for Black, but not White, participants and with obesity for oil spill cleanup workers but not non‐workers.

Few prior studies of redlining and CVD risk factors have been published [8, 9, 10, 30]. The overall findings are consistent with two previous ecological studies that observed higher census‐tract level obesity, hypertension, and diabetes prevalence for D‐graded census tracts [9, 30]. Individual‐level studies, however, have not supported associations in the overall population between redlining and obesity, hypertension, and diabetes. Two cross‐sectional analyses have evaluated redlining and CVD risk factors [8, 10], only one of which presented the results for all racial and ethnic groups combined [8]. In contrast to the present findings, a cross‐sectional analysis within the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) Study observed no association for census‐tract HOLC grade in relation to BMI and waist circumference in a population of N = 3870 young adults (mean age: 24 years) [8]. In comparison, a 42% higher prevalence of obesity was observed for redlined compared to A‐ and B‐graded census tracts in the present analysis. Population and analytic differences could explain the disparate findings. The CARDIA Study participants lived in Birmingham, AL, Chicago, IL, Minneapolis, MN, and Oakland, CA. Participants in the present analytic sample lived in > 135 different cities across the United States, with 4% living in the four CARDIA Study cities combined. The analytic baselines of the CARDIA (1985–1986) and the GuLF (2011–2013) studies also differed by 25 years, suggesting a possible birth cohort effect. CARDIA participants were also younger at the time of enrollment and therefore at a lower baseline risk for hypertension and diabetes. The CARDIA Study analysis modeled BMI as a continuous variable based on measured height and weight, whereas this analysis uses a dichotomous variable based on self‐reported height and weight. It is possible that the association between redlining and obesity has a threshold effect, which would not be detected by linear regression but could be picked up using a dichotomous outcome.

There is some evidence that associations between redlining and CVD risk factors may differ based on race and ethnicity. In the Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA), the authors observed a lower prevalence of ideal blood pressure (< 120/80 mmHg) for Black participants living in grade D compared to grade A neighborhoods that was not observed for White, Chinese, or Hispanic participants [10]. Similarly, a higher prevalence of hypertension was associated with living in a grade D versus grade A, B, or C neighborhood among Black, but not White, participants in the present analysis. However, other findings in the MESA analysis were not consistent with those observed in the GuLF Study population. Black participants in MESA who lived in redlined neighborhoods were more likely to be classified as overweight or obese, and there were no associations with ideal blood glucose level [10]. Differences in outcome definitions (e.g., overweight and obese combined vs. obese alone, ideal blood glucose vs. a physician diagnosis of diabetes) could potentially explain these differences in findings.

Longitudinal studies have not explored incident diabetes, hypertension, or obesity, but studies of the incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events have observed positive associations of redlining with heart failure, myocardial infarction, and mortality in participants with preexisting atherosclerotic disease [31] or chronic kidney disease [32]. Although this analysis was not able to evaluate incidence, the observed higher prevalence of CVD risk factors could have implications for the incidence of worse health outcomes later on, which is supported by the results of these two studies.

Redlining has demonstrated negative consequences on the built environment that may increase the risk of CVD risk factors. Food deserts, defined as areas with limited access to affordable whole foods such as fresh produce [33], and food insecurity, defined as low access to adequate food due to insufficient monetary or other resources [34], have both been associated with historic redlining [3, 35]. Food swamps, a related concept defined as low access to fresh foods paired with high access to fast and junk food [36], along with food deserts, are more common in lower income and predominantly non‐White neighborhoods. Food deserts, insecurity, and swamps have been associated with obesity [33, 34, 36]. Compared to better‐graded neighborhoods, historically redlined ones tend to have less green space and greater air pollution, surface temperatures, and environmental noise [2, 37, 38]. These exposures may increase the risk of diabetes through altered endothelial dysfunction, decreased insulin sensitivity, stress responses, inflammation, and worse glucose metabolism [39]. Air pollution, noise, and green space have been associated with hypertension through arterial stiffness and stress responses [40, 41]. Due to systemic disinvestment, segregated neighborhoods also have reduced access to high‐quality health care services [16], which could impact the ability to receive appropriate preventive care.

The combination of individual‐level factors (reduced wealth accumulation, less upward mobility [1], and persistent differences in neighborhood composition [3]) with the neighborhood‐level factors described above could explain the why the associations appear higher for Black participants in this analysis and elsewhere [12, 16] through the ecosocial theory of embodiment. This theory, developed by Krieger, proposes that “the multilevel dynamic and co‐constituted societal and ecological context within which [people] live, work, love, play, fight, ail, and die” manifests biologically in human bodies [42].

No previous studies have evaluated redlining and CVD risk factors in a population of oil spill cleanup workers. Oil spill workers have substantial but relatively short‐term exposure to chemicals such as benzene, toluene, ethylbenzene, xylene, n‐hexane, and other hydrocarbons as well as air pollutants (e.g., fine particulate matter) associated with burning and flaring of oil [19, 20], and these chemicals and pollutants have been associated with numerous health effects, including heart attacks [43], hypertension [27], and diabetes [26]. This study anticipated that associations between redlining and CVD risk factors might be stronger for the “doubly‐exposed” cleanup workers living in redlined neighborhoods. Elevated associations were observed between redlining and diabetes and hypertension for both workers and non‐workers, although the prevalence of obesity was higher only among workers. Studies of oil spill exposure and obesity are limited. Evidence from laboratory studies suggests that COREXIT, which was an oil dispersant used during the DWH cleanup, has “obesogenic” properties [44]. Air pollutants may alter gut microbiota and impact obesity risk through inflammatory pathways [45].

Strengths of this analysis include the availability of individual‐level data and a racially and socioeconomically diverse study population. This paper is also the first to publish an analysis of redlining and health outcomes in a population of oil spill cleanup workers, which is a particularly vulnerable population due to occupational chemical exposures.

This study was limited by reliance on self‐reported outcomes. Weight, height, blood pressure, and hemoglobin A1C were measured at subsequent study visits, but could not be used for this analysis due to the smaller number of individuals who participated in those visits and also had HOLC data (N < 550). In general, research study participants tend to overreport height and underreport weight, resulting in underreported BMIs [46]. For this analysis, underreporting would likely result in some participants with obesity being classified as overweight and potentially bias results towards the null. Similarly, diagnoses of diabetes and hypertension may also be underreported [47, 48] or underdiagnosed [49, 50], resulting in the exclusion of less severe cases in this analysis.

The cross‐sectional design is a further limitation. The timing of certain covariates (e.g., educational attainment and marital status) in relation to the start of residence at the enrollment address is unknown, which could mean that the second model is adjusting for mediators when the exposure pre‐dated the covariates. However, given that the timing of these events is unknown, and that educational attainment and marital status can have an impact on where an individual can afford to live—educational attainment is a proxy for socioeconomic position and married individuals could live in a two‐income household or require larger housing to accommodate children—the second model includes these covariates.

Additional limitations include a small number of diabetes cases and a small proportion of GuLF Study participants living in redlined neighborhoods. The former means that the diabetes associations are unpowered, which prevents firm conclusions from being drawn from these results. The latter is a limitation of many analyses of redlining given that data are only available for a fraction of urban‐dwelling populations in the U.S. Although over 200 U.S. cities have documented HOLC maps, urban counties have seen dramatic expansion since the time the maps were made in the 1930s [51]. Since the 1930s, the U.S. population has grown from approximately 137 million [52] to over 308 million. A further limitation is that the GuLF Study is a post‐disaster cohort, and while internally valid, the results may not be generalizable to the larger U.S. population living in HOLC‐graded neighborhoods.

Although this cross‐sectional analysis cannot establish causality, it is part of a growing body of evidence that previously redlined neighborhoods are associated with poorer health outcomes for current residents, suggesting the need for better investment of resources in these areas.

Redlining may have persistent impacts on a community's CVD‐related health and may help to explain contemporary differences in CVD health for different vulnerable subpopulations.
