Mislabeling of Case-Control Studies in Ophthalmologic Literature: A Bibliometric Analysis
Authors: Ting Zeng, Ziping Yu, Jinming Guo, Hongzi Shen, Gehan Ai, Xin Tan, Pi Guo, Kunliang Qiu, Jianwei Lin, Yang Zhuang, Chi-Pui Pang, Hongxi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo characterize the prevalence and determinants of case-control study mislabeling in recent ophthalmologic journals.MethodsA bibliometric analysis was conducted on 386 articles self-declared as case-control or case-controlled studies, published from 2022 to 2023 across 95 PubMed-indexed ophthalmologic journals. Two blinded reviewers independently classified study designs using established epidemiological criteria and standardized the Level of Evidence per the Joanna Briggs Institute (2014) guidelines. Associations between mislabeling and bibliometric factors (Journal Citation Reports quartile, place of publication) were evaluated using logistic regression.ResultsA total of 273 articles (70.7%) were mislabeled. Reclassification revealed alterations in evidence hierarchies, with 49% exhibiting inflated level of evidence and 18% demonstrating under-reported level of evidence. Logistic regression revealed that articles published in Q1 journals had significantly lower odds of being mislabeled (odds ratio, 0.511; 95% confidence interval, 0.277–0.943) compared with Q4 journals. No significant association was found between mislabeling and the place of publication.ConclusionsThere is pervasive mislabeling of study designs in ophthalmologic journals that distorts levels of evidence, necessitating standardized guidelines and validation tools.Translational RelevanceThis study enhances the reliability of ophthalmologic evidence by providing a framework to identify and correct mislabeled study designs.

Introduction

Evidence-based medicine establishes a scientific foundation for clinical decision-making through the systematic integration of three core elements—high-quality research evidence, clinicians' expertise, and patients' values.1 Within the evidence-based medicine framework, the quality of evidence is evaluated using a hierarchical grading system termed the Level of Evidence, in which the core criterion is the scientific rigor and methodological soundness inherent in the study design. Among various study designs,2 observational studies such as cohort studies and case-control studies are widely used in clinical practice, although their credibility and clinical translational value depend directly on the accuracy of their design.3,4

Case-control studies identify disease risk factors by tracing exposures backward from outcomes, making them particularly suited for investigating rare diseases or conditions with long latency periods.5 Their advantages include lower resource demands and efficiency in exploring multifactorial disease associations. Furthermore, case-control studies have evolved into multiple variants to accommodate diverse research contexts, including nested case-control studies, case-cohort studies, case-crossover studies, matched case-control studies, and genetic association studies.6 Although the presence of “cases” and “controls” constitutes essential methodological components, not all studies incorporating these elements qualify as case-control studies. Genuine case-control studies necessitate adherence to specific temporal logic, ensuring exposure precedes outcome, and defined outcome-based grouping criteria. In practice, however, the term “case-control study” is frequently misapplied.

Previous analyses have revealed significant case-control mislabeling across multiple disciplines. For instance, Mayo et al.7 specifically compared rehabilitation journals to a sample of other medical journals and reported a mislabeling rate of 97% within rehabilitation literature vs. 34% in nonrehabilitation journals. When synthesizing data from various specialty-specific investigations, the reported mislabeling rate appears to be highest in rehabilitation medicine at 97%,7 followed by orthopedics at 67%.8 In neurosurgery, major reviews have reported rates of 63%9 and 41%.10 In obstetrics and gynecology, a rate of 30%11 has been documented; in ophthalmology, the rate is 26.1%.12

Mislabeled study designs infiltrate evidence syntheses, distorting combined findings and undermining evidence hierarchies critical for clinical guideline development. This compromised evidence propagates through citation networks and automated retrieval systems that rely on author-reported terminology, ultimately undermining clinical decision support frameworks.13 Furthermore, such mislabeling erodes foundational epidemiological principles in medical education, perpetuating conceptual misunderstandings among beginners regarding causal inference and appropriate design selection. Collectively, these effects compromise the translational fidelity of research evidence into clinical practice and undermine the methodological foundations of evidence-based medicine.

Although multidisciplinary evidence has established the prevalence and clinical consequences of case-control mislabeling, current understanding within ophthalmology remains constrained by critical limitations. Existing evaluations are predominantly restricted to six high-impact journals,12 fail to encompass broader research scopes, and lack systematic reviews that comprehensively map mislabeling patterns across observational studies and clinical trial datasets. Consequently, there is a clear need for updated and more comprehensive data across a broader spectrum of ophthalmologic research to fully understand contemporary mislabeling patterns.

This study aimed to estimate the proportion of self-declared case-control or case-controlled studies in the recent ophthalmologic literature (2022–2023) that were mislabeled and to identify factors associated with mislabeling, such as journal impact factor and region of publication.

Methods

Study Design and Search Strategy

The study sample was derived from 95 PubMed-indexed journals classified under the “ophthalmology” category in the 2023 Journal Citation Reports (JCR) (a full list is provided in Supplementary Table S1).

A systematic PubMed search was conducted to identify English-language original clinical research articles published between January 1, 2022, and December 31, 2023. The search strategy employed the terms “case-control study” OR “case-controlled study” in the title or abstract; PubMed's Automatic Term Mapping captured variant forms (e.g., with or without hyphen). Reviews, meta-analyses, letters, corrigenda, and studies involving nonhuman subjects such as animal or in vitro experiments were excluded.

Review Process

All screening procedures incorporated blinded assessment. Journal names and impact factors were masked throughout this process to prevent potential influence of publication prestige bias. Two independent reviewers (TZ and ZPY) conducted standardized structured abstract screening guided by the rigorous methodology outlined in Designing Clinical Research14 and the epidemiological framework established in Modern Epidemiology.15 Studies exhibiting ambiguous methodological descriptions during abstract screening necessitated full-text appraisal to verify design classification. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through adjudication by a senior researcher (HXW), as detailed in Figure 1.

Review flowchart.

Study designs were classified using standardized definitions and established reporting criteria: observational designs (cohort study, case-control study, cross-sectional study) followed STROBE criteria16; experimental designs (randomized controlled trials [RCTs] and non-RCTs) adhered to the CONSORT guidelines17; descriptive studies (case series/reports) were categorized per Gordis' epidemiological framework.18 For composite designs, classification relied on quantitative assessment. Studies were designated other only if two distinct designs each represented 40% to 60% of the method; otherwise, the predominant method (>60%) determined classification. Specifically, studies classified as case-control studies underwent further determination of variant type (e.g., nested, matched). The level of evidence was standardized using the Joanna Briggs Institute's 2014 evidence hierarchy (https://jbi.global/sites/default/files/2020-07/Supporting_Doc_JBI_Levels_of_Evidence.pdf).

Through comparative analysis between author-reported designs and actual methodologies based on these criteria, two primary patterns of mislabeling were identified: (1) Overreported mislabeling: lower-to-higher evidence mislabeling; (2) under-reported mislabeling: higher-to-lower evidence mislabeling.

Data Extraction

PubMed identifiers were extracted for metadata compilation using PubMed2XL, an automated tool designed by Nitin Arora.19 Bibliographic details including publication dates, journal names, geographic regions determined by corresponding author affiliations, and JCR quartiles sourced from JCR were systematically recorded for all included studies. These variables defined the primary analytical dimensions comprising JCR quartiles and geographic regions.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.1.0.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Descriptive statistics characterized mislabeling patterns. Binary logistic regression was performed to evaluate the associations between mislabeling and JCR quartiles as well as geographic regions, with results expressed as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical significance was defined as a P value of less than 0.05.

Results

Our systematic search of 95 PubMed-indexed ophthalmologic journals identified 395 articles self-declared as case-control or case-controlled studies published between 2022 and 2023. After the exclusion of 9 noneligible publications (4 systematic reviews, 2 letters, 2 corrigenda, 1 study declared non-case-control by the authors), rigorous methodological evaluation of the remaining 386 articles confirmed that only 113 (29.3%) satisfied predefined case-control criteria. Among these validated case-control studies, 39 used variant designs including nested (n = 7), matched (n = 8), and genetic association studies (n = 24), alongside 17 composite studies that incorporated case-control components. These composite studies comprised combinations of case-control with retrospective cohort studies (n = 9), case-control with cross-sectional studies (n = 7), and case-control with prospective cohort studies (n = 1).

Critically, the vast majority were mislabeled—273 studies (70.7%). Reclassification revealed that cross-sectional studies predominated (n = 190), followed by retrospective cohort studies (n = 37), prospective cohort studies (n = 22), diagnostic tests (n = 14), RCTs (n = 3), before-and-after studies (n = 2), and non-RCTs (n = 1). Additionally, four composite studies lacked case-control components, consisting of combinations of cross-sectional and retrospective cohort studies (n = 2), cross-sectional and diagnostic tests (n = 1), and cross-sectional and prospective cohort studies (n = 1).

Logistic regression analysis revealed significant variation in the odds of mislabeling across JCR quartiles (Table 1). Using Q4 journals as the reference, articles published in Q1 journals had significantly lower odds of being mislabeled (OR, 0.511; 95% CI, 0.277–0.943). The odds of mislabeling in Q2 (OR, 1.634; 95% CI, 0.892–2.993) or Q3 journals (OR, 0.883; 95% CI, 0.416–1.875) were not statistically different from the Q4 reference. Regarding the place of publication, logistic regression showed no significant association with mislabeling. Compared with Asia (reference), the odds of mislabeling were not significantly different for Europe (OR, 0.966; 95% CI, 0.420–2.222) or North America (OR, 0.767; 95% CI, 0.330–1.784). Reclassification of study designs significantly altered evidence levels, as quantified in Table 2. Systematic analysis demonstrated that 49% of mislabeled studies exhibited upward distortion of evidence levels. This was predominantly driven by the erroneous categorization of 190 cross-sectional studies (Level 4.b) as case-control studies (Level 3.d). Conversely, 18% were subject to downgrading. This category included 59 controlled cohort studies (Level 3.c), 2 before-and-after studies (Level 2.d), 3 RCTs (Level 1.c), 1 non-RCTs (Level 2.c), and 3 composite studies (Level 3.c).

Discussion

This comprehensive analysis reveals a profound and systemic issue of methodological misclassification in recent ophthalmologic literature. Our principal finding is that more than two-thirds of self-declared case-control or case-controlled studies were mislabeled, which directly distorted the assigned levels of evidence in most cases.

Our observed mislabeling prevalence of 70.7% substantially exceeds the 26.1% previously reported within ophthalmology. This discrepancy can be attributed to the difference in journal scope; our study included a broader spectrum of journals, whereas the prior analysis was confined to six selected high-impact journals. When compared with other specialties, our rate aligns with those in orthopedics (67%, based on five high-impact journals from 2014 to 2018) and neurosurgery (63%, from a broad journal spectrum from 2010 to 2016), but is notably higher than the rate in obstetrics/gynecology (30%, derived from four leading journals in 2007). The extreme rate in rehabilitation medicine (97%, from three specialty and three general medical journals from 1998 to 2003) stands apart. These variations underscore that differences in journal inclusion criteria, time frames, and search methodologies significantly influence reported mislabeling rates across disciplines.

The widespread mislabeling of study designs undermines the hierarchical evidence framework that is essential for evidence-based practice. This phenomenon compromises the validity of systematic reviews, distorts the development of clinical guidelines, and can ultimately lead to misguided clinical decisions and resource allocation.20

The finding that top-tier journals exhibited a lower prevalence of mislabeling highlights the potential of rigorous methodological scrutiny to uphold study design standards. This finding points directly to a scalable strategy by systematically integrating robust methodological appraisal into the editorial and review processes across all journal tiers. Immediate, practical steps include mandating explicit study design declaration using controlled terminology,21 enforcing adherence to established reporting guidelines (e.g., STROBE22), and providing reviewers with structured checklists to evaluate design alignment. For sustained improvement, a dual long-term approach is recommended, combining technological support,23 such as artificial intelligence for initial screening, with strengthened foundational training in epidemiological concepts for researchers and clinicians.

The strengths of this study include its scale, systematic blinded review process, and expansion beyond prevalence quantification to assess Level of Evidence impact and bibliometric correlates. Limitations must be acknowledged. Restriction to English-language publications may introduce selection bias. Classification relied on published methodological descriptions, which may be incomplete, although our review process aimed to mitigate this factor. The Joanna Briggs Institute's Level of Evidence hierarchy focuses on design typology and does not fully capture within-design quality variations.24 We did not assess the quality of statistical reporting within studies. Finally, the search strategy was implemented without formal librarian consultation, which may affect sensitivity.

Conclusions

This analysis reveals that mislabeling of case-control studies is highly prevalent in the contemporary ophthalmologic literature and systematically distorts evidence hierarchies. Ensuring the fidelity of clinical evidence will require strengthened reporting standards, technological support, and a renewed commitment to epidemiological education.
