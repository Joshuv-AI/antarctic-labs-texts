Effect of hemostatic agents on the outcome of pulpotomy in primary and permanent teeth: A systematic review
Authors: Aakash Kumar, Monika Tandan, Mrinalini Mrinalini, Sucheta Jala, Anabathula Praharsha, Vishakha Kumar Mendiratta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimThe success of pulpotomy depends significantly on the choice of materials and techniques used, including hemostatic agents. Despite extensive research, there remains a lack of consensus on the most effective hemostatic agent for pulpotomy. This study aimed to systematically review the literature on the efficacy of hemostatic agents on the outcome of pulpotomy.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was done in the different electronic databases namely PubMed, Scopus, EBSCO host. Supplementary search included grey literature The literature search performed included all the relevant articles published up to 31st March 2025. The risk of Bias for the in vivo studies was evaluated by JBI critical appraisal tool and New Castle Ottawa scale for cohort study and retrospective studies and qualitative synthesis was evaluated using National Services Scotland guidelines Meta analysis could not be performed because of the heterogeneity of the studies.ResultsA total of eight studies were included in this review. Five studies concluded that sodium hypochlorite was more effective as a hemostatic agent in both primary teeth and permanent teeth. Two studies found potassium titanyl phosphate (KTP) laser treatment produced superior clinical and radiographic outcomes in permanent teeth. Another study showed better results with cryotherapy in the permanent teeth.ConclusionSodium hypochlorite demonstrated superior hemostatic potential in primary teeth, whereas in permanent teeth, potassium titanyl phosphate (KTP) laser and cryotherapy yielded promising results in clinical and radiographic outcomes with no statistically significant results when compared to sodium hypochlorite.

Introduction

Pulpotomy is regarded as an effective, vital pulp therapy procedure for primary teeth with pulp exposure due to dental caries or trauma. It involves the partial/complete removal of infected or inflamed coronal pulp and preservation of the radicular pulp by the placement of a medicament, followed by an adequate coronal seal.1 However with better understanding of the disease process and availability of the improved medicament recent studies have shown pulpotomy as an effective treatment option even in mature permanent teeth. The factors affecting the outcome of pulpotomy can be broadly classified under patient, intra-operative and post operative factors.

Patient's age, gender, tooth type, stage of tooth development, general drugs used, depth of caries, kind of exposure, degree of pulpal infection, and periapical alterations play an important role on clinical and radiographical outcome. Intraoperative factors like isolation, pulpal lavage, pulpal hemorrhage, hemostatic agents, pulpotomy agent, and type of final restoration also influence the outcomes. The post-operative considerations include hard tissue bridge formation, time interval before definitive restoration placement, periodontal health, and effectiveness of the coronal seal.2

One of the major parameters affecting the success of pulpotomy is the ability to control bleeding from the amputated pulp tissue. Selection of hemostatic agent during pulpotomy has been a subject of considerable research and debate with respect to their influence on clinical and radiographic outcome. The hemostatic agent not only provides haemostasis but also a sterile environment which is necessary for pulp healing. Excessive bleeding that is difficult to control usually indicates that the pulpal tissue has been irreversibly damaged. The commonly used hemostatic agents include sodium hypochlorite, ferric sulphate, LASERs (Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation), and formocresol but American Association of Endodontics (2021) recommends that sodium hypochlorite can be used safely in direct contact with pulp tissue at different concentrations without adversely affecting the pulp.3

Sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) has been used routinely as a hemostatic agent that can control pulpal bleeding by preventing fibrin and clot formation. Sodium hypochlorite also has the ability to disinfect and remove dentin chips, thereby promoting healing following pulpotomy. A randomized control trial used 2.5 percent sodium hypochlorite and 0.9 percent sterile saline as hemostatic agent followed by the placement of mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA) and calcium hydroxide as pulpotomy medicament in immature permanent teeth. The results showed comparable findings in terms of clinical and radiographic outcomes.4 Studies have been done comparing formocresol and ferric sulphate (FS) in primary teeth and showed that formoscresol had better clinical and radiographic success rate after 12 months follow up, although the difference in the results was not statistically significant.5 Recently effectiveness of LASERs in arresting pulpal bleeding has been evaluated. The potassium titanyl phosphate (KTP) LASER or NaOCl-assisted pulpotomy was found to be an effective treatment for pain reduction in permanent teeth with symptomatic irreversible pulpitis 3 and the utilization of KTP LASER demonstrated enhanced efficacy in alleviating pain when compared to sodium hypochlorite.6

Sodium hypochlorite is the most commonly used hemostatic agent for pulpotomy as reported by existing literature and for the review to be consistent, a common comparator group is desired; therefore most of the studies comparing the effect of hemostasis against standard sodium hypochlorite were included in the review. Several clinical trials have been documented with the aim of assessing the outcome of hemostatic agents. However, to the authors’ knowledge no systematic review has been published till date comparing the effect of different hemostatic agent on the outcome of pulpotomy in both primary and permanent teeth.

Aim

The aim of this systematic review was to assess the influence of hemostatic agents on the outcome of pulpotomy in primary and permanent teeth.

Objective

The primary objective of this review was to evaluate the influence of hemostatic agents on the clinical and radiographic outcome of pulpotomy in the primary and permanent teeth.

Materials and methods

Protocol and registration

Protocol for this systematic review was registered in PROSPERO (number CRD42024511297) and followed the PRISMA guidelines (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta- Analysis) using the PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Study) framework (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Prisma flow diagram 2020 depicting the flow from identification to screening and selecting the articles for systematic review.Fig. 1

Eligibility criteria

The study was designed according to the following PICOS framework. Primary and permanent molars undergoing pulpotomy (Population), different hemostatic agents (Intervention), sodium hypochlorite (Comparison), clinical success and radiographic success (Outcome), in vivo studies, cross sectional studies, retrospective studies, randomized controlled trials and clinical trials (Study type).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for the systematic review were as follows: Randomized control trials, clinical trials, in vivo studies, retrospective studies, articles published in English language and, studies which were available in full text were included.

Exclusion criteria included in-vitro studies, case reports, studies without comparison to any group or any comparative hemostatic agents other than sodium hypochlorite, studies without defined/mentioned follow up time period and studies performed on teeth other than molars.

Selection process

The procedure for screening and selecting studies was completed in two stages. In the first stage two independent reviewers (A.K. and M.T.) performed the initial screening of titles of the articles that were obtained from the search results. Following this, the studies were screened based on inclusion and exclusion criteria after reading their abstracts. Articles that failed to satisfy the eligibility requirements were eliminated. The full texts of the selected studies were evaluated for additional assessment. In the second stage, the list of selected studies was compared, and any differences were settled after discussion with the third reviewer (M.M.).

Quality assessment (risk of bias assessment)

Two reviewers (A K. and M.T.) assessed the studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tool (Table 4 a) (Table 5) for assessment of risk of bias for randomized controlled trials. This tool evaluates the following parameters-bias related to selection and allocation; administration of intervention; assessment; detection and measurement of the outcome; participant retention and statistical conclusion validity. They independently answered the thirteen questions on this assessment instrument with the words “yes,” “no,” “unclear,” or “not applicable.” Studies were categorized as high quality if their JBI score was greater than 80 %, medium quality if their score was between 50 % and 80 %, and poor quality if their score was less than 50 %. New castle ottawa scale was used for cohort study and retrospective study (Table 4b, Table 4cc).This scale uses a 9 star rating to determine the quality of the study. Results

Literature search process

The initial search yielded 466 studies in the databases mentioned in PRISMA flowchart. After the initial screening 45 records were removed due to duplication, 5 as ineligible by automation tools and 3 were excluded due to lack of access to full text articles. Out of the remaining 413 records, 395 were excluded based on title and abstract screening and 3 records could not be retrieved. Of the 15 eligible studies which were read in full text, only 8 were included in the present system following the inclusion and exclusion criteria. A manual search for the references of the included studies yielded no additional studies to be included. The following studies were included in this review: Akhil et al. (2024), Ayhan et al. (2023), Dronamraju et al. (2023), Akman et al. (2023), Atasever et al. (2019), Hemavathi et al. (2018), Ozgur et al. (2017), Akcay et al. (2014). Six of the included studies were randomized controlled trials whereas one of the remaining two studies was a cohort study while the other was a retrospective study. The data extracted from these studies has been summarized in Table 2, Table 3 Due to significant clinical and methodological heterogeneity among the included studies, along with variations in outcome measures, generating a forest plot and calculating the I2 statistic was not feasible. The primary sources of heterogeneity included differences in study designs, the type of dentition selected, the hemostatic agent used in the comparator group, the pulpotomy agent employed, the type of final restoration, the duration of follow-up, and the specific outcomes assessed, as detailed in Table (VI). Hence applying meta-analysis in this current review was not possible. Subgroup analysis was also not considered due to lack of sufficient studies with similar outcomes.

Qualitative synthesis of the result

Tooth selected

Among the included studies, 4 studies [Akhil et al. (2024), Ayhan et al. (2023), Dronamraju et al. (2023), Ozgur et al. (2017)] had done pulpotomy on permanent teeth in which 1 study [Ozgur et al. (2017)] included immature permanent teeth. In the remaining 4 studies [Akman et al. (2023), Atasever et al. (2019), Hemavathi et al. (2018), Akcay et al. (2014)] pulpotomy was performed in primary teeth.

Sample size

The number of teeth included in the selected studies ranged from 50 to 142 teeth with 30 teeth treated with cryotherapy, 80 with KTP LASER, 111 with ferric sulphate, 132 with saline, and 355 with sodium hypochlorite (concentration ranging from 1.25 % to 5 %).

Hemostatic agent selected

In all the studies sodium hypochlorite was used as one group with the concentration ranging from 1.25 % to 5.25 % and other hemostatic agents were used as experimental groups. Akhil et al. (2024) used cryotherapy to arrest bleeding in the experimental group. In the studies by Ayhan et al. (2023) and Dronamraju et al. (2023), KTP LASER was used as a hemostatic agent. Akman et al. (2023), and Atasever et al. (2019) used 15.5 % ferric sulphate for 15 s to control bleeding. Hemavathi et al. (2018), Ozgur (2017) et al. and Akcay et al. (2014) used 0.9 % sterile saline solution as the hemostatic agent.

Pulpotomy agent selected

In most of the studies, the pulpotomy agent used was either calcium hydroxide or MTA. Akhil et al. (2024), Ayhan et al. (2023) used MTA as pulpotomy agent. Ozgur et al. (2017) used MTA and calcium hydroxide in both the groups. Dronamraju et al. (2023) used calcium hydroxide as the pulpal medicament. Atasever et al. (2019) and Hemavathi et al. (2018) employed ZOE and calcium hydroxide as pulpotomy medicaments respectively. Akman et al. (2023), Akcay et al. (2014) used resin bonded ZOE as pulpotomy agent.

Method of evaluation

In five of the included studies [Akhil et al. (2024), Atasever et al. (2019), Hemavathi et (2018), Ozgur et al. (2017) and Akcay et al. (2014)] clinical and radiographical finding were used for assessing the outcomes. The clinical evaluation was conducted by taking history of pain, tenderness of the concerned tooth, presence/absence of mobility and fistula. Radiographic evaluation was assessed by evaluating periapical changes like internal/external resorption, widening of PDL space, loss of lamina dura and periapical radiolucency. Akman et al. (2023) evaluated only radiographic findings based on the above-mentioned parameters. In the two studies [Ayhan et al. (2023), Dronamraju et al. (2023) et al.] only clinical outcomes were evaluated by measuring the post operative pain clinically by using visual analogue scale (VAS) at different time intervals.

Follow up period

The follow-up period ranged from 8 days to 1 year. However, in the five studies [Akhil et al. (2024), Akman et al. (2023), Atasever et al. (2019), Hemavathi et al. (2018) and Akcay et al. (2014)] the minimum follow up period consisted of 12 months where as in a study by Ozgur et al. (2017) follow up period of 24 months was considered. In the studies conducted by Ayhan et al. (2023) and Dronamraju et al. (2023), the follow up period was limited to 30 days and 8 days respectively as their evaluation was based on clinical examination and post operative pain score values.

Type of restoration done

The restorations done after pulpotomy ranged from GIC to stainless steel crowns and the decision seemed to be based on the type of tooth being treated. Akhil et al. (2024), Dronamraju et al. (2023), Akman et al. (2023), Ozgur et al. (2017) placed GIC followed by composite while only GIC was placed as a restorative material by Ayhan et al. (2023). However, Stainless steel crowns were given by Atasever et al. (2019), Hemavathi et al. (2018), and Akcay et al. (2014) after using GIC as base in former two studies and reinforced ZOE in the last study.

Main outcome

Six of the included studies [Akhil et al. (2024), Hemavathi et al. (2018). Akman et al. (2023), Atasever et al. (2019), Hemavathi et al. (2018), and Akcay et al. (2014)] had used the clinical and radiographic criteria for determining the outcomes of different hemostatic agent in pulpotomy of primary and permanent teeth and found that there was no statistically significant difference between the various agents. However, two studies [Dronamraju et al. (2023), Ayhan et al. (2023)] had evaluated post operative pain at different time intervals of 8 days and 30 days. One study [Akhil et al. (2024)] evaluated both post operative pain and radiographical outcome at the end of one year. In all these studies the exponential groups, that is [LASER and cryotherapy] showed significantly lower post operative pain at 6 h–24 h mark but no significant difference were observed at other time intervals. Two studies [Akman et al. (2023) and Atasever et al. (2019)] have reported internal resorption as the common cause of failure in primary teeth.

Confounders and control

The various confounders that were present in the studies were material variability, patient age, treatment method, restoration factors, study type and techniques used. However, the variables such as pulpotomy agent, restoration type, and patient age were not controlled uniformly across studies, current evidence suggests that these factors do not significantly confound the observed effects of sodium hypochlorite as a hemostatic agent. Quality of included studies (risk of bias)

The risk of bias was assessed using the JBI critical appraisal tool for the assessment of randomized controlled trials and is shown in Table 4 a. The studies by Akhil et al. (2024), Atasever et al. (2019) Hemavathi et al. (2018), Ozgur et al. (2017) were considered having low risk of bias while two studies Ayhan et al. (2023) and Akcay et al. (2014) showed moderate risk of bias. For studies by Dronamraju et al (2023) and Akman et al (2023) New castle ottawa scale was used for cohort study and retrospective study respectively (Table 4b, Table 4c c). Both studies show good quality and low risk of bias. For each study, the evidence level was determined by following the guidelines given by National Services Scotland. Four studies [Akhil et al. (2024), Atasever et al. (2019), Hemavathi et al. (2018), Ozgur et al. (2017)] had evidence level of 1+ and the remaining two studies had the evidence level of 2+. Cohen's kappa (between author A.K. and M.T.) was 0.71.

Discussion

This systematic review was aimed at assessing the effectiveness of various hemostatic agents on the outcome of pulpotomy in both primary and permanent teeth. A growing area of research from several systematic reviews and randomized clinical trials have shown that the teeth treated with pulpotomy for carious pulp exposures and ensuing pulpitis have significant success rates and increased chances of survival (5,7,8)Most of the studies till date have focused on different types of pulpotomy agents and evaluated their effect on the clinical and radiographic outcomes. There is a paucity of literature available in relation to the effect of hemostatic agents on the outcome of pulpotomy and, to the best of our knowledge, there are no published systematic reviews assessing this parameter.

Before placing any pulpotomy medicament, the control of bleeding from the pulpal tissue is of prime importance. This is because if bleeding doesn't stop then blood would be continuously oozing out resulting in dislodged medicament which will form thick fibro purulent membrane over the pulpal tissue creating a dead space paving way for the secondary infection and subsequent failure of the procedure. Hence to prevent this, hemostatic agents are being employed. There are multiple hemostatic agents being used in primary and permanent teeth.9

One of the most popular hemostatic agents used is sodium hypochlorite. It has tissue dissolution capacity by interfering with the cytoplasmic membrane integrity leading to irreversible enzymatic inhibition, biosynthetic change in cell metabolism, and phospholipid destruction in lipid peroxidation. It also helps in the formation of dentinal bridge10. It has unique properties of simultaneous disinfection of dentine–pulp interface and chemical amputation of necrotic cells and blood clot. This ensures direct contact between pulp tissue and the capping material facilitating wound healing.11

In order to improve the effectiveness of vital pulp therapy, NaOCl has been used to disinfect and remove dentin chips, fibrin, and clot, and to stop the bleeding from the pulpal tissue.9,10,12,13 Inspite of severe toxic effects of sodium hypochlorite, studies suggest that its effect is only limited to the superficial layer of cells and does not cause any damage to the deeper pulpal tissue (10,13).

Ferric sulphate has gained popularity as hemostatic agent especially for primary teeth. When it encounters blood, it produces a ferric ion-protein complex, which is a coagulative and acts as hemostatic agent. The agglutinated protein complex occludes capillary orifices, preventing the development of blood clots in the remaining pulp tissue, and it physically closes the injured arteries, resulting in hemostasis. The most important characteristic of ferric sulphate is devitalization and preservation of the pulpal tissue. The formation of metal-protein barrier prevents the entry of any external component in the pulp and hence minimizing the inflammation.14

Akman et al. (2024) and Atasever et al. (2019) used ferric sulphate, sterile saline and sodium hypochlorite as hemostatic agent in primary teeth and showed that sodium hypochlorite had better clinical and radiographic success rate irrespective of the concentration at which it was used (15,16).

Several studies have also used sterile saline to arrest bleeding owing to its biocompatibility. However, sterile saline has no hemostatic or disinfectant potential of its own. It is generally used in combination with cotton pellets over the pulp with light pressure to achieve hemostasis. Its main mechanism of action is through pressure application.

In the studies done by Akcay et al. (2014), Ozgur et al. (2017) and Hemavathi et al. (2018), sodium hypochlorite was compared to sterile saline solution. On comparing the clinical and radiographic outcomes of these three studies, Akcay et al. (2014) and Hemavathi et al. (2018) showed that sodium hypochlorite along with MTA had better clinical and radiographic success. However, Ozgur et al. (2017) showed greater success rate when calcium hydroxide pulpotomy was conducted using sterile saline as hemostatic agent. One of the main reasons for this disparity might be the concentration of sodium hypochlorite (2.5 %) used. Silva et al. (2006) in their study observed no inflammatory response when saline was used as hemostatic agent and mild inflammatory response with sodium hypochlorite. Accorinte et al. (2007) evaluated the response of human pulp capped teeth with calcium hydroxide after using saline and 5 % sodium hypochlorite as hemostatic agent and concluded that better histological response was seen with saline solution.17

In recent years the use of LASERS has increased tremendously in the field of dentistry. It is because of its several advantages over the conventional techniques like decreased time for hemostasis by coagulating the blood vessels, lower risk of contamination, sterile operating field and alleviation of postoperative complications by increasing the proliferation of fibroblasts.18

In a study by Dronamraju et al. (2023) KTP LASER of 532 nm wavelength was used as hemostatic agent for 2 s in permanent teeth.6 The pain score with KTP LASER was significantly lower than that with 2.5 % sodium hypochlorite. Ayhan et al. (2023) also showed that KTP laser had lower postoperative pain when compared to sodium hypochlorite. The effect of bio stimulation associated with LASERs along with antibacterial activity improves wound healing without any complications. Hence LASERS provide better analgesia.3

KTP LASER (Potassium-Titanyl-Phosphate (KTiOPo4) uses double frequency of a neodymium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet (Nd:YAG). When used at 532 nm, it gets absorbed by oxyhemoglobin in a specific manner. Moreover, it gets absorbed only in the superficial layers of the oral tissues and does not penetrate the deeper structures. Liu conducted a study evaluating the effects of Nd: YAG laser pulpotomy and formocresol pulpotomy in primary human teeth. The clinical and radiographic results revealed that Nd:YAG laser pulpotomy had a significantly higher success rate compared to formocresol pulpotomy.19

Cryotherapy achieves its hemostatic effect by vasoconstriction. When cold is applied, cold-sensitive receptors are stimulated. These receptors not only transmit thermal information centrally but also operate locally on sympathetic vasoconstrictors to trigger the release of norepinephrine, which results in vasoconstriction. It causes vasoconstriction as well as an increase in blood viscosity and a decrease in blood flow velocity. It prevents the production of inflammatory mediators such as histamines, bradykinins, and prostaglandins contributing to its anti-inflammatory properties.

Pulpotomy combined with cryotherapy has shown a considerable decrease in post operative pain as reported by Akhil et al. (2024).20 This is because the removal of the diseased coronal pulp tissue lowers local tissue pressure and inflammatory mediator concentration, nociceptive sense neurons' axonal termination occurs, thus reducing pain. Lowering body temperature also reduces peripheral nerve transmission; myelinated A-δ fibers and unmyelinated C-fibers completely deactivate at 7 °C and 3 °C, respectively. When compared to traditional pulpotomy, cryotherapy's analgesic effects may be the cause of improved post-operative pain management. The overall success rate after 12 months with both sodium hypochlorite and cryotherapy were similar.

Within this review there were only eight studies which satisfied the inclusion and exclusion criteria and most of the studies showed satisfactory outcomes with sodium hypochlorite as being one of the hemostatic agents of choice. However, three studies [Akhil et al. (2024), Ahyan et al. (2023 Dronamraju et al. (2023)] reported cryotherapy and KTP LASERS to have better clinical and radiographic outcomes when compared with sodium hypochlorite. Hence, within the scope of this review, sodium hypochlorite regardless of the concentration continues to serve as the reference standard for achieving hemostasis in pulpotomy which may be attributed to the extensive body of literature comparing sodium hypochlorite with other hemostatic agents. Furthermore, to maintain consistency in the comparator group, only studies that included sodium hypochlorite in at least one arm were considered for inclusion in this review.

Risk of bias was analysed using JBI risk of bias tool, new castle Ottawa scale for cohort study and retrospective study. Most of the studies showed low to moderate risk. For qualitative synthesis NSS tool was used which showed high to moderate quality of evidence. After accounting for all the confounding variables outlined in the review, it was concluded that they have no significant influence on the effectiveness of hemostasis.

Meta analysis couldn't be performed due to significant heterogeneity among the studies and have different outcome measures hence a subgroup analysis was also not possible.

Despite systematic database searches and stringent selection criteria, this systematic review has certain limitations. These include the limited number of included studies, exclusion of non-English publications, inconsistency in the concentrations of sodium hypochlorite used in the control groups, and variation in follow-up durations. Additionally, due to substantial heterogeneity among the studies, a meta-analysis could not be performed. However, the review's strengths lie in its comprehensive evaluation of the efficacy of hemostatic agents in both primary and permanent teeth, as well as the inclusion of studies with low to moderate risk of bias.

This study holds clinical relevance by guiding the selection of effective hemostatic agents for use in both primary and permanent dentition. Appropriate selection may enhance the success of pulpotomy procedures in both children and adults, potentially reducing the need for more complex interventions such as non-surgical root canal therapy or surgical procedures often required in advanced stages of endodontic disease.

Hence in the future, high quality randomized controlled trials comparing efficacy of various homeostatic agents with statistically significant results are required for better understanding for standardized protocols for hemostatic and similar outcome measures so that quantitative measures can be applied for a conclusive result.

Conclusion

Within the scope of this systematic review, sodium hypochlorite appears to be a promising hemostatic agent for pulpotomy in primary teeth. Additionally, KTP laser and cryotherapy show encouraging results in permanent teeth, both in terms of postoperative pain reduction and clinical and radiographic outcomes but the results are statistically insignificant when compared to sodium hypochlorite. However, with the strict inclusion criteria, only a few studies could be included in this study. Most of the studies included in the present review demonstrate statistically non significant results hence more high quality, long term randomized controlled trials are required in the future which assess the hemostatic efficacy of various pulpotomy agents in both primary and permanent teeth having similar outcome measures so that meta analysis can be applied for conclusive results.
