Risk factors for surgical-site infections in oral cancer patients with microvascular free flap reconstruction–A retrospective study in a single medical center in Taiwan
Authors: Pei-Yin Chen, Yu-Wei Chiu, Shih-Min Wang, Min Yee Ng, Cheng-Chia Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/purposeSurgical site infections (SSI) represent a common complication in head and neck cancer surgery, often leading to prolonged hospitalization, increased healthcare costs, and compromised oncological outcomes. This study aimed to identify risk factors associated with SSI in patients undergoing head and neck cancer surgery with free flap reconstruction at a single medical center in Taiwan.Materials and methodsThis retrospective study included 216 patients who underwent free flap reconstruction between August 2017 and December 2023. Cases of incomplete data, preoperative infections, death within 30 days post-surgery, and non-malignancy were excluded. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, and continuous variables using the t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify independent risk factors. Statistical analysis was performed using JMP Pro version 16.0.ResultsThe overall SSI rate was 43 % (93/216). On multivariate analysis, longer operative duration increased the risk of SSI (odds ratio [OR]1.004 per minute; 95 % confidence interval [CI] 1.000–1.008). Patients with SSI had significantly prolonged length of stay (LOS) (OR 1.065; 95 % CI 1.002–1.131). Notably, smoking cessation was associated with a higher SSI risk compared to patients who continued smoking (OR 2.709; 95 % CI 1.349–5.439). Regarding clinical T staging, T4a tumors demonstrated lower infection rates (OR 0.142; 95 % CI 1.213–91.212) compared to T4b cases, with T1 and T2 stages showing similar trends (P = 0.0518, P = 0601). For tumor location, a higher infection rate was noted on buccal mucosa (OR 4.998; 95 % CI 1.129–22.127) and lower gingiva (OR 5.276; 95 % CI 1.202–23.161) compared to upper gingival tumors.ConclusionFormer smoking, prolonged operation time, advanced tumor stage, and location of tumor are significant predictors of SSI in oral and maxillofacial free flap reconstruction. Special attention must be paid to infection prevention when dealing with this patient population.Keywords: Surgical site infection, Free flap reconstruction, Oral cancer, Risk factors, Smoking, Duration of operation

Introduction

Oral cancer remains a major global health burden, accounting for significant morbidity and mortality worldwide. 1 Surgical resection with curative intent is the cornerstone of treatment, yet it often results in extensive soft tissue and bony defects. Over the past three decades, advances in microvascular free flap reconstruction have made it the preferred technique for restoring function and form, surpassing regional or pedicled flaps and enabling more aggressive tumor resections.2 Current studies from high-volume centers report free flap survival rates exceeding 95 %, with average failure rates of approximately 5 %.3

Despite these advances, postoperative complications remain common and clinically consequential. Surgical site infections (SSI) are particularly problematic in head and neck surgery, where a clean-contaminated operative field and exposure to the oral microbiome contribute to infection rates ranging from 3 % to 41 %.4–7 SSI can prolong hospitalization, impair wound healing, predispose to fistula formation, and increase the risk of flap compromise. Multiple risk factors of SSI have been reported, including advanced tumor stage, higher ASA class, prolonged operative duration, perioperative blood transfusion, poor oral hygiene, smoking, and prior chemoradiotherapy.8–10 However, findings across studies remain inconsistent. For instance, some analyses link advanced tumor stage to higher SSI rates,11 other cohort studies and meta-analysis fail to identify tumor stage as an independent predictor.5,12 These discrepancies suggest that the impact of tumor stage may depend on study design and population. Importantly, the relative impact of preoperative versus postoperative tumor staging, and the role of tumor location as an independent predictor, remain poorly defined.

Given the multifactorial etiology of SSI and the conflicting evidence in the literature, further evaluation of independent risk factors in specific populations is warranted. Most published evidence derives from Western cohorts; little is known about SSI determinants in Taiwanese patients, who may differ in oral health practices, comorbidities, and perioperative care protocols. Clarifying the determinants of SSI in Taiwanese patients undergoing microvascular free flap reconstruction may improve perioperative risk stratification and guide targeted preventive strategies.

Therefore, we conducted a retrospective cohort study to analyze the risk factors associated with SSI in oral cancer patients undergoing microvascular free flap reconstruction, with particular emphasis on tumor stage (both preoperative and postoperative) and tumor location. By addressing existing uncertainties within a well-defined Taiwanese cohort, this study seeks to provide clearer insights into the independent predictors of SSI and to inform evidence-based strategies for refining patient selection, perioperative protocols, and infection prevention in head and neck oncologic surgery.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient enrollment

This retrospective cohort analysis was conducted at a Chung Shan Medical University Hospital, reviewing the medical records of 242 patients who underwent microvascular free flap reconstruction following head and neck cancer resection at oral maxillo-facial surgery (OMFS) department in Taiwan between August 2017 and December 2023. The study received institutional ethics board approval. Patients were excluded if they had a pre-operative infection (n = 11), died within 30 days post-operatively (n = 1), or had non-malignant conditions (n = 14). A total of 216 patients were ultimately included in the final study cohort. The flowchart of patient’s selection in this study was outlined in Fig. 1.

Data collection

Patient demographics, preoperative, and intraoperative data were collected from electronic medical records. Key demographic and lifestyle variables included age, gender, ASA score, oral habits, and comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus and hypertension. Tumor and treatment-related variables such as diagnosis, previous history of chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and operations as well as pathological and clinical tumor stages, were also recorded. Lastly, operative data comprised the duration of the surgery, amount of blood loss, whether a tracheostomy was performed, neck dissection or not, type of reconstruction, and whether the use of induction chemotherapy were employed.

Statistical analysis

The outcome was the occurrence of SSI within 30 days postoperatively, defined by Taiwan Centers for Disease Control criteria.13 Data were analyzed for associations with SSI, with categorical variables compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test and continuous variables using the t-test or Mann–Whitney U test. Multivariate logistic regression was utilized to identify independent risk factors. The multivariate model was adjusted for potential confounding variables, including intraoperative blood loss, smoking status, tracheostomy, and tumor laterality. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant, and all analyses were performed using JMP Pro version 16.0.

As shown in Table 1, no significant association was observed between SSI and several demographic or lifestyle variables, including gender (P = 0.6171), betel nut use (P = 0.6271), diabetes mellitus (P = 0.9746) and hypertension (P = 0.3448). However, a higher American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score was significantly associated with an increased risk of SSI (P = 0.0437). Interestingly, smoking status demonstrated an unexpected trend: current smokers exhibited the lowest infection rate compared with both former smokers and nonsmokers (P = 0.0359).

Longer operative duration was independently associated with an increased risk of SSI (OR 1.004; 95 % CI 1.000–1.008; P = 0.0408). Similarly, extended hospital stays correlated with a higher likelihood of infection (OR 1.065; 95 % CI 1.002–1.131; P = 0.0334).

Regarding pathological T staging, patients with T2 tumors had a significantly greater risk of SSI compared with those with T4b tumors (OR 11.706; 95 % CI 1.151–119.022; P = 0.0376), and T4a tumors showed a comparable pattern (OR 10.516; 95 % CI 1.213–91.212; P = 0.0328). In contrast, analysis of clinical T staging revealed that clinical T4a tumors were associated with a significantly lower risk of SSI than T4b tumors (OR 0.142; 95 % CI 1.213–91.212; P = 0.0249). Clinical T1 and T2 stages showed similar trends, although these did not achieve statistical significance (P = 0.0518 and P = 0.0601, respectively).

In terms of tumor location, buccal mucosa (OR 4.998; 95 % CI 1.129–22.127; P = 0.0340) and lower gingival tumors (OR 5.276; 95 % CI 1.202–23.161; P = 0.0275) demonstrated significantly higher infection risks compared with upper gingival tumors. Lesions on the mandible and tongue showed elevated but statistically nonsignificant risks (P = 0.4367 and P = 0.0737, respectively).

These results indicate that longer operative time, increased hospital stay, pathological and clinical T stage and tumor location, are independent key factors associated with SSI development.

Discussion

SSI remains one of the most common complications following free flap reconstruction in the head and neck region, primarily due to the clean-contaminated nature of the surgical field. This retrospective study analyzed cases of free flap reconstruction performed after head and neck cancer resection at the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery of a single institution between August 2017 and December 2023. Reported incidences of SSI in the literature vary widely, ranging from 18 % to 41 %.4–7 In the present study, the infection rate was 43 %, which is at the higher end of the reported range.

The univariate analysis identified several factors significantly associated with an increased risk of SSI, including higher ASA score, history of smoking (particularly former smokers), prolonged operative duration and hospitalization, and greater intraoperative blood loss. Higher ASA score reflects poorer systemic health and diminished physiological reserve, conditions known to impair wound healing and immune response. This association has been similarly reported by Shuman et al.14 and Gugliotta et al.,9 who both found that elevated ASA classification increased postoperative infection rates in head and neck free flap reconstruction. Smoking, even after cessation, has been shown to cause persistent microvascular damage and tissue hypoxia,15 which may contribute to postoperative infection risk.16 However, in the present study, potential reporting bias should be considered, as the exact duration of smoking cessation could not be retrieved, which may have influenced the classification of former and current smokers.

Prolonged operative and hospitalization time and increased blood loss likely reflect greater surgical complexity, leading to extended tissue exposure and reduced perfusion, thereby facilitating bacterial contamination. 8,10 In our multivariate analysis, operative duration and hospitalization time remained as independent predictors of SSI. Each additional minute of surgery increasing the risk (OR 1.004; 95 % CI 1.000–1.008). This finding aligns with previous studies demonstrating a positive correlation between longer operative times and SSI incidence.4,6,7 In these studies, operation time was also treated as a continuous variable, underscoring the cumulative effect of surgical duration on infection risk. Conversely, when operation time was categorized into discrete intervals, as in the studies by Beydoun et al.17 (<9 h vs ≥ 9 h) and Bozikov and Arnez (<10 h vs ≥ 10 h),18 no significant differences were observed. These discrepancies suggest that analyzing operation time as a continuous variable may more accurately reflect its incremental impact on SSI risk.

Similarly, prolonged hospitalization was also an independent risk factor for SSI (OR 1.065; 95 % CI 1.002–1.131); though this should not be interpreted as a direct causal relationship. Extended hospital stay days may reflect the underlying complexity of surgery or patient condition, as well as the impact of postoperative complications, including infections.4,7,9,19 This observation is consistent with existing literature showing that postoperative infections contribute substantially to delayed discharge and increased healthcare costs.8 Prolonged hospitalization can further postpone adjuvant treatments such as radiotherapy, thereby potentially affecting oncologic outcomes. 6,8,20 Reducing the incidence of SSI is therefore critical not only for improving patient recovery but also for optimizing overall treatment timelines and resource utilization.21

The relationship between tumor stage and SSI has been inconsistently reported in previous studies. Eskander et al. found no significant difference in infection rates between early (T1–T2) and advanced (T3–T4) stages,22 while Gugliotta et al. observed a higher infection rate among patients with advanced tumors.9 Although some studies have employed detailed T staging classifications ranging from T1 to T4b, most have not demonstrated significant differences in SSI rates across stages,7 and multivariate analyses have similarly failed to show statistical significance. 4 In the present study, clinical and postoperative pathological tumor stages were analyzed independently to account for observed discrepancies between preoperative clinical assessment and final pathological findings. The results revealed that, compared with pT4b tumors, patients with pT2 lesions exhibited the highest risk of SSI (OR 11.70). In contrast, analysis based on clinical staging showed that cT4a tumors were associated with a significantly lower infection rate than cT4b tumors (OR 0.142), while cT1 and cT2 stages demonstrated a similar trend toward reduced infection risk.

Given that surgical extent and reconstruction complexity are primarily determined by clinical staging, our findings suggest that advanced clinical stages are generally associated with higher SSI risk. This highlights the potential value of clinical staging as a preoperative indicator for infection risk stratification.
