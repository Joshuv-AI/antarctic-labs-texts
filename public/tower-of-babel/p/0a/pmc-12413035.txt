Addressing male HPV vaccination in China: A narrative review of challenges and opportunities
Authors: Li Ping Wong, Xinyu Chen, Zhiwen Huang, Gregory Zimet, Qinjian Zhao, Yulan Lin, Zhijian Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHuman papillomavirus (HPV) poses significant health risks to men, including genital warts and HPV-related cancers. In China, the recent approval of HPV vaccination for males aged 9–26 marks an important step toward gender-inclusive prevention. However, challenges remain in promoting vaccine acceptance among men due to the perception of HPV as a “women’s disease,” limited public awareness, cultural stigma, and low perceived susceptibility, particularly among heterosexual men. Men who have sex with men (MSM) may demonstrate relatively higher willingness to vaccinate. Vaccine confidence may be influenced by historical vaccine safety concerns and the spread of misinformation, particularly online. Although healthcare provider recommendations may significantly improve uptake, past studies indicate that many men report receiving little or no guidance. This review highlights the need for early education, culturally appropriate messaging, and engagement with trusted community figures. Gender-neutral vaccine promotion and appeals to social responsibility may enhance confidence and uptake among Chinese men.KEYWORDS: HPV vaccination, HPV vaccine hesitancy

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is one of the most prevalent sexually transmitted infections and a leading cause of multiple cancers worldwide. HPV causes around 5% of all human cancers.1 It also causes genital warts, the most common sexually transmitted viral manifestation, with a lifetime risk of acquisition at approximately 10%.2 In 2019 alone, HPV contributed to approximately 620,000 new cancer cases in women and 70,000 in men globally. In China, the burden has been especially pronounced, whereby cervical cancer cases increasing by approximately 2.7 folds, from around 40,681 cases in 1990 to nearly 109,760 in 2019. During this period, the average number of new cervical cancer cases per 100,000 people annually also climbed from 8.41 in 1990 to 11.01 in 2019.3

While cervical cancer remains the most common HPV-associated malignancy, the virus is also responsible for other serious cancers, including those of the anus, penis, vulva, vagina, and oropharynx.4 Men, particularly those who have sex with men and individuals living with HIV, face rising rates of anal cancer, with incidence levels comparable to cervical cancer in unscreened women.5,6 Additionally, HPV-related head and neck cancers, especially oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinomas, have sharply increased among men in recent decades.7,8 Another disease caused by HPV infection is recurrent respiratory papillomatosis (RRP), a condition characterized by wart-like growths that can occlude the air passages. Treatment of RRP frequently requires multiple surgeries over time.9

Historically, global HPV prevention strategies have focused on women due to the strong link between HPV and cervical cancer. However, growing international recognition of HPV’s impact on male health, as well as the role of males in the transmission of the virus to women, has led to calls for more gender-inclusive vaccination approaches. That is, males can directly benefit from vaccination and females can indirectly benefit, as well, from male vaccination.

Beyond its role in cancer prevention, HPV vaccination may also reduce the risk of fertility-related complications. Sucato et al. highlighted that HPV infection can adversely impact sperm DNA integrity, motility, viability, and morphology, and may also trigger the production of anti-sperm antibodies, ultimately impairing male reproductive potential.10 Similarly, a recent meta-analysis by Garolla et al. found that HPV infection in men is significantly associated with reduced sperm quality, particularly morphology and motility, as well as decreased couple fertility and an increased risk of pregnancy loss.11 In addition, Sucato et al. reported that HPV was detected in 66.7% of male partners of HPV-positive women attending cervical cancer screening, with high-risk genotypes found in 90% of these cases.12 Their findings demonstrated that the nonavalent HPV vaccine has greater preventive potential compared to the quadrivalent vaccine, covering 62.5% of vaccine-detectable genotypes among male partners versus 18.7% respectively.12 These findings emphasize not only the high burden of HPV among male partners but also the critical role that male vaccination could play in interrupting transmission and reducing cervical lesion risk in their female partners. Taken together, these studies underscore the broader reproductive health benefits of HPV vaccination for both men and their partners.

Promising evidence from HPV vaccine trials has shown efficacy against HPV infection and related anogenital disease in men, including genital warts and anal intraepithelial neoplasia, with efficacy rates as high as 89.9% in HPV-naïve individuals.13 Long-term studies further support its durability, demonstrating over 90% protection against HPV-related external genital lesions for up to 10 years.13 The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) recommends routine HPV vaccination for males beginning at age 11 or 12, with routine catch-up vaccination advised through age 26.14 As a result, expanding vaccination to include males is now regarded as essential to achieving comprehensive HPV control across all genders.

Most earlier economic models suggested that when HPV vaccination coverage among females is high, vaccinating boys is not considered cost-effective. These models argue that once female coverage surpasses 50%, herd immunity will develop, indirectly protecting men who have sex exclusively with women.15,16 However, this threshold has not been achieved in many countries, including China, where female HPV vaccination coverage is still below global averages and WHO targets.17 By 2022, first-dose HPV vaccine coverage among females in China aged 9–45 years was approximately 10.15%, while full-series coverage stood at 6.21%. Nonetheless, these rates showed significant regional disparities, with higher coverage in eastern provinces and much lower rates in western regions.17 As a result, the potential for herd immunity is limited, and men – particularly men who have sex with men (MSM) – receive little to no protection and remain at high risk for preventable HPV-related diseases. While cost-effectiveness models often exclude MSM due to their smaller population size, this overlooks their disproportionate disease burden.18 Vaccinating males against HPV can accelerate the elimination of cervical cancer in women by contributing to herd immunity. As more males are immunized, the overall transmission of HPV decreases, offering indirect protection to unvaccinated individuals, including women who may have missed or been ineligible for vaccination.

A compelling rationale for promoting HPV vaccination among men stems from substantial biological and epidemiological evidence highlighting their contribution to both the HPV disease burden and transmission dynamics. Biologically, men are affected by several HPV-related conditions, including anal cancer (88% HPV-attributable), oropharyngeal and penile cancers, and genital warts (90% caused by HPV types 6 and 11).19,20 Furthermore, evidence suggests that HPV infections may persist longer in men than in women. One study reported that men exhibit lower natural clearance or suppression rates of oral HPV infections, with a median clearance time of 5.3 months compared to 3.0 months in women; men were found to be 37% less likely to clear or suppress the infection than women (adjusted HR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.42–0.95).21 This slower viral clearance may lead to longer infection durations, increased transmission to partners, and a heightened risk of developing HPV-related diseases. However, other studies have found otherwise, reporting comparable or even higher clearance rates in men, particularly those related to high-risk oncogenic types.19,22 These mixed findings highlight the need for more conclusive, large-scale studies to clarify sex-based differences in HPV persistence and clearance or suppression. From a transmission perspective, men play a key role as carriers, with statistics indicating that men have a higher global prevalence of genital HPV infection compared to women, supported by data from the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) 2013–2014. According to the survey, the prevalence of any genital HPV infection was 45.2% among men and 39.9% among women aged 18–69 years. Additionally, the prevalence of high-risk genital HPV types was 25.1% in men and 20.4% in women.23 Additionally, female cervical HPV infection is closely linked to infection in male partners. A study conducted in 2016 among Chinese male outpatients whose female partners had confirmed cervical HPV infection found that 83.5% of these men were HPV-positive on their external genitalia.24 This supports the close link between female cervical HPV infection and male partner infection, emphasizing the importance of including men in HPV vaccination strategies.

In China, as in several other countries, including the United Kingdom and Australia, HPV vaccination programs were historically female-centric, initially targeting only girls.25,26 However, these countries have since transitioned to gender-neutral vaccination strategies. A major turning point came in early 2025, when GARDASIL vaccine (covering HPV types 6, 11, 16, and 18) became the first HPV vaccine approved for use in Chinese males aged 9 to 26.27 Authorized by the National Medical Products Administration, the vaccine protects against anal cancers, genital warts, and precancerous lesions linked to HPV types 6, 11, 16, and 18.4 This approval marked a pivotal shift toward a more inclusive HPV prevention strategy in China. Despite these advancements, HPV infection remains a major public health concern in China, with prevalence rates varying widely by region and population. Among women, the national HPV infection rate is estimated between 13.1% and 18.8%, with regional figures ranging from 9.6% to 23.6%.28 This is unsurprising given the low vaccination coverage. Nonetheless, China launched a Comprehensive Cervical Cancer Prevention and Control Strategy in 2021 targeting a 90% vaccination rate among 15-year-old girls by 2025 in 15 pilot cities, aiming to scale up coverage nationally.29 While progress is being made, HPV vaccine coverage in men would greatly enhance overall population immunity, reduce the burden of HPV-related diseases in both sexes, and help close the gender gap in current prevention efforts, especially for groups like MSM who receive little benefit from female-targeted herd immunity.

Nonetheless, expanding HPV vaccination to males may face challenges, as previous studies examining vaccine acceptance among men have highlighted considerable barriers. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Shen et al. found that the overall HPV vaccine acceptance rate among general male populations was 47.04% (95% CI: 39.23–54.93%), while acceptance was significantly higher among MSM at 62.23% (95% CI: 52.93–71.10%).30 Within studies focusing specifically on Chinese males, vaccine acceptance showed wide variability. Among the general male population, rates ranged from 9.8% to 88.5%, depending on region, demographics, and study design. By contrast, MSM consistently reported higher acceptance, with studies from urban centers such as Hangzhou31 and Tianjin32 noting rates above 80%. These elevated figures likely reflect greater awareness, perceived vulnerability, and proactive health-seeking behaviors in MSM communities. Conversely, heterosexual males, especially university students and young men from the general population, exhibited markedly lower acceptance. Studies among these groups reported vaccine uptake intentions between 23% and 42%, largely due to low awareness of HPV risks and the common misconception that HPV is primarily a women’s health issue.33,34

Based on publicly available data from China CDC Weekly, as of the end of 2022, the cumulative HPV vaccination coverage among women aged 9–45 years in China was 10.15% for the first dose, 8.69% for the second dose, and 6.21% for the full three-dose series. Age-specific coverage for the complete three-dose regimen was 0.31% (ages 9–14), 2.21% (15–19), 8.34% (20–24), 9.39% (25–29), 7.68% (30–34), 7.88% (35–39), and 5.37% (40–45).17 Notably, the lowest coverage is observed among the 9–14 age group, which is the primary target for HPV vaccination according to WHO guidelines. With the recent availability of HPV vaccines for boys and men in China, expanding vaccination to this population presents new challenges. Parental hesitancy and reluctance, especially due to limited awareness and cultural perceptions, may hinder uptake. However, a study conducted in the metropolitan area of Shanghai found that parents demonstrated strong acceptance and financial willingness to vaccinate boys against HPV, with 90.99% of mothers and 85.04% of fathers expressing intention to vaccinate their sons.35 These findings may be largely influenced by socioeconomic factors, indicating that vaccine acceptance could vary significantly across different regions in China.

In sum, while the recent approval of GARDASIL for males in China represents a major policy milestone, significant challenges remain in achieving widespread vaccine coverage among men. The systematic review by Shen et al. underscores the need to address disparities in vaccine acceptance. This paper examines the influence of policy frameworks, cultural norms, and normative beliefs on HPV vaccination decisions among Chinese men.30 It further explores issues related to vaccine confidence in China and emphasizes the role of moral responsibility as a catalyst for enhancing vaccine uptake. Together, these insights inform strategic recommendations and directions for future research.

Policy frameworks

Policy frameworks play a critical role in shaping HPV vaccination uptake in China. Although the first HPV vaccine was licensed in China in 2016, it has not been incorporated into the National Immunization Program (NIP). Vaccination remains voluntary and self-paid.17 This lack of policy integration has led to inequities in access and uptake, particularly in lower-income and rural regions.17 The financial burden is considerable, with a full course of the vaccine costing around CNY 3000, which remains a significant barrier for many individuals. In the absence of public funding or subsidies, HPV vaccination decisions are largely influenced by individual financial capacity, limiting widespread coverage and access.

Without comprehensive national policy support, vaccination efforts may fall short and fail to achieve equitable coverage across the country. To improve uptake and reduce disparities, incorporating the HPV vaccine into China’s NIP could be a crucial step. Evidence from countries that have adopted this strategy shows significantly higher coverage rates. For instance, the inclusion of HPV vaccination in national immunization schedules with school-based delivery has resulted in high coverage rates and measurable reductions in HPV-related diseases in countries such as Australia, the United Kingdom, and Rwanda. These countries serve as valuable models for improving HPV vaccine uptake through coordinated public health policy and accessible delivery systems.

Australia has achieved HPV vaccination coverage rates exceeding 70% among adolescents through its National HPV Vaccination Program, which offers free vaccines primarily via school-based platforms. Coverage among females aged around 15 years has consistently remained above 79%, with male coverage exceeding 76% in recent years, reflecting the success of this inclusive approach.36 Similarly, the United Kingdom launched its national HPV vaccination program in 2008, initially targeting adolescent females with the bivalent vaccine before transitioning to the quadrivalent vaccine. Over more than a decade, this program has achieved high coverage and has led to significant declines in vaccine-type HPV infections, cervical precancerous lesions, and invasive cervical cancer among young women, indicating a strong population-level health impact.37,38 Rwanda, despite being a low-resource country, successfully integrated the HPV vaccine into its national immunization schedule beginning in 2011. By adopting a school-based delivery strategy supported by community engagement and international partnerships, Rwanda achieved coverage exceeding 90% among targeted adolescent girls.39,40 This success highlights the feasibility of high vaccine coverage even in resource-constrained settings when supported by strong policy commitment and infrastructure.

These international examples underscore the potential benefits of incorporating HPV vaccination into China’s NIP. A school-based and publicly funded delivery model could significantly enhance vaccine access, reduce financial barriers, and improve uptake among both male and female adolescents, ultimately advancing HPV-related disease prevention nationwide. Nonetheless, it is important to note that while integrating HPV vaccination into NIPs is a critical step toward equitable access, policy alone is not sufficient to ensure equal uptake across genders. Evidence from countries such as the United Kingdom and Australia shows that even after introducing HPV vaccination for boys into their national immunization schedules in 2019 (UK) and 2013 (Australia), vaccine uptake among boys remains significantly lower than among girls, reflecting persistent gender disparities in coverage.41,42 This disparity is partly due to persistent public perceptions of HPV as a “girls” vaccine, which continue to shape societal and individual attitudes.43 Therefore, alongside policy reform, targeted communication strategies are needed to normalize HPV vaccination for all genders and address underlying gendered misconceptions.

Cultural and normative beliefs

Cultural factors may significantly influence HPV vaccination decisions among men in China, often contributing to low acceptance. A predominant perception that HPV is solely a “women’s health issue,” mainly due to its strong association with cervical cancer, has led to limited perception of its impact on male health, including its role in causing anal, penile, and oropharyngeal cancers.44 A social media discussion study in China found that HPV vaccines are often portrayed as “feminine,” leading the public to overlook the health benefits of vaccinating males.45 This gendered framing of HPV has led many men to underestimate their susceptibility, thereby diminishes their motivation to seek immunization. This issue has also posed significant challenges to public health efforts in the United States.46

Limited knowledge about HPV infection may contribute to the widespread misconception that HPV vaccination is only necessary for girls. A recent meta-analysis including 33 studies with a total of 92,802 Chinese parents found that parental awareness of HPV and the HPV vaccine remains low, with pooled awareness rates of 45.0% and 41.4%, respectively.47 A 2024 study conducted among parents of secondary school students in Beijing, one of China’s largest cities, reported slightly higher awareness levels, with 56.4% demonstrating basic knowledge about HPV.48 Likewise, a study has indicated that Chinese male college students possess suboptimal knowledge about HPV infection, with only 24.34% of participants demonstrating “good knowledge” of the virus.33

To address this, public health campaigns should emphasize the importance of HPV vaccination for males, highlighting both their risk of infection and the protective benefits of immunization. This is especially crucial as studies have revealed a significant lack of awareness about infections caused by HPV.33,47,48 Studies from various countries support the implementation of gender-neutral HPV vaccination programs. A commentary emphasized that vaccinating both boys and girls is more effective in reducing HPV infections and related cancers compared to vaccinating females alone, underscoring the need to protect all genders.49 Additionally, a systematic review found that public health campaigns that foster positive attitudes toward HPV vaccination and raise awareness about HPV risks in men can significantly enhance vaccine acceptability among males.50 In Hong Kong, research showed that parental reluctance to vaccinate boys stems partly from misconceptions about HPV risks and vaccine necessity; targeted public health campaigns that highlight HPV-related cancer risks for boys and the benefits of vaccination have been recommended to improve uptake.51 Evidence from countries with gender-neutral vaccination programs, such as Australia52 and parts of Europe53 demonstrates significant reductions in HPV infections and associated diseases, reinforcing the value of including males in vaccination efforts.

Taboos surrounding sexual health are deeply rooted in many Asian cultures,54–56 including China,47 and continue to pose significant barriers to open discussion and education. Persistent discomfort in addressing topics such as sexually transmitted infections (STIs) hinders public dialogue and limits the effectiveness of health education efforts. These subjects remain highly sensitive, contributing to misinformation, stigma, delay treatment and vaccination hesitancy among men in China.57–59 Furthermore, fear of being perceived as promiscuous has emerged as a significant deterrent to HPV vaccination among men. A study conducted among men in a Chinese community in Hong Kong, an advanced and modern society, found that linking the HPV vaccine to sexual activity raised concerns about being perceived as promiscuous or socially labeled.59 This finding suggests that even in a society shaped by modern thinking, traditional cultural norms, stigma and embarrassment around sexual health continue to affect how Chinese men perceive and accept the HPV vaccine. This perception not only discourages open conversations about HPV prevention but also reinforces the false notion that vaccination is only necessary for individuals who are sexually active or at high risk.

To address these cultural barriers, it is essential to implement context-sensitive and gender-inclusive public health strategies that normalize HPV vaccination for men. Educational campaigns should be carefully designed to separate the vaccine from associations with sexual activity and promiscuity, instead framing it as a routine preventive measure for long-term health. Messaging should emphasize that HPV affects both sexes and that vaccination is a responsible health decision, not a reflection of sexual behavior. Engaging male role models, healthcare providers, and community leaders to publicly support HPV vaccination may help reduce stigma and reshape normative beliefs.60,61 Additionally, integrating comprehensive sexual health education into school curricula and community outreach programs can foster open discussion and improve understanding, particularly among young men.

Heterosexual men in China often perceive themselves to be at lower risk of HPV infection than MSM, which contributes to their lower motivation to get vaccinated. This perception is reflected in their comparatively lower willingness to receive the HPV vaccine than MSM.30 Studies have found that MSM are significantly more likely to develop anal cancer compared to heterosexual men, with HIV-positive individuals facing a risk that is 40 to 80 times higher than that of HIV-negative men.5 This stark difference in risk reinforces the belief that HPV vaccination is mainly relevant for MSM and people living with HIV, leading many heterosexual men to underestimate their own vulnerability and the importance of getting vaccinated. To address the low perceived risk among heterosexual men, targeted public health strategies are needed to correct misconceptions and promote HPV vaccination as a preventive measure for all men, regardless of sexual orientation. Educational campaigns should highlight that HPV can cause serious health problems in men, including genital warts and cancers of the penis, anus, and oropharynx. Messaging should clearly communicate that HPV affects both heterosexual and homosexual populations. Involving healthcare providers in proactive discussions about HPV risks and vaccination benefits during routine visits can also help increase awareness and uptake among heterosexual men.

Research indicates that a strong recommendation from a healthcare provider serves as a powerful cue to action in a patient’s decision to receive the HPV vaccine. For example, a comprehensive study found that among men, 60.8% reported that a recommendation for HPV vaccination from their healthcare provider would be “very important” in their vaccination decision-making process.62 Furthermore, vaccine coverage among males who received a provider recommendation increased dramatically, with coverage rates rising from 17.2% to 75.1% over an eight-year period, compared to much lower rates among those without such a recommendation.63 In addition to prompting action, healthcare providers play a vital role in dispelling misinformation, correcting myths, and addressing safety concerns that men may have about the vaccine. Their trusted position allows them to influence attitudes and facilitate informed decision-making, thus reinforcing the importance of integrating provider engagement into HPV vaccination strategies.

Vaccine confidence

Vaccine confidence refers to trust in the safety, efficacy, and importance of vaccines, as well as in the health system that delivers them. In the context of HPV vaccination in men, low vaccine confidence may pose a significant barrier to vaccine uptake. In China, public distrust toward HPV vaccines, fueled by misinformation on social media and past vaccine scandals, has negatively influenced vaccine acceptance not only among women but also among men. A content analysis of HPV-related posts on Chinese social media platforms like Weibo revealed that 86.8% of messages were from non-expert sources, often lacking scientific accuracy.45 This undermines trust in the vaccine, specifically its safety and efficacy, as many users are exposed to unverified claims. Moreover, China’s past vaccine safety scandals, such as the 2016 incident involving expired and improperly stored vaccines, have had a lingering impact on trust in the system, including confidence in public health authorities and vaccine delivery mechanisms.64 Although these events did not involve the HPV vaccine specifically, they have shaped broader skepticism, particularly among those considering newer or less familiar vaccines, such as HPV for men. As a result, many young women living in mainland China had headed across the border to get vaccinated in Hong Kong, Macao, New Zealand, and South Korea,65 while men who already perceive themselves as at lower risk are less likely to prioritize vaccination and make extra efforts to travel elsewhere for HPV vaccination.

Trust in providers is also compromised when healthcare workers fail to offer clear information or are perceived as swayed by commercial interests. A qualitative study among Hong Kong Chinese men highlighted barriers such as lack of accessible official information on HPV-related topics and the perception that the HPV vaccine signals promiscuity, both reflecting poor communication and provider engagement.59 Additionally, the HPV vaccine is classified as a commercial (Class II) vaccine in China, meaning individuals must pay out of pocket. Although no study has specifically reported mistrust of healthcare workers or pharmaceutical companies due to profit-making motives in the Chinese context, perceptions of profit-driven intentions among providers have been reported elsewhere, contributing to skepticism and reluctance to vaccinate.66

Moral responsibility as a catalyst for enhancing HPV vaccine uptake

Moral responsibility plays a critical role in shaping health behaviors, and this may be useful especially in the context of communicable disease prevention. In China, where HPV vaccination among males remains low due to a combination of limited awareness, gendered misconceptions, and perceived low personal risk, appealing to moral responsibility may offer a novel strategy to increase uptake. Framing HPV vaccination as a socially responsible act, rather than merely an individual health choice, can help shift perceptions. For example, many parents may resonate with the idea that vaccinating their sons helps protect future partners from HPV-related diseases. This sense of responsibility toward others can foster prosocial motivations, a strategy that has been successful in other public health campaigns, such as those promoting COVID-19 vaccination and influenza prevention.67,68

Survey items that measure moral responsibility, such as agreement with statements like “I believe I have a responsibility to vaccinate my son to protect others” or “Vaccinating my son helps prevent the spread of HPV to future partners,” underscore the potential of moral framing to reduce hesitancy. Encouraging the idea that boys should be equally vaccinated as girls can also help dismantle the stigma that HPV is a “girls’ vaccine” and promote greater gender equity in healthcare messaging. Moreover, moral responsibility extends beyond vaccination itself. Even when vaccination is delayed or declined, individuals should be cultivated to ethically inform their partners about the potential risks of HPV transmission. This reinforces the ethical obligation to minimize harm to others, aligning with principles of informed consent and shared responsibility in sexual health.

Recommendation

Public health campaigns in China should integrate moral responsibility framing into HPV vaccine messaging for boys and men. Messages emphasizing protection of future partners, community health, and gender equality in vaccine access may increase motivation among parents and young men. Collaboration with schools, healthcare providers, and influencers who advocate for moral and social values could further amplify the impact. Educational materials should highlight not only the health benefits but also the ethical implications of HPV vaccination. In addition to promoting moral responsibility, boosting HPV vaccine uptake among men in China requires a multifaceted approach. Educational efforts should aim to increase risk perception among heterosexual men by emphasizing that HPV is not just a women’s issue but can also cause cancers affecting men, such as penile, anal, and oropharyngeal cancers. Normalizing HPV vaccination as a standard preventive health measure for men, rather than linking it solely to high-risk groups, can help shift public perception. Strong recommendations from healthcare providers are especially influential, yet many men report never receiving such advice. Engaging male role models and influencers can further reduce stigma and reshape cultural narratives around the vaccine. To counter misinformation, authorities should work with social media platforms to disseminate scientifically accurate content and debunk myths. In parallel, healthcare providers must be trained to offer clear, unbiased communication and proactively address concerns about vaccine safety, efficacy, and necessity – especially among men who may perceive themselves at low risk or distrust commercial motives. Furthermore, incorporating the HPV vaccine into China’s NIP would represent a critical policy advancement, helping to reduce financial barriers, ensure equitable access, and signal governmental prioritization of gender-inclusive vaccination. Additionally, providing subsidies or offering free vaccination programs would reduce financial barriers, while community-based outreach through schools, universities, and workplaces can enhance convenience and trust. Taken together, these strategies not only improve access but also rebuild vaccine confidence by fostering trust in the health system, providers, and the value of HPV vaccination for all genders.

Conclusion

The approval of the HPV vaccine for males in China marks a significant milestone in the country’s efforts to reduce HPV-related disease burdens. However, despite this progress, vaccine uptake among men remains challenging, particularly among heterosexual males, due to a complex interplay of policy limitations, cultural beliefs, misinformation, and low perceived personal risk. The lack of inclusion in the National Immunization Program and the high out-of-pocket costs continue to limit equitable access, especially in rural and low-income communities. Cultural and normative beliefs, including the misconception that HPV is a women’s issue and the stigma associated with sexual health discussions, further deter men from seeking vaccination. Efforts to reshape public perceptions, normalize male vaccination, and increase awareness about HPV-related risks in men, regardless of sexual orientation, are essential. Engaging healthcare providers in proactive discussions and leveraging public health campaigns that de-link vaccination from sexuality can help combat stigma and misinformation. Importantly, promoting HPV vaccination among men should also be framed as a matter of moral responsibility, not only for personal protection but also for the broader public good. By choosing to get vaccinated, men contribute to herd immunity, reduce virus transmission, and protect current and future partners. Encouraging male vaccination is thus not solely a medical or policy issue but also a societal obligation that upholds collective well-being and gender equity in healthcare. Cultivating a sense of moral accountability can play a powerful role in enhancing vaccine confidence and acceptance among men.
