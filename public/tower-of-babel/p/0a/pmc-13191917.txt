Doctor-related Knowledge, Attitudes and Practices (KAP) factors influencing antibiotic prescribing for urinary tract infections in primary care in Singapore: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Wilnard Yeong Tze Tan, Huiling Guo, Tau Hong Lee, Choon Kit Leong, Angela Chow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe incidence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has been on a constant rise for urinary tract infections (UTIs). This study aimed to assess the knowledge, attitudes and practices of primary care doctors on antibiotic prescribing for patients with uncomplicated cystitis, and identify potential areas of intervention to improve appropriate antibiotic prescribing for these patients.Materials and methodsThis is a cross-sectional study conducted on 127 primary care physicians (PCPs) recruited from privately funded primary care clinics in Singapore between January and December 2023. The survey was anonymous and self-administered. Factor analysis was performed to derive latent factors which were included in the multivariable logistic regression analysis to assess for independent factors associated with inappropriate antibiotic prescribing.ResultsThirty-five percent of respondents prescribed antibiotics inappropriately for uncomplicated cystitis. Only 57 (45%) were aware of the symptoms indicative of an UTI and only 63 (50%) primary care doctors reported always using urine dipsticks for patients consulting for UTI symptoms. After adjusting for potential confounding factors, primary care doctors who desire to improve their clinical practice (aOR 0.55, 95% CI 0.35-0.87, P=0.010) were less likely to prescribe antibiotics inappropriately for uncomplicated cystitis. In contrast, doctors who have practised in the current clinic for ten years and more (aOR 3.72, 95% CI 1.05-13.22, P=0.042) were more likely to prescribe antibiotics inappropriately for uncomplicated cystitis.ConclusionsActive promotion of clinical guidance about treatment of UTI targeting experienced primary care doctors can encourage the appropriate prescribing of antibiotics for uncomplicated cystitis. However, more research is needed to assess its effectiveness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03298-2.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, Primary care, Urinary tract infection, Uncomplicated cystitis, Antibiotic prescribing

Introduction

Globally, urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most prevalent infections in the primary care setting [1]. More than 50% of adult women experience at least one UTI in their lifetime [2]. From 1990 to 2019, the absolute number of UTI cases increased by 60% globally, reaching 404.61 million cases in 2019 [3]. UTIs are associated with decreased quality of life and considerable clinical and economic burden [1]. Unfortunately, inappropriate antibiotic prescribing for uncomplicated UTIs remained high globally [4–6]. In Singapore, UTIs are the second most common reason for antibiotic prescription in the primary care setting in 2021, amounting to around 1 in 5 antibiotic prescriptions [7].

One key issue that drives over-prescription in primary care is the diagnostic uncertainty. Clinical symptoms are less sensitive and specific for diagnosis [8], while the utility of urine dipsticks is questionable [9, 10]. Urine cultures have long turnaround times that make them not useful in primary care [11]. Murray and colleagues found that in outpatient settings, more than 1 in 4 patients were treated for UTI even though they did not have any urinary symptoms. This number (> 95% of patients) was markedly worse in the emergency room setting [12]. Locally, Ng et al. found that among patients with uncomplicated UTIs in primary care clinics, 53.4% had inappropriate antibiotic prescription, of which 31% of these patients had inaccurate diagnosis of uncomplicated UTI [13].

Given that UTIs are among the most common reasons for antibiotic prescriptions locally and globally, and are often diagnosed and treated inconsistently with clinical guidelines, they are a critical target for antimicrobial stewardship initiatives. National guidelines can help to improve appropriate antibiotic prescribing practices by primary care practitioners (PCP) for UTIs [14], but patient- and doctor-related factors can drive guideline-discordant practices [15]. Doctors’ knowledge and experience, the availability of resources to support antibiotic stewardship (e.g. audit-and-feedback mechanisms with educational opportunities) and motivation driven by personal beliefs (on AMR and shared decision-making to improve treatment decisions) were found to be key drivers that could improve PCPs’ appropriate antibiotic prescribing behaviours [15–19].

Despite abundant evidence in international literature, little is known about the factors influencing the appropriate prescribing of antibiotics for UTI patients in primary care locally. This study aimed to explore the knowledge, attitudes and practices (KAP) of PCPs on antibiotic prescribing for UTI patients, and identify potential areas of intervention to improve appropriate antibiotic prescribing for these patients, in the context of a developed Asian country and with a focus on uncomplicated cystitis in females, the most common UTI in primary care.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection method

This is a cross-sectional study, with a target sample size of 500. The target sample size was calculated based on a confidence interval of 95%, margin of error of 4% and an estimated primary care practitioner population of 2250, which yielded an expected sample size of 475 [20]. A self-administered anonymous online survey was disseminated to PCPs practising in privately funded general practice (GP) clinics in Singapore from January to December 2023. In Singapore, 80% of primary care attendances occurs in the GP clinics while 20% occurs in the government-funded polyclinics [21]. In 2022, there were up to 2481 GP clinics and 23 polyclinics [22].

To avoid selection bias, invitation letters were first systematically disseminated via post to all GP clinics in Singapore, before targeted invites / reminders were sent via various platforms such as primary care network mailing lists, continuing medical education attendance lists, word-of-mouth, etc. to improve the recruitment process. The novel survey instrument comprised of questions seeking to assess the KAP pertaining to diagnosis of UTI, antibiotic prescribing for UTI patients, including those adapted from published literature or validated scales (see Supplementary) [23–26]. The survey was available in English only. It was piloted amongst three PCPs to ensure content and face validities of the questions prior to use for data collection.

Dependent variable – inappropriate antibiotic prescribing for uncomplicated cystitis

The dependent variable, Inappropriate antibiotic prescribing for uncomplicated cystitis, was defined as prescribing antibiotics other than amoxicillin-clavulanate, fosfomycin or nitrofurantoin for uncomplicated cystitis in adult females, according to Singapore’s Agency for Care Effectiveness (ACE) Clinical Guidance document for UTI [27].

Independent variables

A knowledge score (Knowledge score of UTI symptoms) was tabulated for each respondent. A full score of 7 was given to respondents who correctly identified frequency, dysuria and urgency as presenting UTI symptoms, and nocturia, intermittency, hesitancy and terminal dribbling as non- presenting UTI symptoms, as according to the ACE Clinical Guidance document for UTI [27].

A 5-point Likert scale (1-Never to 5-Always) was used to assess the frequency of use of 6 different diagnostic tests listed by primary care doctors on patients consulting for UTI symptoms. Respondents who always used any of the diagnostic tests listed were defined as ‘Always using any form of diagnostic test’, while respondents who always used urine dipsticks were defined as ‘Always using urine dipstick’.

Statements on shared decision-making (SDM) were adapted from Scholl et al.’s SDM-Q-Doc scale [26]. The statements were presented in a 5-point Likert scale (1-Strongly disagree to 5-Strongly agree), and responses on SDM were dichotomised into two categories: agree/strongly agree vs. strongly disagree/disagree/neither agree nor disagree. Respondents who agreed/strongly agreed to all 9 statements under the scale were categorised as having Engaged in SDM with UTI patients.

Five statements on Anxiety due to uncertainty were adapted from a published literature by Baghdadi et al. [22]. The statements were presented in a 5-point Likert scale (1-Strongly disagree to 5-Strongly agree), and factor analysis was performed to load the statements onto a single factor. Both the Cronbach’s alpha and factor score were calculated.

Four statements on Desire to improve clinical practice were adapted from a published literature [25]. The statements were presented in a 5-point Likert scale (1-Strongly disagree to 5-Strongly agree), and factor analysis was performed to load the statements onto a single factor: Desire to improve clinical practice. Both the Cronbach’s alpha and factor score were calculated. The responses were also dichotomised into two categories: agree/strongly agree vs. strongly disagree/disagree/neither agree nor disagree. Respondents who agreed/strongly agreed to all 4 statements were categorised as having Desire to improve clinical practice.

Attitude statements on AMR were adopted from the World Health Organization’s Antibiotic Resistance: Multi-Country Public Awareness Survey [24]. The statements were presented in a 5-point Likert scale (1-Strongly disagree to 5-Strongly agree), and responses were dichotomised into two categories: agree/strongly agree vs. strongly disagree/disagree/neither agree nor disagree.

Data analysis

Categorical variables were tabulated using proportions, while continuous variables were tabulated using means (standard deviation), and differences were tested using Chi-squared test and Student’s t-test, respectively. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to identify the independent factors associated with inappropriate antibiotic prescribing for uncomplicated cystitis. Covariates were included in the final model to adjust for potential confounding, through assessing the Akaike information criteria, Bayesian information criteria and likelihood ratios. Statistical significance was defined as P-value < 0.05 and statistical analyses were conducted in Stata version 14.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas US).

Results

Basic characteristics of survey respondents

127 responses were received for the survey out of 2481 clinics locally. The mean age was 51.9 (± 12.6) years old, with the majority of them being males, being employed full-time, having postgraduate medical degree, getting their basic medical degree in Singapore, having 10 years or more experience post-graduation and as a primary care practitioner, and having 10 years or more working experience in their current clinic (Table 1). Nearly half (N = 69; 54%) were practising in solo practices while 34% (N = 43) and 12% (N = 15) were practising in small and large group practices respectively. More than three-quarters of the respondents (N = 100; 79%) saw 10 or less UTI patients in a typical month, and more than one-third (N = 45; 35%) prescribed antibiotics inappropriately for uncomplicated cystitis.

Knowledge: UTI symptoms

Most respondents correctly identified dysuria (N = 126; 99%), frequency (N = 123; 97%) and urgency (N = 115; 91%) as symptoms indicative of UTI (Fig. 1). Conversely, more than one-tenth of respondents incorrectly indicated that terminal dribbling (N = 16; 13%), hesitancy (N = 27; 21%), intermittency (N = 35; 28%) and nocturia (N = 42; 33%) were symptoms indicative of a UTI. Out of 127 respondents, only 57 (45%) had provided correct responses to all 7 symptoms, with mean knowledge score of 5.92 (± 1.29).

Knowledge: Use of diagnostic tests on patients consulting for UTI symptoms

Urine dipsticks were the most commonly used diagnostic test, with 63 (50%) respondents reportedly always using it for patients with UTI symptoms (Fig. 2). Next, only 13% (N = 16) and 9% (N = 11) of respondents reported having to always use UFEME/urine microscopy and urine culture, respectively, for patients with UTI symptoms. Procalcitonin test (N = 113; 89%), C-reactive protein test (N = 97; 76%) and full blood count (N = 65; 51%) were never used as diagnostic tests for patients with UTI symptoms.

Frequency of use of diagnostic tests on patients consulting for UTI symptoms

Out of 127 respondents, 98 (77%) thought that urine dipstick was a reasonable alternative to urine culture for diagnosis of uncomplicated cystitis. Those who always used urine dipsticks were more likely to feel anxious when they were unsure of the diagnosis (3.14 ± 1.05 vs. 2.72 ± 0.98, P = 0.020) and more likely to feel uncomfortable with the uncertainty in patient care for UTI (3.14 ± 0.90 vs. 2.55 ± 0.82, P < 0.001), as compared to those who did not always use urine dipsticks (Table 2). Overall, those who always used urine dipsticks were more likely to experience anxiety due to uncertainty (0.23 ± 1.00 vs. -0.23 ± 0.96, P = 0.008), as compared to those who did not always use urine dipsticks (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.8645).

Practices: engagement in shared decision-making

Majority of the respondents (N = 120; 94%) reported making it clear to their patients seen for UTI that a decision on antibiotic prescribing needed to be made, while more than three-quarters of them helped their patients to understand all information (N = 109; 86%) and reached an agreement with their patients about how to proceed for their UTI (N = 104; 82%) (Table 3). Most respondents shared with their patients about different options for treating their UTI (N = 87; 69%), explained the advantages and disadvantages (N = 93; 73%) and weighed (N = 79; 62%) the different treatment options for UTI, and selected the treatment option with their patients for their UTI together (N = 79; 62%). However, only slightly more than half of them wanted to know from their patients exactly how they wanted to be involved in decision-making process (N = 75; 59%) and had ever asked their patients which treatment option for their UTI they prefer (N = 65; 51%). In general, only 39 (31%) respondents engaged in SDM with patients consulting for UTI on antibiotic prescribing.

Attitudes: desire to improve clinical practice

Those who prescribed antibiotics appropriately for uncomplicated cystitis were more likely to hope that a national UTI clinical practice guideline in primary care will be published (4.15 ± 0.69 vs. 3.76 ± 0.88, P = 0.007), to agree that the national guideline will change their antibiotic prescribing practices (4.02 ± 0.82 vs. 3.60 ± 0.86, P = 0.007), and to express interest in taking part in education programmes aimed at reducing antibiotic prescribing in primary care (3.84 ± 0.91 vs. 3.42 ± 0.99, P = 0.017), as compared to those who were prescribing inappropriately for uncomplicated cystitis (Table 4). In general, those who were prescribing antibiotics appropriately for uncomplicated cystitis were more likely to have a desire to improve clinical practice (0.19 ± 0.94 vs. -0.34 ± 1.03, P = 0.004) than those who were prescribing inappropriately for uncomplicated cystitis (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.8447).

Discussion

Overall, this study provided valuable insights about the knowledge PCPs have about symptoms of UTI, their antibiotic prescribing patterns for uncomplicated cystitis in females and how their attitude towards improving clinical practice can influence these prescribing patterns. This study also shed light on potential areas of intervention to encourage appropriate antibiotic prescribing amongst PCPs in Singapore for uncomplicated cystitis.

In terms of knowledge, we found that 35% of survey respondents displayed guideline-discordant antibiotic prescribing behaviours for uncomplicated cystitis, as defined by the ACE Clinical Guidance published in November 2023, after the study had been carried out [27]. We observed that while majority were able to identify the correct at least one symptom of UTI (> 90%), a large proportion of respondents (55%) was unable to correctly identify all presenting symptoms of UTI. This suggested that a sizeable number erroneously attribute other urinary symptoms to UTI, which could lead to overdiagnosis of UTI and in turn, overprescription of antibiotics. In other developed nations like the United States, Shively and colleagues [28] found that 28% of antibiotics were guideline-discordant while Kikuchi et al. found that guideline-concordance was 91% in a military health system, with family medicine having the second highest odds ratio for guideline-concordance at 1.81 [29]. The lower rates of guideline-discordance was however not surprising given that the Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidelines were more established [30], allowing time for PCPs to adopt the recommendations. Conversely, no national guidelines were available in Singapore when the study was carried out.

For attitudes and practices, 55% of surveyed PCPs had a desire to improve clinical practice. They were also found to be 45% less likely to prescribe antibiotics inappropriately for uncomplicated cystitis. This phenomenon was similar to antibiotic prescribing for upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs) in the same PCP population [26]. Furthermore, PCPs who lacked the desire to improve clinical practice were less likely to believe that everyone needed to take responsibility for using antibiotics responsibly and that AMR was one of the biggest problems the world faces. This tendency was also noted in an umbrella review conducted by Thompson et al. [31]. Locally, there is no active, transparent and accessible surveillance system for PCPs to access the local epidemiology of antimicrobial resistance among uropathogens, with only one study conducted with isolates collected from primary care clinics locally [32]. More studies need to be carried out to appreciate the reasons that contribute to a lack of desire to improve clinical practice, which in turn could inform systemic changes to incentivise more PCPs to improve their clinical practice.

Of interest, we found that PCPs who had been practising in the current clinic for 10 years or more (aOR 3.72, 95% CI 1.05–13.22, P = 0.042) were more likely to prescribe antibiotics inappropriately for uncomplicated cystitis as well. This could be due to entrenched practices from their years of practice. From a previous study, 88% of this study population practice in solo and small group practices [33], suggesting that the vast majority have significant autonomy to practice based on their personal beliefs on AMR. The study also showed that those who practice in large group practices were found to conform to antibiotic practices recommended by the key decision-making doctors within a clinic. The direct sharing of the newly released ACE Clinical Guidance document [27] to these experienced PCPs could therefore potentially provide better direction and alignment with nationally recommended antibiotic prescribing practices for uncomplicated cystitis.

SDM positively affected appropriate antibiotic prescribing for URTI in primary care [34–36] but had not been widely explored for UTIs. Univariate analysis showed that PCPs who did not engage in SDM on antibiotic prescribing with patients consulting for UTI were nearly 3 times as likely as those practicing SDM to prescribe antibiotics inappropriately, suggesting a potential role for SDM in encouraging the appropriate antibiotic prescribing amongst PCPs. Our survey showed the majority of PCPs exhibited moderate level of capabilities in engaging patients in SDM. To better facilitate SDM, doctor-targeted interventions could be considered, which include educational sessions that involve communication skills training [36].

Lastly, our study found that urine dipsticks were more frequently administered by PCPs who felt anxious due to uncertainty in the diagnosis of UTI. This practice was supported by the ACE Clinical Guidance also recommended the use of urine dipsticks in the context of uncertainty [27]. This could be related to younger PCPs who had less experience with UTIs, or when the patient’s presenting UTI symptoms were unclear. Given the relative ease of carrying out urine dipsticks, and the diagnostic challenge of UTIs, PCPs could be educated to carry out more urine dipsticks. This could potentially avoid inappropriate antibiotic prescribing due to uncertainty. Further studies should be carried out to understand the factors that influence the use of UTI diagnostic tests by PCPs. With the publication of the ACE guidance [27], a follow up study to evaluate the impact of this guidance on the management of uncomplicated cystitis in females in the primary care setting will be useful.

To our knowledge, this was the first survey conducted in the primary care setting to assess the factors influencing antibiotic prescribing for female uncomplicated cystitis in Singapore. The survey was anonymous, reducing the risk of social desirability bias. However, the survey response rate was low, which could lead to potential selection bias and limit generalisability of the results. The proportion of respondents who reportedly prescribed antibiotics inappropriately for female uncomplicated cystitis was 35%. This could be an underestimate of the actual rate in primary care because of selection bias from the low response rates, as well as social desirability bias, where respondents may respond in a way that they think is socially acceptable. Lastly, there could be potential unknown confounders not adjusted for in the final multivariable logistic regression model.

Conclusion

Multiple knowledge (awareness of local guidelines), attitude (lack of desire to improve clinical practice) and entrenched practices) and practice (being in practice for > 10 years, no engaging in shared decision making) factors drive entrenched practices that hinder appropriate prescription for UTIs. Active promotion of recently-established national guidelines can potentially help to address many of these factors.
