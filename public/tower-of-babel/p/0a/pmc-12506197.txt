Knowledge and attitude of parents towards the human papillomavirus vaccine for their daughters and associated factors in Debre Tabor town, northwest Ethiopia: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Getie Mihret Aragaw, Getie Lake Aynalem, Saron Abeje Abiy, Eden Beshaw Taye, Sewnet Anteneh Chernet, Tsion Tadesse Haile, Nuhamin Tesfa Tsega, Tiruye Tilahun Mesele, Asmra Tesfahun Seyoum, Aginche Gebremichael Tsega, Berihun Agegn Mengistie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThis study aims to assess parents’ knowledge and attitude towards the human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination of their daughters and the associated factors in Debre Tabor town, northwest Ethiopia.DesignA community-based cross-sectional study.SettingDebre Tabor town, Northwest Ethiopia.ParticipantsA total of 702 participants were included in the study, with a response rate of 98.2%. Three out of the six kebeles in the town were randomly selected, and participants within the selected kebeles were recruited through a cluster sampling technique. An interviewer-administered structured questionnaire was used to collect data from 15 December 2021 to 15 February 2022.Primary and secondary outcome measuresParents’ level of knowledge and attitude towards the HPV vaccination of their daughters, and the associated factors.ResultsIn the study, parents’ knowledge and attitude towards HPV vaccination were found to be 46.4% (95% CI 42.7% to 50.1%) and 61.5% (95% CI 58.0% to 65.2%), respectively. Parents with a higher level of education (adjusted OR (AOR)=2.27; 95% CI 1.39 to 3.69), media exposure (AOR=3.36; 95% CI 1.21 to 9.33) and a good attitude towards the HPV vaccine (AOR=8.81; 95% CI 5.78 to 13.44) were significantly associated factors that affect parents’ level of knowledge. Positive subjective norms (AOR=1.53; 95% CI 1.01 to 2.31) and perceived behavioural control towards the HPV vaccine (AOR=3.48; 95% CI 2.37 to 5.10) had statistically significant associations with parents’ attitude.ConclusionsIn this study, more than half of parents had poor knowledge of the HPV and its vaccination, while the majority of the participants showed a favourable attitude to the vaccine. Educational attainment, media exposure and a positive attitude were significantly associated with parental knowledge, and parents’ attitude was positively influenced by subjective norms and perceived behavioural control. This suggests a need to increase the parents’ level of awareness through educational interventions, particularly via media and community engagement. To improve the acceptance and uptake of the HPV vaccination, it is important to address negative attitudes and common misconceptions among parents on the safety, efficacy and necessity of the vaccine for their daughters.Keywords: Papillomavirus Vaccines, Human Papillomavirus Viruses, Caregivers, Awareness

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the most commonly diagnosed gynaecological cancer worldwide. It is the fourth most frequent cancer among women and remains the leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally.1,4 There were approximately 662 000 new cases of cervical cancer, and over 349 000 women died from the disease in 2022. More than 90% of the deaths are from low- and middle-income nations, where access to screening, prevention and treatment services is limited.1 4 Cervical cancer ranks as the second common cancer and the main reason for cancer-related mortality in Ethiopia. Each year, an estimated 7445 Ethiopian women develop the disease, and around 5338 of them lose their lives to this deadly disease.5,7

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is the primary cause of almost all (99%) cervical cancer cases, with high-risk types, particularly HPV-16 and HPV-18, being responsible for more than 70% of cervical cancers and precancerous cervical lesions.46,8 The HPV infection is also the cause of over 34% of various other anogenital, head and neck cancers, and genital warts.2 In Ethiopia, HPV infection is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths, with the high-risk HPV genotypes 16 and 18 accounting for an estimated 53.6% of cases.6 8

Vaccinating with the HPV vaccine is one of the key cancer preventive strategies that has been approved for use and has significantly contributed to reducing HPV infections.9 HPV vaccines are highly effective in preventing cervical cancer and are capable of preventing over 90% of the HPV infections and related cervical cancers.10,13 Likewise, combining HPV vaccination for adolescents with routine cervical cancer screening in resource-limited countries could reduce the risk of acquiring cervical cancer by 97%.14 Furthermore, the vaccine is essential for the WHO cervical cancer elimination initiative. WHO recommends that all girls between the ages of 9 and 14 should receive the HPV vaccine as a priority, preferably before they attempt their sexual debut, as efficacy is highest at this stage.9 12

Despite the many advantages of the HPV vaccine, the global uptake remains insufficient.15 As parents and guardians are the primary caregivers of adolescents, they are responsible for the health of their daughters by making important healthcare decisions, including those related to vaccinations. They play a crucial role in the successful delivery of the HPV vaccine to their adolescent girls.16,18 Parents’ level of knowledge on HPV infection and its vaccine, their attitudes and approval of the vaccine, and decision-making significantly influence the adherence of adolescents’ immunisation schedules for the HPV vaccine.18,21 The knowledge and acceptance of the HPV vaccine poses a challenge in low- and middle-income countries.2 18 22 23

Most parents have misconceptions and concerns about the HPV vaccine, and most of them are affected by cultural values.24 25 Various factors may influence parental knowledge and attitude towards the HPV vaccination for their daughters. Parental age, educational level, occupational type, monthly income, awareness of cervical cancer and HPV infection, and recommendations of the vaccine from important individuals were the contributing factors.2226,33 Besides, parents might delay their decision to vaccinate their children against HPV, believing that their children might be at minimal risk of contracting the virus.34 Their unfavourable beliefs and attitudes, as well as their limited information on the HPV vaccine, may contribute to the low uptake of the vaccine among their daughters.18,20

Concerns about maintaining and improving the health of women and adolescents are shared by governments in Ethiopia and the global community. Since the vaccination programme in the Ethiopian health system targets girls aged 9–14 years, the success of vaccination depends on parental knowledge, attitude and their decision to vaccinate their daughters. It is the responsibility of the Ethiopian Federal Ministry of Health and Maternal, Newborn and Child Health directorate to provide healthcare services, including maternal and reproductive health services such as prevention and treatment of cervical cancer. The Ministry has developed national guidelines for cervical cancer prevention and control to meet the needs of reproductive health and to address reproductive tract cancers.35 Besides, the health policy of Ethiopia has established a primary cervical cancer preventive strategy and initiated the HPV vaccine, the quadrivalent (4vHPV) type, in December 2018 and is being provided through a school-based approach targeting all eligible girls.3 36 37 Different trainings and community-based campaigns have been provided for parents and school-aged girls on cervical cancer, HPV and its vaccination to raise their level of awareness, parental engagement in the vaccination process and to increase the uptake of the vaccine.38 Although Ethiopia has made the HPV vaccination available to all eligible daughters, the uptake of the vaccine remains suboptimal.39,41 Parental engagement is fundamental for enhancing the uptake of the vaccine by their adolescent girls. Understanding parents’ awareness and beliefs about HPV and its vaccination may provide insights for designing strategies to improve the vaccine uptake, and data on their knowledge and attitudes are crucial for developing an efficient vaccination approach. However, studies assessing the knowledge and attitudes of parents towards the HPV vaccination for their daughters are limited in Ethiopia. A previous study conducted in Debre Tabor town on parents’ willingness to vaccinate their daughters against HPV identified that knowledge and attitudes of parents were significant predictors of their willingness to vaccinate.42 While the prior study primarily focused on willingness to vaccinate as the main outcome, the present study aimed to examine the levels and determinants of parents’ knowledge and attitudes towards the HPV vaccine. This focused investigation will provide evidence for more targeted interventions intended to improve awareness and acceptance of the HPV vaccine.

Methods

Study design, setting and period

This community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among parents of daughters from 15 December 2021 to 15 February 2022, in Debre Tabor town. Debre Tabor is the administrative town of the South Gondar Zone in the Amhara Regional State of Ethiopia, located about 665 km northwest of the capital city, Addis Ababa. The town has six small administrative units called kebeles, and according to Ethiopian population projections for all regions in 2017, it had a total population of 96 973.43 Debre Tabor town has one comprehensive specialised hospital, three health centres and five private clinics.

Population

All parents/caregivers having daughters aged 9–14 years in Debre Tabor town were the source population, and parents/caregivers of daughters aged 9–14 years in the selected kebeles of the town were the study population. Parents/caregivers with daughters aged 9–14 years who lived for at least 6 months in the study area were included in the study.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

The sample size of the study was calculated using a single population proportion formula by taking the following assumptions: a 95% level of confidence, a 5% marginal error and a 59.9% proportion of positive attitude of the parents towards the HPV vaccination for their daughters from a previous cross-sectional study conducted in Gondar town.44 This gives a sample size of 370. By considering a design effect of 1.5 and a non-response rate of 10%, the final sample size was 611. To select the study participants, first, three out of the six kebeles of Debre Tabor town were selected by simple random sampling using a lottery method. A cluster sampling technique was employed to select study participants in the selected kebeles, and all eligible parents/caregivers who have daughters aged between 9 and 14 years in the selected clusters were approached for the interview. When there were two parents/caregivers of the daughters from each household during the data collection time, a lottery method was employed to randomly select one respondent as the study participant. Finally, a total of 715 parents/caregivers were included in the study due to the effect of the cluster sampling approach.

Study variables

The dependent variables of the study were parents’ knowledge and attitude towards the HPV infection and vaccination for their daughters. The predictor variables were sociodemographic variables, including age, sex, relationship, religion, educational status, occupation, average monthly income, marital status and media exposure, as well as factors related to reproduction and other health (such as the number of daughters and family history of cervical cancer), subjective norms and perceived control towards the HPV vaccine.

Measurements and operational definitions

Knowledge

In this study, parents’ knowledge was assessed by eight yes-or-no knowledge-based questions, and knowledge scores were computed by giving 1 point to participants who correctly answered the questions and 0 to those who did not (for participants who answered ‘no’ or ‘not sure’). Participants of the study who scored mean and above on the knowledge score were considered to have good knowledge, while those who scored below the mean were considered to have poor knowledge.2 37

Parents’ attitude towards the HPV vaccine

A 5-point Likert scale ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’ was used for each of the eight attitude measurement items. Participants who scored the mean value and higher were classified as having a positive attitude, while those who scored below the mean value were classified as having a negative attitude.2 37

Subjective norms towards the HPV vaccination

Subjective norms are people’s anticipations of important or powerful people about whether or not to engage in a certain behaviour.34 The extent of pressure to which the participants perceived from influential individuals in the area to get vaccinated.45 46 These norms have a direct impact on changing behaviour.47 Subjective norms in the current study describe the degree to which parents’ attitudes towards the HPV vaccine for their daughters are impacted by whether influential individuals around them approve of the vaccine. A 5-point Likert scale ranging from ‘strongly disagree=1’ to ‘strongly agree=5’ for each subjective norm measurement item was used to assess it. Parents who responded to the mean value and higher were classified as having a positive subjective norm influence, whereas parents who scored below the mean value were considered to have a negative subjective norm influence.45 46

Perceived behavioural control of parents towards HPV vaccination

Perceived behavioural control (PBC) is the capacity to carry out an action. The participants thought of easy or complex in getting their daughters with HPV vaccination.34 45 48 49 It is a sense of control and self-governing that either encourages or hinders the performance of certain behavioural actions. Parents were considered to have positive perceived control if they scored the mean value or higher on the PBC measurements using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from ‘strongly disagree=1’ to ‘strongly agree=5’ on each PBC measurement item. Conversely, those who received lower scores were classified as having a negative perception of control.45 46 48

Media exposure

Participants who reported engaging with one or more forms of media (television, newspapers or radio) at least once a week were classified as having media exposure.50,52

Data collection instrument and procedure

A structured and pretested face-to-face interviewer-administered questionnaire was employed to collect the data (online supplemental file 1). The questionnaire was first developed in English and translated into the local (Amharic) language by language experts. Three Bachelors of Science (BSc) and one Master of Science (MSc) in the midwifery profession, who are familiar with the local language, were recruited as data collectors and a supervisor, respectively. Training was given for the data collectors and supervisor for 1 day on the data collection procedures, content of the questionnaire, interview techniques and ensuring confidentiality of the information obtained from the respondents.

While the actual data collection was conducted by the recruited team, the following authors, GMA, GLA, TTH, NTT and BAM were actively involved in the designation of the data collection tools, coordination and evaluation of the data collection process, and subsequent phases of the study, including data analysis and manuscript preparation.

Data quality control

Data quality was ensured through the collection, entry and analysis processes. Before collecting the actual data for the study, a pretest was carried out on 5% of the sample size to assess the clarity, ease of understanding by participants, appropriateness and flow of the questions, as well as to estimate the average time required to complete the questionnaire. Additionally, experts in the field assessed each item of the questionnaire for relevance, clarity and completeness. The reliability test was checked using Cronbach’s alpha and revealed a value of 0.88 and 0.75 for knowledge and attitude, respectively. During data collection, the questionnaires were reviewed and checked for completeness and accuracy by the supervisor and investigator at the end of each day.

Data processing and analysis

After the data were cleaned, coded and imported into EPI Data V.4.6, then exported to SPSS V.26 software for analysis. Descriptive statistics were conducted to present the characteristics of the study participants. To find statistically significant independent variables of the study, the binary logistic regression model was fitted and variables in the bivariable analysis with a p value of less than 0.25 were processed to the multivariable logistic regression. In the multivariable logistic regression analysis, variables were selected using the backward likelihood ratio approach. To identify statistically significant associations, the adjusted OR (AOR) was employed, along with its 95% CI at a p value of ≤0.05.

Patient and public involvement

Parents/caregivers of daughters were included in this study by providing valuable information relevant to the research objectives. However, they were not involved in the study design, protocol, data collection tools, reporting or dissemination of the findings.

Result

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study participants

A total of 702 participants with a response rate of 98.2% participated in this study, and more than half (57.8%) of the participants were females. The mean age of the respondents was 40.85 (SD±6.72) years, and 57.5% of them were within the age group of ≥40 years old. Most of the respondents (94.6%) practised Orthodox Christianity by their religion. In the study, married individuals made up the majority of study participants (87.2%). Of the study participants, nearly 39% had completed college and above in their educational level, and around one-third (34.9%) were governmental employees by occupation. In the study, more than two-thirds (67.9%) and approximately 62.4% of the participants had positive subjective norms and PBC regarding the HPV vaccination of their daughters, respectively (table 1).

Parents’ knowledge and attitudes towards the HPV infection and its vaccination

In this study, more than half (53.6% (95% CI 49.9% to 57.3%)) of the study participants did not know enough about HPV infection and the HPV vaccine. In convers, 61.5% (95% CI 58.0% to 65.2%) of parents had a positive attitude towards HPV vaccination for their daughters.

Factors associated with knowledge about HPV infection and the vaccine

Educational status, media exposure and attitude towards the HPV vaccine were the factors that had significantly associated with parents’ knowledge of HPV infection and vaccination in the multivariable logistic regression analysis.

In the study, participants who had an educational level of college and above were 2.27 (adjusted OR (AOR)=2.27, 95% CI 1.39 to 3.69) times more knowledgeable about HPV infection and the vaccine when compared with participants with no formal education.

Among the participants, parents who had exposure to mass media had a 3.36 times higher likelihood of possessing sufficient knowledge about the HPV infection and its vaccination for their daughters compared with those who did not use a media (AOR=3.36, 95% CI 1.21 to 9.33).

Furthermore, the odds of parents having good knowledge about the HPV infection and vaccination of their daughters against it were 8.81 times more likely to be higher among parents having a positive attitude towards the vaccine than parents who did not (AOR=8.81, 95% CI 5.78 to 13.44) (table 3).

Factors associated with attitude towards the HPV vaccine

Parents’ knowledge of the HPV infection and HPV vaccine, subjective norms and PBC on the HPV vaccine, in the multivariable logistic regression analysis, were the factors independently associated with parents’ attitude towards vaccination of their daughters with the HPV vaccine.

This study revealed that parents who had good knowledge about HPV infection and the vaccine were 8.67 times more likely to have a positive attitude towards the HPV vaccination of their daughters (AOR=8.67, 95% CI 5.62 to 13.38).

Similarly, the odds of having a favourable attitude towards vaccination of their daughters against HPV infection in the study were 1.53 (AOR=1.53, 95% CI 1.01 to 2.31) times higher among the parents who had positive subjective norms as compared with their counterparts.

Moreover, parents who had positive PBC towards HPV vaccination were 3.48 times more likely to have a favourable attitude than parents with negative PBC (AOR=3.48, 95% CI 2.37 to 5.10) (table 4).

Discussion

Possessing accurate information and a favourable mindset about the HPV vaccine is essential for increasing its uptake. Complementing our earlier study that assessed parents’ willingness towards the vaccination of their daughters against HPV in Debre Tabor town,42 the present study focused on exploring the levels and determinants of parental knowledge and attitudes towards the HPV vaccine for their daughters in Debre Tabor town, northwest Ethiopia. While the previous study identified knowledge and attitude as significant predictors of willingness, this study investigates these constructs as primary outcomes and examines the factors shaping these perceptions. It provides a more focused understanding of the cognitive and behavioural factors influencing HPV vaccine uptake, which can provide insight for targeted public health interventions. Importantly, although both studies were from the same geographical area, they involved different study participants and were designed independently, with distinct research questions, objectives and methodological approaches.

In the current study, more than half of the participants had poor knowledge about HPV infection and vaccination, while about three-fourths of them had a positive attitude towards the HPV vaccine. Parents’ educational status, media exposure, subjective norms and PBC regarding the HPV vaccine were significant predictors of parents’ knowledge and attitude about HPV infection and its vaccination.

The findings of this study indicated that less than half (46.4% (95% CI 42.7% to 50.1%)) of the respondents had good knowledge about HPV infection and the vaccine. The finding is in line with studies from Poland in different periods: 49%,53 49.4%,22 China, 48.9%31 and Ethiopia: Debre Markos town, 47.6%.27 However, in this study, the level of parents’ knowledge about HPV infection and vaccination was higher than studies conducted in Malaysia, 38%,54 and Ethiopia: 35.4%,55 39.1%29 and 28.3%.37 This difference might be due to certain sociodemographic factors from a study done in Debre Tabor, Ethiopia. In the present study, more than 57% of the study participants were mothers of daughters, compared with only 50% in the abovementioned study. This might be because mothers are the principal caregivers and the main decision-makers regarding the health of their children, being responsible for more than 80% of the children’s health-related decisions.56,58 Likewise, in studies conducted in central and southwest Ethiopia, the Bench Sheko zone included study participants from rural residences, while urban residences were in this study. This could be explained by the fact that people from urban areas will have better academic achievement, greater proximity to healthcare facilities and improved access to health services. Additionally, they may have better media exposure related to vaccination.

On the other hand, the level of parents’ knowledge about the HPV infection and the vaccine was lower than studies from the USA: 71%,59 80.4%,28 Brazil: 51.8%,60 Indonesia in different periods: 50.8%57 and 60%,61 Nigeria: 98.9%23 and Ethiopia: 59.2%.62 This might be due to the differences in the study population, socioeconomic status, cultural differences, healthcare system and information access. Study participants selected in Brazil and Indonesia are from those having a minimum of junior high school educational level, and more than half of them have college and above in their academic achievement. However, the present study includes all eligible participants, including those who had no formal education. Additionally, the difference in the aforementioned study from Nigeria might be due to some socio-demographic differences, such as more than 71% of the study participants had completed a tertiary level of education, whereas only 38.7% of the participants in this study had attained this level of education. This could be explained by the fact that participants with higher educational levels are more likely to have a better understanding of children’s health, better knowledge of vaccination programmes in general and the HPV vaccination in particular.57 The period of launching the programme might also be another reason for the difference. In Nigeria, the HPV vaccination programme was introduced in 2009, 9 years earlier than in Ethiopia. Over time, the parents will have greater opportunities to access information through different channels, leading to increased awareness and understanding of the vaccine. The study done in Ethiopia, in the same study setting, is conducted from high and primary school students, unlike parents, who are used in the current study, and this could be explained by the fact that students may have learnt more about HPV infection, its primary preventive measures and might have a better understanding of the vaccination from their teachers and media that they could able to access easily.

The present study revealed that approximately three-fourths (61.5%) of the respondents had a positive attitude towards the HPV vaccine for their daughters. This is in line with studies done in Addis Ababa, Gondar and Bahir Dar, Ethiopia: 58%,63 59.9%3 and 63.5%,2 respectively. The present study had also identified that parents’ attitude was higher than those in studies conducted from Malaysia, 42.5%,64 Indonesia, 48%61 and Ethiopia: Arba Minch town, 51%65 and central Ethiopia, 40.2%.29 This might be due to the differences in the study subjects involved, socioeconomic status and health information access. The study subjects who participated in Arba Minch town, Ethiopia, were girls aged between 13 and 14 years, while parents were used in the current study. Parents are more likely to have access to information about their child’s health and the HPV vaccine, which may contribute to a more positive attitude towards the HPV vaccination. Similarly, study participants from rural areas were involved in the study of central Ethiopia, while participants from urban areas were in this study. People from urban areas will have better access to information about child vaccinations, including HPV, through various sources such as social media and healthcare providers. Consequently, they may develop a greater understanding of and a more favourable attitude towards the HPV vaccine.

On the other hand, the attitude of parents towards vaccination of their daughters with the HPV vaccine was lower than studies from Indonesia, 82%,57 Lebanon, 65.5%66 and Ethiopia, 77.4%.27 The difference might be due to variations in the study settings, socioeconomic status, health information systems and access to healthcare services.

The findings of this study identified that educational level was significantly associated with parents’ knowledge of HPV infection and vaccination. Parents who had an educational level of college and above were 2.27 times more knowledgeable about the HPV infection and its vaccine for their daughters than those who did not have a formal education. This finding is consistent with studies from China, Indonesia, Serbia and Ethiopia.27 31 57 67 This might be justified by the fact that those who have higher educational achievement tend to have better knowledge, health-seeking practices, a better understanding of the HPV vaccine and may be able to refer to the vaccine from different sources. Therefore, awareness creation and health education targeting those with lower educational performance are essential to increase the concept of the vaccine among the parents and thereby increase the utilisation of the vaccine among adolescents in the community.

Likewise, consistent with previous studies,27 66 this study showed that a positive attitude of parents towards the HPV vaccination was significantly associated with their knowledge. Parents who had a positive attitude towards HPV vaccination were 8.81 times more likely to know about HPV infection and its vaccination for their daughters when compared with parents who had unfavourable attitudes. The reason for this might be attributed to the significant influence of the parents’ perspectives and opinions regarding the effectiveness of all recommended vaccinations, including the HPV vaccine, which can play a major role in obtaining a better understanding of the vaccine. Another more possible explanation could be due to the general perception that cancer is a dangerous and deadly disease that leads people to know more about it and the preventive mechanisms. Attitudes of individuals may influence their motivation to seek, accept or retain knowledge.68 Parents with a positive attitude or interest towards the HPV vaccination may be more likely to engage with relevant information to acquire greater knowledge on the infection and its vaccination. Therefore, in order to increase parents’ understanding of the HPV vaccine, it would be necessary to fill gaps through public health education initiatives, which will consequently improve the vaccine uptake in the long run.

The present study also identified that media exposure was significantly associated with parents’ knowledge of HPV infection and HPV vaccination for their daughters. The odds of being knowledgeable about the HPV vaccine were 3.36 times higher among parents exposed to media than those who were not. This is supported by a study done in Zambia.69 This could be plausibly explained by the fact that mass media are a means of information dissemination, health education and awareness creation for the whole community. Therefore, it is extremely important to promote the HPV infection, its complications and preventive mechanisms through media to increase vaccine awareness, and in doing so, will increase the uptake for the coming.

The current study identified that parents who had good knowledge about the HPV infection and vaccination were 8.67 times more likely to have favourable attitudes towards the vaccination of their daughters with the HPV vaccine than those who had poor knowledge. The finding is supported by studies from Indonesia, Nigeria, Serbia, Lebanon and Ethiopia.23 27 57 66 67 This could be explained by the fact that individuals with a better level of knowledge will have a behavioural change, perceive positive attitudes and be more motivated in the preventive practices related to HPV infection and cervical cancer. Thus, parental awareness-raising campaigns should emphasise how the virus will spread, its potential to cause cancer and the role of HPV vaccination in preventing cervical cancer, to improve vaccine uptake among daughters.

Similarly, the odds of parents having positive attitudes towards the HPV vaccine for their daughters in the study were 1.53 times higher among parents who had positive subjective norms than among their counterparts. This is supported by studies performed in Thailand, Chicago, Serbia and the USA; Central Kentucky and New York.18 32 67 70 71 This result might suggest that parents who value religion as a significant factor in their decision-making could know more about HPV and cervical cancer and may be more supportive of vaccinating their daughters with the vaccine. Similarly, parents could be more likely to trust and follow the recommendations of healthcare providers when the vaccine is strongly recommended by them. Parents who have received strong advice from medical professionals to vaccinate their children were more likely to accept vaccination for their children, adhere to immunisation appointments and express fewer doubts or concerns about the vaccine.32 33 70 Evidence also highlighted that more than two-thirds of parents would vaccinate their daughters with the HPV vaccine if they received recommendations from physicians.28 Therefore, it is essential to address the religious issues within the community and engage healthcare providers in culturally relevant vaccine promotion strategies to enhance the positive aspect of the community on the vaccine and ultimately improve vaccine uptake in the broader community.

Finally, the finding revealed that parents with positive PBC towards the HPV vaccine were able to express their favour to vaccinate their daughters. The odds of having a favourable attitude towards the HPV vaccine for their daughters were 3.48 times higher among parents with a positive PBC than those with a negative PBC. This is supported by a study done in Nigeria.23 The finding may suggest that perceived barriers in setting up the vaccination programme outside of a school-based system, for financial, time or transportation constraints, can influence parents’ attitudes. Similarly, parents will determine whether to allow their daughters to receive the HPV vaccine without taking the cost of the vaccine into consideration, as providing the vaccine for free of charge will strengthen parents’ perceived control and improve the utilisation of the vaccine by their daughters. Moreover, community-level administration of the HPV vaccine, particularly through village-based programmes, may further facilitate the acceptance and utilisation of the vaccine. On the other hand, control over performing a certain behaviour or action could be a hesitation to decide on the vaccination of their daughters.34 Parents may underestimate the risk of certain diseases in their children and perceive their daughters to be at low risk of acquiring the HPV infection, which can reinforce negative attitudes towards vaccination and affect engagement in preventive behaviours. This highlights the need for interventions on attitudes towards the HPV vaccine as a strategy to promote positive behavioural changes. Efforts should also focus on raising community awareness that vaccinations not only protect their children but also limit the spread of infectious diseases within the community.

Strengths of the study include the use of community-based data with a representative sample of study participants. However, the study has limitations due to the cross-sectional nature of the study design, which precluded the evaluation of the temporality and causality of the observed relationships. The study may also be affected by recall and social desirability bias. However, in order to decrease such bias, participants were first told in detail about the purpose of the study and how the data would be handled to maintain confidentiality. In addition, they were thoroughly briefed on the importance of their participation, which could have a critical influence on the study outcomes. This study mainly focused on the quantitative approach, and it was unable to explore in depth the qualitative insights underlying the observed findings.

Conclusion

The present study identified that more than half of the parents had a limited level of knowledge about HPV infection and its vaccination, despite a majority expressing a positive attitude towards vaccinating their daughters. Parents’ knowledge was significantly associated with educational status, media exposure and their attitude towards the HPV vaccine. Knowledge of HPV, subjective norms and PBC were the factors influencing parents’ attitude towards the vaccination of their daughters. The wide range of knowledge gaps identified in this study provides an important insight for future community-focused awareness-raising and education campaigns. Continuous public education programmes and effective advertisement of cervical cancer, HPV infection and its primary prevention through multimedia are needed to enhance parental knowledge about HPV, HPV-related diseases and its vaccination in the study setting. Similarly, to improve the uptake of the HPV vaccine, measures that reduce vaccination barriers, addressing misconceptions and unfavourable beliefs among parents are required.
