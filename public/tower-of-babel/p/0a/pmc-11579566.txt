Hospital Experiences of Carers in Relation to the Comprehensive Care Standard: A Mixed Method Study
Authors: Beibei Xiong, Christine Stirling, Daniel X Bailey, Emmy Trinh, Ziyinyue Zeng, Melinda Martin‐Khan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo explore the care experiences of informal carers of people with chronic conditions in hospitals and identify areas for improvement in the context of comprehensive care delivery.DesignA explanatory sequential mixed‐method study.MethodsThis study was conducted, involving a survey with 182 carers and interviews with 31 carers of individuals who attended an Australian hospital. Introduction

An increasingly ageing population and individuals with chronic conditions underscore the growing importance of comprehensive care, as the traditional model of disease‐specific care often falls short for those with chronic conditions (Hopman et al. 2016). Comprehensive care, defined as the ‘coordinated delivery of total health care required and requested by a patient’ (ACSQHC 2021, 43), presents a critical approach to address the complex needs of such patients. However, delivering this level of care proves particularly challenging within hospital settings, which are typically oriented towards acute care needs and may overlook conditions beyond the primary reason for presentation, resulting in inadequate care provision (Been‐Dahmen et al. 2015). To address this challenge, some countries have introduced national standards for comprehensive care to ensure patients receive holistic and coordinated care in hospitals (Xiong, Stirling, and Martin‐Khan 2023). Informal carers play a crucial role in the delivery of comprehensive care. This study explored the care experiences of carers in hospitals and identified areas for improvement in the context of comprehensive care delivery. Findings from this study will provide insights that can guide the development of strategies to enhance comprehensive care practices.

Background

In 2017, Australia introduced the Comprehensive Care Standard (CCS) as part of the National Safety and Quality Health Services Standards (ACSQHC 2021). Mandating implementation across all hospitals from 2019, these Standards are subject to accreditation assessments every 3 years to ensure compliance (Xiong, Stirling, and Martin‐Khan 2023). Recognising the important role of carers, who possess intimate knowledge of the patient and devote substantial time to their care, is critical in comprehensive care provision. The CCS emphasises partnership with carers in the whole process of delivering comprehensive care (ACSQHC 2018). Six essential elements of comprehensive care delivery include clinical assessment and diagnosis, goal identification, risk screening, comprehensive care planning, care delivery and care review and improvement, each of which requires carer involvement (ACSQHC 2018).

An informal carer (referred to as ‘carer’), or care partner, is a person who gives regular, ongoing assistance such as family members, friends or neighbours without direct payment from the person they care for (AIHW 2023a). Their involvement is becoming a guiding principle to achieving a high‐quality and high‐value health system in Australia, the UK, the USA and other countries (Frampton et al. 2017; Javed and Herrman 2017). Carers offer unique insights into the needs and preferences of patients with chronic conditions. They provide direct assistance, offer emotional support, advocate on behalf of the patient, provide information and participate in medical decision‐making (Clissett et al. 2013; Gwernan‐Jones et al. 2020). Their role usually increases as the patient's physical and cognitive status deteriorates (Gitlin and Wolff 2011). By undertaking many important caregiving tasks and activities, carers complement the efforts of healthcare professionals in delivering comprehensive care. However, the caregiving role can adversely impact informal carers' physical and mental health, especially in hospital settings, yet their needs and contributions are often overlooked by health services (Clissett et al. 2013).

Several studies have explored the perspective of carers, highlighting key aspects of a positive hospital experience such as receiving adequate support, involvement in care processes and access to information (Rustad et al. 2017). While care experiences in primary care settings have been extensively reviewed for individuals with chronic conditions and their carers, there has been limited research on carers' perspectives on the delivery of care for patients with chronic conditions in hospital settings (Clissett et al. 2013).

The Study

Our study aims to explore hospital experiences of carers of patients with chronic conditions in the context of comprehensive care delivery. This information is imperative for informing best practices in the delivery of comprehensive care in hospital settings.

Specifically, our research aims to address the following questions:

What are the experiences of hospital care among carers of patients with chronic conditions?

What are the experiences of hospital care among carers of patients with chronic conditions?

Are there any unmet needs among carers of patients with chronic conditions, and if so, what are the gaps in care?

Are there any unmet needs among carers of patients with chronic conditions, and if so, what are the gaps in care?

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study used a mixed‐method explanatory sequential design, consisting of a survey followed by interviews, both conducted online. The rationale for this approach is that the survey provides a broad understanding of the carers' care experiences, while the interview offers deeper insights by exploring carers' perspectives in more depth.

The cross‐sectional survey was distributed and administered using the Checkbox survey platform (Checkbox Technology, Inc.) from September 2023 to December 2023. Semi‐structured interviews took place between November 2023 and January 2024, via phone or Zoom, according to participants' preferences. The time period between the survey and the interview was normally 2 weeks.

A Consensus‐Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS) and the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines were followed (Appendix S1).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

For survey participants, inclusion criteria were: (1) aged 18 years or over, (2) capable of understanding and responding to questions, (3) either patients 1 themselves or self‐nominated as individuals providing regular, ongoing assistance to family members, friends or neighbours without direct payment from the person they care for, (4) care recipient had attended an Australian hospital for medical treatment/care in the last 3 months (including inpatient, outpatient or emergency department visits), (5) had direct interactions with hospital staff during the patient's hospital stay. Additionally, for interview participants: (6) the person that they cared for lived with chronic conditions. Exclusion criteria were: (1) choosing not to consent or being unable to give consent; or (2) requiring a translator.

Survey participants consisted of carers of patients with any health condition. Limiting participation to individuals with chronic conditions was impractical as the study invitation was publicly posted, and not all respondents may comprehend the term ‘chronic conditions’. Interview participants comprised individuals with chronic conditions exclusively. This subset of patients presents long‐term and/or multiple care needs and can provide richer insights for comprehensive care during interviews. Therefore, we analysed all data and included a subgroup analysis of surveys on the presence of chronic conditions.

Definition of Chronic Condition

A question in the survey identified whether the care recipient had chronic conditions. Persons with any of the 10 chronic conditions falling within the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare's (AIHW) major groupings and definitions of chronic conditions were considered as having a chronic condition: arthritis, asthma, back pain, cancers, cardiovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, mental disorders or osteoporosis (AIHW 2023b; Zurynski et al. 2022). The question had an ‘Other’ option for participants to write down any other health conditions, which were reviewed to identify conditions meeting the definition of a chronic condition. Persons without chronic conditions chose ‘none of the chronic illnesses or conditions listed’.

Recruitment

The survey utilised convenience, purposive and snowball sampling methods to recruit participants. Recruitment strategies included the use of social media platforms such as X, Facebook and Instagram (including Facebook paid advertisements), industry‐specific websites and newsletters, as well as emails sent to publicly accessible relevant organisations, such as Dementia Australia and StepUp for Aging/Dementia Research, requesting the circulation of the survey. Participants who were interested in a subsequent interview were asked to leave their contact details on a separate webpage that is not linked to their survey responses. Confirmation of eligible interviewees was conducted by data collectors via phone. Participants were provided with an AUD $50 gift card upon completion of the interview.

Data Collection Instrument

Surveys were developed following an examination of previous literature on patient hospital experiences, which included the Australian Hospital Patient Experience Question Set (ACSQHC 2012), the CCS implementation guide and insights from experts. A survey development focus group was held with representatives from the eQC Patient and Advisory Board (eQC Board) to hear their ideas and opinions about the survey drafts, and two rounds of feedback were used to improve the survey.

All items in the questionnaire were assessed for readability, adequacy, relevance, practicality and ethicality of the survey in a pilot test with a convenient group of carers. Following the test, errors that arose during the transition from the paper survey to the online format were addressed, while the survey's general content remained unchanged. The final questionnaire included 13 demographic questions and 13 questions related to hospital experiences (Appendix S2).

The interview guide (Appendix S3) was formulated based on the literature review, preliminary findings from the survey and expert input. The interview guide was piloted with two persons. The addition of probe questions and modifications to the wording were made to reach the final version. Interviews were audio‐recorded with consent. Participants were given the option to review transcripts for confirmation before analysis, although none opted to do so.

Sample Size

According to Roscoe's Simple Rule of Thumb (Hill 1998), each subgroup should ideally comprise samples of 30 or more. As our study involves two sub‐groups (with or without chronic conditions), the total sample size needed would be at least 60. Additionally, Weisberg, Krosnick, and Bowen (1989) suggest a sample size of 100 respondents for e‐surveys if a sample error level of 10% is acceptable. A sample size of at least 100 satisfied both criteria and was the minimum target for our study.

It was estimated that 25–30 interview participants would suffice to address the study's objectives, but we continued interviews until data saturation in code and meaning was achieved.

Quality Control

A verification question was included in the survey: ‘To confirm your understanding of the survey's operation, please select “Agree” to this question.’ Respondents who did not select ‘Agree’ were considered invalid and subsequently excluded. Out of the 287 responses received for this study, 26 were deemed invalid. Survey data was excluded if respondents completed the demographic section but provided no answers in the main section.

Outcome Measures

Participants were asked to respond to questions related to hospital experiences (Appendix S2) using a 5‐point Likert scale ranging from ‘1’ (strongly disagree) to ‘5’ (strongly agree) or from ‘1’ (very bad) to ‘5’ (very good).

Data Analysis

Quantitative data were organised in Microsoft Excel with statistical analyses carried out using R (version 4.1.0) and RStudio (version 1.4.1717) software. A two‐sided p‐value < 0.05 was used to indicate statistical significance. Descriptive statistics, such as cross‐tabulations and proportions, were used to present survey data. Proportions were based on the valid responses for each question, as the number of respondents varied. Continuous variables like age and length of stay were not normally distributed, so median and interquartile were utilised for representation. Subgroup analysis based on diagnosis (with or without chronic conditions) was performed, and the Chi‐square test (or Fisher's test when expected cell frequencies were below 5) was employed to detect group differences.

Interviews were audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim by a professional company (GoTranscript). NVivo (version 12.7.0) was used to manage and analyse all qualitative data, including free text from surveys and interviews, using thematic analysis. BX maintained reflective notes after each interview, documenting emotional responses and thoughts for use during the data coding and theming process to mitigate researcher bias. BX and ZZ independently coded the interview data using a step‐by‐step guide (Maguire and Delahunt 2017) developed from Braun and Clarke (2006), with constant comparison between coding and emerging themes to ensure grounded interpretation. Weekly consensus discussions were held to resolve disagreements, with DB attending those discussions to provide guidance and support for theme analysis. Additionally, CS and MMK offered guidance for data analysis, and all authors assisted with reviewing and refining coded themes.

Integration of Qualitative and Quantitative Data

To integrate data sources, we grouped the survey items into key topics that corresponded to essential facets of hospital experiences, as identified through interviews. We then analysed the response values of these survey items within each identified topic. Additionally, we used data from the interviews to help explain our findings and deepen our comprehension of carers' experiences regarding these key components. Illustrative quotes are provided to exemplify our findings.

Patient and Public Involvement

Before the study commenced, the University of Queensland eQC Board (Xiong et al. 2023) was identified as a partner representing consumers. The primary objective of the Board's involvement was to ensure that the study remained focused on the priorities and needs of patients and their families. The Board was made up of a group of six patients and care partners and met every 2 months for the duration of the project. The Board contributed to the design of the study, participant recruitment, interpretation and dissemination of findings.

Ethical Considerations

Participation in the study was voluntary and confidentiality was assured as no identifiable information was collected. The survey was completed anonymously, and completion of the survey implied consent. Informed consent was verbally confirmed by all participants before interviews began. Ethical approval was granted by the University of Queensland Human Research Ethics Committee (ID: 2023/HE001179).

Research Team and Reflexivity

The interviews were conducted by two researchers: a research assistant with a bachelor's degree in nursing and part‐time employment as a nurse in a public hospital (ET), and a PhD candidate with a bachelor's degree in nursing, a master's degree in health sciences, and expertise in qualitative research (BX). BX was concurrently pursuing her PhD related to this project. ET was not involved in recruiting participants through her job in the hospital. ET led all interviews, with BX providing support via the chat function of Microsoft Teams. Both researchers underwent two training sessions: one focusing on interview techniques and the other on the interview guide. Two practice interviews were conducted with team researchers; however, pilot data from these practice sessions were excluded from the final analysis.

Rigour and Trustworthiness of Qualitative Data

In this study, we employed several strategies to enhance the rigour and trustworthiness of our data. Credibility was ensured through triangulation, cross‐referencing interview and survey data and using participant quotes to support interpretations. Discussions with the eQC Board added diverse perspectives. Transferability was enhanced by providing detailed descriptions of the sampling strategy, research context and participants, allowing readers to assess the generalisability of our findings to other settings. To ensure dependability and confirmability, we maintained a detailed audit trail, documenting every step of the research process. We also engaged in peer debriefing, where research colleagues reviewed and provided feedback on the data collection, coding and interpretation.

Results

Characteristics of the Sample

Survey Participants

We received a total of 261 valid responses, out of which 79 were found to be ineligible (i.e., hospital attendance not in the past 3 months and/or no interactions with hospital staff). This left us with 182 responses that were both valid and eligible for our analysis. On average, participants spent 14 min (SD = 4) completing the survey. The Interview had an average duration of 41 min (SD = 10).

The median age of the participants was 41.5 years with an interquartile range of 32–54 years and a range of 20–79 years (Table 1). Among the respondents, 72.5% were female (n = 132), and in 72.8% of cases, the person they cared for sought treatment at public hospitals (n = 131), while the majority reported being a carer of 1 year or more (n = 146, 81.6%). Additionally, 90.6% of the participants indicated that the person they cared for had a chronic condition (n = 163). Diagnoses were varied and included cardiovascular disease, dementia, arthritis, diabetes, etc.

Interview Participants

A total of 31 carers participated in the interviews. The median age of the participants was 52 years with an interquartile range of 42.5–64.5 years and a range of 31–76 years (Table 1). The median age of the patients for whom they were carers was 77 years with an interquartile range of 63–85 years and a range of 4–97 years. Among the respondents, the majority were female (n = 27, 87.1%). Approximately two‐thirds sought treatment at public hospitals (n = 22, 64.5%). Diagnoses were varied and included heart disease, cancer, dementia, stroke, diabetes, etc.

Overall Hospital Experience

The majority of carers had positive overall hospital experiences, a sentiment supported by both survey and interview findings. In the survey, 69.5% of participants (n = 123/177) reported that their overall experiences in the hospital were either good or very good. Similarly, during the interviews, 71.0% of participants (n = 22/31) expressed positivity regarding their hospital experiences in general, the rest experienced poor hospital experiences.

Unfortunately, the experience was pretty poor, because I had to stand in front of her and sort of almost beg for someone to come and put us in the picture as to where Mum was at. (CF24)

Many participants acknowledged that there was room for improvement.

It was a good experience with room for improvement. (CF08)

Furthermore, it was observed that overall hospital experiences were significantly better in non‐ED (emergency department) settings compared to ED settings (p = 0.005), as evidenced by both survey and interview results. No significant differences in overall experience were found between private hospital settings and public hospital settings. Five interview participants commented on the comparison of their experiences in public and private hospitals, and they had mixed opinions.

Carer Perceptions of Staff Traits

During the interview, many participants shared their perspectives on the characteristics of staff members. Many thought staff were caring, thoughtful, patient, empathetic, proactive and responsive to patients' needs. One participant reflected,

They were very helpful. They were very understanding, kind, and very accommodating. (CF15)

This sentiment highlights the importance of staff members' compassionate manner in fostering a supportive and caring environment.

Furthermore, a notable gesture mentioned was when staff organised a celebratory cake for a participant's mother's birthday, showcasing their thoughtfulness and personal investment in fostering rapport with patients. Moreover, participants also recognised the professionalism and expertise demonstrated by certain staff members. As articulated by one participant,

…very highly trained professionals who care about the work that they're doing. They're extremely hard‐worked and run off their feet …. (CF28)

Conversely, some participants had less favourable interactions, where they perceived certain staff members as rude, impatient and not caring, with instances where staff members dismissed their concerns with statements such as ‘not my job’. Additionally, participants reported experiences of encountering staff members who exhibited unprofessionalism and lacked adequate knowledge, skills or experience. For instance, one participant recalled a concerning incident where a doctor confused them with another patient.

Carer Inclusion in the Assessment Process

In the survey, 75.7% of participants (n = 137/181) expressed agreement or strong agreement with the statement: ‘I was given the opportunity to provide relevant information about the person I care for’. In the interviews, participants shared insights into the assessment procedures they and the person they cared for underwent, revealing a spectrum of experiences ranging from thorough and inclusive assessments to instances where assessments were perceived as lacking.

Many interview participants described positive experiences with comprehensive assessments in which they felt actively involved. For instance, one participant shared how an occupational therapist (OT) sought to understand the patient's preferences, exemplifying a patient‐centred approach where efforts were made to gather information directly from the patient's carers when direct communication with the patient was challenging.

The OT came in and tried to find out the things that he liked or disliked. Obviously, he couldn't tell them, so they rang me and asked me. (CF16)

Moreover, some participants specifically mentioned their involvement in cognitive assessments of their family members, illustrating a holistic approach to understanding the patient's mental state.

They asked what medication she was on. Especially with the delirium, when that happened, how her mental state was beforehand, and was she the type to use her brain and stimulate her brain in different ways. (CF27)

However, not all participants reported such positive experiences. Some expressed dissatisfaction with the comprehensiveness of the assessment and perceived omissions. For example, a few participants felt excluded from the assessment, expressing the belief that their insights as primary carers could have ensured a more holistic understanding of the patient's condition.

It would have been really helpful if the psychologist or psychiatrist who did that assessment could have actually made contact with me because I've probably got the best idea of where his mental health is at as someone who spends the most time with him…. (CF13)

Furthermore, several participants raised concerns about the absence of professional cognitive assessments despite raising concerns about their family member's cognitive decline and behavioural changes.

I thought they might've got someone from the actual geriatric team in, but they didn't. …When I told them … her memory had started to decline and her behaviour was getting a little bit more highs and lows. She'd get angry at me for things. She'd become fixated on things like, I put a new electricity meter on her house and she was convinced that other people were using her electricity that was connected to the internet. That's why I thought they might have thought, well, … because of those issues, we'll look into it. They didn't. (CF11)

Carer Inclusion in Shared Decision‐Making

In the survey, 67.4% of participants (n = 122/181) expressed agreement or strong agreement with the statement: ‘I was involved in making decisions affecting the person I care for’. Similarly, 73.5% of participants (n = 133/181) indicated agreement or strong agreement with the statement: ‘I was given opportunities to discuss the treatment and care of the person I care for’.

In the interview, some participants expressed that carers were involved in, listened to, consulted, discussed or well informed to make shared decisions, especially significant medical decisions.

They were very open to my suggestions. They listened to me. I felt heard. (CF15)

Moreover, participants highlighted care professionals' efforts to ensure that carers remain engaged and informed, even when not physically present. As one participant explained,

I think I was involved and if there were any decisions, actually, because I was on the phone a lot out in the corridor and someone would come and get me to be involved in the decision‐making. (CF02)

However, not all participants felt adequately involved in decision‐making processes. Some expressed frustration with decisions being made without their input or being informed poorly, leading to conflicts. One participant mentioned,

Sometimes probably smaller decisions were made without us and maybe weren't communicated to us, and it would lead to conflict. (CF01)

Similarly, another participant described feeling unheard and marginalised in decision‐making discussions, expressing frustration with the healthcare team's exclusive focus on the patient's perspective.

No, I wasn't [involved in decision making] … Being old school, he doesn't like to annoy anyone or put them out, plus he's deaf as well. He'd just say he's really good. It was hard for me to get that across when they take the patient's word first because that's who they're consulting. They haven't discussed anything with me. … They're not listening. (CF10)

During the interviews, participants shared varying experiences regarding their involvement in care planning for the person they cared for. Some reported having a care plan in place and being actively involved in care planning. For instance, one participant stated,

They would talk with me about them, what the plans were and how I felt. I did have the opportunity to speak up… They were certainly very proactive in listening and acting on some of the issues and suggestions that I raised. (CF05)

However, for others, the care planning process did not meet their expectations, with care plans perceived as basic or lacking sufficient detail. Similarly, another participant highlighted the challenge of not having full visibility of the care plan as a primary carer:

There's no long‐term care plan …as a primary carer not really having full visibility of what the care plan is and what it looks like. (CF13)

This lack of transparency can lead to feelings of uncertainty and frustration for carers who played a central role in providing care and support to the person they cared for.

Carer Participation in the Care Process

During the interviews, participants expressed diverse experiences regarding their involvement in the care process for the person they cared for. Some felt actively encouraged and given opportunities to participate in caregiving tasks and processes. For example, one participant described feeling welcomed to contribute, stating,

I was able to help where I felt I could. … I was always invited to go along and observe, and ask questions if I needed to and so on. … I could observe and learn what I would need to be doing … It was all there for me to do as much as I wanted to do or could do. (CF05)

Some participants, particularly those caring for paediatric patients, felt that staff relied heavily on them in the care process. One participant described how they were often tasked with providing crucial information, stating,

Even though it's all in her notes, people really don't read the notes and they actually rely on us a lot. We would often be taking clinical photos … when the care team would come around and ask … we could just show them. (CF01)

On the other hand, some participants expressed frustration at feeling excluded from the care process.

It's just like no one told me what to do, to take medication or what time to change it. He couldn't remember what's been told because he exactly was not really conscious just after the operation. (CM02)

Additionally, some participants mentioned challenges related to restricted visiting hours, which limited their ability to participate in the care process.

I couldn't apparently approach him. I would have liked to [participate in the care process], but the supervisor that was in charge of the floor or that section of the hospital, I sent a message to her and asked her, ‘Could I have an exemption to be with him because he was dying? …’ She said no. That was denied …. (CF20)

Carers' Needs

In the survey, 75.7% of participants (n = 137/181) agreed or strongly agreed with the statement: ‘My opinions as an informal carer (or care partner) were respected’. In the interview, some participants also expressed a sense of being valued and respected, with their opinions and contributions acknowledged and appreciated. For example, one participant stated,

I was always treated with respect. Not treated like somebody silly and didn't know what they were doing or talking about. I appreciated …. (CF05)

Similarly, another participant highlighted feeling respected and acknowledged, mentioning the provision of a carer medical certificate as a gesture of acknowledgement:

They also gave me a carer medical certificate, so that was very good. …Like being listened to, being respected, and being acknowledged. (CF08)

Moreover, some participants appreciated the care and support extended to them as carers, such as being provided with food, drinks and other assistance during hospital stays. For instance, one participant shared,

I've always been so looked after as a carer. They would always give me food and make sure I'm hydrated and warm, and make sure I have a seat to sit on. When I was looking after my grandma, they actually pulled up an extra bed for me …. (CF08)

Furthermore, participants appreciated the flexibility shown by staff in accommodating the specific needs of carers and patients. For example, one participant mentioned,

In relation to Alzheimer's, I explained that he does get disorientated and gets a bit off‐centre when I'm not around. They were very good at accommodating, allowing me to stay with him right until the time he went into surgery. (CF15)

Not all participants felt adequately respected or listened to as carers. Some expressed frustration at being ignored or disregarded by care professionals.

I kept on explaining to both doctors and nursing staff about his arthritis and the fact that when they were rolling him, they were potentially hurting his back or his hips or his knees, and consequently, he would lash out. Everybody totally ignored all of that about pain. (CF16)

Similarly, some participants felt that the level of care or support provided to them was not adequate. For instance, one participant described feeling neglected in terms of basic needs such as access to food during long hours spent in the hospital:

I was in there with him until I think about 7 PM at night. I hadn't eaten all day. I wasn't offered, I had to ask if there was a tea room so I could go and grab a coffee. I just thought having been in the hospital for hours, hours, and hours that they could have offered, but they didn't. (CF15)

Some participants expressed experiencing carer distress and highlighted the lack of mental health support to manage their challenges. For example, one participant stated,

I was quite stressed. I think I absorbed all that I possibly could, bearing the state of mind I was in at the time, given that it was quite stressful. I think I felt I was doing enough. I would go away drained by the end of the time at the hospital. (CF05)

Similarly, another participant stated emotional and physical strain experienced by carers, particularly when caring for someone with complex health needs and cognitive impairments in high‐stress environments such as hospitals:

It was really stressful and upsetting… It's a big mental load in doing that… I was up the entire night and didn't sleep. I was exhausted. Then it puts pressure on the primary carer if you are caring for an elderly person who fell with dementia, and I'm sleep deprived myself. (CF17)

Communication and Multidisciplinary Collaboration

In the survey, 64.4% of participants (n = 114/177) agreed or strongly agreed with the statement: ‘Staff caring for the person I care for worked together and shared consistent and correct information about them’. During the interviews, participants shared varied perspectives on the level of teamwork and communication among staff members and with carers.

Some interview participants described positive experiences of collaborative teamwork, highlighting effective communication and coordination among healthcare professionals. For instance, one participant mentioned the proactive approach taken by the medical team in consulting specialists,

They actually consulted the specialist, like the patient always sees before when they develop the care plan…The specialist was consulted for this particular occasion, and also the medicine for that day was adjusted to incorporate the current treatment. (CF04)

Furthermore, some participants praised the responsiveness and clarity of communication they experienced and the willingness to address carers' concerns and queries promptly when interacting with staff members. As one participant mentioned,

It was pretty good speaking to the staff. They were quite friendly, quite efficient, and quick to respond to my questions. (CF27)

However, some expressed frustration with what they perceived as disjointed teamwork and poor collaboration among staff members. For example, one participant expressed disappointment at the lack of cooperation observed between doctors and nurses,

From my observation, I did not see any cooperation [among doctors and nurses] at all. [laughs] I only see the nurses. (CF18)

Some participants attributed the lack of consistency in care to challenges related to communication, including delays and inconsistencies in information dissemination among staff and to carers.

I suppose because the wards are so busy and because of the changeover of shifts and nurses, there's this inconsistency or a huge delay in communication. That's probably the main thing. (CF08)

While some participants mentioned family meetings as a means of communication and decision‐making, others felt that these meetings were not always helpful.

Then they just kept scheduling these meetings for us, and it was like, ‘We've already made our decision. Why are you doing this?’ … Everyone's at a different level. They would always have these meetings with us but we would always walk away with nothing…. (CF01)

There was a suggestion for both staff‐initiated and family‐initiated meetings to facilitate shared decision‐making and care planning.

Information and Guidance

In the survey, many participants expressed satisfaction with the support and information provided to them. For instance, 63.0% (n = 114/181) felt they received the necessary assistance to participate in these decisions, while 70.6% (n = 125/177) felt they were given specific information relevant to their role in the ongoing treatment and care. Additionally, 62.7% (n = 111/177) believed they were provided with enough information to help prevent or manage any health problems identified in the hospital.

During the interview, some conveyed positive experiences, indicating that they received adequate information and resources that were well explained to them.

They were explaining the things in a really easy way, easy manner and everything…They were coming in for assistance and telling me to do it rather than they are doing it on their own. I will be doing it at home, so I'm doing it right… They gave me a managing plan or emergency plan so what you have to do. It was really helpful. (CF06)

Similarly, another participant appreciated ‘the provision of a roadmap’ (CF03), which outlined treatment plans and scheduled all future appointments.

Participants highlighted the provision of education and training on self‐care, not only for patients but also for carers.

They trained both me and my husband in central line care. We can now change her Hickman dressing and change these needleless connectors at the end of her Hickman line and that's wonderful because we can do that in our own home…. (CF01)

Participants valued the provision of resources for both patients and carers.

They gave us a list of resources to go to as a patient… Then for me as a carer, carers need care as well, what is currently out there available for me…. (CF08)

On the other hand, many participants expressed dissatisfaction with the level of information provided, feeling uninformed and lacking updates about the condition of the person they cared for.

I was not really well informed about his conditions… When the specialists come to give the results, it's pretty in and out, that type of thing… It's almost like you've got to figure it out yourself… [Need] more information from the specialists. (CF09)

Additionally, some participants expressed a lack of education, guidance and support from the hospital, highlighting gaps in the provision of assistance and guidance. One participant stated,

No. I would have liked a lot more support, and I really didn't receive anything…would like to receive more information from the hospital to manage, like more information about what the conditions are and what's the management like. (CF13)

Similarly, another participant felt left in the dark regarding lifestyle changes necessary for caring:

I don't feel that we got a lot of information from the hospital about caring …I had to do my own research.… I think it was more left up to me, rather than the hospital saying much. It was probably more medication‐related that they told us about what she needed to take rather than lifestyle change. (CF27)

Discussion

The objective of this study was to examine the care experiences of carers of people with chronic conditions and identify areas for improvement. The findings indicate several gaps in care that carers encountered when accompanying their care recipients to hospitals. Carers stressed the importance of staff displaying traits such as compassion, patience, proactiveness, and professionalism, as well as having their role acknowledged and respected by the staff. Carers' involvement in assessment, shared decision‐making and the care process emerged as crucial factors in ensuring a positive experience for carers of patients with chronic conditions and in providing comprehensive care for the patients. Furthermore, they highlighted the necessity of effective communication multidisciplinary collaboration and the provision of information and guidance.

There was limited quantitative data on the carers' perspective on hospital care experiences. Our findings align with some dated findings, which explored carers' views on hospital care experiences, wherein carers generally expressed satisfaction with the overall care received in hospitals (Cowdell 2007; Simpson, Scothern, and Vincent 1995; Whittamore et al. 2014). Similar to patients' hospital experiences (Martin‐Khan et al. 2024), carers' evaluations did not vary based on whether the patient had a chronic condition or not. While some literature reviews (Beardon et al. 2018; Gwernan‐Jones et al. 2020; Luker et al. 2017) have explored carers' hospital experiences with patients having a specific chronic condition (such as dementia or stroke), these reviews were qualitative, lacking quantitative ratings. While these studies did not specifically target carers' perspectives on the care of individuals with diverse chronic conditions, they identified similar themes to our study, particularly regarding the recognition of carers' needs and preferences and the importance of relational aspects of care delivery: a person‐centred approach, overwhelmed with emotions, desiring recognition as stakeholders, carer involvement, and communication and information sharing with carers.

The CCS emphasises the importance of partnering with carers throughout the process of delivering comprehensive care. A thorough assessment is the first element of comprehensive care delivery and is fundamental to ensuring a person‐centred approach. It is crucial to involve carers in the assessment process to help identify patients' needs and preferences. Carers can leverage their knowledge of the patient to ensure that care is tailored to their circumstances and that their needs are addressed (Clissett et al. 2013). Staff traits stood out in effectively engaging carers, with compassionate, patient and supportive attitudes being key to building strong relationships and positively influencing carer satisfaction with care, as found in our study and others (Beardon et al. 2018; Jurgens et al. 2012). When staff demonstrated those good altitudes towards carers, they became reassured that the person that they cared for was in a safe environment (Luker et al. 2017). Carers particularly valued staff members who demonstrated patience, a trait appreciated differently from the patient's perspective (Martin‐Khan et al. 2024).

Carers play a vital role in patient care, especially for patients with chronic conditions, and it is essential for staff to be more attentive and responsive to their needs. Acknowledgement and respect from staff are highly valued by carers, and the provision of emotional, psychological and practical support can alleviate their distress (Luker et al. 2017). Our study, consistent with previous research, identified carers' needs for understanding, psychological support from staff, and assistance with information‐seeking (Beardon et al. 2018). However, despite these identified needs, our findings revealed a gap in care where carers often felt their needs were not met, deliberately overlooked by staff, and their concerns disregarded, with information provided about patient needs often ignored (Gladman et al. 2012; Jamieson et al. 2016). In light of these findings, it is imperative to recognise and address carers' needs and provide them with adequate support and resources to cope with the challenges of caregiving.

Comprehensive care necessitates collaboration among healthcare professionals, patients and carers. Collaboration with carers has been identified as crucial in providing comprehensive care (Edvardsson, Fetherstonhaugh, and Nay 2010). Shared decision‐making occurs through collaboration among care providers, patients, and carers. However, our study found that carers were sometimes excluded from shared decision‐making, albeit less frequently (30%) compared to a larger survey of over 1000 carers of people with dementia in 2009, where 43% reported not being involved in decision‐making to the extent they desired (Alzheimer's Society 2009). Similarly, many other studies revealed that carers often felt excluded from the decision‐making process and disregarded when providing information about care recipients and their needs (Beardon et al. 2018; Gladman et al. 2012; Hamann and Heres 2019). Moreover, staff frequently neglected to solicit carers' input or utilised their expertise in developing care plans, leading carers to feel powerless and distrustful towards the hospital (Jurgens et al. 2012; Simpson 2016). Negative perspectives also arose when discharge planning was executed poorly and without consulting carers (Gladman et al. 2012; Jamieson et al. 2016; Jurgens et al. 2012; Spencer et al. 2013). The lack of involvement of carers resulted in feelings of powerlessness, dissatisfaction and frustration (Beardon et al. 2018). Therefore, it is essential to collaborate with carers in assessment, shared decision‐making and the care process to ensure a positive experience for carers of patients with chronic conditions.

Effective communication and provision of adequate information are the key driver to improve carers' understanding of patient progress, increase their involvement in care, alleviate distress and enhance satisfaction and trust in hospital care (Beardon et al. 2018; Ekstedt et al. 2014; Schaad et al. 2015). However, our study, along with others, revealed instances of poor communication throughout the hospital stay, from admission to discharge (Jurgens et al. 2012; Spencer et al. 2013; Whittamore et al. 2014). Poor communication may lead to dissatisfaction by causing carers to feel out of control and uncertain about the adequacy of care being provided, ultimately impacting their perception of care quality (Clissett et al. 2013; Spencer et al. 2013). Indeed, inadequate communication emerged as the primary cause of dissatisfaction with hospital care and was significantly associated with overall ratings, underscoring its profound influence on carers' perspectives (Whittamore et al. 2014). This finding is further supported by a review of carer perspectives on hospital care, which emphasised the importance of effective communication between staff and with carers, with poor communication and information provision frequently cited as grievances (Beardon et al. 2018). Approximately 30% of carers in our study expressed dissatisfaction with the information provided to them, which aligns with the findings of a survey indicating that 34% of carers were unhappy with how well they were informed (Whittamore et al. 2014). Carers frequently reported lacking the level of information they desired and expressed a need for regular communication and provision of information (Spencer et al. 2013), including details such as changes in the patient's medical condition, disease progression, medication information, treatment options, care plans and guidance for post‐discharge care (Clissett et al. 2013; Jurgens et al. 2012).

Strengths and Limitations of the Work

Existing research tends to concentrate either on patients' hospital experiences or on qualitative examinations of carers' experiences. A significant strength of this study was the use of rigorous mixed methods, which enabled an in‐depth exploration of carers' perspectives and experiences of hospital care. Our recruitment efforts were not confined to several hospitals but encompassed various hospitals and settings (including ED, inpatient and outpatient settings), enhancing the study's generalisability. This review provides insights into carer perspectives on hospital care delivery as a whole, rather than solely focusing on aspects specific to the care of patients with chronic conditions (e.g., rehabilitation). Our findings extend beyond a single chronic disease to encompass various chronic conditions, which constitute approximately 70% of hospital admissions (Martin‐Khan et al. 2024). Furthermore, our findings contribute to the evidence of existing care gaps, three years after the implementation of the CCS in Australian hospitals.

The study was not without some limitations, our recruitment heavily relied on the internet and social media, potentially overlooking perspectives from individuals who are less actively engaged online. Moreover, the study had a small number of male participants, resulting in a weaker representation of male carers' experiences in the results. This gender disparity is not unexpected, as informal carers are predominantly female (Northouse et al. 2010). Also, recruiting male participants for research studies is a common challenge. Additionally, it is important to note that this interview was conducted in English, and consequently, carers from non‐English‐speaking populations were not included in the sample. Future research should try to include non‐English‐speaking populations, as they may have unmet needs that are not adequately represented in this paper due to potential barriers to accessing desired information and support (Ream et al. 2013).

Recommendations for Further Research

There was limited quantitative data on the carers' perspective regarding hospital experiences. Further research, incorporating quantitative evaluation of carers' hospital experiences, is necessary to validate the findings of this study.

Implications for Policy and Practice

To ensure a positive experience for carers of patients with chronic conditions, it is important to develop policies that promote collaboration with carers in assessment, shared decision‐making and the care process. In practice, healthcare providers should recognise and address carers' needs by providing adequate information, guidance, support and resources.

Conclusion

This study offers valuable insights into the experiences, needs and preferences of informal carers of patients with chronic conditions, shedding light on areas for improvement to inform best practice service delivery. It is crucial to recognise that all identified areas are interdependent and must work in synergy rather than in isolation to achieve quality hospital care. We recommend focusing on these areas collectively to enhance the perceived quality of hospital experiences for carers of individuals with chronic conditions.
