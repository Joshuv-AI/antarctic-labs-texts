Comparative outcomes of posterior sub-Tenon triamcinolone for pseudophakic cystoid macular edema in diabetic and non-diabetic patients: a retrospective study
Authors: Kadriye Demir, Erkan Çelik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo compare the anatomical and functional outcomes of posterior sub-Tenon triamcinolone (PST) in the treatment of pseudophakic cystoid macular edema (PCME) between diabetic and non-diabetic patients.MethodsThis retrospective study reviewed the medical records of patients who received PST for PCME following uncomplicated phacoemulsification with intraocular lens implantation between 2018 and 2021, with a minimum follow-up period of six months. A total of 43 eyes were included: 24 from non-diabetic patients (Group 1) and 19 from diabetic patients without diabetic retinopathy (Group 2). The mean HbA1c level in the diabetic group was 7.4 ± 1.1%, indicating moderately controlled diabetes. Best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA, LogMAR), central macular thickness (CMT), and intraocular pressure (IOP) were assessed at baseline and at 1, 3, and 6 months after PST treatment.ResultsBoth groups demonstrated statistically significant improvements in BCVA and reductions in CMT at all follow-up visits (p < 0.05). In Group 1, mean CMT decreased from 412 ± 144 μm to 296 ± 65 μm, and BCVA improved from 0.64 ± 0.22 to 0.14 ± 0.08. Similarly, in Group 2, mean CMT decreased from 426 ± 154 μm to 301 ± 70 μm, and BCVA improved from 0.69 ± 0.26 to 0.15 ± 0.07. No significant intergroup differences were observed in changes in BCVA or CMT (p > 0.05). Intraocular pressure remained stable throughout the follow-up period in both groups.ConclusionsThe findings indicate that the presence of diabetes mellitus without retinopathy does not significantly influence the anatomical or functional response to PST therapy in patients with PCME. PST appears to be an effective and well-tolerated second-line treatment for PCME in both diabetic and non-diabetic patients following uncomplicated cataract surgery.

Introduction

Pseudophakic cystoid macular edema (PCME) remains the most frequent cause of suboptimal visual recovery following otherwise uncomplicated cataract surgery [1, 2]. It is primarily attributed to postoperative inflammation and the subsequent breakdown of the blood–retinal barrier. Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a well-known systemic condition that predisposes patients to increased retinal vascular permeability and chronic low-grade inflammation, both of which may enhance susceptibility to PCME [3]. Even in the absence of overt diabetic retinopathy (DRP), diabetic eyes exhibit higher postoperative cytokine levels and greater macular thickening compared with non-diabetic eyes [4, 5]. Moreover, the degree of glycemic control, often reflected by HbA1c levels, has been correlated with the incidence and persistence of PCME [6]. On the other hand, several studies have emphasized that diabetes itself, in the absence of posterior segment complications and under optimal glycemic control, does not adversely affect the outcomes of uncomplicated cataract surgery [7].

Although acute PCME may resolve spontaneously, some patients develop chronic macular edema (ME) that affects visual outcomes and remains challenging to treat. Various medical options have been investigated for PCME, including topical NSAIDs, posterior sub-Tenon or intravitreal corticosteroids, oral or topical acetazolamide, and intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy. The literature contains numerous reports on the management of PCME in diabetic eyes, both with and without retinopathy, following uneventful cataract surgery [8–10].

Despite advances in intravitreal and topical therapies, posterior sub-tenon triamcinolone acetonide (PST) remains a simple, minimally invasive, and cost-effective option for managing PCME [11, 12]. However, whether diabetes influences the anatomic and functional response to PST has not been sufficiently explored. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the outcomes of PST treatment for PCME in both diabetic and non-diabetic patients.

Materials and methods

This retrospective study included 43 eyes of 43 patients who underwent PST injections for the treatment of PCME. The study population consisted of 24 non-diabetic patients (Group 1) and 19 diabetic patients (Group 2). Information on glycemic control, including HbA1c levels measured within three months prior to PST injection, was available for all diabetic patients. The mean HbA1c was 7.4 ± 1.1% (range, 6.1–9.2%), indicating moderately controlled diabetes in most cases. None of the diabetic patients had any signs of DRP on fundus examination or fluorescein angiography (FA). Patients with glaucoma, uveitis, age-related macular degeneration, retinal vascular disease, ocular trauma, or any history of intraocular surgery other than cataract extraction were excluded. A routine ophthalmological examination was performed 4–6 weeks after cataract surgery and at 1, 3, and 6 months after PST treatment. The examination included best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) using the Snellen chart, intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement with Goldmann applanation tonometry, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT; Cirrus HD-4000, Carl Zeiss Meditec, Dublin, CA, USA), and indirect ophthalmoscopy. Preoperative prophylactic topical NSAID therapy was not used in either diabetic or non-diabetic patients undergoing cataract surgery.

PCME was initially suspected on slit-lamp fundus examination and confirmed by characteristic OCT findings (presence of intraretinal cystic spaces), supported by fluorescein angiography (FFA). FA was performed in all patients to confirm the diagnosis and to exclude other retinal vascular pathologies. All patients had received topical corticosteroids, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), or a combination of these therapies for at least 4–6 weeks before PST injection; PST treatment was initiated in patients who showed an inadequate response to medical therapy, defined as the persistence of cystoid changes on OCT and no improvement or worsening of best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) after at least 4–6 weeks of topical treatment. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Sakarya University (approval number: E-43012747-050.04-475079-282) and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to each invasive procedure, including FA and PST injection.

Posterior sub-Tenon triamcinolone injection

All PST injections were performed in the operating room under strict aseptic conditions. The ocular surface was anesthetized with 0.5% topical proparacaine hydrochloride, followed by preparation with 5% povidone–iodine. A small conjunctival incision was made in the inferonasal quadrant approximately 4.0 mm posterior to the limbus, and the sub-Tenon space was gently accessed using a curved cannula to ensure posterior placement of the drug without scleral contact or resistance. A 27-gauge needle was used to deliver 1 mg/0.1 mL of preservative-free triamcinolone acetonide (Ophthalmos Lab, São Paulo, Brazil) slowly into the posterior sub-Tenon space. No intraoperative or postoperative complications were observed.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Repeated measures comparisons of BCVA, CMT, and IOP values at baseline and at 1, 3, and 6 months after PST injection were conducted using the Friedman test. Post hoc pairwise comparisons between time points were analyzed with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons. A post hoc power analysis indicated that the study had > 80% power to detect clinically meaningful differences in BCVA and CMT at a significance level of α = 0.05. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition, a multivariate linear regression analysis was performed to investigate the predictors of BCVA improvement at 6 months. Independent variables included baseline BCVA, baseline central macular thickness (CMT), and HbA1c level in the diabetic group. The dependent variable was the change in BCVA between baseline and 6-month follow-up.

Results

In Group 1, there were 10 female and 14 male patients with a mean age of 74.2 ± 7.3 years (range: 56–81), while Group 2 included 8 female and 11 male patients with a mean age of 72.3 ± 7.3 years (range: 61–77) (Table 1). The mean follow-up duration after PST treatment was 8.4 ± 3.2 months (range: 6–14 months). PCME was diagnosed at a mean of 5.2 ± 1.6 weeks (range: 4–8 weeks) following cataract surgery, and PST injections were administered at a mean of 2.8 ± 2.1 months (range: 2–5 months) postoperatively. CMT significantly decreased in both groups at 1, 3, and 6 months after PST treatment compared with baseline values (p < 0.001 for all). (Figs. 1 and 2). However, no statistically significant difference in CMT reduction was observed between Group 1 and Group 2 at any follow-up visit (p > 0.05) (Table 2). Similarly, best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) improved significantly in both groups throughout the follow-up period (p < 0.001 within groups) (Fig. 3), with no intergroup difference detected at any time point (p > 0.05). IOP values remained stable over time, and no significant change was observed in either group after PST administration (Table 3). To further evaluate visual outcomes, a multivariate linear regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors of BCVA improvement in diabetic patients. The model included baseline BCVA, baseline CMT, and HbA1c as independent variables. The analysis revealed that baseline BCVA was the only statistically significant predictor of BCVA improvement (p < 0.001), whereas baseline CMT (p = 0.948) and HbA1c level (p = 0.879) were not significant. This suggests that patients with poorer baseline vision tended to experience greater visual gains following PST treatment. No treatment-related complications such as elevated IOP, infection, or scleral thinning were observed during the follow-up period. Furthermore, there was no significant correlation between HbA1c level and either visual or anatomical outcomes in diabetic patients (p > 0.05).

Discussion

Our findings demonstrated that PST injection provided significant anatomical and functional improvement in both diabetic and non-diabetic patients with PCME, with no statistically significant differences between the two groups. Currently, there is no established standard treatment or preventive protocol for PCME. Most patients with clinical PCME tend to experience spontaneous resolution within 3 to 6 months [13]. However, some patients may progress to chronic PCME, which can ultimately affect their vision. Additionally, the literature remains unclear regarding whether active interventions, such as anti-VEGF therapy or steroid injections, provide extra benefits for PCME patients. In studies using topical ketorolac from the NSAID group in treating PCME, a significant increase in BCVA was observed compared to the control group ([14–15]). In another study comparing topical ketorolac and diclofenac in the treatment of acute PCME, both provided a significant increase in BCVA compared to pre-treatment [16]. On the other hand, in the study where ketorolac was used prophylactically for PCME in diabetic patients, it was observed that it had no effect on central retinal thickness (CRT) compared to the control group [17].

There are many studies in the literature showing the effectiveness of PST for PCME treatment [18–20]. In these studies, a significant decrease in CMT and an increase in BCVA were observed after PST treatment, and the authors stated that PST could be used in the treatment of PCME. Yuksel et al. compared PST with topical nepafenac in the treatment of PCME. In this study, CRT was significantly decreased, and BCVA was shown to increase statistically in both groups, but nepafenac was found to be more effective than PST in terms of correlation with visual gain and decrease in CRT [11]. In another study comparing retrobulbar triamcinolone and PST treatments in PCME resistant to topical treatments, visual improvement was found to be similar in both groups [21]. In the study by Kuley et al. comparing 2 mg intravitreal triamcinolone (IVT) versus 40 mg ST treatments, both achieved significant improvement in vision and CMT with no significant difference between interventions at 3- and 6-month follow-up [22] Similarly, our findings demonstrated that PST treatment led to statistically significant anatomical and functional outcomes in patients with PCME, regardless of diabetic status. Notably, PST was administered in our cohort only to patients who failed to respond adequately to initial topical therapy, highlighting its efficacy as a second-line treatment for refractory cases. Although some patients did not achieve complete anatomical resolution at three months, no repeat PST injections were recorded in the medical files during the study period. This likely reflects partial functional recovery in some cases, patient preference, or the real-life nature of clinical decision-making in a retrospective setting.

On the other hand, it should be acknowledged that PCME may resolve spontaneously in a proportion of cases, and the absence of an untreated control group limits the ability to fully distinguish treatment-related effects from the natural course of the disease. Nonetheless, the consistent and statistically significant improvements in both BCVA and CMT across all patients suggest a treatment-related effect.

Diabetic patients are at a higher risk of developing ME following uneventful cataract surgery, and this risk increases with the severity of DRP [23]. In a large U.S. cohort including 139,759 eyes, Schimer et al. (2007) reported that diabetic patients had a 1.78-fold higher risk of developing PCME compared with non-diabetic individuals [24]. Similarly, Patra et al. (2017) analyzed 262 eyes in the United Kingdom and found that patients with diabetes had a 3.06-fold higher incidence of PCME after phacoemulsification than those without diabetes [25]. Ylinen et al. further demonstrated that younger age and poor glycemic control were significant risk factors for PCME development [26]. These findings support the concept that PCME is predominantly driven by inflammatory processes leading to disruption of the blood–retinal barrier. This inflammatory basis also forms the rationale for combining corticosteroids with anti-VEGF agents in diabetic macular conditions. In a study by Karataş et al., the combination of PST and intravitreal ranibizumab resulted in significantly greater anatomical improvement than anti-VEGF monotherapy, especially in patients with serous retinal detachment [27].

Diabetic patients typically exhibit more pronounced retinal vascular alterations, even in the absence of clinical DRP. On the other hand, Danni et al. emphasized that diabetes without posterior segment complications and under good metabolic control does not adversely affect the visual outcomes of uncomplicated cataract surgery [7]. This contrast highlights the complex interplay between systemic metabolic status and ocular inflammation.

A recent meta-analysis evaluating prophylactic strategies for preventing ME after cataract surgery in diabetic patients found that the combination of topical corticosteroids and NSAIDs was effective in preventing 75.8% of PCME events compared with corticosteroids alone in patients without preoperative DME [28]. Moreover, the addition of depot corticosteroids to topical therapy, with or without NSAIDs, further enhanced efficacy but was associated with a higher incidence of intraocular pressure elevation. In contrast, the combination of anti-VEGF agents with topical steroids ± NSAIDs did not significantly influence the incidence of PCME in diabetic eyes. These results underline the multifactorial nature of PCME in diabetic patients, suggesting that both systemic and local inflammatory mechanisms contribute to disease susceptibility and treatment response.

In our study, none of the diabetic patients exhibited any signs of DRP, and the mean HbA1c level was 7.4 ± 1.1%, indicating moderately controlled diabetes. Despite the presence of diabetes, no statistically significant differences were found between diabetic and non-diabetic patients in terms of anatomical or functional response to PST treatment. These findings suggest that diabetes alone, in the absence of retinopathy, does not adversely affect the efficacy of PST in the management of PCME.

Increased vascular permeability and inflammatory cytokine activity are known to contribute to the development of PCME in diabetic eyes [4, 29]. Dong et al. demonstrated that elevated aqueous humor levels of IL-6 and VEGF after uncomplicated phacoemulsification were associated with postoperative macular thickening in diabetic patients [4]. Similarly, Protasio and Aguilar reported that good glycemic control limited postoperative increases in macular thickness after cataract surgery in diabetic patients [29]. Several studies have reported the effectiveness of PST and IVT in the management of DME [30–32]. However, to our knowledge, no previous study has specifically evaluated the efficacy of PST in the treatment of PCME among diabetic patients. A recent study investigating the effects of IVD implants in diabetic and non-diabetic patients without retinopathy demonstrated significant anatomical and functional improvements in both groups, with no significant difference between them—findings that align closely with our results [33]. Multiple reports have also confirmed the efficacy of IVD in PCME refractory to topical treatment [34–36]; however, none of these studies analyzed diabetic patients as a distinct subgroup.

Taken together, these data support the hypothesis that when DRP is absent and systemic metabolism is reasonably controlled, the anatomical and functional response to corticosteroid-based therapies—including PST—does not differ substantially between diabetic and non-diabetic individuals.

Limitations and conclusion

While this study offers valuable insight into the efficacy of PST in managing PCME, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The retrospective design and relatively small sample size, particularly the limited number of diabetic patients, may affect the generalizability of our findings and the strength of conclusions regarding the impact of diabetes.

Nevertheless, this study provides important evidence supporting the use of PST as a second-line therapy for PCME unresponsive to topical treatment. Our findings show that diabetic patients without retinopathy and with moderately controlled metabolic status achieved anatomical and functional outcomes comparable to non-diabetic patients. These results suggest that in the absence of posterior segment complications, diabetes alone may not negatively influence treatment response to PST. Therefore, PST emerges as a safe, cost-effective, and technically straightforward option for treating pseudophakic cystoid macular edema in both diabetic and non-diabetic patients.
