Development and validation of a nomogram using transaminase levels, cycle cost, and hair loss as predictive factors for the response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in esophageal cancer
Authors: Shu-Cong Peng, Jing-Xiao Li, Kun-Lin He, Hua-Fu Zhou, Shang-Wei Chen, Jun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to identify clinical parameters influencing treatment efficacy during neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) for esophageal cancer (EC) patients, with a particular focus on demographic and clinical variables, to establish predictive biomarkers for chemotherapeutic response.MethodsA total of 194 EC patients undergoing NAC were retrospectively included. Significant predictors of pathological response were identified through univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, followed by the construction of a predictive nomogram. The model’s discriminative performance was validated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, while calibration accuracy and clinical utility were assessed using bootstrap-corrected calibration curves (1,000 resamples) and decision curve analysis (DCA), respectively. External validation was performed on an independent cohort of 74 EC patients.ResultsBoth univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses identified abnormal transaminase levels, average cycle cost, and hair loss as significant risk factors affecting the effectiveness of NAC (p < 0.05). The constructed nomogram model demonstrated strong predictive performance, with a concordance index (C-index) and area under the curve (AUC) of 0.735. Calibration curves showed a high degree of fit between predicted and observed outcomes, while DCA confirmed the clinical utility of these predictive factors. External validation yielded an AUC of 0.865, further supporting the model’s predictive accuracy.ConclusionBy incorporating the indicators of abnormal transaminase levels, average chemotherapy cycle cost, and the occurrence of hair loss, the developed nomogram effectively identifies EC patients who are more likely to benefit from NAC.Keywords: Esophageal cancer, Neoadjuvant chemotherapy, Prediction model, Nomogram

Introduction

Esophageal cancer (EC) is among the ten most common cancers worldwide. According to global cancer data from 2020, its incidence and mortality rates rank seventh and sixth, respectively [1, 2]. Surgery remains the primary treatment for EC [1]. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) is commonly used for patients with stage Ⅱ, Ⅲ, and Ⅳ. Preoperative NAC can reduce tumor size, eliminate micrometastatic disease, and lower the risk of postoperative recurrence and metastasis [3, 4]. In recent years, multiple studies have demonstrated that NAC improves survival outcomes in EC patients [3, 5]. For patients who do not achieve clinical remission, complications such as tumor metastasis, worsening dysphagia, infection, and leukopenia can make palliative surgery more challenging [6, 7]. Several studies have proposed potential predictive factors for NAC efficacy in EC, including tumor-related molecular markers, radiomics models, and clinical parameters [8–11]. However, these studies have focused on prognosis prediction either before or during treatment, with no research exploring whether changes in clinical parameters throughout NAC could predict treatment efficacy.

This study analyzed clinical parameters from 194 EC patients at the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University and 74 patients at the People’s Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region before and during NAC to identify potential risk factors influencing the effectiveness of NAC in EC.

Methods

Clinical data

In this study, we utilized neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) with paclitaxel and carboplatin based on institutional practice patterns in Guangxi, China, where NAC is commonly employed for esophageal cancer due to factors such as resource availability, patient comorbidities, and historical treatment preferences.

A total of 845 EC patients who visited the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University between January 2020 and December 2023 were screened based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) Patients who aged ≥ 18 years; (2) Patients who underwent NAC before surgery; (3) Patients who have completed at least two cycles of NAC, with efficacy evaluated by imaging and a 21-day interval between cycles; (4) Patients diagnosed with EC by pathology or imaging and staged according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition staging criteria [12] as clinical stage cⅡ (T3N0M0), cⅢ (T3N1-2N0M0), or cⅣa (T4aN0-2M0); (5) Patients with no prior or concurrent antitumor treatments, such as radiotherapy, anti-angiogenesis therapy, or immunotherapy; (6) All patients received a uniform NAC regimen consisting of paclitaxel and carboplatin to minimize heterogeneity, patients who received a treatment regimen consisting of paclitaxel (175 mg/m²)and carboplatin (400 mg/m²) administered via intravenous infusion on Day 1, with the second cycle starting on Day 22 after a 3-week interval. Patients were excluded if they: (1) Patients who had undergone EC surgery prior to chemotherapy; or (2) Patients who received fewer than two cycles of NAC for any reason. Ultimately, 194 patients were included in this cohort.

Additionally, 162 EC patients who visited the People’s Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region between January 2020 and December 2023 were screened using the same inclusion criteria: (1) Patients who age ≥ 18 years; (2) Patients who underwent NAC before surgery; (3) Patients who have completed at least two cycles of NAC, with efficacy assessed by imaging and a 21-day interval between cycles; (4) Patients diagnosed with EC by pathology or imaging and staged according to the AJCC 8th edition staging criteria [12] as clinical stage cⅡ (T3N0M0), cⅢ (T3N1-2N0M0), or cⅣa (T4aN0-2M0); (5) Patients with no prior or concurrent antitumor treatments, such as radiotherapy, anti-angiogenesis therapy, or immunotherapy. Exclusion criteria were the same as for the first cohort: (1) Patients with prior EC surgery before chemotherapy; or (2) Patients with fewer than two cycles of NAC for any reason. A total of 74 patients were ultimately included in this cohort. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee (Approval number: 2024-E799-01).

Criteria for efficacy evaluation

All patients underwent surgical resection after NAC. Treatment response was evaluated using Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) based on CT imaging, as pathological response data were not uniformly available in this retrospective cohort. According to the RECIST version 1.1 (World Health Organization, 2009 [13]), treatment efficacy was assessed based on the degree of tumor shrinkage observed on computed tomography (CT) scans: (1) Progressive Disease (PD): An increase of more than 25% in the sum of the longest diameters of baseline lesions, the appearance of new lesions, or the development of new pleural or ascitic effusions confirmed by cytology as malignant; (2) Stable Disease (SD): A reduction in the sum of the longest diameters of baseline lesions that did not exceed 50% or an increase that did not exceed 25%; (3) Partial Remission (PR): A decrease of more than 50% in the sum of the longest diameters of baseline lesions; (4) Complete Remission (CR): Disappearance of all target lesions, sustained for at least 4 weeks. Patients who received NAC were categorized based on their treatment response: those achieving CR or PR were classified into the effective group, while those with SD or PD were classified into the ineffective group.

Plan of NAC

NAC for EC patients was administered using the following regimen: paclitaxel (175 mg/m²) and carboplatin (400 mg/m²) via intravenous infusion on Day 1, with each cycle lasting 21 days. After every two cycles, patients underwent follow-up evaluations at the treatment center, including contrast-enhanced CT scans of the chest and abdomen, as well as routine blood tests. These assessments were performed to evaluate the short-term efficacy of NAC.

Collection of data

A retrospective analysis was conducted using inpatient medical records and test results of patients who received NAC. Collected data included gender, age, height, weight, insurance type, occupation, medical history, personal history, healthcare delay (time from symptom onset to treatment initiation), tumor location, pathology results, number of hospitalizations, chemotherapy cycles, blood test results, liver and kidney function, CT reports, total treatment duration, total treatment costs, and post-chemotherapy adverse reactions.

Statistical analysis

Comparison of clinical indicators and Logistic regression analysis

Data preprocessing was performed before analysis. Body mass index (BMI) and body surface area (BSA) were calculated based on patients’ height and weight. The average cycle cost was determined by dividing the total treatment cost by the number of chemotherapy cycles. Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS 23.0. For quantitative data, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was initially performed. If variances were homogeneous, an independent t-test was applied; if heterogeneous, Welch’s t-test (t’) was used, with results expressed as mean ± standard deviation. For qualitative data, the chi-square (χ²) test was performed. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with the efficacy of NAC in EC. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was generated, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the predictive value of transaminase abnormalities, average cycle cost, and hair loss on treatment efficacy. Correlation analysis was performed using the Spearman rank correlation coefficient. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Construction and verification of nomogram

The factors associated with the efficacy of NAC for EC, identified through logistic regression analysis, were incorporated into a nomogram to develop a risk prediction model. Continuous variables were scaled using Z-score normalization before analysis. The nomogram scoring system was derived by translating logistic regression coefficients into points on a 0-100 scale, with cost inverted to align with clinical interpretation. Overfitting was assessed via bootstrap validation (1,000 resamples) and by comparing training vs. validation AUCs. In this model, each risk factor was assigned a specific weight, contributing to the overall evaluation score. The total score corresponded to a percentage, representing the estimated risk of mortality within one year after discharge. To validate the predictive performance of the model, the concordance index (C-index) was calculated during logistic regression analysis. Additionally, a ROC curve was plotted, with the AUC closer to 1 indicating higher accuracy. Moreover, a calibration curve was constructed to assess the agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, ensuring the model’s goodness of fit. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of each risk factor. For external validation, data from EC patients who underwent NAC at the People’s Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region from January 2020 to December 2023 were collected. The C-index and ROC curve were calculated to assess the generalizability and reliability of the risk prediction model.

Results

In this study, a total of 194 EC patients who received NAC were included. Among them, 56 patients (28.9%) experienced effective treatment, while 138 patients (71.1%) had ineffective treatment. Subsequently, qualitative data analysis was performed. No statistically significant differences were found in treatment backgrounds between the two groups. However, regarding the adverse effects of NAC, nausea, transaminase abnormalities, and hair loss showed statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) between the groups, as shown in Tables 2 and 3.

Based on treatment efficacy as the dependent variable, factors showing statistical differences were included as independent variables in the logistic regression analysis model, including average cycle cost, lowest white blood cell count, lowest platelet count, nausea, transaminase abnormalities, and hair loss. Ultimately, transaminase abnormalities, average cycle cost, and hair loss were identified as factors associated with the efficacy of NAC for EC (Table 4). Spearman correlation analysis was performed to assess the relationship between transaminase abnormalities, average cycle cost, hair loss, and the efficacy of NAC for EC. The results indicated that transaminase abnormalities (r = -0.303, P < 0.001), average cycle cost (r = 0.155, P = 0.031), and hair loss (r = -0.279, P < 0.001) were all significantly correlated with the treatment efficacy. In the multivariate analysis, we acknowledged potential confounding from socioeconomic factors but focused on average cycle cost as a pragmatic indicator. The inverse association between cost and response may reflect optimized resource use or patient adherence in low-cost settings, rather than direct efficacy; thus, we interpret it as a surrogate marker requiring further validation.

Subsequently, these variables were incorporated into a nomogram to construct a risk prediction model. Each risk factor was assigned a score: for the transaminase level, “abnormal” was scored as 1 and “normal” was scored as 0; for average cycle cost, a value of 100 was scored as 0, and a value of 0 was scored as 100; hair loss during hospitalization was scored as 1, and otherwise, it was scored as 0 (Figure A). The model showed moderate discriminative ability (AUC 0.735), indicating room for improvement with additional predictors (Figure B). To further evaluate the model, a calibration curve was plotted, showing that the predicted values closely aligned with the actual observed values (Figure C). Additionally, the DCA curve was used to assess the clinical utility of the model’s indicators. As shown in Figure D, the curves corresponding to all the included indicators in the model lie above the extreme curves (gray and black), indicating good clinical utility of these factors (Fig. 1).

Discussion

NAC is a widely used treatment option for patients with stage Ⅱ, Ⅲ, and Ⅳ EC [14]. In clinical practice, treatment outcomes can vary significantly among patients undergoing this therapy. Approximately 29.2% to 43.2% of patients achieve remission; however, those who are resistant to chemotherapy may not experience tumor shrinkage or improvements in prognosis. In fact, chemotherapy resistance can also delay the optimal timing for palliative surgery [14, 15]. Therefore, it is crucial to assess treatment responses early in the process, predict the patient’s prognosis, and guide subsequent treatment strategies.

This study followed 194 EC patients undergoing NAC and identified statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) between the effective and ineffective treatment groups in several factors, including average cycle cost, lowest white blood cell count, lowest platelet count, nausea, liver enzyme abnormalities, and hair loss. These findings suggest that these factors during treatment may influence the efficacy of NAC in EC. Logistic regression analysis further revealed that liver enzyme abnormalities, average cycle cost, and hair loss were key predictors of treatment outcomes. Specifically, liver enzyme abnormalities and hair loss were positively correlated with better outcomes, while average cycle cost was negatively correlated with efficacy. Additionally, these indicators showed strong predictive value in a separate cohort of 74 patients from another center, suggesting their potential clinical significance.

There has been considerable research into the relationship between complications from NAC and patient prognosis in EC. A multicenter study indicated that EC patients who developed pneumonia or infections after undergoing NAC and surgical resection experienced significantly reduced survival times [16]. A clinical trial found that significant weight loss during NAC was a major risk factor for developing treatment-related complications in EC patients [17]. Furthermore, Mungo et al. found that patients who experienced anemia, leukopenia, and weight loss after NAC were more likely to have an increased 30-day mortality rate [18]. Despite these findings, there has been currently no literature exploring the link between NAC-related complications and treatment efficacy in EC, indicating a gap in understanding that warrants further investigation.

Our results suggest that EC patients with liver enzyme abnormalities and hair loss during NAC are more likely to achieve favorable treatment outcomes. However, nearly all patients experience chemotherapy-related side effects, which can contribute to increased complications and mortality rates [19]. It is worth noting that hair loss and liver injury are common adverse reactions of NAC, but not all of these adverse reactions have a negative impact on the efficacy of NAC [20]. Some studies on other tumors have shown that elevated transaminase levels and liver function damage after NAC treatment may indicate sufficient exposure of somatic drugs and liver metabolism of sufficient concentrations of active drugs, which may improve the efficacy of NAC [21, 22]. Hair loss is one of the common adverse reactions of NAC, which may be related to the accumulation of drug toxicity. It also suggests that NAC drugs are indiscriminately attacking somatic cells, which may be indicated that NAC is taking effect [23].The severity of complications during NAC is closely related to patients’ willingness to continue treatment [24]. Compared to those with milder complications (such as mild liver or kidney dysfunction, hair loss, or nausea), patients who develop severe complications (such as worsening dysphagia, distant metastasis, or severe infections) tend to have lower treatment adherence [25]. This phenomenon is not only related with the patient’s economic background but also their cognitive and psychological factors [26, 27]. Therefore, we should closely monitor complications of the patients during NAC and actively address both the medical and psychological aspects of treatment [28].

An earlier study analyzed the relationship between socioeconomic status and treatment choices in EC patients, and found that those with better socioeconomic conditions were more likely to accept chemotherapy [29]. Another study found that patients with commercial or private insurance were more willing to undergo NAC compared to those on Medicaid or without insurance [30]. These findings suggest that a patient’s financial situation may impact their treatment choices and outcomes. Based on this fact, we calculated the average cost per chemotherapy cycle for each patient. The results indicated that lower average cycle costs were associated with better outcomes in NAC. Gao et al. argued that for patients with advanced EC, the efficacy of NAC was positively correlated with treatment costs [31], which was inconsistent with our findings. Gao’s study estimated that patients might need to spend over $50,000 annually on treatment to achieve optimal results [31]. Our analysis of the average cycle cost would like to recommend the clinicians to thoroughly explain the cost differences and expected efficacy between treatment options before developing a treatment plan, rather than to determine an exact cost-efficiency ratio. This would enable patients to make informed decisions based on both their medical condition and financial situation, thereby maximizing both treatment effectiveness and economic benefit.

It is imperative to acknowledge the standard of care for locally advanced esophageal cancer as per NCCN and ESMO guidelines is neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (nCRT) followed by surgery, or definitive chemoradiation for non-surgical candidates [32, 33]. This recommendation is strongly supported by level I evidence, including the landmark CROSS trial and meta-analyses, which demonstrate superior survival outcomes with nCRT compared to surgery alone or NAC [34, 35]. Our study focuses specifically on patients undergoing NAC, a regimen that, while commonly used in some regions like East Asia, is not the international standard.The findings of our study do not advocate for NAC over nCRT, but rather seek to optimize patient selection and outcomes within a cohort that received NAC. The goal is to identify which patients might derive significant benefit from NAC, potentially avoiding its toxicity if unlikely to respond, and to facilitate early transition to more intensive or alternative therapies. Future research should directly compare the predictive value of our model in cohorts receiving NAC versus nCRT. Furthermore, the economic implications of our findings warrant careful consideration against the potentially higher efficacy but also higher cost and toxicity profile of nCRT [36]. Ultimately, the choice of neoadjuvant strategy should be a shared decision-making process between the clinician and the patient, considering tumor characteristics, comorbidities, patient preferences, and available institutional expertise, while always prioritizing guideline-concordant care where appropriate.

In addition, our results suggest that during NAC for EC, the occurrence of mild complications alone could help reduce hospitalization costs. Milder complications and lower treatment costs were found to be key factors that promote patients’ adherence to completing the full chemotherapy cycle and achieving good treatment outcomes. Therefore, effective management of complications and treatment costs might be crucial for obtaining favorable results [37, 38]. However, there were several limitations in this study. Firstly, despite including data from two hospitals, the study was constrained by a small time span and a limited number of patients. Secondly, Our model did not adjust for all potential confounders (such as baseline tumor volume, nutritional status, economic development level, economic fluctuations, and medical policies), which may bias results. We recommend multivariate models in future work to isolate predictor effects. An effective solution is to add macroeconomic indicators, regional economic development level data, medical resource distribution, and other factors to the average cycle cost to improve its universality. The counterintuitive cost-response relationship should be tempered by limitations in cost variability across regions. We recommend future studies incorporate macroeconomic indicators to improve generalizability. It is worth mentioning that due to the presence of multiple confounding factors in the two center samples included in this study, larger sample sizes and more scientific research methods are still needed in the future to fully demonstrate the practicality of the conclusions. Compared to established models, our nomogram offers a low-cost, clinically accessible tool, it may complement advanced techniques. Finally, as a retrospective study, our analysis was limited by the selection of included indicators. Future research that analyzes additional factors related to the efficacy of NAC in EC will be essential.

In conclusion, abnormal transaminase levels, lower average cycle costs, and hair loss were demonstrated to be associated with better outcomes in NAC for EC. Evaluating these factors before and during treatment may aid in developing rational treatment strategies and improving patient outcomes.
