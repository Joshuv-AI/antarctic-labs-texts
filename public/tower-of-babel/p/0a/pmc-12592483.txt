KLF5 aggravates osteoarthritis progression by inhibiting chondrocyte autophagy via transcriptional activation of PLK2
Authors: Bin Wang, Hongliang Zhang, Zhiyong Fan, Haoye Shi, Feiyang Huang, Hua Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aims to investigate the regulatory role of the Krüppel-like factor 5 (KLF5)/Polo-like kinase 2 (PLK2) axis in osteoarthritis (OA) and its effects on chondrocyte function. An OA mouse model was established. Cartilage damage was assessed by hematoxylin-eosin, Safranin O–Fast Green, and Alcian blue stainings. Serum levels of inflammatory cytokines were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. The expression of PLK2, Bax, Bcl-2, and Cleaved Caspase-3 was evaluated by RT-qPCR and Western blotting. Immunohistochemistry was used to detect the expression of PLK2, Collagen II, MMP13, Beclin-1, and LC3. In OA chondrocytes, following knockdown of PLK2 using sh-PLK2 or KLF5 overexpression, cell viability, apoptosis, extracellular matrix proteins were evaluated. The interaction between KLF5 and the PLK2 promoter was analyzed using ChIP-qPCR and dual-luciferase reporter assays. PLK2 was significantly upregulated in the OA mouse model and was closely associated with cartilage damage and inflammatory responses. Silencing PLK2 or KLF5 promoted chondrocyte autophagy, improved extracellular matrix metabolism, enhanced cell viability, and suppressed apoptosis and inflammatory injury. KLF5 directly bound to the PLK2 promoter to transcriptionally activate its expression, thereby upregulating PLK2, inhibiting autophagy, disrupting cartilage matrix homeostasis, and exacerbating cartilage damage and inflammation in OA mice. KLF5 promotes OA progression by transcriptionally activating PLK2, thereby suppressing chondrocyte autophagy and contributing to cartilage degeneration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22865-8.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a prevalent degenerative joint disorder characterized by progressive cartilage degradation, synovial inflammation, and joint dysfunction1. It represents a major cause of disability in the aging population and imposes a substantial socioeconomic burden worldwide2. The pathogenesis of OA is multifactorial, involving mechanical, inflammatory, and metabolic factors that collectively disrupt joint homeostasis3. Among the various pathological alterations observed in OA, the functional decline and apoptosis of articular chondrocytes play a pivotal role in disease initiation and progression4. Chondrocytes are responsible for maintaining the balance between synthesis and degradation of extracellular matrix (ECM) components such as type II collagen and aggrecan5. Under pathological conditions, OA chondrocytes exhibit increased production of catabolic enzymes, reduced matrix synthesis, enhanced apoptosis, and impaired stress responses, contributing to cartilage erosion6. One critical cellular process implicated in OA pathogenesis is autophagy, a conserved lysosome-dependent mechanism that maintains cellular homeostasis by degrading damaged proteins and organelles7. In normal cartilage, autophagy supports chondrocyte survival under stress. However, in OA, autophagic activity is markedly suppressed, leading to increased cell death, matrix degradation, and inflammation8. Therapeutically restoring autophagy has shown protective effects in experimental OA models.

Polo-like kinase 2 (PLK2), a serine/threonine kinase involved in cell cycle regulation, stress response, and mitochondrial homeostasis, has recently emerged as a potential modulator of autophagy and inflammation9. Preliminary evidence suggests that PLK2 expression is elevated in OA and may influence chondrocyte viability and matrix metabolism10. Krüppel-like factor 5 (KLF5) is a zinc finger transcription factor known to regulate cell proliferation, differentiation, and inflammatory responses11. Recent findings indicate that KLF5 may act as an upstream regulator of key signaling molecules12, yet its involvement in OA, particularly in relation to chondrocyte autophagy, is largely unexplored.

Based on these insights, we hypothesize that KLF5 modulates OA by mediating PLK2 transcription, thereby regulating autophagy in chondrocytes and affecting cartilage degradation. This study aims to uncover the molecular mechanism by which KLF5 regulates PLK2 and its downstream effects on autophagy and chondrocyte function, thereby contributing to OA progression. Clarifying the KLF5/PLK2 axis in autophagy will not only advance our understanding of OA pathophysiology but may also identify novel therapeutic targets for disease modification.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

The datasets GSE243421 and GSE264185 were downloaded from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with OA were identified using the criteria of adjusted P.adj < 0.05 and |log Fold Change (logFC)| > 1. Autophagy-related genes were obtained from the Human Autophagy Database (HADb) (http://www.autophagy.lu/). The intersection of the DEGs and autophagy-related genes was used to identify OA-related core targets. Potential upstream regulators of the identified core targets were predicted using the hTFtarget database (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/hTFtarget//#!/quick_search?kw=HLA-A). These predicted regulators were then cross-referenced with DEGs from the GSE243421 and GSE264185 datasets to identify OA-related upstream regulatory factors.

Construction of an OA animal model

Male ICR mice (8 weeks old, n = 32) were purchased from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. and used in the animal experiments. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University and complied with the Replacement, Reduction, Refinement (3R) principles.

The destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) surgical method was used to induce the OA model13,14. Mice were randomly assigned to four groups: sham, model, sh-NC, and sh-KLF5. Lentiviral vectors for sh-KLF5 and sh-NC were provided by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). In the sham group, only joint capsule incision and exposure were performed without ligament damage. In the other groups, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation (3% for induction and 1.5-2% for maintenance), the medial joint capsule was incised to expose the medial meniscotibial ligament (MMTL). The ligament was transected using microsurgical scissors and detached from the tibial plateau to destabilize the meniscus and establish the OA model. The surgical site was then sutured and disinfected. Two days following the surgery, 10 µL of the corresponding lentivirus was injected intra-articularly. After 8 weeks, mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide inhalation followed by cervical dislocation to ensure complete death, and knee joint tissues and serum samples were collected for further analysis.

Construction of an OA cell model

ATDC5 cells (CL-0856, Procell, Wuhan, China) were cultured in a medium containing 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. The experimental groups were as follows: control, interleukin-1beta (IL-1β), IL-1β + sh-NC, IL-1β + sh-PLK2, IL-1β + sh-KLF5, IL-1β + oe-NC, IL-1β + oe-KLF5, sh-KLF5 + oe-NC and sh-KLF5 + oe-PLK2. All lentiviruses were provided by GenePharma. Except for the control group, all cells were treated with 10 ng/mL IL-1β for 24 h. Prior to IL-1β exposure, cells in the sh-PLK2, sh-KLF5, and overexpression groups were transduced with the respective lentiviruses to achieve knockdown or overexpression of PLK2 or KLF5. After treatment, the culture supernatants were collected for further analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR for mRNA expression

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (15596026CN, ThermoFisher, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA; acceptable A260/A280 ratio 1.8–2.0). RNA integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Reverse transcription into cDNA was performed using the Hifair AdvanceFast 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR kit (11156ES10, Yesen, Shanghai, China; 1 µg total RNA per 20 µL reaction) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was conducted on a LightCycler 480 II real-time PCR system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) using the Hieff UNICON Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (11184ES08, Yesen, Shanghai, China) in a 20 µL reaction volume (10 µL SYBR Green Master Mix, 0.4 µL each primer [10 µM], 2 µL cDNA, and nuclease-free water to volume). The cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. A melt-curve analysis (65–95 °C) was performed to verify the specificity of amplification. No-template controls (NTC) were included in each run to monitor contamination, and all reactions were performed in triplicate (technical replicates). Each experiment included at least three independent biological replicates. GAPDH was used as the reference (housekeeping) gene, and relative gene expression was calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences for all target genes are listed in Table 1.

Western blot (WB)

Total protein was extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation assay lysis buffer (R0278, Sigma, Shanghai, China), and protein concentration was determined via the bicinchoninic acid assay (A55861, ThermoFisher, Shanghai, China). Equal amounts of protein samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes, which were blocked with 5% non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 h, followed by incubation with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: PLK2 (1:500, A7066, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), Collagen II (1:1000, ab34712, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), matrix metallopeptidase 13 (MMP13; 1:500, 18165-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), A disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs 4 (ADAMTS-4; 1:500, A2525, ABclonal Wuhan, China), LC3 (1:1000, A12319, ABclonal Wuhan, China), p62 (1:20000, A19700, ABclonal Wuhan, China), autophagy related 5 (ATG5; 1:500, A11427, ABclonal Wuhan, China), Bax (1:1000, A25812, ABclonal Wuhan, China), Cleaved caspase-3 (1:500, ab32042, Abcam. Cambridge, UK), Bcl-2 (1:500, A12009, ABclonal Wuhan, China), ULK1 (1:500, A8529, ABclonal Wuhan, China), p-ULK1 (1:500, AP1196, ABclonal Wuhan, China), Beclin-1 (1:500, A7353, ABclonal Wuhan, China), p-Beclin-1 (1:100, AP1254, ABclonal Wuhan, China) and GAPDH (1:2000, AC027, ABclonal Wuhan, China). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:2000; ab205718, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (MA0186, Meilunbio, Liaoning, China), and densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ software (V1.8.0.112, NIH, Madison, WI, USA), with GAPDH as the loading control to normalize the relative expression levels of target proteins.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) staining

Mouse knee joint tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (441244, Sigma) at 4 °C for 24 h and then decalcified in 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA, pH 7.4; E1171, Sigma) at 4 °C for 2–3 weeks, with the solution changed every 2–3 days, until the bones became pliable. After decalcification, samples were washed with running water for 30 min, dehydrated through graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin.

For all histological staining (HE, Safranin O–Fast Green, and Alcian Blue), paraffin sections were first baked at 60 °C for 2 h, then deparaffinized in xylene (534056, Sigma), rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and rinsed in distilled water.

Sections were stained with HE (G1120, Solarbio, Shanghai, China) for 3 min and 1 min, respectively. After further dehydration with graded ethanol and clearing in xylene, sections were mounted and evaluated under a light microscope (XDS-30, BINGYU, Shanghai, China) for morphological analysis.

Safranin O–Fast green staining

After the common deparaffinization and rehydration step described above. Sections were washed thoroughly with tap water, stained with hematoxylin for 3 min, followed by bluing, then sequentially stained with 0.02% Fast green for 5 min, rinsed with water, differentiated with 1% acetic acid for 10 s, and stained with 0.1% Safranin O solution (G1371, Solarbio) for 5 min. Dehydration was performed using absolute ethanol, cleared in xylene, and mounted. Cartilage matrix and cellular staining were assessed under a light microscope.

Alcian blue staining

Paraffin sections were incubated with 1% Alcian Blue staining solution (pH 2.5) for 1 h in a humid chamber, followed by three rinses with distilled water. Then, 0.1% Nuclear Fast Red was applied for 10 min, followed by a 5-minute rinse with tap water (G1560, Solarbio). Sections were dehydrated rapidly through 70%, 80%, 90%, and 100% ethanol (10 s each), cleared with xylene for 1 min, and mounted using neutral resin. Stained cartilage sections were observed under a light microscope. Cartilage degeneration was evaluated using the Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) grading system and following the previously established scoring criteria15.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Levels of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) in serum or cell culture supernatants were measured using mouse ELISA kits for TNF-α (88-7324-88, ThermoFisher, Shanghai, China) and IL-6 (88-7064-88, ThermoFisher). Samples or standards (100 µL) were added to pre-coated ELISA plates and incubated at room temperature for 2 h. After washing, detection antibodies and Streptavidin-HRP were sequentially added and incubated at room temperature for 1 h and 30 min, respectively. Optical density (OD) values were measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cell viability of ATDC5 cells was assessed using the CCK-8 assay (C0038, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Cells were seeded into 96-well plates, and 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by 2-hour incubation. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (168–1130, Bio-Rad, USA).

Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP Nick end labeling (TUNEL)

ATDC5 cells were treated with DNase-free proteinase K and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. After thorough washing with PBS to remove proteinase K, TUNEL staining was performed following the manufacturer’s instructions (C1089, Beyotime). Slides were mounted and examined under a fluorescence microscope. Red fluorescence signals were recorded, and positive cells were quantified using ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence staining was performed using a commercial kit (P0176, Beyotime). Cells were seeded onto coverslips and cultured until 80% confluence. Cells were fixed with 1 mL fixative at room temperature for 10 min and washed with TBSTx. Blocking was carried out using TBSTx containing 5% BSA for 60 min on a shaker. After removing the blocking solution, cells were incubated with diluted primary antibodies: PLK2 (1:500, A7066, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), Beclin-1 (1:500, A7353, ABclonal Wuhan, China), or KLF5 (1:200, A2989, Wuhan, China), all from ABclonal, for 60 min at room temperature. After washing, fluorescent secondary antibodies were added and incubated in the dark for 60 min. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Coverslips were mounted with antifade mounting medium, and green fluorescence was visualized under a fluorescence microscope. ImageJ software was used to quantify fluorescence intensity of positive cells.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (ChIP-qPCR)

ChIP-qPCR was performed using a commercial kit (P2078, Beyotime). Cell samples were diluted with ChIP dilution buffer containing 1 mM PMSF and pre-cleared with Protein A + G Agarose/Salmon Sperm DNA. Target antibodies were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The next day, Protein A + G Agarose/Salmon Sperm DNA was added and incubated for 1 h. After four washes to remove nonspecific binding, DNA was eluted and de-crosslinked at 65 °C for 4 h. After proteinase K treatment, DNA was extracted using phenol/chloroform and precipitated with ethanol. The interaction between KLF5 and the PLK2 promoter was detected by qPCR.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

A dual-luciferase reporter assay (RG027, Beyotime) was used to assess transcriptional regulation. Binding sites of KLF5 in the PLK2 promoter region were predicted using the JASPAR database. Wild-type (WT) and mutant (MUT) promoter sequences were cloned into the pGL3-Basic vector, and construct validity was confirmed by restriction digestion and sequencing. ATDC5 cells were transfected with pGL3-PLK2 WT/MUT, pRL-TK (internal control), and oe-KLF5 or oe-NC vectors. After 48 h of culture, cells were lysed and luciferase activity was measured using firefly and Renilla luciferase reagents.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Mouse joint tissue sections were subjected to antigen retrieval using boiling sodium citrate buffer (10 mM, 0.05% Tween 20, pH 6.0). Sections were blocked with 0.1% Triton X-100 (X100, Sigma) and 0.5% BSA (V900933, Sigma) for 1 h. Primary antibodies against PLK2, Collagen II, MMP13, Beclin-1, and LC3 were incubated with the sections at 4 °C for 24 h. After washing, secondary antibodies were applied and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. DAB chromogenic reagent was used for signal development, and hematoxylin was used for counterstaining. Sections were observed under a light microscope.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Two-group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t-test. Comparisons among three or more groups were analyzed by one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

PLK2 is a potential therapeutic target for OA and is highly expressed in OA

By intersecting DEGs related to OA identified from the GSE243421 and GSE264185 datasets with autophagy-related genes, PLK2 was identified as one of the candidate genes (Fig. 1A). To explore whether PLK2 is involved in the pathogenesis of OA, we established an OA mouse model and conducted a systematic evaluation. The results showed that both mRNA and protein levels of PLK2 were significantly elevated in the cartilage tissues of the OA group compared to the sham group (Fig. 1B-C). HE staining revealed disorganized cartilage structure and severe surface cartilage destruction in the OA group, indicating a higher degree of damage compared to the sham group (Fig. 1D). Safranin O–Fast Green and Alcian Blue staining further confirmed notable cartilage degeneration and extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation in the OA group, characterized by superficial cartilage erosion, decreased chondrocyte number, and marked loss of proteoglycans (Fig. 1E-F). ELISA results showed significantly elevated serum levels of inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6 in the OA group, indicating a prominent systemic inflammatory response during OA progression (Fig. 1G). Collectively, these results confirmed the successful establishment of the OA mouse model and demonstrated that PLK2 was highly expressed in OA cartilage and closely associated with cartilage damage and inflammation. PLK2 may serve as a potential therapeutic target for OA.

PLK2 knockdown enhances autophagy, promotes ECM metabolism and cell viability, and inhibits apoptosis in OA chondrocytes

To investigate the functional role of PLK2 in OA and its impact on chondrocyte function, we constructed a sh-PLK2 OA chondrocyte model. RT-qPCR confirmed efficient knockdown of PLK2 (Fig. 2A). Compared with the control group, IL-1β stimulation significantly upregulated PLK2 expression in chondrocytes, whereas PLK2 knockdown improved cell viability, reduced apoptosis, and protected the cartilage matrix (Fig. 2B-C). WB analysis revealed that IL-1β treatment increased ECM degradation and inhibited normal autophagic function. PLK2 knockdown restored Collagen II expression while reducing MMP13 and ADAMTS-4 levels, suggesting a crucial role of PLK2 in matrix degradation (Fig. 2D). Additionally, knockdown of PLK2 increased the LC3-II/I ratio and ATG5 expression, while decreasing p62 levels, indicating a potential negative regulatory role of PLK2 in autophagy (Fig. 2E). Immunofluorescence staining supported these findings, showing decreased PLK2 fluorescence intensity and restored Beclin-1 expression upon PLK2 knockdown (Fig. 2F). Furthermore, ELISA results indicated that PLK2 knockdown significantly reduced TNF-α and IL-6 levels (Fig. 2G). These findings suggested that PLK2 modulated OA progression by regulating chondrocyte viability, apoptosis, autophagy, and inflammation.

In addition, to investigate the effect of PLK2 on autophagy signaling, we examined the phosphorylation levels of ULK1 and Beclin-1. The results showed that IL-1β markedly reduced the phosphorylation of both proteins compared with the control group. Silencing PLK2 partially restored phosphorylation, whereas PLK2 overexpression further suppressed it. Notably, the PLK2 inhibitor BI2536 reversed the inhibitory effect induced by PLK2 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. 1). These findings suggest that PLK2 may inhibit autophagy by downregulating the phosphorylation of key autophagy regulators ULK1 and Beclin-1.

KLF5 Silencing promotes autophagy and protects chondrocyte function against IL-1β stimulation

To explore upstream regulators of PLK2 in OA, we identified KLF5 as a potential transcription factor by intersecting data from the GSE243421 and GSE264185 datasets (Fig. 3A). Functional experiments were conducted using a KLF5 knockdown model. RT-qPCR confirmed successful knockdown of KLF5 (Fig. 3B). Compared with the IL-1β + sh-NC group, KLF5 silencing significantly enhanced chondrocyte viability and reduced apoptosis (Fig. 3C-D). Furthermore, Collagen II expression was markedly increased, while MMP13 and ADAMTS-4 levels were significantly decreased in the IL-1β + sh-KLF5 group, indicating a protective role of KLF5 knockdown in ECM maintenance (Fig. 3E). KLF5 silencing also promoted autophagy, as evidenced by increased LC3-II/I ratio and ATG5 expression, along with reduced p62 levels (Fig. 3F). Immunofluorescence staining showed decreased KLF5 expression and increased Beclin-1 fluorescence intensity in the IL-1β + sh-KLF5 group (Fig. 3G). Moreover, KLF5 knockdown significantly suppressed TNF-α and IL-6 secretion (Fig. 3H). In summary, KLF5 silencing exerted a protective effect in OA by enhancing autophagy and improving chondrocyte function.

KLF5 promotes PLK2 transcriptional activation and upregulates PLK2 expression

We further explored the regulatory relationship between KLF5 and PLK2. PLK2 protein levels were significantly increased in the IL-1β + oe-KLF5 group, indicating that KLF5 overexpression upregulates PLK2 (Fig. 4A). ChIP-qPCR analysis confirmed that KLF5 directly bound to the PLK2 promoter region (Fig. 4B). Dual-luciferase reporter assays further validated this mechanism, showing significantly increased luciferase activity in the oe-KLF5 group (Fig. 4C-D). These findings demonstrated that KLF5 directly activated PLK2 transcription by binding to its promoter region.

KLF5 promotes OA progression by enhancing PLK2 expression and inhibiting autophagy in chondrocytes

To further investigate whether KLF5 modulates OA progression via PLK2, we overexpressed PLK2 in KLF5-knockdown OA chondrocytes. KLF5 silencing significantly reduced PLK2 expression, while PLK2 overexpression reversed this effect (Fig. 5A). KLF5 knockdown enhanced chondrocyte viability and suppressed apoptosis, whereas PLK2 overexpression negated these protective effects (Fig. 5B-C). Furthermore, KLF5 knockdown promoted Collagen II synthesis and inhibited MMP13 and ADAMTS-4 expression, effects that were reversed by PLK2 overexpression (Fig. 5D). Similarly, KLF5 knockdown increased LC3-II/I ratio and ATG5 expression, and reduced p62 levels, indicating enhanced autophagy. These changes were counteracted by PLK2 overexpression (Fig. 5E). KLF5 knockdown decreased TNF-α and IL-6 levels, while PLK2 overexpression reversed this suppression (Fig. 5F). Collectively, these results indicated that KLF5 exacerbated OA progression by upregulating PLK2, inhibiting autophagy, and promoting inflammation and cartilage matrix degradation.

KLF5 Silencing alleviates OA by inhibiting PLK2 and enhancing chondrocyte autophagy in OA mice

To validate the role of the KLF5/PLK2 axis in vivo, we constructed a KLF5-knockdown OA mouse model via sh-KLF5 lentiviral injection. KLF5 silencing markedly alleviated cartilage structural damage and degeneration, and improved ECM integrity (Fig. 6A-C). Additionally, Bax and cleaved caspase-3 expression was suppressed by KLF5 knockdown, while Bcl-2 expression was upregulated (Fig. 6D). IHC analysis revealed that KLF5 knockdown significantly decreased PLK2 and MMP13 expression and enhanced Collagen II levels, suggesting a beneficial effect on ECM homeostasis. Furthermore, the proportion of Beclin-1 and LC3-positive cells increased significantly by KLF5 knockdown, indicating enhanced autophagic activity (Fig. 6E). Serum levels of TNF-α and IL-6 were also significantly reduced following sh-KLF5 treatment (Fig. 6F). In summary, KLF5 silencing promoted chondrocyte autophagy through PLK2 inhibition, protected cartilage tissue, and delayed OA progression, offering a promising molecular strategy for OA intervention.

Discussion

Growing evidence now implicates complex molecular mechanisms, including inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and impaired autophagy, in OA pathogenesis2. Among the cellular processes involved, autophagy plays a crucial protective role by maintaining intracellular quality control and responding to stress conditions16. Identifying upstream regulators of autophagy in OA chondrocytes is therefore of great therapeutic interest. In this study, we identified PLK2 as a novel autophagy-related gene upregulated in OA cartilage and demonstrate its functional relevance in OA progression.

Our data revealed that PLK2 was significantly upregulated in OA cartilage tissues, and this expression was associated with severe cartilage structural damage and elevated inflammatory cytokine levels in serum. In line with our results, previous studies have shown that PLK2 participates in the progression of knee OA, where its downregulation alleviates synovial damage10. Moreover, PLK2 is among the genes regulated during the exertion of mediating effects on chondrocytes17. In our study, when PLK2 was silenced in IL-1β-stimulated OA chondrocytes, we observed a significant improvement in chondrocyte viability, which supports previous reports that cell viability is compromised in OA and that restoring key regulatory pathways can promote chondrocyte survival18. Our findings suggest that PLK2 plays a detrimental role in this context, by suppressing autophagic flux and increasing cellular stress. In terms of cartilage matrix metabolism, PLK2 knockdown restored the expression of Collagen II, while suppressing matrix-degrading enzymes MMP13 and ADAMTS-4, enzymes known to be upregulated in OA and contribute to matrix breakdown and cartilage erosion19,20. By reversing their expression, PLK2 silencing suggests a protective effect on ECM homeostasis. This aligns with other studies highlighting the role of autophagy in maintaining matrix integrity and regulating catabolic responses in OA chondrocytes21. Critically, PLK2 inhibition also reactivated the autophagy pathway, as evidenced by increased LC3-II/I ratio, elevated ATG5 expression, reduced p62 accumulation, and enhanced Beclin-1 staining. These molecular changes reflect restored autophagic flux, which is frequently impaired in OA cartilage22,23. Moreover, previous studies have shown that p62 accumulation and reduced LC3-II levels are hallmarks of defective autophagy and are associated with chondrocyte apoptosis and ECM degradation24,25. Therefore, our results suggest that PLK2 may act as a negative regulator of autophagy, and its overexpression in OA may be a contributing factor to autophagic failure. Notably, most of the previously reported autophagy-related pathways in OA, such as mTOR and AMPK signaling, regulate autophagy through classical energy- and nutrient-sensing mechanisms26,27. In contrast, our findings reveal a novel KLF5/PLK2-mediated regulatory axis that influences autophagy independently of these canonical pathways. This distinction suggests that PLK2 functions as a non-classical regulator of autophagy in OA, broadening the mechanistic framework beyond mTOR- and AMPK-centered models and providing an alternative therapeutic target for restoring autophagic flux in chondrocytes. In line with these cellular findings, we also observed that PLK2 knockdown reduced the expression of inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6, both of which are central mediators of synovial inflammation and catabolic activity in OA joints28. This anti-inflammatory effect may be indirectly mediated by improved autophagic function, as autophagy has been shown to negatively regulate inflammation in OA29. These results highlight a multifaceted role for PLK2 in modulating not only chondrocyte metabolism but also the inflammatory microenvironment of the OA joint.

For mechanistic exploration, we revealed a previously uncharacterized regulatory relationship between KLF5 and PLK2 in the pathogenesis of OA. Specifically, we identified KLF5 as a potential upstream regulator of PLK2 in OA. In line with our findings, prior study has reported that KLF5 has been associated with IL-1β-induced chondrocyte injury in OA30. Also, KLF5 facilitates the breakdown of cartilage, playing a detrimental role in OA31. In our functional experiments, knockdown of KLF5 in IL-1β-induced OA chondrocyte models significantly improved cell viability and suppressed apoptosis, indicating a protective role against inflammatory stress. These results agree with reports that reducing pro-inflammatory transcription factors can restore chondrocyte function and ECM synthesis32. Moreover, we found that KLF5 knockdown enhanced autophagic activity in OA chondrocytes. Increased LC3-II/I ratio, ATG5, and Beclin-1 levels, along with decreased p62, indicate that suppression of KLF5 promotes autophagic flux. Importantly, pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-6 were markedly reduced upon KLF5 silencing, further supporting the anti-inflammatory effect of this intervention. Additionally, we established a direct molecular link between KLF5 and PLK2, extending earlier research showing that the KLF family of transcriptional regulators can modulate gene expression by either promoting or inhibiting transcription, functioning through interactions with DNA, RNA, and various proteins11. To further confirm the functional consequence of the KLF5/PLK2 axis, we conducted rescue experiments. The protective effects of KLF5 knockdown on cell viability, ECM integrity, and autophagy were all reversed by PLK2 overexpression. These findings suggest that PLK2 is a key downstream effector mediating KLF5’s inhibitory effect on autophagy. Finally, in vivo experiments using a KLF5-knockdown OA mouse model confirmed that silencing KLF5 alleviated cartilage degeneration, preserved ECM structure, and reduced apoptosis and inflammation.

In summary, our study identifies KLF5 as a key transcriptional activator of PLK2, which exacerbates OA progression by suppressing autophagy in chondrocytes. Targeting the KLF5/PLK2 axis offers a novel molecular strategy to enhance autophagy and preserve cartilage integrity in OA. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the clinical relevance of KLF5/PLK2 regulation requires validation in human OA cartilage samples. Besides, interactions with other signaling pathways involved in autophagy and matrix metabolism remain to be explored. Additionally, the present study mainly employed ATDC5 cells, which are chondroprogenitor cells and differ biologically from mature articular chondrocytes in differentiation status and matrix synthesis capacity. This discrepancy may limit the generalizability of our findings. Ideally, validation using primary mouse or human OA chondrocytes would provide stronger evidence. Future studies should therefore incorporate primary chondrocytes to further substantiate the regulatory role of the KLF5/PLK2 axis in OA autophagy.
