Tuberculous Arthritis in an Adolescent's Elbow Joint: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Kinzang Dorji, Kuenzang Wangchuk, Sonam Choden
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTuberculous arthritis of the elbow is a rare condition that presents diagnostic challenges but can be effectively treated with early initiation of anti‐tubercular therapy, with or without surgical intervention.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) is a bacterial disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (sensu stricto) and other members of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) [1, 2]. One‐third of the world's population has tuberculosis, and approximately two million people die due to tuberculosis each year [3]. According to the WHO Global Tuberculosis Report 2024, an estimated 10.8 million people developed TB and 1.25 million died from the disease in 2023 [4]. In Bhutan, as in many developing countries, TB remains a major public health concern, with an incidence rate of 164 per 100,000 population [5]. The proportion of primary multidrug‐resistant TB (MDR‐TB) in Bhutan is 9%, one of the highest in the region [6].

Pulmonary tuberculosis remains the most prevalent form of the disease, whereas osteoarticular tuberculosis represents a relatively rare manifestation, comprising approximately 1%–3% of all TB cases and 30%–40% of extrapulmonary TB cases [7, 8]. Skeletal involvement typically presents as monoarthritis, although polyarthritis can occur in about 10% of patients. TB of the elbow joint is a rare manifestation, reported in only 1%–5% of patients with skeletal TB [9].

Clinically, it is associated with nonspecific inflammatory symptoms such as joint swelling, redness, and painful restriction of movement. Due to its vague clinical presentation, it is often misdiagnosed, leading to delays in initiating appropriate treatment. As a result, significant joint damage may already be present by the time of diagnosis [3].

We report the case of a 17‐year‐old boy who initially presented with symptoms of septic arthritis but was ultimately diagnosed with TB of the elbow joint.

Case Presentation/Examination

A 17‐year‐old male patient was admitted to our ward with complaints of pain, swelling, redness, and restricted movement in his right elbow. His symptoms began two months prior, without any history of trauma. Initially, he sought treatment from a local bone setter and was later evaluated at a nearby hospital. There was no history of chronic cough, hemoptysis, or other constitutional symptoms. Due to the persistence of his symptoms, he was referred to our outpatient clinic. By the time he presented to our hospital, his pain, swelling, and restricted movement had significantly worsened, rendering him unable to perform activities of daily living. A systemic review revealed no respiratory symptoms such as cough, sputum production, or dyspnea, and no systemic symptoms such as fever, chills, or weight loss. The patient denied any other medical conditions, prior surgeries, or the need for long‐term medication. There was no recent history of exposure to tuberculosis, and the patient received Bacillus Calmette–Guerin (BCG) vaccination at birth.

On examination, the patient's right elbow was swollen, warm, and tender, with intact overlying skin. There was a significant limitation of motion, including a fixed flexion deformity at approximately 70° and complete loss of pronation and supination.

Methods

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnosis of chronic septic arthritis, inflammatory arthritis, and osteomyelitis was established.

Investigations

Laboratory investigations revealed the following: leukocyte count of 16,000/mm3 with 85% neutrophils, erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 108 mm/h, and C‐reactive protein (CRP) level of 69.63 mg/L. The remaining laboratory parameters were within normal limits. Blood for rheumatoid factor was within the normal titer with normal serum uric acid. Blood cultures were negative.

Radiographs of the right elbow demonstrated osteolytic lesions surrounding the joint, without any evidence of dislocation (Figure 1). A CT scan of the right elbow revealed an abscess located at the coronoid and trochlear fossae of the distal humerus, along with periarticular osteopenia and mild erosion of the radial head—findings suggestive of septic arthritis (Figure 2). Although joint aspiration was considered, it was deferred in favor of immediate surgical debridement to obtain definitive cultures and prevent joint damage. MRI could not be performed due to the unavailability of this modality at our institution.

A detailed explanation of the patient's condition was provided to his parents. The discussion included the proposed surgical management plan, the critical importance of initiating early physiotherapy, and the role of tissue sampling in establishing a definitive diagnosis. Informed written consent for the surgical procedure and for the use of case photographs was obtained from the patient's father.

Treatment

In the operating room, the patient was positioned supine, and general anesthesia was administered. The elbow was approached through both medial and lateral longitudinal incisions. Arthrotomy of the elbow joint was performed from both the medial and lateral sides. Intra‐operatively, necrotic tissue with a cheese‐like appearance was identified, although no frank pus was present (Figure 3). Examination revealed intact articular cartilage within the joint. Extensive irrigation and meticulous debridement were performed. Following the complete removal of necrotic tissue from both the anterior and posterior aspects of the elbow joint, full intraoperative range of motion was achieved (Figure 4). Postoperatively, the joint was immobilized using a posterior long arm slab.

Tissue samples obtained during surgery were submitted for culture and GeneXpert testing, both of which confirmed multidrug‐resistant tuberculosis (MDR‐TB). GeneXpert analysis of sputum also yielded MDR‐TB, indicating systemic involvement. Interestingly, the chest radiograph remained unremarkable (Figure 5). Based on these findings, a diagnosis of secondary tuberculosis of the right elbow was established.

Chest x‐ray of the patient.

The patient was started on a multidrug‐resistant tuberculosis (MDR‐TB) treatment regimen and subsequently transferred to the TB ward for ongoing management. The patient was treated with a four‐drug all‐oral regimen—Bedaquiline, Levofloxacin, Linezolid, and Clofazimine—for 18 months, with Bedaquiline given for 6 months and Linezolid for 12 months. In the early phase of rehabilitation, physiotherapy focused on applying a static elbow flexion splint, fabricated from thermoplastic material, to maintain optimal joint positioning and support soft tissue healing. After several weeks, the splinting protocol was modified to intermittent use, primarily during nighttime, to facilitate gradual functional use of the elbow during the day.

As recovery progressed, continuous passive motion (CPM) therapy was introduced to safely and effectively restore elbow range of motion (ROM). To manage swelling and pain, adjunctive techniques such as therapeutic taping and strategic limb positioning were utilized.

A progressive exercise program was implemented, beginning with gentle, graded ROM exercises and advancing to strengthening activities using therabands and weight‐assisted movements. Task‐specific exercises were incorporated to enhance functional relevance and improve patient engagement throughout the rehabilitation process.

The surgical wound healed without complications.

Conclusion and Results

Diagnosing tuberculosis (TB) of the elbow joint is inherently challenging due to its rarity and nonspecific clinical presentation. A high index of suspicion is essential, particularly in patients presenting with chronic elbow pain, swelling, and restricted movement that do not respond to conventional treatments. This vigilance is crucial even in the absence of systemic symptoms or pulmonary involvement.

By two months postoperatively, the patient had regained both strength and full range of motion in the affected elbow. He remained in the tuberculosis (TB) ward for a total of six months, during which successful sputum conversion was achieved. At the end of 6 months, the patient demonstrated a full, pain‐free range of motion in the elbow, with no complications or concerns noted at the surgical site (Figure 6). Radiographic evaluation at 6 months shows resolution of the periarticular osteopenia (Figure 7).

Discussion

TB is one of the major public health problems in Bhutan that has existed for more than three decades and is still prevalent today among the general population. Osteoarticular TB predominantly presents as monoarticular arthritis, with polyarticular involvement occurring in approximately 10%–15% of cases. The disease commonly affects weight‐bearing joints, such as the spine, hips, and knees. Involvement of non‐weight‐bearing joints, like the elbow, is relatively rare. Among upper extremity joints, the shoulder is most frequently affected, followed by the elbow [10, 11]. Tuberculous osteomyelitis often arises as a secondary complication of pulmonary tuberculosis, with Mycobacterium tuberculosis disseminating hematogenously to distant sites [12]. The bacilli preferentially localize in areas with rich vascular supply, such as the metaphysis of long bones and the synovium, due to their high oxygen content. This predilection explains the involvement of joints like the elbow, although such occurrences are relatively rare [12]. Clinically, patients may present with localized symptoms including elbow swelling, edema, and in advanced cases, ulcerations with purulent discharge. Systemic manifestations such as unexplained fatigue, weight loss, or general malaise may also be observed [13, 14]. Radiographic evaluation of tuberculous arthritis can be challenging, especially in the early stages. Initial imaging may reveal non‐specific findings like joint effusion and soft tissue swelling. As the disease progresses, characteristic features such as periarticular osteoporosis, marginal erosions, and gradual joint space narrowing—collectively known as Phemister's triad—may become evident. Notably, acute fractures are uncommon; instead, imaging often shows partial or complete erosion of the humeral, ulnar, or radial ends [15]. The insidious onset and non‐specific clinical and radiological features of osteoarticular tuberculosis frequently lead to delays in diagnosis and appropriate treatment [16].

To the best of our knowledge, there are limited reports detailing surgical interventions specifically aimed at addressing elbow stiffness resulting from tuberculosis‐related joint involvement. In this study, we thoroughly evaluated the outcomes of surgical treatment for this condition, contributing to the existing literature on managing TB‐associated elbow stiffness (Table 1).

The primary objectives of arthrolysis in cases of elbow stiffness secondary to tuberculosis are twofold: to relieve motion restriction and to excise infectious tissue. This surgical intervention not only aims to restore joint mobility but also plays a vital role in controlling the spread of infection. Arthroscopic procedures are often utilized for non‐traumatic elbow stiffness or mild motion limitations, offering advantages such as reduced soft tissue trauma and faster recovery times [17]. However, in cases involving substantial structural damage—such as extensive joint destruction, ligamentous instability, or severe contractures—open surgical release is generally preferred. This approach provides improved visualization and access, allowing for thorough debridement while minimizing the risk of injury to adjacent neurovascular structures.

The choice of surgical approach for elbow contracture release is influenced by the underlying pathology and anatomical considerations. Various techniques have been described in the literature, including lateral, medial, posterior, and combined approaches [16]. For example, a combined medial and lateral approach effectively addresses contractures and obstructions in both the anterior and posterior compartments of the elbow joint, while also reducing the risk of complications such as subcutaneous hematoma and posterior adhesions. Ultimately, the surgical approach should be individualized based on the patient's specific condition to optimize clinical outcomes [18].

A combined lateral and medial surgical approach has shown significant benefits in the management of elbow stiffness associated with tuberculosis. This technique allows for thorough debridement of necrotic and infected tissues while reducing the risk of injury to adjacent neurovascular structures. Patients treated with this combined approach have demonstrated notable improvements in elbow mobility. In many cases, full intraoperative range of motion has been achieved, with flexion reaching up to 135° and full extension to 0°, effectively restoring functional use of the limb. These outcomes highlight the effectiveness of the combined lateral and medial approach in managing tuberculous elbow stiffness, offering a dual advantage of infection control and functional restoration [16].

Globally, approximately 10% of tuberculosis (TB) cases exhibit resistance to at least one of the four standard first‐line anti‐TB drugs: isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol. Multidrug‐resistant TB (MDR‐TB), characterized by resistance to at least both isoniazid and rifampicin, is observed in roughly 2% of cases. In the present case, the patient was diagnosed with MDR‐TB, underscoring the importance of early detection and the implementation of appropriate management strategies to improve outcomes and limit transmission.
