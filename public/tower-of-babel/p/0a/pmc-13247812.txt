Adjuvant therapy after R0 resection for primary hepatic sarcomatoid carcinoma
Authors: Takehiro Noji, Toshimichi Asano, Satoshi Hirano
Published: 2026

Overview primary hepatic sarcomatoid carcinoma (PHSC)

PHSC represents one of the most aggressive primary liver malignancies, characterized histologically by coexisting epithelial and mesenchymal components. Despite its rarity—accounting for less than 4% of primary liver cancers—PHSC poses a distinctive clinical challenge given its rapid growth, early recurrence, and resistance to conventional treatments. Its biological complexity, which bridges hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC), demands nuanced therapeutic strategies informed by both morphological and molecular insights (1,2).

Diagnostic and pathobiological insights

Histopathological diagnostics require immunohistochemical confirmation of epithelial lineage markers, such as cytokeratin or epithelial membrane antigen (EMA), coexpressed with vimentin to verify the dual epithelial-mesenchymal phenotype. The sarcomatoid transformation frequently reflects an epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), supported by downregulation of E-cadherin and overexpression of vimentin, Twist, and Snail. These molecular hallmarks illuminate the mechanism driving PHSC’s invasiveness and therapeutic resistance (2).

Molecular and genomic landscape

Recent genome-based profiling, exemplified by Zhang et al. (3), has begun to delineate the genomic architecture underlying PHSC. Whole-exome sequencing of sarcomatoid HCC demonstrated frequent mutations in TP53 (approximately 60–70%), telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT) promoter, ARID1A, and KEAP1-NFE2L2 signalling components, collectively pointing to dysregulated tumour suppression, chromatin remodelling, and oxidative stress responses. Notably, recurrent alterations in PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and high programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression suggest both proliferative and immune escape mechanisms. Transcriptomic analyses have shown enrichment in inflammatory and mesenchymal gene signatures, supporting potential responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade.

Epigenomic studies further highlight global hypomethylation and upregulation of EMT-related transcription factors. These findings clarify how sarcomatoid transformation may arise as a late-stage reprogramming event from conventional HCC or ICC clones under therapy-induced selective pressure. This biologic heterogeneity explains the diverse clinical responses observed across PHSC cohorts and underscores the rationale for biomarker-informed adjuvant therapy (4).

Therapeutic context and study overview

Surgical resection remains the cornerstone for potentially curative management. However, recurrence rates exceed 70% within 1 year even after R0 resection, and optimal adjuvant strategies remain undefined. Recently, we read an interesting paper on adjuvant therapy for PHSC (5). The study under discussion—an institutional retrospective analysis spanning 5 years and including 36 patients with histologically confirmed PHSC—provides a timely contribution. By comparing outcomes between adjuvant-treated (n=19) and observed (n=17) patients, it evaluates the impact on survival across histologically guided regimens. Adjuvant therapy selection followed dominant histological differentiation: HCC-like tumors often received transcatheter arterial chemoembolization (TACE) or tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), whereas ICC-like phenotypes were treated with gemcitabine-based regimens such as gemcitabine-capecitabine (Gx), gemcitabin + cisplatin (GP) and gemcitabin + tegafur/gimeracil/oteracil potassium. Some patients received immune checkpoint inhibitors combined with TKIs, reflecting an evolving translational approach.

Key findings and clinical implications

The disease-free survival (DFS) benefit observed in the adjuvant group suggests that early intervention against micro metastatic disease may delay recurrence in this highly aggressive entity and may expand the window for surveillance and effective salvage strategies. Importantly, adjuvant therapy did not appear to increase perioperative morbidity, and no serious adverse events attributable to postoperative treatment were documented in this small cohort.

Nonetheless, the lack of a statistically significant overall survival (OS) advantage, the modest absolute difference in median OS, and the inclusion of diverse adjuvant regimens emphasize that these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing. For now, adjuvant therapy after R0 resection in PHSC is best considered in carefully selected high-risk patients within multidisciplinary discussions or clinical study frameworks, rather than as a universal standard.

Biological plausibility and integrative perspective

The benefit of adjuvant therapy in PHSC can be biologically justified. Sarcomatoid transformation entails high proliferative and angiogenic potential as well as active EMT signaling processes, partly targetable by TKIs and cytotoxic chemotherapy. Moreover, the immunogenic milieu of sarcomatoid lesions, characterised by high PD-L1 expression and abundant infiltrating lymphocytes, provides a rational basis for incorporating immune checkpoint blockade into the adjuvant setting.

From a genomic perspective

TP53 and ARID1A mutations are associated with increased genomic instability and enhanced immune responsiveness, potentially identifying a subgroup that may derive particular benefit from immunotherapy (6). Similarly, alterations in KEAP1-NFE2L2 confer chemoresistance but may sensitize cancer cells to oxidative stress-modulating agents, suggesting rational combination strategies (7). Thus, integration of molecular profiling into postoperative management could refine adjuvant therapeutic decisions beyond histological classification alone.

Limitations and critical reflection

Several important limitations of the study should be underscored when interpreting its results. First, the analysis is retrospective and single-centred, with a small sample size, which limits statistical power and increases susceptibility to selection bias, particularly in the allocation of adjuvant therapy.

Second, the adjuvant regimens were heterogeneous and largely individualised, making it difficult to attribute the observed DFS benefit to any specific therapeutic strategy or to formulate a uniform recommendation.

Third, histological subclassification into HCC‑dominant and ICC‑dominant patterns and the assessment of sarcomatoid proportion may vary between observers, potentially affecting treatment decisions and outcome interpretation. Finally, the absence of biomarker‑guided selection and mechanistic correlative studies—such as PD‑L1 expression, genomic profiling, or pharmacogenomic markers—limits our ability to link biological features to treatment response and to identify subgroups most likely to benefit from adjuvant therapy. Taken together, these constraints reinforce that the current evidence should inform future trial design rather than immediately alter existing guidelines.

Future perspectives

Looking ahead, multicenter collaboration and prospective registries are essential to validate the DFS advantage suggested by Ji et al. (5) and to clarify whether it can ultimately translate into a meaningful OS benefit. Future trial designs should prioritize DFS as a pragmatic primary endpoint while ensuring adequate power for OS and incorporate harmonized criteria for PHSC diagnosis, sarcomatoid quantification, and treatment algorithms. Equally important is the integration of translational substudies using comprehensive genomic, transcriptomic, and immunologic profiling to identify predictive biomarkers—such as PD‑L1 expression, tumour mutational burden, and pathway‑specific alterations—that can guide rational adjuvant strategies, including immunotherapy‑containing combinations, in biologically defined subgroups.

Concluding perspective

The study by Ji et al. (5) provides preliminary, practice-informing evidence that adjuvant therapy after R0 resection may extend DFS in patients with PHSC, one of the most intractable primary liver cancers. However, given the methodological limitations and absence of a statistically significant OS benefit, these data should be interpreted with caution and viewed primarily as a rationale for further investigation rather than as a basis for immediate changes in standard care. PHSC thus remains a clinical and biological frontier where multidisciplinary management and biomarker-driven trials are needed to move from empirical adjuvant approaches toward true precision oncology.
