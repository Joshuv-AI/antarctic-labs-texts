Continuous Theta Burst Stimulation Inhibits Contralesional Parietal and Occipital Activity During Imagined Movements After Stroke
Authors: Justine R. Magnuson, Cristina Rubino, Sara M. Klick, Lauren Penko, Anjana Rajendran, Ronan Denyer, Cristina G. Schaurich, Justin W. Andrushko, Christina B. Jones, Jordan Brocato, Chris Lamb, Lara A. Boyd, Sarah N. Kraeutner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRepetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, when paired with skilled motor practice, shows promise for motor recovery after stroke via modulation of cortical excitability. After stroke, continuous theta burst stimulation (cTBS) has been used to reduce excitability in the contralesional cortex. In theory this increases excitability in the homologous motor area in the ipsilesional motor cortex. However, the response to cTBS is inconsistent. In turn, it is difficult to understand the broader impact of cTBS on brain activity after stroke. Here, 30 participants with middle cerebral artery stroke (> 6 months post‐stroke; nine females; 68 ± 13 years; 15 left hemisphere stroke) underwent both active and sham cTBS applied over the contralesional primary motor cortex, in counterbalanced order across individuals. Before and after cTBS, participants completed a functional magnetic resonance imaging experiment where they engaged in a motor imagery (the mental rehearsal of a movement) task involving opening a door with their stroke affected arm/hand. Continuous TBS led to reduced activity (pre > post) in parietal and occipital brain regions including the calcarine sulcus, cuneus, precuneus, and posterior cingulate cortex during motor imagery. No changes in brain activity were observed following sham stimulation. These findings suggest that cTBS can decrease activity in regions involved in higher‐order or compensatory visuospatial processing. Overall, this work furthers our understanding of how cTBS influences brain activity in individuals with stroke, which is critical to inform the use of cTBS as a potential therapy for individuals with stroke.

Introduction

Brain injury caused by stroke leads to chronic motor impairments in 85% of stroke survivors (Nichols‐Larsen et al. 2005). These motor impairments are linked to changes in cortical activity within the sensorimotor network and specifically, reduced activity in the ipsilesional cortex, which ultimately impairs recovery of the affected hemisphere (Murase et al. 2004; Duque et al. 2005; Auriat et al. 2015). Improvements in motor function after stroke are facilitated by repetitive motor practice, which drives changes in brain structure and function, termed neuroplasticity, leading to learning and recovery of motor function (de Sousa et al. 2018; French et al. 2016; Woldag and Hummelsheim 2002). However, current rehabilitation therapies do not fully restore function after stroke, which has led to the development of adjunct interventions to positively augment learning and functional recovery in chronic stroke.

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) alters cortical excitability. When administered at high frequency (> 5 Hz), rTMS increases cortical excitability, whereas at low frequencies (< 1 Hz), it reduces cortical excitability (Pascual‐Leone et al. 2002; Pascual‐Leone et al. 1999; Pascual‐Leone et al. 1998; Pascual‐leone et al. 1994). In theory, modulating activity in a given brain network with non‐invasive brain stimulation to increase excitability prior to skilled motor practice primes cortical neurons and enhances the neuroplastic effects associated with motor learning. It seems simplest to employ excitatory non‐invasive brain stimulation over the ipsilesional cortex to increase cortical excitability in individuals with stroke. However, inhibitory non‐invasive brain stimulation can also increase primary motor cortex excitability in the hemisphere opposite to the site of stimulation via a reduction in transcallosal inhibition (Carey et al. 2010; Sohn et al. 2003; Carey et al. 2006; Vidoni et al. 2010), which is a process where activity in the motor cortex in one hemisphere inhibits the motor cortex in the other through direct transcallosal connections. Due to the difficulty in locating stimulation targets in the stroke‐damaged hemisphere, inhibitory rTMS applied over the contralesional motor cortex may be a more consistent approach (Carey et al. 2010; Carey et al. 2006; Wassermann 1998; Lefaucheur et al. 2020). In past work, we demonstrated that inhibitory stimulation over the contralesional motor cortex enhances practice‐dependent motor learning after stroke, consistent with facilitated engagement and neuroplastic adaptation of motor networks (Meehan et al. 2011). Yet, despite past work examining the effects of inhibitory contralesional hemisphere stimulation on motor performance and TMS‐evoked outputs, we do not know how this impacts whole‐brain networks related to motor function.

The delivery of 1 Hz stimulation is time‐consuming as it takes up to 30 min to deliver 1800 pulses, creating a significant burden for study participants. Alternately, continuous theta burst stimulation (cTBS) delivers stimulation in bursts of three pulses at 5 Hz with 200 ms intervals between bursts. Although both 1 Hz rTMS and cTBS lead to inhibition when delivered over the primary motor cortex (Wassermann and Lisanby 2001), cTBS requires less stimulation time (40 s to deliver 600 pulses) and lower stimulus intensity to produce similar effects (Huang et al. 2005). However, the effects of cTBS are variable across individuals when measurements are obtained at the level of the muscle, with an inhibitory response to cTBS observed in only ~60% of individuals (Hamada and Rothwell 2015; López‐Alonso et al. 2014; Magnuson et al. 2023). Despite its potential (Vink et al. 2023), no studies have used brain imaging to determine the extent to which cTBS over the motor cortex modulates task‐related brain activity after stroke. This work is critical to understand the variable effectiveness of cTBS and determine if this approach may be employed to improve motor function after stroke.

One challenge to studying the impact of cTBS on motor function in the brain is difficulty studying movements in magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scanners in stroke populations. Specifically, many individuals with chronic stroke are unable to reliably execute even basic finger movement tasks that are often performed during functional MRI (fMRI) scans, which can bias samples toward higher‐functioning participants and introduce variability related to movement/head motion. Motor imagery (the mental rehearsal of movement) represents one solution to address this challenge, as it can be used as a proxy to physical movement (see O'Shea and Moran 2017 and Holmes and Collins 2001 for examples). Further, such motor imagery tasks can involved a multi‐articular complex, goal‐directed action, which are not well captured by simple hand movements commonly used in the scanner. Motor imagery shares neural processes with physical movement (Decety and Grèzes 1999; Kraeutner et al. 2014; Hardwick et al. 2018); thus, it is thought to rely on an internal simulation of the movement (O'Shea and Moran 2017; Holmes and Collins 2001; Grèzes and Decety 2000; Jeannerod 1994). Although untested, examining changes in motor imagery‐related brain activation in humans after cTBS represents a unique approach to determining the effect of cTBS on the motor network, in turn advancing our understanding of response to cTBS after stroke.

Accordingly, the current study aimed to assess the effects of cTBS on the network sub‐serving motor imagery, obtained using fMRI. Participants engaged in a motor imagery task of a multi‐articular complex, goal‐directed action (opening a door) during fMRI scans before and immediately after cTBS over the contralesional primary motor cortex. We employed a within‐participant sham design, such that participants also underwent fMRI before and after sham cTBS delivered over the contralesional primary motor cortex to account for placebo effects. We expected that, relative to sham stimulation, cTBS would reduce activity in contralesional sensory and motor areas typically recruited during motor imagery.

Methods

Participants

Thirty‐four individuals with chronic (> 6 months) stroke were recruited for the study. At the outset of the study, all participants completed the Wolf Motor Function Test (WMFT) and the upper extremity portion of the Fugl–Meyer assessment (FMA‐UE) (Gladstone et al. 2002; Wolf et al. 2001) to characterize the motor function and impairment, as well as the Kinesthetic and Visual Imaging Questionnaire (KVIQ) to measure motor imagery ability and cognitive processes associated with movement (Malouin et al. 2007). The KVIQ has good test–retest reliability (Malouin et al. 2007) and is widely used post‐stroke to characterize participants' motor imagery ability and for those who may be unable to perform complex movements. Participants with chronic stroke and who experienced upper limb motor impairment (FMA‐UE score between 15 and 55, Stinear et al. 2007) were included in the study. This ensured that individuals had some voluntary ability to move the stroke affected arm in daily life. Participants were excluded from the study if they: showed signs of impaired cognition defined as a MoCA score of < 24 (Nasreddine et al. 2005); had a history of seizure/epilepsy, head trauma, a major psychiatric diagnosis, neurodegenerative disorder or substance abuse; or reported any contraindications to transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) or MRI as determined during screening. Four participants were excluded for the following reasons: no motor impairments (FMA‐UE score of 66/66, n = 1), poor fMRI registration (n = 1), and movement in the scanner (n = 2 characterized by a framewise displacement value of 0.5 mm, Malouin et al. 2007). Thus, 30 participants (age: 68 ± 13; nine female; 15 left hemisphere stroke) were included in the final analyses. Individual demographics are shown in Table 1. Participants gave written informed consent prior to testing, and all procedures were approved by the institutional clinical research ethics board (H20‐00070). The data reported herein are an extension of work reported in Klick et al. (2025), whereby baseline data related to differences in activation during motor imagery for females versus males after stroke are reported.

Experimental Procedures

Participants attended three laboratory visits, with the first involving the completion of baseline assessments. During the second and third visits (Figure 1), participants received either cTBS or sham stimulation with session order randomized and counterbalanced (via a custom‐built Matlab script written in Version 2023a) across individuals. The average length of time between visits was 20 ± 17 days across all participants. Importantly, a within‐subjects sham stimulation condition was included to assess any placebo effects induced by cTBS. Before and after cTBS or sham stimulation, participants completed the fMRI experiment.

Protocol for cTBS and sham stimulation. Before cTBS and sham stimulation, the motor evoked potential (MEP) was elicited using single‐pulse TMS to determine the participants' resting motor threshold (RMT). Before and after cTBS or sham stimulation, participants engaged in an fMRI experiment involving kinaesthetic motor imagery of an upper limb movement, performed with their paretic limb from the first‐person perspective.

Data Acquisition

fMRI

Magnetic resonance data were acquired at the University of British Columbia MRI Research Centre using a Philips Elition 3.0 Tesla whole‐body MRI scanner (Philips Healthcare, Best, NL). Using an 8‐channel sensitivity encoding head coil (SENSE factor = 2.4) and parallel imaging, the following scans were acquired at each neuroimaging time point: (1) 3D T1 turbo field echo scan (TI = 800 ms, TR = 1900 ms, flip angle θ = 6°, FOV = 256 × 256 mm, 160 slices, 1 mm slice thickness, scan time = 3.2 min), and (2) blood‐oxygen level dependent (BOLD) scan with a single shot EPI sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 30 ms, flip angle q = 90°, voxel dimension = 3 mm3 with 1 mm gap, 36 slices, FOV 240 × 240 mm, scan time = 3.5 min/scan).

During the fMRI experiment (and detailed in Klick et al. 2025), participants engaged in motor imagery with task parameters derived from previous work (Kraeutner et al. 2020; Burianová et al. 2013). The task included a single run performed in a block design, beginning with a rest block and including three blocks of motor imagery task alternating with rest. Each block lasted 30 s. During the motor imagery blocks, participants were asked to imagine opening a door that was familiar to them with their paretic arm; this task was selected as past work shows that prior (physical) familiarity leads to more focal activation within the motor network as compared with a novel task (Milton et al. 2007; Chang et al. 2011; Kraeutner et al. 2018). Participants were instructed to perform this movement using kinaesthetic motor imagery, focusing on the sensations associated with the movement, and adopting a first‐person perspective. Outside of the scanner, before the fMRI experiment, participants were familiarized with the task and type of motor imagery (kinaesthetic) to be performed. During data acquisition, the start of each block was cued visually. Participants performed kinaesthetic motor imagery with their eyes open. During the rest blocks, participants were asked to fixate their gaze on a black fixation cross and think of nothing. fMRI scans during motor imagery were obtained before and after (within 20 min) cTBS or sham stimulation. Immediately following completion of fMRI (while still inside the scanner), participants completed a motor imagery manipulation check where they were asked to rate both their engagement and quality of imagery on a scale of 1 (not engaged; poor quality) to 5 (extremely engaged; excellent quality). To ensure that the resultant brain activation could be attributed to motor imagery uncontaminated by movement (Kraeutner et al. 2014), participants were visually monitored. Visual stimuli were delivered using PsychoPy software (Psychopy Standalone 2023.1.2) (Peirce et al. 2019), synchronized to fMRI data acquisition and were presented on a screen positioned at the back of the scanner bore, which subjects viewed via an angled mirror.

cTBS

During the cTBS session, active stimulation was delivered over the contralesional primary motor cortex with the target muscle being the first dorsal interosseous (FDI). cTBS consisted of a series of bursts of three pulses at 50 Hz, 20 ms apart repeated every 200 ms for 40 s (600 pulses) at 80% of the resting motor threshold (Huang et al. 2005). cTBS was delivered with a Magstim SuperRapid2 Plus‐1 stimulator using a cooled figure‐of‐eight coil or an identical sham coil (7 cm loop diameter; Magstim Co. Ltd., Whitland, Carmarthenshire, UK). Regardless of stimulation type (active, sham), the TMS coil was held over the participant's skull, with the handle pointing laterally and posteriorly at 45° to the midline. In this orientation, the electrical current induced in the primary motor cortex by the magnetic pulse propagated in a posterior‐to‐anterior direction, perpendicularly to the central sulcus. To reduce variability within and across testing days, coil location and trajectory for the contralesional primary motor cortex was recorded and monitored in real time using the Brainsight neuronavigation system (Rogue Research Inc., Montreal, QC, Canada). To facilitate accurate positioning and orientation of the TMS coil, anatomical landmarks were digitized for each participant and co‐registered with their anatomical scan.

At the outset of each cTBS session, participants were seated in a chair with hands resting in a comfortable position. To determine the stimulator output and target for cTBS, the resting motor threshold was first determined by measuring the peak‐to‐peak amplitude of the motor evoked potentials obtained via surface electromyography (EMG) recorded from the FDI of the non‐paretic limb. Resting motor threshold was determined to be the minimum stimulus intensity required to elicit motor evoked potentials greater than 0.05 mV above background EMG activity in at least five out of 10 consecutive trials in the relaxed FDI muscle. Surface EMG signals were recorded using disposable, teardrop‐shaped Ag/AgCl foam electrodes with conductive adhesive hydrogel (Medi‐Trace 130; Cardinal Health, Waukegan, IL, USA), with a total diameter of 32 mm. EMG was recorded over the muscle belly of the FDI, with electrodes placed over the lateral surface of the distal joint of the index finger and the ulnar head as reference sites. EMG was sampled at 2000 Hz, recorded in a 300 ms time window relative to each pulse (100 ms pre‐ to 200 ms post‐stimulus), and monitored using a PowerLab 8/30 data acquisition system and BioAmp biological amplifier (ad Instruments Inc., Colorado Springs, CO, USA). Surface EMG was collected using LabChart software (LabChart 7.0, ad Instruments Inc., Colorado Springs, CO) and pre‐amplified at 1000×.

Data Analyses

All fMRI data was pre‐processed using fMRIprep Version 23.0.0 (Esteban et al. 2019), which involved the following processes. T1 images were corrected for intensity non‐uniformity using N4BiasFieldCorrection and skull‐stripped using antsBrainExtraction OASIS template. Brain surfaces were reconstructed using recon‐all from Freesurfer Version 7.4.1. Spatial normalization was performed through nonlinear registration with ANT's antsRegistration tool. Brain tissue segmentation of cerebrospinal fluid, white matter, and gray matter was performed on the brain‐extracted T1 images using FSL's FAST. Functional data were co‐registered to the T1 using Freesurfer's bbregister and 6° of freedom. ANT's antsApplyTransforms was used to apply Lanczos interpolation to the BOLD‐to‐T1 transformation and T1‐to‐MNI warp. Independent component analysis automatic removal of motion artifacts (ICA‐AROMA) was used to generate non‐aggressively denoised data. Spatial smoothing (6‐mm FWHM Gaussian kernel) was applied as part of the fMRIPrep ICA‐AROMA pipeline.

Statistical Analyses

Final preprocessed data from fMRIprep were used to calculate statistical activation maps for each participant using FSL's fMRI expert analysis tool (FEAT) Version 6.0. Following separate fixed‐effects analyses of pre > post and post > pre changes for cTBS and sham sessions, a fixed‐effects factorial model was implemented to formally test the interaction between time (pre, post) and visit (cTBS, sham) on motor imagery‐related brain activity. A fixed‐effects model was employed to maximize sensitivity to within‐subject effects of stimulation in this repeated‐measures stroke cohort. Thus, results should be interpreted as specific to the sample studied. For group‐level analyses, Z (Gaussianised T/F) statistic images were thresholded using clusters where the z‐value was greater than 2.6 and a corrected cluster significance threshold of p = 0.05 (Esteban et al. 2019).

Discussion

In the current study, we assessed whether cTBS applied over the contralesional motor cortex modulates brain activity after stroke within the network sub‐serving motor imagery. In line with our hypotheses, posterior regions involved in integrating visual and somatosensory information as well as visual processing in occipital regions are preferentially reduced in the contralesional hemisphere during motor imagery after cTBS. Importantly, no differences in patterns of brain activity were found before compared with after sham stimulation in these individuals. This work expands our understanding of how cTBS influences brain activity in stroke survivors, which ultimately informs applications of cTBS when paired with skilled motor practice for individuals with stroke.

The reduced activity in the contralesional hemisphere observed following cTBS was located in posterior regions including the precuneus and posterior cingulate cortex during motor imagery. This finding is intriguing given the direct involvement of these regions in the network associated with the mental representation of motor actions (Hétu et al. 2013). Although these regions are active during both motor imagery and execution, they may play a particularly important role in visuospatial processing and the imagination of motor actions in space, as well as motor episodic memory and self‐referential mental thoughts (Hétu et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2019). More specifically, Wang et al. (2019) investigated the role of the precuneus and posterior cingulate cortex in the visuomotor pathway and found that they may (1) act as a convergence node between the posterior cerebral regions and the frontal cortex and (2) transfer visual and spatial information to the primary motor cortex. Other work examining brain activity during motor imagery of various locomotor tasks showed that the precuneus and posterior cingulate cortex become progressively engaged when demands of the tasks require increasing cognitive and sensory information processing, which supports the understanding that these regions reflect higher‐order cognitive processes (Malouin et al. 2003). It is possible that for individuals with stroke, opening a door with their paretic arm is challenging and not preferentially performed, leading to increased reliance on these brain regions during motor imagery.

Our findings also suggest that the higher‐order processes relating to integrating visual and somatosensory information during motor imagery are preferentially reduced by cTBS after stroke. Bilateral activation of the precuneus has been consistently shown during motor imagery, which aligns with the reduced activation observed in both the ipsilesional and contralesional precuneus observed in the current study. Interestingly, other studies have reported greater activation in bilateral precuneus after stroke during movements of their affected hand compared with healthy participants (Lehéricy et al. 2004) and greater activation in this region in individuals with stroke with more severe motor impairments compared with those with less severe impairments (Wong et al. 2013). Similarly, increased activation in the precuneus during asymmetric hand movements in people with Parkinson's compared with healthy individuals has been shown, especially in those with a more severe condition (Wu et al. 2010). Wong et al. (2013) suggest that the increased activity in the precuneus may reflect increased demand for attention and higher‐order cognitive processes that act as a compensatory mechanism to account for a less organized motor network. Thus, in addition to the reduced activation we observed in the posterior cingulate cortex after cTBS, the “over‐recruitment” of such brain regions implicated in higher‐order processes relating to the integration of visual and somatosensory information may be compensatory after stroke. In turn, cTBS over the contralesional primary motor cortex facilitates increased network efficiency during a motor task (i.e., motor imagery as in the current study), without impacting quality or engagement to perform the task (as shown by similar subjective quality and engagement scores after cTBS despite reductions in brain activity).

Reduced activity in occipital regions including the cuneus and calcarine sulcus suggests that cTBS lessens reliance on not only higher‐order visuospatial‐related brain regions but also early visual systems in the brain. Specifically, activity in the calcarine is enhanced during imagery when individuals are asked to evaluate the physical characteristics of objects (Wu et al. 2010). Our results suggest that although participants were asked to perform kinesthetic motor imagery (i.e., focus on the feelings and sensations of the movement), visual aspects of the movement were involved. Notably, our results show that brain regions that specifically support vision in the ipsilesional cortex were less active as a result of cTBS. The cuneus has also been shown to be important for spatial‐associative strategies during memory retrieval and may code actions at an abstract level (De Rover et al. 2008). Together these functions are thought to optimize the implementation of consolidated movements (King et al. 2017). Thus, reduced activity in this region in the contralesional hemisphere may be important for shifting this brain activity toward ipsilesional activation to improve the producton of imagined movements.

Given that cTBS was applied over the primary motor cortex, it may seem surprising that the current study did not show robust changes in magnitude of activity in this region. Past work suggests activation of the primary motor cortex during motor imagery is inconsistent, particularly when examined via fMRI (Munzert et al. 2009). Further, involvement of the primary motor cortex is typically reduced and more variable than during executed movement after stroke (Sharma et al. 2006; Lotze et al. 1999). Indeed, after stroke, primary motor cortex activation during motor imagery is influenced by lesion location, corticospinal tract integrity, and reorganization of motor networks, leading to substantial inter‐individual variability and reduced group‐level effects (Rehme et al. 2011; Sharma et al. 2009). The absence of robust changes in primary motor cortex activation following cTBS may reflect limited sensitivity to detect changes in this region during motor imagery‐based tasks, or heterogeneity of primary motor cortex activation across the sample. Intriguingly, we also found no evidence that cTBS influenced activation in premotor and supplementary motor areas, well‐established regions to be recruited during motor imagery. However, activation in these regions may be modulated by task demands and recovery stage (Hétu et al. 2013; Sharma et al. 2009). Given our task emphasized internally generated action simulation of a movement participants were highly familiar with, parietal and visuospatial regions may have been preferentially recruited, particularly for individuals with stroke who rely more heavily on compensatory planning strategies (Levin et al. 2009; Verstynen et al. 2005). Yet, whether longitudinal cTBS (i.e., repeated cTBS sessions) enhances sensorimotor function through greater recruitment of ipsilesional primary motor, premotor, and supplementary motor regions remains an important avenue for future research. Importantly, cTBS applied over the primary motor cortex did not appear to impact motor imagery ability. Participants in the current study reported similar motor imagery quality and engagement ratings before and after cTBS.

Although past work measured the effects of traditional low‐ and high‐frequency rTMS paradigms on recovery after stroke (see Keser et al. 2023 for review), few have employed fMRI to measure the impact of cTBS on motor function after stroke (Vink et al. 2023). Vink et al. (2023) showed improvements in recovery of the upper limb in subacute stroke survivors after 10 daily sessions of cTBS applied over the contralesional primary motor cortex combined with physical training. Similarly, two other studies showed improvements in motor function/performance after cTBS paired with skilled motor practice (Meehan et al. 2011; Neva et al. 2019). Recent work using resting‐state fMRI in rodent models of stroke has shown that inhibitory stimulation of the primary motor cortex can lead to distributed changes in brain activity and functional connectivity beyond the stimulation site (Hu et al. 2024). Our findings extend this concept to humans with chronic stroke by demonstrating that contralesional primary motor cortex cTBS modulates task‐related activity in parietal and occipital regions engaged during motor imagery. Together, these results support the idea that inhibitory stimulation of primary motor cortex influences distributed networks rather than being confined to local motor regions.

Notably, cTBS showed the expected inhibitory effect in the contralesional hemisphere despite the heterogeneity of our sample characteristics (e.g., varied lesion location, time since stroke, severity of motor impairment). However, we could not explore these stroke‐related factors in sub‐analyses given our relatively small sample size. Thus, these factors should be considered when determining who is best positioned to benefit from cTBS in future work. Further research exploring stroke‐related factors such as lesion location that may impact the effectiveness of cTBS on modulating brain activity during motor imagery should be performed. For example, damage to posterior parietal cortex impacts perceived ability to generate a motor image (McInnes et al. 2016; Oostra et al. 2016). Thus, it is possible that the extent to which someone with a parietal lesion would benefit from cTBS may be limited. The limited sample size and known inter‐individual variability in response to cTBS after stroke and between sessions may have also influenced the lack of interaction effect between time and visit, despite expected changes to neural activity after cTBS in the active session but not in the sham session when measured independently. Further, although motor imagery engages core motor planning and sensorimotor networks that underlie motor learning and recovery, it lacks sensory reafference associated with actual movement execution.

In conclusion, our study tested the effects of cTBS in individuals with chronic stroke on the network underlying motor imagery, with motor imagery serving as a proxy to physical movement. cTBS delivered over the contralesional primary motor cortex led to reduced brain activity across contralesional regions involved in higher‐order visuospatial processing. Our findings suggest that the ‘over‐recruitment’ of these regions may be compensating for impaired sensorimotor function in individuals with chronic stroke. In turn, cTBS may improve neural efficiency within the network underlying movement‐related processes. By employing a within‐participant sham design to account for placebo effects, we provide further evidence that cTBS has non‐placebo‐related modulatory effects on brain activity. Overall, this work aids in our understanding of how cTBS influences brain activity within the motor network in individuals with stroke and may help to inform the use of cTBS as an adjunct therapy for rehabilitation.
