Comprehensive machine learning identifies anoikis signatures predicting therapeutic resistance and survival in gastric cancer
Authors: Fangchao Liu, Yaoyao Zhou, Yongjie Xie, Ziyi Dong, Chenghuan Dao, Jiahe Wang, Yuan Li, Yaoyang Guo, Zhanyu Pan, Feng Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastric cancer (GC), the fifth most common malignancy worldwide, presents a significant challenge to global public health, with drug resistance emerging as a leading cause of treatment failure. Anoikis is a specialized form of cell death that occurs when cells detach from the extracellular matrix, and it has been shown to play a pivotal role in tumor progression. In this study, we explored potential biomarkers for prognosis and resistance decision-making in GC, focusing on anoikis. By utilizing data from 10 publicly available GC databases, we enriched immune-related and anoikis-related genes and identified a set of drug-naturalized anoikis genes (DNAGs). These DNAGs exhibit a high anoikis index, are highly sensitive to immunotherapy, show significant alterations in the immune microenvironment, and are closely associated with chemotherapy resistance. Through unsupervised clustering analysis of 125 DNAGs, we categorized the gastric cancer samples into two clusters. Cluster 1 was characterized by higher tumor mutation burden, greater immune cell infiltration, and increased oncogene mutation frequency. In contrast, Cluster 2 included a higher proportion of younger patients, was predominantly enriched in the GS molecular subtype, and exhibited a more pronounced immunosuppressive microenvironment with fibroblast enrichment. Subsequently, we constructed a treatment-oriented prognosis signature (TOPS) by applying combinations of 101 machine learning algorithms to 25 genes highly expressed in Cluster 2. This approach resulted in the identification of 11 key genes that constitute the final signature. Using combinations of 101 machine learning algorithms, we identified 11 key genes that constitute this signature. Significant differences were observed between the high and low TOPS groups in terms of survival rate, immune microenvironment, tumor stage, and invasive subtypes, indicating that patients in the high TOPS group have a poorer survival prognosis and more aggressive tumors. Moreover, the high TOPS group demonstrated marked resistance to chemotherapy and immunotherapy, particularly to immune checkpoint inhibitors and various chemotherapeutic agents. In conclusion, through a comprehensive analysis of the molecular and immune characteristics of GC, this study revealed the clinical significance of DNAGs and TOPS in GC, providing insights into new potential targets for personalized treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-38996-5.Keywords: Machine learning, Gastric cancer, Anoikis, Drug resistance, Immune microenvironment and immunotherapySubject terms: Cancer models, Gastrointestinal cancer, Tumour biomarkers, Cancer models

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is one of the most challenging malignancies worldwide. According to the 2022 GLOBOCAN global cancer statistics, GC ranks fifth in incidence and mortality among all cancers globally1. Clinically, GC is typically classified into early and advanced stages. Early GC is confined to the mucosa and submucosa, while advanced GC invades the deeper layers of the stomach wall and may metastasize to distant sites. Due to the subtle nature of early-stage symptoms, the majority of patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, severely limiting treatment options and resulting in poor prognosis2. For patients with advanced GC who have lost the opportunity for surgery, chemotherapy regimens based on capecitabine or cisplatin are the main treatment options. However, chemotherapy resistance remains one of the primary causes of treatment failure and disease progression3.

Over the past decade, immunotherapy, particularly immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), has emerged as a significant breakthrough in first-line treatment of advanced GC. For example, PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, such as nivolumab and pembrolizumab, have demonstrated excellent efficacy in gastric cancer patients with high microsatellite instability (MSI-H) or high PD-L1 expression4,5. Additionally, several prospective clinical trials are investigating the combination of CLDN 18.2-targeted drugs with anti-VEGFR2 monoclonal antibodies in first-line treatment, which is expected to further improve long-term survival rates and treatment outcomes6,7.

Recent studies have highlighted the critical role of anoikis resistance in tumor progression and chemotherapy resistance. Anoikis is a form of programmed cell death that occurs when cells lose contact with the extracellular matrix (ECM) or neighboring cells, preventing detached cells from colonizing ectopic tissues8. Cancer cells evade anoikis through various mechanisms, enabling metastasis and invasion9. Studies have shown that anoikis plays a crucial role in several cancers, such as breast, lung, and pancreatic cancers10–12. Although studies have revealed the role of anoikis in gastric cancer, such as abnormal activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, overexpression of integrin-linked proteins, and dysregulation of E-cadherin, these factors are closely related to enhanced survival and migration of gastric cancer cells13. Nevertheless, the interaction between chemotherapeutic agents and anoikis remains underexplored. Prognostic models based on anoikis-related genes may provide robust support for precision treatment of gastric cancer. In other types of cancers, such models have already shown potential in predicting treatment responses and developing personalized therapeutic strategies14,15.

To develop clinically valuable biomarkers, we constructed a treatment-oriented prognostic signature (TOPS) consisting of 11 key genes, based on 125 drug-naturalized anoikis genes (DNAGs) and a combination of 101 machine learning algorithms. In multicenter cohorts, TOPS demonstrated robustness and consistency in predicting overall survival (OS), immune therapy response, and drug efficacy. Thus, this study provides important clinical evidence for prognosis assessment, stratified management, personalized treatment, and follow-up in gastric cancer.

Results

Screening drug-naturalized Anoikis genes

An overview of the study workflow is shown in Fig. 1. Briefly, Step 1 defined candidate anoikis-related genes by intersecting gene sets associated with a high anoikis index, predicted sensitivity to immunotherapy, and chemotherapy resistance. Step 2 applied non-negative matrix factorization to TCGA-STAD and GSE84437 to derive two bulk-level clusters and evaluate overall survival, TMB/CNV, and immune features. Step 3 projected these clusters onto single-cell data (GSE183904) to assess subgroup heterogeneity, pseudotime trajectories, and cell–cell communication. Step 4 built the TOPS machine-learning model using GSE26901 and GSE15459, stratified patients into high versus low TOPS groups, and examined survival, pathway enrichment, and drug sensitivity. We conducted a correlation analysis of immune regulatory molecules and immune cell infiltration in data from 10 gastric cancer datasets (Fig. 2A and B), and extracted gene sets related to key immune microenvironment regulation, immune suppression, and cytotoxic infiltration. Enrichment analysis identified 20 immune-related gene sets, which were classified into 9 categories through unsupervised clustering: antigen presentation, immune suppression, immune stimulation, EMT score, stemness index, anoikis index, stromal score, metabolic process, and genetic processing (Fig. 2C). Of these, we retained 16 gene sets with the highest anoikis index for further analysis. Subsequently, we stratified 400 gastric cancer patients receiving immunotherapy and evaluated their immune infiltration levels (Fig. 2D), identifying the genes most sensitive to immunotherapy (Fig. 2F). By assessing the immune microenvironment activation score, we identified highly expressed genes in samples showing significant immune microenvironment activation or suppression post-immunotherapy (Fig. 2E and G). By comparing gene expression differences among chemotherapy response groups in TCGA-STAD, we identified gene sets closely associated with chemotherapy resistance (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, by calculating the treatment response index of gene sets, we identified the gene set with the highest chemotherapy resistance (Fig. 2I). Through cross-analysis, we finally screened for the gene set with a high anoikis index, the highest sensitivity to immunotherapy, significant immune microenvironment changes (activation and suppression), and the highest chemotherapy resistance index (Fig. 2J). This gene set was defined as drug-naturalized anoikis genes (DNAGs).

Comprehensive analysis based on consensus clustering of DNAGs

We performed unsupervised clustering analysis based on 125 key DNAGs in the merged TCGA-STAD and GSE84437 datasets(n = 808) and found that the clustering was optimal when the number of clusters was k = 2 (Fig. 3A). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed significant survival differences between the clusters (P < 0.001, Fig. 3B). Age and molecular subtype also showed significant differences between the two groups (Fig. 3C). In comparison to Cluster 1, Cluster 2 was characterized by a higher proportion of younger patients (< 60 years) and a predominance of the GS molecular subtype.(Fig. 3D–E). To further investigate the genomic heterogeneity between Cluster 1 and Cluster 2, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of mutations and copy number alterations (CNA). The results showed that Cluster 1 had a higher tumor mutation burden (TMB) (Fig. 3F). Based on common STAD mutational signatures, we found that mutational signature 1 (related to cell division) was common in both subtypes, while mutational signature 17 (associated with ROS damage) was enriched in Cluster 1, and mutational signature 24 (related to aflatoxin exposure) was more prominent in Cluster 2. We also demonstrated copy number alterations in high-frequency mutated genes (> 15%) and broad copy number changes (> 20%), as well as in four genes closely related to gastric cancer progression. Overall, the mutation frequency in Cluster 1 was significantly higher than in Cluster 2. We also found that classic tumor suppressor genes ARID1A, FAT4, KMT2D, and the oncogene PIK3CA were more common in Cluster 1 than in Cluster 2. Additionally, Cluster 1 showed significantly higher amplifications or deletions at local and chromosomal arm levels, such as amplification at 8q24.21, 7p22.1, 13q22.1, 13q22.2, and deletions at 17p12, 9p23, and 10q23.31. Additionally, analysis of immune-related genes and immune cell infiltration showed higher expression of human leukocyte antigen (HLA) (Fig. 3G), immune stimulatory factors (Fig. 3H), and immune checkpoint-related genes (Fig. 3I) in Cluster 2, while immune cell types with higher infiltration in Cluster 1 included plasma cells, follicular helper T cells, and M0 macrophages (Fig. 3J). However, regulatory T cells (Tregs) and M2 macrophages were heavily infiltrated in Cluster 2, leading to an immunosuppressive microenvironment.

single-cell data analysis identifies fibroblast subgroup heterogeneity

We conducted single-cell sequencing analysis on 26 gastric cancer samples from the GSE183904 dataset. Using the gene sets that were highly expressed in bulk-level Cluster 1 and Cluster 2, we scored and classified the sample cells, determining the single-cell level Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 groupings. Using typical markers, the single-cell data were annotated and classified into plasma cells, T cells, epithelial cells, myeloid cells, mast cells, B cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells (Fig. 4A, B). Notably, Cluster 1 had a higher proportion of immune-active cells, including T cells, B cells, and plasma cells, whereas Cluster 2 had a higher proportion of stromal cells, particularly with a significant increase in fibroblasts (Fig. 4C, D). This suggests that Cluster 2 is more prone to forming an immunosuppressive microenvironment, which promotes tumor progression. Further dimensionality reduction clustering analysis revealed that fibroblasts were divided into four distinct subgroups: COL4A1+, MMP2+, S100P+, and SLPI+ fibroblasts. Differential gene analysis showed that COL4A1 + fibroblasts upregulated genes including COL4A1, RGS5, MCAM, etc.; MMP2 + fibroblasts upregulated MMP2, LUM, DCN, etc.; S100P+ fibroblasts upregulated S100P, FXYD3, etc.; and SLPI+ fibroblasts upregulated SLPI, S100A10, etc. (Fig. 4E-H). Through Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Wikipathway enrichment analysis, COL4A1 + fibroblasts were found to be associated with ECM-receptor interaction and focal adhesion; MMP2 + fibroblasts with extracellular matrix organization and IL-17 signaling pathway; S100P+ fibroblasts with antigen processing and presentation, humoral immune response, and cell adhesion molecules; and SLPI+ fibroblasts with HIF-1 signaling pathway and glycolysis (Fig. 4I)16,17.

Fibroblast pseudotime trajectory and cell communication analysis

Proportional difference analysis between fibroblast subpopulations in Cluster 1 and Cluster 2 revealed that both COL4A1 + fibroblasts and SLPI+ fibroblasts were significantly enriched in Cluster 2 (Fig. 5A). In addition, simulated analysis of the differentiation trajectory of all fibroblasts (Fig. 5B, C) revealed that COL4A1 + fibroblasts tend to appear in the early stages of differentiation, followed by sequential differentiation into S100P + and MMP2 + fibroblasts, and eventually developing into SLPI+ fibroblasts. During this differentiation process, it was also observed that the expression levels of MIF, VEGFA, SLPI, and SLC2A1 gradually increased at different stages of the trajectory (Fig. 5D). This is significant, as MIF expression is closely related to immunosuppression and tumor cell proliferation, while VEGFA is significantly associated with angiogenesis in tumors. Moreover, the upregulation of SLPI has been found to be involved in the progression of colorectal cancer, and SLC2A1 plays a role in regulating glucose metabolism and glycolysis. Therefore, these findings suggest that, as fibroblasts differentiate, the expression of oncogenic genes progressively increases, with SLPI+ fibroblasts potentially playing a critical role in tumor progression.

To further understand the interactions between fibroblasts and tumor parenchymal epithelial cells in Cluster 1 and Cluster 2, we conducted a cell communication analysis using CellChat. The results revealed that the number and intensity of cell communications in Cluster 2 were significantly higher than those in Cluster 1 (Fig. 5E, F). Specifically, SLPI+ fibroblasts in Cluster 2 interacted with epithelial cells through several ligand-receptor signaling pathways, including the MIF and MK signaling pathways. Interestingly, no significant communication between SLPI+ fibroblasts and epithelial cells was observed in Cluster 1 (Fig. 5G, H). Furthermore, pathway identification and visualization analysis revealed that the activities of the SPP1, EGF, SEMA3, IGF, and ANGPTL signaling pathways were significantly enhanced in Cluster 2. Given these observations, it is likely that these pathways play a crucial role in fibroblast-mediated gastric cancer progression and warrant further investigation (Fig. 5I).

Establishment of a novel prognostic model for gastric cancer

Through differential analysis of 125 DNAGs, we identified 23 key genes that were significantly upregulated in Cluster 2 (Supplementary Figure S1). Based on these genes, we subsequently screened 101 combination machine learning models using the LOOCV framework, with GSE15459 as the training cohort and GSE26901 as the test cohort. The optimal prognostic model, a combination of CoxBoost+Enet[alpha = 0.3] (C-index = 0.717), was developed and named TOPS (Fig. 6A).

Prognostic value and clinical characteristics of TOPS

To evaluate the prognostic performance of TOPS, we divided GC patients into high- and low TOPS groups based on the median score. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis clearly demonstrated that the overall survival (OS) of patients in the high TOPS group was significantly shorter than that of the low TOPS group in the GSE15459 training cohort (p < 0.001, Fig. 6B). This trend was further validated, as similar survival patterns were also observed in the test cohorts GSE26901, GSE62254, and GSE84437 (Figs. 6C-E). In terms of discriminatory ability, the TOPS model exhibited a robust predictive performance, with areas under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.767, 0.689, 0.685, and 0.671 in the GSE15459 training cohort and the three test cohorts, respectively. Importantly, the predictive accuracy of the TOPS model outperformed other clinical characteristics such as age, gender, and tumor stage (Figs. 6B-E). Moreover, further analysis of the clinical characteristics of the high- and low TOPS groups revealed that patients in the high TOPS group had a higher mortality rate, more advanced tumor stages, and a higher proportion of mesenchymal phenotype (MP) and invasive subtypes (Figs. 6F, G). Comparative survival analyses further revealed that TOPS outperformed several established immune- and hallmark-related gene sets in prognostic stratification (Supplementary Figure S2).Together, these findings suggest that these factors may collectively contribute to the poor prognosis of patients in the high TOPS group.

Potential biological mechanisms of TOPS

To elucidate the potential functional pathways of TOPS, we conducted Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) in the merged TCGA-STAD and GSE84437 datasets. As shown in Figs. 7A and C, the high TOPS group was significantly enriched in pathways related to cell adhesion and migration, including laminin binding, positive regulation of fibroblast migration, focal adhesion, ECM receptor interaction, and the TGF-beta signaling pathway. These results provide a potential explanation for the more advanced disease stage and poorer prognosis observed in the high TOPS group. In contrast, the low TOPS group was mainly enriched in pathways associated with metabolism and immune response, including ascorbate and aldarate metabolism, nitrogen metabolism, epithelial structure maintenance, innate immune response in mucosa, and organ or tissue-specific immune response (Figs. 7B, D).

Association between TOPS and the immune microenvironment

We further investigated the differences in immune cell infiltration between the high and low TOPS groups using the merged TCGA-STAD and GSE84437 dataset. Using single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA), it was found that various immune cells were significantly enriched in the high TOPS group (p < 0.001), including macrophages, natural killer T cells, natural killer cells, regulatory T cells, and follicular helper T cells (Fig. 7E). Interestingly, the infiltration of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) was significantly increased in the high TOPS group (p < 0.001), indicating that MDSCs may play a key role in gastric cancer invasion, metastasis, and suppression of host immune response. Furthermore, analysis of immune microenvironment scores showed that StromalScore, ImmuneScore, and EstimateScore were all positively correlated with TOPS, implying that as TOPS increases, both tumor purity and immune cell infiltration in gastric cancer patients also increase (Figs. 7F-H).

Differences in immune therapy response and chemotherapy sensitivity

To assess the predictive ability of TOPS in immunotherapy, we evaluated multiple independent cohorts, including GSE91061 (melanoma, n = 107), IMvigor210 (bladder cancer, n = 298), and PRJEB25780 (gastric cancer, n = 45). Patients in the low-TOPS group showed significantly higher response rates compared with those in the high-TOPS group (Fig. 8A, C and F). ROC analysis indicated that TOPS could distinguish responders from non-responders (AUC = 0.658, Fig. 8B). Consistently, TOPS risk scores were significantly higher in non-responders than in responders (Fig. 8D, p < 0.01). In the PRJEB25780 cohort, TOPS scores were significantly higher in non-responders (Fig. 8G). In addition, survival analysis of the IMvigor210 cohort demonstrated that the low-TOPS group had longer overall survival (p = 0.004, Fig. 8E). For chemotherapy sensitivity, drug response data from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database were analyzed. The results revealed that the low-TOPS group was more sensitive to oxaliplatin (p < 0.001), whereas the high-TOPS group was more sensitive to docetaxel (p = 0.030) (Fig. 8H and I).

Single-cell heterogeneity of TOPS and sensitivity to chemotherapeutic drugs

We further integrated single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from GSE183904 to analyze the heterogeneity of cell subpopulations in the high and low TOPS groups. After dimensionality reduction and annotation, eight cell types were identified, primarily immune and non-immune cells, including plasma cells, T cells, epithelial cells, myeloid cells, mast cells, B cells, fibroblasts, and endothelial cells (Fig. 9A). Notably, the proportions of T cells, B cells, and plasma cells decreased in the high TOPS group, while the proportions of fibroblasts and endothelial cells increased, suggesting that the high TOPS group formed an immunosuppressive tumor environment, with lower tumor-killing ability of immune cells compared to the low TOPS group (Fig. 9B). Epithelial cells were further subdivided into five subtypes(Fig. 9C). Analysis of epithelial cell heterogeneity revealed that epithelial cell subpopulations in the high TOPS group were significantly enriched in the TNF signaling pathway, focal adhesion, NF-kappa B signaling pathway, TGF-beta signaling pathway, and NOD-like receptor signaling pathway, indicating their potential involvement in gastric cancer invasion and metastasis (Fig. 9D). In contrast, epithelial cell subpopulations in the low TOPS group were enriched in secretion and metabolism-related pathways, including niacin and nicotinamide metabolism, oxidative phosphorylation, estrogen signaling pathway, and steroid hormone biosynthesis (Fig. 9E). Based on epithelial cell expression profiles, we used the Beyondcell algorithm to predict the sensitivity of chemotherapeutic drugs. UMAP dimensionality reduction analysis showed that drugs such as Vinorelbine, Lapatinib, Sorafenib, and Nilotinib exhibited widespread and significantly higher density distribution in epithelial cells of the low TOPS group (Fig. 9F). As expected, and consistent with TCGA data analysis, these drugs were more sensitive in the low TOPS group, while patients in the high TOPS group may exhibit resistance (Fig. 9G).

Discussion

Despite chemotherapy playing a crucial role in the treatment of localized and metastatic gastric cancer, drug resistance remains a major clinical challenge. Chemotherapeutic agents exert their effects by inducing DNA damage and cell death, with evasion of cell death being one of the primary mechanisms of chemotherapy resistance. The ability of cancer cells to promote survival and resist apoptosis is one of their most notable characteristics. Increasing evidence suggests that the dysregulation of cell survival and death is closely related to cancer cell resistance to chemotherapy18. Anoikis, a special form of apoptosis, plays a critical role in tumor progression and metastasis. While the mechanisms by which anoikis resistance induces drug resistance have been widely discussed in basic research, there remains a lack of effective biomarkers in clinical settings to guide personalized treatment plans. There is an urgent need to translate anoikis-related research into clinical applications, especially regarding its potential impact on treatment strategies and immunotherapy responses.

In this study, we utilized 10 public gastric cancer datasets to cross-analyze and identify 125 target genes with a high anoikis index, sensitivity to immunotherapy, significant immune microenvironment alterations, and a high chemotherapy resistance index, which we innovatively defined as DNAGs. By analyzing these genes, we categorized patients into two clusters. These two clusters exhibited significant differences in survival outcomes, clinical characteristics, genomic variations, and immune features. Notably, Cluster 2 contained more younger patients (< 60 years) and was predominantly enriched in the GS molecular subtype, which has been associated with greater tumor aggressiveness19. In contrast, although patients in Cluster 1 had higher copy number variations and mutation burden, the higher mutation burden may have triggered neoantigen generation, leading to a stronger antitumor immune response, reflected by enhanced MHC antigen presentation, upregulation of immune stimulatory factors, and activation of immune cells20. Although there was higher expression of immune checkpoints, this provided an opportunity for Cluster 1 patients to benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy21,22. Therefore, despite the higher genomic instability in Cluster 1, their survival rate was better than expected23. In contrast, the immune microenvironment in Cluster 2 tended toward immunosuppression, inhibiting an effective antitumor immune response, allowing tumor cells to evade immune surveillance, exhibit more aggressive behavior, and ultimately resulting in a poorer prognosis24.

Further single-cell level analysis revealed a significant increase in fibroblast subpopulations in Cluster 2, consistent with the observed immunosuppressive microenvironment. Fibroblasts were divided into four distinct subpopulations: COL4A1+, MMP2+, S100P+, and SLPI+ fibroblasts. Differential gene analysis showed that these genes were significantly upregulated, potentially contributing to tumor cell colonization, invasion, and metastasis by affecting tissue repair and the tumor microenvironment25–28. Functional and pathway analysis indicated that the HIF-1 signaling pathway, ECM-receptor interaction pathway, and glycolysis pathway played critical roles in promoting angiogenesis and metabolic reprogramming, thereby accelerating tumor invasion and enhancing drug resistance29–31. It is important to note that activation of the ECM-receptor interaction pathway may facilitate gastric cancer cells to detach from the primary site and evade anoikis by degrading the extracellular matrix (ECM), thereby promoting cancer cell survival and drug resistance through activation of the integrin-FAK and PI3K/Akt signaling pathways9.

Pseudotemporal and cell communication analyses in single-cell analysis helped in understanding dynamic intercellular processes, such as signaling pathways and cell fate determination32. We found that during fibroblast differentiation, COL4A1 + fibroblasts appeared first, then gradually transitioned into S100P + and MMP2 + fibroblasts, eventually developing into SLPI+ fibroblasts. As these subpopulations evolved, several tumor-associated genes were gradually activated, particularly the upregulation of MIF, VEGFA, SLPI, and SLC2A1, which was closely related to immunosuppression, tumor angiogenesis, tumorigenesis, and glucose metabolism33–36. Additionally, cell communication analysis further showed that SLPI+ fibroblasts interacted with epithelial cells through the MIF signaling pathway only in Cluster 2. Specific pathway analysis also indicated that SPP1, EGF, SEMA3, IGF, and ANGPTL had significantly enhanced signaling intensity in Cluster 2, participating in extracellular matrix remodeling, cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and metabolic regulation, suggesting that fibroblasts coordinate gastric cancer progression through multiple pathways37.

In the era of precision medicine, anatomy-based TNM staging is no longer sufficient to meet clinical needs, and reliable biomarkers are urgently needed to identify gastric cancer patients resistant to chemotherapy, so that more precise treatment strategies can be developed. To overcome the limitations of modeling algorithms in existing research, we selected 10 commonly used machine learning algorithms and generated 101 models through combinations for evaluation. Of these, the combination of CoxBoost and Enet [alpha = 0.3] performed best, with an average C-index of 0.717, and was selected as the final model. Kaplan-Meier analysis and ROC curve results demonstrated that the TOPS model exhibited excellent predictive performance in the training cohort, test cohort, and two external validation cohorts. Additionally, clinical analysis indicated that patients in the high TOPS group had later tumor stages, higher mortality rates, and a significantly higher proportion of invasive subtypes. This finding further supports the notion that mesenchymal phenotypic gastric cancer is more aggressive and has a poorer prognosis38,39.

The immune microenvironment of the high TOPS group exhibited complex immune responses, with significant aggregation of several immune cells, such as macrophages, natural killer cells, and regulatory T cells, particularly the significant infiltration of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). MDSCs promote tumor immune escape by inhibiting T cell activity and are closely associated with tumor invasion and metastasis40,41. Immune infiltration scores further indicated that the tumor microenvironment in the high TOPS group exhibited more complex interactions between immune cells and stromal components, which may affect patients’ responses to immunotherapy42.

Currently, the major treatments for gastric cancer include chemotherapy and immunotherapy. Although immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown considerable efficacy in certain patients, treatment outcomes remain highly heterogeneous. In this study, we evaluated the predictive ability of TOPS across multiple independent immunotherapy cohorts. Patients classified into the low-TOPS group consistently demonstrated higher response rates to ICIs and, in the IMvigor210 cohort, prolonged overall survival. Moreover, in the PRJEB25780 gastric cancer cohort, non-responders had significantly higher TOPS scores, further supporting the ability of this model to stratify patients according to immunotherapy benefit.

In addition to immunotherapy, we assessed chemotherapy sensitivity using drug response data from the GDSC database. The low-TOPS group exhibited increased sensitivity to oxaliplatin, whereas the high-TOPS group was more sensitive to docetaxel. These findings suggest that TOPS may serve as a biomarker to guide treatment selection, providing insights into both immunotherapy responsiveness and chemotherapy susceptibility in gastric cancer43.

This study has revealed significant heterogeneity in cell composition and function between the high and low TOPS groups through single-cell sequencing analysis. The proportion of immune cells (T cells, B cells, plasma cells) was significantly decreased in the high TOPS group, while fibroblasts and endothelial cells increased substantially, suggesting that the tumor microenvironment in this group exhibited stronger immunosuppressive characteristics, which weakened the antitumor immune response. These changes in the microenvironment may contribute to the enhanced drug resistance and invasiveness of patients in the high TOPS group. Analysis of epithelial cell subpopulations showed that the high TOPS group was enriched in pathways related to tumor proliferation and invasion (TNF, NF-κB, TGF-β), while the low TOPS group was enriched in pathways related to metabolism (e.g., oxidative phosphorylation, estrogen signaling)44,45. These results indicate that the epithelial cells in the high TOPS group possess greater invasive potential, while the metabolic function in the low TOPS group appears to be more active. Drug sensitivity analysis showed that the low TOPS group was more sensitive to Vinorelbine, Lapatinib, Sorafenib, and Nilotinib, with Sorafenib and Nilotinib showing particularly higher density in the low TOPS group, further supporting the idea that they respond well to chemotherapy. In contrast, the high TOPS group demonstrated stronger chemotherapy resistance likely due to its complex microenvironment and enhanced invasiveness.

This study successfully constructed the prognostic model AIDPS by combining gene-directed screening with features such as chemotherapy resistance, immunotherapy sensitivity, and a high anoikis index, while integrating 101 machine learning algorithm combinations. The predictive performance of this model in each test cohort was superior to traditional tumor staging methods. However, it is important to recognize certain limitations in this study. First, overfitting presents a common challenge in biomedical model development, especially when using machine learning and artificial intelligence. Although AIDPS performed well in the training and validation cohorts, its generalizability and robustness require further testing in more independent datasets due to the limited sample size in the test cohort. The results of this study demonstrated that patients in the low TOPS group were more sensitive to immunotherapy and specific chemotherapeutic agents (oxaliplatin), whereas patients in the high TOPS group showed relative resistance and higher sensitivity to docetaxel. These findings are consistent across multiple external immunotherapy cohorts and drug response datasets, though they deviate from our initial screening strategy, which was based on genes associated with chemotherapy resistance and immunotherapy sensitivity. Through an in-depth review of the literature, we propose several possible explanations for this discrepancy. One explanation is that tumor heterogeneity and its complex gene interaction networks may be driving this unexpected outcome46. The dual resistance in the high TOPS group may result from the synergistic effects of multiple gene networks, rather than being explained by single-gene features. This suggests that even though we screened for specific genes, the diversity of tumor cells and their complex evolutionary dynamics could still alter their response to treatment.Second, immune escape mechanisms may play a key role in tumor progression47. Although patients in the high TOPS group may initially respond to immunotherapy, the tumor may suppress immune cell activity by altering its microenvironment as treatment continues, leading to immunotherapy resistance. Such immune escape mechanisms enable tumors to evade immune system attacks, resulting in progressively declining treatment efficacy. Third, the high TOPS group may represent resistant clones with enhanced adaptability and evolutionary advantages. These clones acquire a survival advantage through complex gene mutations or selective pressure, enabling them to survive and continue to proliferate under the dual pressure of chemotherapy and immunotherapy48. Fourth, the resistance observed in the high TOPS group may reflect a combination of multiple resistance mechanisms49.

In summary, although patients in the high TOPS group exhibited dual resistance to chemotherapy and immunotherapy, a combination strategy of immunomodulators and targeted therapy may provide a potential treatment option for these patients. The TOPS model remains a powerful tool for identifying high-risk patients who may not respond to conventional therapy. Ultimately, this model offers a robust foundation for developing individualized treatment strategies, optimizing clinical decisions, improving treatment outcomes, and enhancing patient prognosis.

Methods

Data acquisition

All analyses used publicly available de-identified datasets; no additional institutional review was required. Data use complied with the dataset’s data access policies and consent procedures. TCGA-STAD data were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, http://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). Multiple bulk transcriptome datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo), including GSE84426, GSE84433, GSE84437, GSE26899, GSE183136, GSE26253, GSE13861, GSE26901, GSE28547, GSE15459, GSE62254, and the single-cell dataset GSE183904. For immunotherapy cohorts, we incorporated 12 GEO datasets (GSE35640, GSE67501, GSE78220, GSE91061, GSE100797, GSE111636, GSE115821, GSE126044, GSE135222, GSE145996, GSE165252, GSE173839) and three clinical cohorts: VanAllen 2015 (Anti–CTLA-4, 7 responders and 32 non-responders), Nathanson 2017 (Anti–CTLA-4, 8 responders and 16 non-responders), and IMvigor210 (http://research-pub.gene.com/IMvigor210CoreBiologies; Anti–PD-L1, 68 responders and 230 non-responders). Together, these immunotherapy datasets encompassed approximately 400 patients. Additionally, anoikis-related genes were retrieved from the following open-access databases: GeneCards(https://www.genecards.org/Search/Keyword? queryString=anoikis&pageSize=25&startPage=24&sort=Score&sortDir=Descending) and Harmonizome (https://maayanlab.cloud/Harmonizome/gene_set/regulation+of+anoikis/GO+Biological+Process+Annotations+2023).

Screening for DNAGs

First, based on genotypes related to key immune microenvironment regulation, we performed Spearman correlation analysis to identify gene sets associated with immune stimulatory molecules. Subsequently, we further screened gene sets closely associated with immune suppression and cytotoxic cell infiltration. Functional enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler package, which identified 20 immune-related gene sets, and unsupervised classification was performed, with a focus on gene sets with a high apoptosis index50. Following this analysis, we integrated data from 400 gastric cancer patients who received immunotherapy and grouped them according to their treatment response to identify gene sets sensitive to immunotherapy. Using the immunedeconv and CIBERSORT packages, we assessed immune infiltration levels in each group and analyzed the immune microenvironment activation status post-treatment51,52. By comparing the gene expression differences between treatment groups, we identified gene sets associated with chemotherapy resistance. Finally, through cross-analysis, we identified gene sets with a high apoptosis index, the greatest sensitivity to immunotherapy, significant changes in the immune microenvironment (both activation and suppression), and the highest chemotherapy resistance index. These intersecting genes were visualized using a Venn diagram. We defined the 125 intersecting genes as drug-naturalized anoikis genes (DNAGs).

Comprehensive analysis based on DNAGs subtypes

We performed batch correction and integration on the TCGA_STAD and GSE84437 datasets and conducted unsupervised clustering analysis on the integrated data using the ConsensusClusterPlus package based on DNAGs. Kaplan-Meier curves were used to assess survival differences between cluster 1 and cluster 2 samples. To compare the clinical pathological characteristics between the two groups, chi-square tests and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used. When studying the genomic mutation profiles of the two groups, we calculated the tumor mutation burden (TMB) for each sample in TCGA-STAD and visualized genes with mutation frequencies greater than 15% using the maftools package53. Based on the methodology by Lu et al. (2021), we used the deconstructSigs package to extract mutation signatures for each patient and visualized the high-frequency mutation signatures in each group54. Using the GISTIC 2.0 module in the GenePattern platform, we identified and located genomic regions with recurrent amplifications or deletions. We then selected regions with copy number variation (CNA) frequencies greater than 20% and displayed four hotspot genes involved in the development and progression of gastric cancer. Additionally, we compared the expression of immune molecule-related genes and immune cell infiltration levels between the two groups.

Extended analysis of single-cell subtypes

We implemented the AddModuleScore function in the Seurat package to score gene sets of cluster 1 and cluster 2 at the single-cell level. Cells were classified based on their scores: those with scores greater than 0 were assigned to cluster 1, while those with scores less than 0 were assigned to cluster 255. Based on this classification, we subsequently performed a cell proportion analysis on the two clusters. After observing a significant increase in fibroblasts in cluster 2, we further clustered the fibroblasts and conducted differential gene expression analysis, pathway enrichment analysis, single-cell trajectory analysis, and cell-cell communication analysis for four fibroblast subtypes. For this analysis, the R packages used included limma, clusterProfiler, monocle3, and CellChat56–58.

Construction and validation of TOPS

Given the strong heterogeneity exhibited by cluster 2, we used the limma package to analyze the differential expression of 125 DNAGs between the two clusters. Ultimately, we selected 23 genes that were significantly overexpressed in cluster 2 to construct the STAD consensus prognostic model. Following the previously established workflow: (1) We integrated 10 classical algorithms: Random Survival Forest (RSF), Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Survival Support Vector Machine (Survival-SVM), Supervised Principal Component Analysis (SuperPC), Ridge Regression, Cox Partial Least Squares Regression (plsRcox), CoxBoost, Stepwise Cox, and Elastic Net (Enet). RSF, LASSO, CoxBoost, and Stepwise Cox were combined with the other algorithms to form 101 machine learning algorithm combinations. (2) Using GSE15459 as the training cohort, we constructed the features of each combination based on these 23 genes. (3) In the test cohort GSE26901, we calculated the TOPS score using the features from the training cohort. Based on the average C-index of the two cohorts, we selected the optimal consensus prognostic model for STAD. Subsequently, patients from the training cohort (GSE15459), test cohort (GSE26901), and two external validation cohorts (GSE62254, GSE84437) were divided into high and low TOPS groups based on the median value. To evaluate the model’s performance, Kaplan-Meier curves were used to assess the prognostic value of TOPS, and Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves were drawn to evaluate its predictive accuracy. In addition, we conducted differential analysis of TOPS classification and clinicopathological characteristics in the GSE15459 and GSE62254 cohorts.

Gene set enrichment analysis

By applying Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), we identified specific functional pathways in the high and low TOPS groups. Following the differential expression analysis performed using the DESeq2 package, all genes were ranked in descending order based on log2FoldChange (log2FC). Then, GO and KEGG enrichment pathways were identified using the clusterProfiler package, and the top five pathways based on Normalized Enrichment Score (NES) were visualized16,17.

Evaluation of the immune microenvironment

We used single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) to infer the immune cell infiltration levels in the high and low TOPS groups and explore the correlation between TOPS scores and the immune microenvironment.

Single-cell level heterogeneity analysis

We preprocessed and normalized the single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from GSE183904 using the Seurat package. After dimensionality reduction and annotation, we identified eight cell types. We then performed a cell proportion analysis of high and low TOPS group samples and extracted 18,569 epithelial cells for KEGG pathway enrichment analysis16,17.

Single-cell drug sensitivity analysis

Based on the expression profiles of epithelial cells, we used the BeyondCell algorithm to calculate drug sensitivity scores. UMAP was used to visualize the distribution characteristics of four drugs in epithelial cell populations, and statistical analysis was performed using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests to compare the drug BeyondCell scores between the high and low TOPS group samples.
