From exclusion to inclusion: Analysis of patterns of follow-up in breast cancer
Authors: N. Hariharan, T. S. Rao, S. J. Rajappa, C. K. Naidu, B. Rayani, S. Kodandapani, V. Koppula, D. Gudipudi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Follow-up data in breast cancer is essential and forms the basis for survival metrics and key statistical measures related to morbidity and mortality. Standard practice classifies any woman who does not engage in reviews/follow-up as “lost to follow-up (LTFU),” effectively excluding her from further analysis and disregarding her data. This study aims to evaluate patterns and predictors of LTFU among women with non-metastatic breast cancer.Design:This is a single-center prospective cohort study involving women diagnosed with non-metastatic breast cancer between 2017 and 2018. The study will follow a three-phase approach. In Phase I, 5-year follow-up data will be collected retrospectively through hospital records and electronic medical records (EMRs) up to 2024. In Phase II, patients identified as LTFU will be contacted using phone, SMS, or email to assess health status and update follow-up data. Informed consent will be obtained during outreach. In Phase III, patients who respond will be asked about reasons for missed follow-up visits, including barriers related to logistics, finance, awareness, or physician advice. Data will be analyzed using descriptive statistics, logistic regression, and thematic analysis.Objectives:To determine the proportion of patients who are lost-to-follow-up at 5 years, identify factors associated with LTFU, and explore patient-reported barriers to follow-up in a public sector cancer care setting.Discussion:This study uses a mixed-methods approach, combining quantitative tracking with qualitative insights, with the aim of understanding patient retention and long-term oncology care. This study will provide real-world evidence on follow-up adherence and its determinants in a high-volume, resource-constrained oncology setting. The study is registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (Trial identifier: NCT06927102).

Introduction

Breast cancer remains a major global health challenge, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases diagnosed worldwide in 2022, making it the most common cancer among women. In 2022, India reported approximately 192 000 new breast cancer cases and over 98 000 deaths, underscoring the significant burden of the disease[1]. High mortality rates are attributed to factors such as late-stage diagnosis and inadequate follow-up care. In resource-constrained settings—including charitable trust-run institutions, community hospitals, and government-aided healthcare facilities—patient follow-ups often rely on individual self-motivation due to systemic limitations.

The importance of follow-up data in breast cancer and oncology is immense, serving as the foundation for survival metrics and various statistical measurements related to outcomes including mortality and morbidity. Studies indicate that inadequate follow-up also increases the risk of missing treatable recurrences, ultimately impacting survival outcomes[2]. Current clinical practice categorizes any woman who does not engage in follow-up with the hospital as “lost-to-follow-up”, effectively excluding her from any future analyses. This lack of information creates a blind spot in oncology research and hospital performance metrics, limiting the ability to evaluate long-term patient outcomes and healthcare effectiveness.

This protocol explores the overlooked demographic of lost-to-follow-up breast cancer patients, aiming to identify common patterns and underlying reasons for disengagement. Through a proactive, manual outreach initiative, this study aims to study the impact of re-engagement strategies on follow-up rates and understand the systemic barriers that prevent sustained patient participation. Artificial intelligence tools were not used for the generation, analysis, or writing of the research manuscript[3].

Study objectives

Hypothesis

Self-motivated follow-up is insufficient in ensuring continued engagement among breast cancer patients. Implementing a proactive outreach program for women categorized as “lost-to-follow-up” will significantly improve follow-up rates, leading to better data capture and improved patient outcomes

Primary objective

To evaluate the effectiveness of a proactive outreach program in improving follow-up rates among breast cancer patients who are categorized as “lost-to-follow-up.”

Study design

This is a prospective cohort study involving women diagnosed with non-metastatic breast cancer in the years 2017–2018, who received treatment at our institute. Baseline data collection will include essential demographic information, clinical data including tumor characteristics, stage at presentation and luminal subtype and treatment information including surgical intervention, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, hormonal therapy, and targeted therapy.

Based on institutional records, approximately 2000 women with non-metastatic breast cancer were registered at our center between 2017 and 2018 and will be eligible for this study. Prior literature from India suggests LTFU rates ranging from 14% to 30% at 5 years[5,6]. These figures provide an adequate sample size for identifying LTFU trends and conducting both quantitative and qualitative subgroup analyses without the need for formal power calculations, as the study is exploratory in nature.

Definition of lost-to-follow-up

Conceptually, lost to follow-up (LTFU) refers to the discontinuation of scheduled medical care or surveillance visits after the completion of primary treatment, such that the patient is no longer being actively monitored within the healthcare system.

For this study, the following operational definitions will be used:

Screened for loss-to-follow-up: A patient is screened for lost to follow-up (LTFU) if there is no documented clinical visit, test report, or medical interaction recorded at our hospital for a period of 12 consecutive months or more[5], beginning 1 year after initial registration (i.e. post-treatment initiation). This includes in-person outpatient visits, teleconsultations, or digital communication entries captured through the electronic medical record system (EMR). Patients with explicit documentation of continued care at another facility will not be categorized as LTFU.

Confirmed as lost-to-follow-up: Patients who meet either of the following criteria: No successful contact could be established despite at least three documented attempts via phone, message, or email, orPatients who are contacted and either confirm discontinuation of follow-up care or explicitly decline to provide consent for data or information collection.

Data review and analysis

Descriptive statistics will summarize demographic and clinical variables using means, medians, and proportions, as appropriate. The primary outcome will be follow-up status at five years (retained vs. lost to follow-up). Bivariate analysis using chi-square tests for categorical variables and t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous variables will be used to compare groups. Multivariable logistic regression will be conducted to identify independent predictors of LTFU, including demographic, clinical, and socioeconomic variables. Results will be reported as adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Reasons for LTFU collected in Phase III will be analyzed thematically and reported as frequencies under predefined categories. A P-value of <0.05 will be considered statistically significant. In this study, the absence of follow-up data constitutes a core outcome and will not be treated as missing data; conventional missing data handling techniques are therefore not applicable.

A descriptive cost analysis will be conducted from the institutional perspective. The direct costs associated with the outreach program—including staff time, telecommunications, data management, and administrative effort—will be documented. These will be compared to the number of successful follow-up re-engagements to derive a per-patient outreach cost. Although a formal cost-effectiveness model is beyond the scope of this study, these preliminary data will inform the scalability and sustainability of such interventions in similar resource-constrained settings.

Discussion

Long-term follow-up is an essential component of breast cancer survivorship, enabling early detection of recurrence, monitoring of treatment-related complications, and delivery of supportive care[7]. However, in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), sustained follow-up remains a significant challenge, particularly in the public sector where patient volumes are high, resources are limited, and systemic barriers persist.

Traditionally, cancer has been conceptualized and managed as an acute condition, with care concentrated around diagnosis and active treatment. The acute phase is typically specialist-led, protocol-driven, and concentrated in tertiary care. In contrast, the chronic phase is often decentralized, longer in duration, and requires ongoing monitoring, patient engagement, and management of psychosocial and functional concerns. As survival rates improve, especially in early-stage breast cancer, this chronic phase becomes increasingly relevant[8]. This survivorship phase requires a fundamentally different model of care that emphasizes continuity, long-term monitoring, and patient-centered support.

Multiple randomized controlled trials, including those by Grunfeld et al[9], have shown that follow-up care provided by family physicians is equivalent to specialist-led care in terms of recurrence detection, patient satisfaction, quality of life, and cost-effectiveness. These findings support a shift toward shared care models or primary care-led survivorship, particularly in settings where specialist resources are limited[10]. However, the transition from oncology-led care to primary or community-based follow-up is a vulnerable point in the care continuum. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), such as India, primary care physicians often lack oncology-specific training, and referral systems are inconsistently structured. As a result, transitioning patients from tertiary oncology centers to community-based follow-up remains a significant challenge. While shared-care models hold promise, they must be adapted to local contexts through capacity building, system integration, and robust survivorship planning to ensure continuity of care.

The qualitative component of this study offers the opportunity to interpret disengagement from follow-up thorough the lens of Leventhal’s Self-Regulation Model, a widely recognized framework in behavioral medicine. For instance, patients who perceive breast cancer as fully cured post-treatment may underestimate the need for surveillance, while those with strong emotional distress may avoid follow-up out of fear. Categorizing patient responses this way enables a more structured understanding of disengagement and can inform targeted communication strategies for follow-up adherence.

In addition to addressing a critical evidence gap in follow-up practices, this study also builds a real-world, longitudinal dataset consisting of clinical profiles, dynamic re-engagement status, and structured patient-reported barriers. These data can serve as foundational inputs for developing AI-enabled tools for risk prediction, patient-specific follow-up reminders, and survivorship planning. Recent advancements in clinical AI, such as models using imaging data to predict genomic profiles and guide treatment personalization, underscore the feasibility of integrating machine learning into cancer care delivery[11]. Through this lens, our protocol not only informs current survivorship practices but also positions itself as a stepping stone toward data-intelligent, proactive follow-up systems in LMIC settings.
