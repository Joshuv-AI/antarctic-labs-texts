Risk of venous thromboembolism associated with short-term exposure to fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and ozone air pollution
Authors: Scott C Woller, Scott M Stevens, Joseph R Bledsoe, Daniel Bride, James F Lloyd, Allison Butler, Masarret Fazili, Benjamin D Horne
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile established associations between air pollution and adverse cardiovascular outcomes are known, it remains uncertain whether short-term exposure to air pollution is a risk factor for venous thromboembolism (VTE). The risk of VTE associated with pollution seasonality (summer wildfire season vs winter inversion season) is not understood.ObjectivesThe aim of this study was to assess for an association between short-term exposure to air pollution and VTE.MethodsWe queried the electronic data warehouse of an integrated healthcare system for VTE occurrence from January 1, 2008, to March 31, 2022, at 11 hospitals on Utah’s Wasatch Front. We described air pollution based on small particulate matter (PM2.5) and ozone exposures stratified by day and rolling multiday blocks. We described VTE outcomes in relation to wildfire season (June-October) and inversion season (November-March). We hypothesized that there would be no increased risk of VTE on high PM2.5 days and high ozone days compared with low PM2.5 days and low ozone days.ResultsAmong 25,083 VTE cases, no significant association was observed between short-term PM2.5 air pollution and VTE. Only lag 2-day ozone exposure was associated with an increased VTE risk (odds ratio [OR], 1.03 per +10 parts per billion; 95% CI, 1.01-1.06; P = .02). During the wildfire season, elevated ozone was associated with an increased odds of VTE at lag 1-day (OR, 1.05; 95% CI, 1.00-1.10; P = .0545), mean 3-day lagged average (OR, 1.06; 95% CI, 1.00-1.13; P = .05), and mean 7-day lagged average (OR, 1.09; 95% CI, 1.01-1.18; P = .037).ConclusionWe found little evidence that short-term air pollution exposure increases VTE risk. However, short-term PM2.5 and short-term ozone exposure may variably confer risk for VTE when stratified by wildfire and inversion seasons.Keywords: air pollution, deep venous thrombosis, ozone, PM2.5, pulmonary embolism, venous thromboembolism

Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), characterized by deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE), is the third leading cause of cardiovascular death. Existing evidence, including our former work [[1], [2], [3]], suggests an association between poor air quality and cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, including short-term (routinely defined as the preceding 24-48 hours) poor air quality and an increased odds (with odds ratios [ORs] ranging from 1.06 to 1.15) of myocardial infarction and congestive heart failure exacerbations [[4], [5], [6]]. Recently, it has been suggested that long-term exposure to air pollution increases the risk of VTE [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. However, the impact of acute short-term exposure to small particulate matter (PM2.5) or increased ozone air pollution and the risk of VTE is uncertain. Few studies in this domain exist, and to date, the results are inconsistent, with some suggesting that air pollution, including PM2.5, increases the risk of VTE, while other studies do not [[11], [12], [13], [14]].

A plausible pathway exists for acute air pollution to precipitate VTE events. PM2.5, when inhaled, has been shown to induce vascular inflammation and platelet activation in animal models [15,16]. Wildfires are a significant contributor to PM2.5 air pollution, and it is estimated that they account for 20% of PM2.5 in the United States [17]. Population-based studies have suggested an increased risk of death associated with increasing ozone exposure [18]. Laboratory studies have reported a link between ozone and both airway inflammation [19] and a worsening in gas exchange [20]. This is independent of reports that associated ozone with asthma induction, exacerbation, and the need for hospitalization [[21], [22], [23], [24]]. While the relationship between ozone exposure and VTE is less well-defined, with some studies reporting no correlation [9,25,26], other reports have shown a chronically increased risk of VTE among patients exposed to chronic ozone air pollution [7,27], as well as those with recent hospitalization exposed to short-term ozone [11]. Understanding the risk of VTE in the short term when air quality is poor has important public health implications. Because VTE disproportionally affects the elderly, patients with medical comorbidities, and cancer [[28], [29], [30]], this knowledge might inform public guidance statements to reduce VTE risk, such as staying indoors. Quantifying this risk may also inform pretest probability assessment for VTE among patients presenting for medical care with symptoms of thrombosis when air quality is poor.

Methods

Eligible patients in this study were those diagnosed with acute VTE in Utah who received care from the Intermountain Health system from January 1, 2008, to March 31, 2022. More than 2 million people (approximately 80% of Utah’s population) live on the Wasatch Front, which is a narrow area of land bordered on the east by the Wasatch Mountain range and on the west by the Oquirrh Mountains and the Great Salt Lake, at an elevation of about 4250 feet. This area is approximately 10 to 15 miles wide from east to west and approximately 80 miles long from north to south. It contains 3 nearly contiguous metropolitan areas: the Ogden/Layton area (∼750,000 inhabitants) to the North, the Greater Salt Lake City area (∼1.2 million inhabitants) located in the center, and the Provo/Orem area (∼840,000 inhabitants) to the south [31]. The combination of topography and Utah’s population centers creates conditions that result in air pollution and seasonal changes in air quality, similar to those previously described [4,32,33].

Patients were identified as having a VTE event, determined by natural language processing (NLP) interrogation of the Intermountain Healthcare electronic data warehouse. The electronic data warehouse is the central repository for Intermountain patient data, encompassing all patient demographics, laboratory results, and imaging results for Intermountain Health. During the study period, Intermountain Health hospitals provided care to about 55% of patients residing in Utah. We previously reported that in our legacy electronic health record, the NLP interrogation tool that we developed had high sensitivity and specificity in detecting DVT and PE [34]. Then, in 2021, following the implementation of the Affordable Care Act-compliant electronic health record, we validated the exceptional performance of the subsequently adapted NLP tool for the outcome of VTE [35].

Study participants

Study participants included all patients with imaging results demonstrative of clinically overt VTE, defined as DVT of the lower extremities or PE, documented in our integrated healthcare system from January 1, 2008, to March 31, 2022. Patient information included a residential address at the time of the VTE event, age, sex, smoking status, and preexisting health conditions. The study used deidentified data, ensuring data security and confidentiality, along with other study procedures that followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Intermountain Healthcare Institutional Review Board and was granted a waiver of informed consent.

Pollution data

Particulate air pollution data for PM2.5 were compiled from the Utah Department of Environmental Quality, Division of Air Quality, and the US Environmental Protection Agency Air Quality System. Monitoring was conducted in accordance with the Environmental Protection Agency’s federal reference method [36]. PM2.5 and ozone exposure estimates were primarily based on data from 3 monitoring sites located in the 3 Wasatch Front metro areas: the Harrisville site in the Ogden area, the Hawthorne site in Salt Lake City, and the Lindon site in the Provo/Orem area. As new sites were added across the metropolitan area, data were attributed to the site closest to the subject’s residence. When PM2.5 and ozone data were missing at a patient’s primary site, data were drawn from the secondary site closest to each subject’s residence (North Provo, Spanish Fork, Bountiful, Salt Lake City-Rose Park, and Salt Lake City Air Monitoring Center sites) when available. Over time, the assessment led to decreasing data at 2 sites, which were determined by proximity. In cases where data were missing, data from an alternative site were selected. The average distance from each subject’s residence to the nearest pollution monitor, as measured from the centroid of zip codes, was 5.8 ± standard deviation 3.7 miles for PM2.5, and 5.3 ± standard deviation 3.6 miles for ozone. Missing data included 1% of PM2.5 measurements and 3% of ozone measurements. In developing the model for the influence of pollutants in relation to weather and atmospheric variables, a conditional logistic regression model was employed that included concurrent-day (lag 0) PM2.5 (central monitor plus imputed values) as a simple linear nonthreshold model, controlling for weather variables (minimum temperature, dew point temperature, barometric pressure, and squared terms for each) [1]. Further details about air pollution data acquisition were previously published [3].

Data were partitioned based on season and month of the year. The topography of the Wasatch Front leads to a “sink effect,” uniquely predisposing the study area to an inversion climate during late fall and winter. An inversion is characterized by cold air, along with pollutants being trapped beneath warmer air (usually high pressure), creating a capping phenomenon with stagnant air trapped in the sink. Wildfires increasingly affect the Mountain West and are a major contributor to PM2.5, but do not occur during periods susceptible to inversions. We describe outcomes in relation to the wildfire season (June-October) and inversion season (November-March). April and May rarely have poor air quality, serving as a control period. To provide additional granular information surrounding periods of greatest risk, we also report exploratory outcomes partitioned by month.

Statistical analysis

The primary statistical analysis of VTE events and ambient PM2.5 was based on a time-stratified case-crossover design [[37], [38], [39]]. As in our previous studies [[1], [2], [3]], this analysis matches pollution exposures at the time of an event with ≥1 control (or referent) when no event occurred for the individual. This analytic approach uses conditional regression to estimate potential excess risk. Control periods are matched by weekday for the month and year of the event, resulting in 3 to 4 control days per event. Through this design, time-based risk factors were controlled (eg, weekday, season, and long-frequency time trends). Also, since patients served as their own controls, essentially perfect matching occurred for subject-specific risk factors and demographics that are invariant, at least in the short term (eg, age, sex, race, smoking status, and comorbidities). The regression model was a conditional Poisson regression, which evaluated lag 0 PM2.5 and other pollution exposures as simple linear risk variables. Other lags of pollution exposure were for the prior day (day −1) or 2 days before the event (day −2), and moving average (mAvg) periods of 3 days (mAvg −2 to 0: days −1 and −2 plus lag 0) and 7 days (mAvg −6 to 0: the 6 days before the event plus lag 0). ORs and 95% CIs were estimated using all VTE events for all patients. For comparison purposes, and consistent with various studies and reviews of the literature, ORs associated with 10 μg/m3 incremental increases in PM2.5 concentration are presented. Ozone comparison was calculated as ORs per 10 parts per billion and used the same Poisson methods of analysis as PM2.5. We excluded VTE events that occurred in the same month as a previous event to avoid overestimation of sentinel VTE events. In addition, secondary analyses were performed using the same analytic methods in subgroups defined by various comorbidities and patient characteristics. Analyses were conducted using the gnm package in R statistical software (v4.2.0) [40].

Results

Patient characteristics are reported in Table 1, and a summary of the results on the effect of short-term exposure to PM2.5 and ozone on the outcome of VTE, as well as the component outcomes of DVT and PE among all patients, is found in Table 2. The results of patients who experienced a VTE event during either wildfire season or inversion season are found in Table 3. Increased exposure to PM2.5, defined as an incremental increase of 10 μg/m3 in PM2.5 exposure on the day of the VTE event (ie, lag 0), 1 or 2 days preceding the VTE event (lag 1 and 2, respectively), or an elevated mean PM2.5 exposure during the aggregate 3 and 7 days preceding a VTE event (ie, mAvg −2 to 0 and mAvg −6 to 0, respectively), was not associated with an increased odds of VTE among 25,083 VTE events. Increased ozone exposure, defined as an increase of 10 parts per billion during lag 0, lag 1, mAvg −2 to 0, or mAvg −6 to 0, was not associated with an increase in the odds of VTE. An increase in VTE was observed at lag 2 (OR, 1.03; 95% CI, 1.01-1.06; P = .017). For the component outcomes of PE and DVT, only increased ozone during lag 2 was associated with DVT (OR, 1.03; 95% CI, 1.00-1.07; P = .03).

Discussion

We observed variability in the risk of thrombosis associated with short-term air pollution. We did not observe an increased odds of VTE associated with short-term PM2.5 exposure. This observation is not dissimilar to what has formerly been reported [11,12], while others have suggested that exposure to PM2.5 may indeed increase short-term VTE risk [13,41,42]. We observed results similar to those formerly described by colleagues in our geographic area [11]. Johnson et al. [11] studied 2803 patients over 4.4 years and did not observe an association between short-term PM2.5 exposure and VTE on discrete days 6 and 7 preceding a VTE event. We hypothesized that poor air quality over a preceding series of days (mAvg −2 to 0 and mAvg −6 to 0) would confer increased risk. Regardless, we similarly observed no association between increased PM2.5 exposure and VTE.

One strength of this study is the use of a time-stratified case-crossover design. This analysis permits optimized matching, as each participant serves as their own control; therefore, participant-specific characteristics do not vary over time. This obviates the possibility of confounding by age, sex, heritable thrombotic risk, and personal modifiers of thrombosis risk, including obesity, heart disease, and the like. We matched VTE event days with referent days on the same day of the week before and after the VTE event. This controlled for seasonality, long-term time trends, and long-term changes in individual characteristics by design.

We partitioned VTE events by season. This permitted us to investigate any association between exposure to poor air quality attributed to wildfire exposure and inversion climate. We explored the potential harm associated with wildfire exposure, given that a large proportion of PM2.5 in the US is attributed to wildfires, and because of the described risk of cardiopulmonary disease associated with acute PM2.5 exposure [[1], [2], [3]]. The association with VTE risk that we observed during the wildfire season (the summer months of June-October) occurred in the setting of increased ozone exposure. We observed an increased odds of VTE during the wildfire season in the setting of increased ozone at lag 1, mAvg −2 to 0, and mAvg −6 to 0. This observation aligns with a plausible clinical hypothesis that prolonged antecedent exposure to ozone air pollution leads to an aggregate increased risk over time, which is not evident with shorter duration exposures, such as poor air quality for a solitary day. Furthermore, recent studies have suggested that chronic ozone exposure increases VTE risk [7,27]. We observed a significant correlation between ozone and PM2.5 based on season. We report a positive correlation between PM2.5 and ozone during the summer months, as observed by others [43,44], and a negative correlation between PM2.5 and ozone during the autumn/winter months, similar to others [[43], [44], [45]].

When we studied VTE outcome events partitioned by month, we observed that the odds of VTE appeared to be greatest in the setting of increased ozone exposure in September. Understanding VTE risk attributable to poor air quality by month could be used to inform education on patient risk of exposure, serving as an opportunity for public service announcements. We observed a decreased odds of VTE in association with ozone during lag 0 and lag 1 in November (Figure 2).

In our study, we included more than 25,000 VTE events that occurred among 20,439 patients, with longitudinal data collected over 14 years. Aspects of our methodology that inform our observations merit comment. First, we employed NLP to identify VTE events. We have previously described the highly sensitive and specific performance of this tool in our integrated healthcare system [34,35]. This is a notable strength of the study, given that NLP performance is thought to exceed that of International Classification of Diseases coding for VTE outcome ascertainment [[46], [47], [48]]. In contrast, International Classification of Diseases codes were used for outcome ascertainment in studies that yielded results opposite to ours [41,42]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest study of its kind using NLP for VTE ascertainment and reporting outcome events, partitioned by type of pollutant and seasonality.

This study is potentially limited in generalizability because of the unique geography of our study area. The Wasatch Front is recognized as being susceptible to poor air quality, especially to an inversion climate. However, the inversion climate is not unique to the Wasatch Front [49], and increasingly, cities and planners are interested in understanding the risk of poor air quality to inform development and health implications [50]. A limitation inherent in our study design is the use of average ambient air pollution data without personal or in-home monitoring; therefore, we assumed individualized patient risk based on environmental data. Furthermore, our data are limited by the incidence of poor air quality days. While greater air pollution exists in the winter months, leading to greater certainty surrounding the point estimates, less confidence exists for the point estimates during comparatively better air quality months. For example, the number of days annually that the maximum 8-hour average ozone concentrations were above the national ambient air quality standards during the study ranged from 7 to 44, averaging approximately 21 days per year [51]. As we studied VTE incidence by month and season, confounding factors, apart from air pollution, may have impacted rates of VTE. We report VTE outcome events for PM2.5 and ozone, which are readily available with the most robust data from our monitoring sites. Another limitation of this study is that we did not include the correlation of VTE with other pollutants (eg, PM10, NO2, NOx, etc.), which have been reported to be associated with the risk of VTE [9,[52], [53], [54]]. To attempt to control for the potential confounding of chronic anticoagulant use on the outcome of VTE, we excluded patients with a history of atrial fibrillation and VTE and conducted a sensitivity analysis, which demonstrated no meaningful difference in the odds of thromboembolism stratified by air pollution and timing of exposure (Supplementary Table).

Our findings suggest that acute air pollution exposure, defined as exposure to increased PM2.5 and ozone, does not increase the risk of VTE in all patients. However, we observed that the risk varies based on seasonality, and an increased risk of VTE may be associated with ozone exposure during the wildfire season, especially in September. Wildfire season coincides with the summer months (June-October) when the highest ozone levels are reported in North America. This aligns with the generalized reporting of an increased risk of ozone exposure in our geographic area [32]. While wildfires contribute to PM2.5 and others have reported VTE risk attributed to elevated levels of particulate matter [55,56], our study is not designed to assess whether wildfires contribute to ozone production, which is also elevated during the wildfire season. We propose that additional studies may identify populations that are uniquely at risk due to underlying comorbidities and a predisposition to thrombosis, as others have suggested [57].
