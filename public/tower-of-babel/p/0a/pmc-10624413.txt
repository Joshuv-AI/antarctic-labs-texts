Comparison of Clinical, Economic, and Humanistic Outcomes Between Blood Collection Approaches: A Systematic Literature Review
Authors: Cathy Perry, Kimberly Alsbrooks, Alicia Mares, Klaus Hoerauf
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.

Introduction

Blood testing is essential for diagnosis, treatment, and monitoring. Almost 500 million inpatient blood collections are performed in United States (US) hospitals each year,1 informing 60–70% of clinical decisions.2

Although blood collection via direct venipuncture is considered standard of care,3 there are several potential advantages of collecting blood from indwelling vascular access devices (VADs), which can affect the quality of care. These include reducing the stress and discomfort associated with needle punctures,4,5 and minimizing the number of venipunctures for patients requiring repeated blood sampling. This is particularly important in patients with difficult venous access (DIVA),6 who represent more than one-third of all venipuncture patients7 and often require several attempts to successfully collect blood.8-10

Recent innovations include initial specimen diversion devices (ISDDs) that automatically divert and sequester the initial 1–2 ml of blood from blood culture samples and are intended to reduce contamination rates.11 Others include needle-free blood collection systems, which are intended to reduce hemolysis rates and increase blood collection success rates from peripheral intravenous catheters (PIVCs) in place for several days.12

A systematic literature review (SLR) was performed to gather contemporary evidence on blood collection and compare clinical, economic, and humanistic outcomes across different approaches. The objective was to gain a better understanding of the prevalence, advantages, and disadvantages of blood collection using different approaches, and any interventions/innovations that can been used to mitigate the disadvantages.

Methods

Electronic database searches were conducted on 5th and 8th March 2021 and were date-limited from 2010 to the present day, to only capture contemporary data. The review was not registered, and a protocol is available upon request. Embase, MEDLINE, Cochrane Library databases, and Centre for Reviews and Dissemination databases were searched via the Ovid platform. The full electronic search strategy is presented in Supplemental Digital Content 1 (see Tables S1–S3, http://links.lww.com/JHQ/A195). Hand-searching was also performed by screening bibliographic reference lists of included studies and relevant SLRs/meta-analyses. The population of interest was patients requiring blood collection for diagnostic, prognostic, or treatment monitoring purposes, regardless of disease/indication. Relevant interventions included blood collection by a range of approaches, such as direct venipuncture, PIVCs, midline catheters, and central lines (encompassing peripherally inserted central catheters [PICCs], centrally inserted central catheters [CICCs], and implanted ports). The focus of this SLR was venous blood collection, so studies on arterial catheters were not eligible for inclusion. The full population, intervention, comparator(s), outcomes, and study design (PICOS) elements used to assess study eligibility are presented in Supplemental Digital Content 1 (see Table S4, http://links.lww.com/JHQ/A195).

Two independent reviewers screened citations by title/abstract, with any conflicts regarding eligibility resolved by discussion. Full-texts were also evaluated by two independent reviewers, with any disputes regarding eligibility resolved by dialogue. Arbitration was provided by a third, more senior reviewer if required.

Results

Seven thousand three hundred thirty-five publications were identified through electronic database searches. Overall, 141 publications were included in the final review, comprising 132 publications identified via database searches, and nine publications identified via hand-searching. The included publications reported on studies performed across geographical locations spanning six continents, and a diverse range of study designs, including cross-sectional surveys and questionnaires, randomized and nonrandomized trials, and prospective and retrospective observational studies.

Proportion of Blood Samples Collected by Device Type

Sixteen publications reported the proportion of total blood samples in a particular hospital or ward/department using specific device types (Table 1). As anticipated, blood sampling through VADs was most common in emergency departments (EDs), trauma centers, intensive care units (ICUs), and hematology-oncology units. A survey spanning multiple sites across the US, Puerto Rico, and Canada determined that in ICU patients with existing central lines, blood culture samples were drawn through these existing lines in 52% of adult and 96% of pediatric practices. For hematology–oncology and bone marrow transplant patients, the respective figures were 64% and 98%.29

The studies in Table 1 are categorized based on the purpose of blood collection (blood culture or routine), because many publications reported that healthcare professionals (HCPs) in their institution were explicitly advised not to collect blood culture samples from VADs to minimize the risk of bloodstream infection and sample contamination. A 2018 survey of over 80 hospitals in the US revealed that 90% of hospitals advise staff to collect culture samples via direct venipuncture rather than from central VADs wherever possible.30

Placement and Blood Collection Success by Device Type

Overall, 28 publications reported placement and blood collection success outcomes such as first attempt insertion success rate, number of attempts required to access the vein, blood collection success after venous access, or an overall success rate encompassing insertion and blood collection success.

Summary statistics were challenging to derive because of the variation in measuring and reporting. Nevertheless, the accumulated data indicate that in most patients, even if multiple attempts are sometimes required, blood collection via direct venipuncture is ultimately successful in obtaining a sample.

Importantly, it is not always possible to obtain complete blood samples from indwelling catheters. Across three pediatric studies, overall success rates for obtaining useable blood samples from existing PIVCs were 91.3%, 76%, and 85.1%.31-33 In addition, the ability to obtain a specimen from a PIVC may decrease as the catheter ages. In one of the three studies, the rate of successful PIVC blood collection decreased from 85% in PIVCs aged <24 hours to just 63% in PIVCs aged >24 hours (p = .010).33 The size of the PIVC was not significantly associated with the ability to obtain a specimen; however, results revealed a trend indicating that larger gauge PIVCs were more successful in providing a complete specimen, whereas the smallest gauge PIVC was less successful.33

Only one study reported on the ease of blood sampling from PIVCs using the needle-free PIVO device, which is designed for PIVC blood collection. The blood collection success rate was 81% across 126 blood collections in 62 hospitalized patients performed over 4 days.34

Hemolysis of Blood Samples by Device Type

Of the 36 publications that reported hemolysis rates, 18 compared rates between two devices, and 18 reported rates for a single device category. Hemolysis rates were numerically higher in catheter-collected samples in 14/17 publications comparing direct venipuncture and PIVC blood collection (Table 2). Of these, the difference was statistically significant in all five publications that presented a p-value.

Although hemolysis rates were generally higher in PIVC than direct venipuncture blood samples, the accumulated data suggest that the risk of hemolysis is lowered when specific sampling procedures are followed, such as collecting blood from the antecubital fossa,19,42,45 using larger gauge needles,42,45 ensuring not to underfill collection tubes,45 keeping tourniquet time to under a minute,42,45 and discarding the initial portion of blood.40

Two studies reported on the use of the PIVO device to collect blood samples from patients with PIVCs.12,34 The first reported significantly lower hemolysis rates in blood samples collected from PIVCs using the PIVO device (1.8%) relative to those collected more conventionally, by direct venipuncture or from central lines (3.3%).12 The other reported a hemolysis rate of 0% in an intervention group that underwent daily blood sampling from a PIVC using the PIVO device.34

Sample Contamination/False Positives in Blood Cultures by Device Type

Thirty-one publications reported sample contamination rates. Of these, 10 compared two devices, whereas the other 21 reported rates for a single device, or data pooled across two devices.

In studies comparing direct venipuncture and VAD-collected blood samples, contamination rates were numerically higher among VAD-collected samples in 7/10 publications, but lower in 2/10 publications (Table 3). In both latter studies, blood was collected from PIVCs at insertion rather than over dwell, and samples were collected following strict sterile protocols.16,47

Contamination rate was higher in VAD-collected samples than direct venipuncture samples at the beginning of the study in 2010, but this trend was reversed by the end of the study after an intervention to reduce contamination rates.

Interventions to reduce contamination rates in VAD collections have been shown to be effective; in a US hospital, training in aseptic technique contributed to a reduction in contamination rates from 4.8% to 0% (p = .005) for central lines.15 Another study reported a significant reduction in contamination rates among PIVC-collected samples, from 3.9% to 1.6% (p < .0001), following enforcement of a strict sterile technique for PIVC insertion and blood collection.52 This implies that sample contamination is not inevitable during VAD blood sampling, and if applied strictly, aseptic protocols can virtually eradicate contamination risk.

There is also room for improvement in blood culture contamination rates for direct venipuncture. Kai et al.53 demonstrated a drop in contamination rates from 6.3% to 1.1% (p < .001) in a Japanese ED after implementation of a six-element intervention “bundle” that included alcohol wiping, improved hand hygiene, and the use of sterile gloves during venipuncture.

Impact of the Initial Specimen Diversion Technique on Sample Quality

Multiple publications evaluated the impact of the initial specimen diversion technique (ISDT) on sample contamination or hemolysis rates. In the earliest of these studies, Patton et al. discovered that discarding the first 1 ml of blood sample collected by direct venipuncture significantly reduced false-positive blood cultures.54 The authors concluded that venipuncture needles can soil blood culture specimens with unsterilized skin fragments, and thus the ISDT is effective in reducing contamination. Two subsequent studies involving direct venipuncture sampling reported similar results; blood culture contamination rates dropped from 3.4% to 2.4% after the ISDT was adopted at a Canadian institution,55 whereas a randomized trial in an Israeli medical center documented a 1.7% contamination rate in an ISDT group relative to 5% in a control group (p = .015).56

The SLR identified two publications that testify to the effectiveness of bespoke ISDDs. In a US ED and trauma center, contamination rate was only 0.22% in cultures prepared with an ISDD, compared with 1.78% without one (p = .001).57 In an Israeli hospital, an ISDD was used with an integrated needle or by attaching it to a newly-placed intravenous (IV) catheter; contamination rate was 1.0% with the ISDD and 5.2% without it (p = .008).11

Anxiety, Fear, and Stress by Device Type

Eleven studies presented self-reported measures of patient anxiety, fear, or stress aroused by blood collection via direct venipuncture, and one via IV catheters, but none compared these across device types.

Healthcare Costs, Resource Utilization, and Economic Burdens by Device Type

Thirty-six publications reported data regarding healthcare resource utilization, and 13 publications on specific costs or economic burdens. Of note, two studies showed blood culture contamination to be associated with a longer length of stay and increased costs56,62; in another study, each contaminated blood culture resulted in an additional $2,800 of hospital charges.52 In all three studies, the additional spending was principally attributable to patients' increased length of stay. In an Italian ED, the economic burden attributable to sample rejection because of hemolysis was €19,535 ($22,551) in 2012 (€17,484 [$20,183] for materials and €2,051 [$2,368] for personnel), accounting for 22.8% of the department's annual expenditure on blood sample collection.Currency converted from Euros to United States Dollars on 14th October, 2021 using exchange rates from Google Finance (https://www.google.com/intl/en/googlefinance/disclaimer/).23

Logically, interventions that reduce blood culture contamination rates should also decrease healthcare resource use and hospital spending. In the study by Hall et al.,52 enforcement of a strict sterile technique for PIVC insertion and blood draw decreased blood culture contamination rates, saving the hospital approximately $250,000 annually. Another publication presented a cost-benefit analysis evaluating use of the SteriPath ISDD for ED blood culture collection, compared with direct venipuncture.62 Use of the ISDD was associated with cost savings of $272 (3%) per blood culture in overall hospital costs, and $28 (5.4%) in direct-only costs. The main drivers of these cost savings were decreased contamination rates and reduced duration of antibiotic administration. In a Canadian institution, adoption of a manual ISDT was forecasted to yield savings of between $143,000 and $1,246,960 per year.55

Time Saved or Lost during the Blood Collection Pipeline

Nineteen publications reported data on the time spent waiting for an HCP to arrive to collect blood, the duration of the blood collection procedure, or the waiting time for recollection in the event of sample rejection. The only study to directly compare the duration of sampling between relevant devices revealed that blood collection by direct venipuncture took longer than from an existing PIVC (175.8 ± 229.6 vs. 104.5 ± 53.4 seconds; p = .053).31

Risk of Anemia

Another concern expressed in multiple studies was that repeated blood sampling in hospitalized patients can lead to anemia, and greater efforts should be made to minimize blood wastage during blood collection to safeguard patients' wellbeing.25,28 A study performed in a Canadian ICU established that on average, 5.5 ml of blood is wasted during each draw from a central venous line, compared with 6.3 ml from a PICC.25 In a US-based pediatric ICU, the mean volume of blood drawn in excess of laboratory requirements was 1.9 ml for centrally inserted central catheters, but only 1.2 ml for PIVCs, and 0.8 ml for direct venipuncture.28

Needlestick Injuries

Seven publications reported data on needlestick injuries (NSIs), with none comparing NSI rates between device types. Reported NSI rates varied widely; they were lower in studies involving HCPs from developed countries,54,56,57,63 but higher in less developed countries.64-66

Limitations

The SLR was associated with some limitations because of data availability or ambiguous results. For example, because VADs used for blood collection are almost always used for other purposes (e.g., the infusion of medicines/fluids), it is difficult to establish a causal relationship between blood sampling and specific complications observed in patients. Further research is required to quantify the isolated risks associated with collecting blood from VADs.

No studies directly compared the occurrence of NSIs between VAD and direct venipuncture blood sampling; research is required to quantify the number of NSIs that could be avoided by collecting blood from indwelling VADs. In addition, no quantitative self-reported data were identified on anxiety, fear, or stress during VAD blood sampling. Further research is required to confirm the impact of collecting blood from indwelling VADs on patient (and potentially also HCP) anxiety, fear, or stress, compared with direct venipuncture. It is expected that collecting blood through an existing VAD would be less stressful for patients; Twibell et al.67 have reported significantly lower distress scores (p < .001) for PIVC relative to direct venipuncture blood collection, although their study was not eligible for the present SLR because distress scores were not self-reported. In fact, observer-rated distress (particularly needlestick distress in babies) is a well-researched topic that could warrant a future SLR in itself. Another interesting topic for a future SLR would be the reliability of different blood collection techniques for determining drug concentrations in plasma.

Finally, many of the identified publications offered limited description of the devices used. Where no further details were provided, the term “venipuncture” was assumed to be synonymous with direct venipuncture, but the term “phlebotomy” was not. It is possible that some of the included publications were misclassified as reporting on direct venipuncture alone, when in fact multiple devices were used and data were pooled across device types.

Discussion

This review involved a comprehensive search of the literature drawing on over a decade of published research, six major electronic databases, a broad range of study designs and prespecified outcomes, and no geographical limitations. For the purposes of this review, PIVCs and midline catheters were treated as distinct devices, but only 3/141 publications explicitly mentioned the use of midline catheters, relative to 53/141 for PIVCs, highlighting the lack of published data on midline catheters. Similarly, very few publications were identified on implanted ports (5/141 publications).

The data indicate that blood sampling from VADs is common in EDs, trauma centers, and ICUs; in these departments, the number of blood samples drawn from VADs often exceeds that from direct venipuncture, sometimes with a ratio as high as 4:1.22-24 However, where blood is to be collected specifically for culture, blood draw from indwelling VADs is widely discouraged, because of fears over sample contamination.15,17,30 These recommendations are important for the quality of care and based on historic data suggesting that contamination rates are higher in blood culture samples drawn from VADs compared with direct venipuncture.

Several studies showed that hemolysis and sample contamination place a considerable economic burden on hospitals, because patients may be required to stay in hospital longer, awaiting repeat test results. According to the data gathered in this review, both can be reduced through adherence to strict blood sampling protocols and use of the ISDT. According to a 2018 survey, 40% of US hospitals did not practice the ISDT for peripheral draws or central line draws,30 so regional/national interventions may be required to encourage its uptake.

Limited evidence was available on novel, needle-free blood collection devices, but the two publications included suggest that hemolysis rates may be lower in PIVO-collected blood samples than in samples collected by more established techniques.12,34 Educational programs are also known to decrease contamination of blood cultures and hemolysis rates.68

Conclusions

Blood is already collected from VADs in everyday clinical practice; this approach is most common in EDs, trauma centers, and ICUs, where patients are more likely to have had VADs inserted for other purposes such as the administration of fluids and medicines. Hemolysis and sample contamination are far from inevitable in VAD-collected or venipuncture samples; both can be reduced through adherence to strict blood sampling protocols and utilization of the ISDT. Needle-free blood collection devices offer further hope for reducing hemolysis, although few studies have been published to date. No publications focused specifically on the DIVA population; the benefits of VADs are likely to be higher in these patients.

Implications

Selecting the most optimal approach for blood sample collection is essential for ensuring high-quality healthcare. Healthcare quality professionals and organizational leaders should prioritize the implementation of safe and effective blood collection practices by considering various quality-related factors, including patient experience, provider safety, and delivery of prompt/appropriate care.

Our findings will be useful to hospital administrators, clinicians, and researchers striving for improved patient experience and health outcomes. Adherence to appropriate blood sampling protocols and utilization of the ISDT can reduce hemolysis and sample contamination, which may enable timely medical interventions.

Where blood sampling through VADs is indicated, this approach could offer multiple advantages, including fewer NSIs for providers and reduced anxiety for patients (and potentially HCPs), although these benefits remain to be objectively quantified by empirical research. Novel needle-free technologies may present further improvements in provider safety and patient experience. These benefits of VADs and needleless solutions (i.e., PIVO) will likely be greater in patients with DIVA.
