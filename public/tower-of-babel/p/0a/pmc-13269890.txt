Privacy preserving digital platform for patient reported outcomes in inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Yuki Ohta, Takashi Taida, Sadahisa Ogasawara, Takuma Aizu, Takahito Konagaya, Mitsunori Ota, Ryoko Koborita, Ryuji Suzuka, Toshiyuki Ito, Yusuke Ozeki, Makoto Furuya, Nobuaki Shu, Ryosuke Horio, Ariki Nagashima, Wataru Shiratori, Yuya Yokoyama, Masato Nakamura, Kenichiro Okimoto, Keiko Saito, Tomoaki Matsumura, Tomoo Nakagawa, Minobu Shimazu, Yoichi Sakurai, Kensuke Yoshimura, Jun Kato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe developed a privacy-preserving digital platform for patient-reported outcomes in inflammatory bowel disease using secure multi-party computation (SMPC), which encrypts and fragments data to ensure complete anonymity. In a prospective multicenter study of 322 patients, 277 respondents (86.0%) completed the survey; 60.1% reported greater comfort in providing candid responses under anonymized conditions. The system revealed substantial underreporting: only 65.1% of patients experiencing clinically important symptoms (including urgency and other key concerns) discussed them with physicians; medication adherence discrepancies occurred in 28.9% of patients, with 68.0% unrecognized by clinicians. This approach captured previously inaccessible information about sensitive symptoms and socioeconomic burdens, enabling a more accurate understanding of real-world disease impact. Our findings suggest that SMPC-based anonymization may help reveal patient-reported information that is often underrepresented in routine clinical care. Introduction

Patient-reported outcomes (PROs) have significantly advanced clinical research by providing direct, patient-centered insights into disease and treatment impact1,2. These subjective assessments capture patient symptoms, treatment side effects, and well-being, complementing clinical endpoints and enhancing holistic treatment evaluation3. PRO incorporation has become increasingly vital in bridging clinical findings with patient experiences. Moreover, PROs now serve as key drug approval indicators, supporting comprehensive understanding of treatment effectiveness and patient-centered care4–6.

Recent technological advances have enabled the transition from paper-based PROs to electronic formats6. Compared to paper counterparts, ePROs offer significant advantages: real-time data collection, enhanced accuracy, and improved patient engagement. They align with increasing telehealth adoption and digital health interventions, enabling continuous remote patient monitoring7. However, ensuring patient privacy remains critical in ePRO implementation8. The digital nature inherently increases data breach risks, necessitating robust security measures including encryption, access controls, and authentication protocols9. Safeguarding patient information is essential for regulatory compliance and fostering patient trust. Prioritizing privacy and security encourages honest patient responses, ultimately enhancing ePRO data reliability and clinical utility10.

Another critical issue involves patients feeling hesitant to report experiences when responses are directly accessible to healthcare providers or researchers1,8,11. Social desirability bias represents a well-recognized phenomenon where patients report socially acceptable rather than authentic experiences11. This bias can skew results and undermine data validity12.

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), encompassing ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn’s disease, represents a particularly compelling context for evaluating anonymized ePRO systems. IBD is a chronic, relapsing condition significantly impairing quality of life, with distinct pathophysiology affecting young, digitally fluent individuals13–15. Symptoms such as urgency, abdominal pain, and medication nonadherence are often underreported during clinical encounters, potentially compromising clinical decision-making and treatment optimization. Thus, IBD provides a real-world setting in which to test whether an anonymized ePRO can surface these hidden patient needs and promote more accurate, bias-free data collection.

To address the challenges of privacy protection and response bias, we developed the first fully anonymized ePRO system based on secure multi-party computation (SMPC) and evaluated its feasibility within Far EAST 1000 (Features in Japanese patients with inflammatory bowel diseases: a multicenter prospective long observational study), a multicenter prospective observational study of IBD patients in Japan16,17. SMPC is a cryptographic framework that allows joint computation on encrypted data while keeping individual-level responses inaccessible to clinicians and researchers8,18,19. The objective of this study was to examine whether a privacy-preserving, SMPC-based ePRO platform can reveal patient-reported information that is often underrepresented in routine clinical care.

Results

For clarity, all question numbers referenced in this section correspond to the full list of 52 questionnaire items presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Study population and baseline characteristics

The Far EAST 1000 registry participants were approached for written renewed consent to participate in ePRO assessments using the ASAHI (A Secure network And Health registry system In Chiba for IBD patients). After obtaining consent, participants were required to configure the ePRO system on their smartphones. Of the 322 participants who provided consent (206 UC and 138 CD patients), 277 (86.0%) completed the initial survey following the first Short Message Service (SMS) invitation, and 173 (53.7%; 109 UC and 64 CD patients) completed the follow-up survey after receiving a second SMS invitation (Fig. 1a). The follow-up response rate (53.7%) was within a range commonly reported in registry-based ePRO studies and comparable to that observed in a large health-system implementation, where about half of clinics achieved PRO collection rates above 50%20,21. Following the initial SMS invitation for the second survey, 92 responses were received on day one. Response rates gradually increased over subsequent days, reaching a total of 173 responses by the end of the 21-day survey period. Notably, 53.2% of responses were obtained on the first day following the initial reminder SMS. Response rates showed modest increases on days when reminder SMS messages were sent (Fig. 1b).

The majority of participants were male (173 participants, 62.5%). Participants had a mean age of 44.2 years and an average disease duration of 12.1 years. Collecting sensitive patient information through anonymous ePRO

To examine the utility of the ASAHI system’s anonymous ePRO platform, we assessed its potential in facilitating the collection of sensitive patient-reported data. The ASAHI system’s anonymous ePRO platform was associated with favorable disclosure patterns: 60.1% of respondents reported greater comfort in providing candid responses (21.4% “much easier” and 38.7% “easier”) (Q51; Fig. 2a). Exploratory univariable analyses showed no major systematic differences across most clinical and demographic characteristics between patients who reported increased comfort and those who did not, although age was significantly associated with perceived comfort; this may partly reflect differences in digital familiarity (Supplementary Table 5). This enhanced openness enabled a more comprehensive assessment of various aspects of patients’ lives that they might hesitate to discuss during regular clinical visits.

The ASAHI system’s unique feature of ensuring anonymous responses allowed us to explore patient dissatisfaction and concerns beyond routine consultations. One key finding was that patients expressed significant dissatisfaction with long waiting times at hospitals. Overall, 33.6% of respondents reported dissatisfaction with waiting times, compared to only 19.1% who were satisfied (Q10,11). This dissatisfaction was particularly pronounced among patients who waited over 1.5 h, where 18.4% expressed dissatisfaction versus merely 1.8% who remained satisfied. Despite concerns about waiting times, satisfaction with consultation duration remained high, with 87.4% of respondents believing the allocated consultation time was sufficient (Q12,13, Fig. 2b). Notably, patients dissatisfied with long waiting times showed higher satisfaction when consultations lasted about 20 min. This suggests that while extended waiting remains a major challenge in IBD care, adequate consultation duration may partially offset dissatisfaction. A heatmap analysis indicated no significant association between waiting and consultation times (χ² = 13.1; P = 0.60) among patients dissatisfied with waiting and satisfied with consultation time. However, standardized residuals suggested a pattern whereby patients with long waits were more likely to express satisfaction when consultations lasted approximately 20 min or longer (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 3). Overall, 90.6% of respondents expressed their intention to continue visiting the hospital in the future, reflecting the importance of balancing waiting times and consultation quality to maintain patient satisfaction (Q14).

Next, we analyzed how patients disclose their condition and whom they turn to for advice. Mothers were most frequently informed, yet less often named as primary advisors; overall patterns were broadly similar across genders. Full distributions are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1 (Q20–21). Notably, 25% of patients expressed a desire for additional consultation partners beyond their outpatient physician, highlighting potential gaps in emotional and medical support (Q22). Defecation urgency emerged as a significant challenge, affecting over 40% of respondents during work, school, commuting, and traveling, with travel-related difficulties particularly prominent among patients aged 60 and above (Q34; Fig. 3a). This symptom had a marked impact on major life decisions, particularly job-seeking and travel planning (Q35; Fig. 3b). The socioeconomic impact was particularly evident, with 38.8% of working respondents (57 of 147) reporting annual incomes below 3 million yen (approximately 20,000 USD at an exchange rate of 1 USD = 150 JPY), considerably lower than the 2022 national average of 4.58 million yen (approximately 30,500 USD). Over half of the respondents (54.9%) believed that having IBD negatively impacted their income. A heatmap analysis revealed an association between younger age at disease onset and lower income levels, underscoring the long-term socioeconomic implications of early onset (Q47; Fig. 4a). Statistical testing confirmed a significant overall relationship between onset age groups and income categories (χ² = 29.76, df = 15; P = 0.013; see Table 4 in the Supplement). Additionally, younger-onset patients exhibited substantially longer disease durations compared to those with later onset, particularly among low-income respondents (<3 million yen), where the average disease duration reached 13.1 years in the 0–19 years onset group (Fig. 4a). To address potential confounding by current age, we stratified income distributions by disease onset before and after age 20 years (Fig. 4b). A pronounced disparity persisted: early-onset patients (<20 years) consistently showed higher proportions of low-income status across all current age groups, including those in their 40 s and 50 s. This pattern indicates that lower income reflects long-term socioeconomic disadvantage associated with early disease onset rather than current age alone. The absence of early-onset patients aged ≥60 years further underscores cumulative disadvantage over time.

Furthermore, the survey highlighted the presence of workplace stigma faced by IBD patients. Manifestations of experiential stigma included 67.1% of respondents going to work despite feeling unwell and 44.5% experiencing stress over taking sick leave. Anticipated stigma was also common, with 20.8% avoiding disclosure of their illness and 19.7% attending work out of fear of job loss (Q49). These findings underscore the fact that IBD patients face challenges beyond income-related issues, including social stigma, which contributes to their overall difficulty in daily life and work environments.

Integration of clinical and ePRO data reveals patient-physician perception gaps

Analysis of combined ePRO and clinical data revealed significant communication gaps between patients and physicians, particularly regarding symptom reporting and medication adherence. In UC, urgency of defecation is a crucial clinical symptom, yet it is often inadequately communicated during consultations. Among UC patients who considered defecation urgency important to report, only 65.1% had discussed it with their physicians. More concerning, among those who reported urgency, 68.5% perceived that physicians did not routinely inquire about this symptom during follow-up visits (Fig. 5a). Logistic regression analyses examining the association between reporting urgency and consultation duration (categorized in 5-min increments) revealed no significant relationship at any cutoff (P > 0.05).

Beyond symptom communication, medication adherence also revealed notable discrepancies between patient-reported data and physician awareness. The assessment of medication adherence through consecutive Visual Analogue Scale (VAS)-based questions uncovered additional gaps in patient-physician communication. These discrepancies were identified by integrating anonymized ePRO data with clinical information, including physician documentation and consultation duration. While 28.9% of patients showed a mismatch between their self-reported adherence and actual intake, physicians were unaware of this discrepancy in 68.0% of cases (Fig. 5b). Regarding reasons for medication nonadherence, the most frequently cited reason was forgetting to take the medication without a specific reason or time pattern (33.5%, 58 patients). Other common reasons included skipping doses due to irregular meal patterns (e.g., not having three meals a day) and forgetting doses, particularly at lunchtime. These results emphasize the importance of integrating anonymous ePRO data to address discrepancies between patient-reported experiences and physician perceptions, particularly in areas such as symptom severity and medication adherence.

Discussion

This study demonstrates the potential of the ASAHI system as an anonymized ePRO platform based on SMPC to capture patient-reported outcomes while minimizing bias. The aim of this study was not to demonstrate the superiority of anonymized ePRO systems over conventional approaches, but to examine whether a privacy-preserving platform can reveal patient-reported information that is often underrepresented in routine clinical care. Within this framework, the use of strict anonymization was associated with higher response rates and more candid reporting of symptoms, addressing a well-recognized challenge in IBD management. Embedding secure computing was also associated with increased patient trust and reduced social desirability bias, which is known to distort self-reported health information. Taken together, these findings suggest that privacy-preserving ePRO systems can help uncover aspects of patient experience that may not be readily captured in standard clinical encounters, thereby narrowing the gap between clinical assessments and lived patient experience and providing a more complete picture of real-world disease burden.

Because this was a measurement and visualization study rather than an interventional trial, no direct changes to individual patient management were made based on anonymized responses. As responses were intentionally de-identified, linkage to individual patients was not possible. However, aggregated and anonymized findings can inform clinical practice in several ways. The integration of anonymized ePRO data with structured clinical information enables stratified cohort-level analyses, allowing more granular and clinically meaningful evaluation of specific patient subgroups and facilitating the identification of systematically underrecognized symptoms or concerns, as well as informing quality improvement initiatives and refinement of routine clinical questionnaires. In addition, anonymized summaries may support reflective practice and educational feedback without compromising patient privacy. Together, these applications illustrate how privacy-preserving ePRO systems can enhance situational awareness.

Several approaches have been proposed to improve disclosure of sensitive patient-reported information, including patient portals, validated questionnaires, and surveys administered by third parties1,8,15. These methods can reduce social desirability bias by increasing perceived distance between patients and clinicians and have demonstrated value in routine care and research settings. The SMPC-based anonymized ePRO used in this study differs in that anonymity is not achieved through access restriction or administrative separation, but is guaranteed at the system level through cryptographic design. Individual responses cannot be re-identified by clinicians or researchers, even in principle. We therefore position SMPC-based anonymized ePROs not as a superior alternative to existing approaches, but as one option that maximizes privacy protection and may be particularly useful when addressing highly sensitive symptoms or behaviors.

This enhanced privacy protection introduces an inherent trade-off with individualized clinical actionability. Because the system is intentionally designed to preclude linkage to individual patient records, patient-level feedback and immediate personalized interventions are not possible. Accordingly, this approach does not replace individualized clinical decision-making. Instead, its value lies in identifying aggregate patterns of underreported symptoms and behaviors that may be systematically overlooked in routine care. These insights can be reintegrated into clinical workflows indirectly—for example, by informing revision of routine interview questions, highlighting symptom domains that warrant proactive inquiry, and guiding the design of follow-up assessments using non-anonymized PROs. In this way, anonymized ePROs complement personalized medicine by providing system-level insights while preserving strict privacy protection.

Despite therapeutic advances in IBD, communication gaps persist—especially around symptom reporting and medication adherence. ASAHI surfaced these gaps more effectively than routine consultations. For example, 92·7% of UC patients viewed urgency of defecation as important, yet only 65·1% discussed it with physicians, and 68·5% felt it was not consistently addressed. Additionally, 28·9% of patients showed discrepancies in medication adherence, with 68·0% of these unnoticed by physicians. These results, consistent with prior literature, suggest that anonymized ePRO systems may help identify long-standing physician–patient communication gaps23,24.

Beyond clinical symptoms, patients frequently withheld non‑medical concerns. While long wait times and socioeconomic difficulties were commonly reported via ePRO, only 11·6% raised them in clinical encounters. Although 33·6% expressed dissatisfaction with hospital wait times, 87·4% were satisfied with the consultation itself—underscoring that balancing operational and interpersonal aspects of care is central to patient experience25. ePROs offer a reliable channel for surfacing concerns that might otherwise remain unspoken, enriching the clinical dialogue with a fuller view of patient needs.

This study also identifies a robust association between early‑onset IBD and reduced income levels. Notably, disparities persisted into patients’ 30 s, 40 s, and 50 s, suggesting enduring socioeconomic disadvantage beyond youth. While Japan’s public financial support mitigates direct medical costs, employment challenges—such as reduced work participation or career limitations—may still compound the burden26. Workplace stigma further complicates disease management, as IBD patients frequently report concerns about disclosing their condition and its impact on job retention, consistent with earlier findings27. Addressing workplace accommodations and fostering openness around chronic conditions are essential steps toward improving quality of life.

The ASAHI system also demonstrated clear advantages in real‑time digital data capture. The initial survey achieved an 86·0% response rate, with 53.7% at follow‑up; notably, 53.2% of follow‑up responses arrived on the first day after the SMS reminder. These rates align with evidence that automated digital tools can improve engagement and data completeness in longitudinal settings28. At the same time, declining follow-up response rates highlight concerns about participant fatigue and accessibility. Human-centered interface design, personalized nudges, and device-agnostic access offer practical avenues for sustaining retention.

Beyond IBD, anonymized ePRO platforms have broad utility across clinical research and care delivery. A recent umbrella review supports the effectiveness of digital technologies in IBD management—particularly for engagement and adherence15—yet the core value of platforms like ASAHI is generalizable: privacy‑by‑design architectures that capture high‑fidelity patient input, link it to clinical records without exposing raw data, and support continuous, bias‑aware outcome assessment. As drug development increasingly incorporates patient‑reported measures, systems that minimize bias and strengthen data integrity will be pivotal.

Looking ahead, ePRO systems can help standardize patient‑centered assessment frameworks across chronic conditions—including diabetes, rheumatologic disease, and neurodegenerative disorders—while integration with artificial intelligence and digital health platforms may enable predictive analytics, early exacerbation detection, and personalized therapeutic adjustments. Notwithstanding these strengths, our study has limitations: decreasing follow‑up response rates raise concerns of potential selection bias29; the absence of explicit negative‑response options and technical issues (e.g., registration errors or contact changes) may have influenced participation. In addition, the lack of a non-anonymous ePRO comparator or pre-implementation baseline, as well as the absence of historical data within the same cohort to assess potential underreporting patterns, limits direct assessment of whether anonymization itself reduces underreporting, and cross‑sectional components restrict causal inference for socioeconomic associations. Analyses of disclosure gaps were limited to urgency of defecation and medication adherence. Data on other symptom categories, including more sensitive domains such as sexual or psychosocial concerns, were not collected in this study. Therefore, we did not compare disclosure gaps across symptom categories. This remains an important area for future study. Furthermore, as this study was conducted within a single-country cohort in Japan, differences in healthcare systems, sociocultural norms, and clinical practices may limit generalizability to other settings.

Even within these constraints, our findings provide compelling evidence that cryptographically anonymized ePROs can reduce bias and improve real‑world assessment of disease burden. By decoupling identity from clinical insight, such systems offer a scalable foundation for patient-informed, privacy-preserving healthcare. Expanding implementation—paired with usability improvements and equitable access—will be critical to closing the gap between clinical assessments and lived patient realities.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was conducted within the Far EAST 1000 cohort, a multicenter prospective observational study initiated in January 2019 to examine long-term disease activity, treatment strategies, and complications of IBD in Chiba Prefecture, Japan. The region’s stable population and integrated healthcare network enable standardized care and extended follow-up across 15 medical institutions, including five specialist hospitals with experienced IBD physicians16,17. As of September 2019, 728 patients had been enrolled, ensuring diverse representation and consistent treatment practices. For the present study, a subset of these patients provided additional consent to participate in an implementation experiment evaluating an anonymized electronic patient-reported outcome (ePRO) system.

Participants

Eligible participants were adults with a confirmed diagnosis of ulcerative colitis or Crohn’s disease enrolled in the Far EAST 1000 cohort. All participants provided renewed written informed consent to join this implementation study, which involved completing anonymized electronic patient-reported outcome (ePRO) surveys. During the consent process, patients were explicitly informed that their responses would remain inaccessible to their attending physicians and securely stored using a cryptographic framework based on SMPC—a privacy-preserving technology that enables encrypted data processing without exposing individual-level information. This explanation was provided in clear, patient-friendly language to ensure understanding and trust.

ASAHI system architecture

ASAHI is an innovative ePRO platform designed to securely integrate patient-reported outcomes collected via smartphones with clinical data using SMPC. SMPC enables encrypted data processing through secret sharing technology, which fragments patient-reported data into multiple encrypted “shares”19. These shares cannot reconstruct the original data individually, ensuring that even if one share is compromised, patient privacy remains intact. The same encryption method is applied to clinical data, allowing researchers to perform joint computations without decryption.

For this study, ASAHI utilized SmartPRO v2.0 as the ePRO interface and SeCIHI v1.2.3—a cloud-based SMPC platform developed by NTT DOCOMO BUSINESS, Inc. (Tokyo, Japan)—to enable high-speed processing and compliance with ISO/IEC 4922-2:2024 standards. SeCIHI enforces logical separation of registered data by organization and strict access control, permitting only authorized analysts to access aggregated statistical results. This architecture guarantees privacy preservation while enabling meaningful insights from integrated datasets.

This system addresses privacy concerns inherent in traditional ePRO collection methods (Fig. 6, upper section), where clinicians and analysts have direct access to individual responses. By securing patient data at every stage of processing, ASAHI enhances data protection, reduces response bias, and improves the reliability of patient-reported outcomes. Only aggregated results are accessible to authorized analysts, ensuring individual privacy while enabling meaningful insights.

ASAHI system for privacy-preserving electronic patient-reported outcome (ePRO) and clinical data integration via secure multi-party computation (SMPC).

Fig. 6 illustrates the ASAHI system’s secure data flow. A (upper section) shows conventional patient-reported outcome (PRO) collection, where direct access to individual patient data by healthcare providers and analysts poses privacy and bias risks. B (lower section) details ASAHI’s innovative approach. Patient-reported outcomes from smartphones and clinical data from hospitals are immediately encrypted and fragmented into multiple “shares” using SMPC technology. These encrypted shares are processed on the SeCIHI (Secure Computation and Information Handling Interface) cloud platform. Crucially, SMPC enables all computations on these encrypted shares without ever decrypting or reconstructing individual raw data. This guarantees complete patient privacy, prevents unauthorized access, and minimizes response bias. Only aggregated statistical results are made available to authorized analysts, ensuring meaningful insights while strictly preserving individual data privacy.

Data collection procedures

Multiple ePRO entries were collected from each participant over a defined period, with the system allowing on-demand additions as needed. These ePRO data were securely linked to corresponding clinical records in the Far EAST 1000 electronic data capture (EDC) system using the patient’s phone number as a key. Following ethics approval, renewed consent was obtained from cohort participants beginning October 31, 2022. After consent, participants configured the ASAHI ePRO system on their smartphones and received an initial questionnaire via SMS, which included a link to the web-based survey platform. Reminder messages were sent one and seven days later, and responses completed by March 1, 2023, were considered valid.

The first questionnaire assessed disease impact on daily life and work, availability of confidants, evaluations of outpatient care, routine interview content, and hospital visit duration. A second survey round was conducted on September 29, 2023, with expanded items, including EuroQol 5-Dimensions 5-Levels (EQ-5D-5L)22, urgency of defecation, medication adherence, and income data. EQ-5D-5L evaluates five dimensions of health-related quality of life—mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression—on a five-point scale, and includes a visual analogue scale (VAS) from 0 to 100 for perceived health status. This structured process ensured comprehensive assessment of patient-reported outcomes while maintaining secure integration with clinical data.

Data integration and analysis

All analyses were performed using SeCIHI v1.2.3 (Secure Computation and Information Handling Interface; NTT DOCOMO BUSINESS, Inc., Tokyo, Japan), a cloud-based SMPC platform compliant with ISO/IEC 4922-2:2024 standards19. ePRO datasets from both survey rounds were securely linked to clinical records from the Far EAST 1000 cohort through encrypted record matching. Analytical datasets were generated by merging encrypted tables on relevant keys, enabling cross-tabulations while preserving privacy.

Summary statistics were exported to R (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) for further analysis. Categorical variables were compared using chi-square tests with Monte Carlo simulation when expected counts were sparse. Ordinal variables (e.g., waiting time and consultation duration) were analyzed using proportional odds ordinal logistic regression to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Missing data handling

Missing data were primarily attributable to non-response in the follow-up survey (baseline response rate: 86.0%; follow-up: 53.7%). No imputation was applied; all analyses were conducted on complete cases. For baseline analyses, the denominator was the number of respondents completing the initial survey (n = 277). For follow-up analyses, the denominator was restricted to participants completing both surveys (n = 173). This approach aligns with established practices in registry-based ePRO studies reporting comparable response rates20,21.

Ethics and privacy

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Graduate School of Medicine, Chiba University (approval no. M10239), through a central institutional review board, and registered with the University Hospital Medical Information Network Clinical Trials Registry (UMIN000039131). All participants provided renewed written informed consent after receiving a clear explanation of the ASAHI system’s privacy features. Responses were encrypted and anonymized using SMPC, ensuring that individual-level data remained inaccessible to clinicians, researchers, and system operators. Two-factor authentication was implemented during registration to enhance security and data integrity. Robust safeguards prevented unauthorized external access; even if a single encrypted share were compromised, the original data could not be reconstructed.
