Progression‐Free Survival 2 as a Potential Surrogate for Overall Survival in Glioblastoma: Insights From a Spanish Multicentric Cohort
Authors: Jesús Yaringaño, Oriol Mirallas, Víctor Navarro, Diego Gómez‐Puerto, Gabriel Velilla, María Ángeles Vaz, Teresa Gorria, Álvaro Martínez‐Monino, Ainhoa Hernández, María Aguado, Antonio Di Muzio, Daniel López Valbuena, Fiorella Ruiz‐Pace, Macarena González, María Martínez‐García, Guillermo Villacampa, Carmen Balaña, Joan Carles, Rodrigo Dienstmann, Estela Pineda, Juan Manuel Sepúlveda, María Vieito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIn glioblastoma, first‐line treatment can influence subsequent treatment lines. Therefore, progression‐free survival 2 (PFS2), the time from initial randomization to progression on subsequent treatment or death, may better predict overall survival (OS) compared to PFS. However, this association needs validation in glioblastoma, particularly with novel targeted agents in early clinical trials.MethodsMedical records of glioblastoma patients from a multicentric Spanish cohort with genomic profiling treated between 2018 to 2023 were analyzed. Correlations between OS and PFS or PFS2 were calculated using an iterative multiple imputation approach and time‐to‐event endpoints with Kaplan–Meier methods.ResultsWe analyzed 405 patients. Median OS, PFS, and PFS2 were 25.4, 8.7 and 16.4 months, respectively. Correlation between OS‐PFS was 0.69 (95% CI, 0.62–0.75), whilst for OS‐PFS2 it was 0.83 (95% CI, 0.78–0.87). In patients who received targeted therapy, OS‐PFS correlation was 0.51 (95% CI, 0.02–0.81), whilst for OS‐PFS2, it was 0.72 (95% CI, 0.30–0.91). For patients with ESCAT tier I‐II molecular targets, OS‐PFS correlation was 0.69 (95% CI, 0.26–0.89), weaker than OS‐PFS2 (0.83 [95% CI, 0.29–0.97]). PFS2 also performed better than PFS in ESCAT III‐IV patients (0.80 [95% CI, 0.75–0.85] vs. 0.69 [95% CI, 0.61–0.75]) and in those without targetable alterations (0.86 [95% CI, 0.74–0.92] vs. 0.68 [95% CI, 0.51–0.80]).ConclusionsProgression‐free survival 2 (PFS2) is a more robust surrogate for OS than PFS in glioblastoma, including patients receiving targeted therapies or without actionable targets. These findings support adoption of PFS2 as a candidate surrogate endpoint in glioblastoma, offering a balance between trial feasibility and meaningful survival outcomes.Keywords: clinical trial, glioblastoma, molecular targeted therapy, progression‐free survival, surrogate endpoint

Introduction

Glioblastoma is the most common and aggressive primary brain tumor with almost unavoidable recurrence. Median overall survival is approximately 12 months, with limited effective treatment options, and less than 5% of patients alive at 5 years from diagnosis [1].

The standard approach for adult glioblastoma patients younger than 70 years is to achieve maximal safe surgical resection [2], followed by chemoradiotherapy, consisting of concurrent radiotherapy with temozolomide (TMZ) for 6 weeks, and finally six cycles of adjuvant TMZ [3].

In a systematic literature search performed by Oster and colleagues, only three of eleven trials conducted from 2005 to 2022 reported a statistically significant clinical benefit, including the EORTC/NCIC study (NCT00006353), our standard of care up to date [1, 3], the EF‐14 trial (NCT00916409), and the CeTeG (NCT01149109) trial. The EF‐14 trial evaluated the addition of tumor‐treating fields (TTFields) to TMZ maintenance with OS and progression‐free survival (PFS) benefit [4]. The CeTeG trial found that a lomustine plus TMZ combination had OS, but not PFS, benefit over standard therapy [5].

In the second line setting, the panorama is even more dire. Current guidelines recommend retreatment with TMZ in patients with initial benefit or salvage therapies such as bevacizumab and nitrosoureas. However, there is compelling evidence that targeted therapy in a subset of patients with actionable molecular alterations can lead to prolonged PFS in later lines of treatment. For example, the use of dabrafenib plus trametinib for recurrent or refractory high‐grade gliomas with BRAF V600E alterations, as demonstrated in the ROAR trial [6, 7]. These findings highlight the potential of precision medicine, but translating such results into broader clinical practice remains constrained by challenges in endpoint selection and trial design.

One critical barrier in glioblastoma clinical trials is the difficulty in assessing treatment efficacy due to disease‐specific factors. For instance, pseudoprogression, a phenomenon where treatment‐induced inflammatory changes mimic disease progression on imaging [8, 9], complicates the interpretation of outcomes. Pseudoprogression is particularly problematic when clinical deterioration prompts premature therapy changes [10]. Moreover, definitions of pseudoprogression and imaging timing vary widely across studies, lacking standardization [11, 12].

Similarly, reliance on objective response rate (ORR) as a primary endpoint is also challenging, especially in the first line setting, even if we switched to considering the post‐radiation MRI as the baseline. Achieving a complete response in the first line setting would require the disappearance of all radiation induced lesions and ORR might be influenced by the chosen therapy (e.g., anti‐angiogenic agents, which are known to reduce blood–brain barrier permeability that might alter the degree of contrast enhancement [13]).

Consequently, the surrogacy of endpoints like PFS and ORR remains inconsistent, but switching to OS would delay trial results and increase the number of patients that need to be included in exploratory trials that might be negative [14].

To address these challenges, alternative endpoints such as PFS2 have been proposed. PFS2 is defined as the time from first therapy start (or randomization if under clinical trial) until tumor progression on a subsequent regimen, or death from any cause. Recommended by the European Medicines Agency (EMA), PFS2 may overcome limitations associated with PFS and ORR when there are concerns that the choice of first‐line treatments will impact subsequent lines of treatment.

In this study, we evaluate the association between PFS2 and OS in a comprehensive glioblastoma cohort, exploring its potential role as a surrogate endpoint.

Methods

Population and Study Design

We conducted a multicenter retrospective analysis of clinical and molecular data from patients diagnosed with glioblastoma who underwent systemic treatment across eight Spanish centers between 2018 and 2023. All included cases corresponded to IDH‐wildtype glioblastoma according to the WHO 2021 classification. IDH‐mutant astrocytomas were excluded. Participating institutions included Vall d'Hebron University Hospital – Vall d'Hebron Institute of Oncology (VHIO), Catalan Institute of Oncology (ICO) Hospitalet, ICO Badalona, Hospital del Mar, Hospital Clínic de Barcelona, Hospital 12 de Octubre, Hospital de la Santa Creu i Sant Pau, and Hospital Ramon y Cajal.

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they had undergone next‐generation sequencing (NGS) tumor profiling at any of the participating centers. The main goal of performing NGS was to detect targetable mutations to enter a clinical trial or administer a molecularly matched targeted therapy. The study protocol was approved by the COMITÉ DE ÉTICA DE INVESTIGACIÓN CON MEDICAMENTOS of Vall d'Hebron University Hospital. Patients alive at the data cut‐off provided written informed consent.

Data were extracted directly from electronic medical records into a secure Redcap database. Variables collected included systemic therapies administered, tumor characterization, and survival status. Molecular alterations were classified according to the European Society for Medical Oncology Scale for Clinical Actionability of Molecular Targets (ESCAT) and the Magnitude of Clinical Benefit Scale (MCBS).

Outcome Endpoints and Statistical Methods

The primary objective was to define the correlation between OS and PFS2 in glioblastoma patients, compared with the correlation between OS and PFS. The secondary objective was to analyze these correlations in subsets of patients treated with targeted agents (according to the level of actionability of molecular targets) and non‐targeted therapies.

OS was defined as the time from treatment initiation (or randomization in clinical trials) to death from any cause. Patients alive at last follow‐up were censored at the date of last contact. PFS was defined as the time from treatment initiation (or randomization) to first documented tumor progression or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Patients without progression or death were censored at the date of last tumor assessment. PFS2 was defined as the time from treatment initiation (or randomization) to tumor progression on the subsequent line of therapy or death from any cause, whichever occurred first. Patients who did not receive a subsequent therapy and were alive at last follow‐up were censored at the date of last contact. Deaths occurring prior to the start of second‐line therapy were counted as events for PFS2. PFS3 was not evaluated due to increasing heterogeneity of later‐line treatments and limited data completeness in this retrospective cohort.

Real‐world survival data in clinical‐genomic databases are susceptible to biases. To mitigate the risk of immortal bias due to variations in patient entry times into the cohort based on reported NGS test results, all estimates were adjusted using a landmark analysis, with the NGS test date serving as the index date.

Time‐to‐event outcomes were calculated using Kaplan–Meier methods, with the log‐rank test employed for statistical comparisons. Univariate Cox proportional hazard models were applied to obtain hazard ratios (HRs) along with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and p‐values.

Correlations between OS and PFS/PFS2 were estimated using the iterative multiple imputation (IMI) approach implemented in the survcorr() function of the SurvCorr R package. Spearman rank correlations were calculated using the default 10 iterations of the algorithm, with a Gaussian copula to model the joint distribution of the survival times [15].

A complementary analysis was conducted based on the ESCAT classification for gliomas proposed by Mirallas O et al. [16], taking into consideration the recommendations published in the European Association for Neuro‐Oncology (EANO) guideline on rational molecular testing [17]. Correlations were analyzed for three subpopulations: patients with tier I‐II molecular alterations, those with tier III‐IV alterations, and those without actionable alterations. ESCAT tier V and X molecular alterations were not considered. Additional comparisons were performed between patients who received targeted therapy and those who did not.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.2. Results were considered statistically significant if p < 0.05.

PFS2 As a Potential Surrogate for OS

The median OS (mOS) for the entire cohort was 25.4 months (95% CI, 23.1–29.4). Median PFS was 8.7 months (95% CI, 7.9–10.5), while median PFS2 was 16.4 months (95% CI, 14.1–18.2; Figure 1). The correlation coefficient between OS and PFS was 0.69 (95% CI, 0.62–0.75), whereas OS and PFS2 showed a stronger correlation of 0.83 (95% CI, 0.78–0.87; Figure 1).

Axes represent the estimated cumulative distribution values Ϝ^(𝑡) for each endpoint (copula scale), and each point corresponds to an individual patient.

Among the 86 patients (21%) who participated in clinical trials, those receiving targeted therapy (n = 20) demonstrated a moderate correlation between OS and PFS (coefficient of 0.51; 95% CI, 0.02–0.81), which was lower than the correlation between OS and PFS2 (coefficient 0.72; 95% CI, 0.30–0.91). Similarly, in patients not receiving targeted therapy (n = 358), the correlation between OS and PFS was 0.69 (95% CI, 0.62–0.75), while the correlation between OS and PFS2 was higher at 0.84 (95% CI, 0.80–0.87; Figure 2).

Correlation of Progression‐free survival and Progression‐free survival 2 with Overall survival across subpopulations. Given the well‐known effect of anti‐VEGF agents on radiographic assessment, we performed a specific analysis of patients receiving bevacizumab. Bevacizumab use was restricted to the second‐line setting, where a subset of patients received bevacizumab either as monotherapy (n = 66) or in combination with irinotecan (n = 21), while the remaining patients did not receive anti‐VEGF therapy. In this subgroup, median progression‐free survival to bevacizumab alone or in combination with irinotecan was 5.1 months (95% CI, 3.7–6.8), median PFS2 was 12.2 months (95% CI, 11.2–15.6), and median overall survival (OS) was 17.4 months (95% CI, 15.9–22.3). The correlation between PFS and OS was modest (r = 0.30; 95% CI, 0.09–0.48), whereas the correlation between PFS2 and OS was substantially higher (r = 0.89; 95% CI, 0.83–0.93).

Discussion

This study represents the first cohort evaluating PFS2 as a superior predictor of OS compared to PFS in patients with glioblastoma. Consistent with prior literature, when used to determine the optimal first‐line therapy for these patients, PFS does not capture how frontline treatment impacts the efficacy and availability of subsequent lines of treatment [14, 18]. Combining additional active drugs might lead to superior PFS, but the impact of crossover in later lines in the control arm might lead to a negative confirmatory study [19, 20, 21]. This limitation underscores the need for endpoints like PFS2, which reflect the cumulative therapeutic impact across multiple lines of therapy.

In our cohort, PFS2 demonstrated a strong correlation with OS (0.83, 95% CI, 0.78–0.87). This result complements and extends findings from Chowdhury, et al. who calculated Spearman's rank correlation of OS and PFS2 across different solid tumor types [18].

Patients who present actionable molecular alterations can typically achieve prolonged benefit even when those treatments are initiated in later lines. Using PFS as the primary endpoint in such settings risks trial results being positive for PFS but negative for OS [14, 20]. Our findings suggest that PFS2 performance is better among patients who received targeted therapies in the presence of actionable molecular alterations, overcoming the bias caused by subsequent treatments and the possibility of crossover, two demonstrated factors that can distort the measured therapeutic impact of an investigational drug [22, 23].

The slightly lower OS–PFS2 correlation observed in patients receiving targeted therapies compared to the group of patients who did not receive them likely reflects greater treatment heterogeneity, smaller sample size (< 5% of total population), and prolonged post‐progression survival driven by highly active molecularly matched agents [23]. Notably, despite these factors, PFS2 consistently outperformed PFS as a surrogate for OS across all subgroups, supporting its robustness in precision oncology settings.

The advantage of PFS2 was particularly evident in patients who present BRAF V600E mutations, NTRK 1–3 fusions, BRAF fusions, FGFR 1–4 alterations, FGFR1‐TACC1 and FGFR‐TACC3 fusions. These alterations represent ESCAT tier I and II molecular alterations. By definition, tier I encompasses alterations with a matched drug that has demonstrated improved outcomes in prospective clinical trials. Meanwhile, tier II includes investigational alteration‐drug matches that are associated with antitumor activity, although the magnitude of benefit is yet to be determined [24].

Regardless of the expected longer survival of these patients and the use of different second‐line treatment options, PFS2 performed better than PFS. Moreover, PFS2 was consistently better in the group of patients with tier III‐IV molecular alterations and those without actionable targets, as well as in patients who did not receive targeted therapy as a first or second line.

Beyond molecularly matched therapies, anti‐angiogenic agents represent a distinct and clinically relevant source of bias for progression‐based endpoints in glioblastoma, as their effects on vascular permeability and contrast enhancement may not accurately reflect true tumor control [10, 12]. In this context, our analysis of patients treated with bevacizumab (n = 87) provides additional support for the validity of PFS2 as a surrogate endpoint. Despite the well‐recognized impact of anti‐VEGF therapy on early radiographic response, PFS2 retained a strong correlation with OS, markedly outperforming conventional PFS. This suggests that PFS2 is less susceptible to imaging‐related bias induced by anti‐angiogenic agents, likely because it integrates disease control across sequential lines of therapy rather than relying on transient radiographic changes [14, 18]. These findings are particularly relevant for recurrent glioblastoma trial designs, where anti‐VEGF therapies are frequently incorporated and may otherwise confound the interpretation of PFS‐based endpoints [23].

Although OS remains the gold‐standard endpoint in late‐phase glioblastoma trials [14, 23], PFS2 represents a pragmatic endpoint that captures the cumulative impact of sequential therapies. By incorporating both deaths occurring prior to second‐line progression and progression events on subsequent therapy, PFS2 allows earlier observation of clinically relevant events than OS. Although the total number of events required for statistical analyses remains similar, this earlier event accrual enables more timely assessment of treatment efficacy while maintaining a strong correlation with OS, which is particularly relevant in early‐phase trials [14, 18]. Even though PFS2 is more complex to capture than OS (due to increased risk of missing subsequent treatment line data), our multicenter experience demonstrates its feasibility in well‐curated real‐world datasets.

PFS2 exhibits four main advantages over PFS as a surrogate for OS. Firstly, PFS2 demonstrates a stronger correlation with OS, making it a better predictor of OS in clinical trials for advanced cancer (correlation moderate to strong according to previous reports) [23, 24]. Secondly, PFS2 is less affected than PFS by the impact of salvage therapies, including crossover and prolonged post‐progression survival, resulting in a stronger and more stable association with OS [23]. Thirdly, PFS2 has regulatory endorsement, being recommended by authorities such as the EMA [25, 26]. Lastly, there is only a modest increase in trial requirements over PFS (e.g., 4.3 additional months in follow‐up time) and sample size (12.7% more person‐years of follow‐up). When compared to OS, an additional 16.6 months and 65% increase in person‐years is required to achieve statistical significance [23, 27].

Although the increase is modest, PFS2 still requires longer follow‐up compared to PFS and adds logistical and financial burdens to clinical studies [27, 28]. Additionally, measuring PFS2 is more complex as it requires monitoring disease progression beyond the first‐line treatment. The variability of second‐line therapies and follow‐up procedures further complicate its applicability in trials [23].

Regardless of being endorsed by the EMA, PFS2 has not yet officially been recognized by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as a standard endpoint for oncology trials [29, 30]. Nevertheless, its use in the United States has gained some traction and there are nearly 170 ongoing trials that use PFS2, particularly later‐phase studies and those covering disease areas such as multiple myeloma, and lung, breast, ovarian, and prostate cancer [31].

Our study has some limitations. First, a selection bias is present since only patients who underwent NGS analysis of their primary tumor were included in the analysis. Some patients were screened for clinical trials, so their performance status and comorbidity profiles were better than for standard glioblastoma patients, explaining the longer survival, with mOS around 25 months. Additionally, data comparing the performance of both surrogate endpoints, such as worthwhile reductions in study follow‐up times and trial sample sizes, are unavailable due to the observational nature of our study. Importantly, this study does not constitute a formal surrogate endpoint validation, which would require meta‐analytic evaluation across multiple randomized trials.

Despite these limitations, to the scope of our knowledge, this study represents the first large‐scale evaluation of the association between PFS2 and OS in glioblastoma, including over 400 patients, which is a significant achievement given the rarity of this tumor. Our findings provide exploratory evidence supporting PFS2 as a candidate surrogate endpoint in glioblastoma. Furthermore, given the highly selected nature of our cohort, with 21% of patients participating in clinical trials, our findings may be partially generalized to clinical trial populations.

Conclusions

This study confirms a moderate to strong correlation between PFS2 and OS in glioblastoma patients, consistent with findings for other tumor types. PFS2 emerges as a valuable endpoint, particularly for patients eligible for targeted therapies and in clinical trial settings, where it effectively captures the therapeutic impact across treatment lines while minimizing biases from crossover and salvage therapies. Given its strong predictive value and endorsement by regulatory authorities, we support the inclusion of PFS2 as a secondary endpoint in future clinical trials, particularly those evaluating therapies for molecularly defined subgroups, to facilitate further validation of its potential as a surrogate for OS. Incorporating PFS2 could improve trial design, providing more accurate assessments of treatment efficacy and better aligning surrogate endpoints with long‐term survival outcomes.
