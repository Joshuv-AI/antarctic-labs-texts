Green Tea Extract, Keratin Hydrolysate and Vitamin C Exert Anti‐Ageing Effects Through the Hippo Signalling Pathway in Feline and Canine Mammary Gland Cells
Authors: Yi Gao, Xue Sun, Yan Ma, Jiaxi Li, Yunliang Li, Lei Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAgeing is a progressive decline in organ function and tissue integrity caused by genetic, environmental and physiological factors. With the increasing lifespan of companion animals, effective anti‐ageing interventions for elderly felines and canines have attracted growing attention in veterinary medicine.ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the anti‐ageing effects of green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C in feline and canine mammary gland cells and to explore the underlying molecular mechanisms.MethodsFeline and canine mammary gland cells were treated with green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C in vitro. Cell proliferation, oxidative stress responses and ageing‐associated protein expression induced by actinomycin D were evaluated. The involvement of the Hippo signalling pathway was further analysed to determine the molecular mechanisms underlying the anti‐ageing effects.ResultsGreen tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C significantly promoted the proliferation of feline and canine mammary gland cells. These treatments effectively restored the abnormal expression of ageing‐related proteins induced by actinomycin D and exhibited protective effects against oxidative damage. Mechanistically, the anti‐ageing effects were associated with regulation of the Hippo signalling pathway.ConclusionsGreen tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C exhibit significant anti‐ageing effects in feline and canine mammary gland cells through modulation of the Hippo pathway. These findings suggest their potential as safe and effective anti‐ageing interventions for improving health and alleviating geriatric syndromes in ageing companion animals.

Introduction

Cellular senescence is a multifaceted biological process whose fundamental characteristics were initially elucidated by Hayflick and Moorhead in 1961 (Hayflick and Moorhead 1961). Through continuous in vitro culturing of normal fibroblasts, researchers observed that these cells inevitably enter an irreversible growth arrest state after a certain number of division cycles. This phenomenon signifies the onset of cellular senescence, during which the physiological functions of the cells gradually decline, and they lose their potential for further proliferation (Mohamad Kamal et al. 2020; Ogrodnik 2021). In veterinary medicine, growing attention has been directed toward ageing‐related health issues in elderly pets, especially felines and canines, whose lifespan has increased with improved care. Understanding cellular ageing mechanisms in animals is therefore essential for advancing veterinary healthcare and developing effective anti‐ageing strategies. Cellular ageing can be broadly categorized into chronological ageing, which reflects the genetically programmed and time‐dependent decline in cellular function, and premature ageing, which is driven by external stressors such as oxidative stress, ultraviolet radiation and environmental pollutants. These environmental factors promote excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, leading to molecular damage and accelerating the senescence process. Oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction and chronic inflammation are major drivers of cellular senescence. Environmental stressors such as ultraviolet radiation and pollutants exacerbate these effects by increasing ROS production, leading to DNA, protein and lipid damage. Since green tea extract is rich in natural antioxidants like catechins and polyphenols, it can effectively counteract ROS‐induced oxidative injury and thereby slow down the ageing process (Head et al. 2012). In light of this, anti‐ageing drugs have been developed in humans, including rapamycin (Blagosklonny 2023) and β‐nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) (Hua et al. 2023). These drugs, to some extent, can attenuate the ageing process, but they are also associated with potential side effects. For instance, rapamycin may lead to immunosuppression and gastrointestinal discomfort, necessitating further research into its long‐term safety (Blagosklonny 2023; Hua et al. 2023). Geriatric syndromes, characterized by progressive functional decline, metabolic imbalance and increased susceptibility to chronic diseases, are becoming increasingly common among ageing companion animals. Given the complexity of cellular senescence and the need to address health issues or geriatric syndromes in cats and dogs, it is imperative to continue exploring and developing more effective yet safer anti‐ageing therapeutics. These advances hold the promise of improving the quality of life for pets during the ageing process.

Green tea extract is an active ingredient derived from green tea leaves, comprising primarily of tea polyphenols (catechins), caffeine, essential oils, minerals and vitamins (Hu et al. 2018). These components endow green tea extract with numerous significant benefits and effects (Mancini et al. 2017). As a key component, tea polyphenols effectively reduce blood lipid levels in hyperlipidaemia, restore and protect vascular endothelial function and serve as a natural antioxidant, clearing free radicals, inhibiting lipid peroxidation, delaying cellular ageing and possessing anti‐mutation and anti‐cancer potential (Bakun et al. 2023; Pérez‐Burillo et al. 2021; Miyata et al. 2019). Its anti‐inflammatory, antibacterial, detoxification, alcohol detoxification, liver protection and immune‐boosting effects are also highly regarded. Additionally, green tea extract exhibits anti‐ageing properties, inhibiting melanin synthesis and promoting collagen production to maintain skin youthfulness (Hsu 2005; Fei et al. 2017).

Keratin hydrolysate, a protein derived from animal hair, hooves and horns, can be converted into a form that is easily digested and absorbed by animals through a carefully designed enzymatic process (Mokrejs et al. 2017; Eslahi et al. 2013). This process not only retains the original nutritional value of keratin but also breaks down large molecular proteins into small molecular peptides and amino acids, making them more suitable for the nutritional needs of animals (Peng et al. 2020). Therefore, keratin hydrolysate has become a high‐quality protein source in pet food, providing essential nutrients to promote the healthy growth of pets (Tasaki 2020).

Vitamin C, a water‐soluble vitamin, participates in various metabolic processes in the human body (Padayatty and Levine 2016; Lykkesfeldt and Tveden‐Nyborg 2019). Due to its remarkable physiological functions and extensive application value, vitamin C has always been a focus of medical research (Padayatty and Levine 2016; Lykkesfeldt and Tveden‐Nyborg 2019). First, it enhances immune function by increasing white blood cell activity and promoting antibody production, playing a crucial role in preventing and treating infectious diseases (Carr and Maggini 2017; Wintergerst et al. 2006). Second, as a powerful antioxidant, vitamin C clears free radicals, reduces oxidative stress damage to cells, delays cellular ageing and significantly protects cardiovascular health against ageing (Mumtaz et al. 2021; Jeong et al. 2018; Di Tano and Longo 2020). It also promotes iron absorption, reduces cholesterol and even prevents cancer (Magrì et al. 2020). In veterinary contexts, vitamin C also contributes to immune regulation, collagen synthesis and antioxidant defence in animals, thereby supporting tissue repair and overall health during ageing (Gordon et al. 2020).

Here, we have explored the specific mechanisms and found that green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C exhibit anti‐ageing effects on feline and canine mammary cells, mediated by the Hippo pathway. This is a highly significant finding, and the novel physiological functions and potential applications of oxidative stress anti‐ageing substances warrant further investigation.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

The cell lines canine mammary PETCC#2478 and feline mammary PETCC#81 were obtained from the Cell Resource Center of the Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences, affiliated with the Chinese Academy of Sciences. These cells have undergone rigorous authentication via STR profiling and have been screened for mycoplasma contamination. Subsequently, they were propagated in a controlled environment, specifically maintained at 37°C with 5% carbon dioxide, to mimic physiological conditions. Both PETCC#2478 and PETCC#81 cells were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin (P/S), consistent with standard mammalian cell culture conditions. Among the pharmacological agents employed in this study are actinomycin D (MCE, HY‐17559), H2O2 (Merck, 7722‐84‐1), green tea extract (gift from Nourse Centre for Pet Nutrition), keratin hydrolysate (gift from Nourse Centre for Pet Nutrition) and vitamin C (MCE, HY‐B0166).

Cell Proliferation

To evaluate cell proliferation, the CCK‐8 assay was utilized, a widely recognized method for determining cell viability. Cells were seeded in 96‐well plates at a density of 1000 cells per well to ensure adequate growth conditions. Subsequently, the cell viability was quantified at four distinct time points, with 24‐h intervals between each measurement. This was achieved by adding the CCK‐8 reagent to the wells, which is converted by metabolically active cells into a coloured formazan product. The optical density (OD) of this product was then measured using a microplate reader at a wavelength of 450 nm, providing a quantitative assessment of cell proliferation over the experimental period.

Western Blot

Cells were initially rinsed thoroughly with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) and subsequently lysed in NP‐40 lysis buffer (consisting of 150 mM NaCl and 50 mM Tris‐HCl, pH 7.5), which was supplemented with 1% phosphatase inhibitor and 1% protease inhibitor, at 4°C for 40 min. Following lysis, the cell extracts were heated at 100°C for 15 min with SDS‐PAGE sample buffer to ensure protein denaturation. Subsequently, a standard Western blot analysis was performed to detect specific proteins. In brief, proteins were separated using electrophoresis on an SDS‐polyacrylamide gel with a concentration gradient ranging from 7.5% to 12.5% and then transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies specific to Actin (Proteintech, 81115‐1‐RR), YAP (ET1608‐30, HUABIO), pYAP (ET1611‐69, HUABIO), p21 (Proteintech, 10355‐1‐AP) and p51 (Proteintech, 60283‐2‐Ig). After three thorough washes with TBST, the membrane was incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Following another series of washes with TBST, the protein bands were visualized using a Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (Millipore) on a Chemiluminescent Imaging System (Tanon Science and Technology Co., Ltd). The resulting bands were analysed using a combination of chemiluminescence and densitometric techniques. All antibodies used in this experiment were obtained from commercial suppliers.

Statistics

Statistical evaluations were performed utilizing GraphPad Prism 8 software. Bar graphs illustrate the fold change or percentage relative to the control group, along with the standard deviation (SD) derived from triplicate independent experiments. For data that followed a normal distribution, Student's t‐test was administered. Conversely, for comparisons involving more than two groups, one‐way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test was applied. Additionally, for the assessment of mouse survival, the log‐rank test was utilized.

Results

Green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin c promote the proliferation of feline mammary gland cells and canine mammary gland cells: To investigate the potential effects of green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C on the in vitro proliferation of feline mammary PETCC#81 and canine mammary PETCC#2478 cells, we conducted an in vitro study, considering the reported anti‐ageing properties of these substances. Using the CCK‐8 method to quantitatively assess cellular proliferation, we systematically tested the effects of these substances at different concentrations. For green tea extract, we selected several concentration gradients (10, 50, 200 and 800 µg/mL) to treat the target cell lines. The experimental results showed that when the concentration of green tea extract was 200 µg/mL, it had the most significant promotion effect on the in vitro proliferation of PETCC#81 and PETCC#2478 cells (Figure 1A). Similarly, we tested the effects of keratin hydrolysate at multiple concentrations (10, 20, 40 and 80 mg/mL) on the target cell lines. The results indicated that when the concentration of keratin hydrolysate was 20 mg/mL, it exhibited the strongest promotion effect on the in vitro proliferation of both cell lines (Figure 1B). Moreover, we evaluated the effects of vitamin C at several concentrations (1, 2, 3 and 5 mmol/L). Notably, vitamin C at a concentration of 3 mmol/L exhibited the strongest promotion effect on the in vitro proliferation of PETCC#81 and PETCC#2478 cells (Figure 1C). Based on the above experimental data, we can conclude that green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C can promote the in vitro proliferation of feline mammary PETCC#81 and canine mammary PETCC#2478 cells at certain concentrations. These findings provide valuable references for further research on the applications of these substances in the fields of anti‐ageing and cell biology.

Green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C can partially restore the impaired proliferation of feline and canine mammary gland cells that is caused by actinomycin D: Given numerous reports indicating that actinomycin D can induce cellular senescence (Petrova et al. 2016; Minieri et al. 2015), we designed an experiment to investigate whether green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C can mitigate this effect. Specifically, we induced senescence in PETCC#81 and PETCC#2478 cells using actinomycin D and concurrently administered the three substances mentioned above to evaluate their potential protective effects. The experimental results demonstrate that green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C are all capable of partially restoring the reduced in vitro proliferation ability of PETCC#81 and PETCC#2478 cells induced by actinomycin D (Figure 2A–C). This discovery indicates that these three substances can attenuate the cellular senescence effects caused by actinomycin D, potentially possessing anti‐ageing properties.

Green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C can restore the upregulated protein levels of ageing markers in feline and canine mammary gland cells induced by actinomycin D: Given that p53 and p21 have been widely reported as closely related markers of cellular senescence, we conducted experiments on PETCC#81 and PETCC#2478 cells to examine whether the protein levels of these two senescence markers change after treatment with green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C. The experimental results revealed that the protein levels of p53 and p21 in PETCC#81 and PETCC#2478 cells treated with green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C exhibited a downwards trend (Figure 3A–C). To further validate the potential impact of these substances on the ageing process, we designed another experiment where actinomycin D was added to PETCC#81 and PETCC#2478 cells to induce cellular senescence, while concurrently administering green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C. The objective was to observe whether the protein levels of p53 and p21 could be restored. The experimental results demonstrated that these three substances significantly reversed the upregulation of senescence marker protein levels in PETCC#81 and PETCC#2478 cells induced by actinomycin D, thus verifying their potential intervention in the cellular ageing process (Figure 3D–F). In summary, green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C may exert a positive influence on the cellular ageing process by regulating the expression of senescence‐related proteins such as p53 and p21, providing novel insights for anti‐ageing research and treatment.

Green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C have significant protective effects against oxidative damage in feline and canine mammary gland cells: To evaluate the protective effects of green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C on PETCC#81 and PETCC#2478 cells in an H2O2‐induced oxidative damage model, 500 µmol/L H2O2 was used to treat the cells for 24 h, simulating an oxidative stress state (Figure 4A). Subsequently, we examined whether these substances could mitigate the cellular damage induced by this process. The experimental results indicate that green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C all exhibit significant protective effects against the damage caused by oxidative stress in PETCC#81 and PETCC#2478 cells (Figure 4B–D). This finding provides scientific evidence for the application of these three substances in the field of cellular oxidative stress protection.

The anti‐ageing effects of green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C on feline and canine mammary gland cells are dependent on the hippo pathway: Given that the Hippo signalling pathway balances cell proliferation and apoptosis by regulating the activity of its key molecule YAP, this process is closely linked to the ageing process of organisms. Through our western blot experiments, we observed that in PETCC#81 and PETCC#2478 cells treated with green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C, the protein levels of YAP did not show significant changes, while the protein levels of P‐YAP were significantly reduced (Figure 5A–C). To further explore this mechanism, we designed another experiment where (Sharpless and Sherr 2015) we added actinomycin D to PETCC#81 and PETCC#2478 cells to mimic cellular senescence and simultaneously administered the three substances of green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C to observe the changes in YAP and P‐YAP protein levels. The experimental results demonstrated that these three substances were able to reverse the elevation of P‐YAP induced by actinomycin D (Figure 5D–F). Furthermore, to verify whether the anti‐ageing effects of these substances depend on YAP activity, we performed Hippo pathway interference experiments using YAP siRNA. PETCC#81 and PETCC#2478 cells were divided into three groups: NC, treatment alone (green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate or vitamin C) and treatment combined with si‐YAP. Western blot analysis revealed that each of the three substances markedly reduced the protein level of the senescence marker p21, whereas YAP knockdown abolished this effect and restored p21 expression to levels comparable to the NC group (Figure 5G–I). Collectively, the findings indicate that the anti‐ageing actions of green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C in PETCC#81 and PETCC#2478 cells may be mediated through modulation of the Hippo signalling pathway. This finding provides a new perspective and potential therapeutic targets for future research in anti‐ageing science.

Discussion

In the process of animal ageing, many pets experience a gradual decline in physiological functions and increased disease susceptibility, drawing significant attention to the issue of pet ageing (Landsberg and Araujo 2005; Landsberg and Ruehl 1997). To address these challenges, researchers have developed various drugs aimed at extending pets' lifespans and improving their health (Landsberg and Araujo 2005; Landsberg and Ruehl 1997). However, while these drugs provide certain benefits, they may also cause side effects. For instance, some antioxidants effectively scavenge free radicals and delay cellular ageing, but excessive use may lead to redox imbalance, affecting normal metabolism (Sadowska‐Bartosz and Bartosz 2014). Likewise, hormone‐based drugs can promote growth and metabolism, yet prolonged use may induce endocrine disorders or immunosuppression (Merriam et al. 2003). Therefore, in addressing pet ageing, it remains essential to develop safer and more effective drugs targeting the key mechanisms of ageing.

Green tea extract offers numerous potential benefits for pet cats and dogs based on our research findings, especially as they enter their senior years and often face various geriatric health challenges such as cardiovascular issues, joint wear and tear and cognitive decline. As it has been reported in humans, various active components in green tea extract, including catechins and tea polyphenols, have been proved effective in addressing geriatric symptoms (Pérez‐Burillo et al. 2021). Specifically, catechins and tea polyphenols in green tea extract possess powerful antioxidant properties, helping to eliminate free radicals in the body, slow down cellular ageing and protect cardiovascular and cerebrovascular health (Fei et al. 2017). Studies have indicated that green tea extract can inhibit viral replication and decrease ROS generation during Feline Herpesvirus type‐1 (FHV‐1) infection (Longobardi et al. 2024). Incorporating green tea extract as a functional ingredient in pet food or nutritional supplements may potentially provide natural health protection for pets and help improve age‐related physiological decline, thereby enhancing their quality of life. This discovery brings new treatment strategies to the field of pet health and undoubtedly serves as an important boon for pet owners who prioritize healthy and natural lifestyles for their pets.

Recent studies have suggested that keratin hydrolysate, beyond its role as a nutritional supplement, may possess additional physiological functions (Mokrejs et al. 2017). In ageing pets such as cats and dogs, keratin hydrolysate‐derived peptides and amino acids have been reported to exhibit antioxidant and immunomodulatory bioactivities, which could support skin repair and joint health (Eslahi et al. 2013; Tasaki 2020). In our study, keratin hydrolysate showed a significant anti‐ageing effect on feline and canine mammary cells, as evidenced by enhanced cell proliferation and protection against oxidative damage. Therefore, incorporating keratin hydrolysate as a potential functional ingredient in pet food may provide essential nutrients while supporting healthy ageing in pets.

Studies have shown that vitamins play a crucial role in the anti‐ageing process. As antioxidants, they can neutralize free radicals and reduce cellular damage, thereby delaying skin ageing and enhancing skin elasticity (Mumtaz et al. 2021). Studies have shown that vitamin C, often in combination with vitamin E and β‐carotene, enhances antioxidant capacity, reduces oxidative stress and supports cellular protection in cats and dogs (Longobardi et al. 2024; Yu and Paetau‐Robinson 2006). In this study, we observed that vitamin C exhibited significant anti‐ageing effects on feline and canine mammary cells. Specifically, vitamin C promoted the in vitro proliferation of these cells, effectively restored the abnormal increase in protein levels of ageing markers induced by actinomycin D, and also exhibited excellent protection against oxidative damage. These findings further confirm that vitamin C, as one of the “old drugs with new uses”, will play an increasingly important role in ensuring pet health and extending their lifespans.

The regulation of the Hippo signalling pathway on cell proliferation and apoptosis is closely linked to the ageing process (Yeung et al. 2019). As organisms age, changes in the activity of the Hippo pathway may affect the balance between cell proliferation and apoptosis, thus accelerating ageing. As a key molecule in the Hippo pathway, YAP's activity is regulated by the Hippo pathway and has a significant impact on cellular senescence (Elster and von Eyss 2020). Therefore, the Hippo signalling pathway is crucial in understanding the mechanisms of ageing and developing anti‐ageing strategies. The anti‐ageing effects of green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C on feline and canine mammary cells appear to involve regulation of the Hippo pathway, as evidenced by decreased phosphorylation levels of YAP (P‐YAP) in treated cells. Specifically, these substances reduced P‐YAP levels in feline and canine mammary cells. Additionally, during the process of cellular senescence induced by actinomycin D, YAP protein undergoes phosphorylation, and green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C can restore this effect. Our study reveals that the specific mechanisms of anti‐ageing effects of these substances may be related to the regulation of the Hippo signalling pathway, providing new insights and strategies for anti‐ageing treatments in pets.

In summary, through in‐depth research on the anti‐ageing effects of green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C, we aim to develop a more comprehensive and effective anti‐ageing solution for pets, helping them delay ageing while maintaining vitality and overall health. Nevertheless, this study was primarily conducted in vitro using feline and canine mammary gland cells. Future studies are needed to further validate these findings through in vivo experiments and clinical trials in companion animals. In particular, evaluating the long‐term safety and efficacy of green tea extract, keratin hydrolysate and vitamin C as functional ingredients in pet foods or nutritional supplements will be important for translating these findings into practical applications. Such investigations may provide a scientific basis for developing novel dietary strategies to promote healthy ageing in companion animals.
