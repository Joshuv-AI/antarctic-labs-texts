Lysyl-phosphatidylglycerol promotes cell-to-cell contact and biofilm formation of Staphylococcus aureus as a biofilm matrix component
Authors: Shinya Sugimoto, Keiichiro Hara, Yoshitaka Taketomi, Yuki Nagasaki, Chikara Sato, Makoto Murakami, Yuki Kinjo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStaphylococcus aureus biofilms contribute significantly to persistent infections and antibiotic resistance, supported by a complex extracellular matrix. While their proteins, polysaccharides, and extracellular DNA have been well studied, the role of phospholipids in biofilm architecture remains underexplored. Here, we identify extracellular phospholipids within the biofilm matrix, particularly lysyl-phosphatidylglycerol (Lys-PG), as critical structural elements in S. aureus biofilms. Bacterial phospholipase A1 (PLA1), which hydrolyzes phospholipid acyl ester bonds, effectively dispersed pre-formed biofilms and prevented biofilm formation by hydrolyzing extracellular phospholipids, without affecting bacterial growth or exhibiting cytotoxicity. Microscopic analyses revealed that PLA1 disrupts membranous nanostructures integral to biofilm stability. Lipidomic analysis demonstrated an enrichment of Lys-PG with specific fatty acid species within the biofilm matrix and confirmed their hydrolysis by PLA1. Mechanistically, Lys-PG promotes bacterial aggregation by acting as a molecular glue through electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions. Deletion of mprF, responsible for Lys-PG synthesis, markedly impaired biofilm formation. These findings uncover a previously unrecognized structural role of extracellular phospholipids in biofilm architecture and suggest that targeting Lys-PG and its biosynthetic pathway represents a promising strategy for biofilm control.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Biotechnology, Microbiology

Introduction

Biofilms are intricate communities of microorganisms that adhere to both biotic and abiotic surfaces, embedded within a matrix of self-produced extracellular polymeric substances such as proteins, polysaccharides, and nucleic acids1. While biofilms offer benefits in environmental and industrial processes like carbon, nitrogen, and mineral cycling, wastewater treatment using active sludge, and food and beverage fermentation2, they also pose significant challenges. These include contributing to metal corrosion in water systems, contaminating food supplies, and creating persistent biofilm-associated infections in healthcare3. Biofilm formation on medical devices is especially concerning, as it can lead to chronic infections that are difficult to treat due to the tolerance of biofilms to antibiotics and evasion of the host immune system4. Consequently, there is a growing need for innovative strategies to control and eliminate biofilms across various scientific and technological domains.

Staphylococcus aureus is a prominent opportunistic pathogen frequently linked to biofilm-associated infections, particularly in clinical settings5. Around 30% of healthy individuals carry S. aureus, making them more susceptible to surgical site infections6. Moreover, S. aureus is a well-known food-borne pathogen, responsible for infections transmitted through contaminated food7. Understanding how S. aureus forms biofilms is crucial for developing effective approaches to address these biofilm-related challenges. In our previous research, we developed methods for isolating and characterizing matrix components from S. aureus biofilms using high-salt solutions8,9. Our studies revealed that extracellular DNA (eDNA) is a predominant component of the matrix in S. aureus biofilms, while only a few strains form biofilms through polysaccharide intercellular adhesin, also known as poly-N-acetylglucosamine (PNAG)10. We have further demonstrated that various biofilm matrix components, including secreted proteins, cell wall-anchored proteins, PNAG, and environmental RNA (envRNA), work both cooperatively and redundantly11,12. However, the full composition of the biofilm matrix in S. aureus remains incompletely understood, and identifying previously unknown components could provide critical insights for new biofilm control strategies.

Phospholipids are essential biological molecules that function as major components of cellular membranes, efficient energy storage molecules, lipid-signaling mediators, and structural barriers13–15. In S. aureus, the membrane phospholipid profile typically consists of 40–70% phosphatidylglycerol (PG) and 30–55% lysyl-phosphatidylglycerol (Lys-PG), both containing saturated and branched-chain fatty acids16. Cardiolipin (CL), a diphosphatidylglycerol, is present in smaller amounts (4–9%)17. Beyond their intracellular roles, phospholipids are also components of extracellular, membrane-derived nanostructures such as membrane vesicles (MVs) and interbacterial nanotubes, which are involved in intercellular communication and host interactions18–20. MVs, in particular, have been implicated in biofilm formation, as their hydrophobicity aids in forming intercellular junctions within biofilms21. In addition, imaging mass spectrometry analysis was performed to assess phospholipid distribution, revealing complex and heterogeneous lipid patterns across the layers of the S. aureus biofilm22. Despite these insights, the composition and function of extracellular phospholipids within the biofilm matrix of S. aureus remain largely unexplored.

In this study, we identified phospholipids within the biofilm matrix of S. aureus by lipidomic analysis. Treatment with microbial phospholipase A1 (PLA1) prevented biofilm formation and dispersed pre-formed biofilms in various laboratory and clinical strains of S. aureus. Notably, Lys-PG, one of the abundant phospholipids identified in the biofilm matrix, promoted bacterial cell aggregation by acting as a molecular glue. These findings suggest that phospholipids have a previously unrecognized “moonlighting” function in biofilms, where they not only serve their traditional roles in cell membranes but also play a critical structural role in biofilm architecture.

Results

Membranous extracellular nanostructures in S. aureus biofilms

Membranous extracellular nanostructures, such as nanotubes and MVs, have been observed in biofilms of various bacterial species18,19,23,24 and are thought to contribute to biofilm formation and development. In this study, we examined whether similar extracellular nanostructures are present in S. aureus biofilms using multiple microscopy techniques.

First, we employed confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) in combination with the iCBiofilm method, a recently developed biofilm-clearing technique, to observe thick biofilms25. We examined biofilms formed by the S. aureus strain MR23, which is known for producing robust and thick biofilms and has been frequently used in our previous studies8–12,26. The MR23 biofilm was stained with the lipophilic fluorescent probe FM1-43, which labels both the cytoplasmic membrane and extracellular membranous structures. As expected, MR23 formed a dense and rugged biofilm (Fig. 1A), consistent with prior reports11,25. Notably, at the base of the biofilm, numerous extracellular spherical nanostructures were observed (Fig. 1B).

Next, we used atmospheric scanning electron microscopy (ASEM) to visualize these extracellular nanostructures at higher resolution. ASEM allows for imaging biofilms in an aqueous environment, avoiding dehydration artifacts common with traditional electron microscopy techniques23,27. In agreement with the CLSM findings, we observed many extracellular spherical nanostructures of varying sizes (Fig. 1C). Additionally, filamentous structures connecting these spherical particles were also visualized by ASEM (Fig. 1C).

Finally, we employed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) for the highest resolution imaging of these membranous nanostructures. Thin sections of MR23 cells, harvested from the biofilm, revealed the presence of extracellular MVs (Fig. 1D, E), consistent with previous reports23. Intracellular MVs were also observed in dead cells (Fig. 1F).

Together, these results indicate the presence of various membranous nanostructures, including phospholipid-containing vesicles and filaments, in S. aureus biofilms and suggest that they may contribute to biofilm stability and development.

Phospholipases inhibit biofilm formation and disperse mature biofilms in various S. aureus strains

To investigate the role of extracellular phospholipids in biofilm development, mature biofilms formed by selected strains of S. aureus, which display distinct biofilm characteristics8–10, were treated with various phospholipases. The cleavage sites for the phospholipases used in this study are shown in Fig. 2A. Phospholipases hydrolyze phospholipids into free fatty acids and lysophospholipids and are classified into four groups—A, B, C, and D—based on their catalytic reactions28. PLA enzymes hydrolyze the acyl ester bond of phospholipids at either the sn-1 (PLA1) or sn-2 (PLA2) position of the glycerol backbone. In contrast, PLC cleaves the phosphodiester bond to generate diacylglycerol and a phosphate-containing head group, whereas PLD cleaves the bond distal to the phosphate, producing phosphatidic acid and an alcohol.

Among the enzymes tested, only microbial PLA1 effectively dispersed pre-formed biofilms of the S. aureus strains MR4, MR11, and MR23, which rely on eDNA and/or proteins for biofilm stability8–10. In contrast, neither PLC nor PLD exhibited detectable dispersal activity (Fig. 2B). Compared with MR4 and MR11, MR23 biofilms showed slightly greater resistance to PLA1 treatment, likely reflecting their stronger reliance on protein components for stability10,11. PLA1 dispersed the pre-formed biofilm of MR4 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2C), and its biofilm dispersal activity was significantly inhibited by 2 mM CuSO4, which inhibits PLA1 by direct interaction with the enzyme29 (Fig. 2D). These results indicate the importance of PLA1 enzymatic activity for biofilm dispersal. Next, we evaluated bacterial viability following PLA1 treatment by measuring colony-forming unit (CFU) counts. After a 2-h incubation of MR4 biofilms with PLA1, there was no significant reduction in CFUs among the total bacterial population, including both planktonic and biofilm-associated cells (Fig. S1A). In contrast, CFUs of biofilm-associated cells alone decreased after PLA1 treatment, likely due to biofilm dispersal (Fig. S1B). These findings suggest that PLA1 does not directly compromise bacterial membrane integrity but instead preferentially acts on extracellular phospholipids within the biofilm matrix.

In addition to dispersing mature biofilms, PLA1 also inhibited biofilm formation by the S. aureus strains MR4, MR11, and MR23 (Fig. 2E). Although slight promotion of bacterial growth in MR4 and MR11 and inhibition in MR23 were observed following PLA1 treatment (Fig. 2F), these effects were minimal compared to the marked inhibition of biofilm formation. While bacterial PLD did not show biofilm dispersal activity against the MR23 biofilm (Fig. 2B), it was effective at inhibiting biofilm formation when applied from the start (Fig. 2E). In contrast, PLC showed no detectable inhibitory effect under the conditions tested.

Consistent with these findings, PLA1 prevented biofilm formation in the commonly studied S. aureus strains SH1000 and Newman30,31 without affecting their growth and efficiently dispersed their mature biofilms (Fig. S2). These observations indicate that the biofilm-disruption and inhibition activities of PLA1 are not strain-specific.

For biofilm control applications, enzyme cytotoxicity is a key concern. Notably, none of the tested enzymes, except PLD, exhibited hemolytic activity (Fig. 2G), indicating that PLA1 may be safely used in biotechnological and other practical settings.

PLA1 disrupts extracellular nanostructures in biofilms

To investigate the mechanism underlying the anti-biofilm activity of PLA1, we observed S. aureus biofilms and membranous extracellular nanostructures before and after PLA1 treatment. CLSM showed that untreated MR4 biofilms were dense and thick (Fig. 3A), but after PLA1 treatment, only a thin layer of biofilm remained on the glass surface (Fig. 3A). Field emission-scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM), which enables high-resolution imaging, showed nanospheres located at the interfaces between S. aureus cells within the biofilm, these structures were absent following PLA1 treatment (Figs. 3B and S3).

Collectively, these findings suggest that PLA1 preferentially targets extracellular phospholipids in the biofilm matrix, thereby disrupting biofilm integrity and leading to both inhibition of biofilm formation and dispersal of pre-formed biofilms.

Presence of phospholipids in the matrix of S. aureus biofilms

The presence and role of phospholipids in the matrix of S. aureus biofilms have remained poorly defined. To address this, we isolated the biofilm matrix from the MR4 strain using a Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0) containing 1 M NaCl, following an established protocol (Fig. 4A)8. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) analysis of the isolated matrix revealed three major phospholipids: PG, Lys-PG, and CL, all of which are known components of the S. aureus membrane (Figs. 4B and S4)16,17. Notably, these phospholipids were no longer detected after treatment with PLA1, suggesting that they contribute to the structure and stability of the biofilm and are susceptible to enzymatic cleavage, potentially facilitating biofilm dispersion and inhibition.

To further define the phospholipid composition of the biofilm matrix and bacterial cell membrane, we conducted a detailed lipidomic analysis. Comparisons between the biofilm matrix and S. aureus cells revealed that PG was the most abundant phospholipid in both contexts (Fig. 4C). However, CL and Lys-PG were slightly enriched in the biofilm matrix, increasing from 13% and 10% in bacterial cells to 32% and 13% in the biofilm matrix, respectively (Fig. 4C).

We then analyzed the fatty acid acyl-chain profiles of PG, Lys-PG, and CL. In bacterial cells, the predominant PG species was C16:0-18:1 (50.0%), while the most abundant Lys-PG species was C15:0-15:0 (29.2%) (Figs. 4D, E and S5A, B). In the biofilm matrix, Lys-PG species such as C18:0-15:0 (20.4%), C16:0-15:0 (6.1%), and C15:0-18:0 (5.7%) were enriched (Figs. 4E and S5B). In contrast, PG species showed no obvious enrichment in the matrix (Figs. 4D and S5A). For CL, we focused on species containing C15:0, a major fatty acid in PG and Lys-PG, because quantitative analysis of other CL species was limited by analytical sensitivity. Enrichment of specific CL species, including CL (C63:0-15:0), CL (C64:0-15:0), CL (C62:0-15:0), and CL (C61:0-15:0), was observed in the biofilm matrix (Figs. 4F and S5C).

Finally, we investigated the effects of PLA1 treatment on the fatty acid acyl chains of these phospholipids in the isolated biofilm matrix. PLA1 effectively cleaved various PG, Lys-PG, and CL species, independent of fatty acid composition, demonstrating broad activity toward phospholipid substrates (Figs. 4G–I and S6).

In summary, our lipidomic analysis highlights an enrichment of specific Lys-PG and CL species in the S. aureus biofilm matrix. These phospholipids are susceptible to enzymatic degradation by PLA1, offering potential insights into mechanisms underlying biofilm disruption and inhibition.

Lys-PG induces S. aureus cell aggregation through electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions

Given the presence of nanospheres at the interfaces between S. aureus cells within biofilms (Fig. 3B), we hypothesized that extracellular phospholipids may act as molecular glues, linking bacterial cells. To explore this, we tested the effects of the three major phospholipids identified in our TLC and lipidomic analyses on S. aureus cell aggregation. In tube-based aggregation assays, Lys-PG (C18:1) induced cell aggregation in a dose-dependent manner, whereas PG (C18:1) and CL (C18:1) induced markedly weaker aggregation (Fig. 5A).

We next examined microscale cell aggregation using bright-field microscopy with a ×10 objective lens. Lys-PG (C18:1) rapidly induced the formation of large aggregates within 30 min in a dose-dependent manner (Figs. 5B and S7). In contrast, PG (C18:1) did not cause noticeable aggregation, and CL (C18:1) had only a minimal effect (Fig. 5B). Similar aggregation patterns were observed with phospholipids composed of different fatty acid chain lengths and saturation levels, such as C16:0 (Fig. S8). Lys-PG (18:1) also promoted aggregation of other Gram-positive bacteria, including Staphylococcus epidermidis, Enterococcus faecalis, and Bacillus subtilis (Fig. S9), indicating that its effect is not limited to S. aureus. Real-time light scattering measurements of bacterial suspensions revealed a slight decrease within the first 5 min, followed by a sharp decline after 10–15 min upon the addition of Lys-PG (C18:1) (Fig. S10). These results suggest that Lys-PG (C18:1) induces S. aureus aggregation through two distinct phases: an initial small aggregate formation phase (~5 min) followed by the formation of larger aggregates (10–15 min). This aggregation process was further confirmed by real-time imaging (Movies S1 and S2).

Among the phospholipids tested, Lys-PG (C18:1) had the most pronounced impact on cell aggregation. To further investigate this, we used ASEM to visualize cell aggregates and surface attachment in the presence of Lys-PG (C18:1) under aqueous conditions. While separated and small aggregated cells of S. aureus attached to the ASEM dish under control conditions, extensive multicellular aggregates formed upon exposure to Lys-PG (C18:1) (Figs. 5C and S11A), consistent with the optical microscopy observations. FE-SEM also showed that Lys-PG (C18:1) induced aggregation of S. aureus cells, with nanospherical structures visible at the interfaces between the cells (Fig. 5D).

To confirm Lys-PG localization within S. aureus cell aggregates, cells treated with Lys-PG (C18:1) were stained with FM1-43 and observed by CLSM in super-resolution mode. In the absence of Lys-PG, monococci and diplococci labeled with FM1-43 adhered to the surface (Figs. 5E and S11B). In contrast, Lys-PG (C18:1)-treated cells formed large aggregates with strong fluorescence signals, primarily localized between cells (Figs. 5E and S11B), indicating that Lys-PG (C18:1) is concentrated at cell interfaces, consistent with its proposed role as a molecular glue connecting S. aureus cells in three dimensions.

Since Lys-PG, but not PG, strongly promoted cell aggregation, we hypothesized that the positively charged lysine moiety of Lys-PG (Fig. S3) mediates aggregation through electrostatic interactions with the negatively charged bacterial surface. To test this, we used a high-concentration salt solution (1 M NaCl) to disrupt electrostatic interactions, which markedly reduced Lys-PG (C18:1)-induced bacterial cell aggregation (Fig. 5F). Additionally, high concentrations of NaCl inhibited biofilm formation of S. aureus MR4 in a dose-dependent manner without affecting bacterial growth (Fig. S12). In contrast, treatment with an osmolyte of equivalent osmotic strength (2 M glycine betaine) did not inhibit aggregation, indicating that electrostatic interactions, rather than osmotic pressure, are critical to this process. Furthermore, treatment with PLA1, which cleaves phospholipid acyl ester bonds in Lys-PG, also suppressed aggregation (Fig. 5F), suggesting that the hydrophobic moiety of Lys-PG contributes to aggregation through hydrophobic interactions between Lys-PG molecules.

Next, we explored whether specific surface molecules on S. aureus are required for Lys-PG-induced aggregation. In S. aureus, several negatively charged surface components are known, including teichoic acids and cell wall-anchored proteins. We tested a wall teichoic acid-deficient (ΔtagO) strain and proteinase K-treated wild-type cells that have degraded surface proteins. Both ΔtagO and proteinase K-treated wild-type cells formed large aggregates within 30 min, similar to intact wild-type cells. However, proteinase K-treated ΔtagO cells formed only smaller aggregates compared with the other conditions (Fig. S13), indicating that negatively charged molecules contribute to Lys-PG-triggered aggregation.

Finally, we tested whether negative surface charge alone is sufficient for Lys-PG-triggered aggregation using silica microbeads (0.9 μm diameter) with a negatively charged surface, mimicking S. aureus cells. Lys-PG (C18:1) strongly induced aggregation of microbeads, which was suppressed by PLA1 and partially inhibited by high concentrations of NaCl (Fig. 5G).

Lys-PG is crucial for biofilm formation in S. aureus

To determine whether Lys-PG contributes to biofilm formation in S. aureus, we generated an S. aureus MR4 mutant strain in which the gene encoding the Lys-PG synthase (mprF) was deleted. TLC analysis confirmed that the MR4 ΔmprF mutant lacked Lys-PG, while the production of PG and CL remained unaffected (Fig. 6A), in agreement with previous studies32,33. Complementation with a plasmid expressing mprF partially restored Lys-PG production (Fig. 6A, B), consistent with previous complementation studies in staphylococci34–36. This partial restoration may reflect differences in the expression level and timing of the complemented gene, as well as heterogeneity in plasmid retention within the bacterial population.

We then compared the biofilm formation of MR4 ΔmprF with that of the wild-type strain. To ensure accurate and comprehensive observation, biofilms were visualized using the THUNDER Imaging System25 with tiling mode to capture the entire biofilm area on glass-bottom dishes. The MR4 ΔmprF strain formed a significantly thinner biofilm compared to the wild-type strain (Fig. 6C), consistent with previous findings using the MRSA USA300 strain JE2 and its mprF mutant37. Notably, the central region of the ΔmprF biofilm was particularly reduced in thickness compared with the wild type (Fig. 6C, D). Reintroducing mprF via a plasmid partially restored biofilm formation, in line with the recovery of Lys-PG production (Fig. 6A, B). Analysis of isolated matrix fractions showed no substantial differences in components such as eDNA or proteins among the MR4 variants (Fig. S14).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that Lys-PG plays a key role in S. aureus biofilm formation, particularly in maintaining biofilm thickness, highlighting its importance in promoting a robust biofilm structure.

Lys-PG is associated with MVs in the S. aureus biofilm

Although our results demonstrated that Lys-PG contributes to biofilm formation in S. aureus, it remained unclear where extracellular Lys-PG is localized. Microscopic observations revealed that numerous MVs are present within the biofilm and that the isolated biofilm matrix contains MVs, as observed by TEM (Fig. S15A), suggesting a possible association of Lys-PG with these vesicles. To test this, we performed lipidomic analyses of the isolated biofilm matrix and MVs. As expected, Lys-PG was detected in both fractions (Fig. S15B). Taken together, these results indicate that Lys-PG is associated with MVs.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the role of extracellular phospholipids in S. aureus biofilms, particularly focusing on the moonlighting function of Lys-PG in promoting bacterial aggregation and biofilm formation. Our findings demonstrate that Lys-PG functions as a molecular glue mediating cell-to-cell adhesion during biofilm formation. Both electrostatic interactions between the positively charged Lys-PG and the negatively charged bacterial surface, and hydrophobic interactions between the fatty acyl chains of Lys-PG molecules are critical for Lys-PG-induced aggregation (Fig. 7). Importantly, no single specific surface molecular species on bacterial cells appears to be strictly required for this process under the conditions tested. While extracellular nucleic acids (eDNA and envRNA), proteins, and polysaccharides have long been recognized as key structural components of S. aureus biofilms10,12, this study demonstrates the previously overlooked importance of extracellular phospholipids. Notably, the discovery that Lys-PG is enriched in the biofilm matrix and promotes cell aggregation adds a novel layer to our understanding of biofilm structure. The role of Lys-PG as a molecular glue within S. aureus biofilms offers new insights into biofilm architecture, highlighting the importance of phospholipids in the formation of intercellular junctions within the biofilm matrix, which are now seen as pivotal in forming intercellular junctions. Our results also demonstrate how the enzymatic activity of PLA1 can inhibit biofilm development and disperse pre-formed biofilms.

Proposed model for the role of Lys-PG in cell aggregation.

The positively charged lysine (Lys) moiety of Lys-PG electrostatically interacts with negatively charged molecules on the bacterial surface, such as wall teichoic acids (WTA), lipoteichoic acids (LTA), and cell wall-anchored proteins (CWAP). In addition, Lys-PG molecules can associate with one another via hydrophobic interactions between their fatty acyl chains. Thus, both electrostatic interactions between the Lys moieties of Lys-PG and the bacterial cell surface and hydrophobic interactions among Lys-PG molecules cooperate to mediate bacterial cell aggregation.

The selective enrichment of specific Lys-PG and CL species, such as Lys-PG (C18:0-15:0, C16:0-15:0, and C15:0-18:0) and CL (C63:0-15:0, C64:0-15:0, and C62:0-15:0), within the biofilm matrix compared to their relative abundance in bacterial cells (Fig. 4E, F) suggests a potential specialized role for these phospholipids in maintaining biofilm structural integrity. From a lipid biology perspective, this selectivity suggests that acyl-chain composition, rather than total phospholipid abundance alone, may influence the physical properties of the biofilm matrix, including lipid packing and interfacial interactions. These enriched phospholipid species may interact with other components of the biofilm matrix, such as eDNA, proteins, and polysaccharides, or with structural elements on bacterial cells, including cell wall peptidoglycan and teichoic acids. Unraveling the molecular mechanisms underlying the selective accumulation of these phospholipids in the biofilm matrix represents a key area for future investigation.

Our study demonstrates that PLA1 inhibits biofilm formation and promotes the dispersal of mature biofilms by hydrolyzing multiple phospholipid species. In contrast, neither PLC nor PLD significantly reduced S. aureus biofilm formation, suggesting that the acyl chains of phospholipids may be more critical for biofilm integrity than their phosphate-containing head groups. If Lys-PG was the sole or dominant determinant of biofilm formation, PLD treatment would also be expected to impair biofilm formation by removing the lysine-containing head group. However, this effect was not observed in biofilm dispersal activity (Fig. 2B) and was only partially evident in the inhibition of biofilm formation of MR23 (Fig. 2E). These findings indicate that phospholipids other than Lys-PG also contribute to biofilm formation.

Mechanistically, Lys-PG appears to mediate bacterial cell aggregation through both electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions, with the positively charged lysine moiety mediating electrostatic adhesion to negatively charged bacterial surfaces. This mechanism is supported by experiments showing that high concentrations of NaCl can disrupt these interactions and that PLA1 prevents aggregation. Since our lipidomic analysis did not detect lyso-Lys-PG in the PLA1-treated samples, it is likely that PLA1 cleaved Lys-PG at both the sn-1 and sn-2 positions under the tested conditions. The combined electrostatic and hydrophobic properties of Lys-PG provide a robust and flexible mechanism for biofilm stability. Although relatively high concentrations of synthetic phospholipids were used in several in vitro aggregation assays to facilitate clear and reproducible visualization of aggregation phenomena, lower concentrations of Lys-PG were also sufficient to induce detectable aggregation, supporting the physiological relevance of this activity.

PLA1 stands out as a promising biofilm dispersal agent in this study, primarily due to its ability to cleave Lys-PG and other phospholipids in the biofilm matrix without inhibiting bacterial growth. This selective activity is particularly valuable for both clinical and industrial applications, where removing biofilms without killing bacteria may be crucial, such as in scenarios requiring the preservation of microbial viability for ecosystem balance or fermentation processes. The limited antibacterial and bactericidal effects of PLA1, as demonstrated in Fig. 2E and Figs. S1 and S2, may result from the robust cell wall of S. aureus, which likely limits enzyme access to the bacterial membrane, or from compensatory mechanisms that facilitate de novo phospholipid synthesis in response to PLA1 activity38. Moreover, the absence of hemolytic activity associated with PLA1 (Fig. 2G) further supports its potential for safe application in various biotechnological contexts. This safety profile is particularly significant for use in medical device coatings or surfaces that come into contact with human tissues, where minimizing cytotoxicity is a critical consideration.

This report raises several important questions for future research. For instance, while Lys-PG is shown to play an important role in S. aureus biofilm formation, it remains unclear whether similar phospholipid-based mechanisms operate in other pathogenic bacteria. Although our results show that Lys-PG promotes aggregation in other Gram-positive species, including S. epidermidis, E. faecalis, and B. subtilis, it is not yet clear whether this effect is broadly conserved, particularly among Gram-negative bacteria. Given that Gram-negative species rarely synthesize Lys-PG, they might instead utilize Lys-PG produced by neighboring Gram-positive bacteria within dual- or multispecies biofilms. Comparative studies on phospholipid content in biofilms formed by different bacterial species could reveal whether PLA1 or related enzymes could be more broadly applied to biofilm control. Additionally, the present findings highlight the need for further exploration of structure-function relationships among different Lys-PG species. The enrichment of specific Lys-PG species in the biofilm matrix suggests that particular acyl-chain compositions may be specifically effective at promoting biofilm integrity. Investigating the mechanism presents behind these differences could lead to the development of tailored biofilm dispersal agents or surface coatings designed to disrupt biofilms under diverse environmental conditions. Previously, it has been shown that mprF deletion has pleiotropic effects, including altered sorting and secretion of several proteins such as LukAB, IsaB, and Hla39. In contrast, in the present study, we detected no significant differences in the levels of selected proteins within the biofilm matrix between the S. aureus wild-type and ΔmprF strains. However, whether mprF deletion affects the overall biofilm matrix proteome remains unclear. Comprehensive proteomic analyses will therefore be required to address this question. Finally, this study points to potential roles for phospholipases in both in vivo and environmental settings. Host-derived phospholipases may influence the survival and colonization of beneficial or pathogenic bacteria by inhibiting or dispersing biofilms, whereas microbe-derived phospholipases could be explored to selectively disrupt biofilms in complex microbial communities. These insights open promising avenues for employing phospholipases as targeted tools in biofilm management and microbial ecosystem regulation.

This study provides novel insights into the role of extracellular phospholipids in S. aureus biofilm formation and demonstrates the potential of PLA1 as an effective biofilm dispersal agent. By uncovering the moonlighting function of Lys-PG, this research extends our understanding of biofilm architecture and provides a framework for the development of innovative biofilm control strategies in both clinical and industrial settings. These findings may facilitate further investigations into phospholipase-based treatments and their applications in biofilm management.

Methods

Detailed information on all experimental procedures is provided in Supplementary Methods.

Bacterial strains and media

Clinical strains of S. aureus10 were isolated from de-identified clinical samples at The Jikei University Hospital (Table S1). All strains, plasmids, and primers used in this study are listed in Tables S1 and S2. Growth conditions and construction of mutants and plasmids are described in Supplementary Methods.

Enzymes

Proteinase K from Tritirachium album, PLA1 from Thermomyces lanuginosus (≥10 KLU/g liquid), PLC from Bacillus cereus (≥1000 units/mg protein), and PLD from Streptomyces chromofuscus (≥25,000 U/ml) were procured from Sigma (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Phospholipids

1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-[phospho-rac-(3-lysyl(1-glycerol))] (Lys-PG with C18:1), 1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-[phospho-rac-(3-lysyl(1-glycerol))] (chloride salt) (Lys-PG with C16:0), 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-(1’-rac-glycerol) (sodium salt) (PG with C18:1), 1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho-(1’-rac-glycerol) (sodium salt) (PG with C16:0), 1’,3’-bis[1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phospho]-glycerol (sodium salt) (CL with C18:1), and 1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-ethylphosphocholine (chloride salt) (CL with C16:0) were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids (Birmingham, AL, USA). The lyophilized powders were dissolved in 100% methanol, 100% chloroform, or chloroform/methanol (9:1, v/v).

Bacterial growth monitoring

Growth of the indicated strains was monitored at 37 °C in brain heart infusion (BHI) supplemented with 1% (w/v) glucose (BHIG) in the absence and presence of PLA1 in 96-well flat bottom polystyrene plates (Corning, NY, USA) by measuring the absorbance of each culture at 600 nm every 30 min for 24 h using a Bio Microplate Reader HiTS (Scinics Corp., Tokyo, Japan).

Biofilm inhibition assay

Overnight cultures grown in BHI medium at 37 °C were diluted 1000-fold in BHIG. Two hundred microliters of these suspensions were cultured in 96-well flat-bottom polystyrene plates (Corning) at 37 °C for 24 h. When required, PLA1 (200 μg/ml), PLC (200 μg/ml), or PLD (500 U/ml) were added at the onset of incubation. Biofilms were washed, stained, and quantified as described in Supplementary Methods.

Biofilm destruction assay

Overnight cultures of S. aureus grown in BHI medium at 37 °C were diluted 1000-fold in BHIG. Two hundred microliters of these suspensions were cultured in 96-well flat-bottom polystyrene plates (Corning) at 37 °C for 24 h. Subsequently, PLA1 (200 μg/ml or the indicated concentrations), PLC (200 μg/ml), or PLD (500 U/ml) were added directly to 24-h-old biofilms without removal of the culture medium and incubated for an additional 2 h at 37 °C. Biofilms were quantified as described in Supplementary Methods.

CFU measurement of biofilms following PLA1 treatment

S. aureus MR4 biofilms were grown in 2 ml of BHIG medium at 37 °C for 24 h. PLA1 (200 μg/ml) was then added directly to the 24-h biofilms without removing the culture medium, and incubation continued for an additional 2 h at 37 °C. CFUs of total bacteria, including both planktonic and biofilm-associated cells, and biofilm-associated cells alone were determined as described in Supplementary Methods.

Isolation of biofilm matrixes

Biofilm matrixes were extracted from bacteria grown under biofilm formation conditions as previously reported8. Briefly, 24-h biofilms of MR4 formed in 30 ml BHIG were collected by centrifugation at 5000×g for 10 min at 25 °C. The pellets were centrifuged again for 1 min and the supernatants were removed completely. Then, the pellets were suspended in 10 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 8.0) containing 1 M NaCl solution (100 μl) and mixed by vortexing. The mild alkaline condition minimizes protonation-dependent interactions, while the high ionic strength (1 M NaCl) screens electrostatic forces between negatively charged bacterial surfaces and associated matrix components, thereby promoting dissociation and recovery of extracellular matrix materials in the supernatant. After centrifugation at 5000×g for 10 min at 25 °C, the supernatants were collected as biofilm matrix fractions containing extracellular nanostructures (MVs and fibrils).

TLC analysis

Phospholipids were extracted from planktonic cells and isolated biofilm matrixes for TLC analysis as described in Supplementary Methods. Extracted lipids were separated using TLC on a silica gel plate (SilicaGel 60 with concentrating zone, Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA), developed with a solvent mixture of chloroform/methanol/acetic acid (65:25:10, v/v/v). For lipid detection, TLC plates were sprayed with molybdenum blue reagent (Sigma). PG (C16:0), Lys-PG (C16:0), and CL (C16:0) were used as standards.

LC-MS/MS analysis

The isolated biofilm matrix (20 μl) was diluted 5-fold into BFD buffer [20 mM HEPES-OH (pH 7.4) and 20 mM CaCl2] supplemented with or without PLA1 (200 μg/ml) and incubated at 37 °C for 3 h. The reaction was stopped by adding 900 μl methanol. Phospholipids were analyzed by QTRAP6500+, a triple quadrupole-linear ion trap hybrid mass spectrometer (Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA) equipped with an ExionLCTM AD UPLC system (Sciex). Lipids were extracted using a modified Bligh and Dyer method. Detection and quantification of PG, Lys-PG, and CL were performed as reported previously40–42. Detailed procedures are described in Supplementary Methods.

Hemolytic activity assay

Hemolytic activities of PLA1 (10 mg/ml), PLC (10 mg/ml), and PLD (25,000 U/ml) were analyzed on Columbia Agar with 5% sheep blood (BD). Four-microliters of the enzymes were spotted on the plates, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h.

Fluorescence microscopy

CLSM was performed as previously reported11,12,25. Briefly, biofilms of the S. aureus strain MR4 and MR23 were formed in BHIG supplemented with or without PLA1 (200 μg/ml) at 37 °C for 24 h in glass-bottomed dishes. Biofilms formed on the surface of the dishes were washed, fixed, stained, optically cleared using iCBiofilm-H1 (Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan), and visualized as described in Supplementary Methods.

TEM

TEM analysis of S. aureus strain MR23 biofilms was performed as previously reported23. Biofilms were grown in BHIG at 37 °C for 4 h in 35-mm plastic dishes (Nunc, Roskilde, Denmark), then scraped and collected by centrifugation. The biofilm cells were fixed, dehydrated, sectioned, stained, and observed using an H7600 TEM (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) as described in Supplementary Methods.

ASEM

We used the ClairScope ASEM system (JASM-6200, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) to directly visualize extracellular structures within biofilms under aqueous conditions11,23,27. S. aureus MR23 biofilms were grown in BHIG at 37 °C for 4 h in ASEM dishes (35 mm in diameter, JEOL). Detailed procedures are described in Supplementary Methods.

FE-SEM

We utilized FE-SEM to observe extracellular structures in S. aureus MR4 biofilms and aggregates at high resolution. Biofilms of MR4 were cultivated in BHIG medium with or without 200 μg/ml PLA1 at 37 °C for 24 h. The biofilms were scraped into test tubes and pre-fixed overnight at 4 °C in a solution containing 1.2% glutaraldehyde and 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.0). Detailed sample preparation procedures are described in Supplementary Methods. The specimens were imaged using an FE-SEM (Regulus 8100, Hitachi).

Cell aggregation assay

S. aureus MR4 cells were cultured overnight in BHI at 37 °C. The overnight culture (4 ml) was pelleted by centrifugation at 5000×g for 10 min at 4 °C, washed three times with 1 ml of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in PBS. A 100 μl aliquot of the bacterial suspension was then mixed with the indicated concentrations of phospholipids in 1.5 ml plastic tubes. For light microscopy, a 10 μl aliquot of the bacterial suspension was placed on a glass slide and mixed with the specified concentrations of phospholipids, 1 M NaCl, and/or 200 μg/ml PLA1 to observe cell aggregation. Bacterial cells and aggregates were also imaged using ASEM as described in Supplementary Methods. Additionally, bacterial cell aggregation was evaluated by light scattering as described in Supplementary Methods.

Silica-coated microbeads aggregation assay

A 1 ml suspension of silica-coated microbeads (Silica Microspheres with a diameter of 0.90 μm, Polysciences, Warrington, PA, USA) was washed twice with PBS and resuspended in 1 ml of PBS. The beads were incubated with or without 100 μg/ml Lys-PG (C18:1) for 30 min at 25 °C. When necessary, 1 M NaCl and 200 μg/ml PLA1 were added to the suspension. After incubation, bead aggregation was observed under a microscope using a ×10 objective lens.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9, GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s or Sidak’s corrections, or Student’s t-test, as appropriate. All experiments were independently performed at least twice. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
