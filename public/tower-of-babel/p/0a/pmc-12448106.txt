Chaperone nanomotors with chemotactic ability for the treatment of Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Wenjing Wang, Xue Xia, Ziqiang Zhang, Qingya Zhu, Yu Chen, Yao Zhang, Lin Chen, Chun Mao, Mimi Wan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aggregation of α-synuclein (α-syn) represents a pathogenic hallmark of Parkinson’s disease (PD). Using exogenous molecular chaperone systems has the potential to stabilize native conformations and inhibit the aberrant aggregation of α-syn, yet inefficient blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration and insufficient accumulation at PD sites limit their application. Herein, we developed chemotactic chaperone nanomotors (CNMs) that exploit the unique pathological microenvironment of PD lesions, characterized by elevated inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) levels. The CNMs consist of a chemotactic-targeting module capable of sensing an iNOS concentration gradient, an α-syn–specific recognition module with conformation-sensitive binding domains, and a protein regulatory module with a hydrophobic microdomain that can stabilize α-syn and prevent misfolding. Results demonstrate that CNMs can achieve active chemotactic navigation across BBB and dual modulation of α-syn proteostasis through aggregate dissolution and prevention of misfolding. CNMs not only supplement exogenous chaperone activity but also restore endogenous protein stabilization mechanisms, concurrently reducing neuroinflammatory markers, promoting the application of nanochaperones for PD treatment.

INTRODUCTION

Proteins play a crucial role in a myriad of vital biochemical processes within living organisms, with their structural stability directly determining functional fidelity (1, 2). Pathological protein misfolding often triggers aggregation cascades that underlie multiple diseases. For instance, the aggregation of α-synuclein (α-syn) within neuronal cells is commonly recognized as a key initiator in the pathogenesis of Parkinson’s disease (PD) (3–5). Cellular molecular chaperones, first conceptualized by Laskey et al. in 1978, constitute an evolutionarily conserved defense network that maintains proteostasis through structural stabilization, misfolding prevention, and conformational rescue (6–8). Key chaperone families [e.g., heat shock proteins (HSPs)] actively regulate α-syn dynamics by stabilizing monomers or degrading oligomers, thereby preserving central nervous system functionality (9–12). Notably, neurodegenerative disease microenvironments exhibit dual pathology: protein aggregation and chaperone system impairment (13, 14). This insight has driven therapeutic innovation focused on repairing endogenous chaperone networks or delivering engineered chaperone augmentations as disease-modifying strategies for neurodegeneration (15).

The inherent limitations of natural molecular chaperones—low bioavailability, thermal instability, and prohibitive production costs—have prompted the development of artificial chaperone mimetics termed “nanochaperones” (16–18). Current implementations for neurodegenerative diseases primarily include mixed-shell polymeric micelles and hydrophobically modified porous nanoparticles (19–21). They can rely on the hydrophobic ends of the nanoparticles to adsorb and stabilize disease-causing proteins (19–21), where hydrophobic microdomains that can interact with misfolded proteins are essential. Notably, despite demonstrated in vitro efficacy, translational progress remains constrained by limited validation in biologically complex in vivo systems.

A pivotal translational bottleneck for nanochaperones in neurodegenerative diseases lies in overcoming dual biological barriers: (i) systemic delivery constraints compromising blood-brain barrier (BBB) penetration efficiency (22) and (ii) the imperative to concurrently restore endogenous chaperone functionality within degenerating neural circuits. The currently reported methods for nanochaperones to penetrate the BBB mainly rely on molecular recognition mechanisms, such as modifying peptides that target the brain to recognize relevant receptors on the brain vascular endothelial cells (23). However, these approaches face two fundamental limitations: (i) strict spatial requirements (receptor-ligand proximity <10 nm for effective binding) (24) and (ii) susceptibility to protein corona formation and nonspecific adsorption in circulation (25). Meanwhile, the identification of vascular endothelial cells does not mean that it can efficiently target damaged neurons, which typically only exist in specific parts of the brain, such as PD-damaged areas that are mostly concentrated in the midbrain (26). Therefore, it is important to design a more efficient delivery strategy that enables nanochaperones to efficiently break through the BBB and enrich in damaged neuronal cells, thereby promoting the therapeutic effect of nanochaperones in neurodegenerative diseases.

Clinically, neurodegenerative disease lesions exhibit pathological overexpression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) (27), establishing a self-sustaining chemical gradient across the BBB between the cerebrovascular endothelium and degenerating neurons (28). On the basis of this, we use the phenomenon of iNOS concentration gradient that naturally exists in the PD-damaged area to provide nanochaperones with a chemotactic ability. This allows them to efficiently break through the BBB of the PD region and target the damaged area (Fig. 1). Specifically, a kind of chaperone nanomotor (CNM) composed of targeted, recognition, and protein regulatory modules was constructed. They can penetrate the BBB to PD lesion tissue based on chemotactic behavior and then exert the effect of stabilizing/degrading α-syn aggregates and preventing further misfolding. First, a polymer-based targeted module [l-arginine (l-Arg) derivatives with a specific motion/chemotaxis ability] and protein regulatory module (which can form a hydrophobic microdomain by responding to body temperature, thereby regulating damaged proteins) were synthesized by atom transfer radical polymerization (ATRP), and composite micelles were formed. Then, to achieve specific recognition and adsorption of α-syn aggregates, we introduced the recognition module βsyn36 onto the composite micelles (Fig. 1A). Among them, the l-Arg component in the targeted module actively detects the chemical attractant iNOS, triggering nitric oxide (NO) production and inducing chemotactic movement in the CNMs. This enables them to overcome the BBB and reach the injury area. Once inside neuronal cells, the βsyn36 recognition module of the CNMs identifies α-syn aggregates and directs them toward the hydrophobic microdomains. The protein regulatory module then adsorbs the α-syn aggregates via hydrophobic interactions with their exposed hydrophobic ends, thereby inhibiting further aggregation. Meanwhile, the released NO during the chemotaxis process can promote the degradation of α-syn aggregates by activating matrix metallopeptidase-2 (MMP-2), reduce reactive oxygen species (ROS), alleviate inflammatory responses, and ultimately achieve neuronal repair, providing innovative therapeutic ideas for the treatment of PD (Fig. 1B).

RESULTS

Optimization of the synthesis conditions of the targeted module

As shown in Fig. 1, the CNMs mainly include three modules: a targeted module that can flexibly respond to the high expression of iNOS in the PD microenvironment, thereby breaking through the BBB to achieve effective targeting; a recognition module that can specifically recognize α-syn species; and a protein regulatory module that can stabilize the structure of α-syn aggregates and prevent them from misfolding. Among them, the optimal ratio of the synthesis of the targeted module and the protein regulatory module needs to be explored in detail, so this part mainly explores the synthesis optimization conditions of the targeted module.

The targeted modular system was synthesized through sequential functionalization of bromine-terminated methoxypolyethylene glycol (mPEG-Br) with tailored hydrophilic (l-Arg–based motility component) and hydrophobic moieties. The synthesis protocol commenced with mPEG-Br preparation via hydroxyl group modification of mPEG, as confirmed by characteristic proton signals in 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra (figs. S1 and S2) (29). Subsequently, methacrylated arginine (M-Arg) was obtained through methacrylic anhydride derivatization of l-Arg, with structural verification provided by 1H NMR analysis (figs. S3 and S4). The core modular assembly polyethylene glycol (PEG)/AD was achieved through ATRP using mPEG-Br as a macroinitiator, combined with M-Arg and 2-(diisopropylamino)ethyl methacrylate (DPA) monomers (fig. S5). This design enables chemotactic responsiveness to iNOS concentration gradients, where motility performance critically depends on arginine content in the copolymer. To optimize motion properties, we systematically investigated polymerization time effects by varying M-Arg/DPA reaction durations while maintaining the total ATRP time at 12 hours, generating four distinct architectures: PEG/AD (1-11), PEG/AD (2-10), PEG/AD (4-8), and PEG/AD (6-6) [numerals denote M-Arg/DPA hours, respectively, AD (a block copolymer synthesized from M-Arg and DPA, where A represents Arg and D represents DPA)]. Their structure, micelle size, and motion properties were characterized. The synthesis of PEG/AD can be verified by 1H NMR spectroscopy and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. The results showed that the 1H NMR spectra contained characteristic peaks from both the monomer DPA and M-Arg, as well as from the PEG carbon chain backbone (figs. S6 to S9). The main absorption peaks in the FTIR spectrum include the characteristic peak (1530 cm−1) of the typical amino II band from M-Arg and the characteristic peak of the isopropyl group from DPA (2970 cm−1) (fig. S10) (30). These results showed that different PEG/AD targeted modules were successfully obtained. Moreover, there was no obvious difference in the molecular weights of the four PEG/AD architectures, and the molar ratio of arginine modification in PEG/AD (2-10) had reached the highest ([M-Arg]:[DPA] = 3:1) (table S1) according to the 1H NMR integration results, indicating that the modification efficiency of the M-Arg reaction had reached the maximum for 2 hours. PEG/AD was synthesized into micelles by the sonication method, and the morphology/size distributions of the PEG/AD synthesized under different conditions were observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and dynamic light scattering (DLS), which showed a typical spheroid morphology with a diameter of ~100 to 250 nm and a slight increase in hydrodynamic diameter of about 150 to 300 nm compared to the particle size shown in TEM (Fig. 2, A to D). In addition, PEG/AD displayed good biocompatibility (fig. S11). Next, the in vitro motility of PEG/AD was characterized. The chemotactic potential of PEG/AD variants was systematically evaluated using Cy5-labeled conjugates in preactivated SH-SY5Y microenvironments. Quantitative trajectory analysis revealed that PEG/AD (2-10) and PEG/AD (4-8) exhibited superior motility metrics, demonstrating 2.3-fold greater mean displacement velocities and 1.8-fold longer migration distances compared to PEG/AD (1-11) controls (Fig. 2, E to I). Parallel zeta potential measurements showed a time-dependent enhancement of surface charge magnitude (Fig. 2J), linearly correlated with arginine incorporation duration (R2 = 0.94), attributable to cumulative integration of cationic guanidinium moieties. The guanidine group in the PEG/AD targeted module reacted with iNOS and ROS that are highly expressed in the SH-SY5Y cell environment to release NO, resulting in a motion ability (31). Therefore, the guanidine content in the targeted module synthesized under different conditions was detected, and the results showed that the guanidine content of PEG/AD (2-10) basically reached the maximum value, which was about 1.5 mmol g−1 (Fig. 2K). The guanidine content of PEG/AD and the NO release behavior in the environment of SH-SY5Y cells were further determined. As shown in Fig. 2L, the cumulative NO release of PEG/AD (1-11) was 1.6 times that of the blank control group, while the NO release of PEG/AD (2-10) was 2.5 times that of the blank control group, which tended to be stable. On the basis of the above results, PEG/AD (2-10) was selected for follow-up study.

Synthesis and characterization of PEG/AD/PNC composite micelles with a targeted module and protein regulatory module

In addition to targeted modules, CNMs necessitate a protein regulatory module to ensure the stability of protein structures. In this work, poly(N-isopropylacrylamide)-block-poly(ε-caprolactone) (PNIPAm-b-PCL, abbreviated as PNC) was selected to construct the protein regulatory module. Thanks to the temperature-responsive PNIPAm within its structure, PNIPAm-b-PCL can spontaneously transition from hydrophilic to hydrophobic and collapse above its lower critical solution temperature of 32°C, thereby creating a hydrophobic microdomain (32). First, the diblock copolymer PNIPAm-b-PCL was synthesized by the ATRP reaction (fig. S12). The chemical structure of the copolymer was confirmed using 1H NMR (figs. S13 and S14). Subsequently, the critical micelle concentrations (CMCs) of PEG/AD and PNIPAm-b-PCL were determined by the fluorescent probe method. The CMC values were found to be 0.002 and 0.0008 mg ml−1 for PEG/AD and PNIPAm-b-PCL, respectively (fig. S15). Accordingly, the PEG/AD/PNC composite micelle, incorporating both the targeted module and protein regulatory module, was prepared via self-assembly in an aqueous solution. Notably, the protein regulatory module PNC inherently lacks mobility (as shown in fig. S16). Thus, the molar ratio of the targeted module to the protein regulatory module influences the overall performance of the CNMs. Therefore, we set up four ratios for experimental exploration, which are [PEG/AD/PNC] (1:1), [PEG/AD/PNC] (1:2.5), [PEG/AD/PNC] (1:5), and [PEG/AD/PNC] (1:10). To investigate the recombination of the two modules, the process was conducted using PEG/AD labeled with Cy5 (red) and PNC labeled with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC; green). The results demonstrated that when the composite molar ratio of PEG/AD and PNC was 1:2.5, the fluorescence of FITC-labeled PNC (green) was well bound to that of Cy5-labeled PEG/AD (red). When the composite molar ratio of PEG/AD and PNC was 1:1, the green fluorescence and red fluorescence were proximal but lacked colocalization, and the particles were large and exhibited multiagglomeration. Notably, when the composite molar ratios of PEG/AD and PNC were 1:5 and 1:10, green and red fluorescence appeared separate, with minimal colocalization observed (Fig. 3A and fig. S17). In addition, the structure of the composite micelles was determined by FTIR, and the main absorption peaks in the FTIR spectrum showed characteristic peaks including the typical amino II band from M-Arg (1530 cm−1) and the isopropyl group from N-isopropyl acrylamide (NIPAm; 2970 cm−1) (fig. S18) (33). Subsequently, the morphology of the composite micelles was characterized by TEM, and the diameter of the PEG/AD/PNC composite micelles gradually decreased with the composite ratio of [PNC] (Fig. 3B). This phenomenon might stem from the existence of a preferred micellar composition (PMC) within composite micelles (34). Variations in the hydrophobic core of micelles lead to differences in hydrophobic forces. Micelles form when the system’s composition aligns with the PMC. In contrast, when the system deviates from the PMC, both individual and composite micelles can coexist (34). Subsequently, the in vitro motility capacity of PEG/AD/PNC was explored. Four samples of PEG/AD/PNC were labeled with Cy5 dye, and their motion behavior in the environment of prestimulated SH-SY5Y cells was observed. The protein regulatory module PNC exhibits irregular Brownian motion at a rate of 1.1 μm s−1 in the prestimulated SH-SY5Y cell environment (fig. S16, C and D). Compared with [PEG/AD/PNC] (1:5) and [PEG/AD/PNC] (1:10), [PEG/AD/PNC] (1:1) and [PEG/AD/PNC] (1:2.5) had more pronounced displacement (Fig. 3, C to F). The speed of [PEG/AD/PNC] (1:2.5) (3.5 μm s−1) was slightly higher than that of [PEG/AD/PNC] (1:1) (3.1 μm s−1) (Fig. 3G). In addition, the zeta potentials of the four PEG/AD/PNC samples were characterized, with the composite micelles with higher PNC content having a relatively lower zeta potential, which was attributed to the strong electronegativity of the PNC, and the composite micelles exhibited negative potentials for PEG/AD/PNC (Fig. 3H). Furthermore, the ability of PEG/AD/PNC to form hydrophobic microdomains in response to temperature changes was investigated. As the temperature rises, water gradually becomes a poor solvent for the PNIPAm block. Consequently, the stretched PNIPAm chain transitions from an extended coil conformation to a contracted conformation (35), leading to a decrease in transmittance and causing the clear solution to become cloudy (fig. S19A). The lower critical solution temperature of PNIPAm was ~32°C (32), suggesting that PNIPAm exhibits a hydrophobic collapsed conformation at normal body temperature. In contrast, the transmittance of PEG/AD, another component of the composite micelles, remains unchanged with temperature (Fig. 3I). Next, the transmittance of different composite micelles at room temperature (25°C) and normal human temperature (37°C) under visible light was explored. [PEG/AD/PNC] (1:2.5), [PEG/AD/PNC] (1:5), and [PEG/AD/PNC] (1:10) could change in response to temperature changes, while [PEG/AD/PNC] (1:1) could not change in response to temperature (Fig. 3J and fig. S19), which may be due to the insufficient amount of PNC micelles in the protein regulatory module at this composite ratio. To ensure the optimal motility and hydrophobic response performance of PEG/AD/PNC, the [PEG/AD/PNC] (1:2.5) composite micelles were selected for constructing CNMs in subsequent research.

Synthesis of CNMs and the regulation on α-syn aggregates under in vitro conditions

After determining the optimal ratio of the targeted module and the protein regulatory module in the composite micelles, the recognition module was covalently bonded to the composite micelles to construct CNMs incorporating targeted, protein regulatory, and recognition modules. βsyn36, a short fragment of α-syn (amino acids 36 to 45) capable of specific interaction with α-syn (36), was chosen as the recognition module, and its structure was verified by 1H NMR (fig. S20). βsyn36 was coupled to PEG/AD/PNC composite micelles via covalent amide bonds under 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC)/N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) conditions, forming CNMs whose structure was determined by 1H NMR (fig. S21). For comparative purposes, we synthesized a PEG/BD nonnanomotor, which involves butoxycarbonyl modification of the guanidine group of M-Arg, rendering it incapable of movement under an iNOS concentration gradient. The 1H NMR results showed that the chemical shift representing the guanidine group disappeared and a characteristic peak from the butoxycarbonyl group appeared (fig. S22A). In addition, the successful synthesis of PEG/BD was further validated by comparing the guanidine content and NO release of PEG/AD and PEG/BD (fig. S22, B and C). The PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn nonnanomotor was also synthesized according to the similar method (BD is a block copolymer synthesized from M-Arg modified with BOC protecting groups and DPA, where B represents M-Arg modified with BOC protecting groups and D represents DPA). TEM images revealed that both PEG/BD and PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn were spherical particles with uniform size distributions (Fig. 4, A and B). Moreover, they exhibited similar hydrodynamic diameters and surface charges at room temperature (Fig. 4, B and C). Next, the NO release of CNMs in prestimulated SH-SY5Y cells was explored, which can last for at least 24 hours (Fig. 4D). In addition, the motion behavior of CNMs in different cells was explored. We evaluated the motility of CNMs in the context of bEnd.3 and prestimulated SH-SY5Y cells, as well as the motility of the PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn nonnanomotor in the prestimulated SH-SY5Y cell environment (fig. S23). The results showed that CNMs showed random Brownian motion in bEnd.3 cells. Notably, a similar movement pattern was observed in the PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn nonnanomotor in prestimulated SH-SY5Y cells. These results suggest that the motion effect of CNMs requires an inflammatory environment, whereas nonnanomotors do not have the ability to move voluntarily (Fig. 4E). In contrast, CNMs exhibited more pronounced trajectory in the prestimulated SH-SY5Y cell environment (Fig. 4E). We also selected 30 particles to calculate their speed distribution. As can be seen from the velocity distribution in Fig. 4E, the speed distribution of the PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn nonnanomotor is concentrated at 1.0 μm s−1, while the speed distribution of CNMs is concentrated at 3.3 μm s−1.

Furthermore, the temperature-responsive behavior of CNMs was confirmed. The CNM solution exhibited reduced transmittance and smaller particle size at 37°C compared to that at 25°C (fig. S24). Subsequently, α-syn preformed fibers (α-syn PFFs) were chosen as an in vitro model to study the recognition and capture of α-syn PFFs by CNMs. As α-syn PFFs are structurally unstable, they aggregate to form α-syn aggregates when incubated in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (37). Fluorescence colocalization was used to verify the specific recognition and adsorption ability of CNMs. As shown in Fig. 4F, the red fluorescence of CNMs colocalized well with the green fluorescence of α-syn but not with the green fluorescence of other proteins, indicating that CNMs can selectively recognize and adsorb α-syn. Furthermore, the effect of CNMs on α-syn protein aggregation in vitro was investigated at different time points using the thioflavin T (ThT) method (19). The curve of pure α-syn ThT fluorescence spectra during incubation follows a classical nucleation-growth process, which is consistent with the α-syn fibrogenesis kinetics reported in the previous literature (38). Notably, when CNMs were co-incubated with α-syn PFFs, there was an inhibitory effect on α-syn aggregation, and the maximum ThT intensity was greatly reduced (81.8%) compared to pure α-syn aggregation (Fig. 4G). In addition, fluorescence images of α-syn PFFs co-incubated with different samples for 6 days were continuously observed. Results indicated that a large amount of aggregation occurred on the third day when α-syn PFFs were incubated alone, and CNMs could inhibit the aggregation of α-syn. Co-incubation with other samples inhibited the aggregation of α-syn to some extent, but large aggregations were also formed on the fifth day (fig. S25). The TEM results showed that long and thin fibrils were formed when α-syn PFFs were incubated alone, α-syn PFFs were adsorbed on the surface of CNMs after co-incubation with CNMs, and no large aggregates or fibers can be observed. Furthermore, given that PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn nonnanomotors lack arginine components, they do not exhibit autonomous movement, acting instead as passive particles that limit their ability to interact with α-syn in the solution, making it difficult for them to fully interact with surrounding α-syn, thus showing differences compared to PEG/AD/PNC/βsyn (Fig. 4H). Meanwhile, the α-syn protein solution during co-incubation was detected by the DLS method, and the hydrated particle size of the pure α-syn protein solution gradually increased to about 250 nm at 9 hours, while the protein solution co-incubated with CNMs stably showed peaks from α-syn (hydrated particle size of about 4 nm) and CNMs (hydrated particle size of about 200 nm) (Fig. 4I), indicating that CNMs have excellent efficacy to recognize α-syn and inhibit their aggregation.

Cellular uptake behavior and the ability of CNMs to penetrate the in vitro BBB model

After determining the NO release and motility ability of CNMs in neuronal cells, we proceeded to evaluate their ability to regulate cellular uptake, lysosomal escape, BBB crossing, and intracellular regulation of α-syn misfolding in vitro. First, the effect of CNMs on cell viability was studied. Cell viability assays were performed on SH-SY5Y and bEnd.3 cells treated with different samples. As shown in fig. S26 (A and B), when the concentration of CNMs reached 0.6 mg ml−1, there was a slight decrease in cell viability in SH-SY5Y, which may be due to the high concentration of CNMs producing high concentrations of NO, which adversely affected the cells. However, there was no obvious effect on the viability of bEnd.3 cells (fig. S26C), indicating that CNMs had good cytocompatibility. We then continued to investigate the effect of CNMs on the cell viability of stimulated SH-SY5Y cells after co-incubation for different times. As shown in fig. S26C, the viability of SH-SY5Y cells was enhanced (from 64.3 to 88.9%) after 18 hours of co-incubation with CNMs compared with the model cells. This may be due to the ability of CNMs to reduce ROS levels in model cells and partially restore neuronal cell activity. Figure S26D also shows that after 24 hours of co-incubation with prestimulated SH-SY5Y cells, cell viability in the CNM-treated group was much higher than that in other groups. The chemotaxis effect of CNMs toward iNOS highly expressed in damaged neuronal cells was validated, and the chemotaxis kinetics along the iNOS concentration gradient were investigated. As shown in fig. S27A, to simulate the concentration gradient of iNOS, an agarose gel containing stimulated SH-SY5Y cell lysate is added to Y channel chamber III to continuously generate a concentration gradient of chemokine iNOS, while an agarose gel containing bEnd.3 cell lysate is added to chamber II as a control. The results showed that when the PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn nonnanomotor was placed in chamber I, chambers II and III exhibited similar weak fluorescence intensity, whereas when chamber I was placed with CNMs, a very pronounced fluorescence intensity can be observed in chamber III compared to chamber II (fig. S27, B to E). It could be seen that CNMs moved in the direction of higher chemokine concentration, while PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn nonnanomotors without a motion ability exhibited nonselective random diffusion behavior. The concentration of iNOS in SH-SY5Y cells stimulated at varying densities was quantified. As depicted in fig. S28, the iNOS concentrations at cell densities of 103, 104, 105, and 106 were ~1.69, 3.01, 4.29, and 5.45 μM, respectively. Subsequently, the chemotactic effect of nanomotors in a Y-shaped channel was assessed under different chemoattractor gradients, corresponding to different initial cell densities. As illustrated in fig. S29, when the density of stimulated SH-SY5Y cells was reduced from 106 to 103 cells per milliliter (i.e., the intracellular iNOS concentration decreased from 5.45 to 1.69 μM), the chemotactic behavior of CNMs was no longer detectable. This may be attributed to insufficient iNOS chemoattractant provided by the cells at this density to trigger the chemotactic response of the nanomotors. Moreover, the chemotaxis distance of CNMs was investigated. Cy5-labeled CNMs were placed in storage chamber (I), while agarose gel–embedded lysates of stimulated SH-SY5Y cells, containing high concentrations of iNOS, were positioned in storage chamber (II) (fig. S30A). Fluorescence images of storage chamber (II) were captured at various time points using an inverted fluorescence microscope. The chemotactic ability of different samples toward the microenvironment at varying distances from the single channel was evaluated on the basis of the degree of sample aggregation in storage chamber (II). The results indicated that strong red fluorescence was observed at a single-channel distance of 0.5 cm. As the single-channel distance increased (1.0, 2.0, 4.0, and 5.0 cm), the fluorescence intensity of CNMs at 60 min gradually decreased and, at a distance of 5.0 cm, exhibited fluorescence intensity comparable to that at 0 min, suggesting that diffusive movement had ceased (fig. S30, B and C). In addition, we also detected the directional motion of CNMs in the microfluidic channel. As shown in fig. S31A, when buffers I and III were both PBS and buffer II was a Cy5-labeled sample, neither the red fluorescence of PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn nor PEG/AD/PNC/βsyn CNMs were obviously shifted because of the absence of a concentration gradient of iNOS on either side of the middle channel (fig. S31, B and C). As shown in fig. S31D, when buffers I, II, and III were normal bEnd.3 cell lysate, Cy5-labeled sample, and prestimulated SH-SY5Y cell lysate, respectively, the red fluorescence of CNMs shifted to the side with higher concentration of iNOS because of the presence of iNOS concentration gradients on either side of the middle channel, while there was no obvious shift in the red fluorescence of the PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn nonnanomotor (fig. S31, E and F).

Subsequently, the uptake capacity of SH-SY5Y for CNMs was evaluated by quantitative and qualitative analyses. For SH-SY5Y cells with a high expression of iNOS, the intracellular fluorescence intensity after co-incubation with the arginine-containing samples (PEG/AD/βsyn, PEG/AD/PNC, and PEG/AD/PNC/βsyn CNMs) was at least 12-fold higher than that after co-incubation with the PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn nonnanomotor (Fig. 5, A and C, and fig. S32), indicating that the motility effect of the nanomotor can make it easier to be taken up by neuronal cells. These results also confirmed the effect of CNMs with a motion ability on cellular uptake behavior. In this study, unstimulated cells were used as controls. The control group displayed relatively low fluorescence intensity and similar uptake, further confirming the specificity of CNMs for uptake by damaged neurons (fig. S33). To elucidate the primary pathway by which PEG/AD/PNC/βsyn CNMs enter neuronal cells, SH-SY5Y cells were treated with different endocytosis inhibitors. These inhibitors included chlorpromazine (Chlor.; a clathrin endocytosis inhibitor), nystatin (a vesicle protein endocytosis inhibitor), cytochalasin D (Cyto. D; a macropinocytosis inhibitor), and 4°C cold treatment (energy suppression) (39). As shown in Fig. 5 (B and D) and fig. S34, chlorpromazine and 4°C cryogenic treatment inhibited the uptake of CNMs by SH-SY5Y cells. In contrast, nystatin and cytochalasin D had minimal effects on CNM uptake. These findings indicate that the primary pathway for CNMs entering SH-SY5Y cells is clathrin-mediated endocytosis. The reduced uptake of CNMs by SH-SY5Y cells after 4°C cold treatment is likely attributed to decreased cell metabolism and membrane fluidity under low-temperature conditions, which in turn suppresses energy-dependent endocytosis (40).

Timely lysosomal escape is essential for the delivery of CNMs and their therapeutic effects (41). We investigated the lysosomal escape behavior of Cy5-labeled PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn and PEG/AD/PNC/βsyn CNMs by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) imaging and Pearson colocalization analysis to quantify the escape ability (Fig. 5E and figs. S35 and S36). As can be seen in Fig. 5E, for both PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn and PEG/AD/PNC/βsyn CNMs, the Pearson coefficient at 1 hour after treatment is greater than that at 0.5 hours, which may be due to the fact that 0.5 hours is not sufficient for the different samples to be fully internalized. When the incubation time was extended from 1 to 6 hours, the Pearson coefficient decreased from 0.70 to 0.31 for PEG/AD/PNC/βsyn CNMs compared to 0.90 for the PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn nonnanomotor, indicating that the lysosomal escape ability of PEG/AD/PNC/βsyn CNMs was much better than that of the PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn nonnanomotor under the same conditions. CNMs can escape lysosomes in a short period of time so as to further stay in the cytoplasm to play a subsequent therapeutic role.

For the treatment of PD, therapeutic agents are also facing the challenge of breaking through the BBB. We assessed the ability of CNMs to penetrate in vitro BBB models. An in vitro BBB model was first established (Fig. 5F). bEnd.3 cells were seeded in the upper chamber of a Transwell model for 10 consecutive days to simulate a dense BBB layer. Successful construction of the BBB was confirmed by detecting the expression and conducting permeability tests of tight-junction proteins (zonula occludens–1 and claudin-5) (fig. S37) (42, 43). When the chamber contained PBS (with no iNOS concentration gradient), different Cy5-labeled samples were added to the upper chamber for 12 hours. Compared with other samples, there was no obvious change in the transport rate of CNMs, and the transport rates were all around 10.0% (Fig. 5G). When the lower chamber was replaced with a prestimulated SH-SY5Y cell lysate solution with an iNOS concentration gradient, different samples labeled with Cy5 were added to the upper chamber, and the transport rate of CNMs increased with time compared with the nonnanomotor group, reaching 63.1% at 12 hours (Fig. 5H). In addition, prestimulated SH-SY5Y cells were incubated in the lower chamber of Transwell, and the red fluorescent signal of Cy5 in the SH-SY5Y cells after stimulation in the lower layer was observed with CLSM. As shown in Fig. 5 (I and J) and fig. S38, compared with the nonnanomotor group, the arginine-containing nanomotor group was able to break through the BBB cell layer to a certain extent, indicating that CNMs can penetrate the BBB and be absorbed by SH-SY5Y cells. It is important to note that the integrity of the BBB after penetration of different samples is very important, so we used CLSM to test the structural integrity before and after the penetration of the BBB. As shown in fig. S39, after 24 hours of treatment with different samples, the dense BBB cell layer remained intact. FITC-dextran (FD-40, 40 kDa) was chosen as a detection agent, given that it can reflect BBB compactness by monitoring the fluorescent signal intensity (44). As shown in fig. S40, no obvious increase in FD-40 fluorescence intensity was detected in the lower chamber after treatment with different samples. This indicates that CNMs penetrate the BBB by sensing the iNOS concentration gradient without disrupting the BBB structure.

Next, we further explored the potential role of CNMs in the degradation of α-syn aggregates in neuronal cells. α-Syn PFFs can act as “seeds” to induce endogenous α-syn misfolding and denaturation, resulting in α-syn misfolding and aggregation (45). To demonstrate the power and role of CNMs in clearing α-syn aggregates, we used α-syn PFFs (5 μg ml−1) to co-incubate with neuronal cells for 3 days and treated them with different samples. The results showed that the expression of the dopaminergic neuronal marker tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) in neuronal cells treated with α-syn PFFs decreased and the expression of α-syn aggregates increased, while the TH and α-syn aggregate levels in neuronal cells treated with CNMs basically returned to the similar level to normal cells (Fig. 5K). Notably, pathological aggregation of α-syn can damage neurons and lead to cell death (45). We further investigated the protective effect of CNMs on neuronal cells stimulated by α-syn PFFs. As shown in Fig. 5L, the cell viability of the α-syn PFF treatment group was 67.2%, while the cell viability of the CNM-treated α-syn PFF group was about 94.5%, implying that the cell viability of neuronal cells can be restored. In addition, the qualitative and quantitative assessment of α-syn aggregate level in neuronal cells treated with different samples was performed by CLSM. As shown in fig. S41, α-syn PFFs could induce α-syn aggregation in neurons. The α-syn aggregation was inhibited to some extent during other sample treatments, but a large degree of aggregation occurred at 12 hours (figs. S42 to S44 and Fig. 5M). After 12 hours of treatment of neurons with CNMs, there was a reduction in α-syn aggregates, indicating that CNMs were able to inhibit the aggregation of α-syn for a short period of time and degrade the α-syn aggregates that had formed (fig. S45 and Fig. 5M).

In vivo targeting behavior of CNMs

After confirming in vitro that CNMs have the ability to break through the BBB, enter neurons more quickly, and degrade α-syn aggregates, we continued to study the in vivo brain-targeting ability of CNMs in PD model mice. First, PD model mice were injected with the Cy5-labeled PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn nonnanomotor and PEG/AD/PNC/βsyn CNMs through the tail vein for in vivo targeting. CNMs accumulated in the brain at 1 hour after injection, and Cy5 fluorescence was strong in the brain and was able to maintain it for at least 24 hours (Fig. 6, A and B). The PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn nonnanomotor exhibited less intracerebral accumulation. The brain-targeting capabilities of CNMs were further confirmed by organ in vitro imaging results (Fig. 6C and fig. S46). Subsequently, after grinding the brain and other important organs, the fluorescence intensity of the slurry was measured and the biodistribution of different samples was calculated. The intracerebral delivery efficiency of CNMs was 19.2%ID (percent injection dose) compared to 7.9%ID for the PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn nonnanomotor, and the fluorescence of all samples was mainly accumulated in the liver and kidneys of the metabolic organs of mice (Fig. 6D). To further characterize whether the sample aggregates in damaged substantia nigra (SN) neurons, dopaminergic neurons in SN were stained with TH to assess the colocalization of the sample (red) with TH (green). As shown in Fig. 6E and fig. S47, the accumulation of CNMs in the brain was much higher than that of PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn nonnanomotors within 24 hours after tail vein injection in different samples. Moreover, CNMs had a low red fluorescent signal in non-SN regions, suggesting that CNMs are selective for damaged neurons in SN regions.

Evaluation of therapeutic effect in vivo

The above results indicated that CNMs could accurately target the brains of PD mice and accumulate them at the disease site, which provided a guarantee for subsequent treatment. The in vivo pharmacokinetics of CNMs were also determined, which displayed that CNMs exhibited long blood circulation time (at least 20 hours) (fig. S48). Subsequently, 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine hydrochloride (MPTP)–induced PD model mice were used to study the in vivo treatment effect of CNMs. The experimental process is shown in Fig. 7A, MPTP was injected intraperitoneally for 10 days to construct a PD mouse model, and then different samples were injected through the tail vein for seven consecutive days, and the evaluation began after 7 days. Given that PD mainly manifested as motion and cognitive impairment, the spontaneous motor activity, motion coordination, and memory improvement of mice were evaluated by a climbing pole test, open field test, and Morris water maze test, respectively (31). The normal group represents healthy mice, and the model group represents MPTP-induced PD mice. As shown in Fig. 7B, compared with normal mice, the time for mice in the model group to climb to the bottom of the rod was increased (23.5 s), indicating that the motion coordination ability of the mice in the model group was impaired; the motion coordination ability was improved to a certain extent after treatment with different samples; and the average time to climb to the bottom of the rod in the CNM treatment group was reduced a lot (13.5 s). In the open field test, we recorded the average locomotion speed, total distance walked, and movement trajectory of mice in each group. The results showed that the average speed (5.0 cm s−1) and total walking distance (2.6 m) of the model group were lower than those of the normal mice (8.7 cm s−1, 4.9 m). Mice treated with PEG/AD/βsyn, PEG/AD/PNC, and PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn showed some improvement in speed and distance, preferably to 6.2 cm s−1 and 3.5 m, while the CNM treatment group showed better treatment results (8.8 cm s−1 and 5.0 m) (Fig. 7, C and D). In addition, as shown in Fig. 7E, the motion pathways of PD mice exhibited a trajectory of walking along the wall compared to normal mice. The motion behavior of mice treated with different samples was improved to a certain extent, and their motion trajectories were gradually similar to those of normal mice, but the motion trajectories of mice treated with CNMs were the closest to those of normal mice. In the Morris water maze test, the ratio of escape time and target quadrant time in the plateau hiding period of mice was evaluated (Fig. 7, F and G). After treatment with CNMs, the escape time of mice was shortened (from 28.0 to 7.0 s), and the proportion of target quadrant residence time was extended (from 21.2 to 49.3%). The number of times the mice crossed the target quadrant and the swimming path without a platform were further recorded. Compared with the model group of mice, the frequency of crossing the target platform was increased in the PD mice treated with CNMs, indicating an improvement in their cognitive impairment symptoms (Fig. 7H). After treatment with CNMs, the movement pathways of PD mice were mainly concentrated in the target quadrant, suggesting that CNMs could induce the recovery of spatial memory impairment in PD mice (Fig. 7I). The above results showed that CNMs improved the spontaneous movement, motion coordination, and memory abilities of PD mice.

To further reveal the therapeutic mechanism of CNMs, multiple indexes in SN in mice after treatment were detected. First, ROS in mice SN were stained. In addition, ROS in brain SN treated with CNMs were reduced a lot compared to other sample treatment groups (Fig. 8, A and C, and fig. S49), which may be due to the fact that arginine in CNMs can react with ROS in the PD microenvironment, thereby reducing neuroinflammation in SN. The most direct manifestation of neuroinflammation is the activation of microglia, and ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba-1) is often used as a marker of microglial activation to reflect neuroinflammation (46). As expected, there was an increase in Iba-1 in PD model mice and a decrease in the number of activated microglia in the SN region of PD mice treated with CNMs, suggesting that CNMs were effective in alleviating inflammation within the brains of PD mice (Fig. 8, B and D, and fig. S50). The loss of dopaminergic neurons in the SN region is another pathological marker of PD, so TH, which represents dopaminergic neurons, was also selected as the test index (31). The number of TH-positive neurons was increased in the SN region of PD mice treated with CNMs (Fig. 8, E and K, and fig. S51). Next, we evaluated the ability of NO produced by CNMs to degrade and inhibit α-syn aggregate formation. Research indicates that up-regulated MMP-2 promotes the formation of smaller, amorphous α-syn aggregates, suggesting that MMP-2 may inhibit α-syn aggregation and promote its degradation (47). Consequently, we assessed MMP-2 expression levels in the midbrain. The results demonstrated that the model group exhibited lower MMP-2 expression than the wild-type group. However, administration of samples containing the arginine-based power unit activated MMP-2 expression in PD mice. Notably, the CNM-treated group showed an increase in MMP-2 expression compared to the model group, reaching ~5.0 times that of the nonmotor group (Fig. 8F). Moreover, α-syn in TH-positive neurons was reduced (the fluorescence intensity was reduced by about 6.0 times; Fig. 8, G and L, and fig. S52), indicating that CNMs can inhibit the formation of α-syn aggregates and have good dopaminergic neuronal repair function. Both the down-regulation of α-syn protein levels and reduction of ROS contributed to the curbing of α-syn aggregate formation. As shown in Fig. 8H, the α-syn level in the model group was markedly up-regulated compared to the normal group, yet it was down-regulated to ~40.0% of the model group’s level following CNM treatment. Furthermore, the level of α-syn aggregates in the SN region of the midbrain was detected using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit. As shown in fig. S53, after 10 days of intraperitoneal MPTP injection, the α-syn aggregate levels in the midbrain SN region of mice, except for normal mice, increased a lot (about 9.0 times higher than those in the normal group). Posttreatment, these levels decreased to some extent, with CNM-treated PD mice showing the most reduction in α-syn aggregates. Compared with the model group, it was reduced by 84.4% and basically returned to the normal level (Fig. 8I). CNMs can promote the repair of the natural chaperone system after effectively modulating the inflammatory microenvironment at the site of PD injury (48). To validate this result, we used immunofluorescence staining and a Western blotting quantification system to detect the regulation of HSP70 expression in the midbrains of treated mice (49). The study found that PD model mice had much lower levels of HSP70 in the midbrain (about one-ninth of that in wild-type mice) compared to wild-type mice, while CNM-treated mice exhibited an up-regulation of HSP70 activity in midbrain tissue (increased to levels comparable to wild-type mice) (Fig. 8, J and M, and fig. S54). Western blot results showed that compared with wild-type mice, the level of HSP70 in the midbrains of PD model mice was reduced a lot, while the activity of HSP70 in the midbrain tissue of mice treated with CNMs was up-regulated (Fig. 8N). In addition, CNMs increased the anti-inflammatory factor transforming growth factor–β (TGF-β) and interleukin-10 (IL-10) in the brains of PD mice (Fig. 8, O and P), and these results suggest that CNM treatment can alleviate neuroinflammation in PD mice. In contrast, PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn nonnanomotors lacking targeting capacity and other treatment groups did not have good treatment effects. In addition, a neurospecific protein called growth-associated protein 43 (GAP-43), which is highly expressed during neuronal development and regeneration, is used as an indicator to assess neuronal growth (50). The content of GAP-43 in the SN region of the brain of the model group was much lower than that of wild-type mice, while the level of GAP-43 was increased in the CNM treatment group (Fig. 8, Q and R, and fig. S55), indicating a positive effect on neural regeneration. Last, to assess the biosafety of CNMs, they were validated by in vitro hemolysis assays and hematoxylin and eosin staining. The results showed that CNMs had good hemocompatibility (Fig. S56). The major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) of the treated mice in each group were observed under a microscope. The results showed that the state of the major organs in each group was similar to that of the normal group, with no obvious damage, indicating that the CNMs proposed here had good biocompatibility (fig. S57).

DISCUSSION

In summary, we have successfully constructed the CNMs and verified their practicality in the treatment of PD. Through extensive experiments, we optimized the synthetic ratio of the modules. When the molar ratio of PEG/AD to PNC is 1:2.5, the PEG/AD/PNC composite micelle exhibits both chemotactic motion and temperature responsiveness, rapidly forming hydrophobic microdomains at normal human body temperature. In vitro experiments confirmed that CNMs can maintain α-syn stability and prevent aggregation for at least 6 days. In addition, DLS results confirmed the inhibitory effect of CNMs on α-syn aggregation; α-syn treated with CNMs remained stable and nonaggregated, whereas pure α-syn aggregated within 3 hours. Cellular experiments also proved that CNMs can inhibit the formation of α-syn aggregates. Furthermore, the motility of CNMs enhances their lysosomal escape ability, achieving lysosomal escape within 6 hours, thereby functioning in the cytoplasm. CNMs can sensitively sense the high expression of iNOS in the PD microenvironment, perform chemotaxis, and penetrate the BBB to effectively target the PD microenvironment. The BBB penetration rate of CNMs increased from 9.6% (nonnanomotor) to 61.6%, and the in vivo brain targeting efficiency improved from 7.9% (nonnanomotor) to 19.2%. Moreover, CNMs showed higher targeting efficiency in the PD-affected SN region, with fluorescence intensity 15 times higher than in non-SN areas. Behavioral tests showed that CNMs restored PD mouse models to near-normal levels after 1 week of treatment. Animal experiments also revealed that CNMs can reduce ROS, alleviate neuroinflammation, and degrade α-syn aggregates, with α-syn aggregates in CNM-treated mice being 84.4% lower than in the model group. The chemotactic modification of nanochaperones addresses the challenge of active brain injury targeting and shows the potential as a universal strategy. However, for clinical translation, several challenges must be overcome. First, the scalable production of CNMs needs to be optimized. The current synthesis relies on ATRP and precise self-assembly, which may face batch-to-batch variations at an industrial scale. Developing stable production formulations while maintaining module functions (such as chemotaxis and α-syn recognition) will be crucial. Second, human-specific targeting needs further validation. CNMs effectively target damaged neurons in the mouse midbrain through iNOS gradients, while human PD pathologies involve heterogeneous α-syn aggregation patterns and potential differences in BBB permeability. Testing CNMs in human neurons or primate models could bridge this gap. In addition, the long-term biosafety still needs comprehensive evaluation. While acute biocompatibility and blood compatibility have been confirmed, chronic exposure studies are necessary to assess potential off-target effects or immune responses. Addressing these challenges will accelerate the translation of CNMs into viable treatment strategies for neurodegenerative diseases.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

l-Arginine (l-Arg) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Biotech. 1,4-Dioxane and acetone were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. DPA, mPEG, hydroxyethyl 2-bromoisobutyrate (HEBIB), EDC, and NHS were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. α-Isobutyl bromide, ε-caprolactone, NIPAm, cuprous bromide (CuBr), stannous octanoate [Sn(Oct)2], and 2,2-bipyridine (bpy) were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. βsyn36 was purchased from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd. Agarose was purchased from Shanghai Baygene Biotechnologies Company Limited. MTT [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-acyl)-2,5-dibenzotetrazolium bromide] and MPTP were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich Co., Ltd. Hoechst 33342 was acquired from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. Lysosomal dye, NO content assay kit, and 1,1′-Octaco-3,3′,3′-tetramethylindolecarbonylcyanocyine perchlorate (Dil) were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology. α-Syn protein was purchased from StressMarq Biosciences Inc. The α-syn aggregates assay kit was purchased from Lunchangshuo Biotech. TGF-β, IL-10, human iNOS, and the mouse MMP-2 assay kit were purchased from Jiangsu Meimian Industrial Co., Ltd.

Preparation of the polymer PEG/AD

The arginine monomer (M-Arg) was first synthesized by preparing an ice water bath with l-Arg (2.0 g, 11.5 mmol) in 20.0 ml of distilled water and adding 8.5 ml of 1,4-dioxane and 4.5 ml of triethylamine. The mixture was cooled in an ice water bath and stirred, and methacrylic anhydride (3.0 ml, 18.9 mmol) was added within 10 min. The ice water bath was removed, and the mixture was stirred overnight. The product was slowly added to 400.0 ml of acetone, and the lower precipitate was collected, centrifuged, and freeze dried. The resulting solid is named M-Arg.

The macromolecular initiator PEG-Br was then synthesized. mPEG (5.0 g, 2.5 mmol) was evaporated and dried with toluene. The polymer was dissolved in freshly distilled tetrahydrofuran and placed in a flask. Triethylamine (1.0 ml, 7.5 mmol) was added and cooled to 0°C. Then, α-isobutyl bromide (616.0 μl, 5.0 mmol) was added dropwise. Next, the resulting solution was stirred for 24 hours while slowly warming to room temperature. After the reaction, the white precipitate was filtered out, the solution was concentrated, and the product was dried at room temperature to obtain PEG-Br. The synthesis steps of PEG/AD are as follows: First, CuBr was purified, soaked in glacial acetic acid for 24 hours, then centrifuged and washed with methanol for several times, and stored in vacuum. The macromolecular initiator PEG-Br (132.0 mg, 0.01 mmol, 1 equiv) was mixed with M-Arg (160.0 mg, 0.2 mmol, 20 equiv). After homogenization, 2.0 ml of water was added, and the solution was purged with nitrogen for 40 min. Then, CuBr (4.7 mg, 0.01 mmol) was added to the reaction system along with the same amount of copper powder and bpy (3.2 mg, 0.2 mmol). The reaction proceeded for 2 hours. The mixture of DPA (150.0 μl, 0.7 mmol) and methanol was added to the viscous mixture with a syringe, and the whole reaction system was purged with nitrogen for 10 hours. The reaction was continued for 10 hours. The resulting solution was dialyzed and then freeze dried to obtain the product PEG/AD.

Synthesis of PNIPAm-b-PCL

First, the macromolecular initiator PCL-Br was synthesized by ring-opening polymerization in a toluene solution with ε-caprolactone as the monomer, Sn(Oct)2 as the catalyst, and HEBIB as the initiator. Briefly, HEBIB (0.1 g, 0.3 mmol), ε-caprolactone (2.3 g, 20.0 mmol), and Sn(Oct)2 (0.2 g, 0.4 mmol) will be added to the reaction flask, followed by the addition of 5.0 ml of toluene. After the freeze-degassing-thawing cycle, polymerization was performed at 110°C for 12 hours. The mixture was then diluted with methylene chloride and then precipitated into excess ether. PCL-b-PNIPAm was synthesized by ATRP. Briefly, PCL-Br (0.218 g, 1.125 mol), CuBr (0.045 g, 0.045 mmol), bpy (0.1 g, 0.1 mmol), and NIPAm (0.3 g, 2.3 mol) were added to the reaction flask, followed by the addition of 4 ml of N,N′-dimethylformamide (DMF). After the freeze-degassing-thawing cycle, the reaction system reacted at 45°C for 24 hours. The copper catalyst was removed by methanol/dichloroethane column chromatography, the organic solvent was removed by dialysis, and the product was freeze dried to obtain the product, which was named PNIPAm-b-PCL.

Synthesis of PEG/AD/PNC composite micelles

PEG/AD (1) and PNIPAm-b-PCL (2) were dissolved in DMF according to the set molar ratios ([1]:[2] = 1:1, [1]:[2] = 1:2.5, [1]:[2] = 1:5, and [1]:[2] = 1:10) to prepare a polymer solution with a concentration of 1.0 mg ml−1. Subsequently, a certain amount of distilled water droplets was added to the polymer solution with vigorous agitation until opalescence appears, indicating micelle formation. The solution was stirred overnight and then dialyzed with water to remove DMF.

Preparation of PEG/AD/PNC/βsyn CNMs

First, composite micelles were synthesized, and PEG/AD (2.0 mg, 0.5 mmol) and PNIPAm-b-PCL (11.0 mg, 1.2 mmol) were dissolved in DMF to prepare polymer solutions with concentrations of 1.0 mg ml−1. Subsequently, a certain amount of distilled water droplets was added to the polymer solution under vigorous agitation until opalescence appears, indicating the formation of composite micelles, and the solution was stirred overnight. Then, the ─COOH on PEG/AD was activated, and in short, EDC (140.0 mg, 0.7 mmol) and NHS (84.6 mg, 0.7 mmol) were fed into the reaction system, stirred overnight at room temperature, and dialyzed with water to remove DMF. Then, 1 mg of the peptide βsyn36 was added and stirred overnight at room temperature to obtain the PEG/AD/PNC/βsyn CNM.

Material characterizations

The structure and functional groups of the samples were characterized and analyzed by a Bruker Avance 400 spectrometer and a Cary 5000 Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Varian, US). The prepared samples were dispersed in water, the solution was dropped on a carbon support membrane, and its morphological characteristics and the elemental distribution of the samples were observed using an electron microscope JEM-2100 (TEM, Hitachi, Japan). The hydrated particle size and potential of these nanoparticles were determined by a nanoparticle size and zeta potential analyzer (MALVERN, UK). All fluorescence images of the cells were taken by a confocal laser scanning microscope (HP Apo TIRF 100×, 1.49 numerical aperture, Nikon, Ti-E-A1R, Japan).

Determination of CMC

The CMC of micelles was determined by the pyrene fluorescent probe method (51). The specific operation protocol is as follows. First, a series of concentrations of copolymer solution (1 × 10−3 to 2 mg ml−1) was prepared by the dilution method. Then, the acetone solution of pyrene was prepared: 2 mg of pyrene was dissolved in 16.7 ml of acetone (6 × 10−4 M), and 1.0 ml was added to 99.0 ml of water (the final concentration of pyrene solution was 6 × 10−6 M) so that acetone volatilizes naturally. Next, the pyrene solution was added to the copolymer solution with different concentrations (the final concentration of pyrene solution is 6 × 10−7 mol). The pyrene emission spectra at 25°C were recorded by a fluorescence spectrophotometer with an excitation wavelength of 335 nm.

Colocalization of PEG/AD with PNC

First, PEG/AD was labeled with Cy5 dye (red), and PNC was labeled with FITC dye (green). PEG/AD/PNC composite micelles in different proportions were then prepared according to the synthesis method described earlier. Then, 200.0 μl of the sample solution was added to the confocal dish, and the fluorescence images under different channels were taken using an inverted fluorescence microscope with a 100× objective, and the colocalization of the red signal of Cy5-labeled PEG/AD and the green signal of FITC-labeled PNC was analyzed by ImageJ software.

Cell culture

Human neuroblastoma cells SH-SY5Y (neural cell model, purchased from Shanghai Xinyu Life Science and Technology Co., Ltd.) were cultured in 1:1 minimum essential medium and Ham’s F12 culture medium. bEnd.3 cells (purchased from Wuhan Prosel Life Science and Technology Co., Ltd.) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium. Both cell cultures were supplemented with 10.0% fetal bovine serum and 1.0% penicillin-streptomycin. The cell density at 1 × 105 cells per milliliter in a cell standard incubator at 37°C and a humidified atmosphere of 5.0% CO2 was maintained.

Cell viability assays

SH-SY5Y cells (1 × 104 cells per milliliter) were seeded in a 96-well plate and incubated for 24 hours adherently. Samples of different concentrations (0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.40, 0.60, and 0.80 mg ml−1) were incubated with cells for 24 hours, the supernatant was discarded, 20.0 μl of MTT (5.0 mg ml−1) was added to each well and continued to incubate at 37°C for 4 hours, 150.0 μl of DMSO was added to each well, and the absorbance at 570 nm was measured using a microplate reader to determine cell viability. In addition, similar protocols were used for cell viability for different samples and different treatment times. The cell viability was calculated according to the following formula: Cell viability (%) = [OD (samples) − OD (control)]/[OD (no samples) − OD (control)] × 100, where OD is the optical density.

NO release assay

After seeding SH-SY5Y cells (105 cells per milliliter) for 24 hours in a six-well plate, the sample to be assayed (0.2 mg ml−1) was added. After a certain period of co-incubation, the supernatant was discarded, the lower cells were gently washed with PBS, 200.0 μl of cell lysate was added to fully lyse for 15 min, and the NO content in the sample was determined using the NO kit.

Determination of guanidine content

The guanidine content was determined by the 1-naphthol-diacetyl colorimetric method (52). First, different concentrations of l-Arg aqueous solutions were prepared, and the standard curve was determined. PEG/AD (100.0 μl, 1.0 mg ml−1) was added to the chromogenic solution consisting of 1.0 ml of NaOH aqueous solution (40.0 mg ml−1), 1.0 ml of 1-naphthol/n-propanol solution (80.0 mg ml−1), and 1 ml of diacetylyl/n-propanol solution (0.5 μl ml−1). Then, the mixture was incubated at 37°C for 30 min, the absorbance of the reaction solution at 570 nm was measured with a fully automated microplate reader, and the guanidine content in the sample was calculated from the concentration-absorbance standard curve.

Determination of the temperature sensitivity of polymers

The transmittance of polymer solutions of different concentrations at 600 nm was recorded on a ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer with a heating rate of 2°C min−1. DLS was performed to record the change in hydrated particle size of a polymer solution at a concentration of 1 mg ml−1 at 25° and 37°C.

Motion capture and analysis

SH-SY5Y cells (105 cells per milliliter) were stimulated with 0.1 mM H2O2, incubated at 37°C for 24 hours, and then lysed with cell lysate. Cy5-labeled samples were dispersed in PBS, each was added to a confocal dish containing 200.0 μl of cell lysate, and an inverted fluorescence microscope with a 100× objective was used to capture the movement of the different samples. Then, ImageJ software was used to track the particles to obtain the motion trajectory and coordinates, and 30 particles were taken in each group to calculate their average speed.

Analysis of collective chemotaxis behavior in the Y channel

To visualize the collective chemotaxis behavior of CNMs, a Y-shaped glass substrate channel was used with the following size parameters (fig. S27A): The main channel was 1 cm long and 0.4 cm wide, and the secondary channel was 0.7 cm long and 0.3 cm wide. The Y channel contained three chambers, where chamber (I) contained different samples labeled with Cy5, and chambers (II) and (III) contained stimulated SH-SY5Y cell lysate and bEnd.3 cell lysate, respectively. To prepare gels that mimic chemokine gradients, the agarose was mixed with the stimulated SH-SY5Y cell lysate and bEnd.3 cell lysate with an equal volume under room temperature in chambers (II) and (III), respectively, which was transferred to 4°C for gelation. Next, 400.0 μl of PBS was injected into the Y channel, keeping the liquid level flush with the entire channel, resulting in a chemotaxis concentration gradient. Different samples (50.0 μl) labeled with Cy5 were added to chamber (I), and the fluorescence images of chambers (II) and (III) were captured at certain times (0, 10, 30, and 60 min) using an inverted fluorescence microscope. Fluorescence intensity pseudocolored images are processed by ImageJ software.

Assessment of dynamic chemotaxis behavior

The dynamic chemotaxis of nanomotors was evaluated using a 3-in × 1-out, glass-based microfluidic channel with dimensions of 2.2 cm (length), 1.5 mm (width), and 300.0 μm (height) (fig. S28A). Among them, the H2O2-prestimulated SH-SY5Y cell lysate was buffer I, Cy5-labeled samples in PBS were buffer II, bEnd.3 cell lysate was buffer III-1, and PBS was buffer III-2. The flow rate was controlled at 0.6 ml hour−1. An inverted fluorescence microscope (10× objective) was used to capture video continuously for 1 min near the exit. The fluorescence intensity perpendicular to the flow direction was measured using ImageJ software to analyze the chemotaxis shift of the nanomotor.

Analysis of cell uptake behavior

The uptake of samples from different cells was performed as follows: SH-SY5Y cells (105 cells per milliliter) were seeded in a confocal dish and incubated for 24 hours in the adherent dish. H2O2 at a final concentration of 0.1 mM was then added and incubated at 37°C for 24 hours. In addition, bEnd.3 cells (105 cells per milliliter) were seeded in a confocal dish and incubated for 24 hours adherently. Different samples labeled with Cy5 were added to the dishes of the above two types of cells and incubated for 24 hours. The supernatant was then discarded from the dish, the cells were gently washed with PBS, and the cell membrane was stained with Dil (10.0 μM, in DMSO) for 20 min, followed by nuclei stained with Hoechst 33342 (10.0 μg ml−1) for 10 min. After gently washing the cells with PBS, fluorescence images of bEnd.3 and SH-SY5Y cells were obtained by using CLSM.

Evaluation of the ability to penetrate the BBB model in vitro

For the construction of the in vitro BBB model, 200.0 μl of bEnd.3 cells (cell density of 1 × 104 ml−1) was added to the nested upper chamber of the Transwell model for 10 days, 600.0 μl of SH-SY5Y cells (cell density of 1 × 105 ml−1) was inoculated in the corresponding lower chamber for 24 hours on day 8, and then H2O2 at a concentration of 0.1 mM was added in the lower chamber for further incubation for 24 hours. After day 10, the original medium in the upper chamber was discarded, and different samples labeled with Cy5 were added in the upper chamber for 24 hours. The amount of supernatant in the lower chamber and the amount of material contained in bEnd.3 cells after cell lysis were collected and recorded as the material remained. The transport rate was computed according to the following formula: Transport rate (%) = (Total amount of material added − Amount of material remained)/Total amount of material added × 100%. At the same time, SH-SY5Y cells in the lower chamber were stained with Dil and Hoechst 33342, and fluorescence images of SH-SY5Y cells in the lower chamber were observed using CLSM.

For the detection of the integrity of the BBB model, the in vitro BBB model before and after the addition of samples was treated with FITC-dextran to detect its integrity during the experiment period. Hank’s balanced salt solution (200.0 μl) containing an FITC-dextran 40-kDa solution (1 mg ml−1) was added to the upper chamber for 2 hours. The fluorescence intensity in the lower chamber was detected by using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (492-nm excitation and 520-nm emission). At the same time, CLSM was also used to observe the degree of tight connection of the BBB.

Hemolysis detection

Fresh mouse blood was used for hemolysis experiments. First, blood and separate red blood cells were collected by centrifugation at 2500 rpm min−1 for 10 min, which were diluted to obtain a 2.0% red blood cell suspension. The positive control group was diluted with ultrapure water. Next, samples with different concentrations (1× PBS dilution) were added to the red blood cell suspension and incubated at 37°C for 1 hour. The morphology of the red blood cell suspension before centrifugation was examined and recorded under a light microscope. In addition, the OD of hemoglobin was measured at 540 nm using a microplate reader. The hemolysis rate was computed according to the following formula: Hemolysis rate = [OD (samples) − OD (negative)]/[OD (positive) − OD (negative)] × 100%.

In vitro recognition of α-syn by CNMs

A bovine serum albumin (BSA), globulin (Glo), and whey protein (WPI) solution (1.0 mg ml−1) and α-syn protein solution (5.0 μg ml−1) were prepared, and 10.0 μl of Dil dye (green) was added and incubated at 37°C for 12 hours. Then, the free dye was removed by dialysis to obtain a Dil-labeled protein solution. The mixture was mixed with Cy5-labeled (red) PEG/AD/PNC/βsyn for 4 hours, and CLSM (20×) was performed to observe the colocalization of red and green fluorescence.

In vitro α-syn aggregation kinetics evaluation

α-Syn pure protein (2.0 μg ml−1) was dissolved in PBS (pH 7.4), H2O2 solution was added to form the final concentration of H2O2 to 0.10 mM, and the pure protein was first filtered using a 220.0-nm filter membrane to guarantee the removal of any preformed aggregations and incubated at 37°C at 100 rpm on a shaker for 6 days. Every 24 hours, an equal amount of 80.0 μl of incubated protein samples was taken out, and 80.0 μl of 5.0 μM ThT solution (ThT solution diluted in PBS mother liquor dissolved in PBS) was mixed and added to a 96-well plate protected from light. A fluorescence spectrophotometer was used to detect the fluorescence intensity of ThT, the excitation wavelength was set at 450 nm, the emission wavelength was set at 485 nm, and the oscillation was 20 s. All experiments were repeated 3.0 times.

The particle size of the mixture was also measured at the zeroth, third, sixth, and ninth hour of incubation. The protein solution was taken out, and the particle size distribution of the mixed solution was determined by the DLS method.

Evaluation of the ability of PEG/AD/PNC/βsyn to inhibit α-syn aggregation in SH-SY5Y cells

Cy5-labeled α-syn PFFs were incubated with SH-SY5Y cells for 3, 6, and 12 hours, and after the incubation was completed, the nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (10.0 μg ml−1). Then, the fluorescence images were recorded by CLSM.

Establishment of the animal model

C57BL/6 male mice (6 to 8 weeks) were randomly divided into five groups (n = 12). C57BL/6 mice were intraperitoneally injected with MPTP (30 mg kg−1) for 10 consecutive days to establish the PD model. All animal experiments were performed under the guidelines set by the Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Nanjing Normal University (IACUC-20200802, Nanjing, China).

In vivo targeting ability

To assess in vivo targeting, different samples (PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn and PEG/AD/PNC/βsyn) labeled with Cy5 were injected intravenously into PD mice, and the fluorescent signal in the brains of PD mice was continuously observed by a small-animal in vivo imager (AniView) for 48 hours. In addition, at 24 hours of injection, the brain tissue of the euthanized mice was removed to observe the imaging (excitation at 600 nm and emission at 680 nm). In addition, major organs (brain, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were extracted from normal and PD mice, and their biodistribution was monitored by an in vivo imaging system. Subsequently, the brain and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) of each group of mice were placed in centrifuge tubes (the liver was divided into four parts), and 1.0 ml of lysate was added to each centrifuge tube. After the brain and major organs were ground and broken using a grinder, the supernatant was centrifuged to a volume of 2.0 ml. A fluorescence spectrophotometer was used to detect the fluorescence intensity of the solution at 647-nm Cy5 (representing the sample). Then, the corresponding fluorescence intensity of different concentrations of samples was accurately measured to obtain the standard curve of concentration-fluorescence intensity, the fluorescence intensity of the sample solution was brought into the standard curve to obtain the corresponding sample concentration, and then the delivery efficiency was computed according to the following formula: Delivery efficiency (biodistribution) = Total amount of samples in the solution/Total amount of injected samples × 100%. The percentage of injection dose was computed according to the following formula: Percentage of injection dose (%ID) = (Amount of samples in organ tissue/Total amount of injected samples) × 100%.

Evaluation of the behavior of PD mice after treatment with different samples

After the establishment of the PD animal model, each group was injected with 100.0 μl of PBS, PEG/AD (0.4 mg ml−1), PEG/AD/PNC (0.4 mg ml−1), PEG/BD/PNC/βsyn (0.4 mg ml−1), and PEG/AD/PNC/βsyn (0.4 mg ml−1) through the tail vein for seven consecutive days. The behavioral observation was carried out through three aspects: the open field movement test, the pole climbing test, and the Morris water maze test.

To evaluate the recovery of spontaneous motor activity of PD mice after treatment with different samples, an open field experiment was conducted. Mice from different sample treatment groups were placed in the designated corners of the circular apparatus (d = 28.6 cm; h = 29.5 cm) one by one, their activities were recorded within 1 min, and the mice were tracked and evaluated using ImageJ software.

To evaluate the recovery of motor coordination in PD mice after treatment with different samples, a pole climbing test was performed. Mice in different treatment groups were placed head up on a 55.0-cm-long pole, and the time from the top of the pole to the ground was recorded.

For the Morris water maze test, to evaluate the recovery of spatial localization ability of PD mice after treatment with different samples, the spatial localization ability of different groups of mice was tested in a water maze with a diameter of 120.0 cm. The water temperature was maintained at 20 ± 2°C, the 10-cm platform was fixed 1 cm below the water surface, and highly dispersed food-grade titanium dioxide was added to the water to aid animal tracking. The pool is divided into four quadrants, and the walls of each quadrant are pasted with different symbols (five-pointed star, circle, square, and triangle) to provide clues outside the maze. The experimental setup is placed in an enclosed space with no strong light source and no noise. Each mouse in the different treatment groups was trained to find the hidden platform within 60 s for six consecutive days, twice a day in the morning, with an interval of 20 min. During training, if the mouse finds the hidden platform for more than 60 s, the mouse is guided to the platform and stays there for 15 s, and if the mouse can find the platform within 60 s, the mouse stays on the platform for 15 s. Twenty-four hours after the end of training, the platform was removed; the mice were free-swimming in the water maze for 60 s; the movement trajectory of the mice was recorded and analyzed; the movement trajectory, residence time, and number of times crossing the original platform in the target quadrant were obtained; and the statistical analysis was carried out by ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence staining and detection of inflammatory factors

After treatment with different samples, the brains and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) of mice in each group were immunostained. Brains were immediately retrieved and fixed with 4.0% paraformaldehyde at 4°C for 24 hours, and paraffin sections with a thickness of 5.0 μm were obtained. Immunofluorescence staining of α-Syn, ROS, TH, and GAP-43 in the SN regions of the brain was performed. Iba-1 immunofluorescence staining was performed to label microglia in the SN region of the brain. The main organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) of mice were fixed with 4.0% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours and embedded in paraffin, and 5-μm sections were taken to stain with hematoxylin and eosin. The brain striatum of mice in each group was taken separately, and the content of inflammatory factors (TGF-β and IL-10) in each group was determined by using the kit.

Western blot analysis

After treatment, the mouse brain tissue was immediately removed, the tissue blocks were washed two to three times with prechilled PBS, blood stains were removed, the tissue blocks were cut into small pieces and placed in the homogenization tube, two 4.0-mm homogenate beads were added, the tissue volume of lysate was added 10.0 times (various protease inhibitors were added before use), the homogenization program was set to 12000 rpm and 4°C, the tissue was centrifuged for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected, that is, the total protein solution. Proteins were separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gels and then transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. After 60 min of incubation in 5.0% BSA blocking buffer, the membrane was incubated with the primary antibody (α-syn, Servicebio, GB11773, Rabbit, 1:1000) overnight at 4°C. The membrane was then incubated with the corresponding horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated immunoglobulin G secondary antibody (HRP-goat anti-rabbit, Servicebio, GB23303, 1:5000) for 1 hour at room temperature, and last, the protein bands were detected by a digitized chemiluminescence system.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the means with SD. SPSS software was used to perform an independent-samples t test for two sets of data and a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for more than two sets of data. In a one-way ANOVA, using a least significant difference event comparison or Dunnett’s T3 post hoc test, P < 0.05 indicates a significant difference in the data (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001), and ns indicates no significant difference. At least three independent experiments were conducted to obtain the reported data.
