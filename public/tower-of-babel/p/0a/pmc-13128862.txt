ASCC3 promotes chemosensitivity in colorectal cancer cells
Authors: Anqianyi Tu, Rebecca D C Martyn, Shixin Cui, Nicole L Batenburg, Russta Fayyazi, Fiorella Di Pastena, Line Berthiaume, Kanwaldeep Singh, Ranim Saleem, Graham R Scott, Tobias Berg, Étienne Audet-Walsh, Gregory R Steinberg, Xu-Dong Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe activating signal co-integrator 1 complex subunit 3 (ASCC3), a multifunctional protein, has been implicated as a prognostic marker in several types of cancer. However, mechanisms underlying its prognostic value are not fully understood. Here, we report that ASCC3 promotes sensitivity to chemotherapeutic drugs that induce replication stress, such as 5-fluorouracil, cisplatin, and hydroxyurea, in colorectal cancer (CRC) cells, likely in a cancer type dependent manner. Increased chemoresistance resulting from ASCC3 loss is not due to reduced genomic instability as evidenced by enhanced accumulation of DNA damage and micronuclei following exposure to these drugs. RNA-seq analysis reveals that ASCC3 stimulates the expression of gene sets associated with mTORC1 signaling, glycolysis, and protein folding pathways in CRC cells. While promoting the serine biosynthesis pathway, we demonstrate, through extracellular flux assays and stable isotopes tracer analysis, that ASCC3 reprograms energy metabolism, favoring glycolysis over oxidative phosphorylation. Furthermore, we find that ASCC3 is required for PERK production upon ER stress. Impaired PERK production is associated with reduced levels of CHOP and caspase 3 following treatment with 5-fluorouracil, indicating that ASCC3 promotes PERK production to enhance cell death upon chemotherapy. Collectively, our work reveals an unexpected role of ASCC3 in connecting replication stress to both metabolic reprogramming and PERK-mediated signaling in CRC cells.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43173-9.Keywords: ASCC3, PERK production, Reprogramming of energy metabolism, Chemoresistance, Colorectal cancerSubject terms: Biochemistry, Cancer, Cell biology, Molecular biology, Oncology

Introduction

Chemotherapeutic drugs such as 5-fluorouracil (5-FU), cisplatin, and hydroxyurea (HU) are commonly used in cancer treatment. While 5-FU inhibits thymidylate synthase1, which catalyzes conversion of dUMP to dTMP, a precursor of DNA synthesis, HU inhibits ribonucleotide reductase2, which is responsible for converting ribonucleotides to deoxyribonucleotides needed for DNA synthesis. Cisplatin induces both DNA intra- and inter-crosslinks that impede progression of DNA replication3. While these chemotherapeutic drugs exert their killing effects by inducing DNA damage and/or disrupting DNA synthesis, cancer cells are known to leverage replication stress responses to mitigate their genotoxic effects4,5. Cancer cells also exploit several other pathways, including but not limited to PRKR-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK)-mediated signaling and metabolic reprogramming, to escape from chemotherapy. However, how cancer cells coordinate these pathways has not been fully understood.

PERK represents one branch of the unfolded protein response (UPR)6,7, which also includes two other branches, inositol-requiring enzyme 1 alpha (IRE1α) and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6). PERK is activated in response to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress8, a cellular state associated with an accumulation of misfolded/unfolded proteins. Activated PERK phosphorylates eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2A (eIF2α), which in turn attenuates cap-dependent protein synthesis while at the same time selectively increases translation of activating transcription factor 4 (ATF4), a key regulator of amino acid metabolism and redox homeostasis9. During prolonged ER stress, ATF4 induces transcription of the proapoptotic protein, C/EBP-homologous protein (CHOP), triggering cell death10. PERK-mediated signaling events have been implicated in regulating both pro- and anti-chemoresistance depending upon the context8,11,12.

Cancer cells reprogram their metabolism to promote survival, growth, proliferation, as well as escape from chemotherapeutics13,14. Aerobic glycolysis, also known as the Warburg effect, refers to a phenomenon in which tumor cells preferentially produce energy by converting glucose to lactate even in the presence of oxygen15. While tumor cells require aerobic glycolysis to support their growth and proliferation16,17, chemoresistant cancer cells are reported to be addicted to mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) for their energy needs18,19. Serine, a non-essential amino acid, is central to the biosynthesis of many intracellular molecules required for cell proliferation and growth20 and reprogramming of serine biosynthesis has also been implicated in chemoresistance21,22.

The activating signal co-integrator 1 complex (ASCC), a multifunctional complex, consists of ASCC1, ASCC2, ASCC3, and TRIP423. ASCC3, the largest subunit of ASCC, functions as a linchpin since it interacts with three other ASCC subunits24–26. As part of the ASCC complex, ASCC3 was first reported to function as a transcriptional coactivator of nuclear receptors23. Subsequently, ASCC3 has been implicated in alkylation damage repair24,27 and ribosome quality control28–30. Recently, we have reported that ASCC3 controls cellular responses to replication stress31, which in general refers to any event that slows or stalls progression of DNA replication forks. ASCC3 loss leads to accumulation of chromosome breaks/gaps and micronuclei following exposure to replication stress31, underscoring its crucial role in maintaining genomic stability. Aside from these aforementioned functions, published proteomic studies have revealed that ASCC3 is part of the PERK interactome32 and that the ASCC3 interactome includes mitochondrial proteins and metabolic enzymes33, suggesting additional functions for ASCC3.

ASCC3 has been implicated in cancer progression and treatment. The interaction interface between ASCC2 and ASCC3 are hotspots of cancer mutation34. Homozygous loss of ASCC3 is found in a pediatric rhabdomyosarcoma case35 whereas upregulation of ASCC3 is seen in peripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients with lung cancer36. Knockdown of ASCC3 enhances sensitivity to methylmethane sulfonate (MMS) in prostate cancer cells that overexpress the alkylation repair enzyme ALKBH327. Moreover, increasing studies have suggested that ASCC subunits are prognostic markers of several types of cancers37–39. While high ASCC3 expression is associated with an unfavorable prognosis of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, lung adenocarcinoma, and sarcoma38, low ASCC3 expression is reported to be correlated with worst survival in rectal adenocarcinoma37. However, mechanisms underlying ASCC3’s prognostic value in cancer is poorly understood.

In this report, we have discovered that ASCC3 connects replication stress to both metabolic reprogramming and PERK-mediated signaling and promotes chemosensitivity in CRC cells. Loss of ASCC3 paradoxically promotes resistance to 5-FU, cisplatin, and HU while enhancing genomic instability induced by these drugs in CRC cells. This ASCC3-mediated chemosensitivity was not observed in osterosarcoma cells, suggesting that this chemosensitivity is likely to be cancer-type dependent. Through RNA-seq analysis, we have uncovered an unexpected role of ASCC3 in stimulating the expression of gene sets associated with mTORC1 signaling, glycolysis, and protein folding pathways in CRC cells. Through a combination of metabolomics, Seahorse and cell proliferation assays, we have shown that the loss of ASCC3 reprograms energy and serine metabolism in CRC cells. Furthermore, ASCC3 is required for PERK production upon ER stress in CRC cells. Our finding suggests that impaired PERK-mediated cell death is an underlying mechanism responsible for increased chemoresistance in CRC cells lacking ASCC3.

Results

Loss of ASCC3 promotes chemoresistance despite increased genomic instability in colorectal cancer cells

ASCC3 has been implicated in limiting replication stress to promote genomic stability31. However, whether ASCC3 supports cell survival in response to replication stress was not investigated in this recent report. We set out to address this question using recently reported osteosarcoma U2OS and colorectal cancer HCT116 cells that are either wild type or knocked out for ASCC331 (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Clonogenic survival and cell viability assays revealed that while loss of ASCC3 had no effect on cell survival in U2OS (Supplementary Fig. S1B-S1D), it promoted cell survival in HCT116 cells following treatment to 5-FU, cisplatin, and HU (Fig. 1A-C). To investigate whether this increased chemoresistance is cell line or colon cancer type specific, we knocked out ASCC3 in two additional colorectal cancer cell lines LS174T and DLD1 (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Clonogenic survival and cell viability assays revealed that loss of ASCC3 enhanced cell survival in both LS174T and DLD1 cells following treatment with 5-FU, HU, or cisplatin (Fig. 1D-I). To investigate whether this increased cell survival upon chemotherapy might be due to improved genomic stability, we first measured levels of γH2X damage foci and micronuclei formation in DLD1 WT and ASCC3-KO cells that were treated with or without 5-FU, cisplatin, and HU. In agreement with our recent report31, ASCC3 loss was found to exacerbate 5-FU-, cisplatin-, and HU-induced formation of γH2X damage foci and micronuclei in DLD1 cells (Fig. 1J and K, Supplementary Fig. S2). This exacerbated 5-FU, cisplatin-, and HU-induced micronuclei formation was also observed in HCT116 and LS174T ASCC3-KO cells (Fig. 1L and M). These results altogether suggest that ASCC3 loss paradoxically promotes cell survival in response to chemotherapy in colorectal cancer cells while increasing genomic instability.

ASCC3 promotes expression of gene sets associated with mTORC1 signaling, glycolysis, and ER stress pathway in CRC cells

To gain insight into possible pathways that may promote chemoresistance in the absence of ASCC3, we performed RNA-seq analysis in both HCT116 parental (WT) and HCT116 ASCC3-KO (KO) cells. Among 15,548 genes detected, 2,609 genes (16.8%) were significantly upregulated whereas 2713 genes (17.4%) were significantly downregulated in HCT116 ASCC3-KO cells compared to HCT116 parental (WT) cells (Fig. 2A and Supplementary Table 1). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct clustering of the two groups along PC1, which accounted for 87.4% of the total variance, indicating a substantial impact on transcriptional regulation following ASCC3 loss (Fig. 2B). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) identified decreased enrichment of Hallmark Glycolysis and mTORC1 Signaling gene sets in HCT116 ASCC3-KO cells (Fig. 2C-E and Supplementary Table S2). GSEA also revealed that the GO:BP Protein Folding pathway was down regulated in HCT116 ASCC3-KO cells (Fig. 2C and F, Supplementary Table S2). To further assess differences at the subcellular level, we performed GSEA using GO:CC (Gene Ontology: Cellular Component) gene sets, which showed decreased enrichment associated with the ER lumen, ER protein-containing complex, pigment granule, and cell surface in HCT116 ASCC3-KO cells (Fig. 2G and Supplementary Table S2). Notably, the ER is a major site of protein folding. These results suggest that ASCC3 loss has a broad transcriptional impact in HCT116 cells, down regulating the expression of gene sets associated with mTORC1 signaling, glycolysis, and ER stress pathways.

ASCC3 regulates pathways down-stream of mTORC1 in CRC cells

As our RNA-seq data suggest that ASCC3 loss downregulates expression of gene sets associated with mTORC1 signaling, we investigated whether loss of ASCC3 inhibits the mTORC1 activity. Western blot analyses revealed that loss of ASCC3 had no effect on the level of phosphorylation of ribosomal protein S6 kinase (S6K) on T389 residue (S6K-pT389) (Fig. 3A), which is a direct target of mTOR40, in HCT116, LS174T, and DLD1 cells. These results suggest that ASCC3 does not inhibit mTORC1 activity per se. To gain further insight into the role of ASCC3 in the regulation of gene sets associated with mTORC1 signaling, we analyzed the list of genes that exhibited at least ~ 1.5-fold decrease in their transcript levels in HCT116 ASCC3-KO cells. A total of 12 genes met this criterion (Supplementary Table S2). Interestingly, among these 12 genes were two genes that are involved in the de novo serine biosynthesis pathway, one encoding phosphoglycerate dehydrogenase (PHGDH) and the other encoding phosphoserine aminotransferase 1 (PSAT1). PHGDH and PSAT1 catalyze the respective first and second steps of a three-step process in serine biosynthesis41. To substantiate this finding, we measured protein levels of both PHGDH and PSAT1 in HCT116, LS174T, and DLD1 WT and ASCC3-KO cells. Western blot analyses confirmed that ASCC3 loss led to reduced levels of both PHGDH and PSAT1 in HCT116 cells (Fig. 3B). In LS174T cells, ASCC3 loss affected the levels of neither PHGDH nor PSAT1 proteins whereas in DLD1 cells, it led to a pronounced reduction in the level of PSAT1 while having no effect on the level of PHGDH (Fig. 3B). In agreement with the notion that PHGDH and PSAT1 are engaged in de novo serine biosynthesis, ASCC3 loss sensitized HCT116 and DLD1 cells to serine deprivation (Fig. 3C and D). Interestingly, in the absence of detectable changes in the levels of PHGDH and PSAT1, we observed that ASCC3 loss also sensitized LS174T cells to serine deprivation (Fig. 3E). These results altogether suggest that in the wake of ASCC3 loss, down-regulation of expression of gene sets associated with mTORC1 signaling is not due to a change in mTOR activity per se but rather pathways downstream of mTOR.

ASCC3 favors glycolysis over OXPHOS in CRC cells

Our RNA-seq data that ASCC3 loss down regulates gene sets associated with glycolysis prompted us to investigate whether ASCC3 regulates energy metabolism in CRC cells. We performed the Seahorse mitochondria stress test and glycolytic rate assays in three pairs of CRC cell lines, HCT116, LS174T, and DLD1 parental (WT) or ASCC3-KO. Mitochondria stress test revealed that loss of ASCC3 increased basal respiration, proton leak, and maximal respiration in all three CRC cell lines (Fig. 4A-D and Supplementary Fig. S3A and S3B), suggesting that ASCC3 inhibits oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). In agreement with this finding, ASCC3 loss was also found to stimulate the leak respiration, maximal uncoupled respiration, and the electron transport chain (ETC) complex IV activity in permeabilized HCT116 cells (Supplementary Fig. S3C) whereas it had no effect on levels of ETC complex proteins (Supplementary Fig. S3D). Contrary to OXPHOS, glycolytic rate assays revealed that loss of ASCC3 reduced basal glycolysis in all three CRC cell lines (Fig. 4E and F, Supplementary Fig. S3E and S3F), agreeing with our RNA-seq data. We also analyzed the OCR/GlycoPER ratio, which was higher in all three CRC ASCC3-KO cell lines than their respective CRC WT cell lines (Fig. 4G). These results reveal that loss of ASCC3 favors an OXPHOS-high and glycolysis-low phenotype in CRC cell lines.

To further substantiate our findings, we performed flux analysis of carbon 13-labelled glucose (13C6-glucose) in HCT116 WT and ASCC3-KO cells. Pyruvate, the final product of glycolysis, is a precursor for both lactate and the amino acid alanine (Fig. 5A). We found that loss of ASCC3 had no effect on carbon flux into intracellular lactate levels (Fig. 5B). However, ASCC3 loss decreased carbon flux into alanine as evidenced by a reduced level of the incorporated m+3 alanine isotopomer (Fig. 5C), in line with both our RNA-seq and Seahorse glycolytic rate assays. On the other hand, ASCC3 loss increased glucose flux into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle as evidenced by increased carbon 13 labelling of the TCA cycle intermediates citrate, succinate, and malate (Fig. 5D-F). Increased flux of carbons from glucose was also observed in amino acids that can be synthesized from TCA cycle intermediates, such as glutamate and aspartate, upon ASCC3 loss (Fig. 5G and H). These results altogether suggest that ASCC3 regulates the reprogramming of energy metabolism, promoting glycolysis but inhibiting mitochondrial respiration and the TCA cycle in CRC cells.

ASCC3 promotes PERK production in response to ER stress

Our RNA-seq data suggested that ASCC3 also regulates the ER stress pathway. To substantiate this finding, we next investigated the role of ASCC3 in the UPR, which is activated by ER stress. We first treated HCT116 WT and ASCC3-KO cells with or without thapsigargin (Tg), an inhibitor of sarco/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ ATPase (SERCA) that induces ER stress. Western blot analyses revealed a pronounced increase in the level of IRE1α in HCT116 WT cells following treatment with either 30 nM or 60 nM of Tg for 24 h (Fig. 6A). We also observed a robust increase in the level of spliced XBP-1, a product of IRE1α-mediated splicing of XBP-1 mRNA, in HCT116 WT cells following treatment with either 30 nM or 60 nM of Tg for 4 h (Fig. 6A). These increases in both IRE1α and spliced XBP-1 levels were not affected by ASCC3 loss in HCT116 cells (Fig. 6A). ASCC3 loss also had little effect on the level of cleaved ATF6 seen in HCT116 cells treated with either 30 nM or 60 nM of Tg for 4 h (Fig. 6B). In contrast, ASCC3 loss led to a pronounced reduction in thapisgargin (Tg)-induced PERK level in HCT116 cells (Fig. 6C and D). Loss of ASCC3 was also observed to abolish Tg-induced PERK production in LS174T and DLD1 cells (Fig. 6C). These results altogether demonstrate that ASCC3 stimulates PERK induction in response to ER stress.

We observed that ASCC3 loss did not affect Tg-induced transcription of PERK in HCT116 cells (Fig. 6E), suggesting that ASCC3 regulates ER stress-induced PERK production post-transcriptionally. To investigate whether ASCC3 promotes PERK stability upon ER stress, we treated HCT116, LS174T, and DLD1 WT and ASCC3-KO cells with MG132, an inhibitor of proteosome-dependent protein degradation, or bafilomycin A1, an inhibitor of lysosome-mediated protein degradation. Treatment of neither MG132 nor bafilomycin A1 was able to restore Tg-induced PERK production in all three CRC cell lines (Fig. 6F andG), suggesting that it is unlikely that protein degradation is a cause of impaired PERK induction upon ER stress in the wake of ASCC3 loss.

ASCC3 loss impairs PERK production to enhance cell death upon treatment with 5-FU in CRC cells

During prolonged ER stress, activated PERK up-regulates CHOP, triggering cell death10. To investigate whether PERK-mediated cell death might be an underlying mechanism of chemoresistance observed in ASCC3-KO CRC cells, we focused on resistance to 5-FU since 5-FU is the backbone of standard-care-of chemotherapy regimens for metastatic CRC. We treated three pairs of CRC cell lines (HCT116, LS174T, and DLD1 WT and ASCC3-KO) with or without 40 μM 5-FU for 4 h, 16 h or 24 h, 48 h, or 60 h. Western blot analyses revealed that treatment with 5-FU strongly induced PERK in all three CRC WT cell lines at all the time points except for 4 h for HCT116 (Fig. 7A-F), in agreement with previous reports8,42. This induction was impaired in ASCC3-KO CRC cells (Fig. 7A-F). We also measured levels of CHOP and cleaved caspase 3, markers of cell death that are induced by PERK-mediated signaling events, in the same three pairs of CRC cell lines. Western blot analyses revealed that treatment with 5-FU for a prolonged period (48 h in HCT116 cells and 60 h in both LS174T and DLD1 cells) induced the level of CHOP whereas this induction was diminished by ASCC3 loss in CRC cells (Fig. 7D-F). Like CHOP, loss of ASCC3 also abolished 5-FU-induced caspase 3 in HCT116, LS174T, and DLD1 cells following this prolonged treatment with 5-FU (Fig. 7D-F). These results altogether suggest that ASCC3 promotes PERK production to stimulate cell death in response to treatment with 5-FU in CRC cells.

Discussion

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cancer worldwide43. Systemic chemotherapy is the primary treatment for advanced or metastatic CRC44,45. While chemotherapy has significantly improved survival for patients with metastatic CRC46, 5-year survival remains low47. A major cause of this low survival is chemoresistance. In this report, we have uncovered that ASCC3 promotes sensitivity to three chemotherapeutic drugs, 5-FU, cisplatin, and HU, in CRC cells. 5-FU is a central component of the first-line treatment regimens for metastatic CRC1,48. While cisplatin and HU are not used clinically to treat CRC, oxaliplatin, a derivative of cisplatin, is included in the combined therapy regimens involving multiple drugs48. Our finding suggests that ASCC3 may serve as a prognostic marker for predicting the response of patients with CRC to 5-FU- and oxaliplatin-based treatment regimens. In addition, our work presented here reveals an unexpected role of ASCC3 in connecting replication stress to both metabolic reprogramming and PERK-mediated signaling in CRC cells. Our work suggests that in the absence of ASCC3, CRC cells leverage increased replication stress and genomic instability, altered energy metabolism that favours high OXPHOS and low glycolysis, reduced serine biosynthesis, and impaired PERK-CHOP-mediated cell death to resist killing effects of chemotherapeutic drugs (Fig. 7G).

It has been reported that the noncoding circASCC3 RNA, a circular RNA generated from the back-splicing of ASCC3 pre-mRNA transcript, promotes resistance to genotoxic stress independently of the ASCC3 gene encoding the ASCC3 protein49. In addition, this circASCC3-mediated resistance is dependent upon p5349. In contrast to circASCC3, we have shown that loss of the ASCC3 protein promotes chemoresistance independently of p53, since HCT116 and LS174T cells carry wild type p5350,51 whereas DLD1 cells contain mutated p5351. These findings suggest it is unlikely that the observed chemoresistance in ASCC3-KO CRC cells is due to circASCC3 RNA.

Recently, we have reported that ASCC3 is recruited to stalled forks to control cellular responses to replication stress and maintain genomic stability31. In agreement with this recent report, we have shown here that ASCC3 loss leads to increased genomic instability in colorectal cancer cells upon treatment with chemotherapeutic drugs, 5-FU, cisplatin, and HU as evidenced by enhanced formation of both γH2AX damage foci and micronuclei. Paradoxically, this increased genomic instability was associated with improved cell survival following exposure to these drugs in CRC cells. In contrast to CRC cells, ASCC3 loss had no effect on cell survival in osteosarcoma U2OS cells upon treatment with these drugs. These results suggest that ASCC3 promotes chemosensitivity in a manner likely to be cancer type dependent. Our finding agrees with a recent report that low ASCC3 expression is associated with worst survival in patients with rectal adenocarcinoma37.

Through RNA-seq analysis, we have discovered that ASCC3 promotes expression of gene sets associated with mTORC1 signaling, glycolytic and ER-stress pathways. While ASCC3 loss does not affect mTORC1 activity per se, it impairs the serine biosynthesis pathway in CRC cell lines. However, how ASCC3 loss downregulates expression of genes downstream of mTORC1 signaling is unknown and requires future investigation. It has been well accepted that PHGDH is a rate-limiting enzyme in de novo serine biosynthesis52. Reduced expression of PHGDH is likely responsible for the increased sensitivity to serine deprivation observed in HCT116 ASCC3-KO cells. In DLD1 cells, ASCC3 loss does not affect PHGDH expression but leads to a pronounced reduction in PSAT1. This reduction may be responsible for increased sensitivity to serine deprivation in DLD1 ASCC3-KO cells since PSAT1 has also been implicated in limiting serine biosynthesis53. In LS174T cells, ASCC3 loss affects neither PHGDH nor PSAT1 expression but sensitizes LS174T cells to serine deprivation. While it has been reported that p53 regulates serine biosynthesis in CRC cells54, it is unlikely that mutated p53 is responsible for the increased sensitivity to serine deprivation in LS174T ASCC3-KO cells since parental LS174T cells carry wild type p5350. A deficiency in serine uptake can lead to increased sensitivity to serine deprivation55. It has been reported that the amino acid transporter ASCT2, encoded by the SLC1A5 gene, plays a major role in serine uptake in cancer cells55. Aside from ASCT2, several other transporters have also been implicated in serine uptake, including ASCT156,57 (encoded by SLC1A4), SLC7A558, and SLC7A859. Our RNA-seq analysis has revealed that these genes (SLC1A4, SLC1A5, SLC7A5, and SLC7A8) are down-regulated in HCT116 ASCC3-KO cells (Supplementary Table S1), suggesting that loss of ASCC3 may affect serine uptake in CRC cells. Whether a defect in serine uptake may contribute to increased sensitivity to serine deprivation in LS174T cells requires future investigation. Our work presented here suggests that ASCC3 promotes cell survival in response to serine deprivation in CRC cells, likely through more than one mechanism in a cell line-specific manner. Our finding that impaired serine biosynthesis in the wake of ASCC3 loss is associated with increased chemoresistance in HCT116 and DLD1 cells echoes a previous report that inhibition of serine biosynthesis promotes resistance to cisplatin in gastric cancer60.

While downregulating glycolysis, ASCC3 loss promotes mitochondrial respiration and TCA cycle activity, suggesting that ASCC3 reprograms energy metabolism. It has been reported that chemoresistant CRC cells are addicted to OXPHOS for their energy needs18,19. We have shown that ASCC3-dependent energy reprogramming is associated with chemoresistance. Future studies will be needed to investigate whether ASCC3-deficient CRC cells are addicted to OXPHOS to escape from chemotherapy.

We have shown that ASCC3 is required for ER stress-induced PERK production in CRC cells. This requirement occurs post-transcriptionally since ASCC3 loss does not affect thapsigargin-induced transcription of PERK in HCT116 cells. ASCC3 loss also does not affect PERK stability upon ER stress since inhibition of proteosome- and lysosome-dependent protein degradation fails to restore thapsigargin-induced PERK production in ASCC3-KO CRC cells. ASCC3 is known to participate in ribosome quality control28–30, which regulates protein translation. These findings altogether suggest that ASCC3 may be needed for PERK translation upon ER stress. We have shown that impaired PERK production in the wake of ASCC3 loss leads to decreased cell death following prolonged treatment with 5-FU, suggesting that ASCC3 promotes PERK production to enhance cell death upon chemotherapy in CRC cells. Collectively, the work presented here reveals that ASCC3 loss-induced chemoresistance in CRC cells is associated with increased genomic instability, enhanced OXPHOS, impaired serine biosynthesis, as well as reduced PERK-mediated cell death, underscoring its complexity.

Cell culture and transfection

All cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, L-glutamine, non-essential amino acids, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 0.1 mg/ml streptomycin. Cell lines used: U2OS (ATCC), HCT116 (Life Technology), LS174T (ATCC), and DLD1 (ATCC), HCT116-ASCC3-KO31, U2OS-ASCC3-KO31. Cell cultures were routinely examined for mycoplasma contamination by fixing and staining them with DAPI. siRNA transfections were carried out with RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

For serine starvation assays, cells were first cultured in complete medium (SG+) [MEM supplemented with 10% dialyzed FBS, 1x Vitamin solution, 100 mM sodium pyruvate, penicillin/streptomycin, 0.4 mM serine, and 7.5 mg/L glycine] for 48 h. Subsequently, cells were washed twice in PBS, trypsinized, and seeded in 24-well plates in triplicate at 60,000 cells per well for DLD1 and HCT116, or 120,000 cells per well for LS174T cells. Cells were then cultured in either SG+ or serine-deprived medium (SG-) (complete medium without serine and glycine). Cells were counted every 48 h for HCT116, or every 72 h for DLD1 and LS174T.

For metabolomic analysis, cells were seeded at a density of 1.0 × 10⁶ cells per 6-cm plate and allowed to adhere for 24 h, reaching approximately 70% confluency at the time of harvesting. The culture medium was then replaced with glucose-free DMEM supplemented with 4.5 g/L [U-13C6] D-glucose (CLM-1396, Cambridge Isotope Laboratories) for 16 h. Culture plates were placed on ice, and 1 mL of culture medium was collected and snap-frozen in dry ice. Cells were rapidly washed twice with ice-cold 0.9% NaCl and immediately snap-frozen in dry ice. 800 µL of pre-cold 80% methanol was added to each plate, followed by cell scraping and transfer to microcentrifuge tubes. Samples were stored at -80°C until further analysis. For normalization, parallel plates were prepared under identical conditions, and cell counts were obtained at the time of harvesting to account for variations in cell number.

CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing of ASCC3

The CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing of ASCC3 in four cell lines: U2OS (RRID:CVCL_0042), HCT116 (RRID:CVCL_0291), LS174T (RRID:CVCL_1384) and DLD1 (RRID:CVCL_0248), was done as described31. Briefly, Cells were transiently transfected with gRNA targeting ASCC3 and expressed from the pX459v2 plasmid (#62988, Addgene; RRID:Addgene_62988) containing Cas9 followed by 2A-Puromycin cassette. The gRNA sequence targeting ASCC3: 5’-GACATTTGAAAAGGAACGCA-3’. Following selection with puromycin for 2 days, cells were subcloned to allow single colonies to form. Individual clones were confirmed to lose ASCC3 by western blot analyses with an anti-ASCC3 antibody.

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence (IF) was performed as described61,62. Following seeding, DLD1 WT and ASCC3-KO cells were either untreated or treated with 5-FU (10 μM), cisplatin (2 μM), or HU (2 mM) for 16 h prior to fixation. All cell images were captured on a Zeiss Axio Imager M2 microscope with a Zeiss Axiocam 705 mono camera and processed using Zeiss ZEN lite software.

Bulk RNA-seq analysis

HCT116 parental (WT) and ASCC3-KO cells were lysed in TRIzol reagent and cell fractions were separated using chloroform. Following centrifugation, the supernatant (aqueous phase) was applied to the RNeasy kit (74106, Qiagen). Subsequent extraction and purification of total RNA was done according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The RNA samples passed the BioAnalyzer quality control test. RNA-seq was carried out using the Illumina NextSeq 2000 (P2 Flow cell, 2 x 50 bp configuration) system.

Raw RNA-seq FASTQ files were processed using the Galaxy platform (version 24.1.4, https://usegalaxy.org/). Read quality was assessed with FastQC, and low-quality reads and adaptor sequences were removed using Cutadapt. The remaining reads were aligned to the hg38 Homo sapiens reference genome using HISAT2 and quantified with featureCounts. Raw counts data were exported to R for differential expression analysis using the DESeq2 package (version 1.46.0, Bioconductor). Principle component analysis (PCA) was performed using variance-stabilized transformation (VST)-normalized expression data from DESeq2. Fold change and p-adj values were used to generate a volcano plot with the EnhancedVolcano package (version 1.24.0, Bioconductor). Pathway analysis was performed using Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA, version 4.2.3) with gene sets from MSigDB (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp) and log2fold change values from DESeq2. A false discovery rate (FDR) q-value of 0.05 was used as the threshold for identifying significantly enriched gene sets. Results were visualized as enrichment plots generated by the GSEA software and as heatmaps created with the pheatmap package (version 1.0.12, CRAN).

High resolution respirometry (HRR) analysis of mitochondria complex activities

HRR analysis was done as described63 with modifications. Briefly, HCT116 cells were seeded at an initial density of 750,000 cells per 6 cm plate. Twenty-four hours post seeding, cells were collected and 1 million cells were resuspended in 2 ml of mitochondrial respiration buffer (MiR05: 20 mM HEPES pH 7.1, 110 mM sucrose, 0.5 mM EGTA, 3 mM MgCl2, 60 mM potassium lactobionate, 20 mM taurine, 10 mM KH2PO4, 1 g/L fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin) and were added to the respirometry chamber (Oxygraph-2K, Oroboros Instruments, Innsbruck, Austria). The cell membrane was then permeabilized with two titrations of 1 µL saponin (5 mg/mL in MiR05). Leak respiration (LN) was stimulated through addition of 5 mM pyruvate (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and 1 mM malate (Sigma-Aldrich). Addition of 5 mM ADP (Sigma-Aldrich) stimulated oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS, P) (PPM), followed by addition of 10 mM glutamate (Sigma-Aldrich) to stimulate maximal respiration via complex I stimulation (Ppmg). Addition of 10 mM succinate (Sigma-Aldrich) then stimulated maximal respiration via complexes I and II (Ppmgs). Two titrations of 1 µL carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP) uncoupled mitochondria to measure maximal electron transport capacity via complexes I and II (Epmgs) and finally, 2 mM ascorbate and 0.5 mM TMPD (N,N,N’,N’-tetramethyl-p-phenylenediamine dihydrochloride) for stimulation of complex IV, subtracting background oxidation levels measured after the addition of azide (ETM). For each condition, measurements were made 5 min after substrate/uncoupler addition over a 2-min period.

Mass spectrometry analysis

On the day of analyses, samples stored at -80°C were processed as previously described64,65. Briefly, samples were first derivatized by methoxyamination and silylation with MTBSTFA/TBDMCS prior to injection in an Agilent 8890 GC equipped with a DB5-MS+DG capillary column connected to an Agilent 5977B MS operating under electron impact (EI) ionizing at 70 eV (Agilent Technologies, USA). After GC-MS analysis, the Agilent MassHunter Workstation Software was used for analysis and, after deconvolution, the NIST/EPA/NIH Mass Spectral Library (NIST 2017, Gaithersburg, MD, USA) was used to identify target metabolites. Finally, metabolite levels were normalized for cell number (per million cells) as well as for the internal standard Myristic acid-d27 (CDN Isotopes, Canada). Isotopomer levels were normalized for the observed natural abundancy in samples without 13C-glucose in the culture media.

Seahorse assays

Cells were seeded at 30,000 cells per well in Seahorse Agilent 96-well plates (Agilent Technologies, cat. no. 101085-004). 24 hours post seeding, mitochondrial stress test assays and glycolytic rate assays were performed on an Agilent Seahorse XFe96 analyzer according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Agilent Technologies, cat. no. 103015-100). Briefly, cells were washed twice with XF mitochondria stress test assay media consisting of Seahorse XF DMEM medium, pH 7.4 (Agilent Technologies, cat. no. 103575-100) supplemented with 25 mM glucose, 1 mM sodium pyruvate and 4 mM L-glutamine. The mitochondria stress assay first measures basal O2 consumption rate (OCR) followed by preoptimized concentrations of oligomycin (2.5 μM), carbonyl cyanide-p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP) (0.5 μM), and antimycin (0.5 μM)/rotenone (0.5 μM) (Rot/AA) to measure proton leak, maximal OCR (induced by uncoupling), and residual (non-mitochondrial) OCR, respectively. Cells also underwent glycolytic rate assays, measured as glycolytic proton efflux rate (GlycoPER), in which antimycin/rotenone was initially injected, followed by 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG). Injections to each well were performed at the recommended concentrations (Agilent Technologies). OCR and GlycoPER measurements were normalized to cell number upon completion of the assay. Data analysis was performed by using the Agilent Seahorse Analytics User Interface.

Immunoblotting

Immunoblotting were performed as described66. Chemiluminescent signals were captured using a ChemiDoc imaging system (Universal Hood III, Bio-Rad). Protein molecular weight (MW) markers (161-0373, Bio-Rad) were imaged using the colorimetric application, while target protein bands were detected using the Chemi Hi Sensitivity application. A merged image was generated to reveal the position of MW markers on the western blots. Unmerged images (without MW markers) were cropped to generate western blots in the manuscript. For western blots of PHGDH, PSAT1, and γ-tubulin derived from HCT116 and LS174T in Fig. 3B, images were captured using Hyperfilm (GE28906838).

RT-qPCR analyses

RT-qPCR was performed as previously described67. The pair of PERK PCR primers used are: 5’ GGAACCAGACGATGAGACAG 3’ (forward) and 5’ GGATGACACCAAGGAACCGG 3’ (reverse).

Clonogenic survival and cell viability assays

Clonogenic survival assays were done as described61. Cell viability assays were done as described68 with minor modifications. 24 hours post seeding, cells were incubated with media containing the drug of interest for 72 h. Following wash once in PBS, cells were then stained with 50 μl of crystal violet staining solution (0.5% crystal violet in 20% methanol) for 20 min on a shaker. Measurement of the optical density of crystal violet staining at 584 nm was done using BioTek Cytation 5 Cell Imaging Multimode Reader.

Statistical analysis

A Student’s two-tailed unpaired t-test was used to derive all P values except for where specified.
