Micafungin Sequestration and Late Release During Continuous Renal Replacement Therapy with Polyacrylonitrile- and Polysulfone-Derived Filters
Authors: Julien Massol, Valentin Maulet, Vincent Jullien, Solange Corriol-Rohou, Jean-Herlé Raphalen, Frédéric J. Baud, Lionel Lamhaut, Olivier Lortholary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAdsorption within continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) circuits may reduce exposure to echinocandins. Because micafungin is highly protein bound, the behavior of its unbound fraction during CRRT remains difficult to characterize. We assessed unbound micafungin disappearance from a central compartment and late release/desorption in a protein-free in vitro CRRT model.MethodsMicafungin stability was assessed in a 5-L bag of Hemosol™ B0 over 8 h. In the NeckEpur model, a 5-L protein-free central compartment was circulated at 200 mL/min for 6 h through either a polyacrylonitrile hemofilter (ST™150; post-dilution continuous veno-venous hemofiltration [CVVH], 2.5 L/h) or a polysulfone hemofilter (AV™1000). For AV™1000, one run used CVVH (2.5 L/h) and one used continuous veno-venous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF; dialysis 1.5 L/h plus filtration 1.0 L/h). Initial micafungin concentrations in the central compartment were 2.18 mg/L or approximately 6.8 mg/L. Concentrations were measured by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) with a lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) of 0.1 mg/L. Apparent central-compartment clearance (Cl_CC), sieving coefficient (Sc), and extraction coefficient (EC) were used to describe disappearance from the circuit, filtration, and late release. Sensitivity analyses replaced values < LLOQ by LLOQ/2 or LLOQ/√2. Segmental sampling and within-filter mass balance were used descriptively to localize loss within the extracorporeal system.ResultsMicafungin was stable in Hemosol™ B0 over 8 h (− 5.2 ± 0.5%). With ST™150 at 2.18 mg/L, C_CC reached the LLOQ by 200 min and was below the LLOQ thereafter; apparent Cl_CC was approximately 6 L/h, effluent concentrations were not measurable, and limited late release was estimated over 120–200 min. With ST™150 at approximately 6.8 mg/L, elimination from the central compartment was 92 ± 4% at 6 h (apparent Cl_CC 4.9 ± 0.2 L/h), with within-filter contributions from measurable effluent removal (54 ± 6%) and non-effluent loss (46 ± 6%); small late release was compatible with negative EC values during 180–360 min. For AV™1000, two exploratory runs—one CVVH and one CVVHDF—showed rapid disappearance from the central compartment, with C_CC below the LLOQ by 120 min, no measurable effluent concentrations, and no negative EC values. Sensitivity analyses for values < LLOQ changed the numerical Cl_CC estimates but not the overall pattern. Segmental concentration data and within-filter mass-balance analysis localized the dominant loss to the filter module, with only limited upstream inlet-segment contribution.ConclusionIn this exploratory protein-free in vitro model, unbound micafungin rapidly disappeared from the CRRT circuit with both tested filter systems. ST™150 showed measurable effluent removal at higher concentrations and limited late release, whereas both exploratory AV™1000 runs showed rapid disappearance without detectable late release. Segmental concentration data and within-filter mass-balance analysis localized the dominant loss to the filter module, with only limited upstream inlet-segment contribution. These findings characterize unbound micafungin–circuit interactions under the tested conditions; their clinical translation will depend on in vivo protein binding and rebinding kinetics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-026-01367-8.

Introduction

Invasive candidiasis is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in the intensive care unit (ICU) [1]. Echinocandins, including micafungin, are widely used in this setting because of their broad antifungal spectrum and favorable safety profile [2, 3].

Pharmacokinetic (PK) studies in critically ill patients reveal substantial variability in micafungin exposure, raising concerns about underdosing [4–7]. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) is increasingly used in the ICU and may alter antifungal PK through filtration and/or adsorption within hemofilters or the extracorporeal circuit [8–17].

Clinical PK data indicate that, following standard dosing of micafungin (100 mg infused over 1 h), critically ill patients achieve median peak plasma concentrations (C_max) of approximately 7.7 mg/L and median concentrations 24 h after infusion (C_24) of approximately 1.8 mg/L [4]. These values informed our selection of a low initial concentration in the protein-free medium (2.18 mg/L), approximating trough exposure, and a high concentration (approximately 6.8 mg/L), approximating peak exposure. The SAFE-ICU consortium further highlighted that contemporary antifungal dosing may be insufficient in some ICU patients [3], emphasizing the importance of understanding extracorporeal losses.

Whether unbound micafungin is sequestered within CRRT circuits, whether late desorption can be detected, and how the tested filter systems behave under clinically relevant conditions remain incompletely characterized. We therefore assessed unbound micafungin disposition in a validated in vitro model using polyacrylonitrile- and polysulfone-derived filters [18]. Our objectives were to describe disappearance from the central compartment, filtration, and late release/desorption at clinically relevant concentrations and to characterize the tested systems under these conditions.

Methods

Micafungin

Micafungin was obtained from Astellas Pharma as 100-mg lyophilized vials. Each vial was reconstituted with 20 mL water for injection and diluted in 5 L of Hemosol™ B0 (Baxter) to achieve the target unbound concentrations in the protein-free medium.

NeckEpur In Vitro Model

Drug disposition was studied using the NeckEpur model as previously described [14, 15, 19–21]. Briefly, a 5-L central compartment (CC) containing a crystalloid solution was connected via tubing to an extracorporeal circuit equipped with a hemofilter and a roller pump. Simulated blood flow (Q_b) was 200 mL/min.

The net ultrafiltration rate was set to zero by simultaneous compensation of dialysate and/or filtrate flow rates back to the CC. All compartments used the same crystalloid solution to avoid concentration gradients attributable to electrolyte differences. Because the model did not contain albumin or alpha-1-acid glycoprotein, it specifically assessed the behavior of unbound micafungin in a protein-free system, that is, the fraction directly available for interaction with the extracorporeal circuit.

Micafungin stability in Hemosol™ B0 was assessed over 8 h at room temperature and natural daylight in triplicate (n = 3) by sampling aliquots at 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h.

Filters and CRRT Settings

Two membrane types were tested.

ST™150 (polyacrylonitrile-derived membrane; Baxter-Gambro): sessions lasted 6 h in post-dilution continuous veno-venous hemofiltration (CVVH). The filtration flow rate was 2.5 L/h and Q_b was 200 mL/min. Separate circuits were run at low micafungin concentration (2.18 mg/L; n = 1) and high micafungin concentration (6.96 and 6.67 mg/L; n = 2).

AV™1000 (polysulfone-derived membrane; Fresenius): two exploratory sessions were performed at initial concentrations of 6.97 mg/L and 6.48 mg/L (n = 1 each). One session used post-dilution CVVH (2.5 L/h) and one used continuous veno-venous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF; dialysis 1.5 L/h plus post-dilution filtration 1.0 L/h). Q_b was 200 mL/min. Each session lasted 6 h. A new filter was used for each experiment.

The combined dia/filtration flow rate of 2.5 L/h approximates a dose of 35 mL/kg/h in a 70-kg adult [22]. The two AV™1000 experiments are reported separately to avoid inappropriate pooling; they were exploratory runs performed under CVVH and CVVHDF, respectively, and were not designed to compare CRRT modalities.

Samples were collected simultaneously from the CC, the inlet and outlet of the filter, and the instantaneous effluent line at baseline and at 15, 30, 45, 60, 120, 180, 200, 240, 300, and 360 min, according to the experimental worksheet for each run. Cumulative effluents were sampled whenever the effluent bag was full and at the end of the session.

Analytical Method

Micafungin concentrations were measured using a validated liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) assay with an LLOQ of 0.1 mg/L. The method was adapted from a previously published antifungal drug-monitoring assay [23]. Intra- and interday imprecision and inaccuracy were < 20% at the LLOQ and < 15% at higher concentration levels.

Calculations

Concentrations are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when replicate runs were available. The amount of micafungin in the CC at each time point was calculated as Q_CC(t) = C_CC(t) × V_CC, where V_CC = 5 L.

The percentage eliminated from the CC was calculated as % eliminated = [1 − Q_CC(t)/Q_CC(0)] × 100.

Cl_CC was estimated as Cl_CC = Q_CC(0)/AUC_CC, where AUC_CC is the area under the CC concentration–time curve over the experimental period. Apparent central-compartment clearance is used here to describe transfer out of the sampled central compartment under the experimental conditions and should not be interpreted as effluent clearance alone.

The sieving coefficient (Sc), representing removal by filtration, was defined as the ratio of simultaneously measured effluent and inlet concentrations. The extraction coefficient (EC), representing net removal or release across the filter, was defined as EC = (C_inlet − C_outlet)/C_inlet. Negative EC values indicate net release from the circuit into the simulated blood flow.

To localize drug loss within the extracorporeal system during the early quantifiable phase (15–45 min), we first examined the upstream concentration difference ΔCcc → Cini, reflecting the inlet segment only. In addition, because concentrations were measured simultaneously at filter inlet (C_in), filter outlet (C_out), and instantaneous effluent (C_eff), within-filter mass balance was assessed. In CVVH, inlet mass flow was Q_b × C_in, blood outlet mass flow was (Q_b − Q_f) × C_out, and effluent mass flow was Q_f × C_eff. In CVVHDF, blood outlet mass flow was (Q_b − Q_f) × C_out, whereas effluent mass flow was (Q_f + Q_d) × C_eff. The difference between inlet mass flow and the sum of blood outlet and effluent mass flows was interpreted as non-effluent loss within the filter module. Because the inlet and outlet blood lines had identical material and identical length for both ST™150 and AV™1000 circuits, the upstream concentration difference was also used to assess whether a predominantly tubing-only explanation for the observed losses was plausible.

For the primary analysis, measured concentrations < LLOQ were set to zero, consistent with the original worksheets. Sensitivity analyses repeated the AUC_CC and Cl_CC calculations after replacing concentrations < LLOQ by LLOQ/2 or LLOQ/√2.

Late release/desorption was estimated descriptively by integrating (C_outlet − C_inlet) over the late interval during which EC became negative while paired inlet/outlet concentrations remained quantifiable. This interval was 120–200 min for the ST™150 low-concentration experiment and 180–360 min for the ST™150 high-concentration experiments. No late-release estimate was calculated for AV™1000 because negative EC values were not observed.

Isolated Sc values slightly greater than 1 were interpreted cautiously as values compatible with assay variability, sampling/mixing effects, or concentration polarization, and were not over-interpreted as evidence of true “super-filtration”.

Ethical Approval

Ethical approval was not sought for this study, as the work did not involve human participants, human data, or animal subjects. The study was performed entirely in vitro using synthetic materials, which do not fall under the purview of the local ethics committee.

Results

Stability of Micafungin

Micafungin remained stable in Hemosol™ B0 over 8 h (n = 3). The mean concentration decreased from 5.1 ± 0.3 mg/L at baseline to 4.8 ± 0.3 mg/L at 8 h, corresponding to a mean change of − 5.2 ± 0.5%, which was considered within assay variability.

At the low initial concentration (2.18 mg/L; n = 1), micafungin concentrations in the CC and at the filter inlet/outlet decreased rapidly (Fig. 1). Discussion

This study provides a mechanistic description of unbound micafungin behavior in a protein-free in vitro CRRT model. The main findings are that (1) unbound micafungin disappeared rapidly from the central compartment with both tested filter systems, (2) ST™150 showed measurable contributions from effluent removal and non-effluent loss at higher concentration, and (3) late negative EC values compatible with limited desorption were observed with ST™150 but not with AV™1000 under the tested conditions.

The two AV™1000 experiments were performed under CVVH and CVVHDF, respectively, and are therefore presented separately. The additional AV™1000-CVVHDF run was exploratory and was not performed to test modality effects per se. Nevertheless, both AV™1000 experiments showed the same qualitative pattern: rapid disappearance from the central compartment, effluent concentrations below the LLOQ, and no late negative EC values.

The handling of values below the LLOQ is another important methodological issue. In the primary analysis, values < LLOQ were set to zero, and sensitivity analyses using LLOQ/2 and LLOQ/√2 were also performed. These approaches changed some numerical Cl_CC estimates, especially for the ST™150 low-concentration run and the AV™1000 runs, but did not change the overall pattern. Because several outlet and effluent concentrations were at or below the LLOQ, the data support near-complete extraction within assay limits rather than formal demonstration of complete extraction or 100% sequestration.

Apparent central-compartment clearance (Cl_CC), defined as Q_CC(0)/AUC_CC, describes transfer out of the central compartment under the experimental conditions and should not be confused with effluent clearance alone. In the present setting, it integrates all processes contributing to loss from the sampled compartment, including measurable effluent removal when present and non-effluent loss within the extracorporeal system.

The late negative EC values observed with ST™150 are compatible with limited late desorption, but these estimates remain uncertain. They were derived from low late-phase concentrations, sometimes close to the LLOQ, and therefore should be understood as descriptive rather than definitive quantitative measures. Similarly, isolated Sc values slightly above 1 were not interpreted as physiologically meaningful values; they are most plausibly explained by assay variability, imperfect mixing/sampling timing, or concentration-polarization effects.

Direct clinical extrapolation remains limited. The model deliberately contained no proteins because only the unbound fraction can interact directly with the extracorporeal circuit. This design therefore isolates the mechanistic question addressed here—the behavior of free micafungin within the circuit—but translation to in vivo exposure will depend on protein binding and rebinding kinetics, which remain incompletely characterized [24].

Segmental sampling and within-filter mass-balance analysis allow more precise localization of loss than whole-circuit concentrations alone. The upstream concentration difference between the central compartment and filter inlet was small or absent overall, indicating only limited loss in the inlet segment. By contrast, simultaneous inlet, outlet, and effluent concentrations allowed within-filter disappearance to be decomposed into measurable effluent removal and non-effluent loss. In the AV™1000 experiments, the absence of measurable effluent concentrations indicates that within-filter disappearance was dominated by non-effluent loss within assay limits, whereas in the ST™150 high-concentration experiments both effluent removal and non-effluent loss contributed. Because outlet concentrations were measured at the filter outlet, this balance localizes the dominant loss to the filter module itself rather than to the outlet return line. Since inlet and outlet blood lines had identical material and length, the observed pattern does not support a predominantly tubing-only explanation. A limited tubing contribution cannot be fully excluded at the whole-circuit level, but it does not appear to account for the dominant loss signal.

Our study has several limitations. First, it was exploratory, with very small numbers and several n = 1 conditions. Second, because the model was intentionally protein-free, the data describe free-drug interactions with the extracorporeal system and cannot by themselves predict whole-plasma exposure in vivo. Third, no circuit-only control was performed. However, segmental sampling and within-filter mass-balance analysis still allowed quantitative decomposition of inlet-segment loss and of within-filter disappearance into measurable effluent removal and non-effluent loss. The absence of a no-filter control therefore limits formal confirmatory comparison with a filter-free circuit rather than preventing the descriptive partition reported here. Finally, sessions lasted 6 h only; longer experiments might reveal additional late-phase dynamics.

Conclusion

In this exploratory protein-free in vitro CRRT model, unbound micafungin rapidly disappeared from the extracorporeal circuit with both tested filter systems. ST™150 showed measurable effluent removal at higher concentrations and limited late release compatible with desorption, whereas both exploratory AV™1000 runs showed rapid disappearance without detectable late release. Segmental concentration data and within-filter mass-balance analysis localized the dominant loss to the filter module, with only limited upstream inlet-segment contribution. These findings characterize free micafungin interactions with the circuit under the tested conditions, but their translation to in vivo exposure will depend on protein binding and rebinding kinetics.
