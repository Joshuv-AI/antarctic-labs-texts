Delineating the Triphasic Side Reaction Products in High‐Energy Density Lithium‐Ion Batteries
Authors: Chen Liu, Seth Reed, Arumugam Manthiram
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnwanted cathode‐electrolyte side reactions remain a major challenge to the stability of lithium‐ion batteries, especially at high temperatures. This study presents a quantitative analysis on gaseous, soluble, and solid byproducts generated by reactions between LiNiO2 and carbonate electrolytes. Online electrochemical mass spectrometry (OEMS) outgassing profiles reveal the emergence of a pre‐plateau region as temperature increases, characterized by two small plateaus at 3.7 and 4.0 V, which are attributed to catalytic ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) decomposition and ethylene carbonate (EC) hydrolysis, respectively. Activation energies for electrolyte oxidation reactions are quantified with OEMS, confirming that the main outgassing event is driven by direct oxygen‐electrolyte chemical reactions. Nuclear magnetic resonance and X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy analyses show that EC decomposition yields both soluble and solid byproducts, while EMC produces mainly solid species, leading to a thicker, organic‐rich cathode‐electrolyte interphase; the presence of LiPF6 further accelerates solvent degradation. Additionally, the effectiveness of various dopants in suppressing gas evolution is evaluated across a broad temperature range, with magnesium‐doped cathodes exhibiting the most effective suppression by stabilizing lattice oxygen. Overall, this work establishes a clear correlation among triphasic side‐reaction products, guiding rational design of cathode materials and electrolyte formulations for high‐energy lithium‐ion batteries.

Introduction

As the dominant technology for energy storage, lithium (Li)‐ion batteries (LIBs) are widely used in electric vehicles, portable electronics, and grid‐scale energy systems due to their high energy density and long cycle life.[ 1 ] Among their key components, layered oxide cathodes, such as LiNixMnyCo1‐x‐yO2 (NMC) and LiNixCoyAl1‐x‐yO2 (NCA), stand out as some of the most promising cathode materials.[ 2 ] To further boost energy density and reduce reliance on cobalt, research efforts have increasingly focused on raising the nickel (Ni) content above 80%. While this strategy brings performance benefits, it also introduces new challenges.[ 3 ] High‐Ni cathodes are more vulnerable to degradation, especially at high states‐of‐charge (SOCs) and elevated temperatures, due to their intrinsic structural and chemical instabilities.[ 4 ]

At high SOCs, Ni in the cathode is oxidized from Ni3+ to Ni4+, which weakens the Ni‐O bonds and makes the structure more susceptible to oxygen loss. The release of reactive oxygen aggressively attacks the electrolyte, generating unwanted byproducts at the electrode interface.[ 5 ] Meanwhile, high‐Ni cathodes undergo surface reconstructions, from their original layered structure into spinel‐like and/or rock‐salt phases with a higher degree of Li+/Ni2+ cation mixing.[ 2 , 6 ] These transformations hinder Li+ transport and increase resistance, ultimately degrading the battery performance. Furthermore, the highly oxidized Ni4+ accelerates electrolyte breakdown, cathode‐electrolyte interphase (CEI) growth, and transition‐metal dissolution.[ 7 ] These dissolved metal ions may then deposit on the anode, disrupting the interfacial stability and further compromising cell lifetime.[ 8 ] More importantly, the released reactiveoxygen can attack carbonate electrolytes and trigger the evolution of gases like CO2, CO, and O2.[ 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 ] The gas generation not only contributes to internal pressure buildup and risks of cell swelling or leakage, but also reflects the inherent thermal stability of high‐Ni layered oxides, as oxygen release plays a key role in driving exothermic reactions and accelerating thermal runaway under abusive or elevated temperatures.[ 13 , 14 ]

Overall, the byproducts formed from interactions between high‐Ni cathodes and electrolytes can be broadly categorized into three types: gases, soluble species, and solid deposits (Figure 1 ). Gas generation mainly arises from chemical reactions between high‐Ni cathodes and the electrolyte, often involving lattice oxygen release.[ 10 , 15 ] It is often believed that the release of highly reactive singlet oxygen (1O2) at high SOCs can directly attack solvent molecules and initiate gas evolution.[ 16 , 17 ] Meanwhile, soluble species, such as dissolved transition‐metal ions and organic solvent degradation products, further degrade the electrolyte and gradually accumulate at the anode side and aggravate the anode deterioration.[ 18 , 19 , 20 ] Solid byproducts, including lithium and transition‐metal fluorides, alkaline carbonates, polymeric organics, etc., tend to accumulate on the cathode surface as part of the CEI, increasing the interfacial resistance and hindering Li+ transport.[ 21 , 22 ] Over time, the continuous buildup of these species accelerates structural and chemical degradation of the cathode and cell performance decay.[ 23 ]

Schematic illustration of the triphasic side reaction products—gaseous, soluble, and solid species—generated from the interactions between high‐nickel layered oxide cathodes and carbonate electrolytes.

In recent years, researchers have increasingly turned to advanced material characterization tools to uncover the origins and impacts of side reactions in LIBs. Differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) and online electrochemical mass spectrometry (OEMS) are powerful techniques developed to monitor in situ gas generation during electrochemical cycling, providing insights into real‐time gas evolution in batteries.[ 6 , 23 ] Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy is used to pinpoint soluble degradation products and map out decomposition pathways in the electrolytes.[ 20 , 24 , 25 ] To investigate solid residues and interphase chemistry, researchers have relied on techniques such as X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), time‐of‐flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF‐SIMS), and Raman spectroscopy.[ 21 , 22 , 26 ] Other sophisticated tools, like gas chromatography‐chemical ionization‐mass spectrometry (GC‐CI‐MS), high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), and inductively coupled plasma‐mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS), have helped examine complex reactions at the electrolyte‐electrode interface.[ 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 ] While previous studies have typically focused on one type of side reaction products, a comprehensive study that correlates three types (gaseous, soluble, and solid) of side reaction products is lacking. Importantly, a systematic investigation integrating multiple factors, including dopants, temperature, and electrolyte formulations, is needed to develop a firm fundamental understanding.

In this study, we systematically quantify the triphasic side reaction products (gaseous, soluble, and solid), arising from interactions between high‐Ni layered oxide cathodes and carbonate electrolytes, while also examining their interconnections. We track gas evolution and analyze the gaseous byproducts generated during cell operation with OEMS. Our investigation centers on the highly reactive LiNiO2 (LNO) cathode, along with doped variants containing 5% cobalt (Co), manganese (Mn), aluminum (Al), or magnesium (Mg), to evaluate how different dopants influence both the onset and total gas evolution at elevated temperatures. By applying Arrhenius analysis to the linear regions of gas evolution profiles, we extract the reaction rate constants and calculate the activation energies, offering a kinetic perspective on outgassing mechanisms shaped by dopant chemistry and electrolyte formulation. To complement the gas evolution study, we design specific experiments to decouple the influence of different carbonate solvents on the degradation behavior with high‐Ni cathodes. Solvent and salt decomposition pathways are proposed based on the identification of soluble byproducts via NMR, while solid interphase species are analyzed with XPS. By integrating insights from OEMS, NMR, and XPS, we present a comprehensive picture of the reactions between high‐Ni layered oxide cathodes and carbonate electrolytes. These findings offer practical guidance for designing more stable cathode materials and electrolyte systems for next‐generation LIBs.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of Gas Evolution: Profiling, Product Identification, and Dopant Influence at Elevated Temperatures

The gas evolution experiments were performed with five different cathodes, including LNO and LNO doped with 5% Co, Mn, Al, or Mg, which are denoted as NC, NM, NA, and NMg, respectively. The particle morphology of each cathode was examined with SEM, as shown in Figure S1 (Supporting Information). The secondary particle size for all samples is ≈ 12 µm. XRD analysis in Figure S2 (Supporting Information) confirms that all samples exhibit a single‐phase layered structure with an R‐3m space group with no impurity phase. The combined morphological and structural characterization confirms the successful synthesis of the cathodes, establishing a necessary foundation for rigorous and meaningful comparison of the gas evolution profile.

To begin with, we analyzed the gas evolution behavior of LNO across a temperature range from room temperature (RT, 21 °C) to 60 °C in 10 °C intervals, focusing on CO2, CO, and O2 emissions (Figure S3, Supporting Information). As expected, the total amount of gas increased with temperature, rising from 97 µmol g−1 at RT to 318 µmol g−1 at 60 °C. This is because the elevated temperature accelerates oxygen release and exacerbates the oxidative decomposition of the electrolyte.[ 18 , 20 ] All three gases showed a similar upward trend, with CO2 remaining the most abundant throughout.

Notably, a detailed analysis of the CO2 profiles in Figure 2a reveals two temperature‐dependent features: (i) the onset potential of CO2 evolution and (ii) the curvature of the CO2 release profile. Specifically, at lower temperatures (RT and 30 °C), CO2, along with CO and O2, begins to evolve at ≈ 4.4 V, consistent with the intrinsic chemical instability limit of layered oxide cathodes.[ 32 ] As the temperature increases to ≥ 40 °C, however, the onset of CO2 evolution shifts to lower voltages, indicating an earlier initiation of gas release. Interestingly, this potential shift is accompanied by the gradual development of a pre‐plateau region in the gas evolution profile, as highlighted in Figure 2a. As further illustrated in Figure 2b, this feature can be categorized into four stages with increasing temperature: i) absence of small plateaus at RT and 30 °C, ii) the emergence of small plateaus, iii) a linear growth stage, and iv) fully developed two distinct small plateaus. In contrast to CO2, the evolution profiles of CO and O2 remain largely unaffected by temperature, consistently initiating at ≈ 4.4 V across all temperatures (Figure S3, Supporting Information). The temperature at which these small plateaus first appear is defined as the critical temperature, identified as 40 °C for LNO. The mild linear increase observed in stages (ii) and (iii) before the substantial outgassing event has been reported in other studies and is commonly attributed to the electrochemical oxidation of residual impurities in the carbonate electrolyte, which may originate from the hydrolysis of ethylene carbonate.[ 33 ] However, the development of the pre‐plateau region with increasing temperature suggests a more complex interplay of the degradation mechanisms.

a) CO2 gas evolution profiles of Li | LNO cells charged to 4.4 V at various temperatures. b) Schematic illustration of the emergence of the pre‐plateau region in the CO2 evolution profiles. c) CO2 gas evolution profiles of Li | LNO cells cycled with LP57 and SEC electrolytes at 60 °C. d) LSV curves of the Li | NiO OEMS cells with LP57, SEC, and SEMC electrolytes at 60 °C. e) A heat map of CO2 evolution intensity across different temperatures and cathode compositions. Bar plots comparing f) total gas released (CO2, O2, and CO) and g) Ni oxidation states corresponding to the 3rd gas evolution plateaus (≈ 4.4 V) for five different cathodes at various temperatures.

To delineate the formation mechanism of the two small plateaus of the CO2 profile at 60 °C, we prepared an SEC electrolyte containing only 1 m LiPF6 in pure EC and conducted OEMS measurements with the same batch of LNO to ensure consistency. A comparison of the gas evolution profiles reveals notable differences. While LP57 exhibits two distinct small plateaus in the CO2 evolution curve, SEC shows only one (Figure 2c). Interestingly, the single small plateau observed with SEC aligns closely with the second small plateau in the LP57 profile, suggesting that this feature likely originates from EC decomposition. The earlier small plateau seen in LP57 is, therefore, likely attributed to EMC decomposition on the metal oxide surface. It is worth noting that SEMC (1 m LiPF6 in pure EMC) is not compatible with Li | LNO cell, requiring further investigations to validate this hypothesis.

In this regard, we assembled a cell with NiO as the cathode and conducted LSV tests in an OEMS cell setup. Three electrolytes, including LP57, SEC, and SEMC, were individually evaluated. In Figure 2d, CO2 release was detected in the LP57 cell, indicating that either EC, EMC, or both may undergo decomposition on the metal oxide surface. As shown in the right panel of Figure 2d, EMC exhibits pronounced gas evolution, while EC remains relatively inert, suggesting that EMC is more prone to catalytic decomposition by transition‐metal ions.[ 20 , 34 , 35 ] This catalytic EMC decomposition mechanism is likely responsible for the formation of the 1st small plateau in the CO2 profile as demonstrated by their similar onset voltage of ≈ 3.7 V. The decomposition pathway of EC will be discussed in detail in the subsequent section.

After gaining insights into how individual electrolyte components contribute to early gas formation (pre‐plateau region), the effect of cathode composition on the gas evolution characteristics was evaluated. Elemental doping in layered oxide cathodes is widely regarded as an effective strategy to alleviate gas release.[ 36 ] To explore this, we compared the gas evolution characteristics of pristine LNO, NC, NM, NA, and NMg, as shown in Figures S3–S7 (Supporting Information). Similar to LNO, all doped cathodes experience the major outgassing after the H2‐H3 phase transition (Figure S8, Supporting Information) and exhibit the small plateau features at high temperatures. Despite these shared features, the critical temperature and the small plateau curvature are different for the doped cathodes. First, while LNO starts to show the small plateaus at 40 °C, the doped cathodes delay their appearance until 50 °C, suggesting a suppressed cathode reactivity compared to LNO. Second, the doped cathodes display a single, broad feature in the pre‐plateau region at 60 °C, contrasting with the two distinct small plateaus observed in LNO. This suggests that the catalytic decomposition of EMC, contributing to the formation of the 1st small plateau, is suppressed by elemental doping.

As the dominant gaseous product, CO2 release with each cathode at each temperature was quantified and visualized as a heat map in Figure 2e, while the total gas evolutions are compared in Figure 2f. Compared to LNO, all doped cathodes, except for NC, significantly reduce CO2 release and the overall gas release up to 50 °C, with the total amount of gas release following the trend of LNO ≈ NC > NM ≈ NA >> NMg. Of note, Mn and Al play a key role in suppressing gas evolution by lowering the concentration of highly reactive Ni4+ at high SOCs and strengthening the metal‐oxygen bond, respectively.[ 36 ] Despite this, interestingly, LNO, NC, NA, and NM exhibit a similar amount of gas generation at 60 °C. This is because relatively more reactive cathodes like LNO and NC can be quickly passivated by the formation of surface NiO phase at the beginning of outgassing reactions at such a high temperature, which hinders the gas release afterward. On the other hand, Mg remains consistently most effective in suppressing gas evolution across all temperatures. Mg2+ particularly stands out by occupying partially in the lithium layer and acting as a pillar ion, which can reduce the decrease in the interlayer spacing between the transition‐metal slabs at deep charge, thereby suppressing the lattice oxygen release and outgassing reactions.[ 37 , 38 ]

Furthermore, distribution of relaxation times (DRT) analysis was performed for each cathode to provide deeper insights into the in situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) data and to highlight the role of dopants in enhancing interfacial and ion‐transport processes. As shown in Figure S9 (Supporting Information), a time constant (τ) of ≈ 10 s is typically associated with mass transport or diffusion‐related processes (e.g., Warburg impedance, Zw), while time constants in the range of 1–0.1 s correspond to charge‐transfer resistance (Rct).[ 39 , 40 ] A τ of ≈ 0.01 s reflects interfacial processes, such as Li⁺ transport through the CEI layer (Rfilm). Among all the cathodes evaluated, LNO exhibits the highest CEI resistance (Rfilm), followed by NC, while NM, NA, and NMg show relatively lower Rfilm values. Of note, the differences in Rfilm are less pronounced, which is reasonable considering that the first charge cycle primarily reflects the early stages of CEI formation. However, clear differences in Rct and Zw were observed during charging, with LNO showing the highest resistance and NMg the lowest. Normalized cycling performance for each cathode is also presented in Figure S10 (Supporting Information). LNO demonstrates the lowest capacity retention (78.7%) after 100 cycles at a C/3 rate, whereas NMg shows the highest stability (94.9%). These trends from both DRT analysis and cycling data are consistent with the OEMS results, further supporting the conclusion that dopants play a crucial role in modulating the interfacial stability and transport kinetics.

Lastly, the outgassing SOC (i.e., degree of delithiation) onset points for the five cathodes were further analyzed across different temperatures, as shown in Figure S11 (Supporting Information). LNO, with a 100% Ni content, demonstrates an average SOC stability limit of ≈ 94%, irrespective of the operating temperature. In comparison, the doped cathodes with 95% Ni, show a slightly lower stability limit of ≈ 90% at all temperatures. Another critical aspect of evaluating cathode stability is the Ni oxidation state, which was calculated for each cathode at various temperatures (Figure 2g). Notably, the Ni oxidation state remains largely consistent across temperatures, with values of 3.93+ for LNO, 3.97+ for NC, 3.90+ for NM, 3.95+ for NA, and 3.92+ for NMg, closely matching our previously reported values.[ 36 ] This consistency underscores the reliability of the Ni oxidation state as an indicator of cathode stability limits.

Kinetic Analysis of Gas Evolution: Activation Energy Determination via Arrhenius Equation

While the comparison of released CO2 amount presents a thermodynamic perspective of the outgassing reactions, their kinetic characteristics need further investigation, which requires a closer examination on the CO2 generation rate, namely the slope of CO2 profile. One notable observation is the appearance of a linear region at the beginning of significant CO2 release, consistently seen across all samples and temperatures. Therefore, it is possible to extract the reaction rate constant based on the slope of this linear region, which could be used to calculate the activation energy (Ea).

To begin, different cathode and electrolyte compositions were selected and compared to validate the theory. Specifically, LNO and NMg, respectively, as the least and most stable cathodes were examined; on the other hand, to benchmark against the baseline LP57 electrolyte, we also studied an E19 electrolyte with a higher EMC content, as well as an advanced localized saturated electrolyte (LSE). Both were tested in Li | LNO cells to enable broader screening and demonstrate the applicability of this method. As shown in Figure 3a,b, we focused our analysis on the CO2 evolution profiles at room temperature (RT), 30, and 40 °C. Data collected at 50 and 60 °C were excluded from activation energy calculations, as the rapid formation of surface NiO phases at elevated temperatures can compromise the accuracy of reaction rate determination. To provide additional rational for this approach, we performed electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) following a 6‐hour constant voltage (CV) hold at each temperature during the OEMS experiments. As shown in Figure S12 (Supporting Information), the impedance increases markedly above 40 °C for all cathodes, indicating the onset of interfacial degradation and resistive phase (e.g., NiO) formation. Therefore, RT, 30, and 40 °C were selected to represent the most reliable temperature points for determining activation energy to minimize the influence of thermally induced degradation processes.

CO2 gas evolution profiles of a) Li | LNO and Li | NMg cells with LP57 and b) Li | LNO cells with E19 charged to 4.4 V at RT, 30, and 40 °C. c) Schematic illustration of the Arrhenius fitting approach applied to CO2 gas evolution profiles, along with the Arrhenius equation. d) Arrhenius plots are used to extract the slopes for activation energy calculation. e) Calculated activation energies for gas release reactions. f) Schematic representation of the difference in activation energies between slower and faster reaction pathways. Contour plots of DRT analysis and corresponding voltage profiles for g) LNO‐LP57, h) NMg, and i) LNO‐LSE cells at room temperature, where Li4Ti5O12 (LTO) was used as a stable counter electrode.

Although NMg consistently releases much less CO2 than LNO, both samples display a clear linear region during the initial stage of gas evolution (Figure 3a). Similarly, such a linear region also exists in the CO2 profiles of the E19 and the LSE cells (Figure 3b; Figure S13, Supporting Information). Interestingly, while E19 results in greater CO2 evolution at RT and 30 °C, the total gas release at 40 °C is slightly lower than that with LP57. This indicates that it is easier to form the passivating surface layer on LNO in the E19 electrolyte, which could suppress lattice oxygen release and thus reduce the overall CO2 release. Compared to E19, LSE exhibits lower CO2 release at RT, 30, and 40 °C. Although LSE shows similar CO2 generation to LP57 at RT, it releases significantly less CO2 at 30 and 40 °C, demonstrating its effectiveness in suppressing gas evolution at elevated temperatures (Figure S13, Supporting Information). Overall, despite the differences in cathode and electrolyte compositions, the initial linear region remains consistent across all conditions.

To better understand this linearity feature, a more detailed examination of the CO2 release profile was conducted, which reveals three distinct regions: a non‐reacting region, a linear growth region, and a final non‐linear regime (Figure 3c). In the early stage, no gas is detected because gas release begins only after the Ni3+/4+: eg redox energy enters into the top of the O2−: 2p band, triggering lattice oxygen release that will react with the electrolyte. Following this onset, the profile enters the linear region, indicating a zero‐order reaction where the CO2 generation rate remains constant. This is supported by the excellent linear fitting (R2 ≈ 0.999) of the n(CO2)‐time data. As charging progresses, the profile transitions into a non‐linear regime, likely due to surface reconstruction and the formation of a passivating NiO‐like layer on the cathode surface, lowering the oxygen generation rate.

To evaluate the reaction kinetics, reaction rate constants (k) were extracted by fitting the linear regions of the CO2 evolution profiles for LNO, NMg, and LNO with the E19 and LSE electrolytes. Afterward, the ln(k)‐1000/T plots of different samples are displayed in Figure 3d, which all exhibit a well‐defined linear relationship following the Arrhenius equation in Figure 3c. The slopes of the ln(k)‐1000/T plots can then be used to calculate the activation energy based on Ea = −1000*slope*R (where R is the gas constant). Accordingly, the activation energies of LNO and NMg are calculated as 80 and 82 kJ mol−1, respectively (Figure 3e). Such a similarity demonstrates that the outgassing reaction occurs chemically between the released oxygen, rather than the cathode itself, and the electrolyte, quantitatively validating previous reports.[ 5 ] In contrast, LNO charged with E19 exhibits a significantly lower activation energy of 45 kJ mol−1, while that charged with LSE shows a much higher value of 93 kJ mol−1. This indicates that the oxidative reaction in E19 is kinetically more favorable than that in LP57 and LSE. As a higher Ea corresponds to a greater energy barrier and slower outgassing kinetics (and vice versa), these results underscore the critical role of electrolyte formulation in tuning outgassing reaction kinetics (Figure 3f).

Based on the OEMS analysis, two effective strategies for suppressing gas evolution were identified: i) elemental doping and ii) tuning electrolyte formulations. To further validate these findings, DRT analysis was carried out on LNO‐LP57, NMg, and LNO‐LSE samples (Figure 3g–i). In Figure 3h, Mg doping was found to significantly reduce the Rfilm, Rct, and Zw compared to undoped LNO, indicating a more stable and conductive interphase that facilitates improved Li⁺‐ion transport. Consistently, OEMS results revealed markedly lower CO2 and total gas evolution from Mg‐doped cathodes. These results suggest that elemental doping enhances the structural stability of the cathode and suppresses lattice oxygen release, which in turn reduces side reactions between reactive oxygen species and electrolyte decomposition products at the cathode surface. Similarly, in Figure 3i, DRT analysis reveals that the LSE electrolyte exhibits lower Rfilm, Rct, and Zw than the baseline LP57, confirming improved interfacial stability and faster ion transport. These findings are corroborated with OEMS data, which show reduced CO2 evolution in LSE, indicating the formation of a more robust and passivating CEI that effectively suppresses interfacial side reactions. To further reinforce these conclusions, galvanostatic cycling tests of LNO cells with LP57, E19, and LSE were performed (Figure S14, Supporting Information). The results show that LSE delivers the most stable cycling performance (97% capacity retention), outperforming both LP57 (79%) and E19 (74%), in agreement with the trends observed in gas, kinetics, and DRT analyses.

Quantitative Analysis of Soluble Decomposition Products in Electrolyte

In addition to analyzing gaseous species, we further designed an electrolyte aging experiment to identify and quantify the soluble electrolyte decomposition products, which can offer additional insights into electrolyte breakdown mechanisms. As schematically illustrated in Figure 4a, LNO electrodes were charged to a specific capacity of 260 mA h g−1, then retrieved from the Li | LNO cells, and cleaned with DMC. Afterward, the charged LNO electrodes were soaked in two categories of electrolytes: solvent‐only group (EC and EMC) and salt‐containing group (SEC and SEMC). For comparison, parallel samples were prepared without the addition of LNO electrodes. All samples were aged in an environmental chamber at 60 °C for one month prior to NMR analysis.

a) Schematic illustration of the experimental setup for NMR analysis. 1H NMR spectra of the decomposition products with the b) EC‐based electrolyte group and c) EMC‐based electrolyte group. 19F NMR spectra of the d) EC‐based group and e) EMC‐based group.

Figure 4b shows the 1H NMR spectra of the EC‐based group, comparing pure EC and salt‐containing SEC samples at RT and 60 °C. Notably, after one month of aging at 60 °C, new peaks emerge at 3.19, 3.46, and 3.62 ppm in both EC and LNO‐in‐EC samples. These peaks likely correspond to lithium methyl carbonate (LMC), lithium ethylene monocarbonate (LEMC), and poly(ethylene oxide)‐based oligomers, respectively, which are commonly attributed to EC hydrolysis reactions (Table S2, Supporting Information).[ 18 , 25 ] Further degradation of LEMC can yield ethylene glycol (EG) and CO2, which is likely the formation mechanism of the second small plateau observed in the CO2 evolution profiles of LNO (Figure 2c) as well as other doped cathodes (Figures S4–S7, Supporting Information).[ 18 , 20 , 25 ] Interestingly, the intensities of LMC, LEMC, and oligomer peaks are much lower in the LNO‐in‐EC sample compared to the pure EC sample, possibly due to the consumption of these species through further reactions with the charged LNO.

As a result, these impurities may be eventually transformed into insoluble species as part of the CEI. Surprisingly, these EC decomposition peaks are absent in the SEC samples with or without LNO. This infers that the hydrolysis reactions of LiPF6 and its decomposition products like PF5 are more favorable compared to EC hydrolysis reactions, which is thereby suppressed. Another possible reason could be that LiPF6 decomposition byproducts may react with LMC, LEMC, and oligomers, leading to the formation of solid (e.g., Li2CO3 and polymers) or gaseous (e.g., CO2) products.

The EMC‐based samples show a different trend compared to EC‐based samples. Compared to their fresh counterparts (EMC and SEMC) at RT, the aged samples reveal no significant new peaks in the 1H NMR spectra (Figure 4c). This suggests that the decomposition of EMC mainly leads to the formation of gaseous and/or solid byproducts and only produces trace amount of soluble species. A detailed discussion of this theory is provided in the following section. Figure 4d,e presents the 19F NMR spectra for SEC and SEMC groups. In the aged SEC and SEMC samples, the peaks at ≈ −72 and −74 ppm correspond to LiPF6, while the peaks at ≈ −83 and −85 ppm are attributed to its decomposition product PO2F2 − (Table S2, Supporting Information) formed via hydrolysis reactions.[ 20 , 25 ] Moreover, for all the LNO‐containing samples, the intensity of these salt‐derived peaks is higher than that in the pure electrolyte samples. This is because the cathode‐solvent reactions commonly produce water, which will promote the hydrolysis of LiPF6. Complementary 31P NMR spectra presented in Figure S15 (Supporting Information) reveal similar trends and corroborate the 19F NMR spectra.

Overall, the soluble species resulting from cathode‐electrolyte reactions primarily originate from the decomposition of EC and the salt, whereas EMC exhibits considerably less amount of such species. Of note, the dissolved products can cross over to the anode side and exacerbate the anode degradation.[ 41 ]

Quantification of Solid Reaction Products Involved in Cathode‐Electrolyte Interphase (CEI) Formation

After investigating the soluble byproducts, we further studied the solid side reaction products constituting CEI by XPS sputtering. Specifically, the LNO electrodes aged in electrolytes (EC, SEC, EMC, and SEMC) for the NMR study were retrieved and analyzed, as shown in Figure 5 . XPS scans of the retrieved samples were performed initially at 0 s, followed by a 600 s sputtering step (5 kV, 20 nA), and then another XPS scan was conducted to gain insights into the CEI characteristics.

C 1s and O 1s XPS data of cathodes aged at 60 °C in a) EC, b) SEC, c) EMC, and d) SEMC electrolytes and F 1s data of cathodes aged in e) EC‐ and f) EMC‐based electrolyte groups, highlighting the chemical composition of the surface species formed during electrolyte decomposition.

To start, in the C 1s spectra, where the peak at 284.4 eV corresponds to the C‐C bond in conductive carbon, while the peak at 284.7 eV represents PVDF binder.[ 42 , 43 ] Peaks related to C‐O, C = O, and ROCO2R’ are indicative of organic species formed from electrolyte solvent decomposition during aging. Specifically, as illustrated in Figure 5a–d, cathodes aged in salt‐containing electrolytes (SEC and SEMC) show higher intensities of solvent decomposition‐related peaks in the C 1s spectra, both before and after sputtering, compared to those aged in their respective pure solvents (EC and EMC). Among all the samples, SEMC exhibits the most intense signals of the organic species, corroborating the NMR results in Figure 4c, where aged EMC did not show any soluble degradation products. This trend is further supported by the O 1s spectra in Figure 5d, where SEMC shows stronger peaks at 530.7 and 533.3 eV, which stand for, respectively, C‐O and C = O species, compared to other samples. Furthermore, the peak at 528.4 eV corresponding to the metal‐oxygen (M‐O) bond represents the bulk cathode, the relative intensity of which can be used to evaluate the CEI thickness. It is revealed that all salt‐containing samples exhibit thicker CEI layers, as evidenced by the lower M‐O peak intensities compared to their respective pure solvent samples prior to sputtering. After 600 s of sputtering, the EC sample displays the strongest M‐O peak intensity, while the SEMC sample shows the weakest, suggesting that the EC sample forms the thinnest CEI layer and the SEMC sample the thickest. On the other hand, the concentration of organic species, represented by C‐O and C = O bonding, tend to increase after the sputtering for all cathodes, suggesting that the inner CEI is more organic‐rich. Notably, this trend is less pronounced in the SEMC sample, compared to other samples, which is attributed to the significantly thicker CEI formed in SEMC. The C and O atomic concentrations, shown in Figure S16 (Supporting Information), further support this observation, indicating that SEMC undergoes the most extensive electrolyte decomposition during aging.

The F 1s spectra provide insights into inorganic CEI components (Figure 5e,f). The Li‐F/M‐F peaks at ≈ 684 eV stem from electrolyte salt decomposition and transition‐metal dissolution. Compared to LNO electrodes aged in EC and EMC, aged cathodes in SEC and SEMC exhibit stronger Li‐F/M‐F peaks, suggesting more severe salt degradation and parasitic side reactions at the cathode interface. After sputtering, the intensity of the C‐F peak remains relatively unchanged across all samples. In contrast, the Li‐F/M‐F peak intensity in SEC and SEMC slightly decreases with depth, suggesting that salt decomposition products primarily accumulate near the outer region of the CEI.

Overall, organic species are generally more concentrated in the inner region of the CEI, except in the SEMC sample, whereas inorganic species tend to be predominantly located in the outer layer. Compared to EC, nearly all decomposition products from EMC participate in CEI formation rather than dissolving into the electrolyte, leading to a formation of the most organic‐rich and thickest CEI for the SEMC sample. For LNO in both SEC and SEMC systems, the extent of decomposition is more severe than in pure solvent groups. This suggests that salt degradations at elevated temperatures may further exacerbate solvent breakdown, amplifying interfacial instability.[ 7 , 44 ]

Conclusion

In this work, we systematically quantified the gaseous, soluble, and solid side reaction products of the reactions between high‐Ni layered oxide cathodes and carbonate electrolytes. With a combination of advanced characterization techniques, including OEMS, NMR, and XPS, we delineated a correlation of these decomposition products. A visual summary of our key findings is presented in Figure 6 and outlined below: Gas evolution profiles collected at various temperatures revealed a pre‐plateau region below 4.4 V at 60 °C for all five cathodes, triggering the onset of gas evolution at lower potentials. Among the cathodes investigated, LNO exhibits the most distinct two small plateaus feature in that region, where the first and second small plateaus are attributed to the catalytic EMC decomposition and EC hydrolysis, respectively.The activation energy for electrolyte oxidation reactions was successfully calculated based on OEMS, which enables a quantitative comparison of electrolyte decomposition kinetics. Our results demonstrated that the outgassing reaction occurs chemically between the electrolyte and the released oxygen from the cathode, rather than by the cathode material itself. Therefore, the electrolyte formulation plays a key role in tuning the gas evolution reaction kinetics.Based on NMR and XPS results, EC decomposition leads to the formation of both soluble and solid byproducts. In contrast, EMC decomposition mainly produces solid byproducts, resulting in the formation of a thicker and more organic‐rich CEI. Up to 50 °C, all the doped cathodes, except for NC, outperformed LNO in limiting gas release. Experimental Section

Material Preparation

The hydroxide cathode precursors, Ni(OH)2, Ni0.95Co0.05(OH)2, Ni0.95Mn0.05(OH)2, Ni0.95Al0.05(OH)2.05, and Ni0.95Mg0.05(OH)2, were synthesized via the coprecipitation method reported previously.[ 14 ] The cathode active materials, LiNiO2 (LNO), LiNi0.95Co0.05O2 (NC), LiNi0.95Mn0.05O2 (NM), LiNi0.95Al0.05O2 (NA), LiNi0.95Mg0.05O2 (NMg), were prepared by blending the respective hydroxide precursors with fine‐grinded LiOH·H2O powder at a molar ratio of 1:1.02, followed by calcining in a tube furnace under an O2 flow at 655, 655, 710, 680, and 690 °C, respectively, for 12 h. Of note, the calcination temperatures were optimized for each cathode composition.[ 4 ]

Electrolyte Preparation

The LP57 electrolyte was prepared by dissolving 1 m LiPF6 in a mixture of ethylene carbonate (EC) and ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC) at a 3:7 weight ratio. The E19 electrolyte consisted of 1 m LiPF6 in EC:EMC at a 1:9 weight ratio. The SEC and SEMC electrolytes contained 1 m LiPF6 dissolved in EC and EMC, respectively. The localized saturated electrolyte (LSE) was prepared by dissolving 3 M LiPF6 in a mixture of EC: EMC at a 3:7 weight ratio, and subsequently diluting with 1,1,2,2‐tetrafluoroethyl‐2,2,3,3‐tetrafluoropropyl ether (TTE) at a 1:2 volume ratio (salt‐in‐solvent: TTE).[ 45 ]

Electrode and Cell Fabrication

The cathode electrode slurries were prepared by blending the cathode active material, C65 conductive carbon, and poly(vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) in a 90: 5: 5 weight ratio, with an appropriate amount of N‐methyl‐2‐pyrrolidone (NMP). The mixture was homogenized in a planetary mixer at 2000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting slurry was uniformly cast onto aluminum foil, achieving an active material loading of ≈ 4.5 mA h cm−2 for all cell types used in this study, except for the cell paired with lithium for galvanostatic cycling and the cell paired lithium titanate (Li4Ti5O12, LTO) for galvanostatic electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (GEIS) measurements, in which the cathode had a lower areal loading of 1 mA h cm−2. Immediately after casting, the electrodes were dried in an oven at 100 °C. Once dried, they were punched into disks of 9/16 inch and 12 mm in diameter for OEMS cells and 2032‐type coin half cells, respectively, which were dried under vacuum at 120 °C overnight before use. NiO cathodes were prepared following the same procedure. The LTO slurry was prepared by mixing the active material, carbon black (C65), vapor‐grown carbon fiber (VGCF), and PVDF in a ratio of 94: 2: 3: 1, respectively, with an appropriate amount of NMP. The mixture was stirred at 2000 rpm for 8 min and uniformly cast onto aluminum foil. The coated electrodes were then dried in a 110 °C oven and punched into 9/16‐inch diameter disks, resulting in an active material loading of 1.5–1.8 mA h cm−2. All the cells were assembled in an argon‐filled glove box, consisting of a cathode electrode, Celgard 2325 separator, and a Li‐metal anode or LTO, with 40 µL of electrolyte injected.

Materials and Electrochemical Characterizations

An Apreo 2C LoVac scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to analyze the morphology of pristine cathode materials. Their structures were characterized by powder X‐ray diffraction (XRD) with a Rigaku Miniflex 600 X‐ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation, scanning over an angular range of 10°–80°. OEMS was employed for outgassing analysis. Measurements were carried out with a commercial PAT‐Cell‐Gas (EL‐CELL). The OEMS experiments start with a 4‐h rest at OCV to purge the cell completely and reach equilibria in the mass spectrometry. The cell was then charged at a C/10 rate (1C = 180 mA g−1) to an upper cut‐off voltage of 4.4 V versus Li⁺/Li, followed by a 6‐h constant‐voltage (CV) hold and 10‐h rest to monitor OCV. Potentiostatic electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (PEIS) analysis was carried out for each cell after a 6‐h constant CV hold at the respective cut‐off voltage. All measured gas evolutions are reported per unit mass of active material. A detailed description of the complete OEMS setup and validation of data processing can be found in the previously published work.[ 32 ]

Linear Sweep Voltammetry (LSV) tests were conducted in the OEMS cell configuration with NiO electrodes paired with a Li‐metal anode. The measurements were carried out at a sweep rate of 0.05 mV s−1 from 3.4 to 4.8 V (vs Li⁺/Li).

GEIS measurements were conducted on LTO | cathode cells with a BioLogic VMP3 potentiostat. GEIS was performed with a direct current at a C/10 rate (2.8 V cutoff voltage) and a superimposed current perturbation at a C/20 rate. Impedance spectra were collected over a frequency range of 500 kHz–100 mHz, with each data point acquired over a total duration of 10 min. Distribution of relaxation time (DRT) analysis was carried out with the open‐access DRTtools package, developed by Wan et al., which applies Bayesian ridge regression for DRT computation.[ 46 ]

NMR spectroscopy was employed to investigate soluble electrolyte decomposition products after one month of aging at 60 °C. Spectral data were obtained with a Bruker Avance NEO 400 MHz NMR spectrometer. For sample preparation, 500 µL of the aged electrolyte was mixed with 100 µL of D6‐DMSO, transferred into an NMR tube, and securely sealed with Parafilm inside an argon‐filled glove box to prevent contamination.

X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was conducted with a VersaProbe 4 spectrometer equipped with an Al Kα radiation source (1486.6 eV). To probe the CEI composition, Ar⁺ ion sputtering (5 kV, 20 nA) was performed for 600 s, followed by a subsequent XPS scan. Prior to analysis, the aged cathode electrodes were gently rinsed with dimethyl carbonate to remove residual electrolyte. The cathode powder was then carefully scraped from the current collector and mounted onto the XPS sample holder with a conductive tape before being transferred into the vacuum chamber for measurement.
